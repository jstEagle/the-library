## 7. Ninety-Nine Roads, One Pass

Halvard had forged a hundred documents in his life, and he could tell you the truth about all of them: paper was easy. Paper forgave you. It was people who were hard to fake.

He sat in the back of the derelict mill outside Grayhollow with a Conclave seal pressed into warm wax, and thought about how strange it was that his best work would never be seen by anyone. The writs were beautiful — heavy stock, correct watermarks, the archmagister's cipher rendered so precisely that Halvard half expected the ink to start giving orders. Nine passes sealed, and here were nine sets of papers that said otherwise: *authorized survey party, ward-restoration detail, by order of the Sunward Conclave.*

"You're smiling," Sefi said from the doorway. Her arm was bound in a sling, her color still wrong since the river crossing, but her eyes were sharp as ever.

"I'm proud of my work."

"You're proud of committing fraud against a theocracy."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive." He blew on the wax and set it beside its seven siblings. "Besides, it's not fraud if the authority is illegitimate. It's *counterfeit legitimacy*. Entirely different crime."

Rook laughed from somewhere near the mill's broken roof, where she'd been keeping watch for six hours straight. "Tell that to the warden at Kestrel Pass."

"The warden at Kestrel Pass will be too busy reading my flawless handwriting to count our heads."

But when Sefi had gone back inside and Rook had climbed down, Halvard sat alone with the last writ unsealed, and let himself think the thing he'd been not-thinking for two days: that the papers wouldn't matter. That the Conclave hadn't sealed the passes because they believed smugglers needed roads. They'd sealed them because they knew exactly where Beatrix Okafor was going, and they wanted her to know they knew.

Nine roads closed like nine fingers closing into a fist.

Which left the tenth road. The one that wasn't a road at all.

* * *

They gathered around the map that evening — or what Ilsa insisted on calling a map, though it looked more like a confession scrawled across old vellum. The Underthorn. A labyrinth of caverns beneath the Thornfell range, mapped once, badly, by miners who mostly hadn't come back. Every smuggler knew the name. Every smuggler avoided it.

"There are three entrances," Ilsa said, tapping charcoal marks. "Only one doesn't flood. And there's no guarantee the tunnels connect to anything anymore. The mountain shifted after the wards fell. The whole range is... unsettled."

"Unsettled," Rook repeated. "The mountain has feelings now."

"It always had feelings," Ilsa said quietly. "We just couldn't hear them before."

Beatrix stood at the edge of the lamplight, saying nothing. She'd been doing more of that lately — standing just outside the circle, one hand resting over the covenant mark on her forearm, listening to something none of them could hear. When she finally spoke, her voice was flat and certain in the way that frightened Halvard most.

"The ember wants to go home. It'll pull us the right way underground. I'll feel it."

"And if it pulls us wrong?" Corvin asked. He sat apart from the others, as he always did, hands folded like a man at his own trial. "If what's left of your god is as lost down there as we are?"

"Then we're lost together," Beatrix said. "Same as up here."

Halvard looked around the lamplight at all of them — the exile priestess, the penitent traitor, the twins, the silent god-bearer — and felt the old familiar ache in his chest, the one he usually drowned in ink and ale. He'd spent ten years perfecting the art of being alone in a room full of people. Somewhere between Grayhollow and the river crossing, these idiots had ruined that for him.

"We'll need a guide," he said. "Someone who knows the Underthorn."

"I know someone," Rook said, and her voice did something complicated on the word *know*.

* * *

The someone was named Vetch, and Halvard distrusted him instantly, thoroughly, and with great professional appreciation.

Vetch was a tracker — lean, weathered, with the permanent squint of a man who'd spent decades reading ground instead of faces. He met them at the flooded mouth of the Underthorn with two mules, a crate of glowmoss lamps, and a smile that arrived a half-second after everything else he said.

"Halvard the Forger," Vetch said, gripping his hand. "Heard you died in Marrowgate."

"Exaggerated. I died in three other cities first."

"Then this should feel familiar." Vetch's eyes swept the crew — lingered on Beatrix's marked arm, on Corvin's Conclave-cut robes, on Sefi's sling — and Halvard watched him catalog everything with a tracker's patience. "Underthorn's bad this season. Mountain's still settling. You pay me enough, I get you through. You pay me extra, I pretend I didn't see what you're carrying."

"What am I carrying?" Beatrix asked.

"A dying god," Vetch said pleasantly. "What else walks toward Thornfell's heart these days?"

Nobody laughed. Vetch shrugged and started loading the mules.

That night, Halvard lay awake under the cold stars — real stars again, out here past the last ward-towers, though thinner than they used to be, like a fire burning down — and turned over the memory of Vetch's eyes doing their patient arithmetic. Trackers sold information the way Halvard sold paper: professionally, without sentiment, to whoever paid. It wasn't even betrayal, really. It was trade.

He told himself the unease was just his own guilt looking for company.

He almost believed it.

* * *

The Underthorn swallowed them on the third morning, and Halvard learned quickly why the miners' maps ended where they did.

The caverns were vast and wrong. Glowmoss lit the walls in patches of sickly green, revealing stone that veined and branched like living wood — the roots of the mountain, Ilsa said, the deep thornwork that the veil grew from. The air tasted of iron and old rain. Sound behaved strangely; their footsteps arrived late, as if the dark had to consider them first.

And through it all, Beatrix walked with her hand half-raised, following something none of them could see, turning left where the passage forked, descending where the floor dropped away, never hesitating, never explaining.

"She's different down here," Rook murmured to Halvard as they climbed a ridge of broken stone. "Have you noticed?"

"Everyone's different down here. There's less of them to be."

"No. I mean—" Rook glanced ahead, lowered her voice further. "She stopped saying the ember's name. She used to talk to it. Now she just... listens. Like it's talking louder. Or like she's hearing less."

Halvard filed that away with all the other things he was choosing not to say aloud. They had enough weight to carry.

On the second day underground, the passage narrowed into a gallery of white stone, and Vetch called a halt. "Cave-in ahead. Old one. We go single file, quiet, no lamps past this point — moss-light only. Anything bigger than a rat hears us, we're done."

They went through in silence, pressed shoulder to shoulder against the white walls, and Halvard found himself walking behind Corvin. Up close, in the moss-light, the disgraced priest looked hollowed out — cheekbones sharp, hands trembling faintly, lips moving in what might have been prayer or might have been rehearsal for a confession he still hadn't finished making.

"You pray?" Halvard whispered, mostly to be cruel, partly to know.

Corvin didn't look back. "I'm not sure anyone's listening. But I figure the habit costs nothing."

"That's the worst theology I've ever heard."

"It's the only kind I have left."

Ahead, Beatrix paused at a fork where the white stone split like a wound, and stood so long that Vetch began to fidget. Then she turned right — away from the route Vetch had traced on his palm an hour earlier — and walked into the dark without a word.

Vetch's face, in the moment before he smoothed it, did its arithmetic again.

* * *

It was Sefi who caught it, because Sefi always caught things — the wrongness in a forged signature, the lie in a merchant's smile, and now, on the fourth day, the wrongness in their own trail.

She pulled Halvard aside at the rest stop, her good hand tight on his sleeve. "The cairns. Vetch's cairns. Look at them."

Halvard looked. Small stacks of stone, marking the route back, built at every turn. Perfectly ordinary.

"Count them," Sefi said.

He counted. Then he counted again, cold spreading through his chest. "There's an extra. One too many. He built a false marker — a trail that doubles back—"

"He's marking a second route. Or he's marking ours for someone else to follow." Sefi's voice stayed level, which was how Halvard knew she was furious. "I've been watching him sleep, Hal. He doesn't sleep. He waits."

They told the others at the next halt, quietly, in a huddle behind a wall of root-stone while Vetch tended the mules twenty paces off. Beatrix listened with that terrible calm. Corvin stared at the ground. Ilsa looked oldest Halvard had ever seen her.

"Conclave trackers can move fast when they know the ground," Ilsa said. "If he's signaled them—"

"He hasn't signaled anyone yet," Rook said. "No birds down here, no fires. Which means whatever he's selling, he hasn't delivered it. We can still be ahead of it."

"So we kill him," Sefi said. Not a question. Not cruelty either — just arithmetic, the same arithmetic Vetch did with his eyes.

"No," Beatrix said.

Everyone turned. She was looking down the passage where the ember pulled her, and when she spoke again her voice was quiet and absolute.

"We don't become them. That's the whole point of any of this. If we start deciding who's worth killing to save ourselves, then the Conclave already won, and we're just carrying their fire for them." She looked at each of them in turn. "We tie him. We take the mules. We leave him somewhere with water and a way out, and we move faster than any message he could send."

"And if he gets loose?" Sefi asked.

"Then he gets loose knowing we showed him mercy," Beatrix said. "Let him do his own arithmetic with that."

Halvard wanted to argue. He was, by profession and temperament, a man who believed in contingencies, and mercy was a contingency with terrible odds. But he looked at Beatrix's face — at the mark on her arm, faintly luminous now, always faintly luminous down here — and he understood that she wasn't offering mercy to Vetch. She was offering it to them. Drawing the line they'd all need later, when the choices got worse.

So they tied him. Vetch didn't struggle, which was somehow worse. He watched them with that patient squint while Rook knotted the ropes, and said only:

"You're making a mistake, Okafor."

"We make those professionally," Halvard said. "It's a family business."

They left him in a side chamber with a waterskin and a glowmoss patch, and they took the false cairn route deliberately — doubling back along the trail Vetch had built for whoever came next, then cutting hard left where Beatrix's unseen thread pulled them.

It bought them half a day. Maybe less.

* * *

The battle, when it came, came in the dark, and Halvard would spend the rest of his life remembering it as sound.

It started as light — far behind them, a bloom of white-gold in the black, ward-lanterns, Conclave issue, dozens of them, swarming up the passage like a lit fuse. Then the sound: boots, armor, the flat ritual cadence of hunters reciting binding-verse as they ran. The verse made the air thick; Halvard felt it press on his chest like deep water.

"They found the chamber," Rook breathed. "He talked."

"Or they read his cairns," Ilsa said. "Either way — run."

They ran. The passage narrowed, widened, dropped; the ember-pull dragged Beatrix forward and the crew dragged itself after her, and behind them the light gained, patient as tide. Halvard's lungs burned. His legs were fifty years old and informing him of it in detail. Somewhere in the chaos he lost his satchel of spare writs and did not stop for it, which told him everything about how bad things had gotten.

At a junction where the tunnel split three ways, Ilsa skidded to a stop. "Here. Here we hold, or we die strung out in a corridor." She was already pulling salt and chalk from her pack — old ward-priest habits, repurposed. "I can raise a screen, sixty seconds of it, maybe less. Enough to break their line. Then we take the middle passage and collapse it behind us."

"With what?" Rook demanded.

Ilsa looked at Corvin.

Corvin went very still. "You want me to use the breaking-verse."

"I want you to use the only thing you're still good at," Ilsa said, and there was no cruelty in it, only truth. "You broke the wards. Break this tunnel. Make it mean something this time."

For a heartbeat Halvard thought he'd refuse. Then Corvin set his jaw, and something moved through his face — shame transmuting, in real time, into purpose — and he began to speak.

The fight lasted four minutes and felt like four years. Ilsa's screen flared up, a shimmering lattice of salt-light, and the first wave of hunters broke against it like water on glass. Rook and Sefi fought at the edges — sling and knife, twin-coordinated, wordless, beautiful in the ugly way fights are. Halvard, who had never been brave, discovered that terror and usefulness could occupy the same body: he threw stones, shouted false positions in a warden's cadence he'd once forged orders in, and kept Ilsa's chalk line intact when a stray blade nearly scored through it.

Beatrix stood in the center of it all and did nothing — except that the mark on her arm burned brighter and brighter, and the hunters faltered as they neared her, stumbling, shielding their eyes, as if walking into a wind that came from nowhere.

Then Corvin finished the verse, and the ceiling came down.

Not all at once — in a rolling roar, a white avalanche of root-stone, sealing the junction and burying the lantern-light and the screaming and the verse-chanting under forty feet of mountain. Dust filled the world. Halvard coughed until he saw stars that weren't stars.

When the air cleared, they counted heads by touch. All present. All breathing. Sefi bleeding from a graze, Rook limping, Ilsa gray-faced and empty-handed, her salt spent. Corvin on his knees in the rubble, staring at what he'd done, whispering, "It means something. It means something now."

And Beatrix — Beatrix was looking at the fresh wall of stone with an expression Halvard had never seen on her, and when she turned to them, the mark on her arm had gone strangely dim.

"It heard him," she said softly. "Down here, in its own roots. It heard a man break something on purpose, to protect people, and it *heard*." She pressed her hand flat against the rock. "It's stronger. It's pulling harder. We're close."

Halvard sat down heavily against the tunnel wall, dust in his teeth, heart hammering, and laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound that echoed down the passage ahead of them, into the deep places of the mountain.

Because that was when he understood what they'd all been becoming, day by day, mile by mile, down here in the dark where no one could see them: not a crew, not a contract, not a collection of liars and exiles and failures.

A family. Forged the way anything worth having is forged.

In fire. In the dark. Together.

He got up, brushed off the dust, and followed the light of Beatrix's arm deeper into the Underthorn — toward the heart of the mountain, and whatever waited there.

Behind them, faint through the fallen stone, came a sound that froze every one of them mid-step.

Singing. Ward-hunters' voices, raised in unison — but not behind anymore.

*Around.*
