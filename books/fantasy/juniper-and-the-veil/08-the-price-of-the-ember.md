## 8. The Price of the Ember

Ilsa noticed it first in the way Beatrix said Rook's name.

They were three days out from the Hollow Shrine, camped in the lee of a granite shelf with the Underthorn's mouth a black seam on the horizon. Halvard was arguing about rations. Sefi, bandaged and stubborn, was winning the argument by eating twice her share. And Beatrix had looked straight at the twins — both of them, Sefi and Rook, identical down to the scar on their left brows — and said, "Rook, give her your cloak before she freezes."

It was Sefi who needed the cloak.

A small thing. The kind of slip anyone might make after a sleepless week. But Ilsa had spent eleven years as a ward-priest reading the grammar of marks and bindings, and she knew what she'd seen: not confusion. Subtraction. As if a word had been lifted cleanly out of Beatrix's vocabulary and replaced with its nearest neighbor.

She said nothing that night. She watched instead.

* * *

The mark had changed.

Ilsa saw it the next morning when Beatrix stripped to wash at a snowmelt stream. The covenant sigil — once a closed keyhole, then a burning ring when Juniper's ember had settled into it — now spiraled inward, each coil fainter than the last, like a snail shell drawn in fading ink. And at the center, where the ember sat, the skin was smooth. Unmarked. New.

That was how it fed. Ilsa understood it with a priest's cold clarity and a friend's rising horror. The ember could not burn mortal flesh for fuel; Juniper had built it too well for that. So it took something softer. Something that lived in the same place the mark did — memory, identity, the self written into the body's deepest ledger. It ate outward from the center, oldest memories first, names before faces, faces before love.

Nine days to the mountain's heart, Corvin had said. The ember would not wait politely until they arrived.

Ilsa dried her hands on her coat and made a decision that felt like treason against everything she'd sworn as a girl in white robes: she would not tell the crew. Not yet. Not while Sefi still walked with a spear-scar pulling at her side and Rook flinched at every hawk overhead. Fear was a weight none of them could carry up the mountain.

But she would not let it happen in silence either. Silence was what had killed the old order — priests who saw the wards failing and said nothing, Conclaves that harvested gods and called it stewardship. If the ember was going to take Beatrix Okafor apart piece by piece, then someone was going to write down every single piece it took.

That evening, by the light of a shielded lantern, Ilsa opened the back pages of her route-book — the one with the smuggler's ciphers no Conclave reader could crack — and began a new ledger.

*Day one. She called Sefi "Rook." Corrected herself after four seconds.*

*Day two. She asked Halvard where he'd learned to forge. He told the story of the Vellum Street apprenticeship. She laughed in the right places. She has heard that story six times. This is the first time she heard it.*

Her hand shook on that last line. She pressed it flat and kept writing.

* * *

By day four, Beatrix had stopped saying Halvard's name entirely. She called him "old man," which everyone assumed was affection because Beatrix was generous with nicknames, and only Ilsa knew it was because the name had gone somewhere the ember couldn't follow it back from.

They climbed through a pass where the veil bled through the rock — thin places, Corvin called them, where Thornfell's magic seeped like groundwater and the moss glowed faintly green at midnight. Beautiful country. Wrong country. In the thin places, Beatrix's mark burned brighter, and Ilsa could watch the spiral tighten in real time if she looked long enough.

She looked too long. Beatrix caught her at it.

"Say what you're thinking, priest."

"I'm not a priest anymore."

"You'll be a priest until you die. It's in how you stand." Beatrix wrung out her socks and sat beside her on the ledge, close enough that their shoulders touched. Below them, the twins argued over a card game whose rules changed every hand, and Halvard pretended to referee. "You've been watching me like I'm a patient. Like I'm a ward with a fracture running through it. So say it."

Ilsa had rehearsed this conversation forty times. Every version ended with Beatrix furious, or worse, grateful — trusting Ilsa with the truth and thereby making Ilsa responsible for what came next.

What came out instead was quiet. "How many of us can you name right now?"

Beatrix went still. The wind moved her hair. Somewhere below, Rook cheated outrageously and Sefi threw a pebble at him.

"Four," Beatrix said. Then, slower: "Four. You, the old man, the twins. That's — that's all of us. That's everyone." Her voice held steady on the count and cracked on the certainty, because she was checking, Ilsa realized, actually counting us in her head like a woman recounting coins after noticing one missing. "Why? Why are you asking me that?"

"No reason."

"Liar."

"Yes," Ilsa said. "But a kind one. Let me keep it a few more days."

Beatrix stared at her, and Ilsa watched her decide — watched the mercenary's discipline win over the hunger to know. It cost her. Ilsa could see it costing her. But Beatrix Okafor had carried a silent god for three years without complaint; she knew how to hold a weight she didn't understand.

"All right," she said finally. "But Ilsa —" and there it was, her name, intact, and Ilsa had to turn her face toward the valley so it wouldn't show what that cost either — "if you're keeping something because you think I can't carry it, remember who carried you out of the Sunward cells with two arrows in her back."

"I remember," Ilsa said. *Day four,* she wrote later, by lantern light, while the camp slept. *She knows something is wrong. She is letting me protect her, which is the worst thing she could do, because she thinks she is protecting me back.* She paused, pen hovering. *She remembered my name today. She said it like it mattered. I am going to write down every time she says our names, so that when the ember takes them, there will be a record. Someone will know we existed to her. Someone will know she loved us before she forgets that she did.*

*If she forgets that she did.*

* * *

On the sixth night, Beatrix forgot Sefi.

Not the name — the name survived, barely, retrieved after a visible struggle, hauled up from somewhere deep like a bucket from a well. What she lost was smaller and infinitely worse. Sefi came back from scouting the switchbacks with frost in her lashes, grinning, tossing Ilsa a strip of dried plum, and Beatrix looked at her with the polite blankness of a stranger meeting a friendly face in a tavern.

"Have we met?" she asked. "You seem — familiar. Have we worked together?"

The camp went silent. Rook's cards stopped mid-shuffle. Halvard's pipe went out.

Sefi recovered first, because Sefi always recovered first; she'd been shot three weeks ago and had complained mostly about the quality of the bandages. "Worked together," she repeated. "Oh, that's rich. Who do you think pulled you out of the river at Kessing Ford, you ungrateful wretch? Who carried your pack for two days when you took fever? You owe me eleven silver and a sincere apology, and I intend to collect both."

And Beatrix — Beatrix played along. Ilsa watched her do it, watched her read the room in half a heartbeat and choose the performance, laughing, throwing an arm around Sefi's shoulders, saying "eleven silver, was it? Add interest," while behind her eyes was nothing but courteous fog. She didn't know. She was improvising around the hole in herself the way water flows around a stone, seamlessly, and only Ilsa could see the shape of what was missing.

Ilsa walked to the edge of camp and vomited into the dark.

When she came back, she opened the ledger with steady hands, because her hands had learned steadiness in the cells beneath the Conclave and they would not betray her now.

*Day six. She forgot Sefi. Not the name — the person. Sefi covered for her. They all did, without being asked, without knowing why. Rook invented a debt story in under ten seconds. Halvard backed it with details. This family lies beautifully when it loves someone.*

*Correction: lies lovingly. We are not beautiful tonight. We are terrified, and we are lying, and it is the same thing.*

*She remembered Sefi's name an hour later, in her sleep. She said it out loud, twice, like a prayer checking its own god. I wrote it here so it will exist somewhere outside her head. If the ember wants her memories, it will have to burn through mine first, page by page. Let it come. I have been burned by better institutions than a dying star.*

She blew out the lantern. Across the dead fire, Beatrix slept with her marked arm curled against her chest like a woman holding a wound, and in the dark Ilsa could just see the spiral turning, faintly luminous, tighter than yesterday.

Tomorrow it would take more. Tomorrow or the day after, it would stop taking names and start taking faces, and then the years, and then — Ilsa refused to finish the thought. There were three days of climbing left. Three days, and then the mountain's heart, and whatever waited there.

She lay back and listened to the wind move through the thin place, carrying that faint green glow across the rocks like pollen.

Near dawn, Beatrix woke screaming.

Ilsa reached her first, lantern lit, blade drawn, scanning for hunters — but there was nothing. No ambush. Only Beatrix sitting bolt upright, gasping, clutching her marked arm, staring at the five faces crowded around her with an expression Ilsa had never seen on her before and hoped never to see again.

It was the look of a woman waking in a room full of strangers.

"I know you," Beatrix whispered. Her eyes moved from face to face, hunting, pleading. "I know I know you. Tell me. Please — someone tell me who you are."

Ilsa took her hand, pressed the route-book into it, and said, "Read."
