## 9. Heart of the Veil

The mountain's heart was not a cave. It was a wound.

Beatrix felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the stone, the way you feel a tooth gone bad before your tongue finds it. The Underthorn had spat her out three days ago into a valley of black glass where nothing grew and nothing sang, and she had climbed since then, alone, with an ember in her chest that ate her memories like a slow fire eating a letter.

She could no longer remember Halvard's laugh. She knew she had loved it. She knew there had been a sound, warm and rolling, that had meant *safe* — but when she reached for it, all she found was the fact of it, like a pressed flower with no scent.

*Ilsa is writing them down,* she reminded herself, one foot in front of the other on the glass slope. *Ilsa has a book. Everything I lose, she keeps.*

It didn't help. But it kept her walking.

The ember pulsed against her sternum, a second heartbeat, fainter now than it had been at the shrine. Nine days, Ilsa had said. They were on day eight. Somewhere behind her, cut off by the Conclave's hunters, were four people who had bled for her across half of Thornfell, and Beatrix had made them stay behind at the last fork because someone had to survive to tell the story if she failed.

She'd told Rook it was tactics. Rook hadn't believed her. Rook never believed her lies, which was why Beatrix told them to him so rarely.

The tunnel mouth opened in the mountainside like a throat, ringed with veins of pale light — the last threads of the old wards, still clinging to Juniper's bones. Beatrix stepped through, and the mountain closed around her, and she understood why they called it the heart.

The chamber was vast and round as a held breath. Every surface glittered with ward-glass, shattered and re-fused a thousand times over centuries of mending. And at its center, suspended in a lattice of golden light, hung what remained of her god: a knot of ember-light no bigger than a fist, dimming even as she watched, like a coal breathing its last.

Around it stood the Conclave.

A dozen figures in sun-white robes, arranged in a crescent, staffs planted, faces hidden behind masks of hammered gold. And at their center, stepping forward with the unhurried grace of a man who has never once been told *no*, came the archmagister.

He was older than she expected. Younger, too. His face shifted like something seen through water — silver-haired, then dark, then silver again — and Beatrix realized with a lurch that he wasn't wearing a glamour. He was wearing *harvested years*, dozens of them, layered like coats.

"Beatrix Okafor." Her name in his mouth sounded like a title being read from a ledger. "Bearer of the last ember of Juniper. Do you know how long we have waited for you?"

"I figure about eight days," she said. "Give or take."

Something behind his eyes flickered — irritation, quickly smoothed. "You've lost memories to the ember. Your speech patterns are degrading. You're frightened."

"I'm always frightened. It's how I stay alive." She walked forward, slowly, boots crunching on ward-glass. The gold-masked priests tensed. The archmagister raised two fingers and they stilled. "You want to talk. So talk. But I'm going to keep walking while you do it, because my legs work better than my head these days, and I don't trust men who wear other people's faces."

"A pragmatic woman. Good. Then let me be equally pragmatic." He spread his hands, and the light around the dying ember bent toward him like wheat toward the sun. "You believe we broke the wards out of greed. Corvin told you this, yes? Our repentant little instrument. He wasn't lying — he simply doesn't understand what he served. The veil is *dying*, Beatrix. It has been dying for three hundred years, since Juniper poured itself into it and began to starve. We did not break the border. We amputated a rotting limb so the body might live."

"And replace it with a machine," Beatrix said. "One you control. One that runs on harvested magic instead of a god's sacrifice. Ilsa read your harvest ledgers, Archmagister. I may not remember her face anymore, but I remember that. Whole villages drained to gray. Children born without shadows."

"Necessary costs—"

"Everything's a necessary cost," Beatrix said. "To the man who isn't paying it."

The archmagister sighed, and for just a moment the layered faces beneath his face went still, and she saw something almost human: exhaustion. "I had hoped to offer you reason. Very well. I'll offer you the other thing." He gestured, and the golden lattice around the ember unfurled like a blooming flower, threads of light reaching toward her. "Restoration. That is what you want. What you have climbed a mountain to want. Give us the ember, and we will rebuild the wards under Conclave stewardship — stable, permanent, *kind*. And you—" his voice softened, went gentle as a priest at a deathbed, "—you get everything back. Your god, whole and singing. Your covenant mark, restored. Three years of silence explained, forgiven, undone. And every memory the ember has eaten. Halvard's laugh. The twins' names. Ilsa's face. All of it returned, Beatrix. You would walk out of here whole."

The chamber went very quiet.

Beatrix stopped walking.

Because here was the terrible thing, the thing she would never say aloud to anyone: for one full breath, she wanted it. She wanted it the way a drowning woman wants air. She stood in the heart of the mountain with holes in her mind where her family used to be, and the archmagister was offering to fill them, and Juniper — Juniper would sing again. She would feel her god's presence the way she had at nineteen, kneeling in a roadside shrine, certain for the first time in her life that she was held.

"You're crying," the archmagister observed.

"Am I." She wiped her cheek with the back of her hand. "Huh. Must be the altitude."

"Take the offer," he said. "No one needs to know what it cost. No one will judge you. Even the ones you love — especially the ones you love — would forgive you."

And that was when Beatrix started laughing.

It came up out of her rough and ragged, echoing off the ward-glass, and the gold-masked priests shifted uneasily because madwomen were worse than armed ones. She laughed until her ribs hurt, and then she looked up at the archmagister with wet eyes and a grin like a drawn blade.

"That's the thing," she said. "You really don't get it. You think love is a debt I'd pay down. You think if I can't remember them, the loving stops mattering." She pressed her fist against her sternum, against the ember, against the hollow places. "But I forgot Sefi's name two days ago. Forgot which twin laughs first. And I still climbed this mountain. Do you understand? I don't remember *why* I love them. I just do. It's the only thing the ember couldn't reach, because it isn't a memory. It never was."

"The covenant—"

"The covenant was a lock," Beatrix said. "Your word, more or less. Corvin's too. A lock keeps people out. Keeps a god caged in a border so nobody has to talk to each other ever again." She drew her knife — the old smuggler's knife, Halvard-forged, the only gift she had left that she could still name. "So here's my counteroffer, Archmagister. I break the lock."

* * *

She moved before they could.

Not toward the ember — toward the nearest thread of the golden lattice, the oldest one, thick as her wrist, humming with three years of Juniper's poured-out divinity. The priests shouted. Light flared. Someone loosed a lance of white fire that she took across the shoulder, spinning her, filling her mouth with the taste of copper and ozone.

She got the knife against the thread anyway.

"This mark," she gasped, holding up her forearm, the covenant sigil blazing like a brand, "was the keyhole. Which means—" she set the blade against her own skin, right across the mark, "—the key was me."

She cut.

The pain was total. Not flesh-pain — deeper, structural, like being unmade one law at a time. The covenant mark split down its center, and every ward in the chamber screamed, and the archmagister roared something in a language that predated his stolen faces, and the lattice convulsed—

—and the ember fell.

It dropped out of its cage like a ripe fruit, and Beatrix caught it against her bare chest with both hands, and it *sank in*.

* * *

There was no pain after that. There was no anything. There was light, and inside the light, a voice she had not heard in three years — not screaming now, not silent, but *tired*, so tired, and grateful the way only the dying are grateful.

*You came,* said Juniper.

"Course I came," Beatrix said, though her mouth wasn't moving, though she wasn't sure she had a mouth anymore. "You never abandoned us. You just ran out of yourself. Somebody should've carried you home sooner."

*The wards need a keeper. Not a god. A god burns out. A keeper—*

"Yeah," she said. "I know. I've been a smuggler my whole life. Turns out I was training for this."

*It will cost—*

"It already cost. I paid on the road." And she thought — deliberately, fiercely — of four faces she could barely see anymore, and she poured the thought into the ember like water into dry ground. *Hold onto this. Hold onto them. If I can't remember them, then you remember them, and between us we make one whole person.*

The ember flared.

Then it spread — through her ribs, her blood, the marrow of her, and out, racing along the severed threads of the old wards, stitching them not back into the dead covenant but into something new, something with a heartbeat in it, something anchored in mortal loyalty instead of divine law. The chamber blazed white. The gold-masked priests staggered, their harvest-staffs cracking one by one like ice in spring.

Through the blaze, Beatrix heard the archmagister scream — not in rage. In *loss*. His borrowed faces were sloughing off him, years returning to the wards that had given them shape, and what was left underneath was small, and old, and ordinary.

The great doors of the chamber blew inward.

They came through the smoke battered, bleeding, impossible: Rook first with his blades out, then Halvard with a forged writ held up like a shield, then Ilsa with her book clutched to her chest, and between them, gray-faced and bandaged but upright, leaning on her brother's shoulder —

Sefi.

"You were supposed to stay behind," Beatrix said. Her voice came out strange — doubled, hers and something vaster speaking through the same throat.

"You lied to us," Rook said. "We voted. Mercy means we don't leave you alone either."

And Ilsa stepped forward, opened her book, and held it out — pages dense with her careful hand, every forgotten name, every lost laugh, every face recorded against the dark. "You said hold onto them," Ilsa said. "We did."

The new wards settled over the chamber like snowfall, soft and total, and Beatrix Okafor — half-mortal, half-keeper, wholly theirs — reached out and took the book.
