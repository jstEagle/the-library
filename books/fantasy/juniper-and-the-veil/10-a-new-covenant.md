## 10. A New Covenant

The mountain's heart did not have a heartbeat anymore. It had mine.

I felt it in the hours after — the slow, strange rhythm of the veil settling around me like a coat tailored to a body it had never measured. Every thread of it ran through my chest now. When a bird crossed from Thornfell into the human realm, I felt the crossing as a shiver along my ribs. When wind moved through the Underthorn, I tasted stone and moss. The border was no longer a wall or a ward or a god's dying breath.

It was me. And somehow, impossibly, I was still me.

That was the part I couldn't stop checking. I sat on the broken altar steps with my sleeves pushed back, turning my hands over, counting my fingers like a child. Ten. Still ten. Still Beatrix Okafor, smuggler, mercenary, heretic by three separate governments' reckoning.

Except that for one terrible stretch of road, I hadn't been all of myself. The ember had eaten its way backward through my head — Sefi's laugh first, then Rook's face, then Halvard's name, then whole seasons of my life gone soft and blank as fogged glass. I had walked the last two days of that march not knowing why four strangers kept looking at me like I was something worth dying for.

And then, at the heart of everything, when the archmagister offered me back my god at the price of everyone I loved, I had refused. I had poured the ember into myself instead, into mortal flesh, into a covenant written on loyalty instead of law.

The memories should have stayed lost. That was the arithmetic. Divine law said so. The old covenant would have let them go the way Juniper went — silent, spent, mourned.

But Ilsa had written them down.

* * *

She found me on the steps at dawn, if dawn still meant anything to someone who could feel the sun cross the veil like a hand moving across her skin. She was carrying her journal — the fat, water-stained one she'd kept since her ward-priest days, the one she'd filled on the road while I slept, writing down everything I forgot before the forgetting finished.

"You're doing the thing," she said, sitting down beside me without asking. "Where you check your hands."

"Making sure they're mine."

"They're yours." She opened the journal across both our knees. "So is this."

I looked down at the page. My own handwriting stared back at me — no. Her handwriting, copying mine, transcribing things I'd said half-asleep by firelight: *Sefi cheats at cards but only against people who deserve it. Rook hums when they're scared, always the same song, their mother taught them. Halvard forged his first document at eleven to keep his sister out of the conscription rolls. Ilsa left the priesthood because they asked her to burn a village's records and she couldn't.*

My throat closed. "I remember," I whispered. "Ilsa. I remember all of it."

"I know." Her voice cracked on the second word. "I've been watching you remember since we came down off the mountain. You said Rook's name this morning and you cried into your tea like it was nothing."

"It wasn't nothing."

"No." She pressed the journal into my hands. "It was the point. The old covenant only honored what gods remembered. The new one honors what mortals write down. You made that rule, Beatrix. You just didn't know you were making it yet."

I turned the pages slowly. Everything was there. Not just the names and faces the ember took — the small things too, the cargo manifests and card debts and the night Halvard got drunk and confessed he'd been forging my signature for two years because he liked the way I signed things better than he signed them himself. A life, documented. Witnessed. Held.

"The god is gone," I said. It wasn't a question. I'd known it since the moment the ember settled into me and went quiet — not dead, exactly, but diffused, spread so thin through the veil that there was no *Juniper* left to speak. Just weather. Just borders. Just the long breath of two worlds leaning against each other and holding.

"The god is gone," Ilsa agreed. "But look what it left behind."

* * *

We came down off the mountain into a world already arguing about what we'd done.

The Conclave didn't fall in a day. Institutions like that don't fall; they rot, and rot takes time. But Corvin helped it along. He walked into the Sunward Conclave's own chapterhouse three weeks after the mountain, in his ruined priest's robes, carrying every document he'd stolen on his way out — harvest schedules, ward-breaker orders, the archmagister's private correspondence about "reclaiming veiled assets." He laid them on the floor of the great hall and said, in a voice that carried, "I broke the wards. They told me to. Judge us both."

They did. Loudly, publicly, and for months. Half the Conclave's leadership ended up under arrest by their own enforcers, who had apparently been waiting years for permission. The other half scattered into exile, which suited me fine. I'd spent enough of my life being hunted; I had no appetite for hunting anyone else.

Corvin himself submitted to trial. The new priesthood — whatever it was becoming, something smaller and humbler and built around shrines instead of fortresses — sentenced him to service rather than silence. He spends his days now walking the border villages, teaching people how the wards actually worked, how Juniper had poured itself into them, how the silence had been sacrifice and not abandonment. He told me once, standing in the ruins of the hollow shrine where it all started, that he expected to spend the rest of his life earning back an inch at a time.

"Good," I said. "That's how covenants work now. An inch at a time."

He almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

The border itself became the strangest part.

With no Conclave, no ward-priests, no living god anchoring it, the veil needed a keeper — and the keeper was me, which meant the border answered to a smuggler with a criminal record in four jurisdictions. The first time a Thornfell trader crossed legally, I felt it happen and laughed so hard Sefi thought I was having some kind of fit.

There were negotiations, eventually. There are always negotiations. Human magistrates and Thornfell elders sat across tables from each other, glaring, while I stood between them feeling every word land in my chest like a stone dropped in water. The treaty they finally drafted was short, because I insisted. *The border belongs to no one,* it said. *The border belongs to everyone.* Crossing was free. Toll-taking was banned. Anyone who tried to seal the passes again would answer to the keeper, and the keeper had a crew, and the crew had reputations.

Halvard framed the first copy of the treaty and hung it in the new safehouse, right next to his forgery kit, which he swore he was retiring. He retired it for eleven days.

Sefi healed. Slowly, badly, with Rook fussing over her the entire time, but she healed. The scar runs from her shoulder to her elbow and she shows it off in taverns as proof that mercy was worth it. Rook stopped humming when they were scared somewhere around midwinter. I noticed. I notice everything now — it comes with the territory. Every crossing, every wind, every small human kindness paid across the veil lands somewhere in me and stays.

Ilsa still writes. The journal has siblings now, a whole shelf of them, and she's begun teaching others to keep witness-records the way she kept ours. "Memory is a covenant too," she says, in the classes she holds in the shrine's rebuilt hall. "Someone has to write it down, or it never happened."

Some nights I sit in that hall and listen to her voice and feel the veil breathe around me, and I think about the god who went silent so that any of this could exist. I don't pray. Prayer was the old covenant, and the old covenant is ash. But I talk sometimes, quietly, the way you talk at a grave that's become a garden. *You could have told us,* I say. *Three years of silence, and all you had to do was say one word.* And then I remember that a scream through a covenant mark was the only word it had left, and I shut up, and I pour two cups of tea anyway, and drink them both.

* * *

A year to the night after the stars went out, we went back to the pass where it happened.

The five of us — six, if you counted Corvin, and by then we did, though nobody said it out loud — climbed to the ridge where I'd stood with a dead sky overhead and a god screaming in my arm. We built a fire. Sefi cheated at cards. Halvard drank too much and told the story of the Underthorn battle with himself as the hero, and Rook corrected every detail, and Ilsa wrote it all down, laughing, getting it wrong on purpose because she said history should be a little kind.

At midnight I stepped away from the fire and stood at the edge of the veil, where Thornfell's dark trees met the human realm's dark hills, and I felt the seam of the world under my palms like the neck of a horse I was learning to trust.

No stars had gone out tonight. Above me the sky was thick with them, ordinary and enormous, and beyond the veil the same stars looked down on Thornfell, and there was no line in the light where one realm ended and the other began.

"Still here?" Halvard called from the fire.

"Still here," I said.

"Good. Because there's a job."

I turned. He was grinning, holding up a letter, and even before I read the wax seal I felt the shape of trouble coming through the veil like weather — something moving in the deep places of Thornfell, something the elders wanted looked at, something that would need a keeper and a crew and a route no honest map would show.

Sefi was already shuffling the deck. Rook was already humming.

I looked at my family around the fire, then at the border running quiet and alive beneath my feet, and I went back toward the light.
