## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The widow's grief sat in her chest like a stone dropped into a well, and Paloma Villalobos had learned to reach past it without disturbing the water.

He knelt beside her chair in the dim room above the candlemaker's shop on Vela Street, his fingers resting light as moth wings against her temple. She was called Doña Inés, though he'd never been told that by her—only by the landlady who arranged these meetings and took a cut for the trouble. The widow was small and gray and folded into herself like a letter no one wanted to open, and she had said yes in the smallest voice Paloma had ever heard, as if even consent embarrassed her.

"You'll feel a coldness," he murmured. "Like stepping out of the sun. It passes."

She nodded. Her hands were knotted in her lap around a handkerchief gone soft from washing.

Paloma closed his eyes and let the wolf-blood wake.

It was always like this: a warmth behind his sternum first, then a loosening, as though some door inside him that had been nailed shut since childhood swung open on oiled hinges. The magic didn't roar. That was the lie the priests told—that unlicensed magic announced itself, that it howled and smoked and left scorch marks on the soul. In truth it was quiet as breath. He reached into the widow's memory the way a thief reaches into a pocket, careful not to brush the lining, careful to take only what was offered.

There. Tucked behind decades of ash-gray days, folded small: a memory of snow.

Not Ceniza snow—the city hadn't seen true snowfall in a generation, only the gray ash-rain that gave it its name—but mountain snow, real snow, from before she came to the city, before the marriage, before the child who had died last winter of the lung-fever. A girl of nine standing at a window with frost ferns climbing the glass, watching the first flakes come down over a valley she would never see again. The taste of woodsmoke. Her sister's shriek of delight. The impossible hush, that particular silence snow makes, as if the whole world had agreed to whisper.

Paloma lifted it free.

It came away clean, a small bright thing, warm despite everything, and he cupped it in his mind the way one cups a coal carried between houses. He felt the widow shudder under his fingertips—not pain, exactly, but the strange vertigo of having something removed that you'd forgotten you owned. When he opened his eyes, she was looking at him with an expression he knew too well: relief braided through with loss, the face of someone who has set down a weight and immediately misses its shape against their back.

"Will I remember it?" she asked.

"Not while the charm holds." He drew a sliver of gray wax from his coat pocket, worked it soft between his palms, and pressed the memory into it the way a cook presses herbs into dough. The wax warmed, then cooled, taking on a faint pearlescence that no honest chandler could explain. "When the charm breaks—and they all break eventually—it returns to you. Nothing is truly lost. Only borrowed."

"Then why does it feel like a funeral?"

Because everything worth stealing feels like a funeral, he almost said. Instead he wrapped the charm in a square of blue cloth and tucked it into his inner pocket, where it lay against his heart like a second, smaller heart.

"The buyer pays four silver reales," he said. "You get three. Marta keeps one for the room." He rose, straightened his coat, and became, in the space of a breath, nobody—a thin young man of no particular color, no particular accent, the kind of face a witness describes for an hour without ever getting right. "Thank you, Doña Inés."

"I should thank you," she said, and then, quieter, staring at her empty hands: "I don't remember why I agreed to this."

* * *

The merchant who wanted to feel something kept a shop of imported silks off the Salt Market, and his name was Emilio Ferrán, and he was the loneliest man Paloma had ever robbed.

That was the trade's dirty secret, the one no one spoke aloud in the charm-shops that didn't exist and the alleys where transactions happened in whispers: most buyers weren't chasing pleasure. They were chasing proof. Proof that they had once been capable of joy, of terror, of love—the full animal riot of feeling that Ceniza's respectable life slowly sanded out of a person. The Memory Office called unlicensed magic a debt against the covenant, and maybe it was, but Paloma had never met a debtor who wasn't also starving.

"You have it?" Ferrán asked, before Paloma had even fully closed the shop door behind him. The merchant was fifty, soft-handed, dressed in a robe worth more than Paloma earned in a year, and his eyes had the glassy shine of a man who slept badly among beautiful things.

"I have it." Paloma produced the blue cloth but did not unwrap it. "First snowfall. Mountain snow, the real kind. A girl of nine, watching it come down with her sister. You will be her for an hour. You will taste woodsmoke and cold air and the specific happiness of a child who doesn't yet know what winter costs."

Ferrán's throat moved. "An hour," he repeated, hungry as a beggar.

"One hour. Then it fades until the charm breaks and goes home to its owner." Paloma held up one finger. "The usual terms. You never ask whose memory it was. You never seek out the seller. And if anyone from the Office asks—and they won't, because you were never here—you bought the charm from a dead man in the ash-towns, which is unfalsifiable and conveniently tragic."

"Yes. Yes, all of it." Ferrán was already reaching for his purse.

Paloma watched him press the wax charm to his own temple, watched the man's face go slack and then—slowly, terribly—young. Fifty years fell away like a shed coat. His mouth opened. A sound came out of him that might have been laughter or might have been weeping; with the deep memories it was usually both. He stood in his shop full of silks from six realms and was, for one hour, a nine-year-old girl in a valley that no longer existed, watching the sky perform its oldest miracle.

Paloma took the silver and let himself out.

Outside, the ash was falling again—Ceniza's perpetual weather, fine gray flakes that settled on shoulders and awnings and the leaded roofs of the Memory Office's citadel like the city itself was quietly dying and shedding skin. Paloma turned up his collar and became invisible. It was a skill more practiced than any magic: the slump of the shoulders that said *not worth stopping*, the pace that said *I belong here less than you do*, the eyes that met no one's. In a city where the priests could pull a memory out of your head with a word of doctrine and a length of blessed cord, the safest person was the forgettable one.

He had been forgettable for eleven years. He intended to be forgettable until he died of old age in a rented room, unmourned, unremarked, and—this was the part he told himself mattered—uncaught.

The wolf-blood stirred under his sternum, restless, and he pushed it down the way he pushed down every other inconvenient thing.

* * *

His room was on the top floor of a house in the Tanners' Reach, rented month to month from a landlady who asked no questions because Paloma paid in advance and smelled faintly of tallow, which she chose to interpret as an honest trade. It held a cot, a stool, a window overlooking the smoke-houses, and a loose floorboard beneath which lived the only possessions Paloma Villalobos truly cared about: forty-one charms in various states of brightness, a coil of silver wire, and a tin box he had not opened in five years.

He opened it now. He always opened it after a sale, though he couldn't have said why. Habit, or punishment—one of the two, and with Paloma it was usually both.

Inside the tin box lay a single charm, made of wax gone amber with age, wrapped in a scrap of cloth that had once been dyed juniper-blue. His mother's work. Lira Villalobos had been a charmer too, in the years before the raids, before the night the Memory Office came to their rooms on Candle Lane and she shoved a five-year-old boy out the washroom window with instructions to run and not look back, and he had run, and he had not looked back, and he had spent eleven years being proud of both.

The charm was hers. He had lifted it from the wreckage of their old life the way you lift a splinter from your own flesh. He had never pressed it to his temple. He had never needed to know what memory his mother had thought worth sealing in wax for him—her voice saying his name, perhaps, or just the ordinary golden nothing of a morning when they were together and didn't know it was the last one. Because knowing would make it real, and real things could be taken away, and Paloma had already lost everything once and had no intention of losing it twice.

So he touched other people's pasts instead. He sold widows their snowfalls and merchants their childhoods and lonely clerks the memory of a lover's laugh, and he charged fair prices and kept his hands steady and his face forgettable, and at night he lay on his cot listening to the tanneries breathe and told himself that competence was a kind of peace.

It was a good lie. He had practiced it for eleven years, and he was very nearly fluent.

* * *

Three days later, Marta brought him the widow's name again.

"She wants another one," the landlady said, counting out his cut of the last sale with her quick, dry fingers. "Same memory, she says. Or one like it. She says the hour it gave her was the best hour she's had since—" Marta shrugged. "Since. You know how it goes. They always come back. First taste is free, isn't that what the wine-sellers say?"

"It wasn't free," Paloma said. "She paid with a piece of herself."

"And got an hour of being nine years old for it. Show me the widow in Ceniza who wouldn't make that trade." Marta pocketed the coin and fixed him with a look that was sharper than her years suggested. "Careful with this one, though. She's been talking. Not about you—she doesn't remember enough to talk about you—but about the feeling. Telling her neighbors she feels lighter, like something sad got carried out of the house. People are starting to ask her who did the carrying."

Paloma went still. "The Office?"

"No. Not yet. Just neighbors. But neighbors talk to deacons, deacons talk to priests, and priests—" Marta made a small gesture, the one everyone in Ceniza made when they meant the citadel on the hill: fingers curled, as if snuffing a candle. "Be a rumor instead of a craftsman, boy. Rumors don't leave receipts."

He promised to be careful. He was always promising to be careful, and he was always careful, and it had worked for eleven years, and so there was no reason on earth for the cold that slid down his spine as he climbed the stairs to Doña Inés's room that evening—no reason except that the widow answered the door before he knocked, as if she'd been standing there waiting, and her eyes were wet, and she said:

"I dreamed it. Last night. The snow. I dreamed I was nine years old and my sister was alive and the whole valley was white, and I woke up and I don't own any such memory, I've checked, I've gone through my whole life like a woman going through drawers, and it isn't there, and yet I *dreamed it*—" She grabbed his wrist with shocking strength. "What did you take out of me? What did you take?"

"A memory you sold me," Paloma said gently. "Fairly. It comes back when the charm breaks. I told you—"

"When does it break? Tell me when. Give me a day, an hour."

"I can't. It's not—it doesn't work on a clock, it works on—"

"On what?" Her grip tightened. Behind her, in the little room, the candles were all burned down to puddles, dozens of them, as if she'd been keeping vigil. As if she'd been afraid of the dark in a way she hadn't been able to explain. "On what, boy?"

And Paloma, who had answers for merchants and deacons and his own conscience at three in the morning, found he had none. Because the truth was that charms broke when they broke, and sometimes they broke early, and sometimes—rarely, according to no text he dared read—they leaked. And a leaking charm in a grieving widow's house, in a city where the Memory Office paid informants in absolution, was not a business problem.

It was a bell tolling.

He calmed her as best he could, took her coin, promised to hasten the charm's return—which was a lie, because the charm was already sold and spent and sitting in Emilio Ferrán's silk-lined skull—and walked home through the ash-fall with his collar up and his mind working fast along the rooftops of contingency. He would move rooms. He would cool the trade for a month, let the widow's neighbors find fresher gossip. He had survived tighter spots than a leaky charm. He was the invisible man. Nothing touched him.

He was halfway up the outside stair to his room when he saw that his door stood open.

He had locked it. He always locked it—he had three locks, cheap ones, but locks—and the door now hung ajar on the dark of the room beyond, and from inside came a smell he knew from the one other time he had smelled it, eleven years ago, on a night of shouting and broken glass on Candle Lane.

Incense. Memory-priest incense, bitter as burnt hair.

Paloma stood on the stair in the falling ash, invisible man, careful man, competent man, and understood with perfect clarity that the life he had built was already over, and that the only question left was whether the next thing he did would be running or dying.

Below him, in the street, a dog began to bark—and stopped mid-note, as if a hand had closed around its throat.

Someone else was coming up the stairs.
