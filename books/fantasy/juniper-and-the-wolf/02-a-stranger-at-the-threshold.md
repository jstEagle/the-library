## 2. A Stranger at the Threshold

The knock came at the hour when Ceniza's bells had given up on the day and the lamps along the Vellum Row had burned down to their last finger of oil. Paloma knew every sound his building made—the settling groan of the third-floor joists, the drip in the wall that only ran after rain, the landlady's cat dragging its kill across the roof tiles above his head like a small wet drumroll. So when the knock came, three slow blows that seemed to cost the knocker something each time, he knew it was not the cat, and it was not the landlady, and it was not anyone who had ever knocked before.

He did not answer. That was the first rule of invisibility: never be home to a stranger.

But then came a sound worse than knocking—a body sliding down the other side of his door, the scrape of cloth on wood, the wet slap of something heavy meeting the floorboards.

Paloma stood very still in the middle of his room, one hand already inside his coat where he kept his charms wrapped in oilcloth. He was twenty-three years old and he had survived Ceniza by being no one: the boy with the forgettable face, the tenant who paid early and spoke softly, the charm-seller whose customers could never quite describe him afterward even when they tried. Seven years of careful nothing. And now the careful nothing had a dying man bleeding through its door.

"Open," the man said. Not *please*. Not *help*. Just *open*, in a voice like a hinge that had been left out in the weather too long.

Paloma told himself to go out the window. It was two floors down to the refuse yard; he'd done it before, in the early days, when a customer's wife had followed her husband's purchase home. He could be gone in ten heartbeats and by morning this room would belong to someone else's story.

Instead his hands—traitors, both of them—lifted the latch.

* * *

The man was older than Paloma had first guessed, though it was hard to tell age from ruin. His hair had gone white at the temples in patches, as if frost had taken him piecemeal. Blood had soaked through his shirt from a wound under the ribs, dark and steady, the kind that did not gush so much as *insist*. He looked up at Paloma with eyes that were doing arithmetic—counting him, measuring him, arriving at some conclusion with visible relief.

"You're hers," the man said.

"I think you've got the wrong room."

"Lira's boy." The name went through Paloma like a fishhook. No one had said his mother's name aloud in five years. He had almost convinced himself he'd invented her—that the woman in his oldest memory, the one with ash-colored braids and a laugh she bit back whenever priests walked past, had been someone he'd dreamed up to keep himself company. "I saw you once. At the salt gate. You were small enough to fit inside a memory-charm yourself."

Paloma crouched, keeping his weight ready to bolt. "Who are you? Who sent you?"

"No one sent me." The man laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing brought up blood he wiped on his sleeve without looking at it, the way a man does when he has stopped pretending the blood matters. "That's rather the point. I came because there was no one left to send." He pressed his palm flat against the floor and pushed himself upright against the doorframe, breathing in short, rationed sips. "Help me to your table. I can't say what I have to say lying down. It won't come out right."

Paloma should have run. Every instinct he had honed over seven years screamed it. But the man said *Lira* again—not aloud, just with his eyes—and Paloma found himself taking the stranger's arm, finding it light as kindling, and steering him to the rickety table beneath the window.

From inside his coat the man drew a roll of leather, tied with a cord stained nearly black. He set it on the table between them like a card player laying down everything he owned.

"The thirteenth turn," he whispered.

"What?"

"That's what they'll ask you about, if they find you holding this. The thirteenth turn. Say you don't know. Say it until they believe it or until they stop asking, whichever comes first." His fingers worked at the cord but couldn't manage the knot; his hands had begun to shake in a fine, constant way, like plucked strings. "Open it. I need to see you open it."

"Why me?"

"Because it opened for you." The man smiled, and it was the worst thing Paloma had seen all night, worse than the blood—a smile full of tenderness aimed at a total stranger. "Because I carried this thing through four cities and it stayed shut as a stone. Then I saw you buying bread on the Vellum Row three days ago, and it got warm in my coat. Warm as a living thing. Lira's blood, boy. It knows its own."

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. "You're mad. Or you're Office bait, and this is some new trick—" But even as he said it, his hand was moving toward the leather, drawn the way water is drawn downhill, and when his fingertips touched the cord the knot slipped loose on its own, untying itself like something exhaling after years of held breath.

He unrolled the map across the table.

It was beautiful in the way old things are beautiful, the way wounds are. Leather gone soft as skin, inked in silver that moved faintly when he wasn't looking directly at it—thirteen marks arranged in a ring around a shape he recognized with a lurch as Ceniza itself, its walls, its river, its seven districts rendered in lines finer than any cartographer's hand. Each mark was a gate. Each gate bore a sigil, and the sigils were wrong in a way he couldn't name, except that looking at them made the backs of his teeth ache and made the charms inside his coat hum against his ribs like caged insects.

And one sigil—the one at the ring's northern point, shaped like an open eye crossed by a crescent—made something behind his breastbone turn over and look at him.

"What is it?" he heard himself ask.

"The covenant," the man said. "Or what's left of it. Thirteen gates, thirteen moons, and a seal that's been rotting since before you were born. Your mother spent her life trying to mend it. She bought you time instead." He gripped Paloma's wrist with sudden strength, strength that should not have been in those ruined arms. "Listen to me. There isn't much of me left to listen with, so listen well. The thirteenth moon comes at winter's end. When it rises full, the seal fails. Everything the old guardians locked out comes through, and everything we are goes under. You have to—"

Boots on the stairs.

Not the landlady's shuffle. Not a neighbor's tread. Boots in formation, four pairs at least, climbing with the disciplined patience of men who had done this many times and enjoyed it none and some.

The man's grip tightened. "Back window. Now."

"They'll see me cross the yard—"

"Roofs." He shoved the map into Paloma's chest, folding Paloma's own fingers closed around it. "Ceniza's roofs are a country of their own. Climb like you were born to it." A ghost of the smile again. "Your mother was."

The boots reached the landing. A voice, flat and official, pronounced the words every soul in Ceniza dreaded: "Memory Office. Open in the name of the Covenant Act."

There was no time to be afraid, which was the only mercy of it. Paloma shoved the map into his shirt, took one step toward the window—

—and stopped, because the dying man caught his sleeve one last time.

"Name's Aldric," he said. "In case anyone asks. Tell them Aldric kept the map dry." And then, softer, with his eyes already going somewhere else: "Tell her son she didn't leave him. Whatever they told him. Whatever he remembers. She didn't—"

The door burst inward.

* * *

What happened next, Paloma would spend years reassembling, and the pieces would never quite fit.

The priests came in gray, hoods back, faces bare—which meant business, which meant warrants, which meant someone upstairs had signed papers with real names on them. The lead priest raised his hand and spoke a word of unbinding, and every charm in the room lit up like startled fireflies: the widow's snowfall still hanging in its vial on Paloma's shelf, the merchant's purchased grief cooling in its locket, half a decade of small stolen brightnesses suddenly glowing and shrieking their nature to the room.

"There," the lead priest said, satisfied. "Unlicensed memory-craft. Take inventory."

They were looking at the shelves. They were looking at the charms. For one blessed moment they were not looking at the window, where a thin young man with a forgettable face stood with a dead man's map burning against his sternum.

Aldric saved him a final time. As the priests crossed toward the body, the corpse's hand twitched—or perhaps it was only the settling of death, the last small machinery winding down—and the nearest priest cried out and stumbled backward into his fellows, and in that tangle of gray robes and shouted doctrine, Paloma went through the window.

The drop to the neighbor's awning was eight feet. He had judged it as eight feet a hundred times from his bed, in the insomniac arithmetic of a boy who always kept one exit measured. It was nine. The canvas caught him anyway, dumped him into a gutter full of rainwater and cabbage leaves, and he rolled, came up running, and put the tannery district between himself and the shouting before he let himself breathe.

Behind him, through the window he'd left open, he heard the lead priest's voice rise in something close to delight: "Fresh corpse. Unregistered death. Oh, we'll pull *everything* out of this one. Every face he ever saw."

They would find Paloma's face in Aldric's memories. Of course they would. Three days of watching from a doorway, if Aldric had watched three days. Maybe longer. The invisible life, the careful nothing—it had a face now, and the face was in the Office's hands, waiting to be read like a page.

Paloma climbed.

* * *

The roofs of Ceniza at night were a drowned city of slate and moonlight, and Paloma had never loved anything in his life the way he hated loving them just then. He moved the way the poor and the criminal move across high places—fast, low, trusting the tiles more than his own feet—sliding down a slope of blue clay shingles, leaping the gap above Cooper's Lane where the gap was exactly as wide as a desperate body, catching the drainpipe of the chandler's warehouse and swinging onto gravel that crunched too loud and froze him against a chimney while his heart tried to climb out through his teeth.

Below, torchlight bloomed at street corners. Whistles passed messages he couldn't decode. The Memory Office did not work fast, but it worked thoroughly, the way rust works, and somewhere beneath him its rust was spreading through the streets he had spent seven years learning how to disappear into.

He thought about the widow's snowfall, still sitting in its vial on his shelf. Let them have it. Let them catalog it, crush it, feed it into whatever furnace the Office used. It wasn't his grief. None of it had ever been his grief. That had been the whole design of his life: touch everything, own nothing, feel other people's pasts because his own was a locked room he'd nailed shut the night his mother walked out of it.

*She didn't leave him.*

Aldric's voice again, in memory, in a dead man's cadence. *Whatever they told him. Whatever he remembers.*

Paloma pressed his back against the chimney and made the mistake of letting himself remember, just for a breath. Ash-colored braids. A hand on his head, lighter than it should have been, already saying goodbye without knowing it. The morning he woke and she was gone and the neighbors said *runaway*, and the priests said *unlicensed practitioner, fled investigation*, and five-year-old Paloma had built his whole self on top of those two explanations like a house on two rotten posts.

If the posts were false—if she hadn't left—

He couldn't afford it. Grief was a luxury; terror was free. He filed the sentence away in the place where he kept things he'd examine later, in daylight, with locks on the doors, and he ran.

The aqueduct tunnels began beyond the dye-works, where the old Romanesque arches marched off into the hills carrying nothing but pigeons and history. Paloma knew the maintenance grate that didn't lock, knew the crawl-space behind it, knew the dry junction chamber where the city's water-masons had carved their names two centuries ago and no one had come since. It was the one hiding place he trusted, because it was the one hiding place he'd never shown another living soul.

He dropped through the grate, felt the dark close over him like water, and finally, finally let himself shake.

* * *

In the junction chamber, by the light of a candle stub he allowed himself because no light could reach out of here, Paloma unrolled the map and understood that his life had ended sometime between the knock and the window, and a different life had begun, and he had not been consulted about either.

Thirteen gates. Thirteen sigils. And the sigils *responded*—he could not deny it now, not after feeling the northern one turn over in his chest like something waking. When he held his palm above the map, the silver lines brightened faintly, the way coals brighten under breath. When he pulled his hand away, they dimmed, sulking.

His blood. Aldric had said it plainly: *Lira's blood. It knows its own.* Which meant the magic ban, the Covenant Act, the priests with their doctrine of debts—all of it had been written about people like him, and he had spent his life stealing crumbs of borrowed magic when he'd been sitting on a loaf the whole time.

He laughed, once, and the laugh echoed strangely off the wet stone, and he stopped.

Somewhere above, muffled by earth and brick, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell. The Office bell—the one they rang when a hunt went citywide, when names went up on the notice boards, when neighbors remembered that reporting a mage paid better than most honest work.

By morning his description would be on every corner. By noon, the landlady would be answering questions about her quiet third-floor tenant. By evening, every fence and informant and desperate debtor in Ceniza would know that the Memory Office paid silver for a young man with a forgettable face, last seen fleeing the Vellum Row.

Seven years of careful nothing, shattered in a single night by a dying stranger and a piece of leather.

Paloma sat in the dark with the map across his knees and did the only arithmetic available to him: he could not go home, could not sell, could not surface for food or water or coin. He had the clothes he wore, a candle stub, and a dead man's errand he hadn't agreed to.

*The thirteenth turn,* Aldric had whispered. *Say you don't know.*

But he did know, now. That was the trap of it. Knowing was a debt too, and the Office collected those.

He looked down at the sigil with the open eye and the crescent, the one that had answered him, and the candle flame leaned toward it as if listening.

Something in the tunnels—far off, deeper than any water-main, down where the old aqueduct ran out under the hills—answered back. A sound like a fox, if a fox could laugh.
