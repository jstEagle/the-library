## 3. What the Map Refuses to Say

The aqueduct tunnels beneath the Serrano Quarter smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Paloma had learned, in three days of hiding, that they smelled worse at night—when the water ran high and stirred up whatever had settled in the dark for the last two hundred years. He'd made a nest of sorts in an alcove where the ancient channel curved away from the main flow, a dry pocket behind a curtain of calcified drippings that looked, from the outside, like nothing at all. Ceniza was a city built on top of its own bones, layer after layer, and the aqueducts were the oldest bone of all. Nobody came down here. Nobody wanted to remember what the city had been before it walled itself against remembering.

He should have burned the map.

That thought arrived on schedule, every few hours, punctual as the memory-priests' bells. *Burn it, walk away, find a new room under a new name.* He had done it before. When he was sixteen he'd sold a charm too rich—a general's wedding day, still warm with joy—and had to abandon three years of careful work in the Tallow District because one buyer talked too much. He knew how to disappear. Disappearing was the one craft he'd mastered better than theft.

But he didn't burn the map. He told himself it was caution: better to know what the Office wanted than to guess. He told himself it was the dying man's face, the way his cracked lips had shaped those last words—*the thirteenth turn*—with something that hadn't looked like fear. It had looked like relief, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long his shoulders had grown around it.

Both of those things were true. Neither of them was the truth.

The truth was that Paloma had unrolled the leather across his knees the first night in the tunnel, and the sigils had sung to him, and he had not been able to stop listening.

* * *

The map itself was beautiful in the way old things are beautiful—worn soft at the folds, stained with rain and handling, drawn in ink that had gone brown as dried blood. Thirteen gates circled the city in a ring, each rendered in fine line-work: not the Ceniza he knew, but Ceniza as it might have been before the walls, or after them, or underneath them. The gates weren't placed at the city's actual doors. They sat at strange intervals, some inside the walls, some beyond, arranged in a pattern that made his eyes slide off it whenever he tried to hold the whole shape at once. Only when he looked at each mark singly could he see it clearly, and each mark carried a sigil above it—a small dense knot of lines, like a signature written by someone whose hand shook with purpose.

Thirteen sigils. Thirteen gates. And when he'd traced the first one with his fingertip, the memories in his coat had lit up like coals blown upon.

That was the part he couldn't stop thinking about. Not the gates, not the ring, not even the words in the margin in a script older than any alphabet taught in Ceniza's schools. The *burning*.

He kept his stock close—he always did, forty-odd charms sewn into the lining of his coat, each one a sliver of glass no bigger than a thumbnail, each one holding somebody's remembered hour. A fisherman's dawn catch. A bride's nervous laughter. The widow's snowfall, which he'd lifted two nights before everything went wrong, and which he'd never gotten to sell. They were merchandise. They were other people's lives in little glass coffins, and he handled them with a thief's care and a gravedigger's indifference, because that was how you survived this trade: you did not think of them as anything but product.

When his finger touched the first sigil, every charm in his coat had warmed at once. Not all equally—the widow's snowfall had gone hot enough to sting through the fabric, while the fisherman's dawn only glowed dully, like a banked fire. As if the sigil knew them. As if it were sorting through them, tasting each one, deciding which mattered.

Paloma had snatched his hand back and sat in the dark with his heart slamming, listening to water drip, waiting for the Office to come pouring down the maintenance shafts because surely magic that loud would be heard for miles.

Nothing came. The tunnels stayed empty. The charms cooled slowly, reluctantly, like embers missing the fire they'd been pulled from.

* * *

By the second day he had begun experimenting, because he was a thief and thieves tested locks, and because—if he was honest, and he tried very hard not to be—because some starved thing in him wanted to understand what the sigils wanted.

He laid the charms out on the flat stone beside him in rows, forty-one glass teeth catching what little light filtered down through the grate far overhead. Then he held his palm over the first sigil without touching it. Nothing—or almost nothing; a faint warmth, like standing near an oven door. Closer. Warmer. Touching: the burn, sharp and specific, and now he could feel the *pattern* in it. The widow's snowfall flared white-hot. The fisherman's dawn stayed cool. A child's birthday party—one of his earliest lifts, clumsy work, taken from a boy no older than eight—flickered somewhere in between.

He moved his palm to the second sigil. Different heat. The snowfall dimmed to embers; the birthday flared; a new one woke entirely, a memory he'd nearly forgotten owning—an old man's recollection of his mother singing, bought cheap off a debtor who needed coin more than nostalgia. That one sang under the second sigil like a struck bell.

Third sigil. Fourth. Each one woke a different constellation of his stolen goods, and none of them woke the same constellation twice, and somewhere around the seventh gate-mark Paloma stopped taking notes in his head and started taking them in charcoal on the tunnel wall, because his head couldn't hold it.

It wasn't random. It wasn't even, exactly, magical sympathy, though he supposed it was that too. It was *selection*. Each sigil reached into his collection and chose certain memories and left others, and the ones it chose shared something he couldn't name but could almost feel—a texture, a temperature, a flavor of human experience. The first sigil loved cold and quiet and grief. The second loved mothers. The fourth loved thresholds: doorways, arrivals, the moment before a decision. The ninth—

The ninth loved *him*.

He discovered this by accident, palm hovering over the mark, when the burn leapt off the page and into his skin—not the charms this time, not the glass, but the meat of his own hand, a prickling heat that raced up his wrist and settled in the old scar on his forearm where, at eleven years old, he'd fallen out of a pear tree and watched the gash knit itself closed in the space of ten heartbeats while he screamed and nobody heard.

The scar warmed. The sigil pulsed. And for one suspended moment Paloma felt the map *notice* him—the way you notice a draft in a sealed room, the way you notice being watched.

He threw the map across the alcove. It hit the stone, rolled, lay there innocently, thirteen brown sigils gleaming in the dark like a row of closed eyes.

* * *

He didn't sleep that night. He sat with his back against the calcite curtain and turned the problem over the way he'd turn a lockpick, feeling for the tumblers.

The man had bled out on his floor whispering about turns. The priests had torn his rented room apart within the hour—they'd known, somehow, exactly where to look, which meant the dying man had been followed, which meant the map was worth killing over. And the map, in Paloma's hands, behaved like no enchanted object he'd ever fenced. Charms were passive; they held what was put in them and gave it back on command. This thing *reached*. It sorted. It recognized.

And it recognized *him*, specifically, or something in him. Because here was the deduction he kept arriving at and kept trying to lose, the way you try to lose a bad card: the sigils responded to blood. His blood. He'd proven it on the third day, in the worst possible way, in the manner of a man scratching at a scab he knows should be left alone.

He had cut his thumb. Just a nick, just to test a theory he already hated—pricking his finger over the first sigil the way a girl pricks hers over a spinning wheel in the old stories Marisol-types told their children before the bans, before the purges, before magic became a debt collected by men in gray robes. One drop, welling dark in the tunnel gloom.

He'd touched it to the sigil.

Every charm in his coat had ignited at once. Not warm—*ignited*, light pouring through the seams of his lining, forty-one colors bleeding through the wool, and the whole alcove had filled with ghost-light and ghost-sound: snow falling silently in a courtyard thirty years dead, a fisherman's laugh, a mother's song rising thin and sweet off the glass, dozens of voices, dozens of lives, all speaking at once, all speaking *to him*, and beneath them a deeper sound, a resonance in the stone itself, in the aqueduct's ancient bones, as if the whole buried channel had cleared its throat.

And the sigils on the map had *moved*. He swore it. In that one heartbeat of contact, the fine brown lines had shifted—not much, not obviously, but the way a sleeper shifts: the thirteenth sigil, the one at the ring's crown, had seemed to open, just slightly, like an eye deciding whether to bother waking.

Then the blood soaked into the leather and vanished, and everything went dark and silent, and Paloma sat shaking in a puddle of fading ghost-light with his thumb in his mouth like a child.

* * *

There were explanations. There were always explanations; he'd built a life on them.

Contaminated stock. Some of the memories he'd lifted over the years had come from people with a drop of the old blood—country folk, foreigners, the occasional family that had slipped through the purges—and residue lingered in a charm the way smoke lingered in cloth. That explained the burning. Probably. Mostly.

The scar-warming was harder to explain away. So was the fact that the map had been pressed into *his* hands by a stranger who'd climbed three flights of stairs on a mortal wound to find a memory-thief he could not possibly have known by name. So was the margin-script, which he'd finally matched, after hours of squinting, to the letterforms on a pre-covenant boundary stone he'd once seen in the Office's public museum—back when he'd been foolish enough to visit, back when he'd stood in front of the carved figures of three great beasts and felt, for no reason he'd admitted then, like a dog hearing a whistle pitched too high for human ears.

Wolf. Stag. Owl. The museum placard had called them *the Guardians of the Old Accord, signatories of the Sealing*, and had said, in the Office's dry institutional voice, that their lines were extinct, their oaths void, their names removed from lawful record by decree.

Extinct. Void. Removed.

Paloma looked at his thumb, at the tiny scab already closing, faster than a scab had any right to close. He thought about his mother, Lira, who had left when he was four, who had kissed his forehead in a doorway with snow coming down behind her and said *wait for me, little wolf,* and who had never come back, and whom he had spent twenty years refusing to think about except in the smallest possible doses, like poison taken medicinally.

*Little wolf.*

He had always assumed it was an endearment. Mothers had endearments. But mothers also had secrets, and his mother had kissed him goodbye in a doorway with her eyes full of something that, looking back down the long telescope of years, he could finally name: not love alone, but love wearing the mask of farewell.

The map lay where he'd thrown it. He made himself pick it up. Made himself smooth it across his knees. Made himself look at the thirteenth sigil, the one at the crown of the ring, the one that had opened like an eye.

It was a wolf's print. Four toes, one pad, drawn in a single unbroken line by a hand that had shaken with purpose.

"Damn you," he whispered—to the map, to the dead man, to his mother, to whoever had decided that the invisible life he'd built, the careful gray ordinary life, the life where he was nobody's son and nobody's heir and nobody's business—it wasn't enough. "I didn't ask for this."

The tunnels answered with dripping water. The sigils stayed closed. And that was somehow worse—that it waited. Whatever the map was, whatever it wanted, it could wait, the way debt waits, the way winter waits, patient and total.

He folded it along its worn creases and tucked it inside his shirt, against his skin, because carrying it in his coat among the charms felt suddenly like keeping a wolf in a sheepfold. The leather was warm. It had no right to be warm. He told himself it was his own body heat, reflected back.

Above him, through the grate, the moon was rising—three nights past full, still fat, still bright enough to throw bars of silver down the maintenance shaft and lay them across the tunnel floor like the rungs of a ladder going up to a world he was no longer sure he belonged to. Thirteen turns, the man had said. He didn't know yet what turned, or how fast, or what happened when the counting ended. He only knew that the arithmetic had started without his consent, and that somewhere above him the Memory Office was still hunting a dead man's errand-boy, and that his thumb had already healed.

He was pulling his coat tighter around himself, settling in for another sleepless night, when he heard it: a sound from the main channel, upstream. Soft. Rhythmic. Paws on wet stone.

Something was walking toward him through the dark of the aqueduct, unhurried, unafraid, and it was humming—a low, wordless tune that raised every hair on his body, because he knew it. He had spent twenty years refusing to know it, and he knew it anyway.

His mother's song. Coming closer.
