## 4. Juniper

The fox was there when Paloma woke, and it was there when he slept, and by the second morning he had stopped pretending it wasn't.

He'd first noticed it in the aqueduct tunnels, a rust-colored shape at the edge of the lamplight, sitting with the patience of furniture. He'd told himself it was a stray drawn by the smell of his rations. Strays didn't hold still for an hour. Strays didn't watch him the way the priests watched suspects—except this creature's gaze had no doctrine in it, only a curiosity so old and unhurried that Paloma felt like the animal being studied.

He'd moved twice since leaving the tunnels. Once to a flophouse above the dye-works, where the air tasted of copper and the beds were rented by the hour to people who wanted to forget they'd ever been awake. Once to the hollow beneath the old granary stairs, where pigeons roosted and the dust was sweet with rotting grain. Both times, the fox had simply *been there* when he arrived, already settled, already waiting, as if it had walked ahead of him through some door he couldn't see.

"You're not real," he told it on the first night, lying flat on his back in the granary dark, one hand curled around the leather map beneath his shirt. "You're a memory I lifted off someone and never put back. That happens. It's happened before."

The fox yawned, showing small white teeth, and closed its eyes.

That was the worst part. Not the following. The *patience*. Everything else in Ceniza wanted something from him immediately—the fence who weighed his charms, the landlords who counted his coins twice, the priests who would weigh his bones next if they caught him. But the fox wanted nothing yet. It just watched, with one eye the color of new copper and one the pale gold of candlelight through honey, and waited for him to understand whatever it clearly already knew.

On the second day, he tried to lose it.

He went down into the fish market at its loudest hour, when the stalls bled brine and shouting and the cobbles ran silver with melted ice, and he did the things he was good at: he doubled back through the eel-seller's alley, slipped behind a cart of oysters, traded cloaks with a sleeping drunk for the price of a charm the man wouldn't remember buying. He came out the far side of the market wearing a stranger's coat and a stranger's limp, and he made himself small and gray and unremarkable, invisible as he'd trained himself to be since he was nine years old.

The fox sat on a bollard at the harbor mouth, tail wrapped around its feet, watching him come.

"Fine," Paloma said, out of breath, out of tricks, out of the particular dignity that comes from believing you are cleverer than the world. He crouched in front of it, close enough to see that its fur was not quite fur—there was a shimmer along its spine, like heat rising off stone in summer, like the air above the ash gate where the seam had opened and shown him a forest growing sideways. "Fine. What do you want?"

The fox tilted its head. And then, in a voice like dry leaves settling, like paper turning in an empty room, it said:

"Finally. Two days, cub. Your mother would have noticed me in two minutes."

Paloma sat down hard on the wet cobblestones.

* * *

Around them the market went on—gulls screaming over the fish tables, a woman arguing about the price of squid, somewhere a bell marking the half-hour—and none of it touched the small circle where the fox spoke. Paloma had the strange, swimming sensation of standing in a pocket cut out of the world, the way he sometimes felt near the gates, as though reality here had been stitched loosely and could be pulled apart with fingers.

"You talk," he said. Brilliant. His mother's son, heir to an ancient guardian bloodline, master of the obvious.

"I talk," the fox agreed. "I also know you haven't eaten since yesterday, that you're sleeping in a pigeon roost, and that you've been holding the map so tight your fingers have gone numb. You can unclench. I'm not going to take it."

"Everyone wants to take it."

"Yes." The fox's mismatched eyes held his—one warm, one cool, and both unbearably knowing. "Everyone does. Which is why you're going to stop being visible, cub, because right now you're the most visible thing in this city. A boy with wolf-blood carrying a map of thirteen gates? The Memory Office has every priest in Ceniza walking the streets with their senses open. You're not hiding. You're glowing."

Paloma's hand went to his chest, to the map's leather edge under the stolen coat. "How do you know what I am?"

The fox looked at him for a long moment, and something in its posture changed—not softer exactly, but older, wearier, the way a face changes when it remembers grief it had set down somewhere and now has to pick up again.

"My name is Juniper," she said. "And I knew your mother."

* * *

They ended up in the granary loft after all, because Paloma could not think of anywhere else, and because some traitorous part of him—a part he kept locked in a cellar and fed only at night—wanted to hear it. Wanted to hear her name spoken by someone other than himself, in his own head, in the dark.

Juniper sat on the windowsill where the light came through broken slats in dusty bars, and she did not look like an animal anymore. She was still a fox—that hadn't changed—but the way she held herself had shifted, the weight of her attention redistributed, until it felt less like sharing a room with a beast and more like sharing it with a person who had chosen, for her own reasons, to wear fur.

"Lira," Paloma said. "Her name was Lira."

"It is," Juniper said. "Present tense. Don't look at me like that—I'm not saying she's alive. I'm saying that what she did doesn't fit inside the word *dead*. There isn't a word for it. She'd have hated that. She hated imprecision."

"She hated—" Paloma stopped. Swallowed. "You knew her well enough to know what she hated."

"Cub, I knew her before you were a rumor. I knew her when she first crossed into this city with ash on her boots and a lie in her mouth about where she'd come from." Juniper's tail curled around her feet, the tip twitching. "She was running from the wild realm. Or toward something in it. With Lira it was always hard to tell the difference. She used to say the difference between fleeing and chasing was just which end of the story you stood on."

The words landed in Paloma's chest like stones dropped into deep water. He had built his whole life on a single sentence: *she left.* Fourteen years old, the night the priests took her, or the night before it—he'd never been sure which—and she had pressed a kiss to his hair and said *wait here* and never come back. He had spent fourteen years making that sentence bearable by making it simple. Mothers who loved you did not leave. Therefore she had not loved him enough. It was cruel arithmetic, but it was *his*, and he had balanced his whole self against it.

Now a fox was telling him the equation had more terms.

"Why did she run?" he asked. His voice came out smaller than he wanted.

Juniper was quiet for a moment. Outside, the granary bells rang four o'clock, and pigeons shuffled in the eaves like old men settling into sleep.

"That's not mine to tell," she said finally. "Some of it isn't even hers—it belongs to the covenant, to the gates, to the thing underneath everything. But I'll tell you what is mine to tell." Her mismatched eyes fixed on him, one catching the light like a coin, one drinking it in. "She loved you. Whatever story you've built in the absence—whatever cold little room you've put her in—take her out of it. She loved you the way a dam loves the valley. From upstream. Holding everything back so it could stay dry."

Paloma turned away and busied his hands with nothing—straightening his rations, checking the window slats—because his eyes were doing something he refused to permit them.

"The gates," he said, when he trusted his voice again. "You said they're waking. How do you know?"

"Because I can feel them from here, and I'm not even bound to them. Thirteen knots in the seal, and twelve of them fraying like rope in a current. Each full moon, another loosens. Another leaks." Juniper hopped down from the sill, landing without sound. "And the Hollowing on the far side of them is patient the way winter is patient. It doesn't push. It waits for the ice to thin."

"And the priests?"

"Hunting the map's bearer." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Not the map—the bearer. They've scryed enough to know the Wolf's line survived. Brother Ossian has your description now. Young man, dark-haired, seen near the tannery district the night the ash gate stirred. They burned a list of questions into three informants' memories this morning. By tomorrow it will be in every ear in Ceniza."

Paloma thought of the widow whose snowfall he'd sold, of the merchant who'd paid for the feeling of being young, of all the small warm trades he'd made in the city's shadows—and wondered how many of those shadows had eyes in them.

"So what do I do?" he asked. "Run? Give them the map? Burn it?"

"Burn it and the gates burn with it, and everything on both sides goes into the Hollowing's mouth." Juniper paced the length of the loft, a restless ribbon of rust and shadow. "Run, and you die tired instead of soon. No, cub. There's only one direction from here, and it's the one you've been avoiding since the man died in your room." She stopped in front of him, small and fierce and utterly certain. "The gates wake because they remember their keeper's blood. Twelve moons left. Twelve gates to walk. You go to them, Paloma Villalobos. You go to them before they come to you."

* * *

From the rooftops of the dye-works district, Juniper watched the boy sleep.

He had argued, of course. Argued the way a drowning man argues with the rope—grasping it and pushing it away in the same motion. *Why me. Why now. Why should I pay for an oath I never swore.* Good questions. She'd given him the honest answers: because blood is not a contract but it is a signature; because the moon does not negotiate; because the alternative to walking to the gates is being dragged to them. He hadn't liked any of it. He'd accepted none of it. And then he'd rolled up the map, tucked it against his heart, and asked her where the salt gate was—which was acceptance, in the language of boys who had learned never to agree to anything out loud.

*Two days,* she thought, watching his chest rise and fall in the grain-sweet dark. *Two days of trailing him through this gray city, and he never once looked up.*

Lira would have laughed herself sick.

The thought arrived with its usual cargo of grief, and Juniper let it sit beside her without opening it. Grief was old company. She carried it the way she carried her name—carefully, in a place where it couldn't be taken from her.

Because that was the truth she had not told the cub, the truth coiled tight beneath her ribs like a second heartbeat: she had not found him by accident. She had not followed the scent of wolf-blood out of loyalty or love or any of the clean reasons she'd offered. She had been *sent*. A voice in the space between realms, vast and hungry and terribly polite, had spoken her name and set a geas upon it: *guide the cub to the thirteenth gate, or be nameless.* And a spirit without a name was not dead, exactly. A spirit without a name was *unmade*—scattered into the general weather of the wild realm, becoming rain, becoming wind, becoming nothing that could remember having ever been anything.

So she had come. Hating it. Obeying it. Telling herself, in the small hours, that obedience was not betrayal—that she could carry the cub to the gate and still find some crack in the design, some seam in the Hollowing's patience, some way to serve her geas and defy its author in the same breath.

And then she had watched him lift a memory of first snowfall from a grieving widow and sell it gently, kindly, leaving the woman lighter and himself lonelier. She had watched him sleep in a pigeon roost rather than spend coin on safety. She had watched him flinch at his own reflection in the harbor water—not from vanity, but from recognition, the way one flinches at a relative in a stranger's face.

And she had understood, with a sinking clarity that felt almost like hope: the Hollowing had sent her to deliver a weapon. But weapons were made, not born. What it had actually sent her to was a boy who gave away warmth he couldn't afford, who touched other people's pasts because he couldn't bear his own, who had spent fourteen years balancing his mother's absence against his own worth and always coming up short.

The Hollowing thought it was collecting the Wolf's heir.

It might, Juniper thought, curling her tail over her nose, have collected something else entirely.

Below her, the boy murmured in his sleep—a word, half-formed. She leaned closer.

"...Mama," he said, to no one, to the dark, to the fourteen-year-old still waiting in an empty room.

Juniper closed her mismatched eyes.

*Hold on, cub,* she thought. *Twelve moons. And maybe—maybe—I can find a way to make even my chains into a ladder.*

Far across the city, beneath the Memory Office's black spire, a lamp was lit in a high window, and a priest with a dead sister's face in his memory began, once more, to scry.

The hunt had a name now.

*Paloma Villalobos.*
