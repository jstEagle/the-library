## 5. The First Gate: Ash

The tannery district smelled the way Ceniza pretended it didn't—of rot and lye and the sweetish stink of hides curing in lime pits, a smell that got into your clothes and your hair and stayed there like a guest who refused to leave. Paloma had worked the district twice before, lifting small memories from drunks outside the Salt Door tavern, and he'd learned to breathe through his mouth and keep his hood up and move like a man with somewhere dull to be.

Tonight he wasn't there for charms. Tonight he was following a map that wanted him dead.

He'd come to think of it that way over the three days since Juniper had found him on the aqueduct wall and told him, in her dry, unhurried voice, that the gates were waking. The map didn't want him dead exactly. But it pulled. He felt it through the leather now, a faint tug against his palm like a fish testing a line, drawing him down the crooked lane behind the rendering yards, past vats of dye the color of bruises, toward a door that shouldn't have been there.

*The ash gate lies beneath what burns,* Juniper had said. *Your city built its tanneries on top of it because the ground was already ruined. Your people are good at building on ruins.*

"Stop calling them *my* people," Paloma muttered.

"I didn't say it aloud," said the fox at his heel.

"You were thinking it loudly."

Juniper's mismatched eyes—one green-gold, one pale as winter water—caught the light of the tannery lamps and threw it back wrong, the way fox eyes do. She trotted beside him with the particular insolence of a creature who has never once been sorry for anything. Three days of her company, and Paloma still hadn't decided whether she was a miracle or a symptom.

The map tugged harder.

He stopped beneath an awning strung with drying skins—deer, goat, one enormous thing he chose not to identify—and let himself look at the map openly for the first time since entering the district. In daylight, among crowds, he never risked it. But the lane was empty except for the two of them and the fat black flies singing over the waste channels.

Thirteen gates, inked in a hand so fine it might have been done with a single hair. They circled the city in a ring no surveyor had ever drawn, dipping under districts and rivers and the roots of the Wall itself. Twelve of the sigils were rendered in ordinary ink. The thirteenth—the one at the ring's crown, where the map's leather darkened as though scorched—was burned into the hide rather than drawn on it.

And the first gate, the one nearest his thumb, marked with a spiral like smoke frozen mid-rise: *Ash.*

"It's warm," he said.

"Everything about you is warm lately," Juniper said. "You're a hearth walking around pretending to be furniture."

"That's not comforting."

"It isn't meant to be."

He crouched, spreading the map across his knee, and pressed his thumb to the ash sigil—and the sigil *burned*. Not the leather. His blood. A heat climbed from the pad of his thumb up through his wrist, his forearm, into the hollow place behind his sternum where he kept everything he refused to look at, and for one vertiginous moment every stolen memory he carried flared at once: the widow's snowfall, the merchant's dead daughter laughing, a hundred small lifted joys sloshing through him like lamp oil finding flame.

He bit down on his tongue hard enough to taste copper and the flare guttered.

"Cub." Juniper's voice had changed. All the insolence gone out of it, leaving something old and careful. "What did it do?"

"It knew me." His mouth was dry. "It knew my blood."

"Of course it did. It's been waiting five years for exactly that blood." She sat back on her haunches, tail curled over her paws, and studied him with an expression he was beginning to hate, because it was the expression of someone watching a person walk toward a thing they cannot be talked out of. "We can still walk away. Burn the map. Move cities. You're good at disappearing—I've watched you do it for three days and it's almost artful."

Paloma looked at the sigil. The heat was fading from his arm, but it left something behind, the way a struck match leaves its sulfur ghost. All his life he'd thought of his magic as a disease he managed—a secret sickness, hidden like a limp, controlled like a cough. He stole memories because they were safe, because they were *other people's*, because handling another soul's past meant never having to open his own.

But the gate hadn't asked for a memory. It had asked for *him*.

"No," he heard himself say. "I need to see it."

"You don't know what you need. You're twenty years old and you've spent all of them hiding."

"And you've spent how long following a cub around a city that smells like this?" He stood, folding the map away. "You could leave too."

Something flickered across the fox's face—too fast, too alien to read. "I go where the map goes," she said finally, which was not an answer, and they both knew it, and neither pushed.

* * *

The door was behind the last rendering yard, set into a foundation wall older than any wall had a right to be in Ceniza. The city prided itself on being two hundred years old; the stones here were cut in courses no mason used anymore, fitted without mortar, furred with a moss that grew gray instead of green. There was no handle. There was no hinge. There was only a seam in the stone shaped like a closed eye, and above it, so worn it was nearly smooth, the same spiral as the map.

"This is a bad idea," Paloma said, mostly to hear it said out loud.

"Yes," Juniper agreed cheerfully.

He raised his hand anyway. And stopped.

Because here was the thing he hadn't told her, the thing he'd barely admitted to himself: he wasn't afraid. Or rather—he was afraid, properly afraid, the cold kind that lives in the gut—but underneath the fear ran something else, something that shamed him more than fear ever could. He was *eager*. Twenty years of pressing his magic flat, of wearing his own skin like a borrowed coat, and here at last was a door that opened not despite what he was but *because* of it. Every rule he'd ever lived by said run. Every rule had also said hide, and hiding had gotten him a rented room, a false name, and a trade in other people's happiness.

His blood sang against the stone, and some traitorous part of him sang back.

He pressed his palm flat against the seam and cut it open on the old flint edge of the wall—one clean line across the meat of his hand, the way the map's marginal note instructed in that fine-haired script: *the gates know their own by blood freely given.* The words had unsettled him when he first read them. *Freely given.* As if the gates understood consent. As if they were polite.

The blood touched the spiral and the world exhaled.

Not light—not exactly. It was more like the moment when a shutter opens in a dark room and your eyes haven't adjusted yet, except the room was reality and the shutter opened onto somewhere else entirely. The wall didn't swing or slide. It simply stopped insisting on being a wall, and where it had been there was a seam of silver-dark air, tall as a door and narrow as a coffin, hanging in the foundation stones like a rip in wet cloth.

Through it: forest.

But wrong. Paloma's mind reached for the word *wrong* and then reached past it, because the trees through the seam were growing sideways—trunks running parallel to the ground like fallen pillars, branches leafing upward off their sides, roots gripping nothing, the whole wood canted ninety degrees from sense as if gravity had simply gotten bored elsewhere. Moss hung from what should have been sky. Somewhere in that sideways green, water moved without falling, a river crawling along the underside of the canopy, and the light came from below, gold-green, sourceless, the color of the hour just after dawn in a place that had never agreed to have mornings.

The smell came through the seam too, and it undid him worse than the sight. Loam and rain and growing things and cold clean air—the smell of a world that had never been rendered, never cured, never burned. His eyes stung. He would tell himself later that it was the tannery reek.

"Oh," Juniper said softly, beside his ankle. Her voice had gone strange. "Oh, I'd forgotten how it smells."

"You've been there?"

"I'm *from* there, cub." She didn't look at him. Her tail had gone very still. "Everyone is from somewhere. That's the whole trouble with existing."

He put his hand toward the seam—just toward it, just to feel—and the air at its edge brushed his fingertips like the inside of a cheek, warm and alive, and every stolen memory in him rose up again but differently this time, not burning but *reaching*, the widow's snowfall leaning toward that sideways forest like a plant toward light, and Paloma snatched his hand back with his heart slamming.

For one clear, terrible second he understood what he'd been doing all these years. Not surviving. *Starving.* He'd been eating scraps off other people's tables because he was afraid to find out what his own kitchen held.

"I liked that," he said. The confession came out before he could stop it, low and horrified. "Opening it. I liked using—" He couldn't say the word. "I liked it, Juniper. What does that make me?"

The fox looked up at him, and for once she didn't answer with a joke.

* * *

What it made him, thirty seconds later, was careless.

That was the thing about exhilaration—it ate your edges. He was still staring through the seam, still breathing the wild air, when Juniper's whole body went rigid and her ears swiveled like weathervanes.

"Lamp," she breathed. "Two streets over. Moving fast. Office lanterns burn blue-white—your city's lamps burn orange. Cub, *hide it*."

The seam couldn't be hidden. That was the first thing he learned about gates: they weren't doors you closed, they were wounds that stayed open as long as the blood held. He pressed his bleeding hand against the stone beside it and willed it shut and nothing happened, because wanting things had never once made his magic work, and the blue-white glow was already washing the mouth of the lane, already throwing long shadows from the drying-skins so that the hanging carcasses seemed to dance.

"The rendering pit," Juniper snapped. "Now."

There was a pit behind the last yard—a lime pit, half-full, its surface crusted white. Paloma took one look at it and understood: the lye would mask his scent, the crust would hold his weight if he lay flat, and no priest searching by lamplight would think to look for a fugitive floating on poison. It was a horrible plan. It was the only plan.

He dropped the map inside his shirt, killed the lantern he carried, and lowered himself onto the lime crust as gently as a man lying down on a sleeping animal. The cold bit through his clothes at once. The smell was ammoniac and blinding. Beside him, Juniper poured herself flat, her pale fur going gray-white against the crust, invisible in a way that made his skin crawl—she was too good at vanishing, the way he was too good at vanishing, and he wondered briefly and unpleasantly what she was hiding from.

The blue-white light rounded the corner.

Brother Ossian walked the way Paloma imagined glaciers moved: without hurry, without mercy, certain of arriving. He was taller than most priests, gaunt under his slate-gray cassock, and he carried the Office lantern high in one hand and a slender iron rod in the other—a memory-rod, the tool they used to pull recollections out of a room like splinters out of skin. Two acolytes flanked him, young, nervous, their own lanterns trembling.

Ossian stopped at the mouth of the lane. Raised the lantern. Let the light play across the drying skins, the dye vats, the foundation wall.

Paloma lay on the lime and did not breathe and discovered that a man can survive quite a while without breathing if terror does the work for him.

The priest's gaze traveled along the foundation wall—and stopped. Right where the seam hung open. Right where the silver-dark air shimmered faintly against the ancient stones, spilling sideways-forest light into a Cenizan alley.

"*There*," one of the acolytes whispered. "Brother, the resonance—"

"I see it," Ossian said. His voice was quiet and very level, the voice of a man who has practiced calm the way other men practice swordwork. He stepped forward. The iron rod came up. "Seal your minds. Whatever looks out of that opening, do not meet its—"

Juniper moved.

Later, Paloma could never reconstruct exactly what she did. One moment she was flat on the lime beside him; the next there was a blur of gray-gold crossing the lane in a low, impossibly fast line, and the sound she made wasn't a growl or a scream but something between—a fox's bark pitched wrong, doubled, layered, as if three animals spoke through one throat. The acolytes cried out. The lantern swung wild. And Juniper flowed up the foundation wall like water running backward, straight past Ossian's reaching hand—

—and tore the corner from the map.

Except the map was inside Paloma's shirt. He felt it happen anyway: a tearing sensation against his ribs, in the scar-tissue of the night the stranger died, and a scrap of leather lifted out of his very clothing as though the map existed in two places at once, and Juniper snatched the burning scrap—the corner bearing the thirteenth gate, the scorched sigil—out of the air between her teeth, and leapt.

She hit the top of the wall and was gone. The scrap went with her.

"No!" Paloma was on his feet before he'd decided to stand, lime crusting off him in sheets, hand pressed to his chest where the map suddenly weighed less. "Juniper—!"

Ossian turned.

For a heartbeat—one full, ringing heartbeat—brother and thief looked at each other across a Cenizan alley, lamplight and gate-light mingling on the space between them. Paloma saw the priest's face clearly in that moment, and what he saw frightened him more than shouting would have. Ossian wasn't furious. He wasn't even surprised. He looked at Paloma—at the bloodied hand, the lime-crusted clothes, the open seam behind him—with an expression of grim, weary recognition, like a man finding the exact thing he'd been dreading, and finding it smaller than his dread and somehow sadder.

"Wolf-blood," Ossian said quietly. Not a question. A diagnosis.

Paloma ran.

He went over the dye vats, through the gap between curing sheds, up the drainage ladder with his cut hand screaming, across the rooftops where he'd escaped the priests once before and swore he'd never need to again. Behind him the blue-white light bobbed and fell back; Ossian did not pursue, which was worse than pursuit. Ahead of him, on the ridgeline of the tannery roofs, a gray shape waited at every junction, always three tiles ahead, always looking back, the torn scrap of map still clenched in her jaws like a stolen bird.

He caught her at last on the aqueduct, both of them panting, the city spread out below them in its orange constellations. Juniper dropped the scrap at his feet. It was real leather, warm, singed at the edge—and the thirteenth sigil on it pulsed faintly, like a heart.

"You tore it," Paloma gasped. "Why would you—you took it *from* me—"

"So he couldn't take it from you," Juniper said. She shook herself, fur settling, and her mismatched eyes fixed on him with a fierceness that had no play in it at all. "That man knew what you were on sight, cub. Do you understand? The Office has been waiting for wolf-blood the way farmers wait for rain. He let you run. Priests who want to catch you chase you. Priests who want to *find you later* let you run."

Paloma looked down at the scrap. The scorched sigil pulsed. Pulsed. Like something on the far side of the thirteenth gate, knocking.

"He called me wolf-blood," he said slowly. "Like he recognized it. Like he'd seen it before."

Juniper was quiet a moment too long.

"Cub," she said, "there's something you should know about how your mother was caught."

Below them, in the dark, the bells of the Memory Office began to ring.
