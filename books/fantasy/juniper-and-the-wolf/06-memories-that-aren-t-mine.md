## 6. Memories That Aren't Mine

The Memory Office kept its records in a building that had once been a granary, back before the covenant sealed and Ceniza stopped trusting anything that grew. Paloma knew the shape of it from the outside only—a squat stone block in the Scribe's Quarter, its windows narrow as knife slits, its door flanked by two braziers that burned without smoke. Priests came and went in their grey robes, their hoods cut to shadow the eyes, and the people of the city stepped around them the way water steps around a stone.

He had never intended to go inside.

He told himself, crouched on the roof of the dye-house across the lane, that he was only watching. That Brother Ossian—the priest with the ink-stained fingers and the walk of a man carrying something heavy—had simply been in the wrong place at the ash gate, and that Paloma wanted to know how much he'd seen, nothing more. Surveillance, not theft. Caution, not hunger.

But Paloma had spent nine years touching other people's pasts for coin, and he knew the taste of his own excuses by now. They were sweet, and they were lies, and he swallowed them anyway.

Ossian emerged at dusk alone, unhooded, his grey robe swapped for a merchant's plain coat—a priest walking abroad without his office, which meant he was going somewhere the office would not approve of. Paloma followed him across three districts, along the aqueduct road where Juniper kept low in the shadows of the channel and muttered fox-curses at every dog they passed, down into the tanners' warren where the smell of lime pits covered all other smells, including, Paloma hoped, the smell of wolf-blood.

Ossian stopped at a shuttered shrine to some dead river god, knelt as if in prayer, and wept.

Paloma, flattened against a wall six feet away, felt his breath go strange. He had seen priests chant. He had seen them burn a memory-thief's charms in the salt market square, seen the crowd cheer. He had never seen one cry, alone, in the dark, over nothing visible at all.

*He's carrying something,* Paloma thought. *Something heavy.*

And then—because he was what he was, because the gift his mother had bled to hide rose in him like thirst—he reached out and took it.

* * *

Stealing from an ordinary mind was like lifting a purse: you found the clasp, you slipped the catch, you drew out what glittered. Stealing from Ossian was like opening a door onto a burning house.

The memory hit him whole, unfiltered, five years old and preserved in grief so dense it had hardened into amber:

A room with no windows. His mother—*Lira*, though the men in grey did not use her name, they called her *the subject*, *the wolf-kin*, *it*—sat chained to a chair of cold iron, and her wrists were bleeding where the metal kissed her skin, because iron hated what she was even more than Ceniza hated magic. Her black hair had gone lank. Her eyes—his eyes, the same dark amber, the same too-bright gleam—were steady.

Brother Ossian, younger by five years, still in his hood, stood across from her with a ledger open on his knees.

"The Stag," Lira said, and her voice was hoarse but level. "Her name is Marisol Reyes. She sells star-charts near the salt market. She doesn't know what she is. Leave her be."

"You understand what you're doing," young Ossian said, and there was something awful in his voice—not cruelty, but *procedure*, the sound of a man reciting doctrine while his hands shook. "You are surrendering a guardian's line to us."

"I am surrendering a name I stole from a dying woman's memories to buy my son one more year of being nobody." His mother smiled, and it was the worst thing Paloma had ever seen, that smile, tired and triumphant and full of love. "Debts are collected. Isn't that your doctrine? Then collect me. The boy is not part of the debt. He was never part of anything. Write that in your ledger."

"We will find him eventually. Blood calls to blood."

"Then let it call slowly." She leaned forward as far as the chains allowed. "Five years, Brother. Give me five years of his life before you take the rest, and I will tell you everything about the gates. Every sigil. Every turn."

And Ossian—young, hooded, hands trembling—had written it down. Had taken the bargain. Had watched her be led away toward whatever the Office did with wolf-kin, and had heard her say, over her shoulder, not to him but to the air, to the world, to anyone listening:

"Paloma. Mijo. Don't be sorry. Be ready."

* * *

He came back to himself on his knees in the tanners' warren, sick against the wall, the taste of iron in his mouth that wasn't his own blood. Above him the stars were indifferent. Somewhere a dog barked itself hoarse.

She hadn't abandoned him. He had known that since the river gate, since her echo in the covenant-stone had shown him the prison she'd built with her own body. But this was different. This was *specific*. This was his mother in chains, trading another person's secret like a coin, buying him time with someone else's safety—and it had worked, and Marisol had lived five years ignorant and free because Lira Villalobos had spent a name she had no right to spend.

*She gave up Marisol to protect me.*

And the thought that followed it was worse, small and poisonous and true: *and I have spent five years selling other people's memories for rent money.*

Juniper found him there. He didn't hear her come; he never did. One moment he was alone with the stink of lime pits and the ghost of his mother's voice, and the next the fox was sitting in front of him, mismatched eyes catching the starlight—one gold, one green—and her tail was curled around her feet in a way he had learned to read as concern.

"Cub," she said. "You're leaking."

"I'm what?"

"Grief. It pours off you like rain off slate. Anyone with a nose could find you." She tilted her head. "What did you take?"

"Nothing. I slipped. I'm fine."

The lie came out smooth, worn smooth by years of practice, and Juniper looked at him for a long moment with those impossible eyes, and Paloma realized with a fresh wave of shame that lying to a spirit was probably like lying to weather. She let it go. That was somehow worse.

"Hm," she said, and turned, and trotted off toward the aqueduct, and he followed her because following her was the only direction his life had anymore.

* * *

Brother Ossian believed in debt.

This was not cruelty; it was arithmetic, and arithmetic was the closest thing to mercy the world offered. Every act of unlicensed magic borrowed against the covenant's failing seal. Every stolen memory, every hedge-charm, every whispered cantrip in a cellar took something the seal could no longer afford to give, and when the seal failed entirely—and it would fail, the ledgers were clear, the decay curves were clear—it would not fail gently. The Hollowing would come through the torn places wearing the faces of the beloved dead, and it would eat, and eat, and eat.

So the Office collected. Small debts early, so no one owed a terrible sum at the end. A charm confiscated here. A talent burned out there. A thief made example of in the salt market, so that a hundred others put their gifts away like knives before dinner.

Ossian had believed this the way other men believed in tides. He had believed it especially after Serafin.

His sister had been a garden-witch, back when such words could still be said quietly among family. Nothing grand—she coaxed beans up early, kept blight off the neighbor's vines, made their mother's last winter warm with small green miracles. When the collectors came, Ossian had been nineteen and already in novice grey, and he had stood aside, because the ledger was clear, because debts were debts, because love was not a currency the Office accepted.

Serafin had looked at him through the cart bars with no hatred at all, which was the unbearable part. She had looked at him the way you look at weather.

He still saw her face sometimes. Increasingly often, lately. In the sky-chart seller they'd begun tracking near the salt market—Marisol Reyes, a name that had surfaced in an interrogation five years ago and then gone quiet, quiet, until the ash gate incident put a wolf-blooded boy and a fox-spirit back on the map. In the boy himself, who moved through Ceniza like a man who had rehearsed invisibility his whole life, and who had looked at Ossian across the seam of the ash gate with an expression Ossian recognized from his own mirror: the look of someone tasting power for the first time and hating how much they liked it.

Tonight he had gone to Serafin's shrine—the little alcove by the dead river god where he left dried flowers no god received—and he had knelt, and he had grieved, as he did every seventh day, and he had felt, for half a heartbeat, a touch against his mind. Faint. Skilled. Gone before he could turn.

Someone had tried to steal from him.

Or had succeeded. He sat in his cell in the granary afterward and turned the possibility over like a coin of uncertain weight. If the wolf-boy had taken something, what would he find? The interrogation records? The bargain with the wolf-kin woman—Lira, her name was Lira, though the ledgers called her Subject Nine? The face of Serafin?

Ossian lit his lamp and opened his private ledger, the one not kept in the archive, and wrote: *The heir is close to the truth. The truth is close to breaking him. Consider whether breaking is necessary.*

Doctrine said yes. Break them all early; spare the world the final bill.

But doctrine had never held his sister's hand through a fever. Doctrine had never heard a chained woman offer her whole self for five years of her son's ordinary life, and had to write it down in a ledger as *asset exchange, approved.*

Ossian blew out the lamp and sat in the dark with his debts, and for the first time in twenty years, he was not certain which side of the ledger he stood on.

* * *

"You're lying to me."

Juniper said it conversationally, the way she commented on bad weather or the inferiority of city pigeons. They were camped in the aqueduct tunnel, third arch past the cistern, where the drip of water kept time like a patient metronome. Paloma was cleaning his charm-kit with more attention than it needed. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady. That was the problem with competence; it kept working while the rest of you came apart.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Mm." The fox rolled onto her back, paws in the air, an attitude of elaborate casualness that fooled neither of them. "Your grief changed flavor tonight. Before, it was the old grief, the mother-grief, the long slow kind. Now there's something else in it. Something with edges." She righted herself. "You took something from the priest. And whatever you found, you've decided I can't carry it."

"It's not about what you can carry."

"No?"

"It's about—" Paloma set down the charm-kit. His voice, when it came, was quieter than he intended. "It's about what I can say out loud and still be the person saying it."

Juniper's ears flicked. She waited. Waiting was a weapon she wielded better than any priest's iron.

"My mother gave the Office a name," he said finally. "Five years ago. Under interrogation. To buy time for me." He stared at the tunnel wall, where water had stained the stone into shapes like maps of nowhere. "The name was Marisol's."

The drip counted four seconds. Five.

"And Marisol is walking beside us," Juniper said, "toward a gate that will ask her to die holding a line, because a dead woman sold her name to keep you alive a little longer." Not accusation. Just the shape of it, laid out flat, the way she laid everything out.

"Yes."

"Do you intend to tell her?"

"I don't know." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I steal memories for a living, Juniper. I've always told myself it was harmless. Borrowed years. People sell them to me; I sell them on; everyone gets paid. But tonight I saw what taking looks like from the other side. Iron chairs. Ledgers. *Asset exchange, approved.* And I understood that I've been doing a small version of what they do my entire life, and calling it survival, and—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "My mother traded a person to protect me. And my first thought, my actual first thought, was *at least she didn't trade me.* What does that make me?"

"A cub," Juniper said simply. "Young, and frightened, and carrying more than your spine was built for." She padded closer, and her warmth against his side was startling, animal and real. "The Hollowing wants you to believe that guilt is a place you live. It isn't. It's weather. It passes if you keep moving."

"And if I've hurt Marisol just by existing? By being worth saving?"

"Then you tell her," Juniper said, "when you're brave enough. Or you don't, and it rots between you, and the Hollowing eats easier. Those are the paths. I've walked both." Something old moved behind her mismatched eyes, some debt of her own she did not name, and Paloma was too raw to press.

Instead he asked the question that had been sitting in his chest since the tanners' warren, since his mother's voice said *be ready.*

"Juniper. The thirteenth turn. The seal failing. My mother bought five years." He looked at the fox, and the fox looked back. "How much of them are left?"

Juniper was silent for a long moment. Water dripped. Somewhere far above, the moon was climbing toward full, twelve turns deep into its slow unraveling.

"Fewer than you think, cub," she said. "We need to move. Tomorrow we find the Stag."

"And tell her what, exactly?" Paloma asked—but the fox was already trotting toward the tunnel mouth, tail high, and the question hung in the dripping dark behind him, unanswered, growing heavier with every step he took to follow her.
