## 7. The Salt Market Bargain

The salt market smelled of brine and old money, and Paloma hated how much he loved it.

He moved through the stalls with the practiced looseness of a man who belonged nowhere and therefore everywhere—a nod here, a half-smile there, his hood back just enough to show an honest face. Salt was Ceniza's second religion. It packed wounds, preserved grief, paid debts. The Office taxed it, the priests blessed it, and the fence named Corvus Dain bought and sold everything the other two pretended not to see.

His stall sat at the market's heart, beneath a canopy of faded indigo, draped in charms that were mostly glass and wishful thinking. The real inventory lived behind his eyes. Everyone in Ceniza knew that much, even if no one said it aloud. You went to Dain when you wanted to *feel* something you hadn't earned.

Paloma had sold to him four times before. Never anything that mattered. A baker's memory of her wedding. A soldier's last morning before the war took him. Small goods, clean goods, memories with no fingerprints on them.

Today he carried the one thing he'd sworn he never would.

It sat against his sternum like a coal wrapped in silk—the charm he'd made at fourteen, alone, in the aqueduct dark, because he'd been so lonely he'd thought he might die of it. His happiest memory. The only one that was truly, wholly his.

*The kitchen. Lira's hands dusted white with flour. She is singing something low and wordless, and she lifts him onto the counter so he can press his small palms into the dough, and she laughs—not the careful laugh she gave strangers but the real one, the one that crinkled her eyes—and outside the window the first moon-gate light of autumn comes through gold, and she says,* "You'll be bigger than all this someday, little wolf. Bigger than me. Don't let anyone make you smaller."

*Don't let anyone make you smaller.*

He stopped walking.

A fishwife jostled past him, cursing cheerfully. The market roared on around him, indifferent. Paloma stood in the current of it and did arithmetic he already knew the answer to.

Twelve gates remained. Twelve full moons. And between him and the salt gate—buried somewhere beneath the saltworks themselves, if Juniper's nose could be trusted—stood a checkpoint of priests, informants, and a city that had spent five years teaching itself to hate what he was. He needed papers. Papers required coin. Coin, in this quarter, meant Dain, and Dain didn't deal in small goods anymore. Not since the Office had started squeezing the fence-trade. Not since the price of silence had gone up.

*Sell something else.* The thought came easy, cowardly, familiar. *Sell a stranger's memory. That's what you do. That's what you've always done.*

But the papers weren't for him alone anymore. Marisol was waiting in the tunnels with a fever she wouldn't admit to, and Juniper had burned through most of her strength holding the fox-shape together through the cold, and the salt gate would not open for a boy with empty pockets. Three lives balanced on what he could carry out of this market.

And the memory—he told himself this as he walked, each step landing heavier than the last—the memory wasn't proof of anything. It was flour and laughter and a window. No sigils. No gates. No wolf. Just a mother who had loved him, which was the rarest thing in Ceniza and therefore worthless to anyone else.

That was the lie he carried to the stall, and he carried it carefully, the way you carry a full cup across a crowded room.

Dain looked up from his ledger with eyes like wet slate. He was a narrow man, gray as his wares, with fingers stained silver from years of handling salt-charms. "The thief returns." His voice was dry paper. "I heard you died on the rooftops two nights ago. Priests crawling over the Tanners' Quarter like flies on—" He paused. Read something in Paloma's face. "Ah. So the rumors have weight."

"I need passage papers. Three sets. Clean ones."

"Three sets." Dain closed the ledger slowly. "Clean ones cost more than dirty ones. Dirty ones cost more than none. And none cost more than you can imagine, little thief, because the Office has raised every bounty in this city since the night a certain bleeding man left his map in a certain rented room." He smiled without warmth. "You've become expensive."

"I can pay."

"Can you." Dain laced his fingers. Behind him, charms swayed on their strings, catching the flat gray light. "Show me."

Paloma's hand rose to his collar before his mind gave permission. He felt the charm's heat through the fabric. *Walk away. Find another way. There is always another way.*

There wasn't, and they both knew it.

He drew the charm out. Held it low, between their bodies, where the crowd couldn't see. "One memory. First-rate. Childhood. Unspoiled."

"Whose?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously." Dain's slate eyes had gone bright, and Paloma recognized the look—he'd seen it on merchants appraising meat, on priests appraising sinners. "Unspoiled childhood memory is the rarest stock in this city. Everyone's childhoods are spoiled here. That's rather the point of Ceniza." He extended a hand, palm up. "Let me taste it."

Every rule Paloma had ever written for himself said no. Every rule, and he broke them all in the space of a breath, because Marisol was coughing blood into a rag in the dark and Juniper was running out of name to lose.

He pressed the charm into Dain's palm.

The fence closed his eyes. His lips parted slightly. For three seconds he stood utterly still, and Paloma watched a stranger live inside his mother's kitchen, hear her laugh, feel flour under small hands—

Dain opened his eyes. Something had changed in them. The appraisal was gone; something hungrier had replaced it.

"Where," Dain said softly, "did you get this?"

"A dead woman."

"Liar." He turned the charm over in his silver-stained fingers. "This isn't lifted work. Lifted memories have edges—scars from the taking, seams where the taker gripped. This is *woven*. First-person. Native." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "Someone made this charm from their own memory. Someone who weaves native work doesn't sell it. Not unless they're desperate, or dying, or—" He looked up, and his smile was terrible in its gentleness. "Or unless they're selling more than they know."

"It's flour and laughter. That's all it is."

"Mm." Dain set the charm down on the ledger with the delicacy of a man handling an egg that might hatch. "Papers for three. Clean. Delivered by dusk to the old cistern off Rope-Walk. And I'll forget your face, as agreed." He tapped the charm once. "But I keep the memory. Non-negotiable."

"Done," Paloma said, and hated how steady his voice came out.

He walked away through the salt-smell and the noise, and it was only at the market's edge, in the shadow of the customs arch, that he let himself stop and press a hand flat against his chest, against the hollow where the warmth used to be.

It should have hurt more, losing it. Instead there was just a strange, terrible lightness—as if he'd cut off a finger to save the hand and kept waiting for the pain to arrive.

Behind him, in the market's heart, Corvus Dain drew a slip of gray vellum from beneath his ledger, wrote six words on it in a hand like frost, and sealed it with wax the color of dried blood.

*Native weaver. Wolf-line confirmed. Come.*

By dusk, the note would be in the Memory Office. By midnight, Brother Ossian would be reading it by candlelight. And somewhere in the tunnels beneath the city, a boy who had just sold his only happiness would be counting forged papers by foxlight, believing himself invisible.

* * *

Juniper smelled the loss on him before he reached the cistern.

Fox noses were honest things—they catalogued grief the way human noses catalogued rot—and what came down the tunnel toward her was wrong in a way that made her fur stand up. The cub walked in carrying all his usual scents: ash, river-slime, stolen memories, fear worn smooth from use. But threaded through it now was a new absence, a cold spot, like a tooth pulled from a warm mouth.

He had given away something that was *him*. Not borrowed. Not lifted. His own.

She knew the shape of that sacrifice. She was living inside one.

"You got them?" Marisol asked from her nest of sacking, papers rustling in her lap even now, sky-charts she was mending out of habit because her hands needed work the way lungs needed air. Her fever-bright eyes found Paloma. "Three? All clean?"

"All clean." He held them up. "We move tonight, while the tide's low enough to reach the salt gate."

Juniper circled him once, twice, reading him the way she'd once read weather in the wild realm, back when weather was hers to read. He flinched from nothing. That was worst of all. A boy who had just amputated a piece of his soul should have flinched from *something*.

"What did you give him?" she asked.

"Nothing that matters."

The lie was so smooth she almost admired it. Almost. But she had known liars for three hundred years, and the best ones—her own kind especially—always lied flattest about the things that mattered most.

"Cub."

"Don't." He knelt by the cistern's edge and began sorting the papers, not looking at her. "We have twelve moons, a fevered Stag-heir, and a gate under a saltworks patrolled by men who burn people in the square. Whatever I traded, it bought us past all of that. Let it be enough."

Marisol looked from him to the fox and back again, and said nothing, because she was learning the grammar of these two: that some silences were walls, and some were doors, and you didn't get to choose which until you knocked.

Juniper settled onto the cold stone and curled her tail over her paws, and inside the fox-shape, beneath the fur and the geas and the three centuries of exile, something ached with a recognition she refused to name.

Because here was the truth she carried like a stone in her belly:

She had not found the cub by accident.

She had been *sent*.

The Hollowing's summons had come to her in the wild realm's last green corner, in a voice like snow falling upward, and it had offered her a bargain dressed as a sentence: *Guide the Wolf's heir to the thirteenth gate, or forfeit your name.* And a spirit's name was not a label—it was the thread the realms used to hold her together. Lose it, and she would unravel into weather. Into nothing that remembered being anything.

So she had taken the mission. And then, somewhere between the wild realm and this ash-choked city, somewhere in the watching days when the cub stole memories and touched none of his own, she had done the one thing the Hollowing had not planned for.

She had started wanting to defect.

Not escape—defect. To turn the mission inside out. To deliver the cub to the final gate not as prey but as a weapon pointed backward at the thing that sent her. She told herself it was strategy. She told herself it was survival. But lying in the dark of the aqueduct tunnels, listening to a boy breathe in his sleep, she had caught herself thinking thoughts with no strategy in them at all—thoughts shaped like *he deserves better than both of us*, thoughts shaped like *Lira would have liked to see this*.

And now he had sold his happiest memory to buy papers for a sick woman and a fox who was lying to him, and he called it *nothing that matters*, and Juniper understood with terrible clarity that she was going to betray him. One way or another. Either she delivered him to the thirteenth gate as commanded, or she defied the Hollowing and unraveled into nameless wind—and either path ended with the cub learning what she was.

The only choice left to her was which betrayal happened later, and whether she'd earned forgiveness before it did.

"The tide turns within the hour," she said aloud, rising, shaking off the thought like rain. "If we're moving, we move now."

Paloma stuffed the papers inside his coat and stood. In the cistern's dark, his face was all shadows and cheekbones, and for a moment he looked so much like Lira that Juniper's heart stuttered against its geas.

"One thing first," Marisol said quietly. She had gone very still, chart forgotten, staring at the tunnel mouth behind them. "How long has that been there?"

They turned together.

On the wall beside the cistern steps, where bare stone had been an hour ago, someone had chalked a single mark: a circle, thirteen-pointed, with one point struck through in red.

The Office's sign. *Marked for collection.*

And beneath it, in a clerk's neat hand, fresh enough that the chalk still gleamed:

*The seller is known. The buyer is ours.*

Juniper's hackles rose. Paloma went white in the cistern gloom, and she watched him do the arithmetic—watched him arrive at the only answer, the fence, the charm, the taste of flour and laughter in a stranger's mouth—and watched him understand, too late, exactly what he had sold.

Not a memory.

Himself.
