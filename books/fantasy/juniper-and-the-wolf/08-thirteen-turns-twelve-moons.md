## 8. Thirteen Turns, Twelve Moons

The safehouse was a defunct candle-maker's shop in the Weft, where the ceilings sagged like tired shoulders and the air still smelled faintly of tallow and old smoke. Paloma had chosen it for the same reason he chose everything: no one looked twice at it. Ceniza was a city full of rooms nobody wanted to remember, and he had learned to live inside them.

He sat on an overturned crate with the map spread across his knees, and Juniper sat on the counter above him like a gargoyle that had decided to be beautiful instead. Her fur caught the lamplight in colors that shouldn't have existed—ember-orange shot through with threads of green, as if autumn had argued with spring and both had won. Her mismatched eyes watched him with an patience that made his skin prickle.

"You keep looking at the ash gate's sigil," she said.

"I'm not looking at anything."

"You've been staring at it since moonrise. Your thumb keeps touching it and then pulling away, like it bit you." She tilted her head. "Did it bite you?"

Paloma flexed his hand. The truth was that it had—not with teeth, but with heat. Every time his blood came near one of the thirteen marks inked into the leather, something under his skin answered, a low banked warmth like a hearth he'd spent twenty-three years pretending wasn't there. He'd stolen memories from hundreds of people. He knew what borrowed fire felt like. This was not borrowed. This was *his*, rising up through the floorboards of himself, knocking on doors he'd nailed shut.

"It hums," he admitted. "When I get close. Like it knows me."

"It does know you." Juniper dropped from the counter without sound, which was a small obscenity for a creature her size, and padded across the dusty boards until she sat before him, tail curled around her feet. "And you're going to stop flinching from that, cub, because we don't have time for flinching anymore. Do you know what tonight is?"

"Tuesday."

She bared her teeth at him—not quite a growl, not quite a grin. "Tonight is the turn of the second moon. Full. And while you were sleeping off your rooftop escape, I went up to the aqueduct head and counted." She let that sit. "Twelve full moons remain before the thirteenth turn. After that, there is no thirteenth gate to open, because there will be nothing left on either side of it worth opening."

* * *

He made her start at the beginning, because he didn't trust any story that began in the middle—the middle was where liars lived. So Juniper curled herself on the cold hearthstone, which she claimed remembered fires older than the city, and told him about the world before Ceniza had walls.

"There weren't always two realms," she said. "There was one world, wild and mortal tangled together like roots through soil. Humans dreamed beside foxes. Rivers had opinions. Your grandmother's grandmother would have thought nothing of asking an oak for directions." Her eyes half-lidded. "It was loud. It was dangerous. It was alive in a way this city has forgotten how to spell."

"And then?"

"And then the Hollowing came." The word changed her voice; it went flat and careful, the way people spoke of plague or deep water. "Not a beast. Not a god. A hunger with a shape, if it has a shape at all—it eats meaning, cub. It doesn't devour bodies. It devours the *between*. The space where a thing connects to other things. Where it got here, no one agrees. But where it went, everyone who survived remembers: it ate a whole country once. Left the land standing, the rivers running, the people breathing—and empty. No grief, no love, no names they cared to keep. They walked and worked and starved of something no physician could name."

Paloma thought of the merchant who bought his charms. The man who wanted to feel something so badly he paid silver for a dead widow's snowfall. He had assumed the merchant was simply hollowed out by wealth and boredom. Now the word *Hollowing* settled into that memory like a stone into a pond.

"The guardians stopped it," he said. It wasn't a question. The map had told him that much in its silent, burning way.

"Three of them. Wolf, Stag, Owl." Juniper raised one paw with each name, as if dealing cards. "The Wolf, who ran the borders between all things and knew every threshold by its scent. The Stag, whose antlers held up the sky-chart, the great pattern of seasons and tides. And the Owl, who kept the archive of oaths—who remembered the covenant even as it was being spoken, so it could never be broken by forgetting." She paused. "They couldn't kill the Hollowing. You cannot kill a hunger. But they could starve it, wall it, and bind it beneath a seal—and that is what they did. Three oaths, spoken at three gates, woven into thirteen. The realms were parted. Mortal inside, wild outside, and the Hollowing sealed in the dark between, fed only on the slow leak of stray memory that slipped through the seams."

"That sounds merciful."

"It was necessary. Merciful would have been different." She licked a paw, unhurried, but her mismatched eyes never left him. "Here is what your mother should have been allowed to tell you herself, and I am sorry that my mouth has to do it instead. The seal is not a wall. Walls stand. The seal is a *vow*—and vows are living things. They need tending. Each guardian bound their line to the covenant: heir after heir, generation after generation, renewing the oath at the gates so the weave stayed strong. That was the price of the parting. Forever is paid in installments."

* * *

Paloma stood. He hadn't meant to; his legs simply decided, and he found himself at the shuttered window, pressing his eye to the slat, watching the moon over Ceniza's rooftops. It was fat and white and indifferent, and somewhere behind it, in the arithmetic Juniper was laying out, his life was being subtracted.

"My mother," he said carefully, "left when I was six. She walked out of our room in the Tanners' Row tenements and never came back. I used to think—" He stopped. Started again. "I built a lot of stories about that. None of them had her sealing realms in them."

"No," Juniper said softly. "Because the covenant was already failing when she was young. The Owl's line had thinned to a single daughter who refused the vow—ran from it, married into a merchant house, pretended the blood in her was wine and not lightning. Her children felt nothing at the gates. The Owl's thread snapped." The fox's voice roughened. "A covenant with two strands holds. A covenant with two strands and a fraying third begins to unravel. Lira saw the signs before the priests did. Gates waking. Sigils warming. Stray magic leaking into Ceniza like water through a cracked dam—which is why your Office of Memory exists, though they've forgotten they exist *because of the seal*, not instead of it."

"She went to the gates."

"She went to the gates. She renewed the Wolf's oath alone, year after year, doing the work of a duty meant to be shared. And each year it cost her more. An unshared oath takes flesh for interest." Juniper rose and crossed to him, and for once she did not make it graceful; she moved like something old and tired wearing a young body. "The last turn she could manage was five years ago. She knew she could not hold the seal and raise a cub. She chose to spend what remained of her strength buying you time—hiding your inheritance so deep even the Office's diviners missed it, and going to the dark between to feed the prison her own memories, her own years, her own *name*, so the Hollowing would stay asleep a little longer. So that you could grow up. So that someone, someday, could stand where she stood with more than she had."

Paloma's hand was flat against the window slat. He realized distantly that the wood had gone warm under his palm, that the wolf-blood was answering his grief the way it answered the sigils, and he made himself breathe until it cooled.

"You said the Owl's heir is dead."

"The Owl's line ended with that daughter's refusal. Whatever children she bore carry no spark. The thread is cut, cub. There is no Owl coming to the thirteenth gate." Juniper sat back on her haunches. "Which leaves the arithmetic. Say it aloud. I want to hear you say it, because you are the kind of person who believes numbers only when he speaks them himself."

He almost refused. Then he turned from the window and did it, because she was right—he trusted his own voice against hope better than hope against his own voice.

"Thirteen gates. Twelve moons left. Two heirs needed—one Wolf, one Stag—" He faltered. "We haven't found the Stag's heir either."

"We will. The stag-blood pools in certain families like rain in low ground. I can smell it on the wind when we cross the right districts." She flicked an ear. "But yes. Keep counting."

"Two heirs. One missing. One line extinct. Twelve turns." He laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and sharp as wire. "That's not arithmetic. That's a verdict."

"It's a deadline," Juniper corrected. "Verdicts pretend to be final. Deadlines just mean someone has to move fast."

* * *

He sat back down on the crate because his legs had opinions again, worse ones this time. The map lay on the floor between them, thirteen sigils circling the shape of Ceniza like a crown of thorns drawn by a careful hand. He looked at it and tried to feel what he supposed he was supposed to feel—destiny, purpose, the warm weight of legacy—and instead felt what he actually felt, which was fury.

"I didn't ask for this."

"No."

"I built a life. A small one, a hidden one, but it was *mine*. I had rules. Never steal from the same mark twice. Never sell a memory that could identify me. Never touch my own past." His voice climbed. "I was invisible, Juniper. Twenty-three years of being no one, done *well*, and in one night some bleeding stranger hands me a map and now I'm supposed to be the Wolf? I don't even—I've never run with anything. I've never belonged to a pack or a guild or a family. I'm a thief who sells other people's happiness at four coppers a charm. What part of that qualifies me to hold a seal together?"

Juniper listened to all of it without interrupting, which he suspected cost her something. When he finished, breathing hard, she said only:

"Do you want the honest answer or the kind one?"

"Honest. Always honest. Kindness is how people lie to me politely."

"The honest answer is that none of it qualifies you." She stepped closer, and the lamplight moved in her green eye like water. "Inheritance isn't a qualification, cub. It's a fact. The blood doesn't care whether you trained for it. The gates opened for you because you are Lira's son, and that is the entire interview." She let the silence stretch, then gentled, barely. "But I'll tell you what does qualify you, since you asked. You've spent your life handling things that don't belong to you—memories, griefs, joys—with more care than most people give their own. You know exactly what it costs to carry what isn't yours. That is not nothing. That might be everything. The old guardians were warriors and wardens. Maybe what the covenant needs now, in its last turn, is someone who understands theft and restitution from the inside."

Paloma stared at her. Something in his chest had gone tight and strange, and he hated that it was gratitude, hated that a talking fox had found the one true thing to say to him.

"Don't do that," he muttered.

"Do what?"

"Make me feel like this might be survivable. I had a good sulk going."

"You can sulk while we walk." She trotted to the door and nosed it open onto the night. "Because here is the rest of the arithmetic, and it's the part I like least. The seal decays whether or not we're ready. The Hollowing stirs a little more each turn—it has already begun sending shapes through the cracks, testing the weave. And the Office of Memory has noticed the gates waking too. They don't understand what they're seeing, but they understand *power*, and power is the one thing priests always harvest." Her muzzle pointed toward the dark streets beyond, toward the distant bulk of the Office spire, black against the moonlit haze. "So. Twelve moons. Eleven gates unopened. One Stag to find among a million strangers. One extinct Owl casting a shadow over everything. And a city full of hunters who want the map, the blood, and the bearer."

Paloma gathered the map, rolled it, and tied it with the cord he'd strung his charm-samples on, back when his biggest problem was inventory. The leather was warm against his chest where he tucked it away, warm as a sleeping animal, warm as something waiting.

"One question," he said at the door. "My mother. When she went into the dark between. Did it hurt her?"

Juniper was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer.

"Yes," she said finally. "And she would be furious with me for telling you, and more furious if I lied." She looked up at him, ember and tide, ancient and young at once. "Come, cub. Moonlight's wasting. We have a Stag to find, and I smell rain and copper on the wind from the Salt Market—which means either a storm, or a bargain, or both."

Paloma pulled his hood up, checked the shadows out of twenty years of habit, and stepped out into the night with a dead woman's duty warm against his ribs and twelve moons falling behind him like coins down a well.

Behind them, in the empty shop, the thirteenth sigil on the discarded scrap of leather glowed faintly in the dark—and pulsed, once, like a heartbeat that had noticed it was being counted.
