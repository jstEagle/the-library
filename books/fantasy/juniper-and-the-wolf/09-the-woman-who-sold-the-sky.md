## 9. The Woman Who Sold the Sky

Marisol Quintero had three rules, and she kept them the way other people kept faith.

One: never chart a sky you haven't seen with your own eyes. Two: never sell a chart to a man who asks what it means. Three: never—under any circumstance, for any price, at the point of any knife—believe in magic.

She was thinking about rule three on the morning the fox came to her stall.

The salt market smelled of brine and tallow and the wet wool of fishermen who'd been out since before dawn, and Marisol's stall sat at its edge like an apology—a folding table draped in indigo cloth, weighted with river stones, and across it laid out her charts: squares of vellum painted with skies that did not exist and never had. She sold them as decoration. *For your wall, señora. For your shop, señor. A sky over your bed so you can fall asleep looking up.* That was the story, and it was a good one, because it was almost true. She did paint skies. She simply could not explain how she knew what to paint.

Every chart began the same way: she closed her eyes, and something moved behind them—not memory, not imagination, but *weather*. A pressure. A turning. And when she opened her eyes and set brush to vellum, her hand went where the pressure told it, laying down constellations no astronomer had catalogued, star-paths that bent in ways that made sensible people frown and put the chart back down. The ones who bought them were the strange ones—the widow who swore the chart above her hearth stopped her nightmares, the sailor who paid triple for a sky he said matched nothing but steered him home through fog anyway. Marisol took their coin and did not ask questions, because questions were the first crack in a dam, and she had spent thirty-one years being very good about dams.

Then the fox sat down in front of her table and looked at her with two different eyes—one amber, one the grey of a storm coming—and said, in a voice like dry leaves settling:

"You're the Stag's."

Marisol's brush dragged a black line across six hours of work.

"I beg your pardon?"

The fox licked one paw with maddening calm. "You heard me."

"Foxes don't talk." She said it firmly, the way she said *the charts are just decoration* and *I have never once dreamed in someone else's voice* and all the other sentences that held her life together. "Foxes especially don't talk in the salt market at midmorning."

"This one does." The fox's mismatched eyes flicked left, toward the mouth of the market, and Marisol followed the glance without meaning to. A man stood there in the grey robes of the Memory Office, his cowl pushed back, his face long and tired and watchful. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at a young man loitering by the fishmongers' row—young, dark-haired, dressed in the careful shabbiness of someone who had learned to be unremarkable and was currently failing at it, because he was staring at her stall with an expression Marisol recognized from her own mirror: the look of a person seeing something they'd spent their whole life pretending wasn't real.

"That boy," the fox said, "is going to get us all collected if you keep gawking. Act natural. Sell him a sky."

* * *

His name was Paloma, though he didn't offer it; she got it later, from the fox, who gave it up like a bone she didn't particularly want to share. He bought a chart—the storm one, the one Marisol had painted in a fever-haze last autumn and never quite understood—and he counted out coins with hands that shook only slightly, and he said, quietly, under the fishmonger's bellowing:

"The woman who sold the sky. That's what they called your grandmother. In the highlands."

Marisol's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"My grandmother," she said, "was a goat-herd's wife who died before I was born."

"One of those things is true." He glanced at the grey-robed priest, still drifting closer along the stalls, touching things, murmuring to vendors who grew vague and agreeable as he passed. Memory-priests. Everyone in Ceniza knew what they were for. You saw one coming and you thought very hard about nothing until the danger passed. "The other one is what the Office erased. They took her name off every record in the province. But they couldn't take it off the stones." His eyes came up to hers, and they were brown and frightened and absolutely certain. "There's a shrine up past Vado Seco with her mark cut into the lintel. I've seen it. It's the same mark that's been on your charts since you started painting them."

"That's—" She laughed, and hated how thin it came out. "That's coincidence. That's a common motif. Spiral-and-branch, it's in half the folk art of the—"

"Señora." He leaned in, and his voice dropped to something urgent and low. "I'm not here to hurt you. I'm here because in four turns' time, everything ends or doesn't, and whether it does depends partly on you, and I have maybe ninety seconds before that priest finishes working his way down this row. So: either come with us now, or burn your charts tonight and go back to sleep, and I swear on my mother's grave I'll never trouble you again."

Behind him, the fox yawned, showing too many teeth for a creature its size.

Marisol looked at the chart in the boy's hands—at the spiral-and-branch sigil she'd painted without knowing why, in the corner, the way she always did, the way her hand insisted—and felt the dam strain against thirty-one years of held water.

"I close the stall at noon," she heard herself say.

* * *

They walked her out through the tannery district's back ways, the boy leading, the fox trotting at her heel like a dog nobody had asked for, and Marisol spent the whole walk composing arguments and discarding them, because the terrible truth was that she wasn't arguing with them. She was arguing with herself, and losing.

Her mother had died when she was nine, and before that her mother had been a quiet woman with burned-looking eyes who flinched at church bells and made Marisol recite rules like prayers: *We are ordinary. We want nothing. If anyone ever asks what you see when you close your eyes, you see darkness.* Marisol had assumed, in the way of children, that these were games, then habits, then grief-madness. Only much later—too late to ask—had she understood they were armor, handed down like an heirloom nobody wanted.

And now here was a stranger carrying the same heirloom, saying words like *Stag* and *covenant* and *thirteen gates*, and instead of laughing she was following him into the aqueduct tunnels with her best charts rolled under her arm, which told her exactly how much of her disbelief had ever been real.

The tunnels smelled of wet stone and old iron. Someone had hung lanterns—three of them, cheap oil, guttering—and beneath their light the boy spread the leather map on a flat rock, weighting the corners, and Marisol forgot to breathe.

Thirteen circles ringed a drawing of Ceniza, each circle holding a sigil, and twelve of the thirteen sigils she knew intimately, because she had been painting them for eleven years without knowing what they were. Her hand had known. Her hand had always known.

"And this one." The boy touched the thirteenth circle, at the map's crown. "This is where it has to happen. The thirteenth turn. Before the seal fails completely."

"Explain it slowly," Marisol said. "As if I'm stupid. Because right now I need to be stupid for a while longer."

So he explained. The covenant, the three guardians—Wolf, Stag, Owl—who had sealed the mortal realm away from the wild one to imprison something called the Hollowing. The seal decaying turn by turn, moon by moon. The heirs scattered and ignorant, deliberately, so the Office could never find them all at once. His own blood, wolf-blooded, hidden since birth. And the Stag's line—her line—keepers of the paths between, the walkers of boundaries, the ones whose gift was not power over things but *knowledge of the way through*.

"Sky-charts," the fox said, watching her with that storm-grey eye. "You've been drawing the wild realm's sky your whole life, cub-of-the-Stag. Every chart is a window. You just never opened one."

"Don't call me that."

"What, Stag?"

"Cub-of-the-Stag. Any of it." Marisol pressed her palms flat against the cold rock beside the map, steadying herself. "I want to show you something, and then you're going to tell me honestly what it means, because I have never once said any of this out loud and if I don't say it now I think it will eat me alive."

She took a fresh sheet of vellum from her roll. She took her brush. She closed her eyes.

The pressure came, as it always came—that turning weather behind her eyelids—but tonight it came *fast*, eager, like a dog hearing its name after years of being called *dog*. She let it move her hand. Ink spiraled into branches, branches into antlers, antlers into a lattice of star-roads, and when she opened her eyes the boy had gone pale and the fox had gone utterly still, ears forward, and both of them were staring at the map.

Because the chart matched it. Not approximately. Exactly. Thirteen gates, thirteen sigils, laid out in the same ringed pattern she'd been painting since she was nineteen years old and had told herself was a *style*, a *signature*, a harmless quirk of a decorative trade.

"That's the covenant," Paloma whispered. "That's—it's the whole thing. How do you—"

"I don't know." Her voice cracked and she let it. "I close my eyes and it's there. It's always been there. My mother used to watch me draw as a child and look like someone was standing on her chest, and I thought—I thought she was ashamed of me. Of the drawings. And maybe she was, but not the way I thought." Marisol looked down at her ink-stained fingers. "She wasn't ashamed. She was terrified. Because she knew what I was, and she knew what happened to people like us in this city, and she chose to teach me to hide instead of teaching me to understand. And I have hated her a little bit for that my entire adult life, and now I'm standing in a tunnel realizing she probably saved my life every single day just by lying to me, and I don't know what to do with that."

The silence rang. Water dripped somewhere far off, patient as a clock.

"I used to steal memories," Paloma said at last, quietly. "Before all this. Little ones. Happy ones. Sold them as charms. I told myself it was survival, and it was, but mostly I did it because touching other people's good years was easier than living inside my own head." He shrugged, a small bitter motion. "My mother hid me too. Hid me so well I grew up believing she'd abandoned me. So no—I don't think you should forgive her yet. I think you should come with us, find out what she was protecting, and then decide. Forgiveness is better informed at the end of a road than the beginning of one."

The fox snorted. "That's almost wise. Did Juniper teach you that?"

"You taught me the opposite of that, actually."

"And yet."

Marisol looked between them—the boy with his dead mother and his dangerous blood, the impossible fox with her mismatched eyes—and felt something shift in her chest that she refused, for the moment, to name. Recognition was the closest word. The specific relief of meeting another person who had grown up fluent in a language everyone around them claimed didn't exist.

"If I come with you," she said, "and this is madness—if you're a fraud, or worse, an Office trap—"

"Then you'll have lost a stall," Paloma said. "I'll have lost considerably more. I'd make a very poor trap."

"The Office makes excellent traps."

"Not ones that smell this scared." The fox rose, stretched until her spine curved like a drawn bow, and fixed Marisol with the amber eye, then the grey one, as if offering each in turn. "You've been waiting your whole life for permission, Stag-cub. He can't give it to you. Neither can I. But the thirteenth moon doesn't care whether you believe in it, and neither does the thing eating through the seal. Believe or don't. Walk or don't. But decide tonight, because Brother Ossian already knows your face, and by tomorrow the Office will know your stall."

Marisol looked down at the chart drying on the rock—her own hand's confession, thirteen gates in a ring, the shape of a duty she'd carried blind for eleven years.

She thought of rule three. *Never believe in magic.*

Then she thought of her mother's flinching eyes, and the widow sleeping soundly beneath a painted sky, and the sailor steered home through fog by stars that weren't there, and understood, with a lurching, vertiginous calm, that she had believed all along. Belief had never been the question. Only obedience to the fear.

"Give me one hour to pack," she said. "And someone tell me who Ossian is, and why the fox says his name like it tastes bad."

Paloma and the fox exchanged a glance—quick, guilty, a whole conversation in a fraction of a second—and Marisol, who had spent her life reading what people didn't paint, caught it and felt the fragile new alliance tremble on its foundations.

"There's something you're not telling me already," she said flatly. "Wonderful. What is it?"

"It can wait," Paloma said.

"No," said the fox, and her mismatched eyes gleamed in the lantern light. "It really can't."
