## 10. Brother Ossian's Mercy

The trap was not set in the tannery district, nor at the salt gate, nor anywhere the map's sigils burned. Brother Ossian had learned long ago that memory-thieves were creatures of habit, and that the strongest habit of all was kindness.

So he waited at the widow's door on Candlemaker's Row, where the old woman Amaia lived alone with her cat and her grief, and where a certain charm-seller had been known to visit without charge, leaving small comforts wrapped in wax paper. He had watched the boy do it twice before—slip up the stairs at dusk, knock softly, sit with the widow while she wept over a memory of her husband that Ossian himself had catalogued and condemned as contraband.

It was, Ossian reflected, an absurd way to run an investigation: staking out compassion. But then, everything about this case had been absurd from the beginning. The map of the thirteen gates should have been ash in the Office furnace by now. The boy who carried it should have been in a cell beneath the Basilica of Recorded Sorrows, his blood sampled, his name entered into the Ledger of Debts. Instead Ossian had spent eleven days following him through Ceniza's rooftops and aqueduct shadows, watching him open seams in the world with hands that shook afterward, and telling himself each night that tomorrow he would make the arrest.

Tomorrow kept not coming.

The door opened below him. Amaia's voice, thin and pleased: "Paloma, you're late. The cat missed you."

Ossian stood in the stairwell shadow, hood back, wearing the gray wool of a minor clerk rather than the white-and-ash of his office. He had learned that trick from the thieves themselves: the best disguise is being no one worth remembering.

The boy came up the stairs two at a time, a paper-wrapped honey cake in one hand, and Ossian stepped out of the dark and said, quietly, "Don't run."

* * *

Paloma froze with one foot on the landing, and every instinct he owned screamed at once—the rooftops behind him, the window ahead, the knife at his belt that he'd never once used on a person. But the man blocking the stairwell wasn't wearing priest's white. He looked like a clerk, a tired one, gray at the temples, with ink stains on his fingers and eyes the color of wet slate that did not look tired at all.

"I know you," Paloma said, because he did. The memory-priest from the ash gate. Brother Ossian, whose face he had studied across a crowded square while Juniper hissed *down, cub, down* in his ear. "You're the one who nearly—"

"Caught you. Yes." The man didn't reach for iron or rope or any of the things memory-priests were rumored to carry. He simply stood there, occupying the stairs the way a wall occupies a doorway. "And I could have caught you four times since. The night you slept in the granary loft. The morning you bought bread from the baker on Tallow Street—I was the third customer in line. Two days ago, when you sat on the aqueduct wall for an hour doing nothing but watching the water."

Paloma's mouth went dry. "Why didn't you?"

"Because I wanted to understand you first." Ossian tilted his head toward the widow's door. "You sell memories to merchants who want to feel something, and you give them away to widows who've lost everything. You steal for coin and then spend half of it on people who can't pay. You're either the worst criminal in Ceniza or the most honest man in it, and I've spent eleven days failing to decide which."

"Is that what this is?" Paloma's hand drifted toward his belt. "An interrogation?"

"It's an offer." Ossian said it plainly, without theater, and somehow that was worse than theater would have been. "And I'd ask you to hear all of it before you decide anything. Not because I'm kind. Because you deserve to choose with your eyes open. That's more than most people the Office touches ever get."

Somewhere below, Amaia's cat began to sing its broken evening song against a door. Paloma thought about running. He thought about the window, the rooftops, the seam of wild forest he could tear open if he bled onto stone—and the thought of using it made something hot and ashamed move in his chest, because part of him *wanted* to, wanted the green sideways light and the power of it, and that wanting frightened him more than the priest did.

"Talk," he said.

Ossian talked.

* * *

Brother Ossian had joined the Memory Office at nineteen, which everyone agreed was young, and which no one who knew his sister found surprising.

Her name had been Serafina. She had been born with the same unlicensed gift the ledgers called *theft of self*, though in Serafina's hands it had never once looked like theft. She lifted pain. That was all she did, all she wanted to do: she would press her palm to a burn scar or a grieving temple and draw the worst of it out like a splinter, and she'd carry it herself for a day until it faded, and she never charged a coin. Their mother called it a curse. Serafina called it Tuesday.

Ossian had loved his sister the way you love a fire in winter—helplessly, and with a constant low awareness that it could burn the house down.

She died at twenty-six, in the Hollowing scare of five years past, when the Office was burning charms in the salt market square and half the city was convinced the end of the world smelled like smoke. She had gone into the quarantine rows to ease the dying, drawing their terror out of them so they could die clean instead of screaming, and someone reported her, and the collectors came, and in the struggle—in the ugly, panicked, ordinary struggle of six men trying to subdue one woman who could pull their own worst memories into their heads—she had torn something loose in herself that should not be torn. They called it backlash. The report used forty words to describe what Ossian had seen with his own eyes: his sister, emptied. Sitting in the street with her hands open in her lap, looking up at him with a face that held nothing, asking politely who he was.

She lived nine years after that, in the Office's own charity ward, smiling at walls. Ossian visited every week. He joined the Office six months after it happened, because someone had to make sure the rules were enforced *correctly*, because if magic was debt then debts had to be collected fairly or the whole ledger rotted, because order was the only thing standing between gifts like hers and endings like hers.

He had told himself that story for fifteen years. It fit him like his old uniform: snug in some places, strangling in others, worn smooth enough that he'd stopped feeling where it cut.

Then a dying stranger had pressed a leather map into a thief's hands, and the sigils on that map had begun waking one by one across Ceniza, and Ossian had knelt in the ash gate's tunnel and touched the stone where the wild realm's edge had bled through—a seam in the world smelling of rain and growing things—and felt, for three full seconds, his sister's laugh in his own memory, clear as the day she'd given it to him.

The Office doctrine said magic was debt. But kneeling there, Ossian had understood something the doctrine had no room for: that the debt ran both directions. That the mortal realm had borrowed everything—every harvest, every spring, every dream worth having—from the wild side of the seal, and had spent five hundred years pretending the loan was a gift.

He stood in the stairwell now, looking at Lira's son, and made the offer he had rehearsed for eleven days and hated for ten of them.

* * *

"The Office knows what you are," Ossian said. "Not just a thief. Wolf-blood. Heir-significant. There are perhaps six living people in Ceniza with blood that answers the gates, and you're the only one carrying the map, which makes you the most dangerous object in this city wearing a boy's skin."

"Charming."

"When they take you—and they will take you, Paloma; the informant who bought your charm sold your description along with your childhood, and the Order of the Veil has already been summoned from the coast—they will not kill you. Killing a gate-heir risks breaking the seal faster. Instead they will do what the Office does best." Ossian's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which made each word land like a stone dropped in still water. "They will edit you."

"Edit me."

"There is a rite. Old, sanctioned, rarely used, because it requires consent and most people don't consent to losing themselves." He watched the boy's face carefully. "They call it the Quiet Mercy. They unmake the wolf-blood. Not the body—the *inheritance*. Every instinct, every answering pulse in your veins, every moment you've ever felt the gates call to you. Cut out at the root, cleanly, the way a surgeon takes a limb. You'd wake up remembering your life but not the shape of it. You'd remember your mother without understanding why her absence hurts in a key no other absence hits. You'd walk past the thirteenth gate on the last night and feel nothing at all."

"And the seal?" Paloma asked, and hated how steady his voice came out. "The Hollowing? The thing eating through twelve gates while your Office polices charm-sellers?"

"Not my department," Ossian said, and heard, saying it aloud, exactly how much of a lie it was. Everything was his department. That was the disease of believing in order. "The Office believes it can manage what comes through. Harvest it. Contain it. Turn the flood into a reservoir." He paused. "I believed that too. Until recently."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I arrest you tonight, and the Quiet Mercy happens anyway, eventually, after enough questions. Consent is faster. Kinder." He let that sit. "There's a third option. It's the one I'm supposed to pretend doesn't exist."

Paloma waited.

"You disappear. Tonight, now, before the Veil arrives—you have perhaps two days, maybe less. You go where the map points, you do whatever it is the dead man who gave it to you intended, and I lose my quarry. I report that the thief escaped through means unknown. My career ends; possibly my freedom does; certainly my standing in the Office does." Ossian's jaw tightened. "In exchange, you surrender the map to no one, harm no one with what you open, and when this is finished—if it can be finished—you come back and answer for the thefts. All of them. On the record. In front of whatever new law gets written, if any law survives what's coming."

"That's not mercy," Paloma said slowly. "That's a leash."

"It's both. Most mercies are." Ossian reached into his coat—not fast, telegraphing every motion—and drew out a folded paper. He held it between them. "This is the writ for your Quiet Mercy. Signed. Dated yesterday. Your name is already on it, spelled correctly, which took some doing since the Office still has you listed under three different aliases." His hand trembled slightly, and he let Paloma see it tremble. "All you have to do is say yes, and put your thumbprint here, and by tomorrow sundown you'll be free. Genuinely free. No priests hunting you, no gates calling you, no mother-shaped wound you can't explain. A small life. A safe life. You could keep selling snowfalls to merchants who want to feel something. You'd be good at it forever."

The paper hung in the air between them.

* * *

Paloma looked at it for a long time.

He thought about the nights he woke with his heart hammering, certain the walls were breathing. He thought about the sigil-burn in his blood whenever he neared a gate, the hot sweet ache of it, like a name being called in a language he'd been forbidden to speak. He thought about his mother's face in the stolen interrogation-memory, and how he still hadn't told Juniper what he'd seen, and how the lie sat in him like a swallowed stone.

A small life. A safe life. No wolf.

"No gates calling you."

He tried to imagine it honestly, the way Ossian had framed it—tried to picture walking past the thirteenth gate on the last night of the world feeling *nothing*. And here was the terrible thing, the thing he would never tell anyone: for three heartbeats, it sounded like peace. Like setting down a pack he'd carried since birth. Like the silence after a tooth stops aching.

Then he thought of the seam beneath the ash gate. The forest growing sideways, leaves like green coins, air that tasted of a world before walls. He thought of how alive he had felt standing in it, and how guilty, and how the guilt had not made the aliveness untrue.

If they cut the wolf out, they weren't cutting out a sickness. They were cutting out the only part of him that had ever belonged to anything larger than a rented room.

"My mother didn't abandon me," he said quietly. "Did she. You know something. Everyone in this city seems to know something except me."

Ossian's expression flickered—grief, quickly banked. "I know she was interrogated. I know she gave up a name to protect someone. I know she walked into the river gate five years ago and did not walk out, and that the Office recorded it as a suicide, and that I signed the record." He met Paloma's eyes. "I also know that suicides don't leave echoes in covenant-stone. Whatever she did in there, boy, it was a sacrifice, and sacrifices are the opposite of abandonment. But I won't tell you what she sacrificed for. That's yours to find, if you choose to go find it."

Silence stretched. Below them, Amaia's cat had gone quiet. Somewhere far off, a bell marked the ninth hour, and Paloma thought: *one moon less.*

"If I take your third option," he said, "you lose everything."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Ossian was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice had roughened, the clerk's mask slipping just enough to show the brother underneath. "Because I had a sister who eased the dying, and my Office burned her for it. Because I have spent fifteen years enforcing a ledger that says magic is debt, and I have finally admitted to myself that the debtors wrote the ledger. And because—" he exhaled, "—because when I knelt at that ash gate, I heard her laugh. From the other side. And I am not going to be the man who sealed the door on my sister's laughter twice."

He held out the writ, and a small knife beside it. "Burn the writ. Keep the knife; you'll need steel where you're going. And Paloma—" the use of his true name, spoken aloud for the first time, landed strangely warm, "—the Veil arrives in two days. The informant's report reaches the Basilica tomorrow at dawn. Don't be in Ceniza when it does."

Paloma took the paper. He did not take the knife yet. "And if I come back to answer for the thefts?"

"Then I'll be the first one in line to testify for you," Ossian said. "After I testify against you. Both things are true. Get used to that; it's what covenants are made of."

* * *

He watched the boy go—out the window, across the rooftops, a slim dark shape moving over the sleeping city with a burned writ's ashes still dusting his sleeve—and Ossian stood alone in the stairwell of a building full of borrowed memories, feeling lighter and more damned than he had in fifteen years.

He had until dawn to write his report. He had decided, somewhere between the ninth bell and now, what it would say.

He was still deciding how to survive what came after, when he turned toward the stairs and found the widow Amaia standing in her doorway, holding her cat, looking at him with the clear-eyed calm of a woman who had heard every word.

"You're the one who catalogued my husband's memory," she said. "Contraband, your file called it. Forty-one years of marriage, filed under contraband."

Ossian closed his eyes. "Yes, madam."

"He's waiting downstairs," she said. "The fox. Says you owe the cub a message about the salt gate, and that you'd better hurry, because the tide's turning early tonight."
