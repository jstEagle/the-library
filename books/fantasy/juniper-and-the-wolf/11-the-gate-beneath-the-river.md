## 11. The Gate Beneath the River

The river gate slept beneath the Sorrow, where the water ran green-black and slow over stones the masons of old Ceniza had carved with faces. Paloma had walked that embankment a hundred times in his life, haggling for fish, dodging the tithe-collectors, pressing his shoulder to the wall when the memory-priests passed with their censers of forgetting-smoke. He had never once looked down.

Now he looked down, and the faces looked back.

"They're not decoration," Marisol said. Her voice had gone thin and strange since the highlands, since the antler-shrine, since she'd seen every death waiting for her like coats on hooks. She knelt at the water's edge, skirts darkening with spray, and traced one carved face with a fingertip. "They're wardens. Somebody put them here to watch the door."

"Or to watch what comes out of it," Juniper said. The fox sat on a bollard wrapped in rope gone soft with rot, her mismatched eyes—one amber, one the grey of river ice—fixed on the current. In the two days since the bone orchard, since Marisol had torn the geas out into the open and Paloma had chosen, wordlessly and with his whole chest aching, not to send her away, Juniper had been quieter than Paloma had ever known her. She still made jokes. They just landed softer now, like things set down carefully rather than thrown.

"The map says midnight," Paloma said. He unrolled the leather across his knees. Thirteen sigils circled the city on it like a crown of thorns, and the twelfth from the sun-rise mark—the river gate—had begun to weep. Actual moisture beaded along its grooves, dark against the old hide, as if the map itself were standing in the rain. "And it says blood."

"It always says blood," Marisol muttered. "Whoever wrote this covenant really committed to a theme."

Paloma almost smiled. Almost. His hand drifted to his collarbone, where beneath his shirt the wolf-token hung—the first of three, warm as a sleeping animal against his skin. It had been warm ever since the ash gate. Warmer now, so close to water, and he didn't want to think about why fire-things warmed near rivers, or what that said about what waited below.

* * *

They went down at moonrise, because the map was specific about that too, and because Marisol—chart-seller, sky-reader, a woman who had spent thirty years pretending the stars didn't speak to her—said the moon would be low enough tonight to touch the water's reflection, and gates liked to open where a thing met its own image.

The way down was a maintenance stair behind the fishmongers' arcade, a spiral of wet stone that smelled of brine and old iron. Paloma counted ninety-one steps. At the bottom, the river ran overhead.

That was the part his mind kept snagging on. They descended *below* the Sorrow, through an archway that should have been drowned, and the water did not come with them. It hung above—a ceiling of moving green light, fish shadows sliding through it, the moon's reflection rippling across the underside like a coin seen through tea. The air beneath was cold and dry and tasted of stone dust and something older: crushed leaves, animal musk, the green breath of a forest that had no business being under a city.

"The covenant holds the water up," Juniper said softly. She had stopped joking entirely now. Her fur stood along her spine, not in fear, Paloma thought, but in something like reverence. "It holds everything up. The wall. The river. The line between what your people are and what they used to be. You're standing underneath the world's oldest promise, cub. Mind where you spit."

Before them, the tunnel ended in a door.

Not a door of wood or iron. A slab of black stone, tall as three men, and across its face ran carvings that made Paloma's stolen memories stir in their jars inside him like insects sensing weather. He knew this feeling. He'd felt it at the ash gate, that hot crawling under his skin where the sigils answered his blood. But this was deeper, heavier, a bass note instead of a whistle. The carvings were wolves and stags and owls, running in a circle, and in the center of the circle was a space shaped like nothing he could name—a hollow in the pattern, a negative space, waiting.

Three indentations sat at the base of the door. One was already filled: a smooth grey stone that might have been an egg or a skull, humming faintly.

"The Owl's anchor," Juniper said. Her voice caught. "Left here when the last of them died. It's been holding the door alone for five years." She looked at Paloma, then at Marisol, and something moved behind her mismatched eyes that she didn't let all the way out. "Five years is a long time for one stone to hold a promise."

Marisol lifted the antler-token from around her neck. In the green light it looked less like bone and more like a branch in winter, bare and patient. "So we finish the sentence," she said. "Wolf, Stag, Owl. Even if the third chair is empty."

"It won't be empty," Paloma said, and heard how flat his voice came out, and hated it, because he was thinking of Juniper at the bone orchard saying *a willing spirit can stand as the third signatory*, and of everything that cost, and of how she'd volunteered before he could stop her, and of how he'd sworn he would find another way and hadn't found it yet.

Juniper's ear flicked. She'd heard the flatness too. She said nothing, which was worse.

He pressed his palm to the wolf-indentation and let the token's warmth flow down his arm, and cut his thumb on the edge of the stone because the stone asked, and the door drank.

* * *

The gate opened inward onto darkness that was not empty.

Paloma had expected a chamber, maybe—a vault, a shrine, something built. What he got was a memory given architecture. The space beyond the door was vast and dim, and its walls were not walls but flowing surfaces, like the skin of a pond stood upright, and across those surfaces images moved: forests growing sideways, moons rising and setting in fast-forward, a stag the size of a cathedral drinking from a river of stars, an owl made of dusk folding its wings over a sleeping village. The wild realm, rendered in the covenant's own recollection. The stone remembered everything it had ever held shut.

"Careful," Marisol whispered behind him. "Don't touch the walls."

He wasn't going to touch the walls. He was a professional; he knew what touching memories did.

But the walls touched him.

It happened between one step and the next. A surface rippled beside his shoulder—not reaching, exactly. *Recognizing.* And then the chamber filled with a scent that stopped his heart mid-beat: rosemary and woodsmoke and rain on hot stone, the smell of a kitchen he had spent five years failing to forget, the smell of—

"Lira," Juniper breathed, and her voice broke on the name like ice on a pond.

She stood in the middle of the chamber, or something stood there wearing the shape of her, assembled out of the flowing light the way a figure is assembled out of smoke. Tall. Dark hair going grey early at the temples, which had embarrassed Paloma as a boy until she told him silver was just the moon leaving fingerprints. Hands stained with charm-ink. Eyes the exact brown of his own, looking at him with an expression he had catalogued for five years in the dark behind his eyelids and never once gotten right: not forgiveness, not sorrow, but *recognition*, fierce and immediate, as if he had only been gone an afternoon.

"Mamá," he said. The word came out in a child's voice. He was twenty-six years old and the word came out in a child's voice and he did not care.

"I'm not her." The echo spoke with her voice, though—gods, it spoke with her voice—and it raised her hands, palms out, the way she used to when he came home bleeding from some childhood war and she needed him to hold still. "I'm what she left in the stone. A letter, written in herself. Listen to me, mijo. We have very little time, and I only get to say this once per visitor, and I have been waiting five years to say it to you."

Paloma realized he was crying only when the tears hit his collar. He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, roughly, the way she'd taught him—you don't sob, you *handle* it—and stepped closer. Behind him he heard Juniper sit down, heavily, the way foxes do when grief is too big for four legs. Marisol had turned tactfully toward the door, keeping watch on nothing.

"Then say it," he managed.

The echo smiled, and it was her smile, crooked on the left side, and it undid him completely.

"You thought I abandoned you." Not a question. "I know. I've watched you carry it. The stone shows me things sometimes, when the seal thins—I've seen you in the market, selling other people's yesterdays because you can't bear your own. Oh, my love. My clever, careful boy. Come here. Let me show you what actually happened, because you have earned the truth, and the truth is the only inheritance I have left to give."

She extended her hand. And Paloma—who had spent his whole adult life stealing memories, who knew better than anyone alive what it was to have someone else's past poured into you, who had felt a thousand strangers' joys and griefs run through him like water through a net—took his mother's echo by the hand, and let her give him hers.

* * *

The night came back to him whole.

Five years ago. The covenant already fraying—he'd known nothing, been eleven years old and asleep, actually asleep, in the back room while the adults of a hidden world argued in whispers over his head. Lira had known for months. She was the Wolf's heir, and the wolf-token had been burning against her skin since midsummer, and she had read the signs the way she read everything: completely, and without flinching.

The Hollowing was waking. Not free—never free, not yet—but stirring in its prison, which was not a place but a hunger, sealed into the covenant itself, fed across centuries by an arrangement the old guardians had made in desperation: it ate memories. Willing ones, in the beginning. Offerings. Then, as the generations forgot the terms, it had begun taking what it could reach—cracks in the seal, lonely people, dying strangers whose last moments slipped loose and were swallowed. Every vanished person Ceniza couldn't account for. Every mind that faded early. The city called it bad luck. It was appetite.

Lira had gone to the river gate on the autumn equinox with the intention of reinforcing the seal, and had discovered two things. First: the seal could not be reinforced. The Owl's line had failed; the arithmetic no longer balanced; the best she could do was slow the decay. Second: the Hollowing had learned her son's name.

It had reached for him through the cracks. She showed Paloma that moment and it was like swallowing a knife—the sensation of a vast attention turning toward a sleeping child, patient and curious and *hungry*, tasting the shape of a boy who was heir to the Wolf and did not know it, unprotected, untrained, delicious.

"So I gave it something else to eat," the echo said, and her voice in the memory was steady, steady, steady, the voice she'd used during fever-nights and funerals and the time the roof beam cracked. "Me. A guardian's memory is the richest thing in either realm, mijo. A whole life, freely offered—it would feed the Hollowing for years. Decades, if I made it rich enough. So I made it rich enough. I walked into the prison's outer dark and I let it drink, and while it drank, I wove what remained of me into the covenant's stone, so that someday—when you came, when you finally came—you would know."

The memory showed him the giving. It was not brave the way songs made bravery look. It was terrible. It was her kneeling in a darkness that had texture, that had *mouths*, peeling away her own past layer by layer and feeding it into the hunger—her mother's kitchen, her wedding, the first time she'd held him, each one surrendered deliberately, each one bought a month of his safety, a year, a season—and she had saved the memory of him for last, cradled it, and even then she had not given it away. She had split it. Half to the hunger, to buy the final years. Half into the stone, so that something of her would be here, waiting, when he was ready.

"I didn't leave you," the echo said, and now her composure cracked, and the crack was the most real thing in the chamber. "I left you a *job*. I'm sorry. Gods forgive me, I'm sorry it fell to you. But the covenant was never meant to be a cage we handed down locked. It was meant to be rewritten by every generation that inherited it—that was the design, and the old ones forgot, and their forgetting is what starved the terms and woke the Hollowing. You can't keep my oath, Paloma. It's spent. You have to write your own."

The vision released him. He came back to himself on his knees on the cold stone, Marisol's hand on his shoulder, Juniper's warm flank pressed against his side, the echo standing over him with her hands folded, smaller now, fainter—the showing had cost her, he understood that, cost the stone's store of her.

"How much longer can you stay?" he asked hoarsely.

"Not long. Not after this." She crouched, took his face in her fading hands, and he felt them the way you feel sunlight through glass—warmth without weight. "Listen. The Hollowing knows you're coming. It will offer you anything at the last gate. It offered me you, at the end—showed me a false dawn where I walked home and kissed you goodnight. It is a liar with excellent taste in dreams. When it offers you your mother whole, remember: I am already here. I am already yours. Nothing it builds will be truer than this."

"Mamá—"

"Write well, mijo." Her smile, crooked on the left. "And mijo—one more thing. The Office has a plan for the seal's failure. Harvest, they call it. There is a man among them who carries his sister's face in his heart like a wound that won't close. He is closer to the truth than his masters know. When the moment comes, do not fight him. *Show* him."

Her hands passed through his cheeks like light through water, and she was gone—not violently, not tragically, just gently, the way a candle goes out when its work is done, and the chamber's flowing walls dimmed to a deep, patient green, and the silence that followed was not empty. It was full. For the first time in five years, the silence inside Paloma was full.

He stayed kneeling a long time. Juniper leaned against him and said nothing, because she understood, finally, the difference between comforting and accompanying. Marisol kept watch at the door and pretended her eyes weren't wet, and was fooling no one.

When Paloma finally rose, he felt changed in a way he couldn't yet name. Lighter and heavier at once. The grief was still there—of course it was, grief doesn't leave, it just changes rooms—but it had a shape now, a direction. She hadn't abandoned him. She had armed him. Five years of loneliness, and it had been a garrison the whole time.

"We go back up," he said. His voice was steady. Steady, steady, steady. Hers. "One turn left. One gate."

"And the Owl's chair?" Marisol asked quietly.

Paloma looked at Juniper, who looked back with her mismatched eyes and waited, brave and doomed and willing, for whatever he said next.

"I told you I'd find another way," he said. "I found it. Or I'll die finding it. Nobody in this family gets eaten by anything ever again."

Juniper's ears went back. "Cub—"

"No." He rolled up the map, and the leather was warm in his hands, and somewhere far above them the river went on holding itself up out of sheer obedience to a promise that was about to be rewritten. "We're not trading you. We're not trading anyone. That's the old covenant's whole mistake—it ran on sacrifice. Mine runs on something else."

"What?" Marisol asked.

Paloma smiled, crooked on the left side, and pushed open the door.

"Belonging," he said. "Come on. We have a thirteenth moon to catch—and I think Brother Ossian is about to have a very bad night."
