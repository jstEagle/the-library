## 13. The City Remembers

The salt market smelled of brine and burnt sugar on the morning everything changed.

Paloma had come for practical reasons—Juniper needed dried fish that didn't come from the aqueduct rats, and Marisol had asked him to pick up a vial of lamp-oil for her charts—but the truth was simpler and uglier: he wanted to walk through a crowd and feel like a man again instead of a fugitive. Three days in the tunnels beneath the city had taught him that stone has a smell of its own, a mineral patience that seeps into your clothes and your hair and eventually, he feared, into your thinking. He wanted noise. He wanted vendors shouting prices and children underfoot and the ordinary commerce of people who did not know his face.

He got exactly what he wanted, which should have warned him.

The market filled the long basin between the salt warehouses and the old customs house, stalls striped in faded canvas, awnings throwing pools of shade against the white glare of late morning. Salt came here from the flats beyond the walls in wicker hampers big as coffins, and it left in paper twists and clay jars, in cured fish and pickled eggs and every preserved thing a city needs to keep its dead seasons at bay. Paloma moved through it with his hood down for the first time in days, letting the crowd's noise wash over him like water over stones.

He was bargaining for the lamp-oil—a woman with flour on her forearms wanted three coppers too many, and he was enjoying the ritual of refusing her—when the bells began.

Not the market bells. Not the hour bells. The deep ones, the ones in the Office tower that Ceniza's citizens heard so rarely that most of them had forgotten what the sound meant. The crowd didn't forget all at once; it forgot in pieces, the way a body remembers pain. A fishmonger went still with a knife half-raised over a mackerel. The flour-armed woman stopped mid-sentence, her mouth open around a price she would never finish naming. Somewhere near the customs house a child began to cry before anyone knew why, the way children always know first.

Then the word moved through the market the way fire moves through dry grass, too fast to see, leaving blackness behind.

*They caught one.*

*The Memory Office—they've caught a thief.*

*They're going to burn him. Here. Today.*

Paloma's hand found the oil vial without his permission and closed around it hard enough that he felt the glass creak. Every instinct he had honed across nine years of careful invisibility screamed the same instruction: *walk away, slowly, don't run, don't look.* The crowd was already turning toward the customs house square like a field of flowers following a sun they couldn't see, and the smart thing—the only thing—was to let himself be carried along with everyone else, one more curious face among hundreds, and slip out the far side before the spectacle began.

He told himself that was what he was doing as his feet carried him forward.

* * *

The Office had built its stage overnight, or so it seemed. In truth, Paloma understood later, they must have worked since dawn: the scaffold of black timber rising at the center of the customs house square, the pyre stacked beneath it in a lattice of split pine, the ring of gray-robed priests standing shoulder to shoulder with the city watch. Someone had hung banners from the customs house balconies—the eye-and-key sigil of the Memory Office, stitched enormous, watching the square from four directions at once.

And someone had brought kindling for the crowd, too, though this kindling was made of words.

"They steal your mothers' faces," a man was saying near the fountain, loud enough for thirty people, pitched like a man who had rehearsed. "You give them coin and they put a stranger's grief in your pocket. Your wife's love, sold secondhand. Your own childhood, lifted out of you while you slept. Is that a city? Is that neighbors?"

"It's not true," someone said, weakly. "I bought a charm once. It was just—it was nice, is all. My grandmother's kitchen. I never had—"

"Then you paid a thief to rob a grave," the man said, and the crowd around him made a sound Paloma had never heard a crowd make before—not a gasp, not a murmur, but a low collective exhalation, the sound of two hundred people deciding something at once.

Paloma stood at the edge of it with the oil vial cutting a line into his palm and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the man was not wrong.

That was the worst part. Not the lies—the lies were there, gilded and grotesque, stories of children drained hollow by charm-sellers, of merchants who'd traded away their own names—but threaded through them ran truths Paloma knew from the inside. He *had* taken memories without leave. He had lifted a widow's first snowfall because her grief made it loose at the roots, and sold it to a merchant who wanted to feel something, anything, and had told himself both parties were better off, and had believed it, mostly, on the good days. He had built an entire life inside the gap between what the law said magic was and what he knew it could be, and he had called the gap *survival*, and he had never once asked the widow whether she missed her snowfall after it was gone.

The priests brought the prisoner out at noon.

He was young—younger than Paloma, barely more than a boy—with ink-stained fingers and the raw, scraped look of a man who had been questioned for a long time in a small room. His name, when the herald read it off a scroll, was Teo Almásy, and he had been selling charms from a stall three rows from where Paloma stood, charms Paloma realized with a cold turn of his stomach he had probably walked past a hundred times. Competition, he'd have thought, if he'd thought of him at all. He hadn't thought of him at all. That was what invisibility cost, he understood now, watching the guards march the boy up the scaffold steps: it cost you the ability to see the other invisible people.

Brother Ossian stood at the front of the priestly rank, and Paloma searched his face for—what? Mercy? Recognition? Some sign that the man who had offered him erasure in a dark tunnel also burned boys in public squares? Ossian's expression gave nothing back. It was the face of a man performing a duty he had measured to the last grain and found exact. Only his hands, folded into his sleeves, were held very carefully still, the way a man holds his hands when he does not entirely trust them.

The herald read the charges. Unlicensed memory-craft. Theft of personal history. Trafficking in stolen pasts. Each charge landed on the square like a stone dropped down a well, and each time the crowd answered with a sound that was almost approval but not quite—approval still deciding what it wanted to be.

Teo Almásy did not scream or weep. He looked out over the crowd with the dazed, squinting expression of a man led into sunlight after a long darkness, and his eyes crossed the faces one by one, and Paloma saw the moment they found his.

It lasted less than a heartbeat. The boy's gaze snagged on Paloma's hooded figure at the crowd's edge, and something passed between them—not recognition, Paloma was sure of it, nothing so dangerous, but *kinship*, the wordless knowledge that passes between two creatures of the same species meeting in a hostile country. *I know what you are,* the look said. *I know why you're standing there and not running.*

Then they bound him to the post, and the look was gone, and there was only the boy again, and the pine, and the torch.

* * *

Paloma had seen fires before. Ceniza burned its refuse in the evenings; the tannery district kept a perpetual smoke; once, years ago, he had watched a warehouse fire from a rooftop with the rest of the city, everyone oohing at the orange bloom of it like it was a festival.

None of it was this.

The pine caught fast, the way dry wood does, and for a few seconds the fire was almost beautiful, climbing the lattice in bright ribbons, and then it reached the boy and stopped being anything a person could call beautiful ever again. The sound was the worst of it. Paloma had prepared himself—he'd thought he'd prepared himself—for screaming, and there was screaming, but underneath the screaming was another sound, wetter and smaller, that no amount of preparation could make bearable, and the crowd heard it too, and the crowd did not look away.

That was the thing he would carry out of the square, the thing that would wake him for nights afterward: they did not look away. Two hundred citizens of Ceniza stood in the noon light and watched a nineteen-year-old burn for the crime of dealing in other people's yesterdays, and they watched with their chins lifted and their eyes open, and when the boy's mother—Paloma knew it was his mother by the way she fought the watchmen, by the animal arithmetic of her struggle, two men barely enough to hold her—when she broke free and ran three steps toward the pyre with her arms out, the crowd did not part for her. They absorbed her. They swallowed her grief the way the market swallowed everything, efficiently, without comment, and the watchmen dragged her back behind the priestly line, and the burning went on.

Paloma's legs had gone numb. He noticed distantly that he was gripping the oil vial so hard the glass had cracked and a thin thread of oil was running down his wrist, smelling of the ordinary world, of lamps and kitchens and nights when the worst thing in the city was the price of light.

*I served these people,* he thought. *I gave them their dead mothers' voices. I gave the lonely merchant his snowfall. I made this city kinder by theft, and now it stands in the sun and screams for blood, and I am standing in it, and my feet will not move.*

A woman beside him was crying—weeping, actually, great silent sobs—and for one absurd moment he thought the horror had reached her too, that some part of the crowd had woken. Then he heard her whisper to her neighbor, "Good. Good, it's about time," while the tears ran down her face, and he understood that both things were true in her at once, the grief and the approval, and that no city is ever one thing, and that he had spent nine years selling comfort to people who would burn him for the method.

The fire burned for a long time. Fires do. Long after the screaming stopped, long after the shape on the post stopped being a shape, the crowd stayed, fed on something, unable to leave, and the priests stood in their gray ranks and let the city look, because looking was the point. Brother Ossian turned once, slowly, his gaze traveling the crowd's edge the way a lighthouse turns—and Paloma stepped back into the shadow of the customs house arches, heart slamming, certain for one vertiginous instant that the priest had been looking for him specifically.

But Ossian's eyes moved on. And Paloma stood in the dark of the archway and watched the rest of it through the stone columns, a man watching his own probable future through bars he couldn't see.

* * *

He found Juniper at the tunnel mouth near the old sluice, sitting on a broken millstone with a dried fish in her paws, and she read his face before he said a word.

"Cub." She set the fish down. "What happened?"

"They burned him." His voice came out flat, stripped. "A charm-seller. A boy. They built a stage and rang the bells and the whole city came to watch, and Juniper—" His breath hitched, and he hated it, hated the crack in it. "They cheered. Not all of them. Enough. Enough that it doesn't matter what the rest think."

Juniper was quiet for a moment. Foxes are not built for consolation; her silences had edges. "The Office has done this before," she said finally. "In the years after the sealing. Fear is how they keep the covenant's corpse warm."

"This wasn't fear." He sat down on the millstone beside her, and the cracked oil vial rolled from his pocket and lay between them, glinting. "This was appetite. You should have seen them, Juniper. They *wanted* it. Half those people had bought charms—I know they had, I sold to half that market myself—and they stood there and watched a boy burn for giving them exactly what they asked for, and they called it justice because calling it justice meant they didn't have to call it what it was."

"And what was it?"

He stared at the dark mouth of the tunnel. "Complicity," he said. "Mine too. I stood at the edge and watched and my feet wouldn't move, and I told myself it was prudence, it was tradecraft, one thief can't fight two hundred—but that's the same lie I've been telling for nine years. Invisibility isn't survival. It's just complicity with better posture."

Juniper's mismatched eyes—one amber, one the green of deep water—studied him with an expression he was learning to read and still mistrusted: the look of a creature much older than her body weighing how much truth a wounded thing can carry. "Your mother used to say something like that," she said quietly. "*Hiding is a debt too, cub. It collects.*"

"I'm not my mother."

"No," Juniper agreed. "She hid faster."

The laugh that tore out of him was ragged and unexpected and hurt on the way up, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes until the burning square behind them faded to orange ghosts. When he lowered his hand, Juniper had pushed the dried fish toward him, which was, from a fox, roughly equivalent to an embrace.

"Eat," she said. "Grief keeps better on a full stomach. And then we move, because the boy they burned today bought his charms from the same fence who bought yours, cub, and the Office does not stop at one example. They tipped their hand with the buyer's information. Which means somewhere in that gray-robed rank today was a man who knows a wolf-blooded heir is carrying a map of thirteen gates—and knows the Office wants the seal to fail."

Paloma looked at her. "Ossian."

"Or whoever holds Ossian's leash." She rose, shook herself, tail flicking. "Either way, the city has remembered what it is tonight, and what it is, is afraid. Afraid cities do not wait politely for the thirteenth moon."

Far above the sluice, muffled by stone and distance, the Office bells began to ring again—and this time they did not stop.
