## 14. Ossian's Choice

The cell smelled of lye and old stone, and Brother Ossian stood in the doorway of it for a long time before he could make his feet move.

They had given him the order at dawn, written in the Provost's own hand on vellum so fine it seemed ashamed of its purpose. *The Stag's heir, taken at the salt market. Sentence confirmed by the Office of Memory. Execution to be carried out by the arresting officer, per doctrine.* Per doctrine. As if there were a page somewhere in the codex that described how a man was supposed to hold a knife to a girl who charted skies for coppers and had never, until three weeks ago, done anything wronger than undercharge a customer.

Ossian had read the order four times. Each time, the ink looked fresher.

He carried the lantern low, and the light moved ahead of him across the flagstones like something reluctant. The holding cells beneath the Office were cut from the same gray basalt as everything else in Ceniza—stone pulled out of the mountain when the city was young, stone that remembered being part of something larger. He had always found a bitter comfort in that. Even the walls here had been severed from their origin. Even the walls lived with the amputation.

She sat on the cot with her back against the wall, wrists bound in memory-wax bands that glowed faintly amber where they touched her skin. They kept prisoners docile by sealing their recent hours behind wax—the last day of a life, wrapped up and shelved like any other charm in the evidence vaults. Ossian had invented half the procedure himself, eleven years ago, in a younger man's zeal. It worked beautifully. That was the trouble with everything he had ever built: it worked.

Marisol lifted her head when the light reached her.

She did not look afraid. That was the first thing that undid him. He had executed two unlicensed practitioners in his career—one a poisoner, one a man who had burned a neighbor's memories out of sheer spite—and both of them had wept or raged or begged. This woman simply looked at him with dark, level eyes, the way you'd look at weather.

"You're the one from the tannery district," she said. "The ash gate. You nearly caught the fox-boy."

"You remember that?"

"Someone in this city does." A ghost of a smile. "Though I suppose your Office would prefer I didn't."

Ossian set the lantern on the floor between them, an odd courtesy he didn't examine too closely, and took the key ring from his belt without taking the knife from its sheath. One thing at a time. He was a man of procedure; procedure was all he had left.

"The sentence is carried out at first bell," he said. "You'll be brought up to the plaza. There will be a reading of charges. The crowd will be told what you are."

"What am I, Brother Ossian? In the Office's words. I've been curious."

"*Unregistered bearer of guardian blood. Agent of the failing seal. Accessory to theft of Office property.*" He recited it flatly, and heard how it sounded—the language of ledgers applied to a living woman. "In my words, you're a sky-chart seller who never asked for any of this."

"No," she agreed. "I didn't."

* * *

His sister's name had been Aurelia, and she had died the way the Office said magic killed people: suddenly, messily, with her mind full of someone else's past.

Ossian had been nineteen. She had been seventeen, and she had been beautiful in the way that frightened their mother—not pretty, but *lit*, as if something behind her face were glowing through. She used to sit on the roof of their tenement in the dye district and hold her hands up to catch moths, and the moths came to her instead of the lantern, and she laughed like this was the most ordinary thing in the world.

It started small, the way it always starts. She lifted a memory from a fruit-seller—a taste of summer—and gave it to their dying grandmother so the old woman could leave this life remembering warmth. Ossian had known. He had known and said nothing, because the grandmother had wept with gratitude, and because Aurelia's hands had glowed, and because he loved her more than he feared the Office.

By the time he understood what she was really doing—trading charms to pay their mother's debts, going deeper each time, lifting whole years from strangers who woke poorer and never knew why—it was too late for warnings. The Office found her in a cellar off Rope Street with forty stolen memories sealed in glass and a hole in herself so wide that whatever remained of Aurelia could not answer to her name. They called it *dissolution*. The doctrine had a whole chapter on it. Magic is debt, the chapter said, and debts are collected, and the collector is always eventually you.

He had watched them carry her out on a plank. Her eyes had been open. He had never been able to decide, in the years since, whether there had been anything behind them.

So he had joined the Office. Everyone assumed grief. Grief was permitted; it was even useful, in a priest. But the truth was smaller and worse: he had joined because if the rules were real, then Aurelia's death meant something. If the rules were real, then the world was a ledger after all, and every entry balanced, and his sister had not simply been devoured by an appetite no one had taught her to govern. He needed the arithmetic to hold. He had spent twenty-two years defending an equation because the alternative was admitting his sister had died for nothing.

And now here was this woman in the cell, with guardian blood in her veins and her hands bound in his own invention, looking at him like weather.

"Why did you do it?" she asked, into the silence. "Join them. You don't have the look. The true believers have a look—they enjoy the burning. You look like a man carrying furniture up stairs forever."

Ossian almost laughed. It came out as a breath.

"My sister," he said, and stopped. Twenty-two years, and he had never once said it aloud inside these walls. "My sister practiced unlicensed craft. Small things, at first. Kind things, even. And then the craft began practicing her." He looked at the lantern rather than at Marisol. "They found her hollowed out. Whatever uses wild magic uses the practitioner as fuel, and there is never enough of anyone. That is what the covenant's collapse means, out here among people who aren't guardians. It means everyone becomes fuel."

"That's what they teach you?"

"It's what I *saw.*"

"Yes," Marisol said quietly. "I believe you saw it. But I don't think you saw *why*." She shifted forward on the cot, and the wax bands at her wrists pulsed. "Brother Ossian. I spent thirty-one years not knowing what I was. Do you know how you live that long with guardian blood and never know? You get sick near certain places and call it bad air. You dream of forests growing sideways and blame the wine. You feel a hunger you can't name and you feed it with work, work, work, until the work is your whole self and there's nothing left over to question." Her voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about her; nothing about her rose. "Your sister wasn't devoured because she was weak. She was devoured because no one told her what she was feeding, or how to feed it safely, or that she could stop. Your Office doesn't prevent dissolution. It manufactures it. Every magician driven underground, every child taught to hate the hunger instead of understand it—that's a candle burning in a closed box."

"The doctrine says—"

"I know what the doctrine says. I've heard it through a cell door for six days." She leaned back. "But you didn't come down here at midnight to recite doctrine at me. You came down here because you already know the doctrine has a hole in it, and you've known for a while, and you wanted to see if I'd say it out loud so you wouldn't have to."

Ossian stood very still.

Because the truth—the thing he had carried for months now, folded small beneath his breastbone like a stolen charm—was that he had seen the hole. He had seen it in the Provost's chambers, three weeks ago, when the plan had finally been spoken plainly enough that even he could not dress it in doctrine.

* * *

He had gone expecting a briefing on containment. The seal at the twelfth gate had torn; the Hollowing was leaking; the Office's position, as he understood it, was reinforcement and repair. He had served the repair doctrine his whole career. Mend the wall. Keep the worlds apart. Magic is debt.

Provost Calloway had let him stand a moment, savoring the map spread across the table—the thirteen gates circled in red thread, twelve of them marked with the Office's own sigils of suppression, the thirteenth ringed in gold.

"Do you know why the founders built our Order on top of the gates, Ossian?" Calloway had asked. He was a small man, neat as a pin, with the gentle voice of a schoolmaster. "Not to guard them. Anyone can guard a wall. We were founded to *wait*. The covenant is dying—you've read the same decay tables I have. Twelve turns remain, perhaps fewer. And when it fails—" he had touched the gold ring gently, the way a man touches a wound he loves— "the wild realm will pour through every gate at once. Not a trickle. A flood. Enough raw magic to drown the mortal world."

"And the doctrine of containment—"

"Was scaffolding," Calloway said pleasantly. "Necessary scaffolding, for as long as the flood was coming anyway. We cannot stop the seal from failing. No one can. The Wolf's heir is a gutter-thief; the Owl's line is extinct; the Stag's heir didn't know her own name until a month ago. The old covenant will not be renewed. So the only question—the *only* question, brother—is who stands in the floodplain when the waters come, holding buckets, or who stands upstream with a channel dug." He had smiled. "We dig channels. Every suppression sigil we've laid on those gates for three hundred years wasn't a dam. It was a *reservoir wall*. When the seal breaks, the magic won't scatter into chaos. It will pool exactly where we have prepared it to pool. And the Order that holds the reservoir holds the world."

Ossian had heard himself say, "Harvest," in a voice that did not sound like his.

"Cultivate," Calloway corrected, mild as milk. "Words matter, brother. We are not scavengers. We are stewards of a coming abundance. Think of it: memory-craft made lawful, universal, *administered*. No more gutter thieves selling secondhand summers. Grief made optional. Pain made elective. A managed soul for every citizen of Ceniza." He had rolled up the map with soft, reverent hands. "The Hollowing is a problem of appetite, yes. But appetite can be fed deliberately, from stock, rather than letting it graze freely. We will feed it precisely what we choose, and it will be ours, and it will be *orderly*."

"And the heirs?"

"The Wolf boy dies resisting arrest, ideally. The Stag woman is sentenced tomorrow—her execution will demonstrate the Office's resolve and draw the remaining conspirators into the open. Sentiment is a resource too, Ossian. Martyrs are excellent bait." Calloway had glanced up then, and his eyes had been kind, and that had been the worst part. "Unless you have an objection?"

And Ossian—who had objected to nothing in twenty-two years, who had built the wax-bands and refined the interrogation rites and written two chapters of the very doctrine—had heard himself say, "No, Provost. No objection."

He had walked back to his cell in the priests' quarters and sat upright on the narrow bed until dawn, doing the arithmetic he had spent his whole life defending. *Magic is debt. Debts are collected.* Very well. Then what did the Office owe, for what it intended to take? What was the entry in the ledger for a world harvested like a field? Who collected *that* debt?

There was no column for it. There had never been a column for it. The equation did not balance. It had never balanced. He had defended a lie for twenty-two years because the lie was warmer than the truth, and the truth was that Aurelia had died for nothing, and the moths had come to her hands because she was *good*, and goodness had never once been mentioned in the codex.

* * *

Now he stood in her cell with the key ring in his hand, and Marisol watched him the way you watch a long-exposed plate develop.

"The Provost intends to let the seal fail," Ossian said. His voice sounded strange to him—unlocked, like a room opened after years. "He intends to harvest the flood. To turn the wild realm's magic into a weapon and administer it. He sent me down here to kill you before you could be rescued, because a martyr draws the wolf-boy out of hiding." He turned the key ring over in his fingers. "I'm not going to do it."

"No," Marisol said. "I didn't think you were. You've been standing there for a hundred counts deciding, and a man deciding to kill doesn't take that long."

He crossed to her and knelt, and broke the wax bands at her wrists. They cracked like sugar. Where they had touched her skin, the glow lingered a moment and faded, and he thought: *that's what we do. We mark people and call the marking safety.*

"The cells below the east stair open onto the ash cistern," he said. "The night watch changes at second bell; the cistern grate is rusted; I will have loosened it. Go up through the tannery district and find the river gate. Paloma will come for you—he came for the map, he'll come for you. Tell him—" and here Ossian paused, because the next part was the price, the whole price, everything he believed paid out at once— "tell him the Office isn't trying to stop the seal from falling. Tell him the suppression sigils on the gates are reservoir walls. Tell him the thirteenth gate is the spillway, and Calloway means to stand at the bottom of it with his hand out."

Marisol rubbed her freed wrists, studying him. "And you? What happens to you when they find the cell empty?"

"They'll find me here," Ossian said. "Sedated, allegedly overpowered. It's even plausible—I built the wax-bands; I know exactly how much sedative makes a story convincing." He allowed himself the smallest dry smile. "And if it isn't plausible, then I'll have lost everything I believed in, which I did some hours ago anyway. The rest is furniture."

She stood. For a moment she looked at him with those level dark eyes, and he braced for gratitude, which he could not have borne.

Instead she said: "My grandmother used to say the sky forgives you the night you stop pretending you don't need it." She touched his shoulder, briefly, a weight like a bird landing. "Your sister's moths knew what they were doing, Brother Ossian."

Then she was gone down the corridor, quiet as charted starlight, and Ossian sat down on the vacated cot to arrange his own alibi, and his hands, for the first time in twenty-two years, were steady.

* * *

They found the cell empty at fourth bell, exactly as planned, and they found Ossian exactly where he had arranged to be found—slumped against the wall, the wax-band residue still on his sleeves, a credible bruise blooming along his jaw where he had struck himself against the cot frame. The story held for six hours. Long enough.

What he had not planned—what no procedure covered—was the moment at midday when they brought him before Provost Calloway, and Calloway looked at him for a long, gentle moment, and smiled the schoolmaster's smile, and said:

"Brother Ossian. How fortunate. I had hoped you'd volunteer for the retrieval detail." He set a folded paper on the desk between them, and Ossian recognized the Provost's neat hand, and the date at the top was yesterday's. "You see, we've known about your sister for some time. Aurelia, wasn't it? Rope Street cellar. Forty charms." Calloway tilted his head. "Grief makes such useful leverage, brother. Why do you think I gave you the execution order at all? A man either kills the mirror of his sin—or he frees it. Either way, I learn exactly what he is." He slid the paper across. "Fetch me the wolf-boy and the fox, and bring me the map, and your sister's file burns unread, and you retire to the archives with a pension. Refuse, and I publish her, posthumously, as the monster the doctrine always said she was. Choose."

Ossian looked at the order. Retrieval. The wolf-boy. The fox. The map.

He thought of Aurelia on the rooftop with her hands full of moths. He thought of a ledger with no column for mercy. He thought: *they will use her forever unless someone carries her out of their files, and I am the only one left who remembers her hands.*

"Provost," he said, rising, and his voice was calm, and his heart was a lit thing, "I'll need my travel kit. The wolf-boy is at the river gate—but you should know the seal there is already torn, and the Hollowing is wearing dead men's faces on the far side of it." He met Calloway's kind eyes and lied to them beautifully, with twenty-two years of practice. "If we go tonight, we take it while it's feeding."

Calloway's smile widened. "Excellent. You always were my best student."

That night, Ossian walked out of the Office of Memory carrying a lamp, a knife he did not intend to use on the people he was going to find, and the whole of everything he had believed, spent at last like a coin that had finally reached the merchant it was minted for.

Ahead of him, the moon was rising fat and white over the thirteen hills, one turn short of full, and somewhere beyond the river gate, a thief with a wolf's blood was running out of time.
