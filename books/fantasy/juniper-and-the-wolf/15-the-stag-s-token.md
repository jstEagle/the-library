## 15. The Stag's Token

The highlands did not want them there.

Marisol felt it in the way the wind moved—wrong, deliberate, pushing against their backs as they climbed, as if the whole gray expanse of moor and stone were a hand pressing them back toward Ceniza, toward the low safe streets where a person could pretend the world was only what it appeared to be. The grass grew in silvered tussocks that hissed when the wind combed through them, and the sky above was the color of old pewter, heavy with a rain that refused to fall.

She had charted skies like this once. Sold them, rather—little painted discs of tin and glass, each one promising a customer that the heavens above Ceniza were knowable, orderly, purchasable. She had been good at it. She had been *very* good at it, because she had spent thirty-one years perfecting the art of looking at something impossible and describing it in terms small enough to hold.

"You're doing the thing," Juniper said from somewhere near Marisol's left boot.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes flat and your eyes go far away and you pretend you're not terrified." The fox trotted ahead through the tussocks, her mismatched eyes—one green-gold, one the deep violet of dusk—catching what little light the sky offered. "Your mother did it too, I'd wager. All of you Stags. You look at the cliff and start measuring it."

"My mother sold fish," Marisol said automatically. It was the lie she had told for three decades, worn smooth as river stone now, and it came out of her mouth with the ease of long habit and none of the comfort.

Juniper didn't dignify it with an answer. She just looked back over her shoulder, and her violet eye held Marisol's until Marisol looked away first.

Paloma said nothing at all. He hadn't said much since the river gate—since the echo of his mother in the covenant's stone, since he'd come out of that cold underground dark with his eyes red-rimmed and his spine straighter than she'd ever seen it. He walked two paces behind her, and she could feel him watching her the way she imagined a cartographer watched unmapped coastline: carefully, respectfully, aware that the ground might shift.

"Tell me again what the map says," she said, because saying things aloud made them smaller.

"Gate twelve is here." Paloma unfolded the leather map against his forearm, sheltering it from the wind. His finger traced the circle of thirteen sigils inked around Ceniza's walls. "But the Stag's token isn't kept at a gate. It's kept at the shrine—the old one, before the city, before the covenant even. The map marks it here." His finger stopped on a blank space north of the ring, where the parchment showed nothing but faint water-staining shaped almost like antlers. "It doesn't say how to open it."

"It doesn't say," Marisol repeated, "because it opens for Stag blood. Which is my blood. Which I have spent my entire life pretending I don't have."

"Yes," Paloma said simply. He was very good at simple truths; she envied him that, or pitied him, she wasn't sure which.

The wind gusted hard enough to stagger her, and somewhere below and behind them, so far away it was more memory than sound, Ceniza's bells began to ring. Not the hour. Not the market call. The other ringing—the one the Office used when it wanted the city afraid. She thought of the salt market, of the thief on the pyre, of the crowd screaming for blood that she had stood in the middle of not four days ago, holding her painted skies against her chest like a shield that had never once worked.

They climbed.

* * *

The shrine sat in a bowl of stone at the top of the moor, and it should not have been there.

That was Marisol's first coherent thought: *this does not belong.* Everything else in the highlands was horizontal—wind-flattened grass, slabs of granite laid down by glaciers, a horizon so wide it made her feel like an insect. But the shrine rose vertical, a single spire of pale stone no wider than a door, taller than any tree she'd ever seen, its surface carved so densely with figures that from a distance it looked like bark, like bone, like a column of frozen people all reaching upward. And around its base, impossibly, the grass grew green. Lush. Sheltered. A circle of spring in the middle of all that iron-gray autumn, as if the stone remembered warmth and refused to stop.

Nobody spoke. Even the wind dropped, curling around the bowl of the hill and going quiet, and Marisol understood suddenly that the wind hadn't been fighting them on the climb. It had been testing them. Weighing them. Deciding.

"There are carvings," Paloma said quietly, moving along the base of the spire. "Stags. Look—stags everywhere, but also—" He paused. "People. People with antlers."

"The guardians," Marisol said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, flattened, distant. "Wolf, Stag, Owl. The ones who made the seal." The ones whose broken oaths had doomed everyone. The ones whose blood ran, diluted and denied, in her veins.

She stepped into the circle of green grass, and the temperature changed. Warmth rose up through her boots, gentle as a hearthstone, and the carved figures on the spire seemed to shift—not move, nothing so crude as movement, but her eyes kept finding new details every time she blinked, the way a starfield resolves the longer you stare at it.

At the base of the spire, waist-high, there was a hollow. A basin of smooth stone shaped like a cupped hand, dry as dust.

"I don't suppose," she said, "there's a key."

"You're the key," Paloma said. "Blood to blood. That's how the tokens work. My blood opened the ash gate." He rolled up his sleeve without being asked, offering, and she saw the thin white scar there where he'd cut himself weeks ago, and saw too the way he didn't flinch from it anymore. Somewhere between the ash gate and the river gate, this boy who had hidden his own skin from himself for twenty years had stopped being afraid of it. She wasn't there yet. She might never be.

Juniper sat down on a flat stone at the circle's edge, tucked her tail over her paws, and said, "I'll watch the wind."

"The wind isn't—"

"Cub." The fox's mismatched eyes were grave. "When the token wakes, everything wakes. Just—be quick, and be certain. Shrines like this don't ask twice."

Marisol looked at the basin. At the knife at her belt—the little paring knife she'd used for fifteen years to trim the edges of tin sky-discs, the most honest tool she owned. At Paloma, who nodded once, slow, giving her the choice the way no one had ever given her a choice about anything.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them shaking with a strange clinical detachment, the way she'd once noticed her own hands shaking the night her mother died, when she'd sat alone in the shop surrounded by a hundred painted heavens and realized she believed in none of them.

She drew the blade across her palm.

The pain was small and bright and ordinary, and then it was not ordinary at all, because the moment her blood touched the basin the whole shrine *inhaled.*

There was no other word for it. The stone spire drew breath—a vast, grinding, geological intake—and the blood in the cupped hand sank downward as if swallowed, and light came up out of the stone instead of down from the sky. It was amber-colored, honey-thick, and it moved through the carved figures like dawn moving across a valley, illuminating stag after stag after antlered human figure, and Marisol felt the light touch her blood and *recognize* it, felt something older than cities say her name—

—and the vision took her.

* * *

She died on a wall.

That was the first one. She was standing on the ramparts of Ceniza—or a Ceniza, one cracked and burning, its thirteen gates torn wide—and she was holding a line of light with both hands, a seam of gold stretched taut between her palms like a bowstring, and behind her the city screamed and ahead of her the dark came on like a tide, and she was saying *no, no, not yet, not yet* through gritted teeth, and then the string snapped and the dark came through her and she was gone. No pain at the end. Just absence, total and clean, like a candle pinched out.

Then she died in the aqueduct tunnels, drowned in black water while she held a gate shut with her shoulder.

Then she died in the salt market, burned, though in this version the fire was hers, called up out of her own blood to buy the crowd time to run.

Then she died old, in a bed, having held the line for sixty years, and somehow that one was worst of all, because she saw the cost of the whole long life of it—the friends buried, the name worn down to a title, the loneliness at the end so complete it made the quick deaths look like mercy.

Then she died young. Then she died in winter. Then she died holding Juniper's body in her arms, and then she died holding Paloma's, and then she died alone on this same moor with no one at all, and then—

They did not stop. They came like the tide comes, wave after wave after wave, every future in which Marisol Vega stood where the Stag must stand and paid what the Stag must pay, dozens of them, hundreds, and through all of them ran one unbroken thread: she always held. In every future, whatever it cost, whatever it broke, she did not let go. The line held because she held it. That was the gift of her blood and the curse of it, offered together, indivisible, take both or neither.

And underneath all of it, beneath the deaths like stones at the bottom of a river, one voice. Not words, exactly. Older than words. It said:

*This is what it is. This is all of what it is. Now choose.*

* * *

She came back to herself on her knees in the green grass, gasping, her cut palm pressed to the warm stone, tears freezing on her cheeks in the wind that had returned.

"—risol. *Marisol.*"

Paloma's hands were on her shoulders. His face was white. Behind him Juniper paced the circle's edge, hackles raised, staring out at the moor as if expecting the dark itself to come over the hills at any moment.

"How long?" Marisol managed.

"Four minutes." Paloma's voice cracked on it. "Marisol, you screamed. You screamed and then you went silent and the light wouldn't stop and I couldn't—I didn't know if I should break the circle—"

"If you'd broken the circle," Juniper said without turning, "it would have started over. Or worse." She looked back at last, and her violet eye was soft in a way Marisol had never seen it. "What did it show you?"

And Marisol, kneeling in the shrine's small stubborn springtime with her blood drying on ancient stone, found that she was laughing. It came out of her ragged and half-hysterical, but it was real laughter, the first in longer than she could remember, because the alternative to laughing was screaming and she had done quite enough of that in four minutes to last a lifetime.

"Everything," she said. "It showed me everything. Every way I die, Juniper. There are a lot of ways. I counted at least forty before I lost count." She pushed herself upright on unsteady legs. Paloma moved to steady her and she let him. "I die on walls. I die in water. I die old and alone, which, honestly, rude. In every single one, I'm holding the line. That's the deal. That's what the Stag's blood buys: you hold, and you hold, and eventually the holding kills you, and the shrine wanted me to see all of it before I said yes."

Paloma had gone very still. "You can still walk away," he said. "The token isn't claimed yet. Whatever it showed you—we'll find another way. There has to be another way."

"No," Marisol said. "There isn't, and you know there isn't, and more importantly—" She looked down at her cut palm. The wound had already closed, sealed by the shrine's warmth, leaving only a thin silvered line like a scar of light. "More importantly, I knew the cost before I walked up this hill. Not the details. But I knew. Everyone born with this blood knows, somewhere under the denial. My mother knew. She drank herself to death rather than think about it, and I sold toy skies rather than think about it, and the thinking was always going to catch us." She flexed her hand. It obeyed her. It would keep obeying her, right up until the day it didn't, in whichever of those futures the shrine had chosen to show her.

"That's not courage," Paloma said quietly. "Walking into something after seeing that. That's—"

"It's exactly courage." She met his eyes. "Courage isn't being brave when you don't know the price, Paloma. Anyone can gamble. Courage is standing at the counter with the full bill laid out in front of you, reading every line of it, and paying anyway. Because the thing on the other side of the bill is worth more than what it costs." She turned to the spire. Deep in the stone, at the height of her heart, a shape had emerged from the carving that hadn't been visible before: a great antlered crown of branching tines, and within it, cradled like a seed, a darkness the amber light flowed around but could not enter.

"The token," she said. "Give me your knife, cub. Mine's done its work."

Paloma hesitated—she watched him fight it, watched the boy who had grown up alone weigh the arithmetic of asking someone else to pay—and then he drew the blade and put it in her hand, handle first, and stepped back, and Marisol understood that this too was a kind of courage, his kind: letting people carry what they had chosen to carry.

She reached into the crown of stone.

The antler-token came free with a sound like a held breath released—warm, heavier than it looked, dense as heartwood, its tines fine as frost-fern. Light ran along every branch of it and sank into her palm, into her blood, and she felt it settle into her the way a keel settles into a hull: not comfortably, but rightly. Something in her that had been listing sideways for thirty-one years came level.

The shrine sighed. The green grass at its base began, slowly, to fade toward the silver of the surrounding moor, its work finished. High above, the pewter sky split along a single seam, and one bar of actual sunlight fell across the highlands like a finger pointing north.

Toward the twelfth gate.

Juniper's head came up sharply. Her nostrils flared, and every hair along her spine lifted.

"Cub," the fox said, and her voice was stripped of all its cleverness, down to something old and frightened. "The wind's changed. It's coming *from* the north now. From the gates." She looked at Marisol, at the token glowing faintly in her grip, at Paloma with the map already in his hands. "Something's torn. Something's torn *open*, and it's wearing shapes, and we need to move. Now."

Marisol closed her fist around the Stag's token, around every death she had agreed to, and started running.
