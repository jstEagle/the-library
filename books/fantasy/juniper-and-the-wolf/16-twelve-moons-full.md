## 16. Twelve Moons Full

The twelfth gate stood on a hill that had no business being a hill.

Ceniza was built on salt flats and river silt; the land around it lay flat as spilled milk for miles in every direction. But here the earth had gathered itself upward over centuries—Juniper said the covenant's weight had done it, twelve gates pulling at the world like stones in a hem—and now they climbed through scrub grass and wind-bent pines toward a summit crowned with standing stones so old their sigils had worn to suggestions.

Paloma felt the gate before he saw it.

He had learned the sensation across eleven gates: a warmth behind his sternum, his stolen memories stirring like embers when breath passes over them. The ash gate had made the widow's snowfall taste of cinders. The river gate had turned every memory damp and green. This one—

This one was cold.

Not the clean cold of altitude. A cold like a held breath, like a room where something has just stopped moving. The warmth behind his breastbone guttered and went out, and in its place came a silence so complete he could hear his own blood moving, slow and reluctant, as if even his heart distrusted where it was going.

"Stop." Juniper planted herself in front of him, four paws braced against the slope, her mismatched eyes—one amber, one the grey of old ice—fixed on the summit. Her fur stood along her spine in a ridge that no wind had caused. "Do you feel it?"

"I feel nothing," Paloma said. "That's what frightens me."

Behind them, Marisol caught up, breathing hard, the Stag's antler-token wrapped in wool and slung across her back. She had carried it for three days without once letting it touch bare ground. She looked past them both at the crest of the hill, and her face did something complicated—the expression of a woman who had spent her life charting skies and had just found a star where no star should be.

"The stones," she said quietly. "They're wrong."

Paloma saw it then. The standing stones of the twelfth gate formed a ring, as all thirteen did, but this ring was broken—not weathered, not toppled. *Torn.* Two of the great stones had been wrenched apart from the inside, their fracture faces smooth as river glass, and through the gap poured a light that was not moonlight. The full moon hung overhead, fat and white and one turn from its final rising, and its light fell on the hill like snow. But the light inside the ring was the color of deep water seen from beneath, green-black and moving, and it did not fall so much as *seep*, pooling between the stones like something spilled.

"It's open," Juniper said. Her voice had gone flat, the fox-equivalent of a human whispering a prayer. "It was supposed to be sealed until we unsealed it. It's open from the other side."

* * *

They approached the way you approach a sleeping animal: slowly, downwind, with apologies already prepared.

Up close, the torn stones were worse. The fracture surfaces were not merely smooth—they were *licked*, polished by something that had passed through and taken its time about it. And the air within the ring moved wrong. Wind crossed the hilltop in long steady gusts, but inside the circle the air simply hung, and things drifted there that were not quite mist and not quite smoke, curling upward from the pooled dark light like heat-shimmer in reverse.

"Eleven gates," Marisol murmured. "We opened eleven. Each one took something out of us and gave something back. What takes a gate *for itself?*"

"The Hollowing," Paloma said.

He had said the word before—had heard it from Juniper, read it in the margins of the map, spoken it aloud in the river gate's stone dark while his mother's echo showed him the night she gave herself away. But saying it here, standing before evidence of it, the word changed in his mouth. It stopped being a name for an abstraction. It became a description of the thing in front of him: a hollowing. A place where fullness had been eaten out.

"We don't have to go in," Marisol said. "We could go around. Find another way to the thirteenth gate."

"There is no other way." Juniper's tail lashed once, twice. "The gates are a wheel. You cannot reach the hub except through the spokes. And the thirteenth gate—" she looked up at the moon, and Paloma watched her decide how much truth to spend, "—the thirteenth gate opens onto the place where the first oath was sworn. The Hollowing knows that. That's why it opened this one early. It wants us to arrive already bleeding."

"Then we don't bleed," Paloma said, and stepped into the ring.

The cold took him like water.

* * *

Inside, the world was a memory of itself.

The scrub grass still grew, but grey, each blade distinct and motionless, as if the hilltop had been rendered in charcoal by an artist who loved detail but not life. The pines stood black and exact. Above, through some trick he refused to understand, the stars were visible despite the moon—thousands of them, arranged in patterns Marisol would have wept over, and every one of them faintly, horribly *wrong*, constellations shifted by a hand-width, as if the sky here had been memorized by someone who'd only heard it described.

And in the stillness, shapes.

At first he took them for more of the drifting smoke. Then the nearest shape resolved, and Paloma stopped walking so abruptly that Marisol walked into his back.

It was an old man seated on a stone that wasn't there, hands folded, head bowed, wearing the grey robes of a lamplighter. Paloma had never known a lamplighter. But Marisol made a sound beside him—a small, cracked sound, the sound a door makes when it hasn't been opened in years—and stepped forward.

"Papi?"

The figure lifted its head. It had her father's face. It had, Paloma realized with a lurch, exactly her father's face, down to the scar through the left eyebrow, the particular set of the jaw—but it was like a face remembered rather than a face seen, every detail correct and yet the whole of it somehow *flat*, a portrait pretending patience.

"Mari," it said warmly. "You came back to the hill. I told you the charts would bring you home."

"Don't." Marisol's voice shook. Her hand found the antler-token through its wool wrapping and gripped it like a rail in a storm. "Don't you dare. My father died hating me for the things I wouldn't believe. He never—" Her breath caught. "He never called my charts anything but waste."

The smile didn't falter. That was the worst part. Grief could have survived a frown. The smile simply continued, warm and patient and *offered*, the way a merchant offers goods, the way a charm offers a feeling.

"He changed his mind at the end," the shape said. "He wanted you to know. He was proud, Mari. He was always proud."

Marisol made the small sound again and turned away, pressing the heel of her hand against her eyes, and Paloma understood with sickening clarity that the shape had won something from her anyway—not belief, perhaps, but a wound freshly reopened, salt rubbed in a scar.

More shapes stood in the grey grass. A woman wringing her hands. A soldier still in his uniform. A child, which Paloma could not look at, which none of them could look at, and which nonetheless waited with the terrible patience of the dead.

"They're not real," Juniper said. She had come in last, hackles up, and she moved close against Paloma's leg—an intimacy she permitted herself only in extremity. "The Hollowing doesn't make things. It only *keeps* things. Everything anyone has ever fed it—every soul it swallowed before the covenant, every memory that leaked through the failing seal—it keeps. And it wears them."

"Wears them to do what?" Marisol asked, not turning around.

"To be welcome," Juniper said. "Nothing hunts you if you walk to it willingly."

* * *

Paloma told himself he was prepared.

He had met his mother's echo in the river gate. He had stood in the covenant-stone dark while she showed him the prison she had built with her own surrender, and he had come out of that grief with his spine straightened, resolve where despair had been. He knew what the Hollowing wore. He knew its tricks were borrowed.

So when he saw her standing among the pines at the ring's far edge, his first feeling was not hope.

It was anger.

She looked as she had in the echo—young, sharp-faced, her black hair escaping its braid in the way he remembered from the single year he could truly recall of her, the year before she left. Lira Villalobos, who had been the Wolf's heir before him, who had given her name to a prison so her son could have five years of not knowing. She stood in the grey grass with her arms open, and around her the dead shapes dimmed, as if even they deferred to her.

"No," Paloma said.

"Paloma." His name in her voice. He had spent eleven gates learning what his stolen memories felt like from the inside, and this voice matched his memories perfectly, which meant nothing, because the Hollowing kept memories too, and hers had leaked through the failing seal for years like light under a door. "My son. Look how tall. Look how *tired*."

"You're not her."

"I'm tired, mijo. Aren't you?" She tilted her head, and the gesture was so precisely his mother's—she had done exactly that, once, when he'd fallen and skinned his knee and tried not to cry—that his body betrayed him with a flood of recollection, snowfall-warm, safe. "I've been waiting in the dark for five years. Holding the door. Do you know what I hold it with? Do you know what it costs?" She spread her hands, and he saw—saw, unmistakably—the wear in her, the fraying, a woman holding shut a door with her shoulder for years beyond counting. "You can end it. That's all. You don't have to fight. You don't have to be brave. Just stop holding the line your whole life because a dead woman's oath told you to. Let it fall. Come find me on the other side of it, and we'll be done."

Every word was a key fitted to a lock inside him.

That was the horror of it—not that the words were lies, but that they weren't. Or rather: they were lies built entirely out of truths, the way a counterfeit coin is built out of real metal. He *was* tired. He *hadn't* chosen any of this. He *did* want, with a want that lived below thought, below shame, in the cellar of himself where he kept everything he couldn't afford to look at—he did want his mother back, whole, and he wanted it so badly that the wanting had a gravity, and the shape in the pines stood at the center of that gravity, smiling his mother's smile.

"Paloma." Marisol's voice, distant, alarmed. "Your eyes."

He blinked. The grey hilltop swam. And he understood, in the last sliver of himself still standing outside the moment, that he had stopped walking at some point and started *drifting*, drawn forward across the charcoal grass one step at a time, and that the pooled dark light was closer now, lapping at his boots, and that the shapes of the dead had turned to watch him go with expressions of gentle encouragement, a congregation witnessing a homecoming.

The false Lira opened her arms wider.

"Come here," she said. "Let me forgive you."

*Forgive.* The word went through him like a fishhook.

Because that was the bait under the bait. Not rest, not reunion—forgiveness. For the years he'd hated her. For the nights as a boy in Ceniza's alleys when he'd told strangers his mother was dead because it was easier than *gone*. For the charms he'd sold containing the few true memories of her he'd managed to keep, trading her laugh for bread, her voice for rent, until by fifteen he had sold all of her but one evening—the smell of rosemary, a song hummed off-key—and had sat in his rented room at sixteen deciding whether even that one should go, and had kept it, barely, and had hated himself ever since for how close it had come.

If she forgave him, he could put it all down. Every weight. That was what the shape offered, and that was what made it monstrous: absolution without cost, forgiveness without truth, comfort that required nothing of him except that he stop being himself.

His boot touched the edge of the pool.

The cold climbed him like water finding its level—ankles, knees, waist—and suddenly he was *in* a memory, drowning in one, and it was beautiful.

Rosemary. A kitchen with a crooked shutter. Rain on the roof, and his mother humming, and six-year-old Paloma sitting on the table because chairs were for guests, and she was telling him—she was finally telling him everything, the wolf-blood, the gates, the oath, all of it, and she was saying *I'm sorry I hid it, I'm sorry I left, you were never abandoned, you were loved, you were loved every single day*—and the memory held him the way floodwater holds a swimmer, total and warm, and somewhere very far above the surface a part of him screamed that this was not a memory, that his mother had never told him these things, that this kitchen was a forgery painted from the pigment of his own longing, that the Hollowing had reached into him and found the shape of everything he wanted and filled it in—

*It feels true,* whispered the drowning part of him. *Doesn't that make it true?*

*No,* said the sliver. *A well-made lie is truer than a clumsy truth. That's what makes it a trap.*

He clawed for the sliver. It was small. It was the part of him that had survived Ceniza—the part that checked a buyer's sleeves for knives, that counted coins twice, that had learned at eight years old that the sweetest voice in a market is usually attached to the sharpest hook. The thief's part. The suspicious, unlovely, indispensable part.

*What does she want?* he asked it, gasping under the rosemary rain. *What does the thing wearing her actually want?*

And the answer surfaced with the clarity of cold water:

*It wants me willing.*

The false memory shuddered.

It was slight—a tremor at the edges, like a reflection when the pond is disturbed—and Paloma seized it. If the Hollowing needed his willingness, then his suspicion was a weapon, and every doubt he fed it was a knife. *This kitchen never existed. My mother never got to say goodbye. That's the truth, and the truth hurts, and the hurt is mine, and I will not trade it for comfort.*

He bit down on the memory as if it were a fruit gone bad and spat it out.

* * *

He came up out of the pool on his hands and knees on real grass—real, brown, wind-bent, alive—with Marisol's grip on his collar and Juniper's teeth carefully closed around his wrist, not biting, anchoring, her mismatched eyes wide with a fear he had never seen in her.

The pool of dark light lay flat and patient where he'd left it. The false Lira still stood among the pines.

But her smile had changed.

The warmth had drained out of it the way color drains from a face, leaving something older underneath, something that looked at him with the flat appraisal of a fisherman whose line has snapped, weighing the loss, considering the waters.

"Clever cub," it said, in a voice that was no longer entirely hers—layers under it now, depths, a chorus speaking through one mouth. "The others drowned easier."

"Get out of her face," Paloma rasped. His throat was raw. He did not remember screaming, but his body suggested he had.

"This isn't her face. It's your face. I only borrow." The shape cocked its head, and for one vertiginous moment the features ran like wet paint and reformed into something else—Ossian's face, Marisol's, Juniper's, a gallery of everyone he had come to need—and settled back into his mother's. "One turn, little Wolf. One moon left. You'll stand at the thirteenth gate whether you will or no, and there I won't need your willingness. There I'll only need the seal, and the seal is already dying." The smile returned, thin as a blade's edge. "Bring your friends. Bring your tokens. Bring your cleverness. I have kept every dead thing this world ever surrendered to me, and I am *so hungry*, and you are bringing me a feast."

The shapes of the dead went out all at once, like candles pinched between finger and thumb.

The grey drained from the grass. Wind resumed. The stars snapped back into their proper houses, and Marisol made a wounded noise at the corrected sky, and the hilltop was only a hilltop again—except for the torn stones, and the pool, which remained, quiet and deep and waiting, reflecting the full moon with perfect fidelity.

Paloma knelt in the grass and shook, and let Juniper lean her warm weight against his side, and did not speak for a long time.

When he finally could, what he said was not brave. It was small and honest, and it scared him more than the Hollowing had.

"It knew what I wanted," he said. "It knew better than I did. I didn't—I never let myself *look* at half of that. It had a complete inventory of me. Every want I've never said out loud." He stared at the pool. "How do I fight something that knows me better than I know myself?"

Marisol crouched beside him. She had been crying; her face was blotchy and her jaw was set, and she looked, in that moment, very much like the woman who would someday claim an antler-token knowing every future in which she died. She took his chin in her hand and turned his face from the pool to her.

"You just did," she said. "You drowned in the thing you wanted most, and you chose the truth that hurt over the lie that healed. That's the whole war, Paloma. That's all of it. Everything else is walking."

Juniper said nothing. But she was looking at the torn stones, and her ears were flat, and when Paloma followed her gaze he saw what she saw: fresh scratches in the polished fracture-faces, new marks over old, scored from the *outside*.

Something had been widening the gap. Something had been working at this gate for a long time, patiently, the way water works at stone.

And in the deepest groove of the newest scratch, glinting faintly in the moonlight, was a single silver hair—long, coarse, unmistakable.

Wolf's hair.

His mother's—or his own. From this distance, in this light, there was no telling the difference.

And that, Paloma thought, rising slowly to his feet with the cold of the false memory still lodged in his chest like a swallowed stone, was the most frightening thing the Hollowing had shown him all night: that it had been studying his family for five years.

Learning the difference.

Or learning there wasn't one.
