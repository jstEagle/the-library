## 18. Storm Over Ceniza

The bells began at dusk.

Not the evening bells—those were soft, bronze-voiced, the city's old lullaby. These were iron, hammered from the melted chains of the first covenant's breaking, and every soul in Ceniza knew what they meant. *The Office marches. Lock your doors. Give up what you are hiding.*

Brother Ossian stood in the Memory Office's great hall and watched two hundred priests assemble beneath the vaulted ceiling of white ash-wood, each one donning the gray hood that erased their faces from memory as they passed. He had worn that hood for nineteen years. Tonight it felt like a burial shroud he was being asked to fit to someone else.

"Brothers." Archivist Veyl's voice carried without effort; she had a way of speaking that made silence seem like agreement. "The seal at the summit gate fails within the day. When it does, the wild realm will pour through unfiltered—and we will harvest it. Every gift, every wonder, every power the covenant hoarded for its guardians, made lawful in our hands alone. The age of debt ends tonight. The age of collection begins."

Two hundred voices murmured assent. Ossian kept his own still, his hands folded into his sleeves the way he had been taught, and thought about his sister's face.

She had died eleven years ago, unlicensed magic burning itself out of her like a fever, and he had told himself ever since that the Office's doctrine was mercy: *magic is debt, and debts are collected*—better collected cleanly by us than cruelly by the wild. He had believed it the way you believe a wall is load-bearing, because everything else would fall if you stopped.

Then he had put Marisol's face beside Serafina's and found they were the same face, and the wall had come down all at once, burying him.

"The thief Paloma Villalobos will be at the gate," Veyl continued. "He carries the map. Take him alive if possible—the Wolf's blood has uses even after the seal dies. If not possible—" she shrugged, a small, terrible gesture, "—the archive keeps what it can."

Ossian looked down the ranks of gray hoods and understood, with the clarity that comes only at the moment of betrayal, that none of them knew. None of them had seen the twelfth gate torn open from the outside. None of them had watched the Hollowing wear the faces of the dead like borrowed coats. They had been told a story about harvest, and stories were the Office's oldest weapon, and tonight he intended to turn it in their hands.

He bowed his head with the others. And beneath the hem of his robe, his fingers began to work the small craft he had sworn off years ago—the craft of taking a memory out and leaving something else behind.

*Forgive me,* he thought, though he wasn't sure yet whether he addressed his order or his sister or himself.

* * *

Paloma felt the storm coming before he heard it.

They had made camp in the ruined watchtower below the final gate, the three of them pressed close around a fire Juniper insisted on despite the smoke, because, she said, "a cold cub is a stupid cub, and we cannot afford either." Above them the thirteenth moon swelled fat and wrong-colored, bruised at one edge where the failing seal bled wild light into it. The air tasted of ozone and pine sap and something older underneath, something like the inside of an empty house.

Marisol had her charts spread across the broken flagstones, weighting the corners with stones. "It'll break before midnight," she said for the third time. She wasn't panicking. She was doing arithmetic, which was worse. "When it breaks, everything the Hollowing has been swallowing comes back out at once. We need to be standing at the stone when it happens. Not near it. *At* it."

"I know," Paloma said.

"You keep saying you know."

"Because I keep knowing."

Juniper's ears flicked toward the city. Her whole body went still in that way foxes have, as if stillness were a language she could speak fluently. "Cub," she said quietly. "How many people live in Ceniza?"

"Forty thousand. Maybe more since the famine years."

"Mm." She rose, all four feet finding the broken masonry without sound. "And how many of them do you suppose are climbing this hill right now?"

Paloma went cold. He looked south, past the terraced vineyards gone wild, past the aqueduct where he'd once hidden with a map and his own dread, down to the sprawl of the city—and saw the procession. Torches first, hundreds of them, a river of fire winding up the pilgrim road. Then the sound reached them: iron bells, boots on old stone, and beneath it a low chanted cadence he recognized from childhood, from the times the Office had come to his neighborhood and taken someone away.

"They're not waiting for the seal," Marisol whispered. "They're going to take it while it's dying. While it's *weak*."

"No," Juniper said. Her mismatched eyes—one green-gold, one the deep violet of the wild realm's twilight—reflected the torch-river below. "They're going to take *him*. The seal is just the door. The Office wants the key, and the key has been sleeping in their city for twenty-three years wearing a boy's face."

Paloma's hand went to the map at his chest without his deciding to move it. Twelve gates opened in his blood now, twelve small suns of heat beneath his skin, and the thirteenth waited above them like a held breath. He had imagined this night a hundred ways. In none of them had he imagined forty thousand people's fear marching uphill behind him.

"We run," Marisol said. "There are goat tracks on the north face—"

"And go where?" Paloma asked. It came out gentler than he meant it to. "The gates circle the city, Mari. There's no outside anymore. There never was—that's the whole point of what my mother died to buy time for." He looked at the torches. "We hold the stone. That's all. We hold it until the moon turns, and then I write."

"*If* you can write," Juniper said. "A covenant needs three signatories, cub, and the third one is me, and I am—" she flexed her claws against the stone, a small bright sound, "—I am going to be very busy keeping those priests off you. So will Marisol. Which means you spend the next hour doing nothing but resting your blood. Do you understand me? Sleep. Eat. Be *boring*. Save your cleverness for the stone."

He almost laughed. Almost. Instead he sat back down by the fire, because she was right, and because some tired animal part of him wanted, just once, to be told what to do by someone who loved him.

Above, the bruised moon climbed. Below, the bells came on.

* * *

Ossian broke his first vow four hundred paces from the summit.

It was easier than it should have been. That was the horror of it—he had spent nineteen years perfecting the extraction of memory, and the same hands, the same focus, the same quiet breathing worked in reverse. He walked the column's flank as it wound up the pilgrim road, and where he walked, he touched. A hand on a shoulder here, a brush of sleeve there, murmured blessings that were actually instructions: *you are tired, you have always been tired, the summit is far and your knees are old.*

Priests dropped out of the column by twos and threes, sitting down heavily on the roadside, blinking, unable to say why. He planted exhaustion in them like seeds. He planted doubt: *did the Archivist say harvest, or did she say surrender? You've always been bad at hearing. Better to wait. Better to sit.* By the fifth switchback the column had lost a tenth of its number, and no one had noticed, because he had also planted, carefully, in the mind of the column's marshal, the certainty that the numbers were fine.

But Veyl did not march in the column. Veyl rode above it, on the white mule the Office reserved for doctrine, and her memory was warded with nine layers of official craft, and when Ossian came within ten paces of her the wards woke like hornets.

She turned in the saddle. For a moment the gray hood hid her face, and then she pushed it back, deliberately, letting him see her smile.

"Brother Ossian," she said. "You've been very busy on this road."

"Archivist."

"Eleven of mine are sitting in the weeds wondering why their legs hurt." She dismounted, unhurried. Around them the column flowed on, chanting, paying the two senior priests no attention at all—because he had made sure, hadn't he, that no one would look at them? His own craft turned home to roost. "I wondered which side you'd choose. I confess I hoped for this one. The traitor's role suits you better than the believer's ever did—you always doubted too loudly to pray well."

"Serafina didn't doubt," Ossian said. "She *believed*, and your doctrine ate her, and you called the eating mercy."

Something moved behind Veyl's eyes—old, tired, quickly shuttered. "Your sister exceeded her license. The debt was collected according to law."

"She was nineteen."

"The law does not count years. It counts power." Veyl drew off one glove, finger by finger. Beneath it her hand was tattooed to the wrist with the sigils of high office, the craft that could unmake a mind in a breath. "You know what happens now. I would prefer it happen gently. Kneel, give me the map's location, and I will let you keep your name. You'll wake tomorrow in the archive stacks with no memory of this week and a mild fondness for cataloguing. Many men live long, content lives in exactly that condition."

Ossian looked at her hand. At the column flowing past them both, torchlight sliding over gray hoods. Up the hill, faint through the trees, a fox's voice was screaming a warning he couldn't make out.

"Nineteen years ago," he said, "I would have knelt."

"Yes," Veyl agreed. "That's why I offered."

He moved first—not at her, but at the wards, driving his own memory-craft into them like a wedge into a door. She was stronger; he had known she would be; the point was never to win. The point was that a warded mind defending itself burns fuel, and Veyl's attention, for six full seconds, was entirely on him.

Six seconds was enough for the second thing he had planted that night, the seed he had carried up the pilgrim road in his own breast like contraband: the memory he had taken from the Office's deepest vault three days ago, copied, and slipped into the minds of forty sleeping priests in the barracks—a single image, unattributed, undeniable. The interrogation record of Lira Vasquez, five years ago. Her voice, calm and clear, saying: *the Hollowing is real. Your own founders sealed it. Read your first charter, Archivist. Read what the Owl wrote in the margin.*

Forty priests had woken this morning remembering a document they had never read, with a hunger to read it. Forty priests were, at this moment, turning back toward the city instead of the summit.

Veyl felt it happen—felt her column thinning behind her like smoke—and for one instant her concentration fractured, and Ossian drove everything he had left into that crack.

Not an attack. An offering. He gave her his sister.

Serafina at nineteen, laughing in the archive garden with a stolen peach. Serafina the night before her death, asking him, *Ossian, if the law is wrong, what does collecting the debt make us?* Serafina's face as the unlicensed magic took her, looking not at the priests but at him, trusting him to understand something he had refused to understand for eleven years.

Veyl staggered. The white mule screamed and shied. And in the space she left open, Ossian did the last thing, the unforgivable thing, the thing he had known since the garden that he would do: he reached into his own mind and tore out the ward that protected his memories from extraction, holding it open like a man opening a door onto a flood.

"Take them," he said. "All of them. Nineteen years. Take every one."

Her craft seized him before he finished the sentence.

* * *

Pain, first. Pain like the summit falling on him. Then the memories going—not fast, nothing so kind as fast—in order, oldest first, each one lifted out and examined and filed. His novitiate. His vows. The first memory he ever stole, a baker's wedding, taken to prove his worth. The garden. The peach. Serafina, Serafina, Serafina, each iteration peeled away thinner than the last, until—

Until he learned that a man can stand inside his own forgetting and choose, moment by moment, what remains.

He could not stop the extraction. But he could spend it. And so as each memory was pulled free he threw it outward, broadcast it into the column, into the gray-hooded ranks around him, so that two hundred priests marching to seize a dying seal suddenly *remembered* things that had never happened to them: a girl laughing with a peach. A question about law and debt. A face at a window, trusting.

The chant faltered. Broke. Scattered into argument, into weeping, into forty separate crises of faith blooming at once along the pilgrim road like fires in dry grass.

Veyl felt her army dissolving under the weight of one dead girl's memory, and shrieked—not words, just fury—and bore down harder, and Ossian, kneeling now in the torchlit dust, smiled with the last mouth he had.

*Go up,* he thought at the hill, at the tower, at the boy whose mother's face he had once seen in an interrogation record and never been able to forget. *Whatever you're doing up there. Go faster.*

Then the memories took him, all the way down, and Brother Ossian became a man with no past, kneeling in the road, watching torches with mild and empty interest, while somewhere above him the thirteenth moon began, at last, to break.

* * *

"He's killing them," Marisol breathed. "Look—look at the column, it's coming apart—"

Paloma stood at the tower's broken parapet and watched the impossible: the Office's march unraveling from the inside, priests dropping to their knees, embracing, arguing, some simply sitting down in the road as if the journey had been a dream they'd woken from. He couldn't see Ossian. He understood anyway. Some debts you pay forward.

"Juniper," he said.

"Already gone." But she paused at the parapet's edge, and for a moment her tail brushed his wrist—the only touch she allowed herself, the closest thing to an embrace a fox could manage. "Whatever happens at that stone, cub. Whatever it offers you. My name is Juniper. Make sure I'm there to hear you say it."

Then she was a streak of violet shadow pouring down the hillside into the chaos, and the moon cracked overhead like an egg, and wild light fell across Ceniza for the first time in twenty-three years.

Paloma turned to the summit path, put his hand against the first stone of the final gate, and felt thirteen gates' worth of blood answer him at once.

Behind him, from the darkness below, something vast and patient and hungry began, very softly, to laugh with his mother's voice.
