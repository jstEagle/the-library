## 19. The Thirteenth Gate

The last stair was not made of stone.

Paloma had climbed twelve gates to reach this one—ash and salt and river, bone and briar and the gate of small mercies that had nearly broken Marisol's heart—and every one of them had been built by hands, however ancient. The thirteenth had no hands in it. The stairs simply *were*, rising out of the summit rock like the idea of a staircase given permission to exist, each step faintly luminous, each step humming against his blood like a struck tuning fork.

Behind him, Juniper's claws clicked on the living stone. Behind him, Marisol breathed hard, the Stag's antler-token strapped across her back, its tines shedding a pale light that did not flicker so much as *listen*. And behind all of them, far below, Ceniza burned with torchlight—the Office marching up the mountain road, thousands of lanterns strung along the switchbacks like a rosary being counted.

"They'll reach the summit within the hour," Marisol said. "Ossian bought us more than I thought he could. But not much more."

"Then we don't waste it." Paloma kept climbing. His legs ached from three days without real sleep. His hands would not stop trembling, and he had stopped pretending they would. "One gate left. One signature short."

"One signature we can't provide," Juniper said quietly.

He didn't answer. He couldn't. Every time he thought about what Juniper had offered—her name, her form, her *self*, poured into the Owl's empty seat like wine into a cracked cup—something in his chest closed like a fist. He had spent twenty-two years learning how to take things from people: memories, moments, the warm interiors of other lives. He had never learned how to let someone give him everything.

The moon sat directly overhead now, swollen with its thirteenth fullness, so close it seemed he could have reached up and pressed his palm against its face. Its light fell through him as though he were made of gauze. Around the summit, the ruins of the old covenant ringed them—a circle of standing stones, each carved with one of the thirteen sigils, twelve of them dark and dead and one, at the circle's crown, still faintly alive. Still faintly *hungry*.

The Hollowing's gate.

It didn't look like a gate. It looked like an absence shaped like a doorway, a rectangle of nothing cut into the night air itself, and around its edges the darkness moved with a slow peristaltic patience, the way a throat moves before swallowing.

Paloma stopped three steps away. His stolen memories rose in him unbidden—all those borrowed first snowfalls, those secondhand weddings, those griefs he'd carried for strangers because his own grief was too heavy to lift—and they *burned*, every one of them, hot as coals behind his eyes.

"You're afraid," Juniper said. It wasn't a question. She wound once around his ankles, fox-warm, and looked up at him with her mismatched eyes—one gold, one the deep green of the wild realm's sideways forests. "Good. Fear means you're still deciding. The Hollowing hates a mind that's still deciding."

"What does it love, then?"

"Certainty," she said. "And mothers."

* * *

It came to him wearing his mother's voice.

He should have been ready for it. Chapter sixteen had taught him—he had *drowned* in a false memory at the twelfth gate, had felt his dead mother's arms around him while his lungs filled with comfortable lies, and he had clawed his way out knowing, *knowing*, that comfort was the Hollowing's favorite bait. But knowledge is a thin coat against winter, and when the voice came out of the absence-shaped door, low and warm and slightly rough at the edges the way Lira Vasquez's voice had been rough after she sang, Paloma's whole body turned toward it like a compass needle.

"Mijo."

She stepped out of the dark.

Not the echo from the river gate—that echo had been translucent, a memory given temporary architecture. This was solid. This was *whole*. She wore the gray traveling dress with the mended elbow he remembered, the one she'd worn the last morning he ever saw her, when she'd kissed the top of his head and told him to be brave and quiet, and he had believed, for five bitter years, that brave and quiet meant *gone forever*. Her black hair fell loose past her shoulders. There was flour on her knuckles. There was always flour on her knuckles; she baked bread for the neighbors even when there wasn't enough for themselves, because Lira Villalobos—Vasquez, before Ceniza renamed her—had never once in her life let a kindness go unspent.

"I watched you," she said, smiling, tears already standing in her eyes. "Every gate. Every turn. You found the ash sigil with your own blood, cub. You carried the map through the rooftops when your hands were shaking so badly you could barely hold it. I am so proud of you I could burst with it, and I'm here now, and I'm not leaving again, and all you have to do—"

"All I have to do is nothing." His voice came out flat. Dead flat. He hated how steady it sounded, because inside he was screaming. Inside, some starved animal part of him was already running toward her with its arms open. "All I have to do is stand here and let the seal die. That's the price. That's always the price with you lately."

Her smile faltered—just a fraction, just enough.

"You're not her," Paloma said.

"Paloma—"

"The echo under the river told me about the night she gave herself to the prison. She didn't hesitate. She walked in singing, Ossian's records said so, the interrogators wrote it down—*subject sang until the stone took her.*" His hands were shaking worse now, but he made himself keep talking, made himself look at the flour on those impossible knuckles. "And you're not singing. You're *negotiating*. My mother never negotiated. Not with the priests, not with the covenant, not with anything. She decided, and then she did the thing, and she did it with her whole heart or not at all."

The thing wearing his mother's face went very still.

"And another thing," he said, and here his voice finally broke, and he let it, because Marisol was watching and Juniper was watching and he was done performing competence for anyone, including monsters. "You called me *mijo*. She never did. She called me *cub*—no. Wait." He laughed, wet and ragged. "No. That's Juniper. That's what Juniper calls me. You called me *little wolf*. In the kitchen. With the flour. You'd say it in the old tongue so the neighbors wouldn't hear." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "You got almost everything right. That's the horrible part. You got almost everything right, and you got the one word wrong, and that word is the whole world."

For a long moment, the summit held only wind.

Then the shape of his mother sighed—not with disappointment, but with something almost like respect—and came apart. The gray dress unraveled into threads of dark. The flour dissolved. The face collapsed inward like a reflection disturbed by a thrown stone, and what remained was the Hollowing itself: vast, patient, older than the covenant, older perhaps than hunger's first invention of itself. It did not speak in words anymore. It spoke in pressure, in the weight of a thousand drowned memories, and its meaning settled over the summit like snowfall:

*I can offer you anything. Why do you refuse the only thing you want?*

"Because it isn't real," Paloma said. "And because—" He stopped. Breathed. Started again, slower, finding the truth as he spoke it the way his fingers used to find a memory's seam in the dark. "Because if I took her back at the cost of both realms, I wouldn't deserve her. She died buying me time. Time to do *this*. If I spend her sacrifice on an escape hatch, then she died for nothing, and I will not—I will not make her death mean nothing."

The Hollowing's silence stretched. Somewhere below, faintly, he could hear the Office's bells.

Then the pressure shifted, and something new entered it—something that was not temptation but curiosity, cold and enormous.

*The seal dies tonight regardless. Twelve gates torn. One heir short. What will you do, little wolf, when there is nothing left to sign?*

"That," said a voice from the edge of the stones, "is actually an excellent question."

* * *

She came out of the moonlight the way the false mother had come out of the dark—but where the Hollowing's arrival had been a slow flood, hers was a single clean strike of light. Lira Vasquez stood among the standing stones, and she was translucent, and she was unmistakably, irreducibly *herself*: the echo from the river gate, the fragment of soul she had pressed into the covenant's stone five years ago, drawn up the mountain now by proximity, by blood, by the sheer gravitational pull of her son standing at the world's hinge.

"Cub," she said, in the old tongue, and Paloma's knees nearly gave.

"*Mamá.*"

"Don't kneel. We haven't time, and you'll scrape your hands, and you'll need them." She crossed to him—her feet made no sound, though the frost beneath her bloomed with tiny white flowers where she stepped—and took his face in her hands. Her touch was cool as well-water and more real than anything the Hollowing had worn. "You saw through it. I knew you would. Your father saw through everything too; it's the Vasquez curse. We can't be lied to, only heartbroken."

"He's alive?" The hope leapt in Paloma before he could cage it.

"No, little wolf. Past tense. Past perfect." Her thumb traced his cheekbone. "But listen. Listen, because the bells are coming and I have perhaps a hundred words left in me before the stone pulls me back down. The Hollowing told you a truth wrapped in despair: the seal dies tonight. That part is true. The old covenant is finished—it was finished the day your grandmother's generation let it rot, and finished again when the Owl's line ended, and no amount of blood on old terms will save it. The terms are dead. The *paper* is dead."

"Then what do we sign?"

"New paper." Her eyes—his eyes, everyone in Ceniza had remarked on it, the boy has his mother's storm-gray eyes—blazed. "That is what the founders understood and the Office forgot and the Hollowing hopes you never learn: the covenant was never meant to be permanent. It was meant to be *rewritten*. Each generation of guardians renews it or revises it—that was the third clause, the clause the priests erased, because a covenant that must be renewed cannot be owned. The Wolf, the Stag, the Owl—they aren't jailers. They're *authors*. And authors choose their words."

Marisol had gone very still, the antler-token glowing against her spine. "The shrine showed me futures," she said slowly. "Every one where I held the line. I thought it was showing me my death."

"It was showing you your *signature*," Lira said. "Death is just what holding a line looks like from close up."

Juniper's ears flattened. "And the third seat? The Owl's seat? Rewriting the terms doesn't fill it. A willing spirit forfeits—"

"A willing spirit," Lira interrupted, gentle and iron at once, "under the *old* terms. Read the fine print of forever, fox. The old covenant demanded erasure because the old covenant was written by the fearful. New ink, new law." She turned back to Paloma, and her form was already fraying at the edges, moonlight showing through her shoulders like water through gauze. "I have maybe fifty words. So hear them all. I did not leave you. I left you a job, and the job was never to restore what I helped break. The job was to write something better. Don't inherit my oath. Outgrow it. Sign in your own name, with your own terms, and make the terms generous—generosity is the only magic that compounds."

"Mamá—"

"And one more thing." She smiled, and it was the kitchen smile, the flour-dusted morning smile, the last real thing he would ever carry of her. "When you name your guardians—and you will, tonight, because a covenant made in your name can name its own—choose people who will argue with you. A covenant everyone agrees with is just a wish. Now—" her voice was thinning, stretching, becoming wind, "—now pick up the pen, little wolf. It's been waiting for you your whole life."

She came apart into light. The light sank into the standing stones, and all thirteen sigils flared at once—twelve dead ones kindling briefly, like embers remembering fire—and then the circle went dark except for the Hollowing's gate, which recoiled.

It had stopped pressing. For the first time since the summit, the vast cold attention of the devouring thing seemed uncertain.

*There is no pen,* it said at last. *There is no paper. There is only what remains.*

Paloma looked down at his hands.

His hands were shaking. They had been shaking for three days. But threaded through the tremor, faint as the first line of a sketch, there was light—silver, lunar, the same light that ran through the covenant's stone, the same light his mother had just poured into the circle. The map was long gone, torn and burned and spent across twelve gates. It didn't matter. The map had only ever been a draft.

He crouched, and pressed two fingers to the summit rock, and began to write.

* * *

The first word cost him a memory.

He chose deliberately: the merchant who had bought his first charm, the taste of that first sale, the smug little warmth of competence. It left him cleanly, and where it left, silver script burned into the stone. *Let the realms be bound open,* he wrote, and the words glowed, and the Hollowing made a sound like a continent shifting.

*Open is how I feed.*

"Open is how you're *fed*," Paloma said. His voice steadied with each word, each sacrifice, each small piece of himself surrendered to the ink. "New terms. You eat willingly given memory—shared, chosen, renewed each full moon by anyone who consents. No more stolen souls. No more hollowed towns. You get fed honestly, and in exchange, you hold the border with us instead of against us. Guardianship. Yours too."

Below them, the bells had reached the last switchback. Torches spilled onto the summit path. Marisol drew the antler-token from her back and set it against the stone, and her voice rang out clear and terrified and absolutely certain: "Marisol Reyes, daughter of the highlands, signs as Stag." Light flooded the second line.

Juniper stepped up beside him, mismatched eyes wide, and Paloma caught her paw before she could speak the old vow of forfeiture.

"Not yet," he whispered. "I have a clause."

Above them, the thirteenth moon reached its zenith, and the first torches crested the summit, and Brother Ossian's voice—hoarse, changed, human—called out across the stones: "Hold! Hold your lines! The boy is writing!"

The Hollowing gathered itself at the threshold of its absent door, vast and hungry and, for the first time in an age of hunger, waiting to hear what came next.

Paloma dipped his fingers in his own burning blood and wrote the third guardian's name.
