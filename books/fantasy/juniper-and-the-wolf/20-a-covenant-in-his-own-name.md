## 20. A Covenant in His Own Name

The thirteenth gate was not a door. Paloma had expected a door—stone and iron, hinges and a keyhole shaped like his own hand—but what stood at the summit of the mountain above Ceniza was a ring of standing stones, each one taller than the tannery chimneys he'd grown up beneath, and between them nothing at all. Just air. Just the moon, swollen to its full and final turn, hanging low enough that he felt he could have reached up and pressed his palm against it the way he'd once pressed his palm against frost-glazed windows as a boy.

The Hollowing had been here. He could feel it the way you feel a fever in a room after the sick person has been carried out—a residue of hunger soaked into the stone. The twelfth gate's wounds were still fresh in his memory: the shapes it had worn, the dead it had borrowed, the terrible tenderness of its voice when it offered him his mother whole. He had walked away from that. His legs still shook with the walking-away.

But the stones were intact. The seal, though threadbare as a beggar's coat, still held at its final knot. And threaded through the air between the monoliths ran something visible now—the old covenant itself, laid bare by the moonlight, a lattice of silver script older than any alphabet Paloma knew, written in a hand that trembled even across five centuries. The Wolf's hand. His inheritance.

*You were always meant to rewrite this,* his mother's echo had told him at the river gate. *Every generation that keeps a covenant without rewriting it has only borrowed it.*

He unslung the satchel from his shoulder and knelt at the center of the ring, where the grass grew in a perfect circle of impossible green despite the altitude and the season. From the satchel he took the tokens: the Stag's antler, warm as a living thing in Marisol's hands; the Owl's feather, which Juniper would not touch; and the map, its thirteen corners scarred where Brother Ossian had torn one free and where the salt market fire had licked another. Thirteen gates. Twelve opened or broken. One remaining, and it was not a gate at all but a page waiting to be written.

"Paloma." Marisol's voice came quiet from behind him. She stood at the ring's edge with her arms wrapped around herself, and even in the moonlight he could see how pale she was—not fear-pale, exactly. Decision-pale. He was learning to tell the difference on her face. "Are you sure about the terms? Once ink meets stone, I don't think there's an eraser in either realm."

"There's no ink," he said. "That's rather the point."

He had spent three nights drafting the covenant in his head, lying awake in the shrine at the highlands' edge while Marisol dreamed her futures and Juniper sat watch with her mismatched eyes fixed on the dark. Three nights of arithmetic more brutal than any the Memory Office had ever taught him. What did the old covenant say? *Sealed shut. Guarded by three. Magic hidden, magic forbidden, magic punished.* It had been written by guardians who were terrified, and terror makes for cramped handwriting. Five hundred years of Ceniza's fear had grown from those few cramped lines like mold from a damp wall. The priests. The burnings. The widow whose first snowfall he had stolen because she could not bear to keep it and the merchant who bought it because he could not bear to have nothing. All of it, every grief in the city, traceable back to a signature made in panic.

He would not sign in panic. He would sign the way he wrote everything—in stolen hours, in careful drafts, with the particular attention of a man who knew that words outlive their makers.

"The realms bound open," he said aloud, testing the phrase against the silence of the summit. "Not sealed. Not torn wide either. Open, and guarded."

"Guarded by whom?" Marisol asked. "There are only three of us, and one of us is a fox."

"By everyone who chooses it." He turned to look at her, and then at Juniper, who sat apart on a fallen stone with her tail curled around her feet, watching him with an expression he couldn't read—and with Juniper that meant something, because usually he could read her like a cheap chapbook. "That's the flaw in the old oath. Three guardians holding a door against the whole world. Of course it decayed. Of course it broke. You can't ask three people to carry a city's worth of fear forever. My mother carried it alone for years before she—" His throat closed. He breathed through it, the way she had presumably breathed through worse. "Before she went into the prison. The new covenant spreads the weight. Every person who takes the oath becomes a small guardian. A stitch instead of a wall."

"And the Hollowing?" Marisol's voice dropped, the way voices drop around certain names in Ceniza—as if hunger might be listening. "It has to eat. That's what it *is*. You can't starve it without starving everything near it."

"No," Paloma agreed. "But we can change what it eats."

He rose and walked to the nearest standing stone. Up close, the silver script moved—not flickering, but flowing, the way water flows under ice. He could see the old terms now, could almost parse them through the heat of his own blood responding to the sigils: *sealed, sealed, sealed*, repeated like a prayer, like a lock turning thirteen times.

"The Office harvests memories," he said. "They take them from people who can't refuse. They burn thieves in the salt market and call it debt collection. But the Hollowing doesn't actually want stolen memories—it wants *given* ones. That's why it wears our dead. That's why it offered me my mother. It's starving for consent." He laughed, short and bitter. "Five hundred years of a covenant built on refusal, and the thing on the other side of the wall has been dying of thirst next to a locked well."

"So we open the well," Marisol said slowly.

"We open the well. Willingly shared memory. Grief given freely, joy given freely, fed to the Hollowing not as prey but as—communion. As payment freely rendered. People will choose it, Marisol. Some of them already do." He thought of the widow, pressing the snowfall-memory into his hands like a coin she was glad to be rid of. *Take it. Let someone else carry it awhile.* She hadn't known she was making an offering. But she had been. "The charm trade was never evil. It was just honest work done in hiding, done without law, done without accountability. So we make it lawful. We make it accountable. Memory-craft taught openly, practiced under oath, with the same rules that bind any healer or midwife. No theft. No compulsion. Consent written into the craft itself."

Marisol was quiet for a long moment. Above them the moon seemed to lean closer.

"You understand what you're describing," she said at last. "You're describing a world where what happened to me never has to happen again. Where a girl who sees futures in the sky isn't beaten for it by her father, isn't taught to hate her own eyes." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she steadied it with the antler-token gripped white-knuckled in her fist. "And you're describing a world where what happened to Ossian's sister doesn't happen either. Where the magic that killed her is... held. Held by everyone, together."

"That's the idea."

"It's a mad idea."

"Yes," said Juniper, from her stone. Her voice was odd—flat in a way Paloma had never heard from her. "It is. Write it down, cub."

* * *

Writing it down, it turned out, meant writing it into himself.

The old covenant had been signed in blood, and blood was still the only ink the stones would accept. Paloma knelt again at the circle's heart and drew the knife from his belt—the same knife he'd used for five years to nick the edges of other people's pasts, repurposed now, he hoped, into something cleaner. He opened his palm along the old callus line where he'd gripped pickpocketed charms, and let his blood fall onto the grass.

The grass drank it. The silver script shivered.

And then the knowledge simply arrived, the way his mother's echo had arrived at the river gate—not words from outside but words rising from somewhere below his ribs, some chamber of him that had been locked since birth and was now, finally, unlocked. The grammar of covenants. The syntax of oaths. He understood suddenly why the Wolf's original signature trembled: because signing was not writing. Signing was *becoming*. Every clause he spoke would grow roots in him, would live in his blood alongside the wolf-magic he had spent his whole life smothering.

He began to speak.

"I, Paloma Villalobos, heir of Lira, heir of the Wolf, do set aside the covenant of sealing and write these terms in its place, in my own name, owing nothing to the dead but gratitude and nothing to the living but honesty."

The script flared. Somewhere far below, he sensed, twelve gates groaned in their sleep.

"First term: the boundary between the mortal realm and the wild realm shall stand open. Not sealed, for sealing bred the fear that fed the Hollowing; not unguarded, for openness without care is only another kind of hunger. The boundary shall be tended as a garden is tended—by many hands, in every generation, renewed by every generation, for no covenant survives on the strength of its founders alone."

"Second term: magic is lawful. Magic is accountable. No gift of the blood shall be cause for burning, banishment, or erasure. Those who work with memory, with prophecy, with the wild things, shall swear the crafter's oath: no taking without consent, no working without witness, no power without answerability to those it touches. What the Memory Office called debt, we call relationship, and relationships are kept, not collected."

He paused. His blood was running faster than he'd intended, pooling in the grass, and the script was drinking it greedily, the silver lines thickening, brightening. He could feel the terms taking hold of him—could feel the first clause rooting somewhere behind his sternum, warm and heavy as a second heartbeat. This was the price the old Wolf had paid, and the Stag, and the Owl. This was why they had chosen sealing in the end: because openness demanded this, a self given over clause by clause.

Paloma found, to his surprise, that he didn't mind. He had spent twenty-three years being nobody, being invisible, being a careful emptiness in which other people's memories rattled around like coins in a stolen purse. Being full of something—being *answerable* for something—felt less like a cage than like bones.

"Third term: the Hollowing shall be fed, and fed honestly. Let all who wish bring their memories to the boundary—griefs too heavy to carry alone, joys too large for one life—and let them be given, not taken. Let the Hollowing learn the taste of consent. Let it be fed until feeding becomes its nature, for even a devourer is only what it is made to eat. And should it refuse—should it turn from gift back to theft—then the guardians of the boundary shall meet it, together, in every generation, for as long as it takes."

"Fourth term—" He stopped.

Because the fourth term was Juniper.

She had risen from her stone and come to stand at the ring's edge, and in the moonlight her fur had gone silver as the script, and her mismatched eyes—one gold, one green, the colors of two different worlds—were fixed on him with a steadiness that frightened him more than the Hollowing ever had. The loophole lived in the fourth term. The Owl's line was extinct; the covenant required three signatures; and a willing spirit could stand where an extinct bloodline could not, forfeiting name and form forever. She had told them this at the bone orchard, after the betrayal and the forgiveness, in a voice so casual it had taken him a full day to understand she was volunteering to be erased.

*I've been a fox a long time, cub. Names are heavy. Maybe I'd like to put mine down.*

He had refused to accept it then. He had lain awake three nights trying to draft his way around it, and had failed, because the arithmetic did not care about his feelings: three signatures or the seal fails, the seal fails or the Hollowing devours both realms, and there was no third option except—

Except that he was not signing the old covenant. He was signing a new one. In his own name. With his own terms.

And a covenant made in his name could name its guardians.

He wasn't ready to use that yet. Not quite yet. First, the fourth term had to exist at all.

"Fourth term," he said, and his voice did not shake, though everything under it did. "The office of Owl shall not pass extinct while any spirit stands willing to serve. Let the third signatory be chosen not by blood but by willingness, and let the choosing be free, and let the cost be borne by the covenant itself and not by the one who serves—for a covenant that feeds on its own guardians is only the Hollowing wearing better manners."

Juniper's ears went back. "Paloma. That's not how it works. The forfeiture is the price. There's no version where I stand as the Owl and keep—"

"There's no version under the *old* terms," he said. "I'm writing new ones."

The script blazed. The whole ring of stones hummed like a struck bell, and far below them the city of Ceniza lit up window by window as if the light were climbing toward them, and Paloma understood that the covenant was listening now, truly listening, weighing his words against five centuries of precedent, deciding whether a thief with a knife-cut palm had the right to rewrite the architecture of two worlds.

Then the moon passed the zenith, and the script settled, and the fourth term took root in him beside the others, and he knew—he *knew*, with the certainty of blood meeting stone—that it had been accepted.

* * *

Marisol signed next.

She stepped into the circle with the antler-token held against her chest, and when her blood fell to the grass the standing stones around the ring bloomed—impossibly, out of season, out of soil—with white flowers that opened as he watched. Her voice, when she spoke her acceptance, was steady in the way he had learned to recognize: courage as a decision, made knowing the cost. She signed as Stag, keeper of futures, watcher of the boundary's eastern arc, and the flowers turned to follow her like sunflowers following a sun that wasn't there.

Then only Juniper remained, and the moon was beginning its descent, and the Hollowing was stirring somewhere beyond the ring—Paloma could feel it gathering at the edge of the wild realm's darkness, drawn by the light of a nearly-finished covenant, hungry and curious and perhaps, if the third term held, capable of learning a new appetite.

"Juniper." He crossed the grass to her. Up close, her fur was trembling, though her stance was not. "Before you speak your part. There's a clause I haven't spoken yet. The last one. I want you to hear it before you decide anything."

"You've already decided it for me," she said, but softly, without anger. "I felt it root. Cub, whatever you think you've done—"

"Fifth term," Paloma said, loud enough for the stones, for the moon, for the listening script, for the thing in the dark beyond the world's edge. "A covenant made in my name may name its guardians. Therefore I name the third signatory. I name her *Juniper*. I write her name into the covenant itself, so that she stands as the Owl not as a spirit who has surrendered her name, but as a soul whose name is anchored in the pact between realms—held by the covenant, kept by the covenant, hers for as long as the covenant stands."

The ring detonated with light.

Silver script poured off the standing stones like a waterfall reversing, spiraling upward into the moon, and Juniper cried out—a sound halfway between a fox's bark and a woman's sob—as the old forfeiture closed around her and the new naming caught it, wrestled it, held it. Paloma lunged forward and grabbed her fur, her shoulders, whatever part of her was solid, anchoring her the way the words were anchoring her name, and through his palms he felt the covenant remaking itself around her: the Owl's office pouring in, vast and cold and ancient, and underneath it, refusing to be dissolved, one small warm thread with her name on it.

*Juniper. Juniper. Juniper.* Written into stone. Written into him. Unerasable.

The light faded. The flowers dimmed to a soft glow. And in Paloma's arms, where a fox had been, a woman knelt in the grass—dark-haired, sharp-faced, maybe thirty years old or three hundred, with one gold eye and one green eye and an expression of absolute, incandescent fury.

"You absolute," she said, "stubborn, sentimental, *thieving* little cub. You stole my erasure. Do you have any idea what you've done? I was going to be *free*—"

"You were going to be gone," Paloma said. His voice cracked at last, now that it was over, now that it was safe to crack. "There's a difference. You taught me that."

Behind them, at the ring's northern edge, the darkness gathered itself and stepped forward into the moonlight, wearing a shape it had stolen from a grave, wearing a smile it had learned from the desperate, and the Hollowing spoke with a voice like a well opening:

*"New terms," it said. "How novel. Let us see if you can honor them better than the last wolves did."*

Juniper—woman now, Owl now, named now—rose to her feet beside him, and Marisol took his left hand, and Paloma faced the hunger at the edge of the world with his own covenant burning in his chest like a second heart.

"Terms are terms," he said. "Even for monsters. *Especially* for monsters. Come and be fed."
