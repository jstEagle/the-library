## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley did not allow magic, and Odile Almeida had built a life inside that refusal.

It was the reason they had come, all those years ago — or one of the reasons, though the truest ones were harder to say aloud. Magic could not cross the ridgeline. Whatever old covenant or accident of geography made the valley what it was, the effect was absolute: spells died at the border like moths against glass, and nothing enchanted could enter, and nothing enchanted could leave. The villagers called it God's cupped hand. Maren called it a null field with folkloric accretions. Sef called it home, in the way she called everything she loved home, wordlessly, with her whole face.

Odile called it a workshop.

They sat now on the flat stone behind the cottage they shared with three other people, knees drawn up, a board across their lap, paper weighted at the corners with river stones. In their left hand, a pen that had never held anything but ink. In their right, nothing at all — just air, and the discipline of twenty years.

Because the realm they were mapping was not here. It was *there*, folded into the memory of every person who had ever sung in the Choir of Saint Vessa's, and Odile reached for it the only way a magicless person could: by asking it to be remembered.

*Begin where you always begin,* they thought, eyes half-closed, pen moving. *The gate of white birches.*

And it came — not as vision, exactly, but as certainty. The way you know the layout of a house you dreamed once. The birch gate stood at the realm's western edge, its trunks silvered, its leaves whispering in a wind that belonged to no weather. Beyond it, terraces of song rose like rice paddies cut into a mountainside, each level humming a different note of the same long chord. The Choir's dead walked those terraces — not ghosts, Odile had decided long ago, but *kept things*, memories so loved they had refused to dissolve. They wore the faces of everyone anyone had ever mourned, and they sang, and their singing was the mortar that held the whole realm together.

Odile drew the terraces. Drew the stair of black water that spiraled down from them into the deep vaults, where the singing went quiet and something older waited. Drew the towers of the Choir's cathedral, which were not towers at all when you looked at them directly but the ribs of an enormous throat.

They had never walked any of it. They never would. That was the bargain the valley offered: perfect sight from perfect safety. Odile mapped realms the way astronomers mapped stars — by light that arrived too late to burn them.

"You're doing the thing again."

Bram's shadow fell across the paper. He stood with his sleeves rolled, woodsmoke in his hair, an axe over his shoulder he had no need of and carried anyway because his hands went strange without weight in them. A deserter knight should have looked wrong in a valley of farmers — broad, scarred, a man built for someone else's war — but ten years had worn him soft at the edges, or worn the valley onto him, until he looked like something the place had grown.

"I'm working," Odile said.

"You're holding your breath." Bram crouched, set the axe down, and studied the map with the frank bafflement he always gave it. "Every time. You go somewhere else and forget to breathe, and then I have to stand here looking worried until you come back."

"Then you're doing your job."

"One of us should have one." He tapped the paper, careful of the wet ink, at the spiral of black water. "That's new."

"That's always been there. I've just never been able to hold it before." Odile turned the board so he could see. "It goes deeper than I thought. Under the vaults there are — rooms. Cells. Hundreds of them. And they're not empty, Bram. They're *full*. Full of voices that aren't singing anymore. Just... waiting."

Bram was quiet a moment. He had a soldier's quiet, the kind that meant he was deciding how much to worry. "Waiting for what?"

"That's what I'm trying to find out."

From the open window behind them came a hum — low, threading, unmistakably deliberate. Sef leaned on the sill, flour to her elbows, her dark braid slipping over one shoulder. She couldn't speak; she had never spoken, not since the fever that took her village took her voice with everything else. But Sef did not need words. She hummed a phrase, three notes rising, and Odile felt it land in their chest like a hand pressed flat over the heart.

*I heard you talking about me,* the phrase said. *Come taste the dough before I ruin it.*

"We'll come," Odile said, and Sef grinned and vanished back inside, and the hum continued, weaving itself absently through the afternoon.

That was the thing about Sef that Maren had spent years failing to write a satisfactory footnote about: her songs held spells, but only outside the valley. Here she was simply a girl who communicated in melody, harmless as rain. Here, her gift slept. Which was why they all stayed. Which was why, when the crown's men came up the pass, it would not matter at all.

* * *

Maren found them at supper.

She came in from the road with her coat still buttoned wrong, which was how Odile knew something was wrong, because Maren Voss had spent thirty years in the royal archives and could catalog a shelf in her sleep; she did not misbutton coats. She stood in the doorway with the evening light behind her and said, "Heralds on the north road. Three of them. Crown livery. They'll reach the village square by morning bell."

Bram set down his spoon. "Gift-hunt?"

"They're calling it a census." Maren's mouth twisted around the word like it was spoiled meat. "A census of the gifted. Registration, they're saying. For everyone's protection." She pulled off her coat, sat, pushed her bowl away untouched. "I've read this document before, Odile. I helped file its grandfather forty years ago. It starts as a census. Then the registered start disappearing. Then the census becomes a harvest schedule."

Sef had gone very still, her hands flat on the table. She hummed one low, questioning note.

"No," Maren said gently. "You can't run ahead of them. Nobody outruns heralds; they travel with wardens, and wardens ride relays of fresh horses. We have maybe a day, if we leave tonight and don't look like we're leaving."

"And if we stay," Bram said, "we register. All four of us. A deserter, an archivist who knows where the bodies are filed, a spell-singer, and—" he nodded at Odile, "—whatever the crown decides a cartographer of impossible places is worth."

"A lot," Odile said quietly. "I'd be worth a lot."

Nobody argued. That was the worst part — the room full of people who loved each other, all doing the same arithmetic and arriving at the same terrible sum.

Odile looked at the table. At the bread Sef had made, still warm, its crust split and golden. At the mended places in the tablecloth where the fabric had worn through and Maren had stitched it back together with thread that didn't match, because Maren believed mending should be honest. At the window, where the last light was going amber on the ridge that kept the magic out and them safe.

Ten years. Ten years of mornings on the flat stone, of Sef's flour-dusted humming, of Bram's unnecessary axe and Maren's misbuttoned days. They had built a life out of the valley's refusal, and now the crown was coming to collect, and the ache of it was not sharp at all. It was dull and enormous, like the pressure of deep water. Like grief arriving early, politely, to sit with them at supper.

"We take the south pass," Bram said at last, in the voice he used for orders he hated giving. "Down through the salt flats. If we move tonight—"

"Tonight," Maren agreed. "Pack nothing that matters. Take only what you can bear to lose."

"That's backwards," Sef signed — actually signed, her hands quick and fierce, because some things were too important even for melody. *Take what you cannot bear to lose. Leave the rest. Things can be replaced. We cannot.*

"She's right," Odile said.

They moved through the cottage like people robbing themselves. Odile rolled the maps — twenty years of them, the Choir's realm rendered in ink and patience — and hesitated over the newest one, the one with the black-water stair and the waiting cells. Something in it itched at them. *Full of voices that aren't singing anymore.* They rolled it tight and slid it into the waterproof tube, because a mapmaker does not leave a map unfinished, and because some part of them already suspected that the unfinished parts were the ones that mattered.

Bram took down his sword from above the hearth, looked at it a long moment, and left it hanging. He took the axe instead.

Sef packed bread. Of course she did. She wrapped loaf after loaf in cloth while tears ran silently down her face, because Sef cried the way she did everything — without sound, completely.

And Maren stood in the middle of the room with a small tin box in her hands, and did not pack it, and did not put it down.

"What is it?" Odile asked.

"Old work," Maren said. Her voice was steady in the way that cost her something. "Things I should have burned decades ago. Evidence, maybe. Or a confession. I haven't decided which."

Outside, the first stars were coming up over the ridge. Somewhere down the north road, three riders in crown livery were making camp, sleeping the sleep of men doing ordinary work, and tomorrow they would nail a notice to the village post and ask after four names, and the valley that forgot magic would learn that forgetting offers no protection from being remembered.

Odile stood on the doorstep a final moment. Behind them, the cottage. Ahead, the dark fold of the south pass. They thought: *I will map my way back here someday. I will draw this door, this stone, this exact quality of lamplight, and I will find my way home by it.*

They did not know yet how literally the world would take that vow.

"Odile," Bram called softly from the road. "We're burning moonlight."

Odile stepped off the doorstep, and the valley let them go, and did not say goodbye.
