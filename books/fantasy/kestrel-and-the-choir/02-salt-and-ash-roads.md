## 2. Salt and Ash Roads

Bram had deserted from three armies in his life, and he had learned this much: the first night of flight was always the worst. Not because of pursuit — pursuit took days to organize, and the crown's heralds were fat men on fatter horses — but because of the leaving. The way Odile had stood in the doorway of the valley cottage with their satchel half-packed and looked back at a life that fit inside four walls. The way Sef had touched the doorframe once, twice, three times, as if memorizing it by touch because she could not say goodbye aloud. The way Maren hadn't looked back at all.

He rode rear guard now, which was where he always put himself, because a deserter knows that what you're running from matters less than what you're running toward, and he preferred not to look at either.

The borderlands unrolled before them under a bruised dawn sky: salt flats gone gray with frost, then the ash roads, so named because the crown burned its tax ledgers here every seventh year and the wind carried the cinders for a season. The road was packed earth the color of old scars, and along its edges stood the wayshrines — little stone hollows where travelers once left offerings. Now they left other things.

"Stop," Bram said, and reined in.

Odile pulled up beside him, breath steaming. "What is it?"

Ahead, one of the shrines held a small fire. Not an offering flame — those were banked coals and oil, tended — but a paper fire, curling and bright. A woman knelt before it, hooded, feeding pages into the heat one at a time. As they watched, she fed another, and Bram saw the writing on it catch and flare: a name, written large, in a careful hand. *Maren's* hand would have looked like that, thirty years ago.

"Burning her name," Maren said quietly, riding up beside them. She had not slept. None of them had. "That's the third shrine like this since we crossed the ford."

"What does it do?" Odile asked. Their eyes had that hungry look, the cartographer's look, the look that wanted to map everything even now, even fleeing. Bram had come to love that look and to fear it, because the crown hunted exactly that kind of hunger.

"It does nothing," Maren said. "That's the point. It's a ritual of surrender. You write your name — your true name, the one your gift answers to — and you burn it, and you swear to never answer to it again. The witch-finders have a word for the ones who've done it. *Ashen.* They can walk through a checkpoint singing hymns and the finders' rods won't twitch."

"And their magic?" Bram asked, though he knew.

"Gone. Or sleeping. No one knows which, because no one who burns their name has ever been able to tell anyone what it felt like." Maren's voice was flat in the way Bram recognized from interrogation rooms, from confessions extracted gently over years. "They call it peace."

The woman at the shrine finished. She stood, brushed ash from her skirts, and walked away down the road without looking back, and Bram thought: *that is what I did when I left the king's army.* He had not burned his name. He had simply stopped answering to it. Ser Bramwen of the Ninth Lances, dead in every ledger but the ones that mattered, and some mornings he woke reaching for a sword hilt that belonged to a man who no longer existed.

"Come on," he said. "We ride wide of the next checkpoint. There's a drover's track through the fells."

"The fells add two days," Odile said.

"The checkpoints add a pyre." He met their eyes. "Two days."

Odile held his gaze a moment longer than necessary — they always did, cataloguing him, mapping him the way they mapped realms that existed only in memory — and then nodded and turned their horse toward the hills.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen standing stone, a ring of granite teeth the old faith had raised and the new faith had toppled and the land had simply refused to bury. Sef built the fire without being asked; she had a way with fires, small and precise, and she arranged the kindling like notes on a stave. Maren slept sitting up against the stone, her archive-trained spine refusing even unconsciousness the comfort of lying down. Odile sat apart, sketching by firelight — the fells, the shrine, the shape of the road behind them — because Odile could not stop making maps any more than Sef could stop humming.

Bram sat down across from them and let the silence stretch until Odile looked up.

"You want to ask me something," he said.

"I want to know why you lied to us." Odile's pencil kept moving as they spoke, which meant the question had been waiting a while. "At the ford. The toll-warden asked who we were, and you told him we were salt merchants out of Vell, and you told it so smoothly I almost believed it myself. And then last night, when Maren asked if the fells track was safe, you said you'd ridden it before. You haven't. I've seen your maps of this region. You've never been north of the ash roads." The pencil paused. "You lie easily, Bram. I'm trying to decide if that frightens me."

It was a fair question, and Bram respected that Odile asked it straight, the way soldiers did, instead of circling it the way courtiers did. He turned his hands toward the fire. They were a knight's hands still, scarred across the knuckles, too well-fed for a salt merchant's.

"When I served," he said, "I had a lieutenant. Younger than me. Bright as a struck flint. Her name was Isolde, and she was the best rider in the Ninth, and she loved the king — the real king, the man, not the crown — with the whole stupid burning heart of her." He watched the flames. "There was a village called Harrowmere. You'll find it on no map now; they struck it after. There was plague there, or the crown said there was plague, and the order came down to burn it clean. And Isolde came to me the night before and asked me, Bram, is it true, is there plague, because you've seen the supply manifests and I haven't, and you'd tell me if it wasn't true, wouldn't you?"

Odile had stopped drawing.

"And I told her yes," Bram said. "I told her it was true, and I watched her face go pale and set, and I watched her ride out at dawn and do a thing that lived in her for the rest of her life, whatever length that was. And the truth—" His jaw worked. "The truth was there was no plague. There was a woman in Harrowmere who'd written a song the wrong people heard. The manifests were clean. I knew. I lied to her because I thought if she refused the order, they'd hang her, and if she obeyed it, she'd only hate herself, and I judged that hatred was lighter than rope." He looked up. "I was wrong. It isn't. But I was loyal. That's the thing nobody tells you about loyalty, Odile. Sometimes it means lying to the people you love, because the truth would break them in a place you can't mend, and you'd rather carry the breaking yourself."

The fire popped. Over by the stone, Maren's breathing had changed — awake, listening, saying nothing. Sef had gone very still, her head tilted, the way she tilted it when a melody was forming somewhere behind her silence.

"That's a terrible lesson," Odile said at last.

"It's the only one I have."

"No." Odile set down the pencil. In the firelight their face was all planes and shadow, younger and older at once. "The terrible lesson is that you decided for her. You chose her brokenness for her, like choosing a route on a map she never got to see. If you'd told her the truth — if you'd said, *the order is murder and I don't know how to stop it* — maybe she'd have refused and hung. Maybe she'd have ridden anyway. But it would have been *her* choice, made with open eyes. Loyalty that lies isn't loyalty. It's ownership with good manners."

Bram laughed — a short, startled sound, smoke-colored. Thirty years of soldiering and one exile cartographer had just handed him his own soul back with corrections marked in red.

"You sound like her," he said. "Isolde. After."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. Someone should." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and gave Odile the truth he'd been carrying since the heralds came: "Then hear me plain, and I'll not lie to you again. The gift-hunt isn't a hunt. Hunts are for beasts, and beasts are killed quick. This is a harvest. I've seen the requisition orders — I stole them off a quartermaster's tent two years ago and I've been running ever since, and I never told you why, because telling you would make it real and real things have to be fought and I was tired of fighting. The crown isn't burning the gifted. It's *collecting* them. Something in the palace eats what they take. Names, memories, gifts — it takes all of it, and it grows, and the king lets it, or the king is it, or—" He spread his scarred hands. "I don't know. But I know we're not running from it anymore. We're riding toward it, because Sef hears something in the realm you map, and Maren knows how it was made, and you're the only person alive who can draw the road in."

Odile stared at him. Behind them, Maren had opened her eyes. And Sef — Sef was looking past all of them, up at the toppled stones and the frost-bright sky, her lips parted around a sound that never came, her fingers moving against her knee in shapes Bram slowly understood were not fidgeting.

They were conducting.

She tugged Odile's sleeve and pointed, urgent, at the sketchbook — at the maps of the memory-realm Odile had drawn a hundred times from the safety of the valley, the world with the choir in it, the world with the tower of voices. Then she pressed her palm flat against the page, over the tower, and looked up at them with an expression Bram had last seen on a battlefield: terror and certainty wearing the same face.

"She's saying someone's calling," Maren whispered. "Isn't she. From inside. Someone in the tower is calling for help."

Sef nodded once, hard, and began to hum — low, cracked, a melody none of them had taught her — and the frost on the standing stones shivered and sang back.
