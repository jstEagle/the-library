## 3. A Girl Made of Hymns

Sef had three hundred and eleven ways to say *good morning*, and she used a different one every day.

This morning it was the sound of water over river stones, hummed low in her chest while she braided her hair by the fire. Bram looked up from sharpening his knife, listened, and grunted — which meant he'd heard it, which meant he'd understood, which was its own small miracle for a man who claimed music was "just noise with opinions." Maren smiled without looking up from her ledger. Odile, curled in the window of the ruined chapel they'd sheltered in, pencil still moving across paper, tapped twice on the sill.

*Good morning to you too,* said the tap. Sef had learned their whole vocabulary by now: two taps for greeting, one long drag of the pencil for *I'm thinking*, a sharp scratch-scratch when a map wasn't going the way it should. Odile spoke in ink and gesture the way Sef spoke in song. Between them there was very little need for mouths at all.

The valley lay below the chapel's broken wall, silvered with early mist, and Sef sang at it softly — not a spell, just a listening-song, the one that let her hear what the air carried. The valley answered the way it always did: with nothing. No magic lived here. That was why they'd chosen it, why hunted people came here to be ordinary, why the crown's witch-finders' compasses spun uselessly at the border stones. The valley forgot magic the way other places forgot rain in a drought.

But this morning, the listening-song brought something back.

Sef went still, braid half-finished, hair slipping through her fingers.

It was faint. It was far. It came threaded through the realm Odile mapped — she could feel the shape of it now, the way you feel weather coming in your knees. A voice, singing inside the memory-realm that hung over the capital like a second sky. And it was crying.

Not weeping, exactly. Crying out. Over and over, the same phrase, worn smooth as a stone from saying it so long:

*I am here. I am still here. I am here.*

Sef set down the comb. She stood. She walked to the window and put her hand flat on Odile's map-table, on the great sheet where the Choir-realm sprawled in Odile's careful ink — towers of remembered bells, rivers of lullabies, a palace drawn in strokes so fine they looked like breath.

Then she opened her mouth and gave the phrase back.

She didn't have words. She'd never had words; whatever made children speak had skipped her entirely, and her mother had died before teaching her the hand-signs the deaf weavers used downriver. But a voice is not only words. A voice is pitch and ache and the shape of the throat's grief, and Sef could carry all of that. She sang the stranger's cry into the quiet chapel — *I am here, I am still here* — in a minor key that made the fire pop and Bram's knife stop mid-stroke.

Odile's pencil froze.

They turned slowly, and Sef watched them do the thing she loved most and feared most about them: *listen with their whole body*. Odile's head tilted, eyes unfocusing, the way they looked when they were walking a realm in their mind instead of on paper. Their lips moved. Reading the song like text.

"That's not a hymn," Odile said quietly. "That's not any hymn I've charted. Where did you—" They stopped. Started again. "You heard that? Just now? From the realm?"

Sef nodded. She pointed west, toward the capital, then pressed her fist to her breastbone and pulled it outward — *from deep inside* — then touched the map.

Bram rose first. He always moved first when his body decided something before his brain did; it was why he'd deserted, probably, why he'd once dragged a bleeding stranger out of a burning waystation without asking whose side she was on. "Say it again," he said. "Slower."

She sang it slower. And this time she did what she'd been afraid to do, what she'd been turning over all night since she'd first caught the thread of it three valleys ago: she added the harmony underneath, the second voice she'd heard wrapped around the crying one like a hand around a throat. Cold. Vast. Patient. Singing a counter-melody that sounded, if you weren't listening with your bones, almost like comfort.

Maren's ledger hit the floor.

Not dropped — *hit*, flung, the archivist who treated paper like scripture standing up so fast her chair went over backward. Her face had gone the color of tallow. "Stop," she said. "Sef, stop singing that."

Sef stopped. She stared. Maren never shouted. Maren apologized to furniture.

"Where did you hear that?" Maren's voice shook. "The counter-melody. Where."

Sef pointed west again. Then, because Maren's hands were trembling and trembling hands needed answers, she did the hardest thing she knew how to do: she built the explanation out of everything she had. She sang the king's public voice first — the proclamations, the edicts read in the tyrant's name, the flat herald's cadence every border town had learned to dread. She made it ugly and hollow, a bell with a crack in it. Then she sang the voice from the realm beside it, and let the family hear what she'd heard: that beneath the cracked bell, someone else was ringing. Someone smaller. Someone *begging*.

"I am here. I am still here."

The fire crackled. Mist moved against the broken wall. Nobody breathed.

"That's the king," Odile said at last, very softly, as though saying it loudly might break something. "That's — Sef, are you telling me that's the *king*?"

Sef nodded. Then she held up one finger — *wait* — and sang the distinction as clearly as she could: the cold vast harmony was the one wearing the crown's voice in the world. The small begging melody was the one trapped underneath. Two singers. One throne.

"No." Bram said it flatly, the way he said things he wanted very much not to be true. "No. I served under that man's banners for six years. I heard him speak at review. Whatever sits on that throne ordered the burnings, ordered the harvest—"

"Ordered them," Maren whispered, "or *was made to order them*?"

And there it was. Sef watched it land on each of them in turn, watched the idea move through the chapel like weather. Bram's jaw working. Odile's pencil hovering over the map, trembling, not touching down. Maren with her knuckles white against the fallen ledger, staring at nothing, seeing — Sef would understand later — something old and terrible and hers.

Because Sef had more to give them. She'd been saving it, hoarding it like a child hoards the last sweet, because giving it away meant it became real. But Maren was shaking, and Bram was angry, and Odile was looking at the map like it might look back, and someone had to say the true thing.

So Sef crossed the room, took Odile's hand, and pressed it flat against her own throat.

She sang the crying voice one more time — but close, intimate, the way it had come to *her*, alone in the dark of her skull on the road. And she let Odile feel it: the vibration of a melody that did not belong to her moving through her body like a passenger. The king's voice, singing through a girl's borrowed throat because no one else would listen.

Odile's eyes went wide. Wet.

"It's a prison," they breathed. "The realm — my maps — I thought I was charting a kingdom of memories, I thought the towers were *architecture* — but some of those walls don't hold memories *in*, they hold a person *down*. Look—" Their pencil finally touched paper, flying now, sketching over the palace on the great sheet, circling, circling. "Here. This chamber I couldn't resolve, the one that kept redrawing itself? It's not a room. It's a *cell*. And the entity isn't haunting the palace. It's wearing it. It's wearing *him*."

"The tyrant is a prisoner," Maren said. She said it the way people say things they've known for years and prayed never to hear confirmed. "The king is screaming inside his own skull and the whole country thinks the scream is an edict."

Silence took the chapel again, but it was a different silence now. Not the silence of fear. The silence of a world tilting.

Bram sat down slowly on his bedroll, knife forgotten across his knees. Six years of loyalty, six years of shame for breaking that loyalty, and Sef watched him arrive at the thought that maybe he'd never betrayed the real king at all — that the man he'd deserted had already been stolen, and everything since had been a puppet's war. His hands closed into fists. Opened. "Then the hunt," he said hoarsely. "The harvestings. If he's a prisoner, then the harvests aren't policy. They're *feeding*. Something is eating the kingdom's memories and using his mouth to order the table set."

"And we're on the menu," Odile said. They looked at Sef, and their expression did the complicated thing it did — wonder and dread holding hands, exactly as hers had for three nights. "You heard him across half a kingdom. Through a dead-magic valley. Through my maps. Sef — voices don't travel like that unless they want to be found. Unless they're looking for someone who can *carry* them."

Sef looked down at her own hands. Small hands, chapped from road-dust, hands that had never held anything heavier than a song.

She thought about the voice. How tired it had sounded. How it hadn't stopped singing, not once, not in all the time she'd listened — because stopping meant surrendering, and whoever the king had been before the crown ate him, he was clearly not the kind of man who surrendered.

She raised her chin. She met each of their eyes in turn — Bram, Maren, Odile — and she sang the only answer she had. Not the king's cry this time. Her own. A rising line, four notes, simple as bread: *we go*.

Maren laughed — a wet, broken sound — and wiped her face with her sleeve. "To the capital. To the heart of the thing that eats memories. With what army?"

Sef pointed at the map. At the realm above it, the towers of remembered bells, the rivers of lullabies. Then she pointed at herself, at her throat, and spread her arms wide.

*Every voice it ever stole is still in there.* She made sure they felt it, humming the vault-note low until the window glass shivered. *Nothing sung is ever truly silenced. It's waiting. All of it. Waiting for someone to conduct.*

Odile stared at her. Then down at the map, at the cell that kept redrawing itself, and Sef saw the moment the last piece clicked home behind their eyes — saw them understand that her songs and their maps were two halves of the same instrument.

"We'll need to get inside," Odile said slowly. "Past the borders, past the hunt, into the palace itself. Into the architecture of the thing." They swallowed. "We'll need a plan. We'll need luck. We'll need—"

What they needed, though — what arrived instead — came up the valley road an hour later, while Sef was packing and Bram was kicking dirt over the fire: hoofbeats. Many. The jingle of harness and the flat bright blare of a herald's horn, and a voice rolling ahead of it, amplified by something that was not lungs, a voice with a cold vast patience underneath it that made Sef's blood turn to ice water.

By order of the Crown, the gift-hunt had entered the valley.

And the voice making the announcement was, note for note, the counter-melody from her song.

* * *

Sef stood in the doorway of the chapel with her family gathered behind her, and far down the misty road the riders emerged like ink spreading through water — twenty of them, thirty, black cloaks, and at their center a palanquin draped in royal blue that no honest procession had any business carrying into a magicless valley.

The herald's voice rolled across the fields again, and this time Sef heard what the others couldn't: beneath the proclamation, beneath the orders to search every house and bind every gifted soul, the cold vast harmony was *singing to her*. Searching. A slow sweep of sound moving house to house, field to field, the way a tongue moves across a wound.

Looking for the throat that had sung the king's name.

Bram's hand closed on her shoulder. Odile stepped half in front of her, pencil already gone, eyes already measuring the back slope of the hill, the treeline, the old drover's path that climbed out of the valley north. Maren was stuffing the ledger into her coat like a woman hiding a heartbeat.

There was no time to explain. There was barely time to breathe.

So Sef did the only thing left. She caught Odile's eye, pressed her own palm flat over her heart, and sang — soft, fast, pitched beneath hearing for anyone but the four of them — the plan that was not yet a plan: *run now, sing later, save the king*.

Four notes. Simple as bread.

Then they ran.
