## 5. The City of Borrowed Faces

The capital announced itself three days before we saw it.

We smelled it first — woodsmoke and tallow and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in the sun — and then we heard it: a low, ceaseless murmur, the sound of ten thousand people breathing in unison without ever once raising their voices above it. Bram said cities always sounded like that. Maren said no. Maren had lived in this city for thirty years before her fall from grace, and she said she had never heard it so quiet.

"Vellamere used to be loud," she told us on the last morning of the road, standing at the crest of the hill with her satchel clutched to her chest like a shield. "Bells arguing with bells. Fishwives. Duelists at dawn. You could not buy silence there if you emptied your purse." Her jaw worked around something bitter. "Now listen."

I listened. What I heard was a city humming to itself like a hive that had forgotten why it built the comb.

Sef stood beside me, her small hand finding my sleeve. She did not sing. That was the first thing I noticed, and the thing that frightened me most. Since the borderlands, since the burned names and the ash roads, she had hummed nearly constantly — little threads of melody, testing the air the way a blind woman tests a floor with her foot. Now her throat moved and produced nothing. She pressed two fingers flat against her lips and then pointed down the hill, toward the walls of Vellamere, and shook her head slowly.

*The song won't come here,* she meant. *Or it isn't welcome.*

"We go in as pilgrims," Maren said, all business, because business was how she carried her guilt — bundled and balanced, never set down. "Pilgrims come to see the King's Mercy Fountain. Nobody questions weeping provincials."

"And if someone asks what we're weeping about?" Bram asked.

Maren looked at him for a long moment. "In Vellamere? No one will ask. That's rather the point."

* * *

The gates took our names.

That was literal. A clerk in grey sat at a table inside the gatehouse with a ledger open before him, and each traveler entering the city spoke their name aloud while he wrote it, and then — this was the part I could not stop watching — he struck a small bronze bell, once, and the traveler's face went briefly, terribly smooth. Not unconscious. Just... paused. A held breath. Then they walked on into the city, and I swear to you that every one of them walked with exactly the same gait.

"What does the bell do?" I whispered to Maren.

"It takes a copy," she murmured back. "A taste. Enough for the archive to know you." Her eyes were fixed straight ahead. "It's been doing that since before the entity. It was built to catalog dissenters. Someone taught it to be hungry, and now—" She stopped herself. Swallowed. "Keep your hood up. Don't look at the bell."

I looked at the bell. I am a cartographer; looking is the whole of my trade, and I have never learned to stop. What I saw was this: the bell had no clapper. It rang anyway. And around its rim, where the bronze should have been worn smooth by centuries of striking, there were instead fine grooves cut in spirals — and the grooves were full of light, faint and silverish, moving like water finding its level.

Memory has a look. I had known that since I was nine years old, since the first time I dipped my pen into the valley's still pools and drew out the shape of a dead woman's kitchen. Memory looks like light that remembers being fire. The bell was full of it. The bell was *full*.

Sef's hand found mine and squeezed hard enough to hurt.

* * *

We lodged in a pilgrim house in the Tanners' Quarter, three floors of narrow bunks run by a widow who charged fair prices and asked nothing, which in Vellamere was itself a kind of miracle. From our window on the third floor I could see across the rooftops to the palace mount, where the royal keep rose against the sky like a fist of white stone clutching something.

That first evening I spread my papers on the floor and began to work, because work was the only prayer I knew.

Mapping a memory-realm from outside is like sketching a whale from the shadow it casts under your boat. But Vellamere was not hiding its architecture. It was *displaying* it. The entity wearing the king's crown had rebuilt the palace the way a spider rebuilds a web — everything radiating, everything connected, everything designed to funnel inward. I could see it even from here, even with only my eyes and my memory of how memory behaves.

"Give me the charcoal," I said, and Maren handed it over without being asked, and for an hour the four of us crouched over my paper in the lamplight while I drew the city as it truly was.

Not the streets. Under the streets. Every memory taken by every bell, every harvest, every "gift" freely traded — it all flowed somewhere, and memory flows along channels the way water does, and channels leave marks on the world for those who know how to read them. I marked them. The fountain in the Mercy Square where pilgrims wept: a wellspring, feeding outward. The gatehouse bells: tributaries. The great avenue called the King's Spine ran straight from the gates to the palace mount, and beneath it — I shaded it dark, cross-hatched until the paper nearly tore — beneath it ran the main channel, wide as a river, carrying everything home to the keep.

"There," I said, tapping the palace. "That's the vault. That's where the singing comes from." Sef had described it to us twice now, in her halting finger-speech and her written words: a voice crying out beneath the stone, a man's voice, young and old at once, calling a name over and over. His own name, Sef thought. The king's true name, which no one in the city dared speak because no one in the city remembered it.

Bram studied the map with his arms crossed, his deserter's tattoo hidden under his sleeve but his posture still soldier-straight. "The Spine runs straight up the mount. No cover the whole way."

"No," I agreed. "Which means the front door is a lie. There'll be another way in. There's always another way in — memory hates a dead end. It pools. It seeps. Look here—" I traced the channel where it bent around the eastern cliff below the keep. "The river of it bends. Water doesn't bend unless something blocks it. Something old is buried under the east foundation, older than the vault, and the flow goes around it. If we can reach whatever that is—"

"Tomorrow," Maren said quietly. "Tonight you sleep. All of you. Tomorrow we walk the Spine and you can look with those terrible eyes of yours as much as you please."

I slept. Or something near it. And I dreamed of the valley, of the pools and the birches and the smell of Maren's ink, and I woke with wet eyes and wrote down every detail before it faded, because I had begun, somewhere on the ash roads, to hoard my memories like a miser. I did not yet know why. I only knew that in this city, things that were kept were safe, and things that were given away were gone.

* * *

On the second day we went walking among the borrowed faces.

I want to write down what it was like, and I find I cannot do it honestly except by telling you about the baker.

There was a bakery on a corner near the Mercy Square, and its windows were full of bread, real bread, brown and crusty, and the baker stood behind his counter selling it to a line of customers. He smiled at each one. He made change accurately. He wished them a good day in a pleasant voice. And when the line emptied and no new customer came, he stood there smiling at the middle distance, motionless, until the next person entered — and then the smile snapped back on like a lamp being lit.

"He's not unhappy," Maren said softly, watching me watch him. "That's what no one understands. He isn't sad. Sadness wants things. Grief wants things back. These people don't want anything anymore, because wanting lives in the same room as remembering. You can't long for a future if you can't hold a past. They gave the crown their hunger along with everything else. Safety in exchange for appetite. That was the bargain."

"The gift-hunt," Bram said. His voice had gone flat and hard. "They call it a gift."

"They call it a gift because the alternative word is *tithe*, and tithes are owed to gods, and the crown decided long ago that it would rather be loved than worshipped. Love doesn't ask anything of you." Maren's laugh had no humor in it at all. "I helped build the ledger that tracked the first harvests. I told myself it was mercy. Dissenters spared execution, merely... lightened. I was very good at telling myself things."

In the square, the Mercy Fountain played its silver water, and pilgrims knelt around it weeping, and I finally understood what they were doing. They weren't praying. They were *visiting*. The fountain was fed by the main channel — I could see it now, plainly, the way the water shimmered wrong, the way it caught light that wasn't sunlight — and the harvested memories of the whole city passed through it in fragments. Faces surfaced in the spray. A child's birthday. A wedding dance. A mother's voice saying a name. The kneeling people reached into the water and touched these fragments and wept, because for one moment they remembered something they had sold, and then the fragment sank and the forgetting closed over them again, smooth as pond ice, and they went home lighter than they came.

Sef knelt at the fountain's edge and put her fingers in the water.

She jerked back as if scalded. Her eyes went wide and fixed on mine, and her hands began to move so fast I could barely follow.

*It's him.* Her fingers trembled. *He's in the water. All of him is broken into pieces and poured through the whole city. He's everywhere and nowhere. Odile — he knows we're here. He felt me. He said—*

She stopped. Her hands curled into fists against her chest, and tears ran down her face, and she mouthed the words she refused to sign, shaping them silently, twice, three times, like a prayer or a warning:

*Come quickly. Before it finishes eating me.*

Above us, on the palace mount, the white keep caught the afternoon sun — and for a heartbeat, just a heartbeat, every window in it lit silver at once, all together, like eyes opening.

Then the light went out, and the city went on humming, and somewhere beneath the stone a king finished saying our names.
