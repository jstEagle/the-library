## 6. What the Crown Remembers

The palace smelled of lye and lilies, and Bram knew both were lies.

He had scrubbed floors like this once, twenty years ago, as a boy-soldier with soft hands and a hard belief: that the crown was a wall between the realm and the dark. He'd stood post in these corridors while the courtiers drifted past in silk, and he'd thought the smell of lilies meant cleanliness. Now he understood it was a cover scent, poured over something rotting underneath — the lye for blood on stone, the flowers for the memory of what had been done here.

He walked the servants' stair with a tray of wine he did not intend to deliver, wearing the gray livery of a palace under-steward, his beard trimmed close, his deserter's posture folded away into something servile and small. Twenty years of learning how to be invisible, and it came back to him like a prayer.

Maren's map had been exact. *Servants' stair to the third landing. The old guardroom beyond the tapestry of the Hunt. He keeps the same hours he always kept.* She had said it without looking at him, her voice flat as ledger paper, because she was still paying for chapter four's confession and they all knew it.

*He*, of course, was Commander Aldric Vane.

Bram reached the third landing. The tapestry hung where it had always hung — hunters on horseback, hounds streaming forward, a stag turning at bay with its antlers full of stars. As a boy he'd thought it beautiful. Now he saw the weaver had given the stag human eyes.

Beyond it, the guardroom door stood open, firelight spilling out.

Vane sat alone at a table set for two, eating nothing, drinking nothing, a decanter of wine untouched before him. His hair had gone white at the temples. His uniform was immaculate. And across from him, in the second chair, sat a place setting arranged with perfect care for no one at all.

"You're late," Vane said, without turning around. "You were always late, Halvard. Even at muster."

Bram's hand went cold on the tray. That name. No one living called him that. He had burned that name on the salt roads with the other hunted ones, watched it curl off his tongue as ash.

"Commander." He set the tray down. "I don't know what you mean."

"Sit." Vane turned then, and Bram saw what twenty years had done to him — not age, exactly. *Erosion*. Like a cliff face that had been rained on too long. "You have wine. I have questions. It's almost the old ritual, isn't it? Except you're the one being interrogated now, and we both know you'll lie beautifully, because I taught you how."

Bram sat. There was no point running; the door behind him had closed without a sound, and the corridor beyond it had gone quiet in the specific way corridors go quiet when soldiers are standing very still in them.

"The heralds," Bram said. "The gift-hunt. Tell me what it actually is."

Vane smiled, and it was the worst thing Bram had seen all year, because it was fond. "Still charging through doors. Good. I'd hate to think the borderlands softened you." He pushed the decanter aside. "You want truth? Here is truth, Halvard. The hunt is a harvest. It has always been a harvest. We are not burning witches. We are *gathering* them."

"Gathering them where?"

"The vaults." Vane said it the way a man reports grain stores. "Every gifted soul taken at the border is brought here, and their memories are drawn out — carefully, cleanly, painlessly, if they don't struggle. The memories feed the Crown's Work. The bodies—" he paused, and for one flickering moment something moved behind his eyes, some drowned thing surfacing, "—the bodies serve. Most of them. The ones who can still walk."

Bram thought of Sef, asleep in the safehouse beneath Maren's cloak, her throat working silently even in dreams. Thought of Odile sketching by candlelight, mapping a realm made of stolen things.

"And you command this," Bram said. "You stand in front of children and you take their lives out of their heads and you call it service."

"I keep the hours," Vane said quietly. "That's all I do anymore. I keep the hours and I pour the wine and I set the table, every night, for someone who used to sit there." His gaze went to the empty chair, and stayed. "Do you know what the Crown's Work eats first, Halvard? Not memories. *Reasons*. You wake up one morning and the thing you were loyal to is still sitting in your chest, but the reason has been scooped out, and you can't remember why you ever followed anyone anywhere, and so you follow whoever tells you the schedule." He looked up. "I gave my reasons to the crown thirty years ago. What's left is discipline. Discipline is enough. It has to be."

For a moment — one treacherous moment — Bram felt pity, hot and useless, and hated himself for it.

"There's a girl," Vane said. "Small. Dark-haired. They pulled her out of the choir loft at the sanctuary on Wren Street an hour ago. She didn't fight. She opened her mouth and she *sang*, and do you know what happened?" His hands, Bram noticed, were shaking. "My guards fell down. All six of them. Weeping. One of them started confessing crimes he committed fifteen years ago. And the girl walked through them singing a name — a name I am not permitted to hear, a name that made my ears bleed when I tried to listen — and she went down into the undercroft toward the vaults, and the wards let her pass, because the wards are keyed to the king's voice, and whatever she was singing, the wards thought it *was* the king."

The room tilted. Bram gripped the table's edge.

"She's going to wake him," Bram said. "She's going to free him."

"She's going to die," Vane said, rising. "The entity knows its own name when it hears it sung true. It will unmake her before she reaches the throne. Unless—" and here his erosion-cracked face did something almost human, almost pleading, "unless someone who remembers why loyalty matters gets to her first. I can't. Don't you understand? I can't want anything anymore. I can stand in this room and know she's walking to her death and feel *nothing but the schedule*. So I'm telling the only man I ever trained who ran instead of kneeling. Go save her, Halvard. Or don't. But decide quickly."

Bram was already moving.

* * *

The undercroft stairs went down farther than any palace should have foundations.

He took them three at a time, sword — Vane's own sword, pressed into his hands at the door with the words *it was always yours, I only kept it sharp* — heavy in his grip. The walls sweated. The torches burned low and blue, the color of flame starved for air, and between each bracket the stones were carved with names, thousands upon thousands of names, worn shallow by time or by deliberate sanding, until most were only ghosts of letters.

Memories of names. Burned off the people who owned them, and now burned off the walls that recorded them.

He heard her before he saw her.

Sef's voice — the voice she never had, the voice that lived only in song — filled the stairwell like water filling a hull. It was not loud. It was enormous. She sang a single word over and over, descending, and though the sound bypassed Bram's ears and landed somewhere behind his sternum, he caught the shape of it, the cadence, and understood with a lurch that this was it. The true name. The king's true name, carried out of the memory-realm in a mute girl's mouth, sung down into the dark toward the thing that had stolen it.

The stairs opened into a vaulted hall, and Bram stopped dead.

The harvested memories were visible here. He hadn't expected that. He'd imagined sacks, ledgers, jars. Instead the whole ceiling of the cavern blazed with light — threads, millions of silver-white threads strung from stone to stone like a loom the size of the sky, each one humming faintly, each one a life someone else remembered better than the person who'd lived it. And among the threads, pale shapes moved slowly, tending them, and the shapes had faces he half-recognized from wanted posters and borderland rumors: the taken ones. The gathered. Still here. Still *in* here.

And below the blazing loom, at the far end of the hall, on a throne of black basalt that had no business existing beneath a palace of white marble—

Sef stood in the open, arms raised, singing.

Before her, rising out of the throne like smoke deciding to be a man, the entity turned its attention upon her. Bram could not look directly at it. His eyes slid off it the way rain slides off oilcloth. But he saw its effect: the nearest threads dimming, curling, reaching hungrily toward the small figure and her impossible voice.

*Sef,* he thought. *Run.*

She didn't run. Of course she didn't run. She planted her bare feet on the stone and sang louder, and the name rang off the vault walls, and the threads above began to shiver in resonance, and for one blazing instant Bram thought — *it's working, she's waking him, she's doing it* —

The entity moved.

It did not walk. It arrived. One moment it coiled on the throne; the next it stood before Sef, vast and courteous, and it inclined its head as if greeting a colleague, and it opened what might have been a mouth, and it *sang back*.

The counter-song hit the hall like a landslide. Threads snapped. The pale tenders scattered shrieking into the loom's light. Sef's voice cracked — Bram heard it crack, heard the true name splinter mid-phrase — and the entity leaned down over her, gentle as a nurse, and put one long finger against her lips.

The silence afterward was total. Bram's ears rang with it.

Sef swayed. Her hands came up, grasping at her own throat, at the air, mouthing the name and getting nothing, mouthing it again, her face going from defiance to confusion to a terror Bram recognized from the mirror of every man who'd ever lost a limb — the terror of reaching for something that is simply, permanently gone.

"No," Bram said aloud, and the word echoed stupidly in the vastness.

The entity turned its head. It regarded him across forty feet of broken stone, and though it had no eyes he could find, he felt seen completely — the deserter, the liar, the loyal fool, every name he'd ever burned and every one he'd kept, laid out and itemized.

It did not take him. It only smiled — he was certain it smiled — and gestured, and from the shadows of the side galleries the guards came, the hollow-eyed serving-bodies Vane had described, moving with terrible economy, and Bram raised Vane's sword and fought, because that was the last reason he had left and he would spend it here.

He was good. He had always been good. It bought him eleven seconds.

They took the sword. They took his arms. They held him facing forward, because the entity wanted him to watch, and watch he did as they bound Sef's wrists with silver thread cut from the great loom itself — thread that hummed with other people's birthdays, other people's mothers' voices — and dragged her toward a door of black iron in the vault's far wall.

At the threshold she twisted around. Her eyes found Bram's, and they were wet and furious and alive, and her mouth shaped words with no sound behind them, and he read them anyway, because he had spent a winter learning her language:

*Don't forget me.*

The iron door closed. The humming of the loom resumed, indifferent as weather. And Bram knelt on the broken floor of the vault beneath the palace, in the country he had deserted and the crown he had never stopped loving despite everything, and understood at last the full cost of the bill.

Somewhere above, Odile and Maren were waiting in the safehouse, counting minutes.

He had to reach them. He had to tell them the wards were down, the door was marked, the girl was in the dark — and that the entity had heard the true name sung once, and now it knew exactly which memories it needed to burn next.

It would come for the valley. It would come for the map.

It would come for everything Odile loved, and it would eat it first.
