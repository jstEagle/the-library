## 7. The Choir Beneath the Stone

They took her voice before they took her freedom, and Sef almost laughed at the order of it.

The collar was iron and cold, fitted to her throat by a jailer whose hands shook — a small mercy, that. He had heard her sing on the road, she thought. Or he had heard of it. Word traveled in the capital the way water traveled through cracks: slowly, invisibly, into everything. *The girl who sings without singing.* The girl whose hymns opened locks and closed wounds and once, in a borderland chapel with Bram's sword across her knees and Maren's hand in hers, called a dead woman's name back from wherever names go when no one speaks them anymore.

Now the collar sat against her throat like a second jaw, and when she tried to shape even the smallest note — not a spell, just a hum, just proof that she was still herself — it drank the sound before it could become anything. She felt it happen. Felt the vibration leave her chest, reach her throat, and simply *stop*, swallowed whole, the way the entity's blank-eyed citizens swallowed bread in the feeding halls without tasting it.

*So this is what you do,* Sef thought at the crown, at the thing wearing the king's face somewhere above her. *You take the sound out of the world.*

She had been mute since birth. She knew every variety of silence the way other people knew weather. There was the silence of a room where no one spoke. There was the silence of a mouth that would not open. There was the silence Odile gave her, patient and full, waiting for her hands to finish their sentence. And there was the silence of the vault, when they carried her down through six locked doors into the dark beneath the palace, which was none of these — which was a silence with an appetite.

They put her in a cell of white stone, seamless, windowless, lit by nothing she could name. The light came from the walls themselves, a pale glow like moonlight through milk. No seams meant no mortar to loosen. No hinges meant no lock for her song to turn, if she'd had a song. They had built this place for people like her. Perhaps they had built it *because* of people like her.

She sat on the floor. She pressed her palms flat against the stone.

And she listened.

This was the thing the jailers did not understand, the thing the collar could not touch: Sef had never needed her voice to hear. Sound lived in her hands, in the soles of her feet, in the hollow behind her sternum where music made its home. A building was a body; every body sang its own low note. The palace above her sang of marble and rot. The city beyond it sang thin and frayed, thousands of voices worn down to thread.

But beneath her — deep beneath, deeper than the foundations, deeper than the bedrock's old slow drone — something else was singing.

Sef went very still.

It was not one voice. It was thousands. It was a sound like rain heard from underwater, like a congregation seen through frosted glass, all their mouths moving but the words arriving broken, arriving *faded*, as though the song had been traveling a long way through something that ate it as it went. She could not make out words. But she knew the shape of them. She knew the shape because she had heard it once before, in the memory-realm Odile mapped from the valley — the realm of the Choir, the place that existed only in what people remembered, where Sef had first heard the king's true voice crying out inside his stolen body.

These were not living voices. These were *harvested* ones.

Memories. Thousands upon thousands of them, taken from the mouths of the city above — taken as payment, as punishment, as safety — and stored here in the dark like grain in a tithe-barn. And they were still singing. That was the part that made her eyes sting and her hands curl into fists against the white stone. Whatever the entity was, whatever the crown had made it, it had never understood the thing it harvested. It thought memories were *stores*. Quiet things. Things you could stack and own.

But memories were songs. And songs did not stop being songs because no one was listening.

Sef knelt. She laid her cheek against the floor, and she began, silently, to listen the way she had been taught to listen — not by any teacher, because there had been no one to teach a mute child in the valley, but by the world itself, patiently, over years. She found the lowest voice first, the way you find the root of a chord. An old man's memory of a wedding, she guessed, from the warmth of it, the smell of bread and beeswax folded into the tone. Then a second voice braided near it: a child learning to swim, terror and triumph in the same bright note. Then more. Hundreds. Thousands. Layer on layer, going down into the dark farther than she could follow, the whole buried archive of a city's stolen joy and grief, humming in the vault beneath the vault.

Waiting.

*Oh,* Sef thought, and her silent thought came out shaped like a note. *Oh, you beautiful, forgotten things. You've been holding your breath down here all this time.*

She pressed both hands flat to the floor.

And she began to conduct.

* * *

There was no baton. There was no sound. There was only Sef, kneeling alone in a cell of white stone, moving her arms in slow wide arcs that meant nothing to any watcher there might have been — and everything to the choir below.

Her grandmother — not by blood; no one in the valley was anything by blood — had kept bees, and had shown her once how a hive decided to swarm. No single bee commanded it. But the vibration changed, a thousand small bodies shifting their hum by degrees, until the whole hive moved as one mind toward the new home no bee had chosen alone. Sef had been four years old. She had stood with her bare palm against the warm wood and felt the decision travel through it like weather.

That was what she did now.

She reached down through the stone with everything she had — not her voice, which the collar owned, but her attention, which nothing owned — and she found the wedding-memory again, the old man's warmth, and she held it the way you hold eye contact with a frightened animal. *I hear you. I know you're real. You are not grain. You are not stores.* And then, gently, she shifted the pitch of her listening. Just slightly. Just enough.

The wedding-song wavered. Turned toward her.

Beside it, the swimming-child turned too, catching the change the way a second voice catches a round. Then a third. A tenth. A hundred. Sef's arms moved wider, slower, drawing shapes in the air that were older than notation, older than language — the shapes her own hands made when she dreamed, the shapes of things rising. Down in the dark, the faded chorus thickened. The words were still broken, still eaten on their long climb toward her, but the *melody* was coming clear now, and it was a melody she knew.

It was the king's true voice. The cry she had heard inside the memory-realm, the prisoner's plea that had set them on this road. It was woven all through the harvest — a thread of gold in grey cloth — because the entity had fed on him longest, deepest, and everything it took from him was here, everything he had ever been, stacked in the dark beneath his own throne.

He was in the choir. The real king, the man under the mask, was down here in pieces, singing with everyone he had lost.

Sef's arms trembled. Sweat ran cold between her shoulder blades. The collar pulsed faintly at her throat, sensing — she could feel it, a dull animal attention, something in the iron waking to the fact that the mute girl was doing *something*. She slowed her arcs. Made them smaller. Made them look like nothing, like a prisoner pacing her grief in gestures, while underneath, steady as a heartbeat, the conducting went on.

*Not yet,* she told the choir, in the only language left to her. *Not loud. Not free. Not yet. But soon.*

And the choir — thousands strong, decades deep — answered her the only way it could. It softened. It gathered itself close, a banked fire, a held breath. But it did not stop singing. It would never stop singing now, and Sef felt that truth settle into her bones like a keel settling into water: whatever they did to her next, whatever the collar drank, the song below was no longer drifting. It had a direction. It had a *downbeat*.

Somewhere far above, doors were opening. She felt it through the stone — boots, many, descending, and among the footsteps one gait she knew so well her heart lurched before her mind caught up: uneven, favoring the left knee, a soldier's walk worn soft by guilt. Bram. Bram was coming down. And with him, lighter, quicker, half-running — Maren, or someone wearing Maren's hurry.

And behind them both, faint but unmistakable, threaded through the stone like light through a keyhole: the particular silence of Odile Almeida, mapmaker, who walked through the world leaving quiet everywhere they stepped, and whom Sef loved the way a bell loves the moment before it rings.

Sef rose to her feet. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. She stood in the white cell with her stolen voice locked in iron and her arms loose at her sides, and beneath her, patient as spring under snow, the choir of ten thousand stolen lives waited on her signal.

The first door, six levels up, boomed open.

Sef smiled — the fierce, unguarded smile no one in this palace had ever seen — lifted her chin, and readied her hands.

*Sing with me,* she told the dark. *When I give the word, we bring the whole roof down in song.*
