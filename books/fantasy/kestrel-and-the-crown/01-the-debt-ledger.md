## 1. The Debt Ledger

There are three rules to selling charms in a city where magic is illegal: never sell the same charm twice, never sell to anyone who smiles too easily, and never — *never* — let them see you enjoy it.

I broke the third rule that morning, because Tam Wicker had just come off a night shift hauling herring and he was laughing at his own joke before he'd finished telling it, and some things are stronger than survival instinct.

"Go on, then," he said, slapping three coppers on the barrelhead between us. "The luck one. The one that made old Piet's nets come up full."

"Old Piet's nets came up full because Old Piet finally listened to his wife about where to drop them." I turned the coppers over with my thumb, checking for shaved edges out of habit, though Tam wouldn't cheat me. He was the only dockworker in Vaelmoor who hadn't tried. "The charm is a knotted cord. It does nothing but remind you to be careful."

"That's what luck *is*, isn't it? Being careful."

I looked up at him — big man, salt-cracked hands, eyes red from the night wind off the Graywater — and decided he'd earned the truth, or the shape of it.

"It's a knot tied seven times while thinking of someone who wants you home safe," I said. "That's all. If your mother ties it, it works better than if I do."

"My mum's dead."

"Then think of whoever fed you last."

He laughed again, took the cord like it might bite him, and wound it around his wrist under his sleeve. Three coppers. In Vaelmoor, that was the going rate for hope, and hope was the only commodity I sold that wasn't technically contraband. Knots aren't magic. Everyone knows knots aren't magic. A knotted cord is a craft good, same as a carved spoon or a crocheted shawl, and if the men who bought them came back with fuller nets and unbroken bones and stories about how the fire had gone around their house like water around a stone — well. Craftsmanship.

The temple district smelled of fish smoke and incense fighting each other for ownership of the air, as it always did on audit day. I walked back along Pilgrim's Row past the shuttered apothecary — third one this season; the witch-hunters had taken the owner in for "questioning" two weeks ago and the shop had been empty since, though someone still swept the step every morning, which broke my heart in a small, specific way — and up the worn steps of the Scriptorium of Saint Vess, where I had spent six years of my life paying for a crime I hadn't committed.

Well. Not exactly six years. Six years, four months, and eleven days, because when you're bound by debt you learn to count the way other people count stars.

* * *

The Scriptorium of Saint Vess was the largest of the temple houses in Vaelmoor, which meant it held the ledgers nobody else wanted: shipping debts, guild tithes, indenture contracts, the great slow ledger of the city's obligations, copied and recopied by hands like mine until the ink wore grooves into the paper. Debt was holy work, according to the provosts. Every contract was a covenant. Every covenant was a prayer written in numbers.

I had been copying contracts since I was seventeen years old. I could tell you the standard clauses in my sleep — the term of service, the interest schedule, the forfeiture provisions, the bit about the debtor's heirs being bound in perpetuity unless discharged by act of the crown. I could recite the boilerplate the way priests recite liturgy, which I suppose made me a kind of priest. A priest of the god Nobody Gets Out.

"Ashgrove." Provost Merrin stood in the doorway of the copy hall, holding her audit ledger against her chest like a shield. She was a narrow woman with ink-stained cuffs and the permanent expression of someone who had once hoped for something and gotten bookkeeping instead. "You're wanted in Records. Audit."

"Whose records?"

"Yours."

My pen stopped moving. Around me, forty other scribes kept writing — the scratch of quills in that room sounded like rain on a roof, constant, indifferent — but my own hand had gone strange and light.

"An audit of my contract?" I said. "It's been audited twice. Once at induction, once at the five-year review. There's nothing—"

"There's an amendment," Merrin said, and her voice did something careful. "Come on, then. Don't make it worse by standing there looking like that."

Like what? I didn't ask. I set down my pen, squared the page I'd been working on so the next scribe wouldn't curse my name, and followed her down the corridor toward Records, and the whole way I told myself the thing you tell yourself when the floor tilts: *it's nothing, it's a clerical error, it's a misfiled page.* The temple made mistakes. The temple made mistakes constantly, in its vast indifferent way, the way the sea makes waves.

Records was a long room on the ground floor, windowless, lit by lamps that burned behind glass because paper and open flame have a marriage that always ends in divorce. The contract archive ran floor to ceiling, ten thousand folios in their gray bindings, and somewhere in row fourteen lived the Ashgrove debt, my family's debt, incurred the year I was nine years old.

I want to be precise here, because precision is the only inheritance my mother left me. When I was nine, the temple physicians diagnosed my father with lung-rot from the dye-works where he'd worked since boyhood. Treatment cost more than a dyer earned in ten years. The temple lent the money against the family — this was standard, this was mercy, everyone said so — and when my father died anyway, eighteen months later, the debt passed to my mother, and when my mother was arrested for sorcery four years after that, the debt passed to me. That was also standard. The sins of the parents, amortized quarterly.

The archivist on duty — a young man named Fennick with the complexion of someone raised entirely underground — fetched the folio without meeting my eyes. That was the first wrong note. Archivists meet your eyes. They're proud of their work. This one handled my contract like it was already evidence.

Merrin opened it on the reading table and turned to the amendment page, and there it was.

Amendments happen. People renegotiate terms, extend service, add collateral. But amendments get signatures. An amendment without a signature is just vandalism with good handwriting, and this one had very good handwriting — a clean, formal secretary hand, the kind they teach in the palace chancery, not the temple's rounder style. The clause itself sat in the middle of the page like a stone in a shoe:

*"In consideration of continued forbearance, the debtor's term of service is hereby extended indefinitely upon completion of the original term, the debtor to be conveyed to such custody as the creditor's assign may direct, per royal warrant 44-C."*

Royal warrant. Palace chancery. Conveyed to custody.

"Who amended this?" I asked. My voice came out level, which surprised me. Underneath the levelness, something had begun to move slowly, the way ice moves on a river — too slow to see, too certain to ignore.

"The seal is the crown's countersignature office," Merrin said. "Filed through the temple's legal bureau. Which means—" she stopped herself, which was unlike her. Merrin finished sentences the way other people finish meals.

"Which means it went through channels. Someone approved it. Someone signed off on routing it into my file without—" I touched the margin where my signature should have been. Blank. The blank space was somehow louder than any signature would have been. "Without asking me."

"You're a debtor," Fennick said quietly, from his corner. "Debtors aren't asked. They're notified."

"Notified," I repeated. "When?"

"The notice would have been posted to your lodging."

"I lodge in the dormitory wing. Of this temple."

Silence. Merrin closed the folio with both hands, gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "The audit found it during routine review," she said. "Routine review happens every quarter. This amendment was filed—" she checked, though she clearly knew, "—eleven months ago."

Eleven months. Eleven months of me walking these corridors, copying other people's covenants, sleeping twenty feet from the room where my own had been quietly rewritten. Eleven months of some clerk in the chancery knowing what clause waited in my file like a hook waiting in dark water.

"What does 'such custody as the creditor's assign may direct' mean?" I asked. "In plain language. You're the provost. Translate it."

Merrin looked at me for a long moment, and I saw her decide something — decide how much truth a debtor could be given, which is to say, how much truth the temple could afford to let me carry.

"It means," she said, "that when your original term completes — and by my count that's in fifty-one days — you don't go free. You go wherever the crown tells the temple to send you."

"Fifty-one days," I said.

"Fifty-one days."

I thought about Tam Wicker and his knotted cord, three coppers for hope. I thought about the swept step of the empty apothecary. I thought about my mother, who had been taken in chains when I was thirteen, for the crime of lighting candles that stayed lit, and how the last thing she said to me was *count everything, Lena, count everything twice.*

I had counted. Twice. And the arithmetic said I had fifty-one days of being a person left.

"Thank you for the audit," I said, and my voice was still level, and I hated it for that.

* * *

That evening I sat in the copy hall after hours, which was against the rules, doing the only thing I knew how to do with fear, which was to put it in order. I pulled my own file's history — every transfer record, every routing slip, every bureaucratic fingerprint the amendment had left on its way to my folder. Paper leaves tracks. Everything leaves tracks, if you know how to read it. My mother taught me that too, though she taught it with candle flames instead of ink.

The trail was clean. Too clean. Transfers usually accumulate smudges — a clerk's correction here, a refiled date there. Mine had none. Whoever moved my contract through the chancery had done it in a single pass, with authority, with care. This wasn't a clerk's error. This was a plan wearing a clerk's coat.

And there was one more thing, the thing I kept turning over like a coin I couldn't identify. The clause referenced *royal warrant 44-C*. So, being me, I went to the cross-index and looked up 44-C.

Warrant 44-C wasn't listed under its number. It was listed under a category heading I'd never seen used in six years of archival work: *Conveyances, Special — Sealed by Order of the Crown, Access Restricted.*

Restricted access in a debt archive. Think about that. Somewhere in this city, someone had classified my future alongside state secrets — and beneath the entry, in the column where the index recorded the number of documents filed under the heading, the figure was small. Seven.

Seven conveyances, special, sealed. Seven people in Vaelmoor whose contracts had been quietly rewritten to deliver them, body and obedient soul, to whatever waited at the end of a royal warrant.

I sat in the lamplight with the index open on my knees and felt the dread settle into its final shape — not the hot panic of a moment, but the cold patient weight of a pattern. My mother had been taken for small magics. The apothecary had been taken for unknown reasons. The gifted were disappearing into the palace and not coming out, and now I knew that the machinery didn't only hunt us in the streets.

It bought us. Neatly. In advance. With paperwork.

Outside the high windows, the temple bells rang the hour, and I marked my place in the index, and I began — because counting twice was all I had — to count the days.

Fifty-one.

Fifty days, if the bells were right, and the bells were always right, and that was the problem with living in a city built on covenants: everything was exact except the trap.
