## 2. Ash on the Wind

The proclamation went up on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Catastrophes ought to arrive on Sundays, when the temples were full and the bells were already swinging. Instead it came on a workday morning, pasted to the customs house in neat black letters while dockworkers jostled for a look and fishwives pretended not to.

I read it twice, because the first time my eyes refused to cooperate.

*By order of His Majesty, let it be known that all persons possessing the tainted gift shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those who comply shall be examined and, if found harmless, permitted to live under the crown's watchful mercy. Those who conceal themselves shall be hunted as enemies of the realm.*

Watchful mercy. I'd have laughed if my mouth hadn't gone dry as old paper. There was a particular kind of cruelty in language like that — the kind that made men nod along, thinking *well, if they've nothing to hide.* I'd seen that logic before, in smaller rooms, with smaller stakes. It never ended with anyone being left alone.

"Register," muttered the dockworker beside me, a broad man with rope-scarred hands. "Like a dog at the kennel."

"Or be hunted," said his companion, and spat.

They said it lightly, the way you say things that don't belong to you yet. That's how it always starts. The words are somebody else's problem until one morning they're yours.

I pulled my hood up and walked home the long way, which is what I do when I want to think and also when I want to make sure no one is following me. The long way took me past the harbor, where the gulls were screaming over something dead, and past the chandler's shop with its windows full of tallow candles, and past the little shrine to the Tide-Mother where an old woman was lighting a stub of wax and praying with her lips moving too fast to mean any of it.

Thirty days. I had thirty days to decide whether to hand myself to the crown and hope their *watchful mercy* was more than ink, or to run, or to do something stupider than either.

Here's the thing about hiding a gift in Vaelmoor: everyone hides something. The baker waters his flour. The harbor master takes his cut. Half the city owes money it can't pay and the other half pretends not to notice. Hiding is our civic religion. But there's a difference between hiding a debt and hiding a thing that gets you hanged, and the difference is that debts can be renegotiated.

My gift couldn't. Believe me, I'd tried. When I was nine I spent a whole winter trying to convince myself the warmth in my palms was just good circulation. When I was twelve I tried to give it away — stood at the Tide-Mother's shrine and prayed for her to take it back, whatever bargain my grandmother had struck, whatever blood-debt ran down our line like water through a crack in a wall. She didn't take it. Gods rarely do. They just leave the door open so you know they could have.

So I did what Ashgroves have always done: I made it small. Little charms, harmless ones, sold quietly to people who needed luck more than they needed doctrine. A knotted thread to keep a sailor's rope from fraying. A pinch of ash in a locket to keep a fever down. Nothing that would raise the dead or topple kings. Just enough magic to get through a Tuesday.

And now the crown wanted a list of every name.

* * *

Maren Kettle's shop sat on Candle Lane, wedged between a cobbler and a house that had been empty since the fever year. She sold charms too — bigger ones than mine, flashier, love-knots and weather-bells and little glass amulets she swore could turn aside a knife. I'd always thought her prices were outrageous and her craft was mostly theater. Half her stock probably worked exactly as well as any merchant's trinket, which is to say: not at all, but beautifully.

I was three streets away when I smelled the smoke.

You learn smoke in this city. Cooking smoke sits low and greasy. Chimney smoke rises straight. This smoke climbed thick and black and wrong, and by the time I rounded the corner onto Candle Lane I could see why: Maren's shop was burning from the inside out, flames licking through the windows like something hungry that had finally been invited to dinner.

There was a crowd, because there's always a crowd. They stood in the loose half-circle that crowds form around other people's disasters, close enough to feel the heat, far enough to be safe from blame. And in front of them, unhurried, stood four men in the grey-and-gold of the crown's new hunters, watching the fire with the patience of men who had all day and the authority to take it.

One of them held a ledger.

I stopped at the edge of the lane, heart going like a trapped bird, and made myself look. Made myself watch. Because I knew — I knew before he opened the book — what was written there.

"Hold this," the hunter with the ledger said to another, handing it over, and walked to the crowd. He picked a woman near the front — ordinary-looking, shawl over her head, the kind of face you forget in a market queue. "You bought from this establishment?"

"I—" Her eyes flicked to the burning shop, to the hunters, back. "A weather-bell. For my husband's boat. Last autumn."

"Name?"

She gave it. He wrote it down. Slowly. Letting her watch him write it. Then he looked up with a smile that had never once reached anyone's eyes, and said, "Thank you. Remain available for examination."

She didn't say anything. Neither did anyone else. That was the worst part — the silence, the whole lane holding its breath, forty people watching a woman's name go into a book and nobody saying *this is madness*, because saying so would put your own name one page closer.

Where had they gotten the names? That was the question I turned over and over on the walk home, walking fast, hood up, telling myself the cold sweat on my neck was just the wind off the harbor. Not from Maren. Maren wouldn't give up her customers; she'd sooner burn herself, and maybe that was exactly what had happened here, except the hunters hadn't looked angry enough for arson, only satisfied. No — someone had sold records. Or someone had traced the goods. Charms carry signatures, if you know how to read them. My mother taught me that much before they took her: *every working leaves a fingerprint, Lena. Make yours small. Make yours boring.*

I had made mine small. I had made mine boring. But I had also, over six years, sold two hundred and eleven charms to two hundred and eleven people who trusted me, and every single knot I tied carried the same quiet signature underneath, the same warm hum of Ashgrove craft, and somewhere in the last month someone had learned to listen for it.

They didn't need my name. They just needed to follow the thread backward, charm by charm, customer by customer, until the thread ended at a scribe in a temple scriptorium with ink-stained fingers and a family debt and a secret she'd kept alive for twenty years.

I got home. I barred the door. I sat on my bed in the grey afternoon light and looked at the loose floorboard under the window, and I thought about what was under it.

* * *

Under the floorboard: a cedar box, and in the cedar box, wrapped in oilcloth, the remains of my trade. Fourteen finished charms. A spool of red thread. A bundle of dried rowan. Three blank lockets waiting to be filled. Six years of careful, patient, stupid work, sitting ten feet below a pair of hunters' boots.

I told myself I had time. Thirty days was a long time. Hunters started with the loud ones, the flashy ones, the Marens of the world with their glass knives-turned amulets. A scribe who sold fever-lockets wouldn't even make the middle of the list.

Then I remembered the ledger man's smile, and the way he'd written the weather-bell woman's name slowly, letting her watch, and I understood that the list wasn't the point. The list was the *message*. Every name in that book was a thread, and every thread led somewhere, and the crown had decided — for reasons I couldn't yet see — that it wanted all the threads gathered into one hand.

I pulled up the floorboard.

The cedar box came up heavy and familiar, smelling of oilcloth and the faint sweet rot of dried herbs. I set it on the bed and unwrapped the top layer and there they were, my fourteen little lies: the sailor's knots, the fever-lockets, a birch-bark band I'd charmed against nightmares for a child whose father beat her, and whom I'd never charged a coin. Each one still held a trace of warmth when I touched it, the way a stone holds the day's sun after dark. My fingerprints, all of them. Two hundred and eleven threads leading back to me, and these fourteen the brightest.

Burn them. That was the sensible thing. Burn them, burn the thread, burn the spool of red cord and the rowan and the lockets, scatter the ash in the harbor, and become exactly what my contract said I was: a temple scribe, dull as dishwater, no gift worth registering, nothing to see here, move along.

I built the fire up in my hearth until it roared. I fed it the red thread first, then the rowan, then the lockets, one by one, watching each charm's little warmth gutter out as the flame took it. It should have felt like freedom. It felt like cutting off fingers to save the hand, and doing it slowly, so you had time to count.

Fourteen charms became thirteen became twelve. Somewhere around eight, my hands started shaking, and I told myself it was the heat.

Then I reached the bottom of the box, and found the one I couldn't explain.

It was a kestrel. Carved from river-worn bone, no bigger than my thumb, wings folded as if about to spring. I knew every charm in that box. I could have named the buyer, the price, the hour I'd tied each knot. This one I couldn't. I turned it over and over in my palm and searched my memory and found nothing — no sale, no carving session, no evening when I'd sat whittling bone by candlelight. It was in my handwriting, so to speak: the working in it was mine, unmistakably, that same warm Ashgrove hum. But I had no memory of making it.

And it was warm. Warmer than the others. Warm as a living thing, sitting in my cupped hands on a night when the fire had burned down to coals.

I held it over the hearth. The coals brightened. The kestrel sat in my palm, and the heat of the fire bent around it — I swear this, though no one would believe me — bent around it the way water bends around a stone in a stream, and the little bird did not so much as darken.

I tried twice more. The second time, a coal actually flared *away* from my hand, rolling to the edge of the grate like a dog that's been kicked.

"Well," I said aloud, to the empty room, because talking to yourself is free and everything else in Vaelmoor costs money. "That's new."

Twenty years of keeping my gift small, and here was a charm refusing to burn, carrying a working I didn't remember casting, shaped like a hunting bird. My grandmother used to say the gift knows things before we do — that it ties knots in the dark that our waking minds never see. I'd always filed that under old women's comfort, right next to *the Tide-Mother loves you* and *the fever will pass.*

Now I sat on the floor of my rented room with a box of ash and one unburnable bird, and I thought about my contract. About the audit last week, the amended clause, the neat addition in ink I'd never signed. *Delivered unto the crown's appointed use.* I'd told myself it was clerical error. Bureaucracies make mistakes; that's most of what they make.

But bureaucracies don't amend contracts in secret. Someone does. Someone with access to the temple ledgers, someone who wanted Lena Ashgrove — specifically, particularly, by name — delivered unto the crown's appointed use. And now the crown was sweeping the city for gifts, tracing charm-signatures through the streets, gathering every thread into one hand.

Maybe it wasn't a sweep. Maybe it was a net, and maybe the shape of the net fit one person exactly.

I looked at the kestrel a long time. Outside, the bells of the temple district rang the evening hours, and somewhere down the street a child was crying, and the smell of Maren Kettle's shop still hung in the air like a question nobody would answer.

The sensible thing was to throw the bird in the harbor. Sink it, bury it, be rid of the one object in Vaelmoor that could hang me twice over. The sensible thing was to register, grovel, pass whatever examination they gave, and spend the rest of my life as a registered curiosity under the crown's *watchful mercy*.

I closed my hand around the kestrel. Its warmth pulsed against my palm, steady as a second heartbeat, patient as something that had been waiting a long time for me to catch up.

"No," I said. To the bird, to the room, to whoever had written that clause in my contract. "You don't get to choose for me."

Fear is a strange fuel. It burns cold, and it lasts. I banked the hearth, buried the ashes of my trade in the garden where they'd never be found, and put the kestrel in the pocket over my heart, where I could feel it with every breath.

Thirty days. Let them hunt.

I had work to do.
