## 3. The Hunter's Oath

There are three things every witch-hunter in Vaelmoor learns before he's allowed to carry the silver pin: how to spot a lie, how to break a door, and how to write a report that makes an innocent man look guilty. They teach you the first two honestly. The third they teach you by example, which is to say you learn it by watching your betters do it and saying nothing.

I had been saying nothing for six years. I was very good at it. If silence were a gift, I'd have been burned at my own stake by now.

* * *

The child's name was Tam Wicker, and he was eight years old, and he had no gift whatsoever.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is all I have left of that morning. He had no gift. Not a small one, not a flickering one, not one of those damp little talents — finding lost buttons, souring milk when sulky — that the Purification Office liked to round up for quotas. Nothing. His grandmother had been a hedge-witch of modest reputation, dead eleven years, and someone in his village had remembered this and written to the palace, because in Vaelmoor a dead woman's bloodline was still a crime you could report, and reporting it earned you a stipend of two crowns a month.

Two crowns a month. That was the going rate for a neighbor's soul. I've often thought we should print it on the coin itself, save everyone the paperwork.

Tam sat across the interrogation table from me with his feet not quite touching the floor, swinging them in the way children do when they don't yet know that the room they're in is the last room some people ever see. He had a gap where his left canine should have been and a scab on his knee shaped, if you squinted, like nothing at all.

"Say the words again," I told him. Gently. I always started gently; it was the only part of the job I could do without flinching.

"The words." He recited them the way children recite anything, in a bored sing-song: "By ash and ember, by salt and stone, what sleeps in me must now be known."

The detection candle between us stayed exactly as it was. Grey wax, ordinary flame. If there'd been so much as a drop of gift in him, the flame would have gone green as pond water. It didn't. It just burned, doing the one honest thing in the building.

"And the charm," I said. "Show me."

He dug in his pocket and produced a knotted cord, frayed, the kind mothers made before the ban — a knot-charm against nightmares. Half the knots were wrong. A child had tied half of them himself, probably copying from memory after his mother was taken, or maybe she'd been taken later, I didn't know, I hadn't read the file that closely because reading files too closely was another thing that made the job unbearable.

"It doesn't work," Tam said. "It's just string."

"It's just string," I agreed.

"So am I, then?" He looked up at me with the terrible directness of children. "The man said I'm not. The man said the string proves I'm hiding it. But it's *my* string. I tied it."

The man he meant was Sergeant Halloway, who had conducted the preliminary interview and whose reports had a talent for making "it's just string" read as "the subject concealed evidence." Halloway was up for a commendation. Halloway always seemed to be up for a commendation around the same time the monthly numbers came in thin.

"No," I said. "You're not hiding anything."

His whole body changed. I've seen that change before and I'll see it again until they hang me or promote me, whichever comes first — the way a child goes from braced-for-the-worst to simply *present*, all at once, like a fist unclenching. He swung his feet. He asked me if I'd ever seen the sea. I said yes. He said his gran promised to take him but then she died, and did I think the sea minded, being promised to people who never came?

"I think the sea has better things to remember," I said, which was a lie, because I think the sea remembers everything, and so do I, that's the curse of my particular constitution, and it would remember a boy with a gap-toothed grin asking about tides in a room designed to end him.

I wrote the report myself. *Subject displays no detectable gift. Charm is inert craftwork, non-magical, likely sentimental. Recommend release and removal from registry.* I wrote it in my cleanest hand, the hand they'd praised at my oath-taking, and I signed it, and I walked it down to Halloway's desk personally, because I wanted to watch his face when he read it.

Halloway's face did nothing. That was the worst part. He skimmed it, stamped it, and said, "Shame. Good family line, that one. Could've been useful down the road."

"Useful how?"

"You know how." He smiled with the bottom half of his face. "Vessels don't grow on trees, Vale."

I didn't know what he meant. I want that on the record, wherever records go when regimes fall: I did not know what he meant. I knew only that the sentence slid into me like a splinter and sat there, working its way deeper, in a place I couldn't reach to dig out.

* * *

My commander was named Aldous Crane, and he received petitioners in an office that smelled of ink and lilies, because he believed fear should be administered pleasantly. He was not a cruel man, which I want stated clearly, because cruelty would have been simpler. Crane was a decent man doing monstrous arithmetic, and every day the sums came out the same, and every day he signed them, and every night he slept, and I genuinely believe he slept well. That frightened me more than any fanatic would have. Fanatics burn out. Decent men endure.

"Vale." He didn't look up from the ledger. "Wicker boy. Clean report."

"Cleaner than most."

"Mm." He turned a page. "You've filed four clean reports this quarter. Do you know what that tells me?"

"That the registry's full of neighbors with grudges?"

Crane looked up then, and his eyes were tired and kind and absolutely immovable, the way a wall is kind right up until you walk into it. "It tells me you're developing a reputation. And reputations, in this office, are either currency or debt. You'd prefer yours stay currency."

"I'd prefer," I said, "that we stop measuring ourselves in children."

A pause. Somewhere below us, in the yard, recruits were drilling door-breaks on a practice frame, and the sound came up through the floor like a heartbeat with the wrong rhythm.

"Sit down, Corin." My Christian name. He used it rarely and always as a weapon wrapped in a blanket. "You took the Oath six years ago. Do you remember it?"

*I swear by crown and candle to seek out the gifted, to bind what binds the realm, to hold the line between Vaelmoor and the dark that wore our kings—*

"I remember it."

"Do you remember why?" He closed the ledger at last. "Because I do. I was there, the year after the coronation, when the old king's court went quiet. When ministers stopped disagreeing. When the realm nearly stopped breathing because one man on one throne had stopped being one man. You were a boy. You don't remember the silence. I do. Everything we do — every door, every registry, every wretched candle — is so that never happens again. So tell me the hunt is ugly. I'll agree with you. Tell me it's unjust. Some days I'll agree with that too. But don't tell me it's unnecessary, because I watched the alternative, and the alternative wears crowns."

He let that sit. Outside the window, Vaelmoor went about its business, smoke rising from ten thousand chimneys, none of it green.

"Now." He slid a folded order across the desk. "Temple district. The Ashgrove debt-scribes. Audit turned up irregularities — charms moving through the district with temple provenance, which shouldn't be possible, since the temples surrendered their licensed charmers years ago. Someone down there is selling under the ledger. Find them. Quietly, if you can. Loudly, if you must."

"A scribe," I said. "Debt-bonded. That's the great threat to the realm?"

"The great threat," Crane said, "is always small until it isn't. Go."

I took the order. At the door I stopped, because there was a question sitting in my chest and it had been sitting there since Halloway's smile, and I found, to my surprise, that I was going to ask it.

"Commander. What did Halloway mean? About vessels."

Crane's pen paused above the ledger. Just a moment. Then it moved on, steady as ever.

"Ask him yourself," he said, "if it matters to you. Though I'd caution you, Corin, that in this office, curiosity is a debt like any other. It gets called in eventually."

* * *

That night I did something I hadn't done in years. I took out the Oath.

Not the spoken one — anyone can recite that, I'd heard recruits mumble it hungover at dawn ceremonies a hundred times. The written one. Every hunter signs a copy at induction, and the copy goes into the archive, and you're given a keepsake leaf, a single page in the Commander's own hand, because tradition holds that a hunter should keep his oath where he can see it.

Mine lived at the bottom of my kit bag, under spare shirt-straps and a whetstone, face down, the way you keep things you can't throw away and can't look at.

I spread it on my table beside the lamp. *I swear by crown and candle...* The ink had faded to brown. Six years of being carried rather than read will do that.

*To bind what binds the realm.*

I'd always read that clause as poetry. Binding the gifted, binding the danger — fine, ugly work, but work with a shape. Sitting there with the lamp hissing, I read it differently. *What binds the realm.* As though the realm were already bound. As though the Oath's authors had known something, five generations back, that we'd all agreed to forget.

And beneath it, in smaller script, the part nobody quoted at ceremonies: *and to hold the line between Vaelmoor and the dark that wore our kings.*

Kings. Plural.

I sat with that for a long time. Long enough for the lamp to gutter once. Long enough for the temple bells to ring the midnight hour across the rooftops, three districts away, faint as memory.

Here is what I will say about conscience: it doesn't crack all at once, whatever the ballads claim. It cracks the way ice on a pond cracks — a sound here, a seam there, long stretches where nothing seems to move at all, and you almost convince yourself the surface will hold. And then one day you're standing in the middle of it, over deep black water, and you hear the next crack come up through your boots, and you understand that the question was never whether the ice would break.

The question was what you'd be carrying when it did.

I folded the Oath along its old creases. I put it back in the bag. Face down. Old habits.

But I didn't sleep, and near dawn I got up and dressed, and I took the temple-district order off my desk, and I read it again. *Charms moving through the district with temple provenance.* Someone selling luck to dockworkers and love-tokens to shopgirls, harmless as bread, illegal as treason. Some debt-bound scribe with clever hands and a death wish.

Every instinct I'd been trained with said: find her, pin her, file her.

I told myself I was simply doing my job. I told myself I'd assess first and arrest second, and that assessment was due diligence, and due diligence was professionalism, and professionalism was a virtue. I told myself all of it while I buckled on my kit, and every word of it rang false as a detection candle in a guilty room.

The truth was simpler and worse. After Tam Wicker and his string, after *vessels don't grow on trees*, I needed to see what the other side of this war looked like up close. I needed to know whether the people I hunted were monsters, or mirrors.

The fog was coming off the canal when I crossed into the temple district, grey and thick, swallowing the bell towers whole. Somewhere ahead, a market was waking — crates knocking, gulls arguing, a fishwife singing something obscene about a bishop. An ordinary city, getting on with its morning.

And somewhere in it, a scribe with smuggled charms was about to have the worst kind of visitor.

I checked the address on the order, turned toward the Temple of Ledgers, and went to meet her.
