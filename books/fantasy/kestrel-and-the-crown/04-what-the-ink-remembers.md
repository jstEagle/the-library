## 4. What the Ink Remembers

There is a particular smell to old ink that no one warns you about when they hand you your first quill. It's not the sharp bite of fresh ink — that's just vinegar and soot pretending to be important. Old ink smells like a cellar where something once lived. Like paper remembering it was ever a tree. After six years in Vaelmoor's temple scriptorium, I can tell you the age of a document by scent alone, which is exactly the sort of useless talent that gets you nothing but more documents.

I was copying tithe ledgers on the third of Frostmonth, which is to say I was copying numbers that said poor people had paid what poor people owed, into a book that would sit in a cellar until the poor people were dead and the numbers meant nothing. This is scribe work. You take the world as it was and you make a tidy copy of it, and if the world was ugly, well, that's not the copyist's problem. My mother taught me my letters; the temple taught me to keep them small and even and asking no questions.

I had copied four hundred entries and was beginning to fantasize about drowning myself in the inkwell — a clean death, at least, and the blotting paper would make a decent shroud — when I noticed the gap.

Not a gap in the ledger. A gap in the *binding*.

Here's the thing about temple records: they are bound by monks who have nothing else to do with their hands and all eternity to do it with. The stitching is perfect. Uniform. Every gathering of pages the same thickness, every signature sewn with the same tension, because Brother Halvard has been doing it for forty years and considers a crooked stitch a personal insult to the gods. So when my thumb passed over the spine of Ledger 1189–1194 and felt a signature slightly thicker than its neighbors, my body knew before my mind did. The way you know someone's standing behind you. The way you know a stair has one step too many.

I set down my quill. I looked around the scriptorium — nine desks, eight occupied, everyone bent over their work like penitents, which most of them were — and then I did the thing I'm apparently constitutionally incapable of not doing.

I opened the spine.

* * *

Let me explain what I expected to find, because I'd like it on record that I am not, generally speaking, an idiot. I expected water damage. Rats. A page torn out by some careless reader and replaced with filler stock by a binder who assumed no one would count. The mundane explanations were lined up and waiting, respectable as mourners at a funeral.

What I found was craftsmanship.

The excision wasn't a tear. It was a *rebuild*. Someone had removed pages from the middle of the ledger — I counted the stubs under the new stitching, seven of them, cut close and clean — and then inserted replacement leaves written in a period-appropriate hand, aged appropriately, stained appropriately, carrying entries that flowed seamlessly from the page before to the page after. Dates matched. Ink matched. Even the little marginal doodles — Brother Ellery's habit of sketching ducks in the margins when he thought no one was looking — continued without interruption across the seam.

Except.

Except that whoever had done the forgery hadn't known about the ducks. Or rather, they'd known *of* them but not *how*. Real Ellery ducks are terrible — lopsided, three-legged, drawn with the contempt of a man who hates his own hand. The forged ducks were graceful. Confident. Ducks drawn by someone who could actually draw, which Ellery never could, which was rather the point of him.

Someone had spent weeks — months, maybe — reconstructing five years of temple records, and they had been very good, and they had been undone by waterfowl.

I sat there with my heart going like a dockworker's hammer and did the arithmetic. Ledger 1189 through 1194. Five years. And what happened in the kingdom five years ago, in the year the forgery began?

The king was crowned.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because later — much later — people would ask me when I first understood, and the truthful answer is: I didn't understand anything. I noticed a pattern the way you notice a draft. Cold air moving somewhere it shouldn't. I didn't know yet what door stood open or what was coming through it. But I did know this: temples do not forge their own records over duck-related oversights. Temples forge records because the truth would cost someone something, and in Vaelmoor, the only someones who could make the temple spend months on a forgery wore crowns or spoke directly to those who did.

So I did what any sensible person would do.

I closed the ledger, smoothed my skirts, and spent the rest of the day copying tithes with the serene blankness of a woman who has never had an original thought in her life.

And then I came back after dark.

* * *

The scriptorium at night is a different country. By day it smells of tallow and discipline; by night, of cold stone and the ghost of all that vinegar-ink, and the silence has weight, like snowfall. I knew how to move through it because I'd been sneaking through the temple since I was nine years old — first to steal candle ends, later to practice small magics in the archive cellar where the wards were thinnest, always careful, always hungry. Six years of legitimate service layered over twelve years of illegitimate survival. The debt-contract says I serve the temple until the Ashgrove account is settled. It does not say what I serve it *as*, and I've found that ambiguity to be load-bearing.

I took Ledger 1189–1194 back to my desk, lit a single shielded candle, and read.

Reading a forged record against its neighbors is like listening to a musician play another composer's work note-perfect but phrase-wrong. Everything was *there*. Grain deliveries. Roof repairs. Alms distributed at the Feast of the Veil. Funeral stipends — a lot of funeral stipends, actually, clustered oddly in the second year, but I filed that away the way you file away a sound in the walls. And threaded through it all, the ordinary devotional entries: masses sung, relics dusted, the king's health prayed for.

That last one stopped me. Not because it was there — prayers for the king's health appear in every ledger since the monarchy was invented, probably — but because of the *phrasing*. In the years before the coronation, the standard entry reads: *Prayed for the health of His Majesty King Edvard and the royal line.* Standard formula, used for decades. You can find it in a hundred ledgers.

In the forged section, it reads: *Prayed for the health of His Majesty the King.*

No name. No royal line. Just "the King," the way you'd write about a stranger. About an office rather than a man.

And here's the detail that made the hair on my arms stand up and introduce themselves individually: the omission wasn't consistent. Twice in five years, the forger slipped. Twice, buried among hundreds of entries, the name appeared — *King Edvard* — in the same confident hand as everything else, and both times, someone had caught it. Both times, the name had been scraped off the parchment with a blade so fine and the surface re-polished so skillfully that you'd never see it unless you held the page sideways to the candle and looked for the shine.

Two ghosts of a name, in five years of careful erasure.

Which means the forger wasn't working from malice or laziness. They were working from *fear*. Somewhere above them sat a person who checked, who caught, who made them scrape and polish and start again. A chain of command with terror at the top of it. And whatever King Edvard had been — whether he'd died, abdicated, or simply stopped existing in any way records needed to acknowledge — the temple had been ordered to forget him so thoroughly that forgetting became a full-time occupation.

I copied out the two scraped names' locations, the dates of the funeral-stipend cluster, and the exact wording of the prayer entries, in cipher, on a scrap I could swallow if necessary. Then I put everything back exactly as I'd found it, resewn spine facing the wall, and walked back to my cell feeling two things at once, which is my natural condition but rarely this sharply:

The thrill — God forgive me, the *thrill*. Twelve years of being small and careful and quiet, and here was a thread, an actual loose thread in the seamless garment of the world, and I had found it by touch. For one giddy hour I was not a debt-scribe or a charm-hocker or a girl with a hanging offense folded up inside her ribs. I was the only person in Vaelmoor who knew the ducks were wrong.

And underneath the thrill, cold as cellar stone: the last person who pulled a thread like this probably wasn't scraping names off parchment. They were probably *in* the parchment. People who erase kings do not tend to leave witnesses lying around with their ciphers and their candle-shields.

I told myself I'd stop. I want that noted too. I lay in my narrow bed and told myself, firmly, the way you tell a dog to stay: *Lena. Drop it. You have a contract, a hidden gift, and a face that fits conveniently into crowds. You cannot afford curiosity.*

I lasted four days.

* * *

On the fourth day I went looking for the coronation itself, because if five years of records had been rebuilt, the logical question is: rebuilt *from* what? Forgeries need sources. Somewhere in the temple there had to be original material — guest lists, rite orders, the ceremonial ledger every coronation generates — unless someone had burned it, and if they'd burned it, there'd be a record of *that*, because the temple records even its sins, lovingly, in triplicate.

I didn't find the coronation materials. What I found was the shelf where they should have been.

Archive Vault C, third gallery, between the ordination rolls and the plague-year mortality books. The catalogue — and the catalogue, notably, had *not* been touched, which was either arrogance or oversight — listed eleven items. The shelf held eleven items. Every one of them was a decoy: plausible-looking documents in plausible-looking bindings, all of them, when I examined them closely, written on paper of the wrong era. Too new, artificially aged. The same aging technique as the ledger forgery. Someone had built an entire shelf of lies, and the only reason I could tell was that I'd spent the previous week learning the forger's handwriting the way you learn a lover's face.

Eleven items. Eleven empty promises. And a gap in the dust on the shelf behind them, a rectangle of clean wood where something box-shaped had sat for years and been removed recently enough that the dust hadn't finished reclaiming it.

I was crouched there with my finger in the clean rectangle, doing the thing where you think *one more minute* for the eleventh consecutive minute, when the light changed.

Not dramatically. No thunderclap. Just a shadow falling across the gallery floor where no shadow should have been, and the particular quality of silence that means someone else has stopped breathing.

"Curator Ashgrove."

High Scribe Maren. Of course. Of course it was her. Maren Voss, keeper of the temple's records, a woman so dry that scholars consulted her about desiccation. She stood at the end of the gallery holding a lamp she had evidently carried down four flights of stairs for no reason whatsoever, because the vault lamps were lit, and we both knew it.

"High Scribe." I rose, brushed off my knees, and deployed the expression I'd perfected by age fourteen: pleasant, vacant, faintly apologetic about existing. "Forgive me. I was cross-referencing the mortality rolls for the alms audit. The catalogue misfiled—"

"The catalogue," Maren said, "is accurate."

She crossed the gallery at her usual pace, which is to say the pace of continental drift, and stopped beside me. She did not look at the shelf. She looked at me, and her eyes in the lamplight were the color of old varnish, and I realized I had made a serious tactical error, which was assuming that a woman who maintained five years of forged records wouldn't also maintain surveillance on the one scribe with clever hands and a history.

"You've been in Ledger 1189," she said. Not a question.

"I copy what I'm assigned."

"You've been in Ledger 1189," she repeated, "and Vault C, and I would very much like to hear you say that you found nothing, so that we can both proceed with our lives knowing precisely what that statement is worth."

The wry part of me — the part that narrates my disasters — observed that this was the moment to lie brilliantly. The rest of me, the part that had spent twelve years reading people the way I read ledgers, understood that Maren Voss had not come down here to catch me. If she'd wanted me caught, there'd be hunters at my back and irons on my wrists. She'd come alone, at dusk, carrying a lamp she didn't need.

She'd come to find out how much I knew. Or possibly — and this thought arrived with a sensation like stepping off a stair that isn't there — to find out how much I *was*.

"High Scribe," I said carefully, "the ducks are wrong."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not surprise. Something older and wearier than surprise — the look of a woman watching a fire she'd been assured was contained jump the firebreak.

"Brother Ellery draws with his left hand now," she said quietly. "Arthritis. He hasn't sketched in three years." A pause. "You will stop asking questions, Curator Ashgrove. Not because I doubt your discretion, though I do. Because the last person who asked these questions was Brother Loras, who kept the Vault C catalogue, and who retired very suddenly to a monastery in the eastern provinces, from which he has never once written to us. Monks write constantly. It's practically the whole job."

"And if I don't stop?"

"Then the next audit of your family's debt," Maren said, "may discover certain irregularities in the Ashgrove account. Irregularities can extend a term of service indefinitely. Irregularities can, in extreme cases, transfer a contract to... other parties." She lifted the lamp slightly, and the light caught the clean rectangle of dustless wood behind me. "The temple is old, child, and patient, and it serves things older than itself. Whatever you think you've found — and you've found more than I expected, which reflects well on your teachers — understand that the people who did this work did not do it carelessly. They did it thoroughly, and they are still here, and they are still checking."

She turned to go. At the gallery's mouth she paused, her back to me, and said the thing I would turn over for nights afterward, the way your tongue returns to a broken tooth:

"For what it is worth — and it is worth very little — I am sorry about the ducks. I drew those myself. I never learned to draw badly on purpose. It's harder than it looks."

And then she was gone, lamp and shadow and all, leaving me alone in Vault C with eleven beautiful lies and a heart that would not slow down.

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I sat at my desk by shielded candlelight and looked at my cipher notes — the scraped names, the funeral cluster, the prayer phrasing, the vanished box — and I asked myself the question I'd been avoiding since the spine first crackled under my thumb.

*Who checks the checkers?*

Maren had warned me off with genuine regret, which meant Maren answered to someone. The forger worked in fear, which meant the forger answered to someone worse. And somewhere above all of it, past the high scribe, past the temple fathers, past the whole dusty apparatus of Vaelmoor's official memory, sat the person who had decided that a king's name must be scraped from the record twice and polished smooth — the person for whom *King Edvard* was a danger and *the King* was safe.

Five years ago, the crown changed heads. Five years ago, the records began to lie. And now, this season, the crown's new witch-hunters were dragging gifted folk into the palace, and none of them came back out, and my neighbor's shop was ash, and my charms had been traced, and every instinct I owned was screaming that these facts were not neighbors in time. They were threads. And I was holding one end of every single one.

I snuffed the candle. In the dark I pressed my palm flat against my sternum, over the gift I kept folded up small and silent, the thing that could hang me, and I made it a promise I had no right to make.

*I'm going to find out what they erased,* I told it. *Whatever it costs.*

Somewhere across the city, in a palace I'd never entered, a king whose name had been scraped from his own records sat on a throne, and I would have given a great deal — I did, eventually, give nearly everything — to know that in five years, he had never once been seen to pray.

The knock at my door came three days later. Two hunters, palace livery, and a summons written in ink that smelled, even then, faintly of a cellar where something lived.

They had come to register me.
