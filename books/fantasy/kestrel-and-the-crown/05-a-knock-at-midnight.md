## 5. A Knock at Midnight

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in temple districts after midnight. I'd learned it as a recruit — the hush of a city that has prayed itself to sleep and trusts the gods to take the night shift. It's a trusting kind of quiet. The kind that makes you feel like an intruder for breathing.

I was good at intruding.

The warrant in my breast pocket had been signed by Commander Hask himself, which meant it was either ironclad or a trap, and with Hask those were frequently the same thing. *Temple of the Ledger, Vaelmoor's Reach. Reports of unlicensed workings. Investigate and detain.* Fourteen words, one wax seal, and my name written beneath in his cramped hand, as if he were signing me away rather than the order.

I told myself it would be nothing. It was usually nothing. Nine raids out of ten turned up candle stubs, folk charms, an old woman who blessed fishing nets because her mother had before her. You arrest a grandmother for a net-blessing and you go home and drink until your hands stop shaking, and then you do it again next week, because the oath doesn't have a clause for *unless it's stupid*.

The tenth raid, though. The tenth raid stays with you.

* * *

The Temple of the Ledger was less a church than a filing cabinet with aspirations. Debt-scribes worked there by day, copying contracts and recording payments in a hand so small it looked like the ink was trying to escape. By night it should have been empty except for a warden or two and whatever god kept the accounts.

The side door was locked. That was correct. What wasn't correct was that the lock had been opened recently — I could tell by the way the tumblers sat, loose and satisfied, like a dog that had been fed by someone other than its owner. Someone had come in with a key and not bothered to relock properly, or had relocked badly, or —

Or someone inside wanted the door to look locked without being locked. Which raised the question of who, exactly, planned to leave in a hurry.

I went in through the window instead, because if there's one thing a hunter learns early, it's never use the door your quarry prepared.

Inside: dust, moonlight through colored glass painting saints and accountants across the flagstones in equal dignity, and the smell — faint, but unmistakable once you've spent three years learning to catalog it — of working magic. Not the theatrical kind. Not the burning-hair stink of a battle-caster. This was quieter. Old bread and rain on hot stone. Hearth-magic. Charm-work. The kind the law called harmless right up until the day it decided not to.

I followed it down a corridor lined with ledger shelves, past a warden asleep at his post with a cup of something in him stronger than prayer — I checked; he was breathing; I was almost disappointed — to a door at the corridor's end, where the light under the crack was wrong. Candlelight flickers. This light didn't flicker. It breathed.

I put my hand on the door. I want to be honest about what happened in the next few seconds, because later, when people asked — and they did ask, eventually, in rooms with worse lighting — I told them different versions. But here is the true one:

I hesitated.

Not long. A heartbeat, maybe two. Long enough to hear, through the door, a voice — low, steady, a woman's voice — saying words I half recognized from the banned liturgies we were made to memorize so we could recognize their crime. And under her voice, thinner, cracking: a child. A child whispering, *"Is it working? Is it working?"*

A child.

Hask's orders said *investigate and detain*. The oath said gift-work was gift-work, no matter the size, no matter the hands. The oath said a lot of things. The oath had been saying things all year while the palace filled up with registered gifted folk who never came back out, and I had recited along every time, because that's what oaths are for. They're for the nights when doing the right thing would cost you everything, and you need something to hold onto that isn't your own spine.

I kicked the door in anyway. Habit. If you're going to betray yourself, do it with style.

* * *

The room beyond was a scriptorium gone to seed — desks pushed against walls, a brazier, candles arranged in a pattern I knew and hated knowing. And in the center of it, standing between the candles and a boy of maybe eleven, a woman with ink-stained fingers and the specific posture of someone who has already calculated every exit and found them all wanting.

She didn't scream. She didn't run. She stepped *sideways*, putting herself more fully between me and the boy, and lifted her chin like she was about to negotiate terms.

Later I would learn this was simply how she met the world. At the time, it unnerved me more than any screaming would have.

"Witch-hunter," she said. Not a question. Her eyes went to my insignia, then my face, then — this was the strange part — to my hands. "You're alone."

"Observant." I kept my voice flat, official. "On the ground. Both of you. Hands where I can see them."

The boy made a sound like a kettle giving up. He couldn't have been more than eleven, all elbows and terror, and he grabbed the back of the woman's coat with both fists. She didn't tell him to let go. She reached back and gripped his wrist instead, steadying him, and looked at me over the boy's head with an expression I can only describe as *accounting*. As though she were tallying me up. Weighing what I'd cost.

"You're Vale," she said. "Corin Vale. The one who let the Ferrow girl go last month."

My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"Mm." She didn't push it. That was worse. People who press an advantage are predictable; people who bank one are dangerous. "Well. Here's what's going to happen, hunter. You're going to look at this room, and you're going to see exactly what's in it, and then you're going to make a decision. I can't stop you either way. But the boy—" her hand tightened on his wrist, "—the boy has a small talent for keeping lamps lit. That's all. Lamps. He lights them when he's scared, because the dark is worse for him than it is for you or me, and tonight the dark was very bad indeed. So he came here, to the only person he thought might help, and I was helping. That's the whole of the crime. Write it down however you like."

"Gift-work is gift-work," I said. My own voice sounded borrowed. "No matter the size."

"Yes," she agreed pleasantly. "That's what they taught you. And where do the big ones go, hunter? The ones you detain? Where does the palace take them?"

"I don't ask questions above my station."

"No," she said softly. "You really don't. That's rather the point of you."

The candles around us burned in their perfect, breathless circle. The boy was crying now, silently, the way children cry when they've learned crying loudly makes things worse. I knew that cry. I'd worn it myself, once, in a house that smelled of boiled leather and my father's disappointment.

Here is what I should have done. I should have cuffed them both, walked them to the district lockup, filed my report by dawn, and been commended for thoroughness. Hask would have smiled at me, and Hask's smile was worth more than mine has ever been. The machine would have taken the boy wherever the machine takes them, and the woman — the scribe, the charm-seller, whoever she was — would have followed, and in five years nobody would remember either of their names except as entries in a ledger.

That is what I should have done. I want that on the record, in whatever part of me keeps records. I knew exactly what I should have done, and I stood there in a circle of illegally lit candles and did not do it.

Instead I heard myself say: "How long has the wardens' patrol been bought off?"

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "Bought off?"

"The warden down the hall is drunk on the job. Either he's incompetent, or someone paid him to sleep, or someone paid him to *not notice* paying him to sleep. This room was prepared for a visitor tonight. Maybe me. Maybe someone else. Either way, you expected interruption, and you arranged the door accordingly." I crouched, slowly, and snuffed the nearest candle between finger and thumb. The circle guttered, held. "Who else knows?"

"Why would I tell you that?"

"Because in about four minutes I'm going to stand up, walk out that door, and report that I found nothing but dust and a drunk warden. And whether anyone comes looking tomorrow depends entirely on whether you're worth the trouble of a second visit." I looked at her, and I made myself say the rest plainly, because lies work better on a foundation of truth. "I interrogated a child last month. Nine years old. No gift at all — I tested her twice, because the first result embarrassed me. Her neighbor reported her out of spite over a fence. Do you know what happened to her?"

Something moved behind the scribe's eyes. "Tell me."

"She went to the palace. With her parents. To register, since the test cleared her and the paperwork needed signing." I stood. My knees cracked. I was thirty-one years old and felt sixty. "Nobody's seen them since. I asked. I was told questions above my station have a way of becoming stations below ground."

The boy sniffled. The scribe hadn't blinked.

"So," I said. "You're going to trust me, because you have no better option, and I'm going to trust you, because apparently I've lost my mind somewhere between the door and this room. Put out the candles. Take the boy home. And if anyone asks, I found an empty scriptorium and a warden who'll be flogged for drinking, which will be true, and therefore defensible, which is the only kind of treason worth committing."

"That's quite a speech," she said.

"I practice in the mirror."

And then — this is the part I still can't explain, even to myself, even now — she smiled. Small, tired, real. "Lena Ashgrove," she said, offering nothing so vulgar as a handshake. "Temple scribe. Debtor. And apparently, as of tonight, your accomplice."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"You kicked in a door and then negotiated terms of concealment with a suspect. That's agreement, hunter. Everything after this is just paperwork." She turned to the boy and knelt, and her whole voice changed — softened into something that had probably once sung lullabies, before debts and dead kings got in the way. "Tam. Look at me. We're going to blow these out, and you're going to walk home with me, and tomorrow you're going to be a boy whose lamp went out, and that's all. Can you do that?"

The boy nodded against her shoulder. Then he looked past her, at me, with the terrible directness of children, and said: "Are you going to burn our house down too?"

The words went in under my ribs and sat there.

"No," I said. "Nobody's burning anything. Go home. Lock your door. Light your lamp — with flint, like everyone else."

He went. Ashgrove doused the candles one by one, unhurried, watching me the whole time as if I were a contract she was reading for hidden clauses. When the last one died, the dark came back in, ordinary and honest, and I was abruptly aware that I was standing in a sealed room with a confessed witch and no witnesses, and that everything I was about to do next existed only because I would say it did.

"Second door," she murmured, nodding toward a shelf of tax codices. "Behind the municipal assessments. Everyone forgets the municipal assessments. Even the Inquisition."

"Everyone forgets the municipal assessments," I agreed, "because they are municipal assessments."

"There's hope for you yet."

She slipped through the false door with the boy, and just before it closed, her voice came back through the gap, quiet as the dark: "Vale. The Ferrow girl. Thank you."

Then gone. Footsteps fading. A door somewhere, soft as a sigh.

* * *

I stood alone in the scriptorium for a long moment, doing arithmetic.

One drunk warden, to be reported. One empty room, to be recorded. One set of candle-stubs — I gathered them, wrapped them in my handkerchief, and pocketed them, because evidence destroyed is evidence that can't destroy you. One false door, which I closed and shelved with the municipal assessments, patting the spines like a man burying a body.

And one hunter, standing in the wreckage of his own oath, trying to find the exact moment he'd broken it.

Here's the thing they don't tell you about treason: it doesn't feel like anything. There's no thunderclap, no cold wind, no mark appearing on your skin. You kick in a door, you see a frightened kid, you make a choice, and then you climb back out the window you came in through, and the night is exactly as silent as it was before, and the city goes on trusting its gods to take the shift. The only thing that's changed is you. Some load-bearing wall inside, quietly removed. The building still stands. For now.

I wrote my report at dawn. *Temple of the Ledger, Vaelmoor's Reach: searched. Nothing found. Warden found intoxicated at post; recommend discipline.* Clean, short, true in every particular that mattered and false in every one that counted. Hask read it over his morning tea, grunted, and said, "The Ledger again. Third report of workings there this quarter. Someone in that temple is careless."

"Or someone wants us looking there," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because Hask looked up at me with those pale, patient eyes, the eyes of a man who files everything, and said:

"Do you think so, Vale?"

"I think coincidences make poor policy, sir."

"Hm." He signed off on my report. His pen scratched like a small animal in a wall. "Keep an eye on the temple district. And Vale — the Ashgrove woman. The debtor-scribe. Her contract came across my desk last week. Transferred to crown custody." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Effective within the month. Interesting timing, don't you find?"

I found. I found it all the way home, and all through that day, and I am finding it still: the image of ink-stained fingers lifting a chin in defiance, and beneath it, in a clerk's cramped hand, a clause she never signed, moving her — like cargo, like coin — into the palace's keeping.

I had covered for her tonight.

In a month, it wouldn't matter.
