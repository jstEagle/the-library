## 6. The Thing in the Throne Room

The summons came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. If the crown was going to destroy my life, the least it could do was pick a day with some drama to it. A Friday, maybe. Tuesdays were for ledger-balancing and arguing with the fishmonger about herring prices. They were not for being summoned to the palace to *register*.

The letter itself was beautiful, which should have been my first warning. Heavy cream paper, a wax seal pressed with the royal kestrel, ink so black it looked wet. It sat on the temple's receiving desk like a cat that had decided to live in your house whether you agreed or not.

*Lena Ashgrove,* it read, in a hand so precise it might have been printed. *By order of His Majesty's Edict of Purification, you are commanded to present yourself at Vaelmoor Palace for registration of arcane aptitude, on the third day hence. Failure to comply will be construed as confession.*

*Construed as confession.* I had to admire it. Most threats take a full sentence to ruin your life. This one did it in four words.

"You'll go," High Scribe Maren said, not looking up from her own work. She was an old woman built like a piece of furniture someone had decided was load-bearing and then never moved again. "And you'll smile, and you'll answer their questions dull as ditchwater, and you'll come home."

"And if I don't come home?"

"Then I'll have your desk cleared out by Thursday." She dipped her pen. "Grief is a luxury this temple cannot afford, child. Neither can you."

I wanted to hate her for that. Instead I took the letter upstairs to my cell — room, they called it, but a bed, a shelf, and a window the size of a hymnal is a cell with better manners — and I sat on the bed and held the thing until the wax seal went soft from the heat of my hands.

Here is what nobody tells you about terror: it's boring. It's mostly waiting. It's folding and refolding a summons until the creases go soft, and cataloguing everything you'd miss, and discovering the list is shorter than you'd hoped and longer than you'd feared.

I thought about running. Of course I thought about running. But my name was in the debt ledger, and my name was now in the crown's registry of suspected gifted, and both of those documents had a way of following a person like dogs that had learned one trick very well. Besides — and I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only charm I have left — some small, stupid, stubborn part of me wanted to see it. The palace. The court. The king. Whatever engine had been turning under five years of missing pages and burned shops and neighbors who didn't come back from registration.

Curiosity has killed better women than me. But it hasn't killed me yet, and I've decided to be insufferable about that fact for as long as possible.

* * *

They sent a carriage.

Not a prison wagon — a carriage, lacquered black, with a driver who never once looked at me and two escort hunters who never once stopped looking at me. One of them was young enough that his uniform still had creases in it from the shop. The other had the thousand-yard stare of a man who had done his job long enough to stop believing in it, and I recognized the look because I wore it every time I balanced the temple accounts.

"Name?" the young one said, though he was holding a paper with my name on it.

"Lena Ashgrove."

"Aptitude?"

"I press letters into vellum," I said. "It's hardly arcane."

He wrote something down anyway. People like him always write something down; the writing is the point, not the information. A record exists, therefore the world is orderly, therefore whatever happens next is procedure.

Vaelmoor rose out of the morning fog the way a headstone rises out of grass — inevitable, ornamental, and meant for someone else. I'd seen it my whole life from across the river, all spires and banners, gilded like a promise the city couldn't afford to keep. Up close it was worse. Up close you could see the scaffolding on the east wing where they were building something new, and the windows of the old wing, and how many of them were dark. Palaces are supposed to be full of light. This one hoarded its light the way misers hoard coin, spending it only where visitors would see.

We went in through a side gate. Of course we did. Kings don't show their rot through the front door.

* * *

The throne room antechamber smelled of lilies.

That was the first wrong thing, though it took me a while to understand why. Lilies are funeral flowers. Someone had arranged them — hundreds of them, white and heavy-headed — along every surface of the waiting hall, and beneath their sweetness was that other smell lilies get when they've been standing too long: green rot, sweetness curdling at the edges. The whole room smelled like a beautiful thing pretending not to die.

The second wrong thing was the courtiers.

There were perhaps thirty of them, arrayed in the antechamber in loose clusters, dressed in silk worth more than the temple's annual budget. And they were *still*. Not politely-still, not bored-still. Still the way furniture is still. A woman near the window held a fan half-open and hadn't moved it in the entire quarter hour I watched her. A man across the room smiled — a pleasant, courtly smile — and the smile did not change, not by a hair, not by a breath, for as long as I could see him.

I told myself they were listening to a speech. I told myself it was some court fashion, some game, statuesque composure as the season's new virtue. I told myself a great many things, the way you talk loudly to yourself in a dark house so the dark knows you're not afraid.

Then a bell rang somewhere, soft and silver, and all thirty of them turned their heads toward the throne room doors at exactly the same speed, at exactly the same angle, like a field of flowers tracking the sun.

I stopped telling myself things after that.

"Gifted registrant Ashgrove," announced a steward whose voice had the flat polish of a stone worn smooth by water. "His Majesty will receive you."

The doors opened. The courtiers filed in ahead of me, silent, orderly, taking positions along the walls with the precision of dancers who had rehearsed this forever. Nobody spoke. Nobody breathed, as far as I could tell, above the minimum required to remain upright.

I walked down a carpet the color of dried blood, between walls hung with portraits of dead kings, and I did what any sensible woman does when walking into horror: I counted. Forty paces. Sixteen portraits. Three guards, two stewards, one throne.

One king.

* * *

King Aldric sat on the Vaelmoor throne the way a coat sits on a hook.

That's the truest sentence I know how to build, and I've spent years learning to build them. He was dressed magnificently — deep blue, gold thread, the works — and he was a handsome man, or had been, broad-shouldered and gray-templed, with the kind of face that gets put on coins. But there was no one behind it. You know how a coat hangs when there's no body in it? Shoulders right, sleeves right, everything in position, and yet somehow *empty*, deflated, held up by the hook alone? That was Aldric of Vaelmoor. Something was wearing him the way he was wearing the blue and gold.

"Courtier of the realm," the king said, and his voice was warm, resonant, trained. "Be welcome. Be registered. Be at peace."

The words came out evenly spaced. Perfectly evenly spaced, like beads on a string, like a clock striking hours. And then, before the steward could even gesture me forward:

"Be welcome. Be registered. Be at peace."

The same three phrases. The same spacing. The same warmth, poured from the same mold, identical down to the breath between them. Somewhere in the gallery a courtier murmured approval, a low contented sound, and thirty heads nodded in unison.

"Approach," said the steward.

I approached. My legs worked, which I considered a personal triumph. Twenty feet away, ten. Close enough now to see his eyes, and I wish to every god I don't believe in that I hadn't been close enough to see his eyes.

They were brown. Kind brown, once, I'd have guessed. Now they had the quality of pond water — depth without bottom, a surface that reflected you perfectly and showed nothing of what lay beneath. And beneath, when I let myself look, when my gift slipped its leash for half a heartbeat the way it always does around strong magic —

Beneath was *vast*.

I have touched magic my whole life, small magics, charm-magics, the kind that fit in a pocket. Luck knotted into thread. Warmth coaxed into a locket. Little domestic miracles, the size of a cupped hand. What lived under King Aldric's skin was not that. It was the difference between a candle and the thing the candle is afraid of. It pressed outward against him from the inside, patient and enormous, the way a flood presses against a door — not pushing, not yet, just *waiting*, with the serene confidence of something that has waited a very long time and been rewarded every time.

Five kings' reigns, Sable would later tell me. Three vessels before this one. But standing there, I knew none of that. I only knew that I was looking at something old wearing something beloved, and that everyone in the room thought the beloved thing was still home.

"Kneel," the steward said.

I knelt. Marble is cold through skirts; file that under things I learned too late to be useful.

"State your gift," the king said. Beads on a string. "State your gift. State your—"

"*Your Majesty,*" the steward said quickly, softly, and the repetition cut off mid-word, and the king's face did something terrible. It flickered. Just for an instant, the mask slipped sideways, and underneath the trained warmth was something else — confusion, maybe, or fear, or the reflexive flinch of a man waking briefly inside his own body and finding strangers at the wheel. His hands, folded on the arm of the throne, tightened. Knuckles whitening. Holding on.

Then it smoothed away, and the warmth returned, and I understood that I had seen the real king for less than a second, drowning, reaching up through dark water, and that everyone in this room would call what I'd seen madness if I ever dared describe it.

"State your gift," the king said, once, pleasantly.

"Letters," I said. "I'm a scribe, Your Majesty. I copy records at the Temple of the Quill. My only magic is a fine hand and a talent for spelling."

Which was, technically, a lie wrapped around a truth, which is the best kind of lie. My gift stayed curled up small and quiet in my chest, doing its best impression of nothing, and I prayed — actually prayed, first time in years — that whatever wore the crown couldn't hear heartbeats.

The king regarded me. The pond-water eyes considered. And then the crown—

I need to be careful with this part, because I have told it so many times since, in so many rooms, to people who needed to believe it, and every telling wears the memory smooth like a river stone. So let me say it plainly, the way I saw it, before the telling wore it down:

**The crown breathed.**

Not gleamed. Not caught the light. *Breathed.* The circlet of gold and kestrel-wings that sat on Aldric's brow expanded, contracted, expanded — slow, deep, rhythmic, the way a sleeping animal breathes, the way a lung fills. The gold moved like it was soft. Like it was flesh. And with each slow inhalation, the king sat straighter, and the pond-water eyes grew calmer, and the vastness beneath his skin settled deeper, more comfortable, more *at home*.

It wasn't a crown on a king. It was a mouth, and the king was what it was eating. Slowly. Over years. A bite at a time, chewed fine, swallowed gentle, so the courtiers watching would never see anything but their monarch, serene upon his throne.

"Registered," the king said warmly. "You may rise. You may go. You may—" and there, just barely, the string of beads snagged, the voice catching on itself, repeating "*you may, you may, you may*" in a whisper that no one else seemed to hear, before the steward's hand moved at his side like a man adjusting a mechanism, and the voice smoothed over. "—go in peace, daughter of Vaelmoor."

Daughter of Vaelmoor. As if it knew me. As if it had read my file, my amended contract, my clause-I-never-signed, and found me *satisfactory*.

I rose. I bowed. I walked backward down the blood-colored carpet the way protocol demanded, forty paces, sixteen portraits, three guards, and every step I took the vastness behind the king's eyes watched me go, and I could feel it the way you feel sun on your neck — warm, attentive, *pleased*.

At the doors I made my mistake. Or my salvation. Historians can argue about which.

I looked back.

And King Aldric — the coat on the hook, the drowned man, the vessel — looked back at me. Not the thing wearing him. *Him.* For one clear second his face was his own, terror and shame and a desperate, drowning hope all fighting for space in it, and his lips shaped two silent words, aimed at me alone, across forty feet of marble and thirty pairs of glassy eyes:

*Help me.*

The doors closed. The lilies' sweet rot closed over me. The carriage took me home through streets where people were nailing the Edict to walls, and I sat with my hands folded in my lap like a good registrant, like a harmless scribe, like a woman with nothing under her skin worth noticing.

I did not cry until the temple doors shut behind me. Then I cried exactly as long as it takes to fold a summons into a hard little square, and I put it in the fire, and I watched it burn.

Because here is what I knew, walking out of that throne room, that I had only suspected before: the hunt, the registrations, the disappearances — none of it was policy. None of it was a king's fear of magic. It was a *pantry*. The Regent — I didn't have the name yet, but I had the shape of it — was stocking its larder, sorting the gifted from the ungifted the way a cook sorts grain from chaff.

And somewhere in a ledger in this city, in ink I had never signed, my name was already written among the stores.

Maren found me at the fire. She looked at my face, and then at the burning paper, and she said nothing for a long moment.

"They registered you," she said. Not a question.

"They registered me," I said.

"Then you have perhaps a week." She set something on the table beside me — a thin book, plain-covered, no temple mark. "A hunter left this for you. He said to tell you: *the fifth shelf remembers what the ledgers forgot.*"

I looked at the book. I looked at her.

"Maren. Who is hunting whom?"

The old scribe finally looked tired. "Child," she said, "I genuinely no longer know."
