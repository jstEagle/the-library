## 7. Bargains in Borrowed Light

### LENA

The summons came at dusk, folded into a hymnal I'd left on the temple's lending shelf, which told me two things: whoever wrote it knew my habits, and whoever wrote it wanted me to know they knew.

*The old granary on Tanner's Row. Come alone. Come tonight. Or don't, and we'll both find out what the crown does with unregistered gifts.*

No signature. But the handwriting was precise in the way of men who've been trained to write reports — every letter standing at attention. And there was a smear of ash on the corner of the page, as if the pen had been dipped by candlelight and the writer's hands weren't quite steady.

I considered not going. I want that on the record, somewhere, in case anyone ever asks: Lena Ashgrove weighed her options like a sensible woman and nearly chose the sensible one.

But "or don't" was doing a lot of work in that sentence. A hunter who wanted me dead would have kicked down my door already. A hunter who wanted me *registered* would have dragged me to the palace in irons. This one wanted something else, and men who want something else can be bargained with — or they can be traps. The trick was finding out which before the teeth closed.

So I went, because apparently I'd learned nothing from watching Mrs. Halloway's shop burn.

Tanner's Row smelled exactly as advertised. The old granary squatted between two tanneries like something the city had tried to digest and failed. Its doors hung open on darkness. Inside, one candle burned on an upturned crate, and beside it sat Corin Vale, witch-hunter, out of uniform, looking like a man who'd been arguing with himself for several hours and losing.

"You came," he said.

"You threatened me."

"I offered you an alternative." He gestured to the crate opposite him. "Sit."

"I'll stand. Standing's closer to the door."

Something flickered across his face that might have been respect. He didn't push it. That, more than anything, made me wary. Hunters pushed. It was their whole profession.

"Three nights ago," he said, "there was a raid on the Temple of Ledgers. My raid. My warrant. In the course of it, a scribe named Lena Ashgrove was found performing an unsanctioned rite over a frightened child."

"Protecting," I said. "The word you're looking for is protecting."

"In my report, that's what I wrote." He let that sit. "In my report, the scribe Ashgrove was questioned and released. The apprentice was questioned and released. No charges filed."

I looked at him properly then. Candlelight is generous to everyone, but even so — he had the face of a man who hadn't slept, and the posture of one who intended to keep not sleeping until something was settled.

"Why?" I asked.

"Because I've spent four months dragging people into that palace, and none of them come back, and last week I interrogated a nine-year-old girl who has no gift at all — none, I checked twice — and her mother confessed to witchcraft anyway just to stop them taking the girl." His voice stayed flat, but his hands didn't; they kept folding and unfolding around each other like birds that couldn't roost. "And because when I searched your desk, scribe, I found what you'd copied out of the coronation records. The pages that don't exist anymore."

My stomach dropped through the granary floor and kept going. "You went through my things."

"It's called a search. It's in my oath." He leaned forward. "Five years of excised entries. Every mention of the king's crowning, cut out with a razor and burned. You noticed. You started asking questions. And now you're standing in front of me instead of running, which means either you're very brave or very stupid, and I've read enough of your copying work to know you're not stupid."

"Flattery. From a hunter. Careful, Master Vale, people will talk."

"People will do worse than talk if we don't move." He reached into his coat and set something on the crate between us: a folded paper, sealed with the palace's black wax. "This is a writ of access. Archives, sub-levels, restricted stacks. My name opens doors in this city that yours never will. I'm offering to open them for you."

"And what does it cost?"

"Proof." The word came out like a stone he'd been carrying in his mouth. "Not rumors. Not feelings. Proof that the king is enchanted. Possessed. Bound — whatever word you like. Because if he's merely mad, then everything I've done — every arrest, every interrogation, every person I put on that cart — was duty. Ugly duty, maybe, but duty. And if he's *bound*, then the hunt isn't duty at all. It's bait. It's someone fishing with the crown's hook, and I've been the line." He stood, and the candle threw his shadow huge against the granary wall. "I need to know which man I am. You need those archives. That's the bargain."

I should have said no. Let me be clear about that too. Everything in me that had survived twenty-six years in Vaelmoor said *no, walk away, hunters don't change, they just change tactics.*

Instead I heard myself say: "If I prove it, what happens to me? Because I notice your bargain covers what you get and what you need, and says nothing about what I am afterward."

He had the grace to look uncomfortable. "You'd have my protection."

"Your protection." I laughed, and it came out uglier than I meant. "Master Vale, three weeks ago your colleagues burned Mrs. Halloway's shop with her stock inside it because she sold *headache tea*. Your protection is worth slightly less than the paper your writ is printed on."

"Then consider it mutual blackmail instead." He said it without flinching, which I'll admit I admired. "You know I falsified a report. I know you've been digging into the coronation. We hang together or we hang separately — I believe that's how the saying goes."

"That's treason, what you just described."

"Yes," said Corin Vale, witch-hunter of the Crown of Vaelmoor, "it is."

I took the writ.

### CORIN

She took the writ, and I felt — I don't know what I felt. Lighter, maybe, the way a man feels after confessing to a crime. Guiltier, certainly. Both at once, which I was learning was simply what treason felt like: lighter and heavier in the same breath.

"You understand I could still arrest you," she said, turning the paper over in her fingers. "Right here. Right now. Walk out of this granary and hand you to Commander Rusk with my compliments."

"You could."

"And why haven't you?"

"Because you'd have done it already if you were going to." I blew out the candle. In the sudden dark, her voice moved toward the door — she'd been tracking it the whole conversation, of course. Sensible woman. "We leave at dawn. The archives open to hunters at the sixth bell. Wear something that doesn't look like a scribe."

"Scribes have a look now?"

"Everything has a look. That's half of what hunting is — knowing the looks." I hesitated at the door, and then made myself say the thing I'd been circling all night. "Scribe. Ashgrove. Whatever I'm supposed to call you. If this goes wrong — if you're playing me, if this is some gifted conspiracy and I'm the fool walking you into it—"

"Then you'll arrest me," she said from the dark, "and I'll tell them you falsified a report, and we'll both hang, and the king will go on repeating himself in that throne room until the whole city learns to nod along." A pause. "I don't want that any more than you do. For what it's worth."

It wasn't much. It was the first honest thing anyone had said to me in months, and I hated how much it helped.

"Dawn," I said, and walked out into the tannery stink, feeling like a man who had just signed something in invisible ink.

* * *

### LENA

Dawn found us crossing the Palace Quarter side by side, which was its own particular nightmare. Corin wore his hunter's grey, insignia polished, every inch the crown's dog. I wore my plainest dress and my most forgettable face and spent the entire walk cataloguing exits.

The palace gates opened for him without a question. That was the part that chilled me — not the guards, not the banners, but the *ease*. Four months ago I'd have needed a miracle to get within a hundred yards of the royal archives. Now I strolled in on a hunter's arm, and nobody looked twice, because the machinery of the hunt had grown so accustomed to feeding itself that it no longer checked what went in and out.

"The restricted stacks are below the record hall," Corin murmured as we descended a spiral stair that smelled of cold stone and older cold. "Sub-levels one through three are ordinary — tax rolls, land grants, correspondence. Sub-four is where they keep the things the crown doesn't want remembered. Coronation records will be there, if anywhere."

"And if they're not?"

"Then we're back to blackmail, and I start following the money instead." He glanced at me. "You look pale."

"I'm always pale. It's a scribe thing."

"Lena." First time he'd used my name. It landed strangely — too familiar, too soon, like a hand on the shoulder from a stranger. "Whatever's down there, you don't have to touch it. Point. I'll pull the books."

"You think I'm afraid of books?"

"I think you're afraid of what's in them. There's a difference, and it matters."

I didn't answer, because he was right, and because the stair had ended at a door banded in black iron, and beyond it lay sub-four.

The archive below was vast and dim and utterly silent — not library-silence but held-breath silence, the silence of a place where sound itself had been asked to leave. Shelves ran back into dark farther than the lamplight reached. And the air had a texture I recognized from somewhere I couldn't name: a faint pressure against the skin, like the moment before lightning.

Corin felt it too. I saw him slow, saw his hand drift toward a weapon he wasn't carrying.

"What is that?" he whispered.

"If I knew," I said, "we'd be having a very different conversation."

We worked by lamplight among the shelves, pulling indices, cross-referencing. It was Corin who found it — a ledger of court expenditures, five years old, and an entry dated eleven days after the coronation:

*Payment rendered to the Order of Quiet Scribes, for services of binding and forgetting. Sum: substantial. Witnessed under seal.*

"Binding and forgetting," Corin read aloud, and his voice cracked on the second word. "That's not madness. Madness doesn't get invoiced."

"No," I said quietly. "It doesn't."

He sat down slowly on a step-stool, holding the ledger like it might bite. I watched a man's whole architecture of belief rearrange itself behind his eyes — watched duty collapse and something else take its place, something rawer and more dangerous.

"All those people," he said. "All those people I took."

"We don't have time for that yet."

"I know." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I know. Give me ten seconds anyway."

I gave him fifteen. It seemed the decent thing.

Then he stood, wiped his face with the efficiency of a man closing a shutter, and said, "There'll be more. Binding rites leave paperwork — they always do. Find me the Order of Quiet Scribes, and we'll find the rest."

We were still searching when the lamps above us, on the third sub-level, began to go out one by one. Footsteps on the stair. More than two sets.

Corin snuffed our lamp and pulled me into the gap between two shelves, his arm across my chest, his mouth at my ear.

"Rusk checks the sub-levels on quarter-days," he breathed. "Today's a quarter-day. I forgot."

"You *forgot*?"

"Hunters forget things now. Apparently it's going around." The footsteps grew louder. "Back exit's through the map room, past the shelf of drowned cities. Stay behind me, stay quiet, and whatever you hear me say up there — remember I'm lying."

And that, I thought in the dark, pressed against a stranger-turned-ally-turned-co-conspirator while the crown's boots came down the stairs toward us, was probably the truest sentence anyone had spoken in this palace in five years.

The footsteps stopped at the top of the stair. A voice — dry, unhurried, familiar from the throne room — said:

"Someone's been reading down here. How lovely. Do come up, Master Vale. The Regent so enjoys meeting its readers."

Corin's arm went rigid against my chest.

He hadn't told me about that voice. Which meant either he'd never heard it —

— or he had, and hadn't known how to say it out loud.
