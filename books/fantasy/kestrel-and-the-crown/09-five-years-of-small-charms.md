## 9. Five Years of Small Charms

Sleep has never come easily to me, but since the under-archive it has abandoned me entirely. Sable's words keep circling: *The Regent has worn three kings already.* Three kings. And somewhere in that sentence, unspoken but unmistakable, was a fourth — a vessel being prepared, groomed, purchased.

I know how contracts work. I have spent five years copying them in a temple scriptorium, and before that I spent my childhood watching my mother's signature dry on one. So when I couldn't sleep, I did what any sensible woman does at three in the morning: I went back to the ledger room, pulled my own debt-contract from the iron cabinet, and read it by candlelight for the hundredth time.

That's when I found the amendment.

But to understand why my hands shook reading it — why I sat down on the cold floor among the record chests like a puppet with cut strings — you need to know about the small charms. You need to know about my mother.

* * *

I was seven the first time I understood what I was.

Not gifted — that word came later, wrapped in proclamations and fear. No, at seven I understood something simpler: that when I held a broken thing and wanted it whole, it listened.

Our kitchen in the Tannery Row tenements had a chipped blue bowl that my mother refused to throw away because it had been her mother's. One evening it slipped from my hands and cracked clean down the middle, and I cried, and I pressed the halves together and *wished*, the way children wish, with their whole bodies — and the crack sealed like water closing over a stone. Not a mark left. Not even a scar.

My mother went very still. Then she knelt down, took my face in her flour-dusted hands, and said, "Lena. Listen to me carefully. Never do that where anyone can see."

"Why?"

"Because the world is cruel to things it doesn't understand, and it is cruelest of all to people who can do what you just did." She kissed my forehead. "Small charms, little kestrel. Small charms only. A stitch here. A luck-turn there. Nothing that sings."

She called me her kestrel because I was always perched on things I shouldn't climb, always watching, always ready to bolt. It was a joke between us. It would become an instruction.

For six years after that, we lived by small charms. My mother had no gift herself — or if she did, she'd smothered it so long ago even she couldn't find it anymore — but she understood the trade of it better than any gifted person I've met since. She taught me which charms sold and which charms got questions asked. A knot tied a certain way to keep a fisherman's nets from rotting: safe. A fever-cool cloth for the neighbor's baby: safe, if you called it good nursing. A charm to make a landlord forget the rent was due: *not* safe, no matter how satisfying, because landlords talk to magistrates and magistrates talk to temple auditors.

"Everything you make," she said, "make it look like luck. Luck can't be prosecuted. Yet."

*Yet.* She said it lightly, the way you'd say *maybe it'll rain*. She knew. Mothers always know before daughters do.

* * *

They came for her on a Tuesday in the month of Frostmelt, when I was thirteen.

I remember it was Tuesday because market day, and she'd sent me to buy eels, and I came home to our door standing open and two men in grey-and-gold livery going through our things with the unhurried thoroughness of people who are paid by the hour. A third man stood in the middle of the room holding my mother's sewing box, and inside the sewing box, beneath the buttons, were forty-one small charms wrapped in wax paper.

Forty-one. She'd been saving them for the spring festival, when the temple ran its charity stalls and a gifted seller could move stock without suspicion. Forty-one harmless charms. Knots and stitches and folded papers. Things that wouldn't lift a feather on their own.

"It's not hers," I said. I was thirteen and stupid and brave in the way only the young and stupid can be brave. "None of it's hers. It's mine."

One of the grey-and-gold men looked at me for a long moment. He had kind eyes, which I have since learned is the most dangerous thing a hunter can have. Kind eyes mean they've done this enough times to develop a conscience about it, which means they do it anyway.

"No," he said gently. "It isn't. But it will be yours someday, and we both know it."

They took her out through the crowd in irons. She didn't struggle. That's what I couldn't understand for years afterward — she didn't struggle, didn't call my name, didn't do anything but turn her head once to find me in the doorway, and mouth one word.

*Kestrel.*

Fly, it meant. Be small, be quick, be gone.

I didn't fly. I stood there clutching a string bag of eels until the crowd closed over the grey-and-gold coats, and then I went inside and sat among the turned-out drawers until dark, and then I did the thing she'd told me never to do.

I sang. Just once, just a little — a finding-charm, whispered into the seam of the air, asking the city to show me where they'd taken her. And the city answered. It always answered me; that was the horror of my gift, that it was less a tool than a loyalty, and loyalties don't care about proclamations.

It showed me the palace. The high tower on the east face, a window lit yellow, and behind the glass — not clearly, the way dreams aren't clear — my mother, sitting very straight at a table, writing.

Writing. They hadn't thrown her in a cell. They'd given her paper.

I didn't understand that either, for years.

* * *

Here is what happens to the family of a convicted gift-bearer in Vaelmoor, and it is the part nobody says out loud because everyone knows someone it happened to: the conviction comes with costs. Court costs. Incarceration costs. The cost of the investigation itself, itemized down to the hunters' boot-leather. The crown bills the family, and when the family cannot pay — and they never can — the debt converts.

Into service. Into labor. Into a child signed over to whatever institution will take the burden off the state's books.

The Temple of the Ledger took me. Of course it did. The temple runs the debt-records for half the city; taking debt-orphans as scribes is practically a tradition, and everyone calls it mercy. The High Scribe who interviewed me was a soft-spoken woman named Mother Casselin, with ink-stained fingers and a voice like warm milk, and she looked at my file for a long time before she looked at me.

"You're the Ashgrove girl," she said. "Your mother's case was unusual."

"Was it?" I kept my eyes down. Thirteen-year-olds who've learned the rules keep their eyes down.

"The charges were dismissed," Mother Casselin said. "Did you know that? Quietly, three weeks after her arrest. Insufficient evidence. The charms were all certified harmless." She set the file down. "And yet she remains in custody. Administrative hold. Do you know what an administrative hold is, child?"

"No, Mother."

"It means someone with authority wants her somewhere specific, and hasn't yet decided why. Or has decided, and is waiting." Her voice stayed warm as milk the entire time. "Your debt will be five years of service here. At the end of it, your mother's account is settled and she is released. Those are the terms. Do you accept them?"

Five years. I was thirteen. Five years meant I'd walk out at eighteen with my mother's hand in mine and nothing behind us but a paid ledger.

"Yes," I said. "I accept."

Reader, I want you to admire, as I have been forced to admire, the elegance of it. Because here is what I learned later, piecing it together from temple records and Sable's banned histories and finally from that amended clause in my own contract:

My mother's arrest was never about her.

* * *

The temple years were — I won't lie to you, because lying is for proclamations — good to me, in the way a well-run cage is good to a bird. Warm rooms. Regular meals. Work I was frighteningly good at, because it turned out my gift loved ink the way it loved broken bowls: records *wanted* to be legible, ledgers *wanted* to balance, and when I copied a page my hands moved like they remembered the words before my eyes reached them. Mother Casselin noticed within a month. She moved me from general scribery to the restricted stacks, where the old contracts lived, and told me I had a talent for "the weight of documents."

I thought that was a compliment. I was sixteen and still stupid in some directions.

I served my five years. I counted every day. On the last morning of the fifth year I walked to Mother Casselin's office with my head up for the first time in living memory, ready to collect my release papers and my mother's freedom, and found her not alone. Beside her desk stood a man in grey-and-gold livery — palace colors, not hunter's — and he held a document I recognized on sight, because I had helped rebind the volume it came from two winters before.

The master debt-ledger. My page.

"There's been an amendment," Mother Casselin said, and her voice was still warm as milk, and I hated her in that moment more than I have ever hated anyone, including the Regent itself, because warmth in that room was a costume and we both knew it. "A renewal clause. Your service term has been extended indefinitely, pending resolution of certain outstanding matters regarding your mother's account."

"That's not legal," I said. "There's no such clause. I've read every page of my contract. I *copied* it."

"You copied the contract as it was executed," the palace man said. He had kind eyes too. They all had kind eyes; I begin to suspect kindness is issued with the uniform. "Amendments are executed separately. By authorized parties." He slid the page across the desk, and there it was, in a clerk's hand I didn't recognize, bearing a seal I did — the privy seal of the crown itself:

*...the debtor's obligation shall persist until such time as the debtor's particular aptitudes may be rendered unto the Crown's service, at the Crown's sole determination...*

Particular aptitudes. Rendered unto. I stood there doing arithmetic with my stomach while the adults watched me, and the sum I arrived at was: *they know what I am, and they have known since I was thirteen, and the five years were never payment. The five years were ripening.*

I asked one question. I'm still proud of myself for asking only one.

"Where is my mother?"

Mother Casselin looked at the palace man. The palace man looked at his boots. And neither of them answered, which was an answer, and it was: *gone wherever people go who have finished being useful.*

* * *

That was four years ago. Four years of selling luck-knots in the dock district and copying dead merchants' contracts by day and telling myself, every single night, the story I needed to survive: *it was bad luck. It was the times. The hunt caught her, the system ground her up, the temple exploited a loophole. Cruel, ordinary, impersonal.*

Bad luck. I sold charms against it for a living.

But tonight, sitting on the floor of the ledger room with my amended contract open across my knees, I finally made myself do the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Sable spoke the word *Regent*. And the numbers refuse to come out any other way.

Item: a gifted girl in the Tannery Row, discovered at seven, hidden carefully for six years by a cautious mother.

Item: a raid that finds exactly the hidden cache, on exactly the right day, based on charges that are quietly dismissed three weeks later — because the arrest was never meant to end in conviction. It was meant to end in a debt.

Item: a debt engineered to place that girl in the one institution that keeps the crown's records, teaches her their scripts, measures her aptitude for five years, and reports upward.

Item: an administrative hold on the mother that ends the moment the daughter's contract is secured — because the mother was never the prisoner. The mother was the bait. And bait gets discarded.

Item: an amendment invoking "particular aptitudes," signed under the privy seal, five years before anyone in Vaelmoor had heard the word *Purification* — five years before the hunt began, five years before the palace started collecting the gifted.

Item, final: Sable Ives, in a basement full of forbidden books, saying *the Regent has worn three kings,* and looking at me the way you look at a door you're afraid is already open.

Three kings worn. Which means, somewhere in the deep records, there should be a pattern — three vessels, three preparations, three histories shaped by careful hands into the shape of a door. And if the Regent chooses its vessels the way I now suspect it chose me...

Then my mother didn't die for nothing worse than my own blood. She died because my blood was *suitable*.

I sat on that cold floor until my candle guttered, and I did not cry, which surprised me. Grief, it turns out, has stages beyond weeping, and the far stage is very quiet and very cold and looks remarkably like purpose. Everything I thought I had survived — the arrest, the debt, the lost years, the mother-shaped hole — none of it was weather. It was architecture. Someone built my life the way you build a trap, plank by plank, and called each plank misfortune.

Well. I have read enough contracts to know that anything built can be unbound.

I folded the amended page into my sleeve — yes, stole it, let the temple audit *that* — and blew out the candle.

Tomorrow I have an appointment with a disgraced witch-hunter who wants proof the king is enchanted. Tonight I finally have something better than proof.

I have a motive.
