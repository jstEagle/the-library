## 10. The Loyal Blade

There are three ways to lie to Captain Dree, and I have tried all of them.

The first is the direct lie — look her in the eye and say something false. This fails within four seconds. Dree has a face like a siege gate and eyes that count your heartbeats. The second is misdirection: change the subject, make her laugh, get her talking about supply requisitions or the scandalous state of the barracks latrines. This buys you maybe a minute, because Dree does not forget a question any more than a hound forgets a scent. She just files it away, next to all the other questions she's ever asked anyone, in what I can only assume is a very orderly and very damning little cabinet.

The third way is the half-lie. A truth with a trapdoor in it. You give her something real enough to satisfy the counting of heartbeats, and you let her walk away believing she has the whole of it.

I had been using the third way for eleven days, and I could feel it going bad in my mouth like milk left out in summer.

* * *

It started, as most of my problems do, with paperwork.

"Vale." Dree didn't look up from the duty roster when I came into the garrison office. "You've filed for leave three times this month."

"I'm owed leave."

"You're owed leave, yes. You've never once taken it. In six years, Corin, you have taken four days off, and one of those was your mother's funeral, which I granted under protest because you tried to attend it on foot between shifts." She looked up then. Siege gate. Counting eyes. "So explain to me why a man who once worked through a broken wrist is suddenly desperate for afternoons."

"The wrist was overrated," I said. "Barely used it."

"Corin."

"Dree."

She set down the quill with the deliberate care of a woman who owns several knives and knows exactly where each one is. "Commander Hask wants a report on your temple district rounds. Your rounds have gaps in them. Hours, Corin. Whole hours where my best hunter is unaccounted for in the one district of this city where people are currently being disappeared."

"They're not being disappeared," I said. "They're being processed."

The word came out of me flat and official, the way I'd been trained to say it, and I watched it land on her face and do nothing. That was the thing about Dree. She'd been a hunter longer than I had. She knew all the words. She'd helped write some of them.

"Processed," she repeated.

"Registered. Interviewed. Whatever the current terminology is."

"And the hours?"

"Interviews run long." I shrugged, and I want it noted for the historical record that the shrug was flawless. Casual. Almost insulting in its ease. "Old people ramble. Children cry. You know how it goes."

"I do know how it goes," Dree said quietly. "That's rather the problem."

She let me go after that. Signed my leave slip without another word, stamped it, handed it back. And as I walked out of the office I felt the particular cold satisfaction of a man who has gotten away with something, followed immediately by the much less pleasant understanding that Dree did not sign things because she was satisfied. She signed things because she was patient.

Dree was the most patient person I had ever known. It was one of the many reasons I loved her, and it was about to become one of the reasons she destroyed me.

* * *

Here is what I could not tell her, arranged in order of treason:

Item the first: there was a scribe at the Temple of Ledgers named Lena Ashgrove, and I had found her mid-ritual during a raid and let her go, and then kept letting her go, night after night, while she taught me things about the hunt that made my oath taste like copper.

Item the second: beneath the palace archives there lived an archivist who was supposed to be dead or exiled or both, surrounded by books that would get every one of us hanged, and this archivist had shown me a binding rite older than the crown itself.

Item the third, and this was the one that sat in my chest like swallowed ice: the king was not mad. The king was occupied. And everything we were doing — the registrations, the interrogations, the Great Purification with its tidy proclamations and its tidy pyres — was not the crown protecting the realm from magic. It was magic wearing the crown, harvesting the realm.

I could not tell Dree any of this, because Dree was loyal the way bedrock is loyal. She did not bend, did not erode, did not ask whether the mountain above her deserved her. She held. If I told her and she believed me, she'd be a traitor by sundown. If I told her and she didn't believe me, I'd be in irons by the same sundown, and Lena and Sable would be in the ground by week's end.

So instead I gave her the half-lie, and I fed it to her slowly, like medicine to a horse that's already suspicious of the bucket.

* * *

The bucket arrived on a Thursday, in the form of mutton stew and a direct question.

"You're seeing someone," Dree said.

We were in the mess hall, which was generous terminology for a room with benches, a hearth that smoked, and food that had been a sheep in a previous life. Around us, two dozen hunters ate in the silence of people whose profession was listening. Dree had waited until we were in public to say it, which meant she wanted witnesses to my answer. Or insurance against it. With Dree it was often both.

"That's your theory?" I said. "I'm seeing someone?"

"It's the only theory that fits. A man sneaks out at odd hours, lies about where he's been, comes back smelling of candle smoke instead of tavern smoke—" she tapped the table, "—and starts taking leave he's refused for six years. Either he's seeing someone, or he's joined a cult. And you don't have the imagination for a cult."

The candle smoke. Of course. I'd been burning my coat over the hearth in Sable's archive room to kill the smell of old paper and lamp oil, and apparently I'd only managed to trade one incriminating smell for another. There was a lesson in that about half-measures, and I chose not to learn it.

"A woman," I said. "At the temple district. She works in the records office."

Every word of that was true. That was the architecture of a good half-lie — you built it entirely out of true stones and left out the mortar that connected them to anything that mattered.

Dree's eyebrows performed a small, skeptical maneuver. "A temple clerk."

"Scribe."

"A scribe." She said the word the way she'd say suspected smuggler. "And this scribe requires you to disappear for entire afternoons and come back looking like you've seen a ghost."

"She's teaching me to read Old Vaelic."

Silence. Somewhere across the hall, a spoon clinked against a bowl, and I felt two dozen hunters pretend very hard not to listen.

"Why," Dree said.

"Because the archives are full of documents in Old Vaelic, and Commander Hask keeps assigning me the ones nobody else can work through, and I've been faking my way through them for two years by recognizing maybe one word in ten and inventing the rest." I spread my hands. "You want to report me for self-improvement, be my guest. I'll write my confession in Old Vaelic so no one can read it."

That got a laugh out of the table, which was the point. Dree didn't laugh, but the corner of her mouth moved, which for Dree was practically applause. She studied me for a long moment — the counting eyes, the siege-gate face — and I sat there and let her count, and my heartbeat stayed level, because here was the terrible secret of the half-lie: it's easy when the lie is wrapped around something you actually feel. I *was* spending my afternoons with a woman in the temple district. She *was* teaching me things that rearranged how I saw the world. I simply declined to mention that what she was teaching me was that my life's work was a slaughterhouse with good stationery.

"All right," Dree said finally. "Your scribe. What's her name?"

And there it was. The trapdoor under the trapdoor.

Because here's the thing about names in Vaelmoor right now: they were currency, and they were ammunition, and the difference between the two depended entirely on who was holding them. If I gave Dree "Lena Ashgrove," and the Purification lists crossed her desk — and they would, because everything crossed Dree's desk, that was her function in the machine — then Lena Ashgrove would move from column A to column B, from *registered* to *summoned*, and summoned people went to the palace, and nobody came back from the palace.

"Lena," I said. "Lena Ashgrove."

I heard myself say it and felt something in me tilt, like a floorboard giving slightly underfoot. Not the name itself — the fact that I'd given it. Because I hadn't needed to. I could have said *she prefers I not say*, and Dree would have accepted it, because Dree respected privacy the way she respected walls: as things that existed for reasons. But I gave the name anyway, and I understood, in the slow cold way one understands things at the bottom of a stew bowl, exactly why.

Because if it ever came to it — if the truth ever came out, if I ever stood somewhere with my choices laid bare — I wanted Dree to remember that I lied to her face and used a real name doing it. I wanted her to have that. Loyalty deserves honesty about its betrayal, even if the honesty arrives late.

Or possibly I was just a coward who couldn't hold the shape of a second lie in one conversation. Both explanations fit. I've since decided they were both true, which is usually how it goes with the worst things we do.

"Ashgrove," Dree repeated, tasting it. "Debt-family? The ones with the ledger at Saint Verren's?"

"Something like that."

"Hm." She went back to her stew, and I thought — briefly, foolishly — that I'd cleared it. That the bucket had gone down smooth.

Then, without looking up: "Bring her by sometime. I'll want to meet the woman making my best hunter literate."

* * *

I did not bring her by. Obviously. Bringing Lena Ashgrove within a hundred yards of Captain Dree would be like bringing a lit match to inspect a powder store. But I also couldn't refuse outright, because refusal was a flag on the map, and Dree collected flags.

So I did what I was becoming expert at, which was worse. I stalled. I said the temple kept odd hours, that Lena's superiors frowned on visitors, that I'd arrange something soon. Each excuse was small. Each excuse was reasonable. Each excuse was another stone in a wall I was building between myself and the only person in the world whose opinion of me I actually feared.

And Dree let me build it. That was the part I didn't understand yet. She asked nothing further, pressed nothing, and I mistook her silence for acceptance, because I was arrogant enough to believe I was better at lying than she was at listening.

What I know now — what I learned later, at cost — is that Dree wasn't accepting anything. She was watching. She'd already pulled the duty logs. She'd already noted that my "reading lessons" coincided, hour for hour, with restricted-access periods in the under-archive. She'd already sent a quiet query to the temple records office asking after a scribe named Ashgrove, and received back a personnel file that listed Lena Ashgrove's debt-contract as *transferred to crown custody, by order of the Privy Seal*, which was a phrase that appears in exactly one kind of file, and Dree knew that kind of file intimately, because she'd written dozens of them herself.

She knew, in other words, before I ever opened my mouth, that the woman I was protecting was property of the thing on the throne. She just didn't know yet whether I was its agent or its enemy.

She was giving me rope. Dree always gave rope. It was how she'd caught three smugglers, one assassin, and a corrupt quartermaster, and it was how she was going to catch me: not by chasing, but by standing very still while I ran in circles around her, tying myself up.

* * *

The last time I saw her before everything broke open was on the garrison steps, near midnight, in the rain.

I was coming in late again — later than even my new pattern allowed, because Sable had found something in the rite texts that had made Lena go white and had made me go out and stand in an alley for twenty minutes breathing like a landed fish. I climbed the steps and Dree was there, alone, hood up, watching the rain come down on the drill yard.

"Long lesson," she said.

"The Old Vaelic verb system is a war crime."

"Mm." She didn't turn. Rain ran off her hood in a fine silver curtain. "Corin. When you were twelve, and your father's shop burned, and everyone said it was the Ashen Circle, and you swore you'd hunt them to the last man — do you remember what you said to me?"

I remembered. I remembered everything about that year; grief files things sharply. "I said I'd never be on the wrong side of a fire."

"And I said, *then you'd better make sure you know who's holding the torch.*" Now she turned, and her face under the hood was unreadable, which from Dree was louder than shouting. "I've been thinking about that lately. About torches. About who's holding ours."

"Dree—"

"Whatever it is you're doing," she said, "whoever she is, whatever you think you've found down there in the dark — you come to me. Before it's too far to come back from. That's not an order. That's the oath underneath the oath, the one we made each other when we were stupid and nineteen and the world still had clean edges in it." She stepped past me toward the door, and paused, close enough that I could see the rain beaded on her lashes. "I'm giving you until the new moon, Corin. After that, I stop asking."

She went inside. I stood on the steps in the rain for a long time, doing arithmetic I didn't like.

The new moon was nine days away. In nine days, either I brought Dree into the worst truth in Vaelmoor, or she came for me with the full weight of the hunt behind her — and behind her, if it came to that, I'd stand there and let her take me, because there are limits even to treason, and mine had her name on them.

Nine days. I turned the number over and found it wanting, because Sable's latest translation had put a different timeline under it. The rite texts spoke of a convergence, a night when the Regent's hold would be strong enough to finish what five years of slow possession had begun — and by Sable's reckoning, that night fell eight days from now.

One day short of Dree's deadline. One day short of a choice, delivered to me by the universe with its usual sense of humor.

I went inside, dried off, sharpened my knife, and wrote in my private log — the coded one, the one that would hang me twice over if anyone ever read it — a single line:

*Told her nothing. Told her almost everything. Pray the difference doesn't bury us all.*

Then I blew out the candle, and lay in the dark listening to the rain, and somewhere across the city a bell tolled midnight, and beneath the palace, in the dark under the dark, something old turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to count the days too.
