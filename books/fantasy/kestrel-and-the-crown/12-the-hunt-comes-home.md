## 12. The Hunt Comes Home

I have a theory about guilt. I've had time to develop it, the way a man in a leaky boat develops theories about water: intimately, and against his will.

My theory is this — guilt is not a weight. Everyone says it's a weight, something you carry, something that presses on your shoulders like a pack full of stones. That's wrong. Guilt is a sound. It's the sound of your own machinery running smoothly, gears turning exactly as they were designed to turn, and somewhere inside you a small voice screaming *stop* while every other part of you keeps saying *this is what we're for.*

I heard that sound very clearly on the night the hunt came to the under-archive.

* * *

It started with Dree.

Captain Dree has a nose for trouble the way hounds have a nose for blood — not trained into her, just there, some old animal inheritance she never bothered to apologize for. She'd been watching me for two weeks. I knew it the way you know weather's turning: not from any single sign, but from the accumulation of them. The way she stopped joking with me at muster. The way my patrol assignments started arriving with gaps in them, hours unaccounted for, hours she'd marked herself.

"You've been going somewhere," she said that evening, leaning against the doorframe of the hunters' quarters while I buckled on my gear. Not accusing. Worse than accusing. Curious.

"I walk," I said. "It helps me think."

"Corin Vale doesn't walk. Corin Vale drills, reports, drinks exactly two cups of wine, and sleeps like a man who's earned it." She tilted her head. "You've been sleeping badly too. Don't ask how I know. You snore differently when you're lying to someone."

"That's absurd."

"It's true." She pushed off the doorframe. "Whatever it is, Vale — bring it to me before it brings itself to me. That's all I'm asking."

And there it was, the half-lie I'd fed her weeks ago coming home to roost, fat and feathered and ready to ruin everything. I'd told her I was investigating irregularities in the temple district's registration rolls. A tidy little fiction. It explained my absences, my questions, my late nights. What it did not explain was why I was about to spend those late nights in a banned library with a fugitive archivist and a debt-scribe who could set the air on fire if you looked at her wrong.

"I will," I said. "When there's something worth bringing."

She looked at me a long moment, and I watched her decide — visibly, the way you watch someone count coins — to believe me one more time. Then she left, and I stood there feeling like the worst kind of thief: the kind who steals from people who leave their doors unlocked because they trust him.

Then I went to betray her anyway. Because that's the thing about machinery. It runs.

* * *

The under-archive smelled of dust and lamp oil and, lately, of Sable Ives's terrible cooking — she'd taken to boiling some root or other over a brazier down there, claiming it kept her mind sharp, though I suspect it was mostly an act of war against our noses.

We'd been at the binding rites for six days. Six days of Lena copying passages by lamplight while Sable lectured, pacing between shelves like a general reviewing troops made entirely of paper. Six days of learning things I wished I could unlearn. Three kings worn like coats. A rite of unbinding requiring consent, a name, and a life offered freely — that last part still sitting in my chest like a swallowed hook, because nobody would say plainly what "a life offered freely" meant, and nobody saying it was its own kind of answer.

"The Regent won't wait forever," Sable said that night, jabbing a finger at a diagram of the coronation rite. "It's been patient for five years because patience was working. But the Purification changed the arithmetic. It's burning through the gifted now, testing vessels, and when it finds one it likes—"

A knock.

Not on a door. There are no doors down there worth knocking on. It came through the floor — three slow impacts, deliberate, spaced like a man tapping a staff. And then, fainter, from above: boots. Many boots. The particular rhythm of a search detail moving through a building they intend to empty.

Lena's head came up like a kestrel's. "That's the temple," she whispered. "They're in the temple."

"They can't be," I said, and hated myself even as I said it, because I knew exactly how they could be. "The under-archive isn't on any survey. I checked the surveys myself."

"When?"

"Three weeks ago."

Sable was already moving, snuffing lamps with her palms, her voice dropping into that flat register she used when the lecture was over. "Surveys get amended, hunter. Kings get new advisors. Advisors get ambitious." She pressed a bundle of papers into Lena's arms — the rite, copied in her cramped hand — and shoved both of us toward the far wall, where a crawl-space behind the shelving opened onto the old cistern tunnels. "Go. Through the cisterns, up by the tannery. Don't stop, don't talk, don't be heroes."

"What about you?" Lena asked, and I saw it hit her face a half-second before it hit mine — the arithmetic of three people and one exit.

"I'm going to stay here," Sable said pleasantly, "and misfile things."

"Sable—"

"Ives!" A voice from above, muffled by stone but unmistakable. Commander Hask's voice. My commander. Leading the detail personally, which meant this wasn't a sweep. This was a targeted strike, which meant someone had talked, which meant—

Which meant it didn't matter whose fault it was. Later I'd turn over every candidate: Dree, a temple clerk, my own sloppy footwork through the district. Later I'd do that. In the moment there was only the sound of my own machinery, running beautifully, carrying me toward the exit while an old woman stayed behind to be devoured by it.

"I've survived thirty years down here by knowing when to be found and when not to be," Sable said, pushing us harder. "If they find nothing but a madwoman among books, they'll write me up as a vagrant. If they find *you*, they'll write up something worse. Go."

Lena went. I went. The last thing I saw of the under-archive was Sable Ives lighting a single lamp, settling into her chair, and folding her hands like a woman preparing to receive guests she'd been expecting for years.

The last thing I heard was the ceiling giving way behind us as they broke through the floor.

* * *

The cistern tunnels run beneath the temple district like veins under skin, and we moved through them in the dark, Lena's hand locked around my wrist, both of us guided by the trickle of water and the growing thunder of boots overhead. Every few yards another impact shook grit from the ceiling. They weren't searching the archive. They were dismantling it. Shelving, I thought. They'll burn the shelving in the yard, and the books with it, and thirty years of the only honest history Vaelmoor has left will go up as fuel for the lie.

"We should go back," Lena said.

"No."

"There are books down there. The *rite*—"

"She gave you the rite." I tapped the bundle against her ribs. "Everything else is ash waiting to happen. Keep moving."

She kept moving, but I felt the argument in her silence, coiling. Lena Ashgrove argues with her whole body when she's not allowed to argue out loud. Her shoulders do most of it.

We reached the tannery ladder as the first light of the fires found the tunnel mouth — orange flicker, rising smoke, and threaded through it, thin and awful, a sound I recognized from a dozen raids. The sound of a person being read their crimes. I couldn't hear the words. I didn't need to. I knew the liturgy. I'd spoken it myself more times than I could count, back when each word fit my mouth like it belonged there.

Lena gripped the ladder and didn't climb. "Corin."

"We go up."

"She stayed for us."

"She stayed because staying was the plan, and the plan was hers, and she's forgotten more about survival than I'll ever learn." I put my hand over hers on the rung. It was shaking. So was mine; I chose to believe it was cold. "Up, Ashgrove."

She climbed. I followed. And that should have been the end of it — escape, regroup, grieve later — except that the tannery yard sits below the street level, and the street level, that night, was full of hunters.

* * *

We came out through a grate into the alley behind the rendering sheds, and I did what I'd done a hundred times: took stock. Two exits. One blocked by a patrol walking the lane. The other opening onto the tanner's yard, where a wagon stood loaded with barrels, and beside the wagon—

Dree.

Of course it was Dree. The hunt comes home and it sends the person who trusts you to meet it, because the universe is a scribe with a cruel sense of structure. She was directing the loading of seized material — crates of books, wrapped bundles, a brazier already lit for the burning — and she was doing it efficiently, competently, without pleasure, which is the worst way to watch someone do a terrible thing well.

I pulled Lena back into the shadow of the shed. "New plan," I breathed. "We wait for the patrol to pass, then we walk out the front like citizens. Slowly. Boredly."

"And Dree?"

"Dree is looking for a fugitive archivist and contraband texts. She is not looking for a hunter taking his evening constitutional with a scribe from the temple." I hoped. I prayed, in the wordless way of men who haven't prayed since boyhood. "Keep your eyes down. You're tired, you're my responsibility, you want your bed."

"Is any of that going to require acting?"

"Ashgrove, you once convinced a tax assessor you were dying of a wasting illness to dodge a levy. You'll manage."

The patrol passed. We stepped out into the lane, and I arranged my face into the particular blankness of a man thinking about nothing, and for eleven steps it worked.

On the twelfth step, Dree looked up.

I watched it happen — watched her eyes find me, flick past, snap back. Watched the recognition land, and then the question behind it: *why is Corin Vale emerging from a tannery alley at midnight during a raid he wasn't assigned to?* Her hand didn't go to her sword. That was the mercy. Her face did something worse; it went carefully, deliberately neutral, the face of a woman deciding not to decide yet.

"Vale." Flat. Even. "You're a long way from your sector."

"Escorting a temple scribe home. The district's unsettled tonight."

"So it is." Her gaze moved to Lena, catalogued her — the ink stains, the bundle clutched too tightly, the fear she was holding the way you hold a full cup across a crowded room. "Your scribe looks frightened."

"It's an unsettling night, Captain."

Something passed across her face then, quick as a fish under ice. Doubt, maybe. Or the beginning of doubt. Or the decision to postpone doubt until a more convenient hour, which is how loyalty survives most of its bad nights.

"Get her home," Dree said finally. "Then report to me. Tomorrow. First bell." A pause, and quieter, almost gentle, which was somehow the most frightening tone available to her: "Bring me the truth this time, Corin. Whatever shape it's in. I'd rather have it ugly than have it neat and false."

"Yes, Captain."

We walked. She let us walk. I did not breathe properly again until we'd turned three corners, and then I made the mistake of breathing out a laugh that came out closer to a sob, and Lena grabbed my sleeve and steered me into a doorway and held onto me while the shaking finished having its way.

"She knows," Lena said.

"She suspects. There's a difference, and tomorrow morning it stops mattering, because suspicion with evidence becomes certainty, and I have until first bell to decide what I tell her."

"What *will* you tell her?"

I looked at the bundle in her arms. The rite. Consent, a name, a life offered freely. Somewhere across the city, in a cell or a cart or a room with comfortable chairs and terrible questions, Sable Ives was being asked what she knew, and every answer she gave or refused was buying us time we hadn't earned.

"I don't know," I said. It was the truest sentence I'd spoken in weeks, and it tasted like ash and like relief, both at once. "Ask me at first bell."

* * *

Here is what I did instead of sleeping.

I sat in the hunters' quarters with a cup of wine I didn't drink, and I reconstructed the raid from the reports coming in. Seized: four hundred volumes, banned or unbanned — the distinction had stopped mattering to the detail doing the hauling. One prisoner: female, older, identified provisionally as "Ives, S., archivist, sedition suspected." Held pending interrogation at the palace. Transferred at dawn.

The palace. Where the courtiers' eyes are glassy and the king repeats himself like a broken music box and the crown, if you look too long, breathes.

They weren't taking Sable to a cell. They were taking her to *it*. And the Regent, whatever else it was, was not stupid. It had spent five years wearing a kingdom; it would peel an archivist open in an afternoon and know everything — the rite, the plan, the debt-scribe with the amended contract, the hunter who'd gone soft.

I wrote my report to Dree four times. The first three versions were lies of increasing elegance. The fourth was one line:

*The king is not mad. He is occupied. I can prove it. Come alone.*

I sealed it. I held it. I thought about Sable's hands folded in her lap, waiting for guests she'd expected. I thought about the child with no gift, months ago, tears cutting tracks through soot, and how I'd told myself the hunt was salvageable, reformable, that good men inside it could steer it. Machinery doesn't steer. Machinery only runs, until something breaks it.

At first bell, I put the letter in Dree's hands, and I watched her read it, and I said, "Before you decide what I am, Captain — come see what the king is."

Her eyes came up. Behind the suspicion, behind the hurt I'd put there and couldn't take back, something else was moving — the same animal nose that had scented my trouble for weeks, now scenting something larger.

And that was when the palace bells began to ring, all of them at once, the alarm pattern I'd only ever heard drilled and never lived — and a runner burst through the doors shouting the words that turned my blood to ice water:

"The archivist talked! The Regent wants the debt-scribe brought in — the girl Ashgrove — by nightfall!"

Dree looked at the letter. Looked at me. And reached for her sword.
