## 13. Audience with the Crown

They came for me at dawn, which I thought was a bit theatrical even for witch-hunters. Dawn is for executions and weddings. For arrests you want the gray hour before, when the streets are empty and nobody's awake to notice a woman being folded into a carriage like laundry.

But no one had asked my opinion on staging, so I kept it to myself as four of them marched me down the temple steps — *my* temple steps, where I'd copied ledgers for six years without so much as a raised eyebrow — and into a black carriage with the crown's sigil on the door. The sigil had been repainted recently. The gold was still tacky.

"Comfortable?" asked the hunter across from me.

"No."

"Good." He seemed satisfied with that, as though discomfort were proof of guilt, or perhaps proof of honesty. With this crowd it was hard to tell the difference.

The ride to the palace took longer than it should have. Vaelmoor isn't that large, but the palace sits at the city's heart like a stone in a fruit, and every road leading to it has been rerouted, walled, or renamed since the Purification began. We passed through three checkpoints. At each one, a guard looked at my face, then at a paper, then back at my face, with the particular concentration of a man comparing a portrait to its subject and finding the subject wanting.

At the third checkpoint, the guard hesitated.

"She matches," my escort said, bored.

"I know she matches. It's just—" The guard lowered his voice, though carriages carry sound terribly. "The summons went out yesterday. Before the report on her charms was filed."

My escort paused. Then he said, "Noted," in the tone people use for things they intend never to think about again, and we rolled on.

I sat very still and did some arithmetic I didn't like.

* * *

They took my boots at the servants' entrance. Not my laces, not my belt — my boots. A small woman with a clipboard handed me felt slippers and wrote something down when I stepped out of them, as if the act of surrendering footwear were itself evidence.

"Why the slippers?" I asked.

"The king dislikes the sound of heels on marble," she said, not looking up.

"The king used to hold midwinter feasts in the great hall. I've heard the stories. You couldn't hear yourself think over the dancing."

Her pen stopped. Just for a moment. Then it resumed, and she said, "The king dislikes the sound of heels on marble," exactly as before, same inflection, same pause between *dislikes* and *the*. Like a coin dropped in the same groove twice.

I shut up after that.

They walked me through corridors that grew quieter and grander by degrees, the way water grows colder as you descend. Servants pressed themselves against walls when we passed. Not bowing — *pressing*, the way you press against a wall when a bull is coming down the corridor and you don't want to be interesting. Their eyes slid over me and away. One young maid looked directly at me, and her expression wasn't fear. It was apology. She mouthed something I couldn't catch, and then an older servant pulled her back into a doorway, and the corridor swallowed them both.

Here is what I noticed, and I noticed it because I've spent six years reading documents for a living, which teaches you to read everything: the palace was clean. Not tidy — *clean*. No dust in the corners of the window frames. No scuff marks at the baseboards. No candle wax dripped and hardened on the sills. Buildings live, and living things leave traces, and this place had been scoured of every trace except the people moving through it, who left nothing either. They walked softly. They spoke softly. When a courtier crossed our path, he inclined his head to us, smiled, and held the smile a half-second past the point where smiles naturally end, like a door left open on a room you'd rather not see.

And under all of it — under the marble and the silence and the smiling — there was a hum. Not a sound. A pressure. The way the air feels before lightning, stretched thin and waiting. My gift woke in my chest like a cat hearing a dog two streets away, hackles up, growling at nothing.

*Something is wrong here,* it said.

*Yes,* I told it. *Thank you. Very helpful.*

* * *

The throne room doors were oak and iron, tall enough to admit a mounted rider, and they opened without anyone touching them. I want to be clear about that. There were guards on either side, ceremonial, holding halberds they clearly hadn't swung in years. Neither moved. The doors simply decided to be open, and we walked through.

The throne room of Vaelmoor was built to intimidate petitioners: forty yards of polished floor, banners of every province hanging dead-still in air that should have been moving them, and at the far end, the dais, and on the dais, the throne, and in the throne—

King Aldric.

I'd seen him twice from a distance, at festival processions, back when festivals were still allowed. He'd been a big man then, broad through the shoulders, with a beard going gray at the edges and a laugh people said could be heard three streets off. The man in the throne was the right shape. That was the first thing I understood, standing there in my felt slippers: whoever sat in that seat had been fitted to King Aldric the way a hand is fitted to a glove, and the fit was good, and that was somehow worse than if it hadn't been.

He was talking to a courtier. Or rather, he was saying sentences at a courtier, who nodded at intervals like a metronome.

"—and the harvest will be sufficient. The harvest will be sufficient. Tell the eastern provinces the harvest will be—"

"Sire." My escort dropped to one knee. I stayed standing, partly out of defiance and partly because I genuinely didn't know the protocol for prisoners in felt slippers.

The king turned his head.

I have seen a lot of wrong things in the last year. I watched a neighbor's shop burn with everyone cheering. I found five years of my temple's records cut out like a tumor. I saw Corin Vale commit treason with his face perfectly blank. None of it prepared me for the king's eyes.

They were brown. Kind brown, once, I'd have guessed. And behind them, deep down at the bottom like a stone at the bottom of a well, something looked out. Something that had been looking out for a long time and had gotten very, very practiced at wearing the face above it.

It looked at me, and it smiled with Aldric's mouth, and it said:

"Lena Ashgrove."

My whole body went cold. Not fear-cold. Recognition-cold. Because here's the thing about your own name: when someone says it correctly, with the weight in the right places, the *Ash* falling just so — you relax. Your body says *home*. And my body started to relax, and then caught itself, horrified, like a foot finding a stair that isn't there.

"You know my name," I managed.

"I have known your name since before you could write it." The Regent — and I knew, suddenly and completely, that it was the Regent speaking, that Aldric was somewhere far below this voice like a man locked in a cellar under a party — rose from the throne. The courtiers sank with him, all together, a field of wheat bending under wind. "Dismissed," it said, and the room emptied the way water empties from a basin, quickly and without argument, until there was only me, four hundred tons of marble, and the thing wearing the crown.

It descended the dais steps. Slowly. Savoring it, maybe, or maybe the body was old and the thing inside it hadn't bothered learning to move it well. Up close, I could see the seams. The blink that came a fraction too late. The breath that happened on schedule rather than on need. And the crown — gods, the crown. Everyone knows the Crown of Vaelmoor, iron worked with kestrels in flight, older than the city. What nobody knows, what I would have given anything not to know, is that it *moves*. Not visibly. But the kestrels weren't in quite the same positions each time I looked, and between looks, I could feel them shifting, settling, like birds rearranging themselves on a branch.

"Do you know why you're here, Lena?"

"To register. As gifted. Under the Edict of Purification."

"That is why you were *told* you are here." It stopped three paces away, close enough that I could smell it — and it smelled like nothing, which is its own kind of terrible, because every person smells like something, and this smelled like a room that had been aired. "You are here because the contract has matured."

"What contract?"

It tilted Aldric's head, and the gesture was almost fond. Almost paternal. That was the worst part; it wore kindness the way it wore the king, competently, and I could see the competence, and knowing made it no easier to resist.

"Your debt. Your family's debt to the Temple of Records. Sixty crowns, incurred the winter your mother was taken. Amended, seven years ago, by a clause you never signed." It let that sit. It wanted to watch me do the arithmetic. So I did it, hating every number.

Seven years ago. The year I came of age. The year the high scribe gave me my own copying desk and told me I had a gift for the work. The year somebody's pen had moved across page nine of my contract while I slept in the dormitory, and written *me* into a ledger I'd never seen.

"The temple sold me," I said. My voice came out flat. "To the crown."

"The temple sold you to *us*, yes. Though 'sold' is ungenerous. We prefer 'conveyed.' Property changes hands; vessels are conveyed." It spread Aldric's hands, conciliatory. "You were bred for this, in a sense. Three generations of small magics, quietly encouraged, quietly recorded. Your grandmother's charms. Your mother's knotted threads. Your little luck tokens sold at the docks. Each generation a little stronger. Each contract a little deeper. You are the finest work the Ledger has ever produced, Lena Ashgrove, and I mean that as praise."

The floor seemed to tilt. I put my weight on my heels and refused to sway.

"My mother is dead," I said. "She was taken when I was nine. Nobody comes back from the hunts."

"No," it agreed pleasantly. "Nobody comes back from the *hunts*."

* * *

I want to describe what it did next, and I'm going to fail, because language was built for describing weather and bread and the faces of people you love, and this was none of those things.

It reached out — not with Aldric's hands, though the hands rose slightly, palms up, a showman's gesture — and it *opened* something in the air between us, the way you'd open a book. And inside it was a kitchen.

Our kitchen. Ashgrove kitchen, above the charm shop, with the crooked shelf my father never got around to fixing and the blue pot with the dented lid and the window that fogged in winter because Mother always had water boiling for tea whether anyone wanted tea or not. And at the table, her back to me, hair pinned up with the bone comb I still keep at the bottom of my trunk, humming. Off-key. She always hummed off-key, and I had forgotten that, and the forgetting had been a wound I'd stopped noticing, and now it was torn open and salted and I could hear the tune.

"Mother," I said. My voice cracked down the middle like cheap plate.

She turned.

Lena. *Little kestrel.* Come sit, the tea's gone cold again, I swear you'd let the kettle die before you'd admit you want—

"Don't." I squeezed my eyes shut. Tears anyway. Of course tears anyway. "Don't do this."

Do what? Warm and amused. The vision-Mother leaned her chin on her hand, exactly the way she used to when I was stalling over homework. I'm only talking to my daughter. Is that a crime in Vaelmoor now too?

"It's not her." I said it to myself, to the floor, to whatever was left of me that could still count numbers and add two and two. "She was taken eleven years ago. The hunts don't return people. Whatever this is, it's bait."

Bait, the Regent agreed, and the kitchen flickered like a candle in wind, and for one merciful second I saw through it — the shape beneath, vast and patient and *pleased* — and then the kitchen was back, steadier than before, and Mother laughed, and my knees nearly went.

But here is what the Regent had not counted on, or had counted on and misjudged, and I'll be grateful until I die for the arrogance: it showed me my mother's hands.

And her hands were wrong.

Mother had a scar on her left thumb, a white line from a paring knife, from the day she taught me to cut apples and I learned more from watching her flinch than from any lecture. The hands in the vision were smooth. Unscarred. Hands remembered by someone who had seen them ten thousand times but never *looked* — hands rendered by a thing that collected memories the way I collected charms, without understanding what made any of them precious.

"You're reading her from my memory," I said. "You don't have her. You never had her. You're showing me my own recollection with the flaws sanded off, and you got the scar wrong."

The kitchen stopped.

For a long moment the Regent stood there in Aldric's body, and the smile drained out of the king's face by degrees, like water out of a cracked bowl, until what remained was worse than the smile: naked attention. The well-stone rising toward the surface. Somewhere far beneath the crown, I felt — or imagined, or hoped — something else stir. Something that heard the word *wrong* and pressed against the inside of its prison, just once, like a fist against a wall.

"You are cleverer than the projections allowed for," the Regent said at last. No warmth now. Good. I could bargain with cold. Warmth was a knife I couldn't see.

"Everyone says that," I said, wiping my face with my sleeve, because dignity was already dead and I might as well be comfortable. "What do you actually want?"

"Willingness." It began to walk, slow circles around me, and I turned with it, refusing to let it get behind me, which amused it — I felt the amusement, a dry rustle at the edge of my mind, like pages turning. "The binding requires consent freely given. That is the oldest clause and the deepest. I have worn three kings, Lena, and each was taken, and each fights me, and the fighting costs. Aldric has cost me five years of strength. Do you understand? Every day he pushes against the walls, and every day I must rebuild them. I am tired of building walls."

"So find another king to steal."

"Kings are heavy," it said, with something like weariness, and I hated that I recognized the weariness, hated that some part of me thought *yes, carrying other people's expectations is exhausting, I know that feeling.* That's how it gets in. That's how it's always gotten in. Not through gates. Through the ordinary ache of being a person who wants rest. "A vessel must be strong enough to hold me and willing enough to stop resisting. Kings are rarely willing. They like their thrones. But a debtor—" it stopped in front of me, and Aldric's kind brown eyes looked into mine, and the crown's kestrels shifted, all together, like a flock deciding on a direction, "—a debtor knows all about serving terms she never chose. I am offering you a choice, Lena Ashgrove. Which is more than this kingdom has offered anyone in a generation."

"Say it plainly."

"Consent to the binding, and your debts are void. All of them. The temple's claim, the crown's claim, the amended clause — ash. Your mother's name is struck from every hunt record in the archive, retroactively, immaculately. And the woman in the kitchen—" a pause, delicate, surgical, "—is alive. In a cell beneath this palace, has been for eleven years, because I do not waste useful vessels, and hers was a fine one. Refuse, and you go to the registration rolls with the others, and the others do not return, and she dies of old age in the dark, and you will spend the rest of your life knowing that a kitchen existed, once, in which she was alive, and you walked out of it."

The throne room was silent. The banners hung dead. Somewhere in the walls, faint as a heartbeat, the hum continued.

And I stood there in my felt slippers, and I did the most dangerous thing I have ever done, which was to *want it*. Fully. Honestly. To let the wanting fill me up like floodwater, my mother's off-key humming, the blue pot, the crooked shelf, eleven years of grief handed back to me mended — and then, standing waist-deep in the flood, I looked down and checked my pockets.

The scar. The kettle. The way she'd said *little kestrel* — warm, yes, but the Regent had said *Lena Ashgrove* warmly too, and warmth proves nothing from a thing that wears people. If my mother was truly below this palace, I'd find her with my own feet, not with a leash of longing that led straight into a crown.

"No," I said.

The word fell into the silence like a coin down a well.

"No?" The Regent's head cocked. Genuinely curious. That frightened me more than anger would have.

"I don't accept gifts from creditors," I said. "I've read enough contracts to know the interest always comes due. And you got the scar wrong."

For a heartbeat — one true heartbeat — something moved behind the king's face that was not patience, not curiosity, not the dry rustle of pages. Something old and hungry and *surprised*, and in that surprise I saw the truth underneath all the velvet: it had never been refused before. Three kings, a generation of purges, a city trained to kneel, and no one had ever stood in front of it in felt slippers and said no.

Then the mask reassembled, piece by piece, smile last.

"Guards," it said, mildly, to the empty room, and the doors opened, and this time men came through them, real ones, with irons. "Take her to the cells. The good ones." And as they took my arms, the Regent leaned close, and Aldric's voice dropped to something meant for me alone, soft as a secret:

"We will continue our negotiations, Mistress Ashgrove. I am very patient. Ask the king."

They dragged me out past the dais, and as I went, fighting the irons, my heel — slipper, rather — caught the bottom step, and I stumbled, and in the stumble I looked up one last time at the throne.

And the king looked back.

Not the Regent. The eyes were different — wet, wide, human, *terrified* — and his lips moved around words with no breath behind them, shaping them over and over, and it took me three tries to read them.

*Don't save me.*

*Don't save me.*

*Don't—*

The doors closed. The guards tightened their grip. And somewhere ahead of me, down stairs I couldn't yet count, the cells of the palace waited — and beside whichever cell they'd give me, I'd have sworn on my mother's false, scarless hands I heard a familiar voice already arguing with a jailer, low and furious and unmistakably Corin Vale's.
