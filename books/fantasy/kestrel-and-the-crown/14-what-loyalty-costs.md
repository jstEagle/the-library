## 14. What Loyalty Costs

There is a particular way a soldier cleans her sword when she wants you to know she's cleaning it *at* you. Captain Dree had been doing it for ten minutes.

I stood in the doorway of the garrison armory and watched her run the whetstone down the blade's edge — slow, even strokes, the sound like a held breath being let out over and over. She didn't look up. She didn't need to. Twenty years of serving beside Maren Dree had taught me that she did her most dangerous thinking in silence, and that the silence got longer the worse the news was going to be.

"Corin," she said at last, without looking at me. "Shut the door."

I shut it. The latch clicked, and the sound was very small and very loud, the way a latch sounds when you're on the wrong side of it.

"Sit."

"I'd rather stand."

"I know you would." She set the sword across her knees and finally looked up, and I wished she hadn't. There was no anger in her face. Anger I could have handled. What was there instead was something far more terrible: grief, already finished grieving, folded away neat as a burial shroud. "You'd rather stand because standing means you can leave. Sit down anyway."

I sat.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew about Maren Dree before that evening, so you understand what I lost.

She'd pulled me out of a burning granary when I was nineteen and too stupid to be afraid. She'd taught me to read a room the way other men read a page — where the exits were, who was lying, whose hands were too clean. When my father died she sat with me through the wake and drank exactly one cup of wine and said nothing, which was worth more than every speech the priest made. She was the only person alive who called me by my mother's name for me — *Kit*, soft, only when we were alone — and she was the reason I'd survived eleven years in an order that ate its own.

And she was loyal. That was the thing about Dree. Not pious-loyal, not oath-on-the-wall loyal. She was loyal the way stone is heavy: not a choice she kept making but simply what she was. The crown, the order, the chain of command — she believed in them the way farmers believe in rain. You don't argue with rain. You build your life around it or you drown.

Which meant that when she said, "Tell me about the temple district," I understood, with a cold settling in my stomach, that this conversation had only one ending, and that she already knew it too.

"Routine sweeps," I said. "You signed the rotation yourself."

"Mm." She picked up the whetstone again. One stroke. Two. "Funny thing about routine sweeps. They generate reports. And funny thing about your reports, Kit — they've gotten very dull lately. A man can write dull reports two ways. Because there's nothing to say." Stroke. "Or because he doesn't want anyone reading what he has to say."

"Maybe I've just gotten boring."

"You were never boring. Boring men don't get promoted past me." She smiled, briefly, without any warmth in it at all. "Three weeks ago you requisitioned a key to the temple under-archive. Sealed level. Your authorization came from no one above me, which means either someone forged the requisition or you used a dead man's seal, and I checked both, and neither happened. Which leaves a third option I don't like."

"Dree—"

"Two weeks ago, a raid on the temple scriptorium. Your name's on the manifest. Six arrests ordered. Zero arrests made. The report says the premises were found empty." She looked at me then, fully, and her voice went quiet in the way that made recruits stand straighter. "I walked that scriptorium myself yesterday, Corin. It isn't empty. There are scorch marks on the floor in the shape of a warding circle, and candle wax still soft in the corners, and a child's chalk drawing behind a shelf that says *thank you* with a little bird drawn next to it. Somebody was mid-ritual when your squad arrived. Somebody left through a door somebody else opened. And the hunter on record for that raid has stopped writing anything in his reports that could hang him."

The whetstone went down. She laid both hands flat on the table between us, and I noticed they weren't quite steady, and that frightened me more than anything else she'd said.

"So," she said. "Tell me about the temple district."

* * *

Here is what I should have done, if I were the man my oaths said I was.

I should have lied well. I'm a good liar; the hunt teaches you that, teaches you to wear sincerity like armor plate. I should have given her a story about following a smuggling ring, about a sealed archive full of contraband grimoires I was tracking alone, about how the empty arrests were bait I hadn't sprung yet. She might have believed it. She wanted to believe it — I could see the want sitting behind her eyes like a lamp behind a shutter, and God help me, I loved her for it.

Instead I heard myself say: "If I tell you the truth, you'll have to arrest me."

Her jaw tightened. "Try me."

"The king is possessed."

The words came out flat and plain, and once they were out I couldn't take them back, and I found I didn't want to. Eleven years of carrying them in pieces — the glassy courtiers, the repeated phrases, the crown that moved when nothing moved it — and now they were out in the open air of a garrison armory, hanging between us like smoke.

Dree didn't laugh. That was the worst part. A laugh I could have worked with. She just went very still, the way she went still before ordering a charge, and said, "Say that again."

"The king is possessed. Has been since his coronation. There's an entity — the old records call it the Regent — and it's worn three kings before Aldric, and it's wearing him now, and the Purification—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "The Purification isn't a hunt for traitors, Maren. It's a harvest. Every gifted person dragged into that palace is feeding it. We're not the crown's sword. We're its hand. Its *mouth*. We've been delivering meals to the thing on the throne and calling it duty."

Silence. Long enough that I could hear the armory clock, the one her predecessor had stolen off a merchantman and never been punished for.

"That's madness," she said at last, but softly, testing the words rather than throwing them. "That's sedition dressed up as revelation. Where did you hear it?"

"In the under-archive. From a woman named Sable Ives, who's spent nine years living among banned books and knows more real history than the entire College of Heralds. From temple records with five years cut out of them. From the king himself, standing thirty feet away, saying *the realm endures, the realm endures, the realm endures* like a man reciting lines he's forgotten the meaning of." I leaned forward. "Come with me. Tonight. I'll show you. If I'm mad, arrest me for madness and be done with it. But come and look first."

"And if you're not mad?" Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "If you're right, Corin — do you understand what you're asking? You're asking me to believe that everything I've given my life to is a feeding trough. That every arrest I made, every sentence I carried out, every family I broke apart in the king's name—" She stopped. Her hands had closed into fists on the table. "You're asking me to burn down the inside of my own head and call it clarity."

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly what I'm asking."

"Because it's true?"

"Because it's true."

She looked at me for a long time. And I watched her do the arithmetic — I saw it move behind her face, the terrible honest arithmetic of a woman weighing twenty years against five weeks of evidence, loyalty against truth, the self she'd built against the self the truth demanded. I have never in my life wanted anything as much as I wanted her to finish that sum the right way.

She didn't.

* * *

"No," she said.

Not *no, you're lying.* Not *no, prove it.* Just — no. Flat, final, the word of a woman closing a door from the inside.

"Maren—"

"No. Listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once." She stood, and she was crying — I realized it with a jolt, tears running silently down a face that otherwise might have been carved from parade-ground granite — and she went on speaking in that same level voice as though the tears belonged to someone else. "I can't do it. Do you hear me? I can't. You're asking me to become a different person, and I don't have a different person in me. There's just this one. This stupid, faithful one, who buried a husband the crown paid for and a son the crown spent, and who got up the next morning and put the uniform on because the alternative was admitting it had all been for nothing." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, briskly, like brushing off rain. "If it's all for nothing, Kit, then I'm nothing. And a woman can survive losing a son. She can't survive being nothing."

"It's not nothing," I said. "It was never nothing. But the crown that took them wasn't the crown you thought—"

"Stop." Her hand came up. "Please. Stop. Because every word you say makes it harder, and I need—" She breathed. Steadied. And when she spoke again, the captain was back, the grief packed away somewhere it would keep until a better hour that I suspected would never come. "Corin Vale. In the name of the Crown of Vaelmoor and the Order of the Hunt, I am placing you under arrest for sedition, obstruction of lawful process, and consorting with unregistered gifted persons. You will surrender your insignia, your warrant, and your weapons. You will be held pending review by the Office of the Regent's Justice."

"The Regent's Justice," I repeated. "You hear it, don't you? Even now. Even crying. *Regent.* Kings reign, Maren. Regents hold a throne for someone else. Ask yourself sometime who the regent is regent *for*."

"Don't." Her voice broke on the single syllable. "Don't make this harder than it is."

"It's already as hard as it gets."

I unbuckled the warrant case and set it on the table. Then the belt. Then the insignia — eleven years of it, silver and black enamel, the kestrel-and-crown that had defined every hour of my adult life — and setting it down felt less like removing a badge than like peeling off skin. My hands shook. Hers didn't, anymore. She'd found her footing on the solid ground of procedure, and I understood that too: procedure was the only thing holding her upright, and I would not take it from her, even now. Especially now.

She cuffed me herself. Refused the guards at the door — sent them away first, so that no one else would see, so that the last dignity of it would belong to just the two of us. Her hands were gentle about it, absurdly gentle, adjusting the irons like she was dressing a wound.

"Kit," she whispered, close to my ear, and her breath hitched. "The child's drawing. Behind the shelf. The little bird."

"What about it?"

"I didn't report it." She stepped back, and her face was composed again, captain-straight, except for her eyes, which were ruined. "It's not in any record. Remember that. Whatever happens."

Then she marched me down the corridor toward the cells, her hand on my shoulder, firm as ever, steady as ever, loyal as ever — and I have turned that walk over in my mind a hundred times since, trying to decide whether she was my captor or my last friend, and I have finally accepted that the answer is yes.

* * *

They put me in a cell in the palace under-hold, which I noted, with the grim humor that has kept me alive this long, was directly below the throne room. I could feel it overhead — a pressure, a wrongness, like weather that never breaks. Somewhere up there, the thing wearing the king was learning my name.

Somewhere up there, too, was Lena Ashgrove. I'd gotten her out of one cell's worth of trouble; now the building had collected us both. There was a lesson in that about the shape of my luck, and I chose not to examine it closely.

I lay on the cot and listened to the stones breathe — because they did breathe, down here, faintly, in a rhythm that matched nothing human — and I thought about Dree's face when she'd said *no*. Not cruel. Not blind. Just a woman standing in the wreckage of a question she couldn't afford to answer, choosing the rubble she knew over the clear ground she didn't.

Loyalty, I decided, watching the torchlight through the bars, is not a virtue. It's a direction. And you can march faithfully in the wrong direction your whole life, and everyone will call you good, and you will even be good — good the way a well-made lock is good, keeping the right people out of the wrong rooms, until the day someone needs the door opened.

Footsteps in the corridor. Slow ones. Then a scrape, and a whisper through the gap under my door — a young voice I knew, thin and terrified and absolutely determined:

"Hunter Vale? Hunter Vale, it's me — from the temple. I've got notes. Sable's notes. She got them out before they took her, and I've been walking them past four checkpoints in my spelling book, and—" a shaky breath, "—and the girl Ashgrove says to tell you the rite needs three things, and the third one's a lie, and you'd both better read this before they come for us at dawn."

An envelope slid under the door, pages fanned out across the stones like a dealt hand.

I reached for them as the torches guttered, all along the corridor, one after another, as if something vast overhead had just exhaled.
