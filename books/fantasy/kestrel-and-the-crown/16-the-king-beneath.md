## 16. The King Beneath

They came for me in the dark, which was considerate of them, because I'd been expecting the dark to come for me on its own.

The cell had no window. It had a bucket, a blanket that smelled of mildew and old prayers, and a door with a slot through which meals arrived twice a day — gruel, bread, water, in an order that suggested even the palace kitchens had given up on imagination. I had counted my heartbeats until counting stopped meaning anything. I had recited every charm formula I knew, not because they worked without my hands free but because the rhythm of them was a kind of company. And then, somewhere past what I judged was midnight, the dark stopped being empty.

It started as warmth. Not the warmth of a body or a brazier but the warmth of a hand pressed flat against your back by someone who wants you to walk forward. I felt it between my shoulder blades before I felt anything else, and every instinct I owned stood up and screamed at once.

"No," I said to the empty cell. My voice cracked on it, which was embarrassing. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

The warmth didn't argue. It simply increased, patient as tide, and the stone floor beneath me went soft as loam, and the mildew smell of the blanket gave way to cut grass and river water, and I understood — the way you understand things in dreams, all at once and too late — that I was no longer entirely in the cell.

I was standing in a garden.

Not the palace gardens. I'd seen those from a distance once, geometric and dead, hedges clipped into obedience. This garden was older and wilder than any gardener's ambition: apple trees grown crooked and generous, roses gone feral over a collapsed wall, bees working the lavender with a sound like distant bells. The light was late afternoon, gold and thick, the light of a summer that has decided to go on forever out of sheer stubbornness.

And there was a man kneeling by a beehive.

He didn't look like a king. He looked like a man who had been a boy here once and had never quite managed to leave. His sleeves were rolled to the elbow; his forearms were scarred with old stings; his hair was going grey at the temples in a way that suggested worry rather than age. He worked the hive frame with careful, competent hands, murmuring to the bees in a low voice, and when he noticed me he didn't startle. He just looked up with an expression I can only describe as *relief*, and it frightened me more than any scream would have.

"You're her," he said. "The ashgrove girl."

"Lena," I said automatically, and then hated myself for it. Rule one of being hunted: don't give your name to strange men in impossible gardens. But something about him made rules feel rude.

"Aldric," he said, and rose, wiping his palms on his thighs. He was taller than I expected and stooped anyway, like a man perpetually apologizing for his own height. "Though I suppose you knew that already. They usually send people who know."

"Who sends people?"

"The thing wearing my crown." He said it the way you'd report the weather. *Rain expected by evening. Possession ongoing.* "This is my garden. Or it was. It's the last room of me it hasn't furnished over." He glanced around at the roses with something like fondness and something like grief. "It keeps the rest accurate. The throne room, the council chamber, my mother's funeral. It maintains those perfectly. But it doesn't understand gardens. Why anyone would want one. So it leaves this." He smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing I had seen in years, and I have seen some genuinely competitive sadness. "You're dreaming, by the way. Or it's letting you think you are. With the Regent, the distinction matters less than you'd hope."

*The Regent.* Sable's word. The entity that had worn three kings. That had been fed five years of excised pages and a debt-contract amended in secret, clause by clause, until a temple scribe named Lena Ashgrove became, legally and cosmically, its property.

"I'm not doing this," I said. "Whatever this is. You're a dream. A trap with good lighting."

"Probably," Aldric agreed pleasantly. He offered me an apple from a basket at his feet, and when I didn't take it, he ate it himself, crunching thoughtfully. "But you're here regardless, so we may as well talk. It's been four years since I've had a visitor who wasn't me." He considered. "Or it. It does impressions. It does me very well, actually. Better than I do, lately. It's had practice."

My chest hurt. I recognized the feeling — it was the same one I got reading ledgers where the numbers almost balanced, where the lie was *nearly* perfect and only a small human stubbornness refused to sign off on it. Here was the king of Vaelmoor, standing in the ruin of his own mind, offering me apples and making jokes about his usurper, and every compassionate bone in my body wanted to sit down on the grass and stay.

Compassion, I reminded myself, is how traps catch careful people.

"Why am I here?" I asked.

"Because it wants something from you and it finds conversation cheaper than force." He tossed the apple core into the grass. "Also, I suspect, because it likes showing off. It brought you to the one place in me that's still mine. That's either mercy or mockery, and after eleven years I've stopped trying to tell the difference."

Eleven years. Sable's research had said three kings. Five years of missing pages. The arithmetic of it turned my stomach.

"Your coronation," I said. "The records were cut. Every page. What happened?"

"What always happens." He walked, and I walked beside him because there was nothing else to do, down a row of apple trees whose fruit hung heavy and wrong — each one bearing, if you looked too long, the faint suggestion of a face. "There's a rite. Old ones, older than the crown itself. A binding, meant to be temporary. A king agrees to share his head with something wise and ancient, for counsel, for a season. One season." He laughed, short and bitter. "I was twenty-two. I wanted to be a good king more than I wanted to be a whole one. Nobody tells you those are different ambitions."

"And the season ended."

"And the season ended, and I asked it to leave, and it explained — patiently, reasonably, the way it explains everything — that leaving is complicated. That the realm needs continuity. That I agreed, didn't I? Signed, witnessed, sealed." He stopped under a tree and looked at me directly, and his eyes were the worst part, because they were ordinary eyes, tired and kind, and they belonged to a prisoner. "It's very good at contracts, your Regent. I hear you'd know."

The words landed like a slap. "My contract—"

"Amended. Yes. It dictated the clauses through three different clerks so no single hand would know the whole. Your family's debt, sold upward, year by year, until the final holder was the crown itself. You were never paying off a debt, Lena Ashgrove. You were being *ripened*." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "A vessel with a gift strong enough to hold it, and compliant enough not to run. It's been cultivating you since you were nine years old."

The garden seemed to tilt. Nine years old. The year of the arrest. The year my mother was taken and the temple took me in and I told myself for fifteen years that I had survived, that luck or stubbornness or my mother's last hidden charm had saved me. Nothing had saved me. Something had *invested* in me.

I sat down in the grass, because my legs had opinions and none of them involved standing. Aldric sat beside me, unhurried, as though kings sat in the dirt with debtors' daughters every day.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I asked it to show you gently. It doesn't do gently, but it humors me in small things. I think it finds my sentimentality quaint."

"Why?" I managed. "Why humor you at all? If it has your crown and your court and your kingdom — why keep you conscious in here at all?"

And he went quiet for a long moment, long enough that the bee-bells faded and the golden light thinned at its edges like old varnish.

"Because it needs my consent," he said finally. "That's the shape of the binding. It can wear me, speak through me, rule through me — but the deep rite, the one that makes the wearing permanent, irrevocable, beyond any unbinding — that requires the vessel to say yes. Freely. With understanding." He turned the words over like stones. "Eleven years it has been asking. And for eleven years I have said no."

The garden flickered. Just for a heartbeat — the roses went grey, the bees went silent, and I saw through the skin of the dream to what lay underneath: a throne room vast and cold, a crown on a cushion like a mouth waiting to be fed, corridors of glassy-eyed courtiers standing in ranks. Then the gold poured back in, and Aldric was watching me with an expression of terrible hope.

"It thinks you're the answer," he said. "Not to the rite — to *me*. It's worn me thin, Lena. I'm fraying. Another decade and there won't be enough of me left to say no with. So it went looking for a successor. A fresh vessel. Younger. Stronger. Someone whose consent could be... obtained." He reached out and touched my wrist, lightly, the way you'd touch a bird. "It will offer you everything. Freedom. Your mother. It showed me my father once, the year it broke me down. It finds the wound and pours honey in it."

"My mother is alive?" The question escaped before wisdom could stop it, raw and stupid and enormous.

"I don't know." His honesty was a kindness and a cruelty at once. "It lies with true things. That's what makes it dangerous. Whatever it shows you will be built around a grain of real. Enough real to drown in."

I thought of my mother then — really let myself think of her, first time in years — her quick hands, the way she'd hum while grinding herbs, the look she gave me the morning the hunters came, which I had spent fifteen years deciding meant *run* but might have meant *live*. If the Regent had her. If there was even a chance—

"That's the offer," Aldric said quietly. "Isn't it. I can see it landing."

"She might be alive."

"She might. And here is the price of finding out." He took a breath, and when he spoke again, each word was placed like a mason sets stone. "If you say yes to it — if you take the crown's bargain, become the vessel, open yourself — it will release me. Fully. I walk out of this garden, out of my own head, alive and hollow but *mine*. Eleven years of captivity ends. That's the trade it's constructing: my freedom for your selfhood. And it knows — it has *learned*, these eleven years — that the fastest way to make a person like you say yes is to put a suffering innocent on the other side of the scale."

The golden light was dimming now, honestly this time, a real dusk coming on. Somewhere beyond the garden walls I could hear it — not a sound exactly, more a pressure, the Regent's attention settling over its own dream like a hand smoothing a bedsheet.

"So I'm asking you," Aldric said, "and I need you to hear all of it, because it will edit this conversation when you wake, and this may be the only unedited minute you get." He gripped my hands, both of them, his palms rough with hive-work and imprisonment. "Do not save me. Do you understand me? Whatever it offers, whatever it shows you, however real my face looks when it begs — do not buy my life with yours. I have watched this thing rule my kingdom for eleven years. I know what it costs. I will not be the reason it gets a newer, better me."

"You'd rather rot in here?"

"I'd rather *end* clean than continue borrowed." His voice cracked, finally, the king's composure failing down to the man beneath. "There's another way. There has to be. Sable Ives found the binding rite — it knows that much, it's furious about it — and where there's a binding there's an unbinding. Find it. Use it. But use it on the *crown*, not on me. Don't give it a vessel. Don't give it anything. Starve it."

"And if the unbinding needs what the legends say it needs?" I asked, thinking of Sable's notes, the third requirement that went conveniently unexplained. "A life offered freely, Aldric. Whose life does a rite like that take?"

His grip tightened on my hands. For a moment the garden held utterly still — no bees, no wind, no dusk — the whole dream suspended like a held breath, and I understood that the Regent was listening now, close as skin.

"Then let it take mine," he whispered. "Freely offered. I've been dying in here for eleven years; at least let my death mean the door locks behind it. Promise me, Lena Ashgrove. Promise me you won't trade yourself. Promise me you'll spend the old coin before you mint new."

The pressure was rising. The apple trees were going transparent, their branches sketching into lines of cold throne-room architecture. Aldric was fading with them, becoming outline, becoming suggestion, and still he held my hands as long as his hands existed to hold with.

"Promise," he said, and the word came from everywhere now, from the dissolving garden, from the dark underneath, from the cell I was waking back into.

"I promise," I said — and meant it, and already grieved it, because I had read enough contracts in my life to know that promises made in dreams are still binding, and I had just sworn to let a good man die.

* * *

I woke on the cell floor with tears on my face and a taste like copper coins in my mouth.

The dark was just dark again. The bucket, the blanket, the meal-slot. Ordinary captivity. Except that scratched into the mortar beside my head, in handwriting I recognized with a jolt that went through me like lightning — small, precise, archivist-neat, the hand of someone who wrote banned books for a living — were four words:

*THE RITE NEEDS THREE.*

Sable was alive. Sable had been here — or passed within reach of here — and had risked everything to leave me a message in the one language I was guaranteed to read.

Three. Not one life. Three requirements, three anchors, three names. And somewhere above me, in a palace full of sleeping courtiers, a thing wearing a king was about to learn that its newest acquisition had just stopped being property.

I pressed my fingers to the words until the mortar dust marked my skin, and I began, quietly, to plan.
