## 17. Breaking the Circle

*Corin*

The cell door opened at the wrong hour.

I knew the hours of Vaelmoor Palace the way a sailor knows tides — when the guards changed, when the kitchens ran, when Captain Dree took her evening walk past the cells with her boots striking stone like a metronome she'd been born keeping. Midnight was quiet. Half-past midnight was quieter still. So when the lock clicked at an hour that belonged to no one, I sat up on my cot and prepared to die with whatever dignity a man can manage in his smallclothes.

Instead it was Lena Ashgrove, holding a stolen ring of keys and wearing the expression of someone who had recently discovered that fear and fury look identical from the inside.

"You're escaping," she said. "Get up."

"That's usually how it works, yes." I found my boots. "How did you—"

"The apprentice. Tam. He picked the lock with a hairpin and a prayer, which tells you everything about the quality of hunter training these days." She tossed me a bundle — dark clothes, a hood, a knife that wasn't mine but would do. "Sable's notes say there's a way down through the old foundations. Under the throne room. The Regent doesn't watch what it considers beneath itself."

"Charming creature."

"It's not a creature. It's a *landlord*." Her voice cracked on the word, and I understood — I'd read enough of Sable's smuggled pages by candlelight through the bars to understand exactly what she was to that thing in the crown. Property. A clause in a ledger. "We have until dawn. After dawn they move us somewhere I don't think we come back from."

I dressed in the dark. Somewhere above us, the palace breathed — that slow, wet rhythm I'd felt since the day I first stood guard in the throne room and told myself the king was merely ill. The walls did that now. They *breathed*. I had spent eleven years learning every corridor of this building, and in the last month it had become a stranger wearing the palace like a coat.

"Corin." Lena paused at the door, keys silent in her fist. "Once we're out of these cells, there's no coming back. Not for you especially. You know that."

"I've known that since the night I didn't arrest you," I said. "I'm just late filing the paperwork."

She almost smiled. In the dark, with the walls listening, it was the most valuable thing either of us had produced all week.

* * *

*Lena*

The stairs behind the cells went down farther than stairs should.

That was my first thought, and my second, and my third, because counting thoughts is what I do instead of screaming. Eleven steps, then twenty, then a number I stopped trusting because the air changed — warmer, thicker, smelling of iron and old smoke, the way a room smells after a fire that happened before anyone alive was born.

Corin lit a stub of candle he'd bribed off a guard weeks ago, hoarding it against exactly this night. Hunter training, apparently, included paranoia as a core competency.

"Foundations are pre-coronation," he murmured. "Older than the city charter. When they built the palace up top, they just... put it on top of whatever this was."

"And nobody asked what 'this' was?"

"Vaelmoor has never once asked what anything is. It's our chief export."

The passage widened into a low vaulted hall, and the candlelight found the bones.

Not scattered. Arranged. Thousands of them — femurs and ribs and skulls too yellowed to be recent — packed into the walls in patterns too regular to be accidental, spirals and interlocking rings that hurt to look at directly, the way Sable's notes said binding sigils hurt. My gift stirred against its chains in my chest, the kestrel-shape of it beating once, hard, and I pressed a hand flat over my sternum until it settled.

"It's a cage," I said. "The whole foundation. We're walking through the bars."

"Of what?"

"Of him. Of Aldric." I kept moving because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant sitting down on ten thousand dead men's bones and weeping, and we were on a schedule. "Five years. He's been in there five years, and this is what they built around his mind while everyone upstairs complimented the king's health."

Corin was quiet for a long moment. Then: "I stood guard for him. Post after post. I told myself if the king was mad, at least he was fed and warm and no one was hurting him." His jaw worked. "I was one of the bars, Lena. That's what I was."

"Yes," I said, because lying to him now would be its own kind of cruelty. "And so was I, in a different ledger. The difference is what we do about it tonight."

He nodded once, sharp, and walked faster.

The bones watched us go. I could feel them doing it, which is a sentence I hope none of you ever has cause to write.

* * *

*Corin*

Three gates between the cells and the under-archive, Sable's notes promised. Two were locks. The third was worse.

The first gate yielded to Lena's hairpin-and-prayer technique, which I am professionally humiliated to report worked better than most of the Crown's official methods. The second was simply old — hinges dissolved to rust, and we shouldered through into a chamber where the ceiling dripped something that wasn't water. I chose not to identify it. There are limits to a man's curiosity at half past one in the morning.

The third gate stopped us cold.

It was iron, floor to ceiling, set into a wall of bone-lattice so dense it looked woven. And across its face, where a lock should be, was a seal I knew intimately: the crowned eye of the Witch-Hunters' Order, rendered in silver inlay, with a socket at its center the exact size and shape of an oath-medallion.

"No," Lena said, reading my face the way she read everything, too fast and too accurately.

"There'll be another way around."

"There won't. Look at the wall." She was right, damn her. The lattice ran unbroken in both directions, bones fused into each other like roots, and my candle showed no seam, no side passage, nothing. "Corin, there has to be another—"

"There isn't, and even if there were, we don't have time to find it." I pulled the chain from around my neck.

Eleven years I'd worn that medallion. Sworn on it at nineteen with my mother crying in the front row because her son was going to be somebody. Hunted liars and predators with it, and hunted frightened children with it too, near the end, and told myself the oath covered both because the alternative was admitting it never had. It sat in my palm now, silver worn dull at the edges where my thumb had worried it through a thousand long postings. Captain Dree had a matching one. We'd had them made from the same pour, years ago, drunk on cheap wine and certain we'd die old and honored.

"If I put it in that socket," I said, "the seal breaks. And when the seal breaks, the Order knows. The medallion reports to the registry — location, bearer, status. It'll register me as *spent*. Oath discharged, authority revoked, legal existence..." I shrugged. "Cancelled. I won't be a citizen. I won't be a hunter. I'll be a name struck through in a book."

Lena stared at me. "You'd be nothing. Legally. They could hang you for trespass and no court would even have to record why."

"They're going to hang me anyway. This way I pick the hour." I heard myself and almost laughed. "Gods. Eleven years of oaths and it comes down to a keyhole."

"Corin—" She caught my wrist. Her grip was stronger than it looked; debt-scribes develop forearms like dockworkers. "There will be a life after tonight. Whatever happens with the rite, whatever it costs — there has to be a morning after, and you can't spend your whole self getting to it. That's what the Regent does. It spends people. Don't do its work for it."

For a moment I let myself want that. A morning after. A shop somewhere, maybe, or a cottage, or just a road with no destination and no one checking papers. A life where my hands had only done what hands are for.

Then the walls breathed again — deep, satisfied, patient — and somewhere far above us a bell began to toll, and it was the wrong bell, the alarm bell, and Lena's face went white in the candlelight.

"They've found the empty cells," she whispered.

I kissed her forehead, because I am a coward about words and always have been, and I set the medallion into the socket.

It went in like a coin into a well. For one heartbeat, nothing — and then silver light crawled out along the inlay, tracing the crowned eye, and the seal screamed. Not metal-on-metal. A voice, thin and furious, some clerk-of-heaven's voice reciting my name and rank and revocation in a language that made my teeth ache, and I felt it *taking* things as it spoke. My commission. My citizenship. My right to walk Vaelmoor's streets. Eleven years of who I was, burned as fuel, feeding the gate.

The iron split down the middle and swung wide.

I staggered. Lena caught my arm, and I gripped her shoulder and stayed upright, mostly. The space behind my sternum where the oath used to live was cold and echoing, like a room after the furniture's been sold.

"Well," I managed. "That's the paperwork filed."

* * *

*Lena*

He shouldn't have been able to joke. That's what frightened me. The gate had eaten his name out of the world's books and he was making *jokes*, and I understood suddenly that this was what Corin Vale did with unbearable things — he sanded them down into something dry enough to carry. I'd seen him do it in the cells for three weeks. I'd mistaken it for resilience. It was closer to bleeding quietly.

The passage beyond the gate sloped down and then, blessedly, sideways, and the bone-lattice thinned until the walls were honest stone again. Behind us, faint but growing, came boots. Many boots. The alarm bell still tolling its one stupid note.

"Under-archive's two turns left, one right," Corin panted. "Sable's map."

"Sable's map is five years old."

"So is most of the palace. Walk."

We walked. We ran, rather, hunched and stumbling, and twice we flattened into alcoves as lantern-light swept past intersections ahead — hunters, my hunters now too, though I'd never sworn anything except debts. Once a patrol passed close enough that I could hear a young voice saying, nervous, *"Captain says sweep the foundations, they say the prisoners went down"* and Corin's hand found my wrist and squeezed: *not yet.*

At the last turn the air changed. Book-dust and lamp oil and the particular smell of paper that has been argued over for decades — Sable's smell, the under-archive's smell, home in a way I hadn't expected anywhere in this palace to resemble.

The door was ajar. That was the first wrong thing.

The second was the scuff marks on the floor, and the dropped lamp, still burning, oil spreading in a slow dark tongue toward a shelf of irreplaceable manuscripts.

The third was the note, weighted under the lamp base, in Sable Ives's cramped, furious hand:

*They came for me an hour ago. I bought you time — the east stair is clear, use it, don't be noble about it. Everything you need for the rite is in the black folio, third shelf, behind the false Kestrel histories. Lena — read the last page TWICE. There's a part I couldn't verify and I need you to know I wouldn't leave that ambiguous if I had another hour. Forgive me. Burn this. — S.*

Corin read it over my shoulder. Then he looked at the east stair, and at the spreading lamp-oil, and at me.

"She's alive," he said. "They interrogate first. That's procedure — *my* procedure, gods help me, I wrote half of it. She'll be in the upper cells by dawn."

"Where we just came from."

"Yes."

I looked at the black folio on its shelf. I looked at the east stair. Above us the Regent's palace went on breathing, and somewhere in it Sable Ives was being marched toward a room with good lighting and bad intentions, because she'd stayed behind to buy us minutes.

"We take the folio," I said. "We finish the rite. And then we go get her."

Corin was already folding the note into his pocket, next to the cold space where his oath used to live. "Dree will have her in the high cells," he said grimly. "Which means we have until dawn to break an unbinding, storm a prison, and rob the crown of its favorite possession."

"Any of those alone would be ambitious."

"I've given up my entire legal existence tonight, Ashgrove. Ambition is all I have left."

I pulled the black folio from behind the false Kestrel histories, and turned to the last page, and read it twice, just as she'd told me to.

What it said made my hands go cold around the paper — and made me understand, with terrible clarity, that the price of saving King Aldric had only begun to be counted.
