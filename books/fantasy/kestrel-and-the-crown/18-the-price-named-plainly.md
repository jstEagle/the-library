## 18. The Price Named Plainly

They took my boots first.

I want to say I understood immediately that this was a statement — that the Regent's people had read my file and knew an archivist is only as dangerous as her ability to run — but honestly, my first thought was that my feet were cold. That was day one. The cell was stone, the blanket was wool in name only, and somewhere above me the palace went about its business of pretending to be a government.

By day three I'd mapped the cell by sound. A drain in the corner with a rhythm to it — drip, pause, drip-drip — which meant the pipes ran through a wall shared with something wet. A door that opened on oiled hinges, because whoever ran this place wanted prisoners surprised. And footsteps at irregular intervals, never on a schedule, which told me the guards had been instructed to be unpredictable, which told me they were afraid of me, which was flattering and useless in equal measure.

My name is Sable Ives. I have spent eleven years living beneath the Vaelmoor archives among books the crown would burn if it knew they existed, and I would like the record to show that when they finally caught me, I was not doing anything clever. I was holding a door.

That's what I told myself for the first two days, anyway. *You held the door. Lena and Corin got out. The rest is arithmetic.* It's remarkable how long a person can live on arithmetic before the cold gets into the numbers.

* * *

The interrogation began on the fourth day, and it did not begin with a man holding pliers.

It began with tea.

The door opened at what I judged to be midmorning — there was a line of grey light from a grate near the ceiling, and I'd learned its moods — and a guard I hadn't seen before set down a small table, then a tray, then a pot of actual tea in actual porcelain. Then he stepped aside, and the thing wearing the king came in.

I had read descriptions of the Regent. I had read accounts from three separate centuries, cross-referenced them, annotated them in margins no one else would ever see. None of them prepared me for the ordinariness. King Aldric — or the shape of him — walked in like a tired administrator late for a budget meeting. Grey eyes. Grey skin, faintly, like paper left too long in a window. And the crown, sitting on his head the way a leech sits on flesh, snug and patient.

"Archivist," it said, using Aldric's voice, which was a cruelty I hadn't anticipated. I'd heard recordings of the king's coronation speech — the few seconds that survived the excisions — and this was close enough to hurt.

"I'm flattered," I said. "I expected a functionary."

"The functionaries report to me." It sat across the table, folded its hands, and poured two cups with a precision that was almost tender. "You wrote the marginalia in the Codex of Rites. Third shelf, under-archive, behind the false binding of the *Lay of Marrow and Salt*. Do you know how long I have wanted to meet the mind that found that hiding place?"

"Eleven years," I said. "Give or take."

"Nine." It smiled with Aldric's mouth, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near the eyes, because the eyes weren't home. "I found your room six years ago. I left it intact. Do you know why?"

Because you were fishing, I thought. Because a trap you spring early catches nothing but small fish. I said none of that. I drank the tea instead, because it was good tea and I am not a martyr, and because I wanted my hands to have something to do besides shake.

"You're going to ask me about the unbinding rite," I said.

"I'm going to tell you about it." Something moved behind its face — not an expression; more like weather seen through glass. "That is what makes this conversation different from the others you've imagined. Everyone imagines I will ask questions. But I already know everything you know, Archivist. You learned it from my library. I merely want to watch your face while I explain what your friends are about to do to themselves."

* * *

Here is what I knew going in, and I set it down now so the record is honest: we had the rite. We had studied it for weeks in the under-archive while the palace slept above us like a beast dreaming of teeth. Three requirements, plainly written in a hand four hundred years dead.

A willing vessel's consent. The king — Aldric, the buried man — had to open his own doors. No forcing, no trickery. The Regent could be evicted only by invitation, which is the kind of elegant cruelty old magic specializes in.

A name spoken truly. Not the name on coins and proclamations — the true name, the one given before crowns, the one the Regent had spent five years burying under ceremony until even Aldric might have forgotten it himself.

And a life offered freely.

We had read that third clause perhaps forty times between us. Lena had gone pale over it. Corin had gone quiet, which for Corin is worse than pale. And I — I had done what archivists do. I had gone looking for the footnote. Because in my experience, when an ancient text says *a life offered freely*, it means either less than it says or more, and the difference is always in the margins.

I hadn't found the margin yet. That was the work I'd been carrying up the archive stairs when they took me.

So when the Regent leaned across its little porcelain table and said, "Shall I tell you what the rite costs?" — I should have refused. I should have sat silent and let it waste its own time. I was trained for this. I have read every treatise on interrogation the old orders ever produced, most of them banned, several of them written by people who were themselves interrogated shortly after finishing.

Instead I said, "Yes."

Curiosity, my mother used to say, is the only sin that punishes itself. She was hanged for witchcraft, so her authority on self-punishment was considerable.

* * *

"The gift," the Regent said. "That part your texts preserve correctly. The caster who speaks the unbinding pours their own gift into the severing — it is the solvent. It does not return. Whatever talent burns in them when the rite begins will be ash when it ends. Permanent. Total." It watched me over the rim of its cup. "Your friend Ashgrove has a considerable talent. Considerable, and carefully hidden, and wasted on luck charms for dockworkers. She will feel it leave her the way a man feels a tooth pulled — the ache of absence where something lived."

"We guessed that," I said. My voice was steady. I was proud of that, later, in the dark, when pride was all the ration I had.

"Yes. Here is what you did not guess." It set down the cup, and the sound of porcelain on wood was very loud in the little cell. "The rite requires an anchor. Unbinding is not destruction, Archivist — it is *separation*. Two things that have grown together must be peeled apart, and peeling hurts, and the one being freed will thrash. He will want to stay. Five years of possession braids the possessor into the possessed. Memories blur. Loves blur. When we speak the severance, Aldric will not know which griefs are his and which are mine, and a soul that cannot tell its own sorrows from a parasite's will cling to both rather than lose either. He will drag me back inside him like a child dragging a nightmare back under the blankets."

"So the anchor—"

"The anchor is a memory of love. One memory, chosen by the caster, held in the mind's forefront for the whole of the rite — spoken aloud, described truly, in every particular, without flinching. It is the rope thrown to the drowning man. *This* is yours. *This* is real. Hold to this and let the rest go." The Regent's grey eyes fixed on me, and for a moment I saw past them, down something long and cold, like looking into a well and seeing the well looking back. "And here is the price named plainly, since you asked plainly: the memory does not survive the rite. It is consumed. Burned as fuel, exactly as the gift is burned. Your caster will speak her most beloved memory aloud, hold Aldric's soul together with it — and when the crown falls, she will not remember it. Not dimly. Not as a scar. It will simply be a hole, shaped like something she used to love, and she will spend the rest of her life walking around it."

The cell was very quiet. Even the drain had stopped dripping.

"That's not in any text," I said.

"No. The scribes who recorded the rite were casters themselves. They knew what they were asking of each other, and they were ashamed, and shame edits manuscripts." It refilled my cup, uninvited. "There were four unbindings attempted across the nine centuries I have worn this realm. Three failed — the anchors broke, or the casters faltered, unwilling to feed their dearest thing into the fire. The one that succeeded — the caster was a woman who unbound a petty spirit from a village elder in the reign of the second Aldric. She gave up the memory of her wedding day. She lived thirty more years beside a husband she no longer remembered loving. He tended her garden. She thought him a kind stranger." The Regent paused. "He agreed to it, beforehand. They chose together which memory she would burn. I have often wondered whether that made it worse or better. I confess I lack the equipment to wonder properly."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because you will get it out." It said it the way a chess player says *check*. "You are an archivist. Knowledge hoarded in you would rot, and you know it, and within a week you will have passed this to whatever sympathetic fool guards your door, and it will reach Ashgrove, and then comes the interesting question." It stood, smoothing Aldric's coat with Aldric's hands. "Will she still do it? Knowing the cost? Will the hunter let her? Will anyone?" At the door it stopped, and without turning around, added: "For the record, Archivist — I hope she does. I have worn three kings. I am so tired. But I cannot leave unless someone makes me, and everyone who could make me keeps trying to save me instead. It is the great flaw of your species. You mistake mercy for strategy."

Then it was gone, and the tea was cold, and I sat in my cell with the worst knowledge of my career and nowhere to shelve it.

* * *

The guard's name was Pell. I learned it on the sixth day, when he brought the food tray and I said, "Pell, the bread is stale," purely to see if he'd flinch at being addressed like a person.

He flinched. Good sign. Flinching means you expected to be ignored and were seen instead.

He was young — twenty, maybe, with the palace's grey uniform hanging off him like a borrowed future — and he had the particular careful blankness of a boy who has decided not to think about his job. I've recruited three archivists out of that exact expression. People who refuse to think are thinking hardest of all; they just need permission.

I gave him six days of nothing. Six days of "thank you" and "cold again tonight" and once, deliberately, a laugh at nothing, just to prove I could still make sound that wasn't begging. On the seventh day I said, "You have a sister."

He went very still. "What?"

"The way you fold the blanket. Military, but the corners are tucked twice — that's domestic training. Someone taught you neatness at home, and it wasn't a drill sergeant, because sergeants teach efficiency, not care." I shrugged. "Also you hum. Four notes, same four notes, when you think no one's listening. Lullabies are regional. That one's from the Weir districts."

"My sister's in the Weir," he said, and then looked horrified at himself.

"And you haven't written to her, because letters out of the palace are read, and you don't want your name in anyone's ledger. I understand ledgers." I kept my voice low and easy, a conversation between two people shoveling snow. "I was an archivist, Pell. I know what happens to names in ledgers. Which is why I'm going to give you something that isn't a letter, has no name on it, and can't hang you even if it's found — because it's nonsense. It's a quotation from a banned poem that no one outside a certain basement has ever read, and if anyone asks, you'll say the mad archivist recites rubbish at you constantly, which will be true."

His throat worked. "Why me?"

"Because you hum lullabies," I said, "and the last thing I'm going to trust with this is someone who doesn't."

I had spent two days composing it. Not the content — the content was simple, terrible, and burned into me word for word — but the form. It had to look like delusion. Fragments. A woman talking to herself in a cell, which no one punishes and no one reads closely. I scratched it into the underside of the wooden tray with a splint of bone I'd worked loose from my boot heel — they'd taken the boots, but not the eyelets, and arrogance is a wonderful source of raw materials.

The message read:

*TO THE KESTREL. THE RITE COSTS TWICE. FIRST THE GIFT — KNOWN. SECOND: AN ANCHOR. ONE MEMORY OF LOVE, SPOKEN TRUE AND HELD THROUGH THE SEVERANCE. IT BURNS WITH THE GIFT. GONE ENTIRELY. SHE MUST CHOOSE WHICH LOVE TO LOSE BEFORE SHE ENTERS THE ROOM, AND SAY IT PLAIN TO ALL WHO STAND WITH HER, OR THEY WILL STOP HER AT THE DOOR AND THE KING DIES INSIDE HIMSELF FOREVER. ALSO — TELL HER THE REGENT IS TIRED. THAT IS NOT A TRICK. THAT IS THE ONLY TRUE THING IT SAID ME.*

I signed it with a kestrel's wing, three strokes, the mark we'd agreed on in the under-archive in another lifetime.

Pell took the tray out that evening. I watched him walk away with my whole life balanced on a scrap of wood, and I thought about the Regent's parting words — *everyone who could make me keeps trying to save me instead* — and hated how much sense they made.

* * *

On the ninth day they moved me.

Not to a worse cell. To a better one, which frightened me more. New room, higher grate, and — after a day of listening — voices. Distant, through stone, but voices with stress in them, the clipped cadence of soldiers receiving orders they don't like. Something was happening above. Something with a schedule.

On the tenth day, Pell came with the dinner tray, and under the bread was a folded scrap of clean paper, and on it, in a hand I didn't know — round, hurried, a scribe's hand trained in a temple — four words:

*She knows. She goes.*

And beneath that, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear:

*Tomorrow.*

I sat on the floor of my improved cell and laughed until it turned into something else, because that is the thing no one tells you about knowledge arriving too late to be kind: it arrives anyway. It arrives and it sits down next to you and it stays.

Somewhere above me, Lena Ashgrove was choosing which love to burn. And tomorrow morning, whether any of us were ready or not, she was going to walk into the throne room and start saying goodbye to it out loud.

I put my palm flat against the cold stone wall, the way I used to press my hand against the spines of books I couldn't open yet, and I said, to no one, to the stone, to the tired thing in the crown:

"Hold on, Aldric. She's coming. And she's bringing the bill."
