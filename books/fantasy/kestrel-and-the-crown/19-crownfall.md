## 19. Crownfall

There is a particular quality to the silence of a throne room just before everything goes wrong. I'd imagined it would be dramatic — trumpets, thunder, the sky cracking open like an egg. Instead it was the hush of three hundred courtiers holding their breath at once, and the soft, wet sound of a crown breathing.

Yes. Breathing. If you haven't heard it, count yourself lucky, and if you have, then you already know there's no describing it to anyone who hasn't.

They had marched us in through the Hall of Glass, past windows that showed not the grey Vaelmoor morning but something else entirely — a green country that doesn't exist, hills rolling under a sun with too many rays. The Regent liked its visitors to see what it was offering. *Look how lovely surrender can be,* those windows said. *Look how green.*

Corin walked beside me in his hunter's blacks, minus the insignia he'd burned two nights ago in a brazier beneath the palace, along with his name in the rolls, his pension, his legal existence. He walked like a man who had decided that if he was going to be no one, he might as well be no one loudly.

"Three guards at the dais," he murmured. "Two more behind the pillars. Dree will be at the king's right hand."

"Will she try to stop us?"

"She'll try to stop *me*." His jaw did the thing it does when he's swallowing something too large. "Lena. Whatever happens up there—"

"Don't." I kept my eyes forward. "If you say something noble, I'll start crying, and I refuse to give this room the satisfaction."

That got half a breath of a laugh out of him, which was, I suspect, the entire point of my saying it. You learn things about people when you've escaped through bone-choked foundations together. Mostly you learn their jokes are worse than yours.

The doors at the far end boomed shut behind us. And the court turned as one body — three hundred faces, glassy and patient, like dolls waiting for someone to wind them — and looked at me.

I looked back at the throne.

King Aldric sat slumped in it, or rather, something sat slumped in him, wearing his body the way a hermit crab wears a stolen shell. His skin had gone the color of candle tallow. His eyes were open and fixed on nothing. And above his head, the crown of Vaelmoor — iron and old gold, seven points, each set with a stone the color of dried blood — rose and fell. Rose and fell. In rhythm. Like lungs.

*Hello, little ledger,* said a voice that wasn't a voice. It arrived inside my skull fully formed, the way a debt arrives in your letterbox: no warning, all consequence. *You came after all.*

"You knew I would," I said aloud, because apparently I'm constitutionally incapable of being sensible even at the end of the world. "You wrote me into the contract five years before I could sign it. Of course you knew."

*I wrote you into many things.* The king's mouth moved. It was obscene, watching lips shape words while the eyes stayed dead. *Your mother wept beautifully, by the way. They always do, at the end.*

The floor tilted. Not literally — though give it a moment — but something in my chest folded up like a struck tent. My mother. Chapter thirteen's vision, the one I'd spent every waking hour since trying to disprove. Alive, they'd shown me. Alive and smiling in a garden that smelled of rosemary.

*You may have her,* the Regent offered, gentle as a moneylender. *Walk away now. Take the hunter. Take your freedom papers — I'll sign them myself. The realm is large. Vaelmoor is only one city, and kings are only ever one man. What is one man?*

And here is the thing I want written down somewhere, in some archive Sable will eventually get her hands on: for three full seconds, I considered it. Three seconds where my mother's face hung in front of me and the whole rotten city could go hang itself. I am not a hero. Heroes don't keep ledgers of what things cost. I do.

Then I thought about the apprentice, Tamsin, nine years old, who'd smuggled us Sable's notes rolled up inside a doll. About the dockworker who'd bought a luck charm because his daughter was sick and hope was cheaper than medicine. About every gifted child who'd learn, if I walked away, that the world really was the thing in the crown's shape.

"No," I said. "But thank you for the offer. Very generous. Terrible terms."

*Then we proceed to collection.*

The Regent stood up.

I mean the king stood — but it was the crown that led, hauling the body upright on strings, and the movement was wrong in a way that made three hundred courtiers sway in sympathy like wheat. Somewhere behind the pillars, a woman began to scream, quietly, continuously, the way people scream when they've been standing in a nightmare too long to remember how to stop.

"Lena." Corin's hand found my elbow. "Now or never."

"Now," I said, and stepped forward onto the dais steps, and spoke the name.

Not the name on the coins. Not Aldric the Third, Beloved of Vaelmoor, Keeper of the Bridges. Sable had found the true one buried in a coronation rite three hundred years old, inked over twice and hidden under a psalm, because names are doors and the temple had long ago decided this door should be bricked up.

"Aldric Vael-son-Maren," I said, "last-born of the bridge-line, son of Maren who loved you enough to hide your name from the world so that no one could ever own you."

The crown screamed.

I don't have a better word. Iron doesn't have vocal cords, but the sound that came off it bent the air, and every window in the Hall of Glass shattered outward at once, and the false green country spilled across the floor like floodwater — hills rolling, that many-rayed sun burning cold — and the courtiers dropped like cut puppets, all except the ones the Regent caught and lifted, because it needed witnesses. It wanted an audience for what it did next.

What it did next was tear the room apart.

The dais split down the middle. Stones the size of coffins rose into the air and hung there, trembling. The banners of Vaelmoor unwove themselves thread by thread and the threads whipped around the hall like a swarm of furious insects. And the king's body — poor hollow Aldric — arched backward against the throne, mouth open, and from it poured a darkness that was not smoke and not shadow but *absence*, the shape of a thing that had been eating light for three hundred years and did not intend to stop.

*YOU DO NOT HAVE THE RIGHT,* it said, and now it wasn't bothering with inside-my-skull subtlety; the whole hall heard it, and the whole hall cowered. *THE RITE REQUIRES THREE THINGS, LEDGER-GIRL. A WILLING VESSEL. A TRUE NAME. AND AN ANCHOR OF LOVE FREELY GIVEN. WHERE IS YOURS?*

"Working on it," I said, which was honest, and began the rite.

Sable's notes were clear on the mechanics, if not the poetry. Unbinding is not banishing — you cannot throw the Regent out, because it has grown roots; you must instead dissolve the marriage, name by name, vow by vow, until the contract between crown and creature has no clauses left to stand on. I know contracts. I have spent six years copying them, resenting them, and finally reading mine closely enough to survive them. This, at least, I could do.

I spoke the first vow loose — *the vow of provision, made at coronation, that the crown shall feed the land and the land feed the crown* — and the Regent shrieked and threw a pillar at me.

It missed, mostly. Mostly is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The edge of it caught my shoulder and spun me into the steps, and I tasted blood and kept talking, because that's the whole job, really. Keep talking. Everything else is decoration.

Which is when Captain Dree came over the wreckage with twelve soldiers, sword drawn, face like a closed door.

Corin stepped into her path.

I saw it sideways, between syllables of the second vow — *the vow of protection, that the crown shall shield the realm from what waits beyond* — and what I saw was two people who loved each other standing in the ruins of everything they'd served, and neither willing to move.

"Dree." Corin's hands were open at his sides. Empty. He hadn't taken a weapon. "Stand down."

"You're under arrest, Vale." Her voice cracked on his name. She covered it with volume. "Treason. Sedition. Aiding a witch in regicide—"

"I'm aiding a scribe in a rescue," Corin said. "The king is in there, Dree. He's been in there for five years, screaming. I've heard him." He stepped closer, into sword range, which is either bravery or a death wish and with Corin it has always been both. "You taught me the oath. *To guard the realm against all enemies, foreign and domestic.* Well. Look up."

Dree looked up.

Everyone did. Because the Regent, mid-tantrum, had lifted the entire ceiling — beams, slate, the stone effigies of six dead kings — and held it there, forty feet of palace poised over three hundred heads, a promise it was clearly composing.

And in Dree's face I watched the exact moment the arithmetic changed. She was loyal. Loyalty was the load-bearing wall of her whole self. But loyalty to a crown that was about to drop a ceiling on the realm it was sworn to protect — that's not loyalty anymore, that's just obedience with better manners, and Dree was many hard things but she was never obedient.

"Swords down," she said hoarsely. "All of you. Swords *down*."

Her sergeant stared at her. "Captain—"

"That was not a request, Hask."

They lowered their swords. Twelve blades pointed at the flagstones, and Hask's face said he'd be filing a complaint with whatever government survived the afternoon, and I could have kissed every one of them, including Hask.

*TRAITORS,* observed the Regent, conversationally, still holding the ceiling. *ALL OF YOU. VERY WELL.*

The third vow was the hard one. *The vow of vessel-hood* — the clause the temple had been quietly inserting into debt-contracts for generations, breeding girls like me the way farmers breed oxen. To speak it loose, I had to stand in front of the thing that owned me and declare myself unowned, and contracts, as any creditor will tell you, do not release their collateral willingly.

"The vessel is not property," I said, blood in my teeth, shoulder screaming. "The vessel is a person. The vessel chooses. And the vessel—" my voice shook, and I let it, because Sable's notes said the rite wanted truth, not performance, "—the vessel *refuses*."

The Regent came apart.

Not gently. Contracts torn make noise. The darkness pouring from the king's mouth convulsed, and the false country flooding the floor burned away like fog in sun, and the ceiling — blessed, ordinary, mortal ceiling — came down in pieces that Corin and Dree, moving together for the last time as comrades, shoved the crowd clear of. The threads of banner-whip lashed and stilled. The crown stopped breathing.

And the Regent, in its last coherent moment, did the thing I will think about for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be.

It laughed.

*Clever ledger,* it said, almost fondly, folding inward, shrinking toward the crown like water down a drain. *But you have spoken only two-thirds of the rite. The anchor remains. Love, freely given, to hold the door open while the vessel walks out. Where is yours, little girl? Who loves you enough to burn?*

And the answer, the horrible obvious answer, the one Sable's notes had flagged with a single underlined word — *anchor: unspecified* — arrived in my head with perfect clarity:

Nobody here. That's why it chose me.

The dark collapsed into the crown. The crown went quiet. King Aldric slid sideways off his throne like a coat slipping off a hook, and I caught him — ninety pounds of starved old man in a young man's body — and lowered him to the broken stones, and his eyes opened, and they were *his*, clouded and terrified and present, and he looked at me and whispered:

"Did it work? Am I — is it gone?"

"It's gone," I said. "Your Majesty. It's gone."

He started to cry, silently, the way people cry who have forgotten how to do it loudly, and I held his hand and felt absolutely nothing magical anywhere in my own chest, which I filed away to panic about later, because later was going to be very crowded with things to panic about.

Because the anchor question was still sitting there. The rite had *worked* — the Regent was bound, contained, sealed in dead iron — but Sable's marginal note had been specific: *unanchored bindings fray. Something must hold the door. Ask what the third price is BEFORE, not after.* Underlined twice. And I had underlined it myself, and read it aloud to Corin, and then we'd run out of time, because the hunt had come home, and now—

"Lena." Sable's voice, ragged, from the wreckage of the west doors — she'd gotten free in the chaos, guards' keys jangling from her belt, ink-stained fingers black to the wrist. She was staring at the crown. At me. At the space between. "Tell me you didn't finish it without the anchor."

"I finished it without the anchor," I said.

She closed her eyes. "Then we have until moonrise."

Above us, through the shattered roof, through the smoke and the drifting feathers of ruined banners, the afternoon sun was already leaning west.
