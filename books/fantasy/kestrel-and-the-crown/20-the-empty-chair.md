## 20. The Empty Chair

The silence after the Regent left was the loudest thing I had ever heard.

You'd think a thing like that would go out with thunder. With the throne room splitting down its seams and the chandeliers raining glass and the king's voice tearing into something that wasn't a king's voice at all — which, to be fair, all of that happened first. But the ending itself was quiet. The way a tooth comes out quiet, after all that grinding and gripping and the dentist's hands braced against your jaw. One moment it's part of you. The next there's just a hole where it lived, and your tongue keeps going back to touch the absence because it can't believe the absence is real.

That was my mouth. That was also, apparently, my soul.

I was on my knees on the marble — when had I gotten on my knees? — with the last syllable of Aldric's true name still burning on my tongue like I'd swallowed a coal. The name had come from somewhere deeper than memory. Sable's notes said the caster must speak the name *truly*, and I'd worried, in the frantic hours before, that I didn't know it truly enough. But it turned out the name had been waiting in me since the dream-walk, since Aldric pressed it into my hands in that buried room of his mind like a man handing over the last coin he owned.

"Aldric Vael," I had said. "Son of Maren. King by accident, man by stubbornness. *Aldric.*"

And the crown had screamed.

Not the king. The crown.

I watched it happen from very close up, because I was the epicenter and the epicenter doesn't get a viewing gallery. The gold went soft. Not melting-soft — *breathing*-soft, the way it had breathed all along, except now everyone could see it, every courtier in that glassy-eyed assembly, every soldier Corin had talked down to a standstill with his empty hands raised and his voice doing the impossible work of holding a line without a weapon. They all saw the crown flex like the throat of a swallowing snake, and then convulse, and then — this is the part I'll be describing badly to people for the rest of my life — *let go*.

It lifted off the king's head. On its own. Like a bird deciding it was done perching.

For one heartbeat it hung in the air above him, and I swear on my mother's grave — which is a phrase I use carefully these days, given everything I've learned about graves and mothers — I swear it looked at me. Not with eyes. Crowns don't have eyes. But there was an attention in it, ancient and offended and, worst of all, almost *fond*, the way a farmer looks at a field he's worked for years right before he sells it.

Then it came apart. Not shattered. Unraveled. Gold unwinding into threads of light that thinned and thinned until they were nothing, and the nothing made a sound like a long-held breath finally released, and then the throne room was just a room again. Marble. Banners. A hundred people remembering how to blink.

The silence after that was the loudest thing I'd ever heard.

* * *

King Aldric woke the way a drowning man surfaces — badly, in stages, with a lot of coughing.

He slid sideways off the throne and would have cracked his skull on the dais steps if Corin hadn't moved faster than I've ever seen anyone move, crossing the room in four strides and catching him under the arms. And that was its own kind of strange, wasn't it? The witch-hunter holding the king upright while half the palace guard stood around with drawn swords and no idea anymore whose orders mattered.

"Easy," Corin was saying. "Easy. You're out. You're out."

"I know." The king's voice was a ruin. Thirty years of disuse will do that; the Regent had spoken through his mouth so long that his own voice had gone feral, unpracticed, a voice like a door opening on a house nobody had lived in for a generation. "I know where I am. I remember — I remember the coronation. I remember being crowned." He stopped. His face did something terrible and slow, like ice shifting on a lake. "I don't remember anything after."

Nobody said anything. What was there to say? We'd known this might be the shape of it. Sable's notes had been clear: the binding didn't kill the vessel, it *hollowed* him, packed thirty years of the man away somewhere unreachable and ran the kingdom through the empty space. Unbinding opened the door. It didn't give back what the dark had eaten.

"Your Majesty," Corin started.

"Don't." Aldric held up a hand. It shook. He looked at it shaking with the expression of a man meeting a stranger. "Don't call me that yet. Let me — let me stand in it a moment. See if it fits."

So we let him stand in it. The whole throne room, hundreds of us, stood in it together, in the wreckage-smell of ozone and old gold and the particular stink of fear that's been sitting too long in fine clothes. Courtiers who'd spent years with glassy eyes were rubbing their faces like men waking from a nap they couldn't account for. Somewhere near the doors a woman began to cry, quietly, the way you cry when you're not sure yet what you're crying about.

Captain Dree came through the crowd with her sword still drawn and her face still set for battle, and for one ugly moment I thought she'd decided to die on the hill of the old order after all. Then she saw Corin — bloodied, insignia-less, legally a ghost since he'd burned his hunter's credentials to open the gate beneath the palace — and her sword arm dropped, and she looked at him the way you look at a puzzle whose answer breaks your heart.

"You're under arrest," she said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle. "You're under arrest, you absolute idiot, and then you're going to explain everything, and then—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Then we'll see."

"That's the most merciful sentencing I've ever heard," Corin said.

"Don't push it."

* * *

They gave the king a smaller room. Of course they did. Nobody knew what else to do with him, so they did what Vaelmoor always does with problems: put them somewhere out of sight and assigned a clerk.

It was a sitting room off the royal apartments, modest as palace rooms go, with a fire that someone had lit and a window looking out over the city — our city, smoke-stained and debt-ridden and, as of yesterday morning, free. Aldric sat in a chair that was not a throne and stared at his hands, and I sat across from him because he asked, specifically, for "the girl who spoke my name," and because Sable elbowed me and whispered, *you broke it, you buy it.*

Sable was propped in the corner looking like a wrung-out rag, which after two days in the Regent's cells was frankly heroic. They'd been freed in the chaos — pulled from their cell by a guard named Pellis who'd been passing out their notes for weeks and finally worked up the nerve to pass out the whole guard — and they'd anchored the rite exactly as promised, standing behind me in the throne room murmuring the counterwords that kept the unbinding from unraveling mid-scream. I owed them my life and probably my sanity. They intended to collect in book loans.

"I keep trying to count backward," Aldric said. "From today. I get to the coronation and then it's — fog. Warm fog. Something humming." His jaw tightened. "There are laws in my name I've never read. There's a hunt in my name that—" He looked at me. "That took your mother."

"Yes."

"And thousands like her."

"Some worse. Some luckier. Mostly worse."

He nodded slowly, absorbing it, and I watched him do the arithmetic that kings presumably do — the weight of a reign measured in other people's damage — and I watched him arrive at a total he couldn't carry.

"I abdicate," he said.

Just like that. No ceremony, no council, no dramatic pause for the historians. He said it the way you'd cancel an appointment.

"Your Majesty—"

"Stop that. Please." He almost smiled. It was a tired, crooked thing, and it was the first genuinely human expression I'd seen on a face that had spent decades being worn like a coat. "I woke up this morning owning a country I can't remember governing, full of people I can't remember wronging, except that I evidently wronged nearly all of them. You want me to sit back on that chair and issue pardons? Sign reforms? Be the symbol of a new age?" He turned his hands over, studied the palms. "I'm fifty-three years old and I feel like I died at twenty-two and somebody forgot to tell me. Whatever rules Vaelmoor next should be chosen by people who were awake for the last thirty years. Not by whatever's left in here." He tapped his temple. "There's not much. I checked."

"It doesn't work like that," I said. "You can't just — kingdoms don't have return policies."

"Then they should." He leaned forward, and for a second the fog in him burned off and I saw the man the Regent had buried: sharp, stubborn, a little bitter, the kind of king history gets when it's lucky and doesn't deserve it. "You spoke my true name, Mistress Ashgrove. Do you know what that means? Not the ritual part. The human part. It means you're the only person in this room — possibly in this city — who knows exactly who I am. Everyone else knows who I was. So I'm asking you, as the only expert available: is there enough of me left to rule?"

I wanted to lie to him. I want that noted for the record. Some kind-hearted liar version of me wanted to say *of course, Majesty, you'll grow into it,* because he'd suffered enough and kindness costs nothing when you're spending someone else's hope.

But I'd spent a year learning what happens when people don't say the true thing plainly. It gets written into contracts. It gets amended in small clauses. It wears a crown.

"No," I said. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. But that's not the same as nothing. You could testify. Everything you remember — the binding, the rite, who brought the crown to your father, all of it. You're the only living witness to how this started. That's worth more than a throne."

He considered that for a long time, firelight moving on his hollowed face.

"A witness," he said at last. "Kings end up statues or villains. Witnesses get to be useful." He almost smiled again. "All right. Fetch whoever's in charge of not being in charge. I'll abdicate properly, with paperwork, because I'm told this country runs on paperwork, and then I'll talk until they carry me out feet first."

* * *

My gift was gone.

I want to describe it accurately, because everyone kept asking, and every answer I gave was a lie of convenience. "I feel fine." "It's quieter." "Like setting down something heavy." All lies. Here is the truth:

It felt like a pulled tooth.

Not the ache — the hole. The way your tongue goes back, again and again, to the gap where the tooth lived, probing a shape that isn't there, and every probe confirms the same unbelievable fact: something that was *part of you* has been removed, and the body keeps expecting it, keeps reaching for it, keeps finding only absence wearing absence's shape.

All my life the gift had been there the way hearing is there. I never decided to sense magic any more than I decide to hear rain. Charms sang to me — little songs, mostly, luck and warmth and mended hinges, the harmless music I'd built a black-market livelihood on. When I walked past a gifted person I felt them the way you feel a draft under a door. When the Regent had reached for me across the palace, I'd felt that too, vast and cold, a whale passing under a rowboat.

Now: nothing. I stood in the palace corridor afterward and held my hand over a warding sigil some panicked clerk had chalked on a doorway, a sigil that should have hummed against my skin like a struck bell, and I felt *chalk*. Rough grain. Nothing else. My tongue went to the gap. The gap answered.

People say you don't miss what you never had. People are idiots. I'd had mine since before I had words for it. It was the first thing that was ever truly *mine* — not my family's debt, not the temple's ledger, not the crown's contract. Mine. And I had fed it into a rite like wood into a stove, willingly, eyes open, because the alternative was letting a five-thousand-year-old parasite finish digesting a kingdom.

Worth it. I want to be clear about that, even alone, even now. Worth it. I'd do it again before the sentence finished forming.

But knowing a wound is honorable does not stop it from being a wound, and that night, walking home through streets where people were already tearing down the Purification proclamations, I caught myself reaching — reflexively, stupidly — for the charm in my pocket. The one I'd saved from the fire in chapter one of all this, the little brass kestrel I'd never been able to explain keeping. Old habit: hold it, let it warm, let it sing.

It was cold. It was just brass. It weighed what brass weighs and nothing more, and I stood in the middle of Lantern Row with my fist closed around a dead piece of metal and cried like a child, briefly, furiously, hating every second of it, because grief that won't even grant you dignity is the worst kind.

Then I wiped my face, because I'm Lena Ashgrove and my mother didn't raise criers, and I kept walking.

* * *

Three days later, the abdication happened, and Vaelmoor discovered it had opinions.

The ceremony was held in the same throne room — someone on the new council insisted on the symbolism, closing the circle, though I privately thought the room deserved a decade's vacation. The Regent's throne had been removed entirely; in its place stood a plain wooden chair, and beside the chair, on a table, the crown itself, or what remained of it: dead gold, inert, a museum piece already. People kept coming to look at it the way they'd once come to look at executions. Same hunger, different meal.

Aldric read the instrument of abdication in his ruined voice, steady as a man walking on a leg that hasn't healed. He got through it without faltering until the last line — *"and I do this because I cannot remember the country I am asked to surrender"* — and there his voice went, just for a moment, and the whole hall pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing Vaelmoor has ever done collectively.

Then it was done, and he was just Aldric Vael, witness, and the chair sat empty, and the question that had been circling the city for three days landed in the silence like a kestrel on a windowsill:

*Who rules now?*

The council had answers — seven of them, all mutually exclusive, all loudly held. The merchant houses wanted a republic they controlled. The temples wanted a regency they controlled. The districts wanted the debt ledgers burned and didn't much care who held the pen afterward. And out in the streets, ordinary people who'd spent a generation learning that authority wears masks were asking the only question that mattered: how do we make sure the next thing in the chair is a person?

Corin found me on the palace steps afterward. He looked strange without his insignia — lighter, somehow, and lost, a man whose name had been his uniform and who'd burned both. Dree stood a few paces behind him, arms crossed, radiating the specific energy of a woman who has arrested her best friend, un-arrested her best friend, and is still deciding how she feels about the entire transaction.

"They're asking for you," Corin said. "The council. Both factions. Even the ones who called you a witch six days ago."

"What for?"

"Witness. Testimony. Possibly scapegoat, depending on the day." He shrugged. "You spoke the king's true name. You're the only person alive who knows how this ended. Turns out that makes you the beginning of the record."

"I'm a scribe," I said. "I write records. I don't *become* them."

"Sable says you become them whether you like it or not. Sable also says if you refuse, they'll write you anyway, and they'll get it wrong."

Of course they did. Sable Ives, rebel archivist, terror of three governments, currently recovering from torture with a stack of banned books and zero patience for my self-pity. They'd already started drafting the true history of the Regent — I'd seen the pages, covered in their cramped furious handwriting, and at the top of the first page, underlined twice: *the record is the rite. Whoever holds the story holds the binding.*

Down in the square, the crowd was chanting something — not a name yet, not a faction, just noise, the raw sound of a city trying out its own voice after thirty years of ventriloquism. The empty chair gleamed dully on the platform behind us. Somewhere in the palace, clerks were already pulling ledgers, including, I'd been told, a certain debt-contract with a certain amended clause, which the council had voted unanimously to burn publicly, on the steps, with ceremony, as the first act of whatever came next.

They'd asked me if I wanted to light it myself.

I hadn't answered yet. I kept thinking about my mother, who'd signed that contract's original ancestor to save my life, and about how fire takes things whole and gives back only warmth, and about whether warmth was enough. Whether it had ever been enough. Whether the girl who'd sold luck charms out of a coat pocket had anything left to sell now that the luck was gone.

"Tell them I'll come tomorrow," I said.

Corin raised an eyebrow. "Tomorrow?"

"Tonight I'm going home, eating something that isn't prison bread, and sleeping for approximately nine years. Tomorrow I'll decide what the record says." I looked back once at the empty chair, at the dead crown beside it, at the crowd below surging like a tide that had finally remembered it was allowed to move. "But tell Sable to start a fresh page. Whatever we write next — I want it written honestly. No amended clauses. No small print. Starting with mine."

The kestrel charm sat cold in my pocket, just brass, just weight.

I carried it home anyway.
