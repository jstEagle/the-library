## 21. Ledgers Closed

The order dissolving the Witch-Hunters came down on a Tuesday, which felt right somehow. Tuesdays were the day we'd always used for paperwork — arrests filed, interrogations logged, names entered into ledgers that would now be fed to a fire in the palace yard along with everything else the old order had written.

I stood in the muster hall of the Hunter's Barracks for the last time and watched Commander Voss read the proclamation aloud. His voice didn't shake. I'll give him that. Thirty years of service, a career built on the premise that magic was a rot to be cut out of the body politic, and when the king himself — the *actual* king, hollow-eyed and apologetic in a way that made your chest hurt to look at him — declared the hunt a fabrication of the thing that had worn his crown, Voss read the paper like it was a supply requisition.

"Effective immediately," he said, "the Office of Purification is dissolved. All active warrants are void. All detained persons are to be released with formal apology and compensation." A pause. "By order of King Aldric."

Nobody cheered. Nobody wept, either, though I saw Haskin's jaw working like he was chewing on something too big to swallow. We were forty-one hunters standing in a hall built for two hundred, and the silence had a texture to it, like wet wool.

Voss set the proclamation down. Then he did the one thing I never expected: he took off his insignia — the silver kestrel-and-crown pin every hunter wore over his heart — and laid it on the table.

"I believed I was protecting this city," he said. "Some of what we did was protection. Some of it was not. You each know which of your own days were which." He looked at me, just for a moment, and I couldn't read his face at all. "Go home. Try to be worth the pardon."

Then he walked out, and forty-one hunters stood in a hall that wasn't theirs anymore, and slowly, in ones and twos, they began to take off their pins.

Mine had been gone for weeks by then. Burned, technically — sacrificed in the bone-choked dark under the palace to open a gate that should not have opened, traded away along with my name in the crown's registers, my legal existence, the whole tidy architecture of Corin Vale. The clerks had spent the last fortnight rebuilding me from scratch, which is an odd experience, being reissued to yourself. New papers. New seal. A line in a fresh ledger: *Corin Vale, hunter, retired.* As if the man who'd walked into those archives with Lena Ashgrove and come out the other side of a god was someone I merely knew by reputation.

In a way, he was.

* * *

Dree found me at the docks three days later.

I'd taken to walking there in the mornings, mostly because it was the last place anyone would look for a former witch-hunter. Dockworkers don't care about your history; they care whether you can lift a crate. I couldn't, particularly, but I could stand around looking useful, and there was a kind of peace in being judged solely on my back and shoulders.

She came down the gangplank of the customs sloop in full uniform, captain's braid and all, and I knew before she said anything that this conversation had been coming since the night she'd arrested me. She'd been the one holding the keys, after all. When the palace fell apart — when the Regent tore the throne room open and the truth came out screaming — Dree had been the one who unlocked my cell. She hadn't said much then either. There'd been a battle to fight, soldiers to keep from panicking, a city teetering on the edge of believing its king had been a puppet for five years. Dree was good in a crisis. It was the quiet afterward she'd never learned to survive.

"You look terrible," she said, by way of greeting.

"I've been told I'm improving."

"That must have been someone with low standards." She sat down next to me on the bollard, which creaked in protest. Dree had never been a small woman, and she'd never once apologized for it. "I've been reviewing the archives. The real ones. Sable Ives's people catalogued everything the Regent's clerks buried."

"And?"

"And I spent four days reading about what I served." Her voice was flat, the way it went flat when she was carrying something heavy and refused to let anyone see her stagger. "Four days. Then I stopped, because if I read one more page I was going to burn the barracks down with myself in it."

"Dree—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Let me finish, because I've rehearsed this and if you interrupt I'll lose my place and have to start over, and neither of us wants that."

I shut up. Twenty years of friendship teaches you when to be furniture.

"I arrested you," she said. "On evidence I half-believed, because believing it would have meant the oath I swore was a lie, and I would rather have put my best friend in irons than find out I'd spent my life serving a lie. That's what loyalty did to me, Corin. It made me choose the comfortable treason over the uncomfortable truth, and I want you to know I understand exactly what that cost you, because I paid the mirror version of it." She turned and looked at me then, and her eyes were dry, which somehow made it worse. "So here's what I've decided. My oath was to the crown. The crown was a hat on a monster. But underneath the monster there was a man, and that man — Aldric, the actual Aldric — signed the order dissolving the hunt, and that order is the first honest thing the crown has asked of me in five years. So I'm keeping my oath. To *him*. To what it was supposed to mean." She exhaled. "That's the speech. You may respond."

I thought about it for a while. Gulls were arguing over something near the fish market. Somewhere behind us a crate dropped and a dockworker swore with real artistry.

"I'm sorry," I said finally.

"For what?"

"For letting you arrest me instead of telling you everything sooner. For making you choose. I told myself I was protecting you, and maybe I was, but mostly I was a coward, and you deserved better than a coward with a badge."

Dree nodded slowly, as if I'd passed some examination she'd been grading against a strict rubric. "Apology accepted. It doesn't fix anything."

"No."

"But it's made anyway, and apparently that counts for something, according to a pamphlet Sable Ives wrote about civic reconciliation that everyone in the palace is reading and pretending they aren't." The corner of her mouth twitched. "You're quoted in it. Badly."

"Gods help us all."

We sat there a while longer, two people who had spent years trusting each other with their lives and then spent months not trusting each other with the truth, watching the water do its patient, indifferent work. It wasn't fixed. I don't think it ever fully fixes, once loyalty curdles. But she'd chosen, and her choosing had landed on my side of the ledger, and when she finally stood up and said, "They're offering me command of the city watch. Actual watch. Thieves and smugglers and drunk noblemen. No magic required," I heard something under it that sounded almost like hope.

"Take it," I said. "You'd be good at honest work."

"I'd be terrifying at honest work." She paused at the top of the street. "There's a ceremony Thursday. The burning. You should come."

"I know. I'm supposed to be in it."

"Then wear something clean, for once in your life." And she was gone, braid swinging, off to terrify the forces of petty crime.

* * *

Thursday was gray, the kind of sky that can't decide whether to rain and so just hangs there, brooding, like a bureaucrat with bad news.

They built the pyre in the temple square, which was either poetry or poor planning depending on your view of institutions. Not a funeral pyre — nothing so dramatic. A great iron basin, wide as a wagon, filled with paper. The debt ledgers. Every contract of indenture the temples had issued in the name of public charity, every amended clause, every quietly transferred obligation, every family bound generation to generation for sums no one could ever quite finish paying. Lena had insisted on all of them, not just hers. "If mine burns alone," she'd said, "it's a spectacle. If they all burn, it's a policy."

King Aldric presided, because he insisted on that too, over the objections of three separate councils. He looked older than his years — the unbinding had taken something out of him that sleep couldn't restore, five years of his life spent buried alive inside his own skull while the Regent wore his face and repeated its little phrases through his mouth. He moved carefully now, like a man relearning the weight of his own limbs. But his eyes were his own. That was the thing people kept saying, in the markets, in the taverns, in the whispered conversations that stopped when officials walked past: *his eyes are his own again.*

He spoke briefly. He was learning to speak briefly, having spent half a decade as a mouthpiece for a creature that never used one word where fifty would do.

"The crown owes debts," he said. "Today we begin paying the smallest ones first." He nodded to Lena.

She stood at the basin with a torch, and I watched her face while she waited. She wasn't triumphant. I'd expected triumph, or at least satisfaction — this was the document that had owned her, the amended clause that had made her legally the property of an ancient hunger, the paper chain her mother's arrest had forged link by link. But she looked at the ledger in the clerk's hands the way you look at a scar. Not with hate. With recognition.

The clerk read her contract aloud first, because she'd demanded that too. Every clause. Including the amendment, the one inserted without her signature, the one that had been the whole design — *the vessel shall be delivered unto the crown upon maturity of debt.* Hearing it spoken in daylight, in front of a thousand people, stripped it of its power the way sunlight strips a shadow of its substance. It was just words. Ugly words, cowardly words, words written by frightened clerks serving a thing in a crown. But only words.

Lena touched the torch to the ledger, and the clerk fed the rest of the stack into the basin, and the fire took it all with a sound like a long exhale.

People wept. I'll admit that freely, because I was one of them, quietly, at the back, where nobody important could see. A woman beside me — dockworker's wife, salt-rough hands — held up a scrap of her own family's contract, saved from somewhere, and burned it in a candle flame she'd brought for the purpose, and when it was ash she brushed her palms together like she was finishing a chore twenty years overdue.

Aldric stepped forward one last time. "The debt of House Ashgrove," he said, "is closed. So are all the others. Let no temple, no office, no officer of this realm revive them." And then, quieter, almost to himself: "May we be forgiven for what we collected."

The crowd dispersed slowly, the way crowds do when something has ended and no one is sure what begins next. Lena found me by the fountain, smelling of woodsmoke, her sleeves singed where sparks had caught her.

"Well," she said. "That's that."

"That's that."

"You're crying."

"It's ash. It gets in your eyes."

"Liar." But she said it gently, and we stood there together watching the basin smoke, and neither of us pretended the moment was bigger or smaller than it was. An apology doesn't fix things. Neither does a fire. But both are made anyway, and apparently that counts for something.

* * *

The reckonings came in small after that, one at a time, the way debts always do.

Haskin came to my door with a bottle and no speech, which was the correct amount of speech. He'd hunted for eleven years. He'd dragged in three gifted people whose faces he still saw when he closed his eyes. We drank most of the bottle and said very little, and when he left he shook my hand like we were equals, which we had never been and now, in the strange arithmetic of the aftermath, somehow were.

Sable Ives sent me a book. No note, just the book — a banned treatise on binding law, one of the ones we'd nearly died retrieving from the under-archive, with a single line marked in the margin: *An oath is a tool. Tools are not guilty. Only hands.* I've read that line perhaps forty times. I still don't know if it forgives me or convicts me, and I suspect Sable intended exactly that.

And one evening, a week after the burning, I climbed the hill to the temple district and knocked on the door of the high scribe's residence. Old Master Ellery, who had warned Lena to stop asking questions, who had known — how much had he known? — about the missing pages, the amended contracts, the slow delivery of a girl to a monster. He answered the door himself, small and stooped in his gray robes, and he looked at me for a long moment before he said, "Hunter Vale. Or whatever you're called now."

"Just Corin."

"Just Corin, then." He stepped aside to let me in. "I wondered when someone would come asking."

"I'm not asking anything. I'm not here for the temple's records or its crimes. The councils are handling that." I stood in his entryway, awkward as a boy. "I came because I arrested people on the strength of documents your office maintained. Because I trusted the ink. And because you're the only man left alive who might understand what that's like — finding out the ledger itself was lying."

Ellery lowered himself into a chair and was silent for so long I thought he wouldn't answer. When he did, his voice was thin as vellum.

"I suspected," he said. "For years, I suspected. And I told myself that suspicion without proof was a sin of pride, and that obedience was humility, and I wrote my pages and excised their pages and went home each night and slept." He looked at his hands. "Do you know what I learned, hunter? That evil rarely needs our enthusiasm. It needs only our neat handwriting."

"Yes," I said. "That's about the size of it."

"I am sorry," he said, and it was the first time I'd heard him say anything without checking it against protocol first. "It does not fix things."

"No," I agreed. "But it's made anyway."

He poured me tea we both let go cold, and we sat like two men at the bottom of a well, and when I finally left, the stars were out over Vaelmoor, and the city smelled of rain coming, and for the first time in my adult life no one was hunting anybody.

* * *

Which brings me to today, and the reason I'm writing this down at all instead of drinking it into oblivion like a sensible man.

This morning a runner brought me a letter with no seal. Inside, in a hand I recognized — the cramped, fast script of a woman who learned to write in hiding — was a single sentence:

*The under-archive wasn't empty when we escaped. Come see what the Regent left behind. — S.*

And beneath it, in different ink, added later, in a hand I knew even better — quick, slanted, impatient, the hand of a woman who sold luck charms and unmade kings:

*Bring breakfast. This one's strange, even for us. — L.*

I read it twice. Then I put on my coat, checked that my new papers were in my pocket — *Corin Vale, retired*, a man rebuilt from spare parts — and went out into the morning to find out what kind of trouble survives its own death.

Old habits. Apparently some ledgers never close.
