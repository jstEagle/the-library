## 22. Kestrel, Flying

The trouble with running a bookshop is that everyone assumes you've read the books.

I learned this in the first week, when a fishwife with forearms like mooring rope asked me whether *The Lament of the Salt Kings* was any good, and I had to admit that I'd shelved it upside down for three days without noticing. She bought it anyway. Said she liked a book that kept its own counsel.

That was eight months ago, back when the shop was still called Ashgrove & Ives out of some stubborn sense of symmetry, before Sable pointed out that nobody in Vaelmoor could pronounce the ampersand and we might as well call it what the children already called it: The Owl's Window, because of the round glass pane Sable insisted on installing over the door, which does not resemble an owl at all unless you squint and have recently suffered a head injury.

We kept the name. Sable keeps most things. It's one of her virtues, or her diseases, depending on the day.

This morning the shop smells of ink and woodsmoke and the particular staleness of bread that has been argued over rather than eaten. Six children sit cross-legged on the floor between the poetry shelves — the only section tall enough to feel like a fort — copying letters onto slate while I pace the aisle with a stick I use exclusively for pointing at things and never, ever for hitting them, no matter what Tam Weller tells his mother.

"Ash," Tam says. He's seven and built like his name suggests, all cinders and stubbornness. "Ash, why's the F got two lines but the T only got one?"

"Because," I say, "the people who invented letters were paid by the line."

He considers this with the gravity of a customs official. "You're lying."

"I'm teaching. It's different."

Sable appears in the doorway with the tea tray, which is our morning ritual and also our only reliable clock, because neither of us has owned an hourglass since the palace confiscated hers along with everything else she'd smuggled out of the under-archive. She sets it down on the counter with the care of someone handling relics, which, to be fair, she is. Half the books in this shop were banned six months ago. Some of them still have the ash marks where their siblings burned.

"Tam asked me if letters are magic," Sable says, pouring.

"What did you tell him?"

"That they're better than magic. Magic gets outlawed." She hands me my cup. "Letters just get mispronounced."

From anyone else this would be bitterness. From Sable it's practically a hymn. She lost eleven years to the under-archive — living on candle stubs and stolen histories beneath the temple, cataloguing a kingdom that didn't want to remember itself — and she came up into the daylight thinner, grayer, and somehow lighter, like a book finally returned to its shelf. She still flinches at boots on stairs. But she laughs now, sometimes, and when she laughs the whole shop seems to settle, like a house acknowledging its owner.

The children finish their slates. I collect them, praise the legible ones, gently redirect Tam, whose F has migrated off the page entirely and appears to be attacking the margin. Then they're gone, out the door and down Lantern Row, shouting about lunch, and the shop goes quiet in that way I've come to love more than any silence I knew before — not the held-breath quiet of hiding, but the ordinary kind, the kind you can move around inside.

I shelve the slates. I water the plant on the windowsill, which Sable swears is a rare southern fern and which I privately suspect is a weed with ambitions. And then I do what I do every morning, the thing that took me four months to stop being ashamed of:

I check the ledger.

Not the debt ledger. That one's ash. Corin burned it publicly on the palace steps, page by page, while Captain Dree stood beside him in full uniform and read out the names of everyone the hunt had taken — the gifted, the accused, the merely inconvenient — until her voice gave out and a clerk took over, and then another, and the reading went on so long that the crowd stopped being a crowd and became something else, something Vaelmoor hadn't seen in a generation: a witness.

No. This ledger is mine. Debts owed to the shop, mostly. A column of who borrowed *Salt Kings* and hasn't brought it back (the fishwife; I'm not chasing her). And underneath, in ink I mixed myself because I can't charm ink anymore, a list that has nothing to do with money:

*Names spoken truly.* Fourteen of them so far. People who came to the shop after Crownfall, quietly, usually at closing, usually not to buy anything. People who needed someone to say aloud what happened to them during the Regent's years — the glassy-eyed courtier's daughter, the guard who couldn't remember giving orders he'd given, the temple scribe who'd excised pages on command and wanted, more than anything, to write them back. I listen. I write their names in my ledger, spelled correctly, dated properly, because Sable taught me that a record is the only spell that survives its caster.

It isn't magic. I'd know. I used to have some.

* * *

People ask about it differently, depending on how polite they are.

The polite ones don't ask at all; they watch my hands when I wrap their parcels, waiting for the old flourish, the little twist of charm I used to fold into luck-knots and blessing-cords. When it doesn't come, they look away quickly, embarrassed, as if they've caught me missing a limb.

The impolite ones ask directly. "Do you miss it?" a merchant's wife said to me last month, over a stack of almanacs, with the bright cruelty of genuine curiosity.

And here's the honest answer, the one I've been circling for eight months and am only now able to hold still long enough to look at:

Yes. The way you miss a tooth.

Not the ache of it — the ache fades. What stays is the absence itself, the strange smooth gap where something used to live. For twenty years my gift sat behind my ribs like a second heartbeat, a low warm hum I stopped hearing because it never stopped. Now the space where it was is very quiet, and some mornings — fog mornings, mornings when the harbor bells sound far away — I reach for it out of habit, the way your tongue finds the gap, and find nothing, and stand there in the middle of my own shop feeling briefly like a stranger in a borrowed life.

But here's the other half, the part I owe to a king who begged me not to save him and a hunter who burned his own name to open a gate and an archivist who traded eleven years of her life for a rite's fine print:

I would do it again.

Not bravely. Not with music swelling. I would do it the way I actually did it — terrified, furious, my voice cracking on Aldric's true name while the Regent peeled the throne room apart around us — because the alternative was a kingdom of glassy eyes and a crown that breathed, and because somewhere in that tearing-apart moment I understood what the rite's last requirement had been asking all along. A memory of love as anchor. They never told us which memory. They didn't have to. The rite takes the one you're holding hardest when you cast it, and I went in holding my mother's hands in the prison cart, her whispering *hide it, Lena, hide it well*, and I came out the other side with the gift gone and that memory intact, which I have decided, after long consideration, counts as mercy.

The Regent took plenty else. But it didn't get that.

"You're doing the face," Sable says from the counter.

"What face?"

"The one where you're having a conversation with yourself and losing."

"It's a staff meeting. You weren't invited."

She snorts and goes back to cataloguing. This is, roughly, how we say *good morning* now.

* * *

Corin comes on Thursdays.

He arrives early, before the children, and he never uses the front door anymore — not because he's unwelcome, but because the front door has a bell, and bells still do something to him. So he knocks on the storeroom window, twice, soft, and I let him in through the back, and he stands in the smell of paper and dust with his hat in his hands like a man visiting a grave he's grateful to.

He has no legal existence, Corin Vale. He burned his insignia in the palace foundations and with it his registry entry, his commission, his name in the crown's rolls. On paper, the man who helped unbind King Aldric never existed. In practice, he's the most famous non-person in Vaelmoor, though he'd rather be dead than famous, and says so weekly.

Dree fixed the paperwork eventually — or unfixed it, or forged whatever needed forging; I've stopped asking questions about the mechanics of Dree's loyalty, because watching her choose Corin over the order that made her was like watching a tree decide which way to fall, and I don't need to know how it managed. What matters is that Corin Vale exists again, officially, as of midwinter. What matters less, but matters to me, is that he still knocks on the storeroom window.

Today he brings bread and news, in that order.

"Bellamy's reopening the harbor school," he says. "Wants Sable to consult on the curriculum."

"She'll say yes and then complain about it for a decade."

"She also wants—" He hesitates. Corin hesitating is new; the old Corin, the hunter, moved through the world like a drawn blade. This one weighs things. I've decided I prefer him, though I'd never tell him, on account of his head. "She wants you. To teach the letters class. Properly. Wages, a room, the lot."

I turn a slate over in my hands. Tam Weller's runaway F glares up at me.

"I have a class," I say.

"You have six children and a stick."

"And a shop."

"You have a partner who could run this shop in her sleep and would enjoy the peace." He sets the bread down. "Lena. You turned the hunt's records into a public archive. You rebuilt the temple scribes' charter. You teach letters in a doorway every morning because nobody asked you to. At some point somebody's going to *ask* you, and it should probably count."

I look at him for a while. He endures it. He's gotten good at enduring things from me; practice makes permanence.

"I'll think about it," I say, which we both know means yes, later, once I've finished being difficult about it, which is my right and my craft.

He nods, satisfied, and lets himself out through the back, and I stand there holding a child's slate in a shop full of rescued books, thinking about how strange it is that the hardest conversations of my life now happen over bread.

* * *

The kestrel comes at dusk.

I'm alone in the shop — Sable gone to the harbor school meeting, taking her complaints with her — and I'm doing the closing ritual: shutters, till, the small lamp on the counter, the ledger squared to the desk's edge. Outside, Lantern Row is going amber and blue, lamps catching one by one, and the air has the clean cold edge of a season turning.

I hear it before I see it — that thin whistle, wings cutting air — and when I look up, it's on the windowsill.

A kestrel. Small, russet-backed, wind-hoverer, the shape my gift wore when it wore anything at all. Twice in my life I turned into one: once as a girl, fleeing my mother's arrest, and once — the last time — above the palace, the night before Crownfall, when the gift was already guttering and I flew the walls one final time just to know the sky from that height while I still could. Hovering. Holding still against the wind by beating against it, which is, I realize now, the whole of my biography in a single bird.

It looks at me. Its eye is dark and absolutely uninterested in my feelings, which is correct. Birds don't mourn with you. That's what I liked about the shape, back when it was mine — the kestrel felt no debt, kept no ledger, carried nothing but hunger and wind. For a few minutes at a time, twenty years ago, it was the closest thing to freedom I ever touched.

My hand rises toward the glass. Old habit. Twenty years of reaching for the hum behind my ribs, and even now, even knowing the space is empty, the body remembers the gesture: *come here, little shape of me, come home.*

It doesn't come, obviously. It's a bird. It's not my bird. It was never my bird — that was the lie the gift told, that the shapes it took belonged to me, when really I was only borrowing the sky the way I once borrowed everything, on credit, against a contract written by other hands.

The kestrel tilts its head. Somewhere behind its dark eye it decides I'm not food and not threat and therefore boring, and it opens its wings —

— and here is the moment, the one I'll carry, the one I'm writing this whole account toward, though I keep it in the ledger of names rather than telling it at markets. Because the old me, the charmer, the girl who hid her light in luck-knots and sold blessings door to door, would have done something foolish. Would have pressed her palm to the glass. Would have whispered some half-remembered word, some residue, trying to summon the hum, trying to make the bird mean something, trying to turn even this into a sign, a payment, a proof that the price bought something.

Instead I stand very still, and I let it go.

Not *let it go* the way people say it, meaning surrender, meaning grief. I mean the plain thing. I open nothing, hold nothing, keep nothing. The kestrel drops off the sill into the blue evening and the wind takes it, and it hovers — that impossible stillness, beating against the air to hold one point of the world steady — and then it banks and is gone over the rooftops, toward the harbor, toward wherever kestrels go when they are simply kestrels and belong to no one's story but their own.

And I stand in my shop, in my lamplight, with my ledgers and my slates and my borrowed life that is not borrowed anymore, and I find that what I feel is not the gap. Not the pulled tooth. Not the ghost-hum reaching for a wingbeat that will never answer again.

What I feel is the shop around me. The books Sable saved and I sell. The letters Tam Weller is learning, badly, gloriously. The fourteen names in my ledger, spoken truly, recorded properly, alive. The knock on Thursday's storeroom window. The whole ordinary, unblessed, unenchanted weight of a world that runs on ink and bread and showing up, and asks nothing of me but that I keep doing it.

Survival's price, named plainly: everything I was, traded for everything this is.

Worth paying. I check the sum every morning, and every morning the ledger balances.

I bank the lamp. I square the ledger. Tomorrow there will be bread to argue over and letters to invent etymologies for and a harbor school to be difficult about joining, and none of it will require magic, and all of it will require me.

Outside, over the rooftops, something small and russet rides the wind home — not mine, never mine, free — and I turn the key in my own door, from the inside, and stay.
