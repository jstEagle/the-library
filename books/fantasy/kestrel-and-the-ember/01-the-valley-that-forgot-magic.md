## 1. The Valley That Forgot Magic

The valley of Ashgrove had forgotten magic the way old men forget their first love — not all at once, but slowly, and with a great deal of pretending it had never mattered much anyway.

Bram Ashgrove had been born into that forgetting, named for it, and raised in the one place where it had never quite taken hold: the little stone shrine at the valley's mouth, where the god Kestrel sat silent on his cracked altar and listened to whatever anyone cared to tell him.

He never answered. He hadn't answered in three hundred years, if the temple records were to be believed, and Bram believed them because she'd read every one of them twice by candlelight before her twelfth birthday. The priests called it the Silence. The farmers called it common sense — a god who kept his mouth shut was a god who couldn't ask you for anything. Bram, orphaned at six and given to the shrine as a tithe nobody wanted to collect, called him the only conversation partner who'd never once interrupted her.

She talked to him the way other people talked to their mothers.

*Good morning,* she said that particular grey morning, setting down two copper coins and a heel of bread on the altar. *I've got the Harrow contract today. Wool wagons to Millbrook and back. You remember Millbrook — no, of course you don't, you don't remember anything, that's rather the point of you.*

The silence answered, as it always did, with silence. But there was a quality to this silence, Bram thought — she'd have been laughed out of the valley for saying so — a quality like a held breath. Like someone listening very hard and refusing, out of some ancient stubbornness or grief, to speak.

*I'll be back in four days. Five, if Doss has drunk the toll money again.*

She touched the altar's edge with two fingers, the way she'd done since she was small enough to need a stool to reach it, and left.

* * *

The escort company assembled at the ford by midmorning: five hired blades, three wool wagons, and a driver named Doss who smelled like he'd already begun drinking the toll money.

"Morning, Captain," said Tam Weller, who was nineteen and earnest as a spaniel. He'd been with her two years and still polished his sword every night as though someone might inspect it. "Weather says rain by evening."

"Weather," said Bram, "is a liar and a coward."

Beside Tam stood the Fenwick brothers, big and small — Hesk built like a door and quiet as one, Piet small enough to hide behind Hesk and loud enough that hiding was purely theoretical. Then Marta Crane, who'd fought in the border wars and refused to discuss them, and who sharpened her knives during conversations as a form of punctuation.

And finally, slouching against the lead wagon with the expression of a man attending his own funeral, Doss.

"Doss," Bram said. "How much of last night's pay is currently inside you?"

"Captain Ashgrove." He pressed a hand to his chest, wounded. "I am a professional."

"You are a man who once tried to pay a bridge troll in buttons."

"He accepted them, though."

"He was laughing too hard to refuse. Mount up."

They crossed the ford in a long grey line, water chuckling over the stones, and began the slow climb up the valley road toward the pass. The valley spread out below them as they rose — green and orderly, hedgerows stitched neat as embroidery, smoke rising straight from forty chimneys. It was, Bram thought, the kind of country that made maps boring. Nothing here but wool, turnips, and the deep settled conviction that nothing anywhere else could possibly matter.

That was the valley's whole character, really. It had decided, centuries ago, that magic was an embarrassment best left unmentioned, like a relative who'd died in disgrace. No charms worked past the ridgeline. No fae crossed the boundary stones. Priests of the loud gods came through occasionally on missionary rounds and left within a week, pale and frustrated, complaining that their prayers went out like letters to a wrong address. Even the weather was suspiciously reasonable. Storms lost their temper halfway down the slopes and arrived as mere weather.

The scholars had theories. The farmers had better things to do. And Kestrel, silent on his altar, had never once explained himself.

Bram rode rearguard and let the wagons' creak fill the space where a conversation should have been.

"You're quiet, Captain," Marta observed, drawing alongside. She didn't look over. Knife, whetstone, knife.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're always *listening*. There's a difference. Today you're just quiet."

Bram considered denying it and decided against it; Marta could smell a lie the way Doss smelled a tavern. "It's nothing. Restless, maybe. The contract's easy. Easy contracts make me itch."

"Easy contracts pay for winter."

"Easy contracts are how people get comfortable, and comfortable people get dead." She heard herself say it and winced inwardly. It was something the old temple swordmaster used to bark, back when she was all elbows and fury, and it had come out of her mouth more and more lately, uninvited, like a guest who wouldn't take the hint.

Marta gave her a long sideways look. "You've been saying things like that for a month now. Like a woman quoting scripture at a funeral she hasn't admitted is happening."

"That's grim."

"That's observation. I'm paid to observe." Whetstone, knife. "You're twenty-nine years old, you run the best small escort company in the valley, and you sleep alone in a shrine talking to a rock. Something in you is waiting for something. I'd wager my pension on it."

"Your pension is eleven coppers and a grudge."

"And I'd still wager it."

Ahead, Piet's voice carried back over the wagons, mid-story: "—so the miller's wife says, *that's not a cat, that's my husband's brother*, and honestly the resemblance was uncanny—" and Hesk's low rumble of agreement, and Tam's bright laugh. Ordinary sounds. Good sounds. The sounds of a life that fit, mostly, the way an old boot fits: worn to the shape of the foot, and slightly embarrassing to look at directly.

Bram rode and watched the ridgeline and felt the itch Marta had named, and told herself it was nothing. A woman could be restless without it meaning anything. A woman could guard a valley her whole life without the valley ever needing guarding. Some lives were simply quiet, and quiet was not the same as wrong.

She almost believed it.

* * *

They made the waystation at Hollowmere by late afternoon, a low stone barn with a leaking roof and a hearth that smoked unless you fed it exactly right. Doss claimed feeding hearths was a sacred art and used it as an excuse to sit closest to the fire. The others drew lots for watch order. Bram cooked, because she always cooked, because the alternative was Doss cooking, and the valley remembered the Stew Incident of last spring with a shudder.

Over the pots, talk turned, as it always eventually did, to the world outside.

"They say the border lords are raising levies again," Tam offered. "For what, nobody knows."

"They say the Order's buying up every relic east of the mountains," said Piet. "Paying stupid money. My cousin sold them a spoon that allegedly belonged to a saint. It was his gran's soup spoon."

"Which saint?"

"Does it matter? They paid."

Bram stirred the pot. "The Hollow Order isn't paying for spoons. They're paying to make sure nothing holy survives outside their vaults."

"Gods, listen to her," Piet grinned. "You'd think you cared about religion, Captain."

"I grew up in a shrine, Piet."

"A shrine to a god who doesn't *do* anything." He said it without malice — Piet couldn't manage malice before his third drink — and it landed anyway, the way true things land when they're said carelessly.

"The god does plenty," Bram said. "He keeps the valley safe. Three hundred years of safe. Name another god who's managed it."

"Name a thing he's done since your grandmother's grandmother," Piet countered cheerfully. "Silence isn't protection, Captain. Silence is just silence. Maybe the valley protects itself. Maybe it always did."

Hesk spoke then, which he did perhaps four times a day, each word rationed like wartime bread. "Valley's got a shape to it," he rumbled. "Like a bowl held by somebody. Been here all my life. Somebody's holding it."

Everyone looked at him. Hesk shrugged and returned to his supper.

"*Somebody's holding it*," Piet repeated, delighted. "There's poetry in the big man yet."

But Bram looked at Hesk across the fire, and Hesk looked back, and something passed between them that neither would have put words to: the sense of a weight, vast and patient, resting on the valley from underneath. Held. Not abandoned. Just — quiet. Quiet the way a man is quiet when he's carrying something heavy and doesn't want to drop it.

She slept badly, dreaming of a bird made of embers, wings folded, waiting on a high branch for a wind that never came. When she woke before dawn, she walked out under the cold stars and found herself facing south, toward the boundary stones, toward everything the valley had agreed to forget.

*Nothing,* she told herself firmly. *It's nothing. Go back to bed.*

She went back to bed. The itch stayed.

* * *

The second day passed in wool-smelling tedium. The third brought them to Millbrook, where the wool buyer haggled like a man being robbed personally by each individual sheep, and where Bram, waiting out the negotiations in the market square, noticed a thing she'd noticed before and never mentioned: a charm-seller's stall, and on it, a row of little woven knots meant to bring luck.

They didn't work here either. Millbrook lay well inside the valley's borders. But the seller was new, from over the mountains, and he kept touching his wares with a puzzled frown, like a musician playing to a room that wouldn't dance.

"No call for them here?" Bram asked, mostly to be polite.

"Strange country," the man said. "My knots work everywhere. Everywhere. I've sold them in cities where they burn charm-sellers. Here?" He shook his head. "Here it's like the air itself has agreed not to listen."

Bram bought one anyway — a small brown knot, ugly and honest — and hung it from her sword belt, where it did absolutely nothing, faithfully, day after day. She found she liked it for that. Everything in the valley was like that: loyal, useless, and quietly certain that loyalty was its own reward.

Like her, said a small voice in her head that sounded uncomfortably like Marta.

She told the voice to shut up.

* * *

They started home on the fourth morning under a sky the color of dishwater, and it was on the road home, within sight of the boundary stones, that the day stopped being routine.

Later — much later — Bram would try to reconstruct exactly how it happened, and fail. There was the rain starting, fine and cold. There were the wagons strung out along the river road. There was Tam singing something tuneless, and Doss complaining about the rain getting into his boots, which were mostly mouth anyway.

And then there was a sound from the trees above the road — not a sound, precisely, but the absence of one. Birds stopping. All of them, all at once, the way they do when a hawk passes, except no hawk flew.

Bram's hand was on her sword before her mind caught up. Twenty years of escort work lived in the hand, and the hand knew.

"Hold the wagons," she said, and her voice came out flat and calm, the voice she saved for emergencies, and everyone heard it and went still.

"What is it, Captain?" Tam whispered.

"I don't know."

That was the honest truth, and it frightened her more than any answer would have. Because the birds had gone quiet, yes — but so had something else. Something underneath. In all her years in the valley, riding every road, sleeping under every ridge, Bram had never once felt the valley's held breath falter. Hesk had called it a bowl held by somebody. And just now, for half a heartbeat, Bram would have sworn she'd felt the grip loosen.

A stag burst from the treeline ahead of the lead wagon — no, not a stag, a man, stumbling, crashing through the bracken with his arms out like a drowning swimmer. Grey-cloaked, bareheaded, young-ish, or maybe not young at all under the dirt and the blood. An arrow stood in his shoulder. Another in his thigh. He made it three steps onto the road, saw the wagons, and changed course toward them with the last of whatever was driving him.

"Down!" Bram was already moving, sliding off her horse, meeting him as he fell. His weight took her to one knee. Up close he smelled of blood and woodsmoke and something else, something scorched, like a page burned too near a candle. "Marta — the trees, watch the trees! Tam, with me!"

The man's eyes found hers. They were light eyes, grey-green, and they were not afraid, which was somehow worse than terror would have been. He had the calm of a man who has already done the arithmetic and accepted the total.

His hands came up — shaking, blackened at the fingertips, burned — and pressed something against her chest. Folded leather, stiff with heat, warm as a fresh loaf. She took it without deciding to; her hands simply closed around it, the way they'd close around a falling child.

"Take it," he rasped. Blood at his lips. "East. Over the stones. Follow the— " A cough that sounded wet and final. "Follow the seals. Only— only Kestrel's own can—"

Then his gaze went past her shoulder, up, toward the valley's heart, toward the distant smudge of the shrine, and something moved through his face that Bram had no name for. Recognition, maybe. Relief. Grief.

"The Ember remembers," he whispered.

And died.

Just like that — the light going out of him between one breath and the next, his weight settling into her arms with the terrible specific heaviness of the finished. Around her the rain kept falling, indifferent, and Tam was saying her name, and somewhere Piet was shouting about riders, and Bram knelt in the mud of the road she'd ridden ten thousand times, holding a stranger and a warm folded map, in a valley that had forgotten magic —

— and felt, for the first time in her life, the valley hold its breath around *her*.

The brown knot on her sword belt, faithful and useless for two years, was glowing. Faintly. Softly. Like a coal remembering fire.

"Captain," Tam said again, very quietly. "Captain, we've got trouble."

She looked up. On the ridgeline, brief as lightning between clouds, movement — cloaked figures, bows, the glint of steel, gone.

Arrows came out of the rain.

Everything after that happened fast, the way bad things do. Shouting. Wagons lurching. Hesk hauling Doss bodily behind a wheel. Marta's knives leaving her hands with the smooth economy of long practice. And Tam — earnest, spaniel-faithful Tam, who polished his sword every night — stepping in front of the second arrow meant for her, because that was the kind of man he was, and taking it high in the chest instead.

Bram caught him as he fell. She held the map in one fist and her dying friend in her other arm, and the rain came down on the valley of Ashgrove, and far away on his silent altar the god Kestrel said nothing at all.

But the knot on her belt burned brighter, and brighter, like something waking after three hundred years — and in the space of one impossible heartbeat, Bram Ashgrove understood that the life she'd been guarding all this time had just ended, and something else had begun, and it had begun with her name on it whether she consented or not.

"Tam," she said. "Stay with me. Tam—"

His eyes found hers, and he smiled, apologetic even now, even bleeding, and the rain fell, and the Ember remembered, and nothing in the valley would ever be quiet in the same way again.
