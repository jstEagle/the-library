## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The rain had been falling for two hours, and Doss Marlowe had been complaining about it for two hours and ten minutes.

"I'm just saying," he announced from beneath the wagon's oilcloth, where he'd wedged himself with the practiced cowardice of a man who had survived thirty years of mercenary work by never once being in front of anything, "that if the gods wanted us to travel in weather like this, they'd have given us gills. That's all. It's a theological point."

"The gods don't want anything," said Wren, who was driving, hunched under her hood like a wet crow. "That's rather the point of them."

"Kestrel wants things," Bram said from her saddle.

She said it mildly, the way you'd mention the price of oats, but she felt the old familiar settle in her chest as she said it — the small stubborn warmth she'd carried since she was six years old and an orphan with nothing but a temple blanket and a wooden pendant of a bird that would not sing. Kestrel, the silent god. The god who had no priests anymore, no temples but one crumbling shrine up-valley, no voice, no miracles, no demands. Just silence, and the strange loyalty of people like Bram Ashgrove, who kept faith with it anyway, the way you'd keep tending a grave.

"Kestrel wants things," Doss repeated, delighted. He loved this argument the way some men loved dice. "Name one."

"Silence," Bram said. "He wants silence. And you're ruining it."

Wren snorted. Even the mule seemed to smirk, which was impressive for a mule.

They were four days out from Harrow Ford with a cargo of salted fish, wool bales, and one nervous merchant's daughter being delivered to an aunt in Lowmarsh, and the contract was as routine as contracts came. Escort work. Bram had done it a hundred times. You walked the valley road, you watched the tree line, you got paid, and nothing ever happened, because nothing *ever* happened in the Ashen Valley, where magic had gone out of the world like a candle pinched between wet fingers three hundred years ago and never come back.

Bram told herself she liked the routine. She mostly believed it.

But there had been a restlessness in her lately, a feeling like a boot grown suddenly half a size too small, and the rain wasn't helping. She caught herself scanning the northern ridge — the direction of the ford, the direction of *out*, the direction of everything the valley was not — and made herself stop. Twice.

The third time, she didn't stop, because that was when the man came stumbling out of the trees.

* * *

He came down the slope like a puppet with half its strings cut, arms windmilling, feet finding the mud by accident more than intention. Bram had her crossbow up before her brain finished the thought, which was the correct order of operations and the only reason she was still alive at twenty-nine years in her line of work.

"Hold!" she called. "That's far enough!"

The man held, mostly because his legs gave out. He went down into the mud on his knees, and Bram got her first good look at him and revised every assumption she'd been building.

He wasn't a bandit. Bandits in the Ashen Valley were a sad, hungry lot, and they dressed like it. This man had worn fine clothes once — a traveler's coat with silver frogging, now scorched black across the whole left side, the burn edges curled like autumn leaves. His hair was gray and plastered to his skull. There was blood on him, a lot of it, and not all of it his own, though enough was. His hands were burned too, she saw. Burned badly, the way hands get when someone holds on to something that is trying very hard to be fire.

"Water," he rasped. "Please. Water."

"Bram," Wren said low, "we don't know him."

"We know he's dying." Bram was already swinging down from the saddle. "Dying men get water. That's not policy, Wren, that's just being a person."

She crouched beside him with the skin and held it to his mouth. He drank like a drowning man in reverse, desperate and graceless, and some of it ran down his chin and some of it came back up, and she steadied his head with one hand and felt how hot he was. Fever-hot. Worse than fever.

"They're coming," he said.

"Who's coming?"

He didn't answer. His eyes had found the wooden bird at her throat — Kestrel's knot, the little carved kestrel every child in the valley wore until they grew out of believing in anything, except Bram had never quite grown out of it — and something moved across his face that she would spend many sleepless nights afterward trying to name. Recognition? Relief? Fear?

"You wear him," he whispered.

"He doesn't mind."

A ghost of a smile. Then his hand shot out and closed around her wrist with a strength that should not have been in that body, and Bram's crossbow instincts flared and died all at once, because the man was looking at her now with terrible clarity, the way a lamp looks just before the oil runs out.

"Listen to me." Each word cost him visibly. "They burned the archive. They burned everyone. I ran because—" A cough took him, wet and deep. "Because someone had to run. Someone had to carry it out."

"Carry what?"

His other hand went inside his coat, and Wren's bowstring sang taut behind them, and Doss yelped something unhelpful from the wagon, and the merchant's daughter stuck her head out of the canvas to see what all the fuss was, because she was nineteen and had no survival instincts whatsoever.

What the man drew out was wrapped in oilcloth, and even through the wrapping Bram felt it — a heat, faint and wrong, like standing near an oven door. Not the heat of the body. Something older.

He pressed it into her hands.

"No," Bram said automatically. "No, friend, I'm not— I'm an escort. I deliver fish. Whatever this is, you want a priest, or a magistrate, or—"

"*The Ember remembers.*"

He said it quietly. Four words, and yet they landed in Bram's chest like stones dropped down a well, falling and falling without hitting bottom. The Ember remembers. She didn't know what it meant. She knew, with the same wordless certainty that told her when a bridge was rotten or a client was lying, that she would know what it meant eventually, and that she would wish she didn't.

"The Ember remembers," he said again, and now his grip was failing, sliding off her wrist, and his eyes were going glassy and distant, fixed on something over her shoulder — the ridge, maybe, or the rain, or three hundred years ago. "Tell my daughter— Isa— tell her I didn't—"

He never finished the sentence. His hand slipped from hers into the mud, palm up, open, the way children sleep.

For a long moment nobody moved. Rain ticked against the oilcloth. Somewhere upslope, a stream muttered over stones.

"Oh," said Doss, from the wagon, with the delicacy of a man who had seen death before but preferred it at a distance. "Oh, that's not good."

* * *

They buried him fast and shallow, because the ground was soft and the rain was hard and none of them were gravediggers. Wren said words over the mound — not prayers, exactly; Wren didn't hold with gods, or claimed not to, though Bram had noticed she always took her hat off passing the wayside shrines, which suggested a theology more complicated than advertised. Doss contributed a flask of whiskey poured into the earth, "so he has something for the road," which was either piety or an excuse to open the flask early.

Bram stood apart with the oilcloth bundle under her coat, against her chest, and tried not to think about how warm it was.

It was Tam who said what everyone was thinking.

"So we take it to the magistrate at Harrow Ford. Right?" Tam was the youngest of the hired hands, a farm boy with forearms like ham hocks and a face that hadn't learned to lie yet. "Man dies on the road carrying stolen goods, that's magistrate business. We hand it over, we give our account, we collect our fee, we go home. Clean."

"That's the sensible thing," Bram agreed.

"You say that like there's another option."

"I say it like I want to hear it out loud before I decide whether I believe it."

Tam grinned at her. He was always grinning. It was why she'd hired him over three other applicants with better references — well, that and the fact that he'd once talked a tax collector out of impounding his mother's cow through sheer relentless cheerfulness, which Bram considered a martial skill.

The bundle shifted against her ribs. Or she imagined it did. Warmth pulsed there, slow, patient, like a second heartbeat that had been waiting a long time for its turn.

She looked north, toward the ford, toward the magistrate's tidy office with its tidy ledgers, and thought about the burns on the dead man's hands. Burns like that came from holding on. Somebody had made him hold on to something while it tried to destroy itself — or while somebody else tried to destroy it. Magistrates didn't do that. Magistrates stamped papers.

But whoever had chased him across half the world to a valley where magic couldn't follow — they'd followed anyway. Into the one place in creation where their quarry should have been safe. Which meant they weren't afraid of the valley. Which meant they knew something about the valley that Bram didn't.

Which meant the valley wasn't what she'd always thought it was, either.

"Right," she said aloud, and heard how thin her voice came out. "Ford first. We'll see what the magistrate says."

"And if the magistrate's the one who burned him?" Wren asked, mild as milk.

Bram didn't have an answer for that. So she said, "Mount up," in her best captain's voice, and everyone pretended it was one.

* * *

They made the ford by late afternoon, if you could call it making it. The river had come up brown and muscular with the rain, and the crossing was a cold, swearing, knee-deep argument between men, mules, and current that the current mostly won. The merchant's daughter lost a boot to the river and laughed about it, high and bright, and Tam fished the boot out downstream and presented it to her with a bow so elaborate he nearly fell in himself, and Bram watched them and felt the ache of the day ease by one degree.

This, she thought. This was the job. Wet boots and bad jokes and getting paid at the end. Whatever the dead man had pressed into her hands, whatever *the Ember remembers* meant, it belonged to magistrates and priests and anyone but her.

She was wringing out her cloak on the far bank when the first arrow came.

It took Tam through the chest.

There was no warning cry, no dramatic moment — arrows don't announce themselves, that's the whole horror of them. One instant Tam was laughing, shaking river water out of his ear, and the next he was sitting down in the mud in a way that sitting-down-in-mud does not normally work, with a black-fletched shaft standing out of him below the collarbone, and his grin was still on his face but nothing behind it was grinning anymore.

Bram was moving before the sound reached her — the flat crack of a bowshot rolling down the valley — dragging Tam behind the wagon, shouting names, names that came out cracked. Wren had the mules down and was loosing blind into the trees, which was useless and human and right. Doss lay flat and prayed, loudly, to several gods at once, hedging.

Three more arrows fell, spent short in the mud. Then nothing. The treeline across the river stood gray and dripping and empty, and whoever had loosed those shafts was already gone, already moving, patient as winter.

Bram knelt in the mud with Tam's head in her lap.

He was still alive. That was the cruelest part. He looked up at her and his lips moved and she bent close and heard him say, "Boss... the cow story... tell it at my..." and then he coughed, pinkly, and stopped finishing sentences forever, the way the stranger had, and Bram understood that this was apparently what her life was now: a place where men stopped finishing sentences.

She stayed there a long time. The rain thinned. The river kept on going about its business, indifferent, enormous.

When she finally stood, her knees were stiff and her face was dry, which surprised her, because she felt like she ought to be crying and simply couldn't find the mechanism. Grief would come later, she supposed. Grief always sent its bill later.

Wren came and stood beside her. Neither of them spoke for a while.

"They let the others live," Wren said at last. "You notice that? Three shots at a crowd of seven, and every arrow goes to the man standing nearest you." She turned her head. Her eyes under the wet hood were sharp and sorry. "That wasn't a warning, Bram. That was a message. *Give it back, or we start taking more.*"

Bram's hand found the bundle beneath her soaked coat. It pulped warm against her palm, steady as a sleeping heart, and for the first time since the ford she let herself feel the full shape of what she was holding — not a package, not evidence, not magistrate business. An inheritance. The kind you don't get to refuse, because refusing it just means someone else pays the estate's debts.

The dead man's voice sat in her ears. *The Ember remembers.*

And underneath it, fainter, older, in a register she had no name for — a warmth behind her breastbone that had nothing to do with any bundle, answering something, calling something, quiet as it had been all her life and somehow, tonight, less silent than it had ever been.

"Break camp," Bram said. "We're not stopping at Harrow Ford."

Wren raised an eyebrow. "Where are we stopping?"

Bram looked north, past the ford, past the edge of the map she'd lived her whole life inside. "There's a woman in Lowmarsh," she said. "Odile Vane. They threw her out of the archives for saying dragons were real." She swallowed. "I think she's about to have a very good year."

Behind them, the river ran on. Ahead, the road climbed out of the valley, into the country where magic had died — or so everyone said.

The bundle against Bram's heart beat on, warm and patient, like something that had waited three hundred years and could wait a little longer.

But not much longer. She could feel that now, too.
