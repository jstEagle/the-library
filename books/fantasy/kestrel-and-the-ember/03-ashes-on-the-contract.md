## 3. Ashes on the Contract

The town of Marrow's Ford had exactly two things going for it: a bathhouse that didn't smell entirely of sulfur, and a magistrate who paid bounties in coin rather than promises. Bram Ashgrove counted both among life's small mercies, and after the night she'd had, she intended to collect on them.

She rode in at midmorning with the dead man's map sewn into the lining of her coat and one of her hired hands — Piet, twenty-two years old, fond of bad jokes and worse dice — wrapped in canvas behind her saddle. The other three of her crew had scattered at dawn like startled pigeons, which was fair enough. Escort contracts did not generally include arrows out of the dark and dying strangers pressing secrets into your palms. She'd told them she'd square their wages herself. It was a lie she could afford for another week.

The ford itself was quiet. Too quiet, some part of her noted, the part that had kept her alive through eleven years of contracts. The river ran low and brown, the ferryman was mending a net that needed no mending, and the crows on the tollhouse roof were watching the road south instead of scavenging the market scraps like sensible birds.

"Morning, Ashgrove," the ferryman said without looking up. "Heard you had trouble up-valley."

"Heard wrong," Bram said. "Had a quiet escort and an early finish."

"Mm." He pulled a knot tight. "Quiet, was it."

Bram decided not to have that conversation. Conversations with men who mended nets that weren't broken tended to end with her learning things she'd have to act on, and she wanted a bath first. Priorities.

* * *

The bathhouse was as advertised, which is to say the sulfur smell was present but polite about it. Bram soaked until her fingers pruned, scrubbed the valley dust out of her hair, and tried very hard not to think about the weight against her ribs where the map lay hidden.

It was no use. She thought about it anyway.

*The Ember remembers.* That was what the man had said, right before he'd stopped being anything at all. His blood had been sticky and cold by then, and his hand had gripped hers with the desperate strength of someone spending their last coin on a message. She hadn't asked for it. She wanted that on the record, wherever records were kept — before whatever gods were still listening, before any magistrate or knight-captain who came asking. She had been minding her own business, escorting a wool merchant's goods through a valley where nothing interesting had happened in four hundred years, and then a stranger had died on her and made her responsible for something.

That was how curses worked, wasn't it? Nobody ever *accepted* the cursed object. It just died into their hands.

"You've got a look," said the bath attendant, a round grandmotherly woman named Tansy who had known Bram since Bram was small enough to be bathed rather than bathe. "The look you get when you're arguing with yourself and losing."

"I'm always losing that argument."

"Aye, but usually you enjoy it." Tansy wrung out a cloth and studied her. "Word from up-valley is there was killing at the old shrine crossing. Arrows. And now here you come, looking like a woman carrying more than wet laundry."

"The valley's full of rumors."

"The valley's full of *you*, girl, riding fast with a body under a tarp." Tansy's voice gentled. "Whatever it is — give it to Magistrate Corren and let the law carry it. That's what he's for. That's what we pay him for, when we pay him."

And that, Bram thought later, was the exact shape of the trap: not a snare in the road or a knife in the dark, but a kind old woman saying *that's what the law is for*, because Bram had spent her whole life wanting very badly to believe it.

"That's the plan," Bram said, and was surprised to find she mostly meant it.

* * *

Magistrate Corren held court in a low stone building that smelled of ink, lamp oil, and the particular staleness of a man who ate every meal at his desk. He was sixty-ish, soft-handed, with spectacles he polished when he was thinking and a reputation — earned over fifteen years — for fairness so relentless it bordered on tedious. He had once fined his own nephew for poaching and made the boy work off the fine hauling stones for the shrine wall. People told that story the way they told stories about saints.

"Bram Ashgrove." He looked up as she entered, and something flickered across his face too quickly to name. "Sit. Sit. I heard about your contract. A death?"

"One of my hires. Piet Weller. Arrow through the throat, up at the shrine crossing. I brought him back for his mother." She set the canvas bundle's paperwork on his desk — she'd written it out herself at dawn, hands steady, which had taken effort. "There was also a man. Stranger. Died of wounds before I could get a word of sense from him. No name, no papers. I'll cover the burial if the town won't."

Corren read the report. Polished his spectacles. Read it again.

"And this stranger," he said carefully, "carried nothing? No effects?"

Here it was. The moment. Bram had rehearsed this in the bath, in the saddle, in the gray space before waking. *Hand it over. Let the law carry it.* She reached inside her coat.

And stopped.

Not because of danger. Not because of any sixth sense. Because of something small and stupid: Corren had asked *no effects?* before asking *how did he die?* Fifteen years of fairness, and his first question about a dead man was whether the man had been carrying anything worth having.

Or maybe that was just what magistrates asked. Maybe she was tired and jumpy and seeing conspiracy in clerical habit. Gods knew she'd spent two days sleeping in shifts with one eye open.

"No effects," Bram said, and heard herself say it, and understood in the same instant that she had just lied to Magistrate Corren for the first time in her life, and that some part of her had decided before her mouth caught up.

"Nothing at all?" Corren set down his spectacles. "No documents? No... maps?"

The word landed like a dropped glass.

"I wouldn't know," Bram said. "I searched him. Coin purse, a knife, a saint's medal worn smooth. That's all."

"A saint's medal." Corren smiled. It was a warm smile. It should have been reassuring. "Which saint?"

"I didn't look that close."

"Hm." He rose, went to the window, looked out at the street with his hands clasped behind his back — a posture Bram had seen him take a hundred times while deliberating fines and fence disputes. "You should know, Ashgrove, that there are people looking for a man matching your stranger's description. Serious people. They came through yesterday evening. They asked me to hold anyone who might have... assisted him."

"They came through *yesterday?*" The shrine crossing was a hard day's ride north. "They were ahead of him?"

"Behind him, I think. Or flanking. They didn't explain their business." Corren turned back. "They wore grey. Unmarked grey, which is its own sort of mark, if you follow me. Their leader was a woman. Tall. Very calm. She gave me this."

He slid a paper across the desk. It was a warrant — not a local writ but something broader, sealed with wax the color of ash and stamped with a sigil Bram didn't recognize: a circle, hollow at its center. An empty ring. A ring with nothing in it.

"The Hollow Order," Corren said. "Ever heard of them?"

"No."

"Neither had I, until last night. They claim authority under some old charter I've never seen. They're offering a hundred crowns for recovery of 'stolen property' the stranger may have carried." He took the paper back, folded it with neat precision. "A hundred crowns, Ashgrove. That's ten years of escort work."

"I'm aware of arithmetic, Magistrate."

"I don't doubt it." He sat back down and laced his fingers together on the desk, and for a long moment he just looked at her, and Bram felt the temperature of the room change the way it changes when a friendly dog goes still. "So here is my difficulty. These Order people are camped at the mill. They've asked me to send word the moment anyone comes forward about the dead man. And you've just told me he carried nothing — which means either you're telling me the truth, in which case none of this concerns you..."

"...or I'm lying," Bram finished quietly.

"Or you're lying." He said it without heat, almost sadly. "I've known you since you were nine years old, stealing apples from my own tree. You were a terrible liar then. You cried when I caught you."

"I was eight."

"You were eight, and you cried, and I gave you the apples anyway because your mother had just died and I didn't have the heart." He removed his spectacles entirely. "You're not crying now. So I have to ask myself what changed."

Everything, Bram thought. Everything changed two nights ago when a man bled out in my arms and said *the Ember remembers* like it was a prayer and a warning in the same breath.

"Magistrate," she said slowly, "if I *had* found something on him — hypothetically — would you give it to those people at the mill?"

Corren was silent long enough that the clock on his shelf got loud.

"I'd give it to the law," he said finally. "And the law, right now, wears whatever face walks in with a charter and a hundred crowns. That's the honest answer. You deserved the honest answer."

Something in Bram's chest went cold and clear. "Then I'm sorry, sir. But I can't help you."

"I was afraid you'd say that." He didn't sound sorry at all. He sounded — and this was the worst part, the part that would keep her up that night — *relieved*. As though a decision he'd already made had finally been handed an excuse. "Ashgrove. Don't make this harder than it has to be."

The door behind her opened.

Two men came in wearing unmarked grey, and they moved the way soldiers move when they've done this before — one to flank the door, one to stand between Bram and the window, unhurried, courteous, closing distances. The tall calm woman from Corren's description followed them in. Up close she was younger than Bram expected, forty maybe, with a face like a well-kept ledger and eyes that assessed Bram the way a butcher assesses a carcass: not with cruelty, just with professional completeness.

"There she is," the woman said pleasantly. "The good samaritan."

"I don't know you," Bram said.

"No. But I know you, Bram Ashgrove. Orphan of the Kestrel temple, valley-raised, eleven years under contract, no debts, no vices except cheap tobacco and sentiment." The woman smiled. "You prayed at his side, didn't you? When he was dying. I'd wager my horse on it. Devout little mercenary. Which makes you predictable."

"Serah—" Corren began.

"Not yet, Magistrate. We're just talking." The woman — Serah, apparently — drifted closer, boots quiet on the stone floor. "The man who died. What did he say to you? Think carefully. This is the only time I ask nicely."

Bram's mind was moving fast now, cold and practical, cataloguing exits. Door: blocked. Window: one man, but windows in Marrow's Ford opened onto a market street, and markets meant crowds, and crowds meant chaos, and chaos was a currency she'd spent wisely all her life. The map: against her left ribs, sewn into the coat lining, and Serah's gaze had not gone there even once, which meant either she didn't know it existed or she was better than Bram at hiding where she looked.

"He said," Bram answered, "that he was sorry to die on a stranger's floor. Then he died."

"Nothing else? Nothing about fire? About remembering?"

Bram kept her face still through sheer professional spite. "Should he have?"

For a heartbeat — half a heartbeat — something moved behind Serah's calm. Not doubt. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman hearing an echo she'd hoped was buried.

"Search her," Serah said, stepping back. "Gently. She's cooperated beautifully."

"Gently" lasted about four seconds. The grey men were thorough and impersonal, and Bram stood for it with her arms out because fighting two armed men in a magistrate's office over a coat lining was a losing proposition — until the taller one's fingers found the seam along her ribs and paused.

"There's stitching here," he said. "Fresh."

The room changed. Bram felt it change, the way weather changes before hail. Corren stood up. Serah's pleasantness dropped away like a mask coming loose.

"Coat off," Serah said.

And Bram — who had rehearsed surrender in the bathhouse, who had genuinely planned to hand the wretched thing over to the law, who had lied to a decent old man's face and hated herself for it — found that being *told* to disrobe by strangers in a magistrate's office cured her of every remaining scruple in a single hot flash.

"No," she said, and threw the inkwell at the lamp.

It was not a subtle move. It was not supposed to be. The lamp shattered, oil splashed, flame caught the spilled ink and the papers on Corren's desk with a sound like a gasp, and in the sudden shouting dark Bram went through the window.

Glass took a strip of skin off her forearm and something hotter took the rest — a lick of flame off the burning desk, catching her sleeve, searing a line across the back of her hand before she rolled it out against the cobblestones. She came up running. Behind her the office door banged open, boots on stone, Serah's voice cutting through the smoke: *"Alive! I want her alive — she knows the route!"*

The route. So they didn't have the whole map. Something in Bram's chest unclenched and immediately clenched again for different reasons: *she knows the route* meant they knew there *was* a route, which meant the dead man's secret was already half-dead, which meant everything from here on was a race.

* * *

She lost them in the market, which was less skill than luck and less luck than the fact that Marrow's Ford's market day crowd regarded armed strangers in grey with the deep rural hostility of people who had never once benefited from anyone's charter. Someone tripped the tall grey man. Someone else released a goat. Bram was around the tannery and over the back wall before the screaming properly started, and by the time she reached the livery her hands had almost stopped shaking.

Almost.

Her horse was saddled in ninety seconds flat, a personal record she owed to pure animal terror. She swung up, turned toward the north road —

— and stopped.

North was the valley. North was home, the shrine, the quiet, the place where nothing interesting happened and nothing ever would. Every instinct she owned screamed north, home, hole up, hide.

But Serah had said *she knows the route*, and routes went somewhere, and the map's first seal — Odile Vane's name surfaced in Bram's memory like a fish rising. The disgraced archivist. The madwoman in the cellar two towns east who claimed dragons still lived and had been laughed out of three universities for it. The dead man's map was covered in seals and script nobody in the valley could read.

Somebody had to read it. And the law, as of this morning, wore grey.

Bram looked back once at Marrow's Ford — at the smoke still rising from the magistrate's office, at the little town where she'd stolen apples and buried her mother and believed, right up until an hour ago, that fairness was a real thing that lived in stone buildings.

"Sorry, Corren," she murmured. "About the desk."

She put her heels to the horse and rode east, cradling her burned hand against her chest, the map a small hard weight against her ribs like a second heart. Behind her, faint on the wind, she thought she heard the mill bell start to ring.

By nightfall, she knew, the grey riders would fan out along every road. By tomorrow, her description would be in every waystation and ferry landing within fifty miles. She had perhaps a day's lead and a hand that already throbbed like a struck thumb, and the only person in the world who might make sense of what she carried was a disgraced scholar everyone said was mad.

Bram Ashgrove rode east into the gathering dusk and allowed herself one bitter laugh at the sky.

"You could say something," she told the silent god of her childhood. "Any time. Really. I'll wait."

The sky, faithful as ever, said nothing.

But against her ribs — just once, just barely, easy to blame on the road's jostling — the map felt warm.
