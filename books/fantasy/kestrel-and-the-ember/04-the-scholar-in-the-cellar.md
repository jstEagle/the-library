## 4. The Scholar in the Cellar

Bram had been told three things about Odile Vane, and she believed none of them.

The first was that the woman was mad. This came from a tanner's boy who also swore the millpond was haunted by a drowned wedding party, so Bram filed it under unreliable. The second was that she'd been run out of the University of Corveth for insisting dragons still lived — which, given what Bram had pressed against her ribs two nights ago in the form of a scorched map that glowed like a banked coal, sounded less like madness and more like bad timing. The third was that she lived under a cobbler's shop in Lowmarsh and would curse you for breathing her air.

That one, Bram suspected, might be true.

Lowmarsh smelled the way its name promised: mud and tanneries and the sweet rot of the canal. She left Doss and Merrick at the corner with instructions to look bored — they were excellent at it; Merrick had made boredom a career — and went down the alley alone, counting doors until she found the one with no number and a door knocker shaped like an owl missing half its face.

She knocked. Nothing. She knocked again, harder.

"Go away," said a voice from below, muffled through stone. "I'm not buying relics, I'm not signing petitions, and if you're from the tax assessor, I have been dead for six years and dead women pay nothing."

"I need an archivist," Bram said.

A pause. Then, suspiciously: "What kind of archivist?"

"The kind who got exiled for being right."

Another pause, longer. Bram imagined a thin woman behind a thick door, weighing curiosity against caution. Curiosity won — it usually did with scholars, or so Bram hoped, because her other plan involved kicking the door in, and she doubted that would win her any favors.

The lock turned. The door opened on darkness and a smell of old paper, lamp oil, and something faintly burnt, like a candle that had once caught a curtain and been forgiven.

"You'd better come down," said the voice. "And wipe your boots. I have documents older than your grandmother's grudges."

* * *

Odile Vane was not what Bram expected, though she couldn't have said what she'd expected instead. Small, sharp-eyed, gray-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be two pencils and a fishbone. Her hands were stained with ink to the second knuckle, as if she'd dipped them past the wrist and given up halfway. She wore a man's coat over a nightgown, which suggested either eccentricity or that she hadn't slept, and the cluttered cellar suggested both.

"So," Odile said, lighting a lamp with hands that were not quite steady. "You know who I am. Which means someone talked, which means I'll have to move again, which means—" She stopped. Looked at Bram properly. "You're armed like a soldier but dressed like a courier. Mercenary?"

"Escort work, mostly."

"And you've come to me because?"

Bram hesitated. Every instinct she owned — and she owned a good many, all of them scarred — said don't show anyone, don't trust anyone, burn the map and go home. But the map had other opinions. It had grown warmer every day since the ford, warm enough now that she carried it wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, and twice in the night she'd woken to find her hand resting over it like a woman guarding a sleeping child.

She unwrapped it.

Odile Vane went very still.

It was the stillness of a bird dog scenting game, Bram thought later. The scholar didn't reach for the map. She leaned close instead, hands clasped behind her back like a child forbidden to touch the jam, and studied the scorched leather, the faded lines, the seal stamped at its center — a circle broken by seven spokes, each spoke marked with a symbol no one in the valley could read.

"Where did you get this?" Odile asked quietly.

"A dying man gave it to me at the ford. His name was Varrow. He said—" Bram swallowed. "He said 'the Ember remembers.' Then he died."

Odile sat down. Not gracefully — she simply folded, the way a puppet does when someone cuts a string, landing on a stool stacked with folios.

"Edran Varrow," she said. "Oh, you absolute fool, Edran. You were supposed to come to me first." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "He was my colleague. Before. Before Corveth decided I was a lunatic and he decided I was an embarrassment he could live without. He wrote to me last spring. One letter. It said: *I found the third proof. Burn this.* Naturally I didn't burn it."

"The third proof of what?"

"That dragons lived." Odile laughed, a short, cracked sound. "Not *had* lived. Everyone agrees dragons had lived; there are bones big enough to build chapels out of. No — I stood up in the great hall of the University of Corveth, with the chancellor frowning and forty students smirking, and I said: dragons are not extinct. They are *hidden*. And the fire we call the Ember — the fire the old wars were fought over — is not a legend. It is alive. And it is guarded." She tapped the map with one ink-stained finger, not quite touching it. "They took my fellowship. My rooms. My name off the rolls. My mother stopped writing. All of that, for saying out loud what this map says in ink."

Bram felt something loosen in her chest — not relief exactly, but the near cousin of it. "Can you read it?"

"Oh, I can read it." Odile's mouth twisted. "Reading it is the easy part. The question is whether I should help you."

"Why wouldn't you?"

"Because, mercenary, think about what you're carrying." Odile rose and began to pace between her shelves, talking with her whole body the way some people talk with their hands. "This is a route-map to the Ember. To the last living dragon-fire in the world, the thing that keeps — depending on which heresy you prefer — either an ancient war sealed shut, or an ancient god asleep, or both. There are people who have spent centuries trying to find it. And there are other people who have spent just as long trying to make sure no one ever does. Neither group is known for their mercy toward couriers."

"The Hollow Order," Bram said.

Something flickered across Odile's face. "You've met them."

"They killed the man who gave me this. Their arrows. And yesterday I learned the magistrate back home wears their badge under his chain of office. I have a burn scar on my arm to remember him by." Bram held the older woman's gaze. "So yes. I've met them."

Odile stopped pacing. For a long moment the only sound was the lamp hissing and, somewhere above them, the cobbler hammering away at a sole as though the world were a reasonable place.

"Then here is my answer," Odile said at last, and her voice was flat and final as a slammed book. "No. I will tell you what the first seal says, because you'll only get yourself killed stumbling around ignorant, and Edran would haunt me. But I will not come with you. I have been exiled once for this obsession. I rebuilt a life here — such as it is. I am not going to die in a ditch for a dead man's map and a stranger's stubbornness."

Bram nodded slowly. She'd expected refusal; refusals were cheap and common. What mattered was that Odile hadn't thrown her out.

"Tell me about the seal," she said, and sat down to listen.

* * *

The cellar smelled of dust and vinegar and, beneath it, the ghost of smoke — old smoke, soaked into the stone years ago, the kind of smell that never fully leaves a place once fire has visited. Odile had catalogued it along with everything else. *Cellar, former smokehouse, damp in the north corner, one window (barred), one occupant (unlicensed).*

She worked by lamplight with the map pinned flat under glass weights, copying the seven-spoked seal onto fresh paper, breaking it into components the way she broke everything into components because components couldn't lie to you. Whole truths could. Components couldn't.

*First spoke: the river-sign. Second: the ford-mark, doubled. Third—*

Her pen paused. The third symbol was a stylized flame enclosed in a ring of small marks, and those marks, when she rotated the paper and squinted, resolved into letters of Old Corvethan script. A word. A warning, most likely. Or an instruction.

*Only the blood remembers.*

Odile set down her pen and rubbed her eyes. That phrase appeared in three sources she knew of: the Ashfall Litany, a fragmentary hymn recovered from the caldera country; a marginal note in the Vell Codex, dismissed by generations of scholars as a monk's doodle; and a children's rhyme still sung in valley villages, stripped of meaning, about a god who "went quiet to keep his promise." Three sources, three contexts, one phrase. In scholarship, that was practically a shout.

*Only the blood remembers.*

Which raised the obvious question: whose blood?

She looked at the mercenary, dozing upright in the corner chair with her sword across her knees like a faithful hound. Bram Ashgrove — the name she'd given, though Odile had already noted the way she'd hesitated on the surname, a hesitation Odile had filed away next to a hundred other small observations. The woman had walked into a stranger's cellar carrying the most dangerous document in the world and shown it to the first disgraced archivist she'd heard of. That wasn't desperation. Desperation hoarded. That was something else — a person looking for a reason to hand over a burden.

Interesting. Also inconvenient. Odile had a life. It was a small life, a dusty life, a life consisting mainly of vinegar and marginalia, but it was hers, and she had paid for it with everything else.

"No," she reminded herself softly. "Absolutely not."

From the chair, without opening her eyes: "Talking to yourself is the first sign."

"The first sign of what?"

"Of having spent too long alone. Trust me. I'm an expert."

"Hm." Odile returned to the seal. "Your man Varrow opened the first seal before he died. Partially. See here — the river-sign is scored through, deliberately, with a knife. He was marking his progress. Which means the seals aren't locks in the ordinary sense. They're *permissions*. You don't break them. You satisfy them. Go to the place the first spoke names, do whatever the mark requires, and the map reveals the next leg of the road. Like a letter that only reads itself as you travel."

"And the first place is?"

"A drowned chapel, upriver, past the veil-line." Odile said it lightly, watching Bram's face. The mercenary's jaw tightened. Everyone from the valley knew the veil-line — the boundary where magic stopped working, where charms went dead and blessings curdled. Nobody crossed it for fun. "Beyond it, your sword will still cut and your legs will still walk, but anything touched by the old fire behaves strangely. Including, I suspect, that map. Including, possibly, you."

"Me?"

"You said the shrine-prayers feel different lately." Bram stiffened. Odile smiled thinly. "You mentioned it while describing the weather. People only describe prayers while describing weather when the prayers are doing something the weather isn't."

For a moment Bram looked genuinely dangerous, and Odile was reminded that under the courier's clothes was a woman who killed people professionally. Then the danger drained into something wearier.

"It's nothing," Bram said. "An old habit."

"Mm." Odile bent back over the map. "Well. Here is what I can give you, since I refuse to give you myself. The translation of the first seal. The location of the chapel. And this advice: whatever the Order has told the world about the Ember, the truth is worse and better than their story, and you will not be able to unlearn it once you know. Now go away. I have work."

Bram stood, rolled the map, tucked it away. At the foot of the stairs she paused. "Vane. If you change your mind—"

"I won't."

"The cellar door sticks. Anyone burning you out would come from above. Consider a second exit."

"I have considered several. Goodbye."

But after the mercenary left, Odile sat for a long time without writing anything, listening to the cobbler's hammer overhead, thinking about the phrase *only the blood remembers*, and about how the woman in the corner chair had flinched at it — flinched, Odile would swear, before Odile herself explained what it meant.

Components couldn't lie. People flinched for reasons.

She wrote the observation in the margin of her private notebook, sealed it, and told herself it changed nothing.

* * *

They came at midnight.

Bram was three streets away, walking back to the inn, when she saw the glow — wrong-colored for lamplight, too orange, too jumpy — and heard the bells start. Lowmarsh had no fire brigade worth the name, just buckets and shouting, and by the time she fought her way back down the alley the cobbler's shop was an open furnace and the street was full of neighbors passing water hand to hand and accomplishing nothing.

Four men in plain dark clothes stood at the alley mouth, not helping, not leaving. Watching. One held a lantern with the shutter half-closed, and as it swung Bram saw the badge on his breast: a hollow circle.

She melted into a doorway and did arithmetic. Four men. Her sword. The crowd between them. And a scholar somewhere in that burning building, because Bram had watched the windows and seen no one come out.

Doss materialized beside her, breathing hard. "Boss. Fire's spread to the tannery wall. We should go."

"In a minute."

"Boss—"

"Get me that lantern-man's attention," Bram said. "Then be ready to run."

What followed was not, she would later insist, a plan. It was more of a strong suggestion delivered at speed. Doss stumbled into the alley mouth crying drunkenly about his burned cart, the lantern-man turned to shove him aside, and Bram came out of the doorway low and fast, put her shoulder into the second man, and used the resulting confusion to be through the shop's side door and into the smoke before anyone sensible could vote on it.

Inside was hell with shelves. Fire had taken the stairwell; heat pressed down like a hand. She got her scarf over her mouth and shouted Odile's name until her throat tore.

"HERE," came a cough from the far wall. "The WINDOW, you idiot, everyone forgets the window—"

Bram followed the voice through falling embers and found Odile Vane standing on a table in the corner of her burning kingdom, shoving armfuls of books through a barred window into the canal-dark beyond, of all the mad, magnificent—

"The BOOKS?" Bram roared.

"SOME OF THEM ARE IRREPLACEABLE."

"SO ARE YOU, PROBABLY. MOVE."

To her lasting surprise, Odile moved. Bram boosted her at the window; the bars, Bram discovered, had been sawn nearly through long ago — so much for *I have considered several exits* — and one good kick finished the job. They went through together into the cold black water of the marsh channel, surfaced gasping among reeds and floating folios, and lay on the muddy bank while the fire roared behind them and the cobbler's hammer, somewhere impossibly, kept its rhythm, the cobbler himself having apparently decided his cellar tenant was someone else's problem.

They lay side by side, dripping, coughing, watching the sky turn orange.

"Well," Odile said at last, in the tone of a woman revising a ledger entry. "It appears I have been evicted."

"Looks like it."

"By the Hollow Order. Who now believe I helped you, which I did, technically, by translating a seal and giving directions, so they're not even wrong, which is the most insulting part." She sat up, wrung out her sleeve, and stared at the flames with an expression Bram couldn't read — grief and fury and, underneath both, a terrible bright relief, like a debt finally called. "I had a life, Ashgrove. It was small, but it was mine."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." Odile got to her feet, swaying, and stuck out an ink-stained, canal-water-soaked hand. "The first seal opens at a drowned chapel past the veil-line. I can translate the rest of this map, and you cannot, and neither of us can stay within fifty miles of here. These are the facts. I am coming with you, and I want it noted that I am doing so under protest, and that if you die stupidly I will be extremely annoyed."

Bram took the hand and pulled her up. Somewhere across the water, the four watchers had vanished; somewhere behind them, thirty years of scholarship went up in sparks that drifted over the marsh like angry fireflies.

"Noted," Bram said. "Under protest."

"Good." Odile retrieved one floating book, hugged it to her chest, and looked at the burning skyline with narrowed eyes. "Now. About that phrase on the third spoke — *only the blood remembers*. Before we go anywhere, mercenary, you and I are going to have a conversation about why you flinched."

Bram's stomach dropped through the mud of the marsh, all the way down.

Because the truth was, she hadn't flinched at the phrase at all.

She'd flinched because, standing in that cellar with the map glowing warm against her ribs, she had understood it instantly — the way you understand your own name — and she had known, in the wordless way she'd known Kestrel's silence all her life, that whatever waited at the end of this road already knew her back.
