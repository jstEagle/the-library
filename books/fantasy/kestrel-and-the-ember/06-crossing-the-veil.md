## 6. Crossing the Veil

The valley let them go the way a mother lets go of a child's hand at a schoolhouse door — slowly, reluctantly, with a squeeze that said *come back*.

Bram felt it in her teeth first. She always did. Twenty-nine years of living inside the silence had taught her that the valley's quiet wasn't empty; it was *held*, like breath, like a hand cupped over a candle flame. And when she rode north along the pilgrim road with Odile Vane rattling along beside her on a mule named Turnip, that held breath began to thin.

"You're doing the thing with your jaw," Odile observed from atop her mule. "The grinding thing."

"I'm not."

"Your whole face is a millstone. I can hear flour being made."

Bram unclenched her teeth with an effort. The morning was cold and clean, the kind of mountain morning that made even the dead pines along the roadside look dignified rather than blighted. Behind them, three days back, lay the magistrate's hall and the burn scar across her left forearm where his men had tried to take the map off her body with fire. Ahead of them lay the ford at Greywater, the old boundary stones, and everything her grandmother had spent a lifetime warning her about.

*Nothing good comes over the mountains, little wren,* Gran used to say, kneading bread in the temple kitchen while Bram sat on the flour bin. *Magic's like weather. It doesn't care if you've got plans.*

"I still think we should have gone south," Odile said. "Through Lowmark. There are archives in Lowmark. Archives with *catalogues*."

"The magistrate was in Lowmark's pocket. You said so yourself."

"I said he was probably in someone's pocket. A scholar hedges." Odile pushed her spectacles up her nose. They were cracked, one lens webbed with fine white lines, and she refused to replace them because — she claimed — the crack gave her a more honest view of the world. "Besides, my charms don't work in the valley. If we're going to test whether any of my life's work is worth the ink it ruined me for, we'll need to be past the stones."

That was the other reason for the northern road, though neither of them said it aloud. The map glowed. Faintly, fitfully, like a coal remembering fire — and it glowed *stronger* the closer they came to the boundary. Bram had taken to sleeping with it wrapped in oilcloth inside her shirt, against her sternum, and twice in the night she'd woken convinced someone had laid a warm palm over her heart.

She didn't tell Odile about the warmth. She wasn't sure why. It felt like something you kept, the way you kept a first kiss or a cowardice.

Behind them rode Tam Weller, the last of her hired hands, leading the packhorse. Tam was nineteen, freckled, and had signed onto the escort contract on the understanding that it involved guarding grain shipments, not fleeing a secret order of god-hating knights with a burning map sewn into a fugitive archivist's coat lining. He had not yet quit, which Bram counted as either loyalty or shock.

"Miss Ashgrove," Tam called up. "Question."

"If it's about pay—"

"It's about whether we're all going to die."

"That's also about pay," Odile murmured, "in a sense. Death voids most contracts."

"We're not going to die," Bram said, with the flat confidence of a woman who had absolutely no way of knowing that. "We're going to cross the boundary, find out what the map wants us to find, and come home richer and wiser."

"That's what my uncle said before the war," Tam said. "He came home richer, technically. He found a helmet."

They crested the ridge at midday, and there it was: the line.

You couldn't see it, exactly. That was the trick of it, the thing outsiders never believed. From the ridge the world looked continuous — grey-green forest flowing down into the river valley, the boundary stones just mossy markers like any others, the far hills blue and ordinary. But Bram could feel the edge of it the way a fish feels the glass of its tank. Twenty-nine years she'd lived inside the silence, and only now, standing at its border, did she understand how loud the silence had been.

Beyond the stones, the world was *waiting*.

"Right," Odile said briskly, dismounting with the creak and complaint of a woman whose knees had opinions. She dug through her saddlebag and produced a small leather roll, which she unrolled on a flat stone with the reverence of a priest laying out relics. Inside were perhaps two dozen charms: knotted cords, wax seals stamped with sigils, a dried newt that Bram chose not to ask about. "Now. In the valley, these are decorative. Pretty knots. Very sad newt. Past the boundary—" she picked up a cord woven from three colors of thread, "—if I have wasted eleven years of my life, this will remain a pretty knot. If I have not wasted them, it will do *this*."

She stepped over the boundary stone.

The knot caught fire.

Not dangerously — a small, steady flame blooming in the heart of the braid, shedding light but not heat, burning without consuming. Odile stared at it. Her mouth worked. For a long moment the disgraced archivist of the Royal Collegium, expelled for heresy, stripped of her title, laughed at in three cities, stood very still in the road holding a burning string and looking like a woman who had just been told her father loved her after all.

"Well," she said finally, voice cracking straight down the middle. "*Well.*"

"Odile—"

"It works." She laughed, wet and startled. "It — Bram, it *works*. Eleven years. They called me Vane the Vacant. The Collegium provost said I had a talent for finding things that weren't there." She turned the charm in her fingers, watching the little flame gutter and hold. "It's not even a good charm. It's early work. The knot tension is appalling."

Tam had backed his horse up several feet. "Should it be doing that?"

"Yes," Odile and Bram said together.

Bram looked past them, down the slope, to where the boundary ran — invisible, patient — between the valley and everything else. Her old wounds ached. Not the fresh burn on her arm, though that throbbed too; she meant the *old* ones. The break in her left leg from the bridge collapse at Corben's Mill, twelve years gone. The knife scar under her ribs from a job in the lowlands she never talked about. The shoulder she'd wrecked falling from a horse at seventeen. All of them woke at once, deep and dull, like instruments tuning in a dark room.

And underneath the ache — warmth. The map against her sternum had gone from ember to hearth-coal.

"Your turn," Odile said gently.

"My turn for what?"

"To cross. You're hovering, dear. Mercenaries don't hover."

"I'm assessing."

"You're hovering *tactically*."

Bram swung down from the saddle before she could think better of it, which was, she suspected, the only way she would ever do anything that mattered. She walked to the boundary stone. Up close she could see old carvings in it, worn nearly smooth — spirals, and beneath the spirals, a bird. A kestrel, wings folded, watching.

Her chest went tight. She'd passed this stone a hundred times. She'd never once looked at it.

"For the record," Tam called, "I want it noted I think this is a bad idea."

"Noted," Bram said, and stepped over.

The world changed.

There was no thunderclap, no wall of light. It was subtler than that and vastly worse: it was as if she had been deaf all her life and suddenly could hear — and the world was *loud*. Color came first. The forest beyond the boundary was green in a way the valley's forests had never managed, green the way blood is red, essential and excessive. Moss on the stones glowed faintly where the shade was deepest. A spiderweb strung between two branches held beads of light that were not quite dew. Somewhere upslope, water was running uphill — she could see it, a thread of silver climbing over a root and vanishing, casual as anything.

And the air. The air tasted of things. Rain that hadn't fallen yet. Woodsmoke from a fire no one had lit. Something sweet and rotten and ancient, like honey left in a tomb.

Bram's leg buckled.

She caught herself on the boundary stone, gasping. The ache in her old wounds had sharpened from tuning to playing — every scar singing one long low note, and beneath it, under her breastbone, the map pulsed like a second heart answering the first.

"Bram!" Odile was beside her, hands hovering. "What is it? What do you feel?"

"Everything," Bram managed. "I feel everything. Is it always like this?"

"No." Odile's brow furrowed. She pressed two fingers to Bram's wrist, professional despite everything. "For most people crossing, it's a headache. A day of vivid dreams. This is—" she hesitated, and Bram watched her choose her words with visible care, which frightened her more than the pain did. "This is more like a resonance. Like the boundary knows you."

"The boundary doesn't know me. I grew up in a valley with *no magic in it*. That's the entire point of the valley."

"Mm," said Odile, in the tone of a scholar filing something away for later, which was its own small menace.

The map's warmth spread outward from Bram's sternum, down her arm, into the hand braced on the stone — and where her palm touched the carved kestrel, the old spiral grooves filled with soft gold light, tracing themselves bright and then fading, like breath on cold glass.

None of them spoke for a moment. Even the uphill stream seemed to pause politely.

"That," Tam said from safely on the valley side, "is a bad idea with *lighting effects*."

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of a toll house, half a mile past the boundary, because Bram refused — on principle and exhaustion both — to sleep with one foot in each world. The toll house had been abandoned long enough that a birch tree was growing through the roof, and long enough that the magic had gotten into the masonry: pale lichen crawled the walls in patterns too regular to be accidental, and when Odile hung her little lantern charm from a beam, moths gathered around it that glowed faintly green themselves, as if taking notes.

Odile cooked. She was a terrible cook, but she cooked anyway, on the theory that a scholar who couldn't feed her colleagues deserved exile on additional grounds. Tonight it was barley porridge with wild onions and, inexplicably, cinnamon, which she guarded like state secrets.

"Eat," she commanded. "Crossing takes it out of you. Magic has a cost, whatever the ballads say. Every charm I light tonight will cost me sleep. Every ward I set costs a little memory. Small prices, usually. But they add up."

"What does it cost the valley?" Bram asked quietly. "To keep it all out?"

Odile paused, spoon halfway to her mouth. "I don't know that anyone's ever asked that question."

"Somebody built the silence. Somebody pays for it."

"Perhaps the valley pays itself. Perhaps it's less a wall than a — a held breath." Odile stirred the pot. "Or perhaps whoever built it is still paying, somewhere, and we've simply never seen the bill." She glanced at Bram, quick and sharp above her cracked spectacles. "You looked at the boundary stone today like you'd seen a ghost."

"I saw a bird."

"A kestrel."

"Don't make it strange, Odile."

"I'm not making it strange. I'm noting that your god is a kestrel, the boundary stone bears a kestrel, and the map that burned a hole in a dying man's hands glows when you touch it. I am a scholar. Noticing is the entire job." She pointed the spoon. "I promised myself I wouldn't push. I am now officially breaking that promise, gently: something is happening to you, Bram Ashgrove, and you know more about it than you're saying."

Bram looked into the fire. Beyond the boundary, fire behaved differently — the flames leaned toward her, subtly, like flowers toward sun, and she decided not to mention that either. The list of things she wasn't mentioning was getting long enough to have chapters.

"When I prayed at the shrine," she said at last, "before all this. After the stranger died. I told you Kestrel didn't answer."

"You told me the god was silent. As it has been for a century."

"It answered." The words came out of her like splinters. "A pulse. Warmth. The same warmth the map gives off. I didn't tell you because—" She stopped. Started again. "Because if the silent god was answering *me*, then either I was mad, or something was beginning, and I wasn't ready for either."

Odile was quiet for a long time. The glowing moths circled. Somewhere in the dark, the uphill stream chuckled to itself over some private joke.

"Thank you," Odile said finally. "For telling me. Late, and incompletely, but telling me." She ladled porridge into a bowl and pressed it into Bram's hands. "Here's what I know, as a scholar. The Order believes dragon-fire is a leash, and gods are the hands that hold it. They may even be right — gods have commanded men before; it's in every archive I ever copied. But there's another reading. The oldest texts I was exiled for defending say the Ember isn't a weapon at all. It's a *covenant*. And covenants require parties." Her eyes, behind the cracked glass, were very steady. "Parties, Bram. On both sides."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning perhaps the silence in the valley isn't absence. Perhaps it's *concentration*. Perhaps the god didn't leave." She rose, brushing off her skirts, and busied herself with the wards — knotting cords along the doorframe, each one catching with its tiny flame. "Sleep. Tomorrow we reach the river, and rivers past the veil have customs. Toll-keepers. Of a sort."

"What kind of customs?"

"The kind," Odile said, "where it matters enormously who does the talking. Goodnight."

* * *

Bram took first watch, because she knew she wouldn't sleep.

The night past the veil was nothing like the valley's nights. The stars here moved — slowly, gracefully, wheeling in patterns no astronomer of the silent lands had ever charted, and among them drifted lights that were not stars at all: small, wandering, curious. One of them descended while she watched and hovered at the edge of the firelight, about the size of a plum, pulsing softly. It regarded her. She regarded it back.

"Don't," she warned it.

It drifted closer.

"I'm armed."

It drifted closer still, and she got the distinct impression — absurd, undeniable — that it was *smelling* her. Or smelling the map. The warmth under her sternum flared, and the little light flared in answer, gold matching gold, and then it bobbed once, almost a bow, and zipped away upslope like a messenger with news.

Bram sat very still, her heart hammering, her old wounds humming their low chord.

*Messenger,* she thought. *News of what? News to whom?*

She thought of the carved kestrel on the boundary stone, wings folded, watching. She thought of the stranger at the ford, his scorched hands, his last breath: *the Ember remembers.* She had assumed he meant the Ember remembered its enemies, its purpose, its age. Lying under moving stars with a coal of warmth beating against her heart, Bram considered, for the first time, a much worse possibility.

Maybe it remembered *her*.

Toward dawn, Tam shook her for second watch and collapsed into his bedroll muttering about helmets. Bram walked the perimeter of the toll house, checking Odile's wards. All the little flames burned steady — except the one nearest the road north, which flickered, bent, and went out.

Not wind. There was no wind.

Bram stood in the greying light and looked north, down the long slope toward the river, where the morning mist lay thick on the water. And in the mist, far off but unmistakable, she saw lights. Lanterns. Many of them, strung out along the far bank in a careful line, like beads on a thread.

Someone was watching the crossing.

Behind her, inside the ruin, Odile's voice floated out, sleep-rough and dry: "Bram? Why have all my wards on the north side just died?"

Bram drew her sword, and the blade — old, honest steel from the silent valley — caught the first light of a sunrise that came up over the veiled world in colors her language didn't have words for.

"Get up, Odile," she called back. "We're about to meet the toll-keeper."
