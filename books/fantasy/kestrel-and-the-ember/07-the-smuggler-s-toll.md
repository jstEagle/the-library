## 7. The Smuggler's Toll

Rennick Cael had been running the Graywater checkpoint for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned three things worth knowing.

First: never trust a man who volunteers information. Second: always count the boats twice. Third — and this was the one he'd carved into the rail of his favorite skiff, half as a joke and half as scripture — *everyone pays the toll.*

He was counting boats for the second time that morning when one of his cousins came pelting down the reed-path with her hat in her hand, which among the Cael clan meant either a wedding or trouble, and nobody on this river was getting married.

"Three of them," she panted. "Coming down the sheep track from the valley side. One's got a soldier's walk. One's got a scholar's boots. And the little one keeps looking over her shoulder like the hills owe her money."

"And?"

"And they're asking after passage east at every farmstead between here and the ford. Paying too much. Which means they're in a hurry, which means somebody's behind them."

Rennick finished his count. Eleven boats where there should have been twelve, because Denny had taken the twone out to check his eel lines without telling anyone, again, and Rennick made a mental note to drown him later, gently, in the family way.

"Well," he said, rolling his sleeves down over the faint green shimmer of his forearms — no sense advertising what he was to strangers, though on the Graywater most folk could smell fae blood like rain coming. "Let's go meet our toll."

* * *

The three of them came around the bend of the sheep track just past midmorning, and Rennick took their measure from beneath the leaning roof of the toll-shed while pretending to mend a net he had no intention of mending.

The tall one first, because she was hard not to look at. Broad-shouldered, sword worn honest at her hip, hair cropped short like she'd cut it herself with a knife and hadn't cared how it turned out. She walked like a woman who'd spent years guarding something and had recently stopped being sure what it was. Mercenary, then, or worse — an honest one.

Behind her, a thin woman in scholar's boots gone soft at the soles, carrying more books than clothes, if the shape of her pack meant anything. She kept touching the strap like it might bite. Nervous. Educated. Dangerous combination.

And last, a girl of maybe seventeen, wiry and watchful, with a bruise of grief under her eyes that hadn't finished setting. She looked over her shoulder exactly as advertised. Rennick had seen that look before. He'd worn it himself for about four years, once.

"Morning," he called out, cheerful as a Sunday bell. "Welcome to the Graywater crossing. Passage east is eight silver a head, ten if you're carrying anything that bites, twelve if it bites me."

The mercenary stopped. Looked at him. Looked at the river, brown and slow and deceptively deep, then back at him.

"We need to cross today," she said.

"Course you do. Everyone who comes here needs to cross today. It's why I charge extra for urgency." He set down the net and spread his hands. "Eight silver. Each. And I'll want to know what's in the packs, because my boats are old and opinionated, and they sink on principle if you insult them with contraband."

The scholar made a small strangled noise. The girl put her hand on her knife.

"We don't have time for this," the mercenary said.

"Nobody does. That's the whole business model." Rennick leaned against the shed post, enjoying himself in the way he only ever enjoyed himself when something interesting was about to go wrong. "Now. You can pay me, or you can try the ford two miles south, which floods this time of year and has eaten three soldiers, one priest, and a very large pig since spring. Or you can go north to the bridge at Harrow's Mill, which is fine, except the Order's got a checkpoint there, and I'd wager my left ear — a good ear, mind, pointed at the tip — that's who you're hurrying away from."

That landed. He watched it land: the mercenary's jaw tightening, the girl going still as a heron, the scholar's hand freezing halfway to her pack.

"I don't know what you're talking about," the mercenary said.

"No," Rennick agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't. Being innocent folk, out for a stroll, with a soldier's walk and a dead man's map sewn into your coat lining."

He hadn't been sure about the map until he said it. But he'd watched her flinch toward her left breast, just slightly, the way people guard things stitched close to the heart, and twenty years of smuggling had taught him that everyone carries their secrets in the same three places, and the heart-lining was the most popular by a wide margin.

The knife came out. Not the mercenary's — the girl's, quick and shaking, held low the way someone holds a blade who's angry enough to use it and new enough to regret it.

"You're with them," the girl said. "You're Order."

"Do I look like Order?" Rennick gestured at himself: the patched rivercoat, the bare feet, the ears he'd given up hiding an hour ago because it was hot and they were his ears. "Look at these. The Hollow Order takes one look at fae blood and starts composing sermons. No, little miss, I'm the opposite of Order. I'm chaos, but punctual."

"Rennick." The mercenary's voice was flat and tired. "What do you want?"

"There's that word. *Want.*" He pushed off the post and strolled closer, unhurried, hands visible, because frightened armed people needed visible hands the way fires needed air. "Here's what I want. I want to see that map. Show it to me, and maybe we talk about passage. Don't show it to me, and you can swim, and I'll wave."

"Why?" The mercenary didn't move. Her eyes were gray and level and missed nothing. "Why does a smuggler care about a map?"

"Because," said Rennick, and found, to his considerable surprise, that his voice had gone quiet without his permission, "I've been waiting eleven years for someone to come down that track carrying it. So show me."

* * *

They didn't show him, of course. They argued about it first, off to the side, in the furious whispering huddle that people always assumed he couldn't hear, forgetting or never knowing that fae ears caught whispers the way nets caught minnows.

*"We can't trust him."*

*"We can't trust anyone. That's rather the point of our current situation."*

*"He knew about the map. How did he know about the map? Bram, how did he—"*

*"I'm thinking."*

*"Thinking faster would be—"* A pause. *"Someone's coming. Downstream. Riders."*

Rennick heard it a heartbeat later, because he'd been listening for it since dawn: hooves on the north road, more than two, moving with the particular disciplined rhythm of men who'd been told to move fast but not to sweat the horses. His cousin Maren appeared at the edge of the reeds making the signal — three fingers, folded twice — that meant *soldiers, coming, hide your grandmother.*

"Well," Rennick said brightly, clapping his hands. "Decision time. My boats are behind that shed. Your choices are: get in one now and argue on the water, or stand here and be very politely murdered by whatever's coming up the road. I should mention my rates double under duress."

The mercenary — Bram, apparently; he filed the name away — stared at him for one long moment. Then she pulled open her coat, tore the stitching at the lining, and drew out a roll of oilskin no bigger than her forearm.

"If you're lying to us," she said, "I'll feed you to your own river."

"Everyone says that. Nobody ever does. Rivers are surprisingly loyal." He took the oilskin, and the moment his fingers closed on it he felt it — a warmth, faint and wrong and wonderful, like holding a bird that had been sleeping near a hearth. His breath went out of him.

He unrolled it with hands that were not quite steady.

The map was scorched at the edges, drawn in ink that shifted color when the light touched it wrong, marked with seals and routes through country that hadn't appeared on any honest chart since before his grandmother's grandmother. And along its western margin, in a hand he knew better than his own — because he'd learned his letters tracing it, sitting on a dock, copying it out letter by letter while his father laughed at him — ran a line of route-notes in cipher.

Cael cipher. Family cipher. The one his father had used to mark safe passages, hidden landings, houses that would open their doors.

His father had died keeping this route. Him and Ma, and Uncle Tam, and the whole branch of the family that had run the eastern leg of it, burned out of their houseboat one winter night by men in gray who asked questions first and apologized never. Rennick had been fourteen. He'd survived by diving into the reeds and staying there until dawn, listening to the fire eat everything, and in the morning he'd fished his mother's ring out of the shallows and sworn off gods, causes, and maps forever.

Eleven years, he'd told them. It had actually been longer. He'd rounded down, the way you do with grief.

"This is the Ember road," he said. His voice came out strange. "This is *my family's* road. We carried for it. Three generations. Before the Order—" He stopped. Started again. "Where did you get this?"

"A dying man gave it to me," Bram said quietly. "At a ford. Men were chasing him. They killed one of my hired hands. I think—" She glanced at the girl beside her, and something passed between them, heavy and unsaid. "I think he was her father."

The girl's chin came up. Defiant, braced for pity, daring him to offer it.

Rennick looked at her — at the dead man's stubborn jaw reproduced faithfully in a younger face — and felt eleven years of careful nothing crack straight down the middle.

"Right," he said. "Boats. Now. Toll's waived."

* * *

They got the last boat — Denny's, as it happened, which served him right — into the water with perhaps thirty seconds to spare. Rennick poled them out into the reed-channels while the riders came clattering up to the empty toll-shed above, and he heard the Order sergeant's voice, clipped and cold, demanding answers from Maren, and Maren giving him the full Cael treatment: helpful, rambling, catastrophically stupid-seeming, and completely useless. By the time the riders figured out the sheep track branched six ways and none of them were marked, the boat was three miles downstream and Rennick was laughing so hard he nearly put them into a snag.

"It's not funny," the girl said.

"It's extremely funny. You'll see. Give it ten years." He wiped his eyes. "Maren once convinced an entire customs patrol that the river changed course seasonally and their maps were out of date. Took them two days to find the bridge. Two *days.*"

The scholar — Odile, she'd finally admitted, in the tone of someone surrendering a hostage — peered at him over her spectacles. "You realize you've just made yourself a target. Helping us. The Order doesn't forgive that."

"The Order burned my family's houseboat when I was fourteen," Rennick said, conversationally, watching the channel ahead. "They asked my father where the road went. He told them it went nowhere. They disagreed with him." He adjusted the pole. "So no, madam archivist, I'm not worried about becoming a target. I've been a target since before some of you were weaned. I simply got tired of swimming."

Silence in the boat, except for water and birdsong. Then Bram, from the bow: "Why help us? Truly. Grief is a reason to hate them. It isn't a reason to follow a stranger's map."

Rennick considered the question honestly, which was rare for him and unpleasant, like biting tinfoil.

"When my da died," he said at last, "he was carrying the same thing you're carrying. A secret, a route, a job nobody thanked him for. And for eleven years I told myself the smart thing was to let it stay dead with him. Stay small. Stay on the river. Take tolls." He shrugged. "But here's the thing about tolls, mercenary. Everybody pays them. Sooner or later, everybody pays. Da paid. Maybe it's my turn to pay something other than silver." He grinned, because the alternative was something wetter. "Also, you're hopeless. All three of you. You'd be dead inside a week east of the Graywater without me. Someone has to keep you alive long enough to spend whatever fool hope you're hauling around."

"That's the nicest insult I've received all year," Odile said.

"I have others. Rates available on request."

The girl — Isa, the dead man's daughter, who had not spoken since the shed — spoke now, without looking at him. "My father talked about the Cael family. Once. When I was small. He said if anything ever happened to him, find the river people." Her voice was carefully, deliberately flat. "I thought he was telling me a story."

"He wasn't," Rennick said. "We were real. We were just mostly dead." He let the pole carry them around a bend, into the wider water where the current took hold and the far shore came green and close. "Your da's cipher's still good, by the way. First three marks on that map — the reed-house, the salt cache, the ferryman at Dunlow — those are ours. Were ours. I can read every line of it."

Isa finally turned and looked at him fully, and what was in her face wasn't gratitude yet, but it was the ground gratitude grows in.

"Then you know where it leads," she said.

"I know where it *starts* leading." Rennick spat over the side, ritually, for luck. "East, past Dunlow, up the salt road, into country where the magic's come back thick enough to chew. Toward Mount Sarn, if the old stories hold." He met Bram's eyes across the boat. "And I know the Order will burn every landing, every friend, every crooked dock between here and there to stop you. Because that's what they do. It's all they do."

"Then we go fast," Bram said.

"We go *smart,*" Rennick corrected. "Fast gets you killed. Smart gets you killed slightly later, ideally somewhere scenic." He settled into the rhythm of the pole, feeling something in his chest that had been boarded shut for eleven years creak open a hand's width, letting in light and river-smell and a purpose he hadn't asked for and couldn't seem to refuse. "Dunlow by nightfall. There's a woman there who owes me a favor and a boat that doesn't ask questions. After that—"

He stopped.

Because ahead, where the channel narrowed toward Dunlow, where there should have been nothing but herons and morning mist, there was smoke. Thin and black and wrong, rising from the reed-house that had stood on that bank for three generations of Caels — the first mark on the map, the first safe door.

Rennick stood very still in the boat, pole dripping, and heard his own voice go quiet and cold, a voice he hadn't used since he was fourteen years old in the reeds.

"They're already on the road," he said. "Somebody's burning my father's marks."
