## 8. Hollow Men

The village of Thistlebrook had one street, two wells, and a church with a bell that hadn't rung in forty years because the rope had rotted and nobody could be bothered to replace it. Serah Dain noted all of this in the way she noted everything: precisely, without affection, and with the quiet certainty that most of the world was held together by things people couldn't be bothered to fix.

She sat her horse at the head of the street and let the silence do the first hour of work for her.

Twelve knights of the Hollow Order behind her, grey cloaks greyer than the drizzle, faces hidden under plain steel helms that reflected nothing you could name. No sigils. The Order wore no sigils. A sigil was a promise, and the Order promised nothing except that fire would die.

"Brother Aldous," she said.

Aldous rode up on her left, a young man with a farmer's shoulders and a scholar's squint, who had once told her over camp stew that he'd joined the Order because it was the only institution in the world that had never lied to him. She had liked him for that. She had also written in his file that he was too fond of explaining himself, which was the same flaw seen from the other end of the telescope.

"You'll take the inn," she said. "Buy a round. Listen."

"Buy a round, Captain?"

"The fastest way to a village's heart is through its tab. They'll tell you everything they know and half of what they don't." She passed him a small purse. "Count what's left when you're done. If they've cheated us, that tells me something too."

"And if they haven't?"

"Then they're honest, poor, and frightened, which is three kinds of useful." She turned her horse toward the well at the street's center, where an old man was drawing water with the slow, deliberate movements of someone pretending very hard not to watch soldiers arrive. "I'll take the elder."

* * *

The old man's name was Hobbet, and he had the particular stillness of a rabbit that has decided the hawk might be a stump if it just doesn't move.

Serah dismounted, walked to the well, and stood beside him while he cranked the bucket up. She didn't speak. The rain ticked against her helm. Somewhere a chicken made an argument and lost.

"Rain's late this year," Hobbet said at last, because farmers could bear any silence except one about weather.

"A week, maybe more," Serah agreed. "Your barley will want it."

"It'll want it whether it comes or not. That's barley." He set the bucket down and finally looked at her — at the blank steel, then away, as if her face were something owed to him and he didn't want to seem greedy. "You'll be wanting to ask me questions, m'lady. I can see it hanging off you like wet wool."

"I'll be wanting answers. The questions are optional."

He almost smiled. Almost. "That's fair."

"Four travelers came through here. Two days ago, perhaps three. A woman built like a soldier, dark-haired, sword worn down at the edge from use rather than show. A scholar — a woman, older, ink-stained, likely complaining about the state of the road. A young woman, angry, probably armed badly. And a man who smiles too much and pays in coins from cities you've never heard of."

Hobbet's hands went still on the well-rope.

"There it is," Serah said quietly. "You know them."

"I know *of* them." He said it fast, the way men said things when they'd already decided to lie and were trying to make the lie load-bearing. "Folk pass through. We're on the salt road spur. Folk pass."

"They stayed at your inn."

"Might have."

"Hired your farrier. Bought oats. The tall one — the smiling one — won eleven coppers off your miller at dice and then bought the whole taproom a round out of the winnings, which is either generosity or strategy, and I'm curious which."

The old man's jaw worked. Rain dripped off the well's little roof between them, patient as a metronome.

"Captain," he said, and his voice dropped into something older and wearier than before, "I've lived in Thistlebrook sixty-one years. My father helped build that bell tower, and my grandfather helped build the one it replaced. In sixty-one years, kings have come and gone, wars have come and mostly gone, and the tax man has come every single year without fail. You know what none of them ever did?" He looked at her directly now, fear and stubbornness fighting openly on his face. "None of them ever asked me to hand four strangers to their deaths. So you'll forgive me if I need a moment to decide how much I like you."

It was, Serah thought, a better answer than most men gave her. Most men groveled immediately, which told her nothing except that they would grovel again tomorrow for whoever came next. This one had spine. It would cost him, but it would cost her less time.

"I'm not going to kill them," she said.

"No, m'lady."

"No. I'm going to stop them." She reached up and, deliberately, slowly, took off her helm.

It was a calculated thing and she knew it. Her face did the work that twelve faceless knights never could: a woman of forty-odd years, brown hair cropped short for the helmet, a scar running through her left eyebrow where a god-touched cultist had opened her forehead to the bone eleven years ago. Tired eyes. Honest eyes, or eyes honest enough. Villages trusted faces. That was exactly why the Order's founders had banned them from the uniform — and exactly why Serah broke the rule whenever the mission required a human being instead of a symbol.

"My name is Serah Dain," she said. "Eleven years ago, in the town of Marrow's Ford, a group of people calling themselves the Kestrel's Hands burned a granary to prove their devotion. Forty-one people died in that fire, most of them children, because the god they served demanded a sign and they were eager to give one. I know because I pulled my sister out of the ash. Or I pulled out what was left of her, which is the part of the story people usually skip."

Hobbet had gone very still.

"The four travelers are carrying something," Serah continued. "A map. It leads to dragon-fire — the last of it, the Ember, the thing our grandmothers' grandmothers sang songs about. And I promise you, on my sister's name, that everyone who touches that fire becomes a servant of it. Not a keeper. A servant. Gods do not share, old man. They command. And the people who carry their commands always, *always* burn something."

The rain filled the pause.

"So no," she said. "I'm not asking you to hand anyone to their deaths. I'm asking you to help me stop a fire before it starts. You've spent your whole life watching weather, Hobbet. Tell me — when you smell smoke on the wind, do you wait to see whose house it means to eat?"

* * *

He gave them the direction within the hour. South along the salt road spur, toward the river crossing at Greywater, moving fast, hiring fresh horses. The farrier confirmed it. The miller, still sour about his eleven coppers, confirmed it twice with embellishments.

Serah stood in the inn's common room while Brother Aldous counted out coin for the oats the company had bought, and listened to the village breathe around its own guilt.

"They'll hate themselves for it," Aldous murmured, coming to stand beside her. "By tonight. Old Hobbet especially."

"Yes."

"Does that trouble you, Captain?"

She considered the question honestly, which was her habit and her burden. "It troubles me that it doesn't trouble me more," she said. "That's the whole trade, Aldous. Every clean thing we do is built on some gray little moment in a taproom. If the day comes when I stop noticing the gray, you have my permission to put a knife in me."

"That's—" He laughed, then saw she wasn't laughing, then stopped. "I'll note it in the ledger, Captain."

"Do that." She pulled her helm back on. "Mount up. We ride for Greywater."

* * *

They made nine miles before the light failed, and camped in a hollow of gorse with the horses picketed close. Serah took first watch herself, as she always did — not because she didn't trust her knights, but because the watch was the only hour of the day that belonged entirely to her, and she guarded it like a miser.

The Order's creed fit inside a sentence, and she carried it the way other women carried lockets: *No flame above the law; no god above the living.*

People thought it was hatred. Let them. Hatred was easy to argue with, easy to pity, easy to dismiss around a warm fire. What Serah Dain felt was not hatred. It was arithmetic.

Every god in history had been a debt taken out against the future. Dragon-fire had sealed the ancient war — yes, fine, the songs were lovely about it — but a seal needed keepers, keepers needed power, power needed worship, worship needed priests, and priests needed someone to burn. Always. Without exception, across six thousand years of recorded covenant, the pattern held: give men a god, and within three generations some of those men would be standing in front of a granary with torches, certain that heaven itself had signed the order.

Her sister had been nineteen. She had liked bad poetry and good cider and a boy named Tam who whistled off-key. She had died because forty-one people needed a sign, and the sign required fuel, and fuel was a word that meant *people*, if you followed the ledgers all the way down.

So: arithmetic. One small fire, extinguished now, forever, versus every future granary in every future century. Even a child could carry that sum.

The hard part — the part she never said aloud, the part that kept her on first watch — was that the sum only balanced if you never looked at the faces. Four travelers. A soldier, a scholar, a girl, a gambler. People. People who laughed at dice and complained about roads and bought rounds for taprooms full of strangers.

She had read the reports. The Ashgrove woman was a mercenary, competent, unaffiliated, no cult record. The Vane woman was a disgraced archivist — dangerous in the way books were dangerous, which was to say not at all until someone acted on them. The Cael man was a smuggler with a dead family and a grudge against the Order, which was fair; the Order had burned his family's route records and gotten his kin killed in the process, and Serah had signed that order, and she remembered signing it, and she kept remembering, because forgetting was how granaries started.

And the girl. Isa Varrow. Daughter of the courier. Seventeen, maybe eighteen, according to the informant in Vell. Her father had died holding a map he barely understood, killed — the evidence suggested — by Order outriders acting without warrant.

Without warrant. Serah had hanged two of those outriders herself in the spring. It hadn't brought anyone back. It hadn't even made her feel clean. It had just been the ledger balancing, a little, in the wrong column.

*We are becoming the thing we hunt,* whispered a voice in her that sounded unpleasantly like her sister, who had argued theology with her right up until the end, and usually won.

*No,* Serah answered it, as she always answered. *We are the ones who hang the arsonists. The difference matters.*

The voice, being dead, declined to reply, which she chose to count as a victory.

* * *

Near midnight, Brother Cassian woke her for second watch with news.

"Rider came through the hollow an hour back, Captain. Didn't stop. But the ostler at Thistlebrook — the one we leaned on — sent his boy after us with a message." Cassian handed over a scrap of paper, rain-spotted, the writing hurried. "Seems there's a fifth traveler. A girl. Alone. Armed. Asking after the same four, riding hard, and telling anyone who'll listen that she means to kill them."

Serah read the note twice. *Girl on a bay mare, red scarf, sword too big for her. Says the tall one owes her a death. Heading south past the standing stones.*

"A daughter?" Cassian guessed. "Of the courier?"

"Likely." Serah fed the note to the campfire and watched it curl. A grief-mad girl with a too-big sword, chasing four armed adults across open country. The Order's own path ran straight toward Greywater, and the fugitives were between them, and now somewhere in that gap was a child carrying a blade and a grave's worth of anger.

"She'll get herself killed," Cassian said. "Or worse — she'll reach them first, they'll take her in, and then we're cutting through five of them, and the story writes itself. The Order slaughters a mourning orphan. Every village from here to the coast will hear it."

"I'm aware."

"What are your orders, Captain?"

Serah looked at the fire. Sparks climbed and guttered, each one briefly bright and then nothing, which was the correct behavior for sparks and the incorrect behavior for gods.

"We ride tonight," she said. "Hard. We reach Greywater before they do, take the crossing, and let them come to us — on ground we choose, with terms we set." She stood, rolling her shoulders against the cold. "And if the girl reaches them first..."

"Captain?"

"Then we find out what kind of people they are by what they do with her." She pulled her cloak tight. "Either they turn her away, and they're cowards. Or they use her, and they're what I say they are. Or—"

She stopped. The third option sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: *or they take her in, feed her, teach her to hold the sword properly, and become the kind of thing that makes villages lie to my face and makes my own knights hesitate.* A found family. She'd read the phrase in a confiscated letter once, in a cultist's diary, and burned the diary, and never quite burned the phrase.

"Or?" Cassian prompted.

"Or nothing. Mount up, Brother. Greywater by dawn."

The column moved out under a sky rinsed with stars, twelve riders and one woman doing arithmetic in the dark, each of them certain, each of them following a captain who was certainest of all — and somewhere south, past the standing stones, a girl with a red scarf and her father's map-shaped hole in her life was already riding toward the answer none of them had bargained for.
