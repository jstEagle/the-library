## 9. The Daughter of the Dead Man

Isa Varrow had been following them for six days, and in that time she had learned three things about her father's killers.

First: they were terrible at covering their tracks. The tall one — the mercenary, Ashgrove, the one whose name was scrawled across the last letter he'd ever sent home — walked like a woman who had never once worried about being followed. Isa could have tracked her blindfolded by the sound of her boots alone.

Second: they argued constantly. About salt. About whether the scholar's charms actually worked or just smelled expensive. About who had eaten the last of the dried plums, which was, apparently, a matter of national importance to the half-fae one.

Third: they did not look like murderers.

That third thing was the problem. Isa had spent six days rehearsing what she would say when she finally stood in front of them, and every rehearsal required murderers. She had imagined their faces going pale with guilt. She had imagined the map pressed back into her hands, still warm from her father's grip. She had not imagined the half-fae singing badly to a mule, or the scholar tripping over her own satchel straps, or Ashgrove stopping on the road to move a beetle off the stones so nobody stepped on it.

*Beetles,* she thought bitterly. *She moves beetles. And my father is dead.*

The road climbed out of the river valley here, narrowing between two walls of grey rock furred with moss. A good place for an ambush. Isa had chosen it two days ago and had spent the intervening time telling herself that the tightness in her chest was anticipation rather than dread.

She checked her knife for the fourth time. It was her father's knife, the one he'd used for everything from skinning hares to opening letters, the handle worn smooth as river glass. It had never been a fighting knife. Neither, if she was honest, had she ever really been a fighter. She'd shot rabbits. She'd once thrown a pot at a man who insulted her mother's weaving. That was the full inventory of her violence.

The plan was simple. Simple plans were best, her father always said, because simple plans survived contact with reality. Step one: step out of the rocks with the knife drawn. Step two: demand the map. Step three: —

Step three had never quite crystallized. In her head it involved them handing it over, weeping, and then presumably she walked away and something in her chest unclenched for the first time since the Order's rider had come up the lane with his hat in his hands and his pity arranged neatly on his face.

Below, voices drifted up through the trees. They were coming.

Isa pressed herself against the cold stone, breathed the smell of moss and her own fear-sweat, and waited.

* * *

They came around the bend in their usual disorder: Ashgrove in front, moving with that unhurried soldier's stride; the scholar Odile behind her, reading while walking, which explained the tripping; the half-fae Rennick at the rear leading the mule and, yes, singing. Something about a girl from the coast and a boat with no oars. He was rhyming "tide" with "inside," which was a crime in itself.

Isa stepped out onto the road, knife raised, and said, in a voice she had practiced until it didn't shake:

"Nobody moves."

Everyone moved.

The mule shied sideways. Rennick grabbed for its halter and missed. Odile looked up from her book, saw the knife, and — this was the part Isa would replay for weeks afterward — simply closed the book, marked her place with one finger, and said, "Oh, good, you're real. I thought Rennick was imagining you."

"I wasn't imagining anyone," Rennick said.

"You've been humming about 'the shadow on our trail' for two days."

"That's called foreshadowing. It's a professional habit."

"Ashgrove," Odile said mildly, "there's a young woman pointing a knife at us."

"I see her." Bram Ashgrove had stopped walking. She hadn't drawn a weapon. Her hands hung loose at her sides, and she was looking at Isa — not at the knife, at Isa — with an expression Isa couldn't read and hated herself for wanting to read. "You've been behind us since the ford."

"I have not—"

"The willow thicket, the second night. You sneezed."

Heat crawled up Isa's neck. "I did not sneeze."

"It was very small," Rennick offered helpfully. "A polite sneeze. Very considerate, given the circumstances."

"*Nobody moves,*" Isa repeated, louder, because the scene was escaping her the way water escapes a cupped hand. "You're going to give me the map. The one my father carried. The one he died carrying."

Something changed in Ashgrove's face then. Not guilt exactly. Something older and wearier than guilt, like a door opening onto a room where someone had been sitting alone for a long time.

"You're his daughter," Ashgrove said quietly. "You have his eyes."

"Don't." The word came out cracked down the middle. "Don't talk about his eyes. You don't get to talk about him. You were there. You held him while—" Her voice betrayed her entirely. She steadied the knife hand by gripping it with the other hand, which made her stance ridiculous, and she knew it, and knowing it made her angrier. "He wrote to me. From the road. He said he'd found something important, something worth dying for, and I thought — I thought that was just how he talked. He talked big. He was a cartographer, not a — he drew maps of *harbors.*"

"He drew more than harbors," Odile said softly.

"And then he died on a stranger instead of coming home!" Isa's voice broke loose from her control altogether, galloping. "And you — you took it. You took the last thing he touched and you just *walked away with it*, and now you're — what, sightseeing? Singing songs? Moving *beetles* off the road?"

Ashgrove blinked. "How do you know about the beetle?"

"Rennick told me," Isa snapped, and then realized what she'd said, and Rennick's eyebrows went up in delighted realization at the same moment hers did.

"She's been close enough to hear camp talk," Rennick said. "For days. Odile, our assassin has been eavesdropping on the plum dispute."

"There were no plums left to dispute," Odile said. "Someone ate them."

"I *restocked* them—"

"Enough." Ashgrove hadn't raised her voice. She almost never did; Isa had learned that much from listening in the dark. But the word landed on the road like a dropped stone, and even the mule went still. Ashgrove took one slow step forward. Isa's knife arm trembled and she hated it. "Girl. Isa Varrow. If I wanted the map gone, or wanted you gone, you'd never have seen us. Six days is a long time to let a knife walk behind you."

"Then why didn't you stop me?"

"Because I wanted to know what you'd do when you got here." Ashgrove stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Isa could see the burn scar crawling up under her jaw, shiny and new. "And because you deserve to hear it from someone who was there. So put the knife down, or don't. Either way, I'm going to tell you what happened at the ford, and you're going to listen, and then you can decide what kind of enemy I am."

Isa's whole body wanted to run or stab, and both impulses canceled each other out into a horrible trembling stillness. "Talk, then."

* * *

Ashgrove told it plainly, without decoration, which somehow made it worse. The camp at the ford. The man stumbling out of the reeds with three arrows already in him, holding his side like a man bailing a sinking boat. How he'd refused to lie down. How he'd pressed a scorched leather tube into Bram's hands and said words Isa had heard secondhand from a ferryman and had refused to believe: *the Ember remembers.*

"He said that?" Isa whispered.

"He said that. Then he said—" Ashgrove paused, and Isa watched her decide whether to soften it, and watched her choose not to. "He said, 'Tell Isa the tide comes in.' Then he died."

The tide comes in. Their private nonsense. When Isa was seven she'd cried because her father was leaving again, and he'd crouched down and told her that families were like tides — they went out, but they always came back in. She'd rolled her eyes at it for fifteen years. She'd rolled her eyes at it in his last letter, written in the margin: *Tide comes in, little fish. Wait for me.*

She sat down. Not gracefully — her knees simply resigned — and found herself on the roadside moss with the knife still stupidly in her hand, looking at nothing.

"Three arrows," she said. "Who shot him?"

"Hollow Order riders. Grey cloaks, blank shields. They weren't trying to capture him."

"They wanted him dead before he talked."

"Yes."

"So it wasn't—" Isa looked up, and the question came out smaller than any question she'd ever asked. "It wasn't you. You didn't kill him."

"No," Ashgrove said. "One of theirs killed one of mine, same volley. His name was Doss. He owed me money and he cheated at cards and I'd have followed him anywhere." She said it without drama, and it landed harder than drama would have. "We're both grieving, Isa. We're just doing it on the same side of the field, whether you like it or not."

Silence. The mule shifted. Somewhere above them a wood pigeon made its idiot cooing sound, indifferent to the rearrangement of Isa's entire world.

"But you kept the map," she said at last. "His map. You didn't send it to me. You didn't come find me. You just — took it and went."

"We tried to give it away," Rennick said. "To the magistrate at Thornhollow. It went poorly."

"Poorly meaning?"

"The magistrate was Order," Odile said. "She burned Bram's arm to the bone and we left through a window. Since then, delivering the map to anyone wearing an official face has seemed inadvisable."

"Your father trusted no one with it," Ashgrove said. "That's why he ran instead of riding to some lord's gate. Whatever he found, he decided it was too dangerous to hand to power. He chose a stranger with a sword and a reputation for keeping contracts." She crouched down then, slowly, to Isa's level, forearms on her knees. "I didn't know he had a daughter. If I had, things might have gone differently. But I'll tell you what I won't do. I won't hand a thing like this to a girl with a skinning knife and six days of rage, to carry home alone through country full of people who shoot messengers. Because they'd find you. And they wouldn't ask questions first. I know because I've met them."

"You don't know anything about me."

"I know you tracked four armed people for six days without being caught except by accident, on foot, alone, in unfamiliar country." Ashgrove's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "I know plenty."

"She also knows your sneeze," Rennick added.

"*Rennick.*"

"What? It's relevant. It establishes a pattern of competence punctuated by vulnerability. Very humanizing."

Isa laughed. It burst out of her before she could stop it — a wet, ugly, half-sob of a laugh — and she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes and hated everyone on this road, including especially herself, for making grief and laughter share a mouth.

Odile crouched beside her, skirts pooling, and did something Isa didn't expect: she waited. Just waited, hands folded, until Isa lowered her hand.

"May I show you something?" the scholar asked. "It won't take long, and then you can go back to pointing the knife at us, if you still want to."

Isa nodded, suspicious.

From her satchel Odile produced a slim bundle of papers, travel-worn and water-stained, tied with string. "Your father corresponded with me. Did he mention that?"

Isa stared. "No."

"He wouldn't. My name is poison in certain rooms. I was cast out of the Archives at Corvess for publishing a paper arguing that dragons survived past the Purge. Your father read it and wrote to me — twice a year, for eleven years. Field observations. Sightings. Measurements of old burn-scars in bedrock that shouldn't exist if the histories are true." She untied the string with careful fingers. "He was gathering proof, Isa. Not treasure. Proof. And whatever he found at the end — whatever the Ember actually is — he found it because he spent eleven years being laughed at and kept looking anyway."

She spread the letters out on the moss between them. Isa knew the handwriting the way she knew her own heartbeat — the cramped leftward slant, the way he crossed his sevens. There were dozens of them. Years of them. Her father, alive on paper, talking to a stranger about dragons, and never once mentioning it at the supper table.

"Why not tell me?" Her voice was barely there. "Why hide it from *me*?"

"Because you'd have worried," Odile said simply. "And because people we love are the first ones we protect from our own obsessions. He didn't want you to carry this weight. He wanted you to have supper and a mother's laugh and a life with no burn scars in it." She glanced up at Ashgrove. "In that, I suspect, he and this woman understand each other."

Ashgrove said nothing, but her jaw tightened, and Isa — watching her now, really watching — thought: *she carries it anyway. All of it. She just doesn't set it down where anyone can see.*

* * *

They made camp early that evening, none of them suggesting it aloud — it simply happened, the way weather happens. Rennick built a fire in a hollow screened by brush. Odile produced tea things from somewhere in her impossible satchel, including, inexplicably, a second cup, which she set at the fourth place without comment.

Isa sat apart at first. An arm's length, then less. She told herself she was staying to watch them, to catch the lie when it showed. But the lie didn't show. What showed was Rennick burning the tea and blaming the mule; Odile correcting his card-shuffling technique unprompted and receiving a look of profound betrayal; Ashgrove sharpening her blade by firelight and, when she thought no one was looking, touching the scorched map tube at her belt the way other people touch relics.

"Tide comes in," Isa said quietly, to the fire.

Ashgrove looked over. "Little fish?"

Isa's throat closed. She managed a nod.

"He said it like a man spending his last coin," Ashgrove said. "Like it was the most important thing he owned. I didn't know what it meant. I've been carrying it around for weeks wondering if I'd failed some instruction."

"You didn't fail anything." Isa wiped her face briskly, angrily. "It wasn't an instruction. It was just — him saying goodbye in code, because he was a sentimental idiot who couldn't say plain things." She pulled her father's knife from her belt and looked at it, at the smooth worn handle, and then — before she could think better of it — held it out handle-first toward Ashgrove.

Ashgrove didn't take it. "What's this?"

"You should have it. You were there. I wasn't."

"No." Ashgrove pushed it gently back. "He gave that to you. I got the last thing he *said*. That's more than most people get." She hesitated, then added, gruffly, as if the words cost toll-fees: "Keep the knife. Walk with us as far as the next town, at least. After that, if you still want the map and want to go home, I'll — we'll figure something out. Nobody hands you to the Order. That I promise."

"I'm not promising not to stab anyone," Isa said.

"Noted," said Rennick. "I'll sleep facing away, it's more sporting."

"Sleep facing the mule," Odile advised. "She's the only one of us who's innocent."

The fire settled. Above the black shoulders of the hills, stars came out in the fierce, uncomplicated way they do far from cities, and Isa sat among strangers who were not strangers anymore — her father's correspondents, her father's witnesses, the last people on earth who had heard his voice — and felt the rage in her chest do something terrifying.

It didn't leave. But it made room.

It was Rennick, later, dousing the fire, who said it lightly, not looking at her, giving her every escape: "There's a market city three days east. Vell. If you're walking with us that far, fair warning — Odile haggles, Ashgrove tips too much, and I win at cards."

"You cheat at cards," Odile said.

"I *win* at cards. The mechanism is my business."

Isa pulled her cloak tighter and looked east, toward the dark line of the hills, where somewhere ahead lay a map, an Ember, and the people who had made her father die running.

"Three days," she said. "Then we'll see."

But she was already unpacking her bedroll among theirs, and everyone pretended not to notice, and that, she would think much later, was the moment it began — not with a promise, not with a vow, but with a bedroll laid down in the dark among people who moved the beetle aside, and let her keep her knife, and saved her a cup.
