## 10. Salt Road

The salt flats began where the river forgot itself.

Bram had seen them once as a child, from the temple tower — a white glare on the horizon like a second moon fallen and shattered. Up close they were worse and better than she'd imagined: a crust of crystal stretching to every edge of the world, cracked into hexagons the size of dinner tables, so bright by day that the company traveled at dawn and dusk and slept through the killing noon under awnings Rennick rigged from his boat's old sail.

"Three days across," he said, spitting thoughtfully over the side of the cart. "Four if Isa keeps asking how much farther."

"I've asked twice."

"Twice an hour is still a pattern, little Varrow."

Isa made a gesture at him that her father would have scolded her for, and Bram turned her face away so neither of them would see her smile.

The cart was Rennick's doing — he'd traded a ring he swore he'd won honestly for it in the last river town, along with two mules named Debt and Interest, because, he explained, "one of them's always behind and the other one's always costing me." Odile rode in back among the crates with her books wrapped in oilcloth, reading even as the cart jolted, occasionally calling out corrections to Rennick's geography that he ignored with the serenity of a man who had never once been lost, only creatively redirected.

Bram walked. She always walked when there was walking to be done. It gave her time to think, and thinking was a thing she'd been doing too much of since the ford — since a dying man's hand had pressed a scorched map into hers and a god who hadn't spoken in three hundred years had, she was almost sure, almost, said her name.

The salt crunched under her boots like old snow. Ahead, the world wavered white and endless, and heat ghosts danced on the horizon like things waiting to be met.

Three days, she told herself. Just three days of nothing but light and salt and the sound of her companions arguing about card rules.

She could almost pretend that was the whole journey.

* * *

Rennick Cael had a system.

It was not cheating, exactly. Cheating implied dishonesty, and Rennick was scrupulously honest about the fact that he would take advantage of anyone foolish enough to sit down across from him. What he did was more of a philosophy. A way of life. His mother had taught him the pass-and-palm before she'd taught him letters, and his grandmother — half-fae herself, a woman with river-green eyes and no patience whatsoever for losing — had refined it into art.

The problem was Odile Vane.

"Your left sleeve," she said, without looking up from her book, "is heavier than your right."

The campfire crackled. Night on the flats was cold enough to see breath, and the four of them sat close around the flames, the salt glittering beyond the firelight like a field of dead stars. Rennick froze mid-shuffle, cards fanned in his long fingers, his expression settling into wounded dignity.

"My left sleeve," he said, "contains my arm."

"There are five aces in that deck. I counted the box this morning."

"That deck came pre-loaded. I bought it off a one-eyed woman in Vell who swore on her mother's grave—"

"You bought a marked deck to cheat us with and you're blaming the manufacturer."

"I'm establishing provenance." He gathered the cards, shuffled again — and this time Bram, watching closely, saw the little ripple of his fingers, the card that slid home like a fish returning to deep water. "Besides, what would I even win? We're splitting everything anyway. Isa has six coppers and a hair ribbon. You have books. Bram has—" he gestured at her, "—a sword and a personality."

"Aces high or low?" Isa asked, dealing herself in before anyone agreed to anything.

"High," said Rennick.

"Low," said Odile, turning a page.

Bram laughed. It startled her, the sound of it — rough from disuse, like a door opening in a house long shut. The other three looked at her, and she cleared her throat and picked up her cards and pretended it hadn't happened.

But Rennick was smiling at her over his hand, that crooked half-fae smile, and he dealt her three good cards on purpose, and if anyone noticed, nobody said.

Later, when Isa had lost her sixth copper and accused the entire concept of mathematics of conspiracy, and Odile had won the hair ribbon in a hand she played with terrifying quiet competence, Rennick sat back against the cart wheel and looked out at the dark.

"My mother used to play this game," he said, conversationally, to the fire. "On the boat. Between runs. She'd let me win when I was small, and then one day she stopped letting me win, and I cried, and she said—" his voice did something complicated, "—she said, 'The world will never let you win, Rennick. Better you learn it from someone who loves you.'"

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere out on the flats, wind moved over the salt with a sound like distant applause.

"So now you know why I cheat," he added briskly. "Sentimental reasons. Deal again, Varrow, and this time stop looking at my hands, look at my eyes. That's where the real trick lives."

* * *

Isa Varrow had decided, somewhere around the second night, that she was going to kill Bram Ashgrove.

Not permanently. But the woman had woken her before dawn with a boot toe in the ribs and the words "up, blade-work," delivered in that flat valley voice, and Isa had been raised better than to argue with someone holding two practice staves.

"Why," she said, standing in the gray pre-light with frost on her eyelashes, "are we doing this."

"Because you fight angry."

"I fight fine."

"You fight like a girl whose father taught her everything except how to lose." Bram tossed her a stave. Isa caught it on reflex, which she suspected was the point. "Anger is fuel. Fine. But it burns fast and leaves nothing, and the first man who figures that out will wait you out and open your throat while you're panting. So. Again."

They drilled. Isa had learned knife-work from her father in kitchens and alleyways, quick and dirty and meant to end things; Bram's style was older, patient, built for people wearing armor and meaning it. Every correction landed like a small insult. Drop your shoulder. No — your *other* shoulder. You're watching my blade. Watch my hips. Blades lie; bodies don't.

By the third morning Isa's arms shook and her pride was bleeding out quietly somewhere in the salt, and Bram finally stepped back and nodded.

"Better."

"Say the whole sentence."

A pause. Then, grudgingly, like a coin pried from a fist: "You're getting better. Your father would have—" She stopped. Started again. "He fought the same way. Angry. I saw him hold the ford for nearly a minute against men who should have killed him in ten seconds."

Isa went still. They didn't talk about the ford. None of them did; it hung between all four of them like a lamp nobody wanted to light.

"He held it," Bram said, "long enough for me to reach him. That wasn't anger. That was love with a blade in it. Anger just looks like it."

Isa looked down at the stave in her hands, at her own knuckles, white around the wood. When she spoke, her voice came out smaller than she intended. "I keep dreaming about the arrows. Not hitting him. Just — coming. And me running, always running, and never arriving."

"I know."

"How could you possibly know."

"Because I dream about the same ford," Bram said, "and in mine, I arrive late." She turned away, ending it, lifting her stave. "Again. Guard's high. Come at me like you mean it."

And Isa did — and found, to her fury and something adjacent to gratitude, that she was starting to mean it differently than she used to.

* * *

Odile Vane kept a list.

It lived in the back of her third-best notebook, and it was titled, in her precise archivist's hand, *Things I Am Not Paid Enough to Catalogue*, and its entries ran thus:

*Item one:* Rennick Cael sleeps with one eye open. Confirmed twice. Unsettling both times.

*Item two:* Isa Varrow has begun humming while she sharpens her knives. The song appears to be one Rennick taught her. Contamination of morale proceeding normally.

*Item three:* Bram Ashgrove prays every night, silently, facing east. On the second night the map, wrapped in my satchel beside me, was warm to the touch. I did not mention it. I am not certain why. I am less certain why she did not mention it either.

She closed the notebook and watched the fire.

She had spent eleven years telling herself that exile was a professional condition, not a personal wound — that she had lost a career, not a family, and that scholars needed families the way fish needed bicycles. Eleven years of cellars and borrowed names and talking to books because books were the only conversation that never ended in *get out*.

And now here she was on a salt flat with a mercenary who prayed to a silent god, a smuggler who cheated at cards to feel close to dead people, and a nineteen-year-old orphan who hummed when she was happy and didn't seem to know she was doing it — and Odile had caught herself, twice now, laughing. Out loud. At things that were not footnotes.

It was undignified. It was, she suspected, exactly what her colleagues at the Athenaeum had always predicted she'd come to.

"Vane." Rennick, from across the fire, tossing something. She caught it: a strip of dried fruit, the good kind, the kind he claimed they were out of. "You're making your thinking face. Whatever you've deduced about my sleeves, I deny everything."

"I've deduced that you're hoarding fruit."

"Prove it in court."

"The court is me, and I find you guilty. Sentence is the rest of the bag."

He grinned and, to her genuine surprise, produced the bag from inside his coat and threw it over. "For services rendered," he said. "You're the only reason we haven't walked into a sinkhole yet. Bram navigates by stubbornness and Isa by grudges. Someone has to read the actual ground."

Odile ate her contraband and said nothing, and wrote nothing, and let the moment go uncatalogued — though later, alone with the notebook, she added a final line:

*Item four:* I believe I understand, now, why people do this. The warmth thing. It is inefficient and it will end badly and I find I no longer care. Note for future review: this is either growth or a symptom.

* * *

On the third evening, the far edge of the flats rose out of the haze — a dark line of stone, and beyond it, faint as a memory, the green smudge of the hill country where the drowned chapel waited.

They made camp early, in the lee of a salt ridge, and for once nobody cheated anybody at anything. Rennick told a story about a customs official in Vell with a memorable hat, and Isa laughed until she wheezed, and Odile corrected three details of the story and improved a fourth, and Bram sat with her back against the cart wheel and let the noise wash over her like weather.

This, she thought. This is the thing I never had room for in the valley. Not peace — peace I had, plenty of it, acres of it, silence stacked to the rafters. This other thing. People who argue about card rules and save you the good dried fruit and notice when you don't sleep.

Kestrel, she thought, into the silence where a god used to be. Are you seeing this?

The map, in her satchel, stayed cool. Quiet. She tried not to feel disappointed, and failed, and was grateful no one could see her face in the firelight.

It was Isa who spotted it first — she had the sharpest eyes of any of them, a gift of her father's she was only beginning to trust. She stood, shading her eyes toward the eastern dark, toward the hills.

"Bram. There's a light out there."

"Campfire? Herders?"

"It's moving." Isa's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a girl who had learned what lights on roads could mean. "Against the slope. Toward the chapel road."

Rennick was already on his feet, the humor gone out of him like a tide pulling back. Odile closed her notebook. Bram rose slowly, hand finding her sword hilt without her deciding to reach for it, and looked east into the dark where a single light crawled along the flank of the hills — steady, unhurried, deliberate.

Heading, she realized, for the drowned chapel.

Heading for the first seal.

"Well," said Rennick softly, into the silence. "So much for our head start."
