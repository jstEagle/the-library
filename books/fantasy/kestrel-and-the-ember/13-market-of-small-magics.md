## 13. Market of Small Magics

Isa Varrow had stolen her first purse at nine years old, from a man so drunk he thanked her for the trouble. She had lied to priests, customs officers, and one memorable duchess who'd wanted to know if Isa was her husband's by-blow (Isa had said yes, and eaten like a queen for a week). She was, in short, not an innocent.

So it annoyed her profoundly that walking into Vell felt like being nine again — everything enormous, everything glittering, everything promising something for nothing.

The free city did not have walls so much as suggestions of walls, half-tumbled ramparts overgrown with wisteria that bloomed out of season because, Odile explained, a hedge-witch had won a bet with it two hundred years ago and the wisteria was still honoring the terms. Beyond them sprawled the Market of Small Magics: three streets' worth of stalls under awnings dyed every color that had ever been invented, selling charms against toothache, love, rain, honesty, and — on one hand-lettered sign — *grandmother's ghost, gently used, knows all family recipes*.

"Is that legal?" Bram asked, staring at the ghost stall.

"Nothing in Vell is illegal," Rennick said. "That's the whole point of it. There are only things that get you stabbed afterward."

"That's reassuring."

"It's Vell," Rennick said, with the fondness other men reserved for their mothers. "It's the last honest city in the world, because everyone here admits what they are."

Isa hung back as they entered the crowd, and did what she always did in crowds: counted exits, marked purses, noted who watched whom. Old habits. Her father had taught her them on market days in towns much smaller than this, his hand heavy and warm on her shoulder. *Watch the watchers, little bird. Everyone in a market wants something. Your job is knowing what.*

Her father was eleven weeks dead, and she still caught herself turning to tell him things.

"Stay close," Bram said, low, without looking back. She had a way of speaking that made orders sound like weather reports. "Rennick knows a factor who can move us through the city quiet. We resupply, we rest one night, we're gone before the Order's informants finish their breakfasts."

"And if I don't stay close?" Isa asked, mostly to be contrary.

Bram glanced at her then, and there was nothing soft in her face, but there was nothing unkind either. It was a face built for long silences and sudden decisions. "Then you'll find your own way," she said. "You've managed worse alone. But you'd miss Odile's snoring."

"I do not snore," said Odile, scandalized, already drifting toward a bookstall like a compass needle finding north.

"You snore like a landslide arguing with itself."

They bickered their way into the crowd, and Isa let herself be carried along in their wake, and told herself she was only staying close because the pickpockets here were probably excellent and she wanted to study technique.

That was the story she was telling herself. She would keep telling it for most of the day.

* * *

The factor's name was Mother Quill, which was not her name, and her warehouse smelled of pepper, tallow, and secrets. Rennick handled the negotiation while the others waited outside, and Isa heard enough through the door to gather that he was trading favors rather than coin — *you'll owe me a Tuesday, Quill, and you know my Tuesdays are worth more than your Thursdays* — in a language of debts that seemed to be half the smuggler's actual soul.

Which left Isa, Bram, and Odile with two hours and money that needed spending, in a market that sold everything except, apparently, peace of mind.

Odile vanished immediately. One moment she was beside them; the next she had been absorbed by a stall of bottled memories, and when Isa spotted her again she was holding a little green vial up to the light with an expression of religious anguish.

"What is it?" Bram asked.

"*The Siege of Harrowgate, firsthand,*" Odile read, voice cracking. "Recorded by a common soldier. Do you know how rare that is? Histories are written by officers. By victors. This is a man who was cold and frightened and *there*, and someone caught him in glass—"

"How much?" Isa asked.

"Four hundred crowns." Odile set the vial down with the gentleness of a woman laying flowers on a grave. "I once earned four hundred crowns in a year. Before." She didn't say *before the disgrace*. She never did. "Come along. If I look at it longer I'll start calculating what a kidney fetches."

They moved deeper into the market. Isa kept her hands visible and her eyes busy. Charm-sellers called out from both sides — *protection from lies, lady? protection from truth?* — and a relic-thief with honest eyes showed them a sword he swore had killed a god, until Bram picked it up, sighted along the blade, and said, "This was forged within the last ten years, in Kettering, by a left-handed smith who rushed the tang," and handed it back. The thief had the grace to look delighted rather than offended.

"You could work here," he told her.

"I have a job," Bram said.

"It pays badly and everyone wants to kill you," Isa said.

"Yes," Bram agreed. "But I'm good at it."

It was almost easy, walking beside her. That was the thing Isa kept forgetting and kept having to re-remember: it was almost easy. Bram Ashgrove did not fill silences or demand conversation. She walked like a wall that had decided, provisionally, to be on your side. Her father would have liked her, Isa thought, and then hated herself for thinking it, because liking her was dangerous. Liking any of them was dangerous. They were a means to an end. The end was the people who had killed her father, and she would burn the whole company for that end if she had to.

She repeated this to herself the way Odile repeated prayers: often, and without much conviction.

They stopped at a food stall because Bram had the unglamorous wisdom of all soldiers about meals, and Isa ate spiced eel off a skewer and pretended not to enjoy it, and watched Bram pay for three portions when there were only two of them, because Rennick would materialize eventually and be insufferable about being remembered.

"Your father," Bram said suddenly, around a mouthful of eel. "What did he do? Before the map."

The question landed like a dropped plate. Isa made herself chew, swallow, answer lightly. "He was a courier. Legitimate, mostly. He carried things people wanted carried quietly."

"And you?"

"I was his daughter. I carried things too." She shrugged. "Small things. Notes. Sometimes heavier notes."

Bram nodded slowly, absorbing this, and Isa realized with a small shock that Bram wasn't interrogating her. She was just — asking. Making conversation. The woman had the social instincts of a siege engine but apparently she tried.

"He taught me markets," Isa heard herself say, before she'd decided to say anything. "How to read them. He said a market is just a town with its sleeves rolled up. You can tell everything about a place by what its people will pay for."

"What do you think Vell will pay for?" Bram asked.

Isa looked out over the awnings, the glitter, the smoke of a hundred frying pans, the charm-sellers and relic-thieves and the gently used grandmother's ghost.

"Hope," she said. "Cheap hope, by the dram. And underneath it—" she nodded toward a shadowed arcade where men with soft boots were watching the crowd with the patient attention of cats at a mousehole, "—information. Someone's already been asking about us. See the one in the gray coat? He's looked at Bram twice and at me three times, and he doesn't know either of us, which means someone described us to him."

Bram didn't turn her head. "Rennick's contact should have swept the approaches."

"Maybe. Or maybe sweeping isn't what Rennick paid for." Isa finished her skewer. "Don't glare at him yet. Could be nothing. Markets are full of gray coats."

But she filed the gray coat away, because her father had also taught her that *could be nothing* was the most expensive phrase in any language.

* * *

It happened in the third hour, in the lane they called the Reliquary Row, where the relic-thieves worked their trade behind curtains of dusty velvet.

Isa had drifted from the others — deliberately, she'd tell herself later, though some honest corner of her knew it was drift, plain drift, the pull of a current she'd been fighting since the ford. And there, in a booth hung with saints' fingers that were obviously pigs' toes, she saw it.

A buyer. Not a stall-keeper — a buyer. He sat behind the velvet with the stillness of a man whose money spent faster than his mouth, and on the table before him, laid out on black cloth, was a collection that made Isa's professional heart stutter: a saint's ring that wept real tears, a key that opened any lock once, a mirror that showed the face of whoever you missed most.

And a letter of credit. From a Vell banking house. For a sum with more zeroes than Isa had ever seen outside of a debt collector's nightmare.

The buyer was purchasing. Anything strange. Anything true. Especially, said the chalked notice at the booth's edge, *maps, seals, and documents of the old wars*.

Isa stood in front of that notice for a long time.

Here was the arithmetic, and it was simple, and simple arithmetic was the most seductive kind. The map sewn into her jacket lining — her father's map, scorched at the corner where his blood had dried into it — was worth more than this entire street. More than this city. A buyer like this one would pay a fortune and ask no questions, and with a fortune Isa could go anywhere. South, past the salt flats, to the island cities where names could be changed for the price of a boat ticket. She could stop sleeping in ditches. Stop flinching at arrows. Stop watching Bram Ashgrove walk point and feeling something in her chest that was dangerously close to trust.

Her father was dead. The map hadn't saved him. What use was a dead man's errand?

*Little bird,* her father's voice said in her memory, amused. *You're haggling with yourself. Bad sign. Never buy when you're haggling with yourself.*

"Shut up," Isa murmured.

"Everything to your taste?" The buyer had noticed her. Of course he had; noticing was his trade. He was perhaps fifty, soft-handed, with the courteous blankness of a man who had bought many things he preferred not to think about. "You have the look of someone carrying something interesting."

"Do I?"

"Mm. Grief does. It sits in the shoulders." He gestured at the table. "I pay fairly. I pay quietly. Whatever you're hesitating over — I promise you the hesitation costs more than the item."

Isa laughed despite herself. "Does that line work?"

"Constantly." He smiled. It was a kind smile, which somehow made it worse. "You're the daughter, aren't you. Varrow. The courier's girl."

Every hair on her arms rose. "I don't know what you mean."

"No, of course not." He began, unhurriedly, to fold the black cloth over his treasures. "Then let me speak hypothetically. Hypothetically, there is a document moving south with four travelers. Hypothetically, certain interested parties would pay a great deal for it — and more than money for the people carrying it. Hypothetically, a sensible young woman might consider that the document has already cost one life, and ask herself what it intends to cost next." He tied the cloth with a neat bow. "Hypothetically, my offer stands until sundown. I lodge at the Sign of the Scales. Ask for Master Fenwick."

"And if I'm not carrying anything hypothetical?"

"Then you've lost nothing but a pleasant conversation." He inclined his head. "Good day, mistress Varrow."

She walked away at a normal pace. It took everything she had. Behind her she felt his attention like sunburn, and ahead of her the market roared on, cheap hope by the dram, and somewhere in the middle of it Odile was agonizing over a vial and Bram was buying eel for a man who hadn't arrived yet, and Isa Varrow stood in the middle of the Market of Small Magics and understood, with terrible clarity, exactly what she was tempted by.

Not the money. The money was just the shape of it. What she was tempted by was the exit. The chance to stop being the daughter of a dead man and become, simply, a woman with a fortune and no obligations. To put the map down. To put *them* down, before they became heavy, before losing them hurt, before—

Before she cared.

"Oh, hell," Isa said aloud, and a charm-seller nearby offered her a curse-removal for two coppers, and she paid it, because it seemed appropriate.

* * *

She found the others at the fountain at the center of the market, and the scene she found was this:

Rennick had finally materialized and was sitting on the fountain's rim with his boots off, drying his feet in the sun like a man at a seaside holiday, while a circle of children watched him cheat at cards with open admiration. Odile sat beside him clutching a paper parcel she clearly had not bought, judging by her expression, which hovered between guilt and defiance. Bram stood over them both with her arms crossed, radiating the specific exasperation of a camp commander whose troops have gone feral.

"—and that," Rennick was saying to the children, "is why you never play cards with a man who has six fingers."

"You have five fingers," said a small girl.

"On each hand, yes. Count again, sweetheart."

"There's still *five*."

"Then I'm cheating even better than I thought." He swept the pot — which appeared to consist of the children's marbles, several buttons, and one live beetle — into his hat, tipped it to his audience, and distributed the marbles back with a flourish, keeping only the buttons and, after consideration, the beetle. "For the road."

"Where have you *been*?" Bram demanded.

"Working. Which is more than I can say for the lot of you — Odile, is that parcel moving?"

"It's a book," Odile said.

"It's a *stolen* book."

"It's a book that was being *underpriced*. There's a difference. There's a moral distinction. Theft implies loss; no one has lost anything; the owner didn't even know what he had—"

"You lifted a book off a stall."

"I liberated scholarship."

Isa leaned against the fountain and watched them, and the arithmetic came back, and this time it wouldn't balance. Here was the other side of the ledger, the side she'd been refusing to total. A scholar who stole books for the crime of loving them too much. A smuggler who gave marbles back to children and kept the beetle. A mercenary who bought eel for absent friends. None of them were hers. None of them owed her anything. Her father had died in their camp by accident, an arrow meant for a stranger's secret, and they had taken her in anyway — rage and all, knives-out and all — because Bram Ashgrove apparently collected strays the way other people collected scars.

If she sold the map, she would never see them again. That was the deal. That was the *point*.

And the Order would catch them, sooner or later, four against a crusade, and it would be her fault in the only way that mattered: she could have ended it, and chose a boat ticket instead.

"Isa." Bram was looking at her. Bram was always looking at her; the woman missed nothing, which was exhausting. "You went pale. What happened?"

"Nothing. Shopping." Then, because lying to Bram felt increasingly like lying to a very perceptive rock: "Someone offered to buy something I might be carrying."

The fountain went quiet. Even the beetle stilled.

"Who," said Bram, in the voice she used for weather reports that mentioned hail.

"A buyer. Reliquary Row. He knew my name, Bram. He knew my *father's* name." Isa hugged her arms. "He was polite about it. That's what frightens me. He wasn't threatening. He was *waiting*. Offer's open till sundown, he says. Master Fenwick, at the Sign of the Scales."

"Order?" Rennick asked, feet back in his boots, all the holiday gone out of him.

"Didn't say. Didn't wear it. But who else buys maps of the old wars and knows a dead courier's daughter on sight?"

"The Hollow Order doesn't usually buy," Odile said quietly. "They burn. If they wanted the map badly enough to negotiate, that means they can't simply take it — which means we're closer to something than they'd like, and they know it." She adjusted her spectacles, and Isa saw the fear in her and saw her hold it steady anyway, and added another entry to the ledger she was no longer pretending not to keep.

"So we leave tonight," Bram said. "Ahead of schedule. Rennick — the factor, can she move us out quiet?"

"For a Tuesday? She can move us out *invisible*."

"Good. We resupply in an hour and—"

"I'm not done," Isa said.

Bram stopped. Waited. The whole fountain waited; even the children had drifted back to watch, sensing drama the way gulls sense fish.

"I could take it," Isa said. "The map. Tonight. Sell it to your Master Fenwick, take the letter of credit, and be on a southbound barge before any of you woke up. I want everyone to understand that I know that. I want it on the table." Her voice was steadier than she felt. "Because I've been doing the sums since the ford, and I want you to hear me do them out loud for once."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere across the market, the grandmother's ghost was loudly reciting a recipe for dumplings.

"My father died carrying that map," Isa said. "Eleven weeks ago. I've had eleven weeks to hate all of you for it, and I have, and it's been very restful, hating people. Hating you meant I didn't have to —" She stopped. Started again, angrier, because the anger was easier. "He raised me on couriers' roads. He taught me that everything has a price and the trick is never being the price. And then he went and *was* the price, for a scrap of goatskin, for somebody else's war, and I keep thinking if I sell it, if I cash him out, at least it becomes about money. Money makes sense. Money I know how to carry."

She pulled the map's corner free of her jacket — just the scorched edge, just enough that they could see it — and held it in the sunlight.

"But it isn't about money," she said. "It never was. He *chose* this. He knew what it would cost and he ran the route anyway, because he was the best courier in the valley and the best courier doesn't drop the package. So." She tucked it away again. "I'm not selling. Sundown can come and go. Tell your Master Fenwick the market's closed."

The silence afterward had a different texture than the silence before. Rennick was grinning at her like she'd won the pot. Odile had taken off her spectacles to polish them, which Isa was beginning to understand was how the scholar cried in public. And Bram —

Bram just nodded. Once. As if Isa had reported the weather and the weather was fine.

"Welcome aboard properly," Rennick said. "Terrible decision. You'll fit right in."

"Shut up, Rennick."

"Never. It's in my contract."

* * *

They resupplied in an hour, as ordered — rope, oil, salt pork, Odile's liberated book wrapped in oilcloth and hidden under the salt pork, which she defended as *poetic justice*. Isa bargained for the lot with a fluency that visibly impressed Bram, and if she skimmed a little off the top for herself, well. Traditions mattered.

It was near dusk when they regrouped at the fountain for the final time, packs shouldered, ready to slip out of Vell invisible. The lamps were being lit. The gray coat from the morning had gone, and Isa didn't trust that either.

"One thing," she said, as they turned toward the river gate. "Fenwick knew my name. My father's name. That's not a market rumor — that's a file. Somebody's been building a picture of us since before we got here."

Bram's jaw tightened. "Then somebody's ahead of us."

"Or," said Rennick softly, looking past them all toward the river gate, where the evening crowd parted — the way crowds part, unhurried and unnatural, for a woman in traveling grays with a knight's bearing and a hollow-cheaped sword, walking toward them through the lamplight with three soldiers fanning out behind her —

"Or somebody's *here*."

Isa's hand found her knife. Beside her, Bram went very still, the stillness of a drawn bow.

The woman stopped ten paces away, and inclined her head with terrible courtesy.

"Bram Ashgrove," she said. "We should talk."
