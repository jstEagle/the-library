## 14. The Order's Offer

The market of Vell smelled of frying onions and hot iron, and Bram had learned by the second day that you could trust neither the onions nor the iron.

She'd left the others at the lodgings — Odile hunched over her notes with three candles burning because she refused to admit her eyes were going, Isa sharpening everything in reach including, at one point, a spoon, and Rennick flat on his back complaining about his ribs in the inventive, poetic way that meant he was actually fine. Bram had said she was going for supplies. Rope, salt, lamp oil. Ordinary things.

She bought the rope. She even bought the salt. It was on the way back, cutting through the tanners' lane where the awnings hung low and the light went amber and thin, that she felt the wrongness settle over her shoulders like a wet cloak.

Kestrel had gone silent years ago — everyone knew that, the whole valley prayed to a god who no longer answered — but Bram had spent her childhood in a temple of the mute god, and some habits outlive their reasons. One of those habits was the stillness-sense: the way her spine prickled when someone stood too quietly behind her. Soldiers were taught to breathe loud so they wouldn't startle horses. Assassins and knights-errant breathed like this. Like they'd practiced not existing.

"You walk like a temple brat," said a voice behind her. "Heels first. Always listening for the bell."

Bram turned slowly, hand nowhere near her sword yet, because drawing steel in a crowded market lane in a free city was how you ended up hanged before lunch, and she had plans for the afternoon that did not include hanging.

Serah Dain stood at the mouth of the alley in civilian wool, grey as river fog, hands folded in front of her like a woman waiting politely at a shrine. No tabard. No escort. Her two swords would have been impossible to hide, so she wore them openly, which in Vell passed for fashion among a certain kind of person. Up close, without the white-and-ash armor, she looked younger. Tired around the eyes in a way that armor usually covered.

"Knight-Captain," Bram said. "You're a long way from your column."

"My column is four days east, being very visible." Serah's gaze flicked down the lane, cataloguing exits with the same ease Bram used. "You're alone."

"I'm shopping."

"Mm." Serah stepped aside to let a tanner's apprentice squeeze past with a basket of hides, murmuring an apology so automatic it was clearly habit. That was the thing about Serah Dain that Bram hated most: the courtesy. Monsters were easy. This woman held doors open for children and burned villages on Thursdays. "Then let me buy you a drink while you decide whether to reach for that sword. You won't. Not here. And I'd rather talk than bury you, if I'm honest, though I've done both this month and buried is less paperwork."

"That's a terrible recruitment speech."

"I'm not recruiting." Something moved behind her eyes — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look one professional gave another across an unbridgeable distance. "There's a wine shop off Tanner's Row. The owner owes me a favor he doesn't know about. Nobody will bother us."

Every sensible part of Bram said *walk away*. But sensible parts of Bram had been overruled all season — by a dead man's map, by a scholar's stubbornness, by a smuggler's grin, by a girl with her father's jaw and her father's temper — and besides, she wanted to hear what the Order thought she was worth.

"All right," she said. "But I'm sitting facing the door."

"I'd be insulted if you didn't."

* * *

The wine shop was low-beamed and dim, smelling of vinegar and old smoke, and the owner took one look at Serah's swords and found urgent business in the cellar. She poured for both of them anyway — a small courtesy that Bram noted and did not thank her for.

"To absent friends," Serah said, lifting her cup, and drank like a woman taking medicine.

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a eulogy in advance. I'm told I'll need several." She set the cup down. "Ashgrove. Bram Ashgrove, raised in the Kestrel temple at Harrow Vale, orphaned, indentured to the order of wardens there until it disbanded, mercenary since. Eleven contracts of record, no blemishes. Your employers speak well of you. One of them — a grain factor in Doss — cried when I told him what you're carrying. Cried, Ashgrove. Grown man, tears in his ledgers."

"What did you tell him?"

"The truth. That you'd been deceived by people who want to wake something that should stay sleeping." Serah turned her cup slowly on the table. "I read the same histories you did. Temple schooling, wasn't it? So you know the shape of the story. Gods and dragons, burning together, and the world underneath them like grass under a scythe. Three centuries of war. Whole kingdoms as kindling. And when it finally ended — not won, *ended*, the way a fever ends — the survivors made a promise. Never again. No fire for anyone to hoard. No god for anyone to kneel to who could turn around and demand cities."

"The Ember isn't a god," Bram said. "It's a fire."

"Everything is a fire, given enough believers." Serah leaned forward. "I've seen what happens to towns near a relic. First it's charms and cures. Then it's tithe. Then it's men with brands on their arms telling you the flame speaks to them and the flame wants your sons. I have watched faith eat a valley down to the stones. Have you?"

Bram thought of the silent shrine above Harrow Vale, the little stone Kestrel with its weathered face, the offerings of bread and buttons and once, memorably, a live chicken that Odile would have had opinions about. A god who asked nothing. Who, for years now, answered nothing.

"No," she said. "I've watched faith keep a valley quiet and kind. Different sermon."

"For now." Serah's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing; it never rose. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it once, plainly, because I don't like games and you strike me as a woman who hates them worse. Surrender the map. All seals intact, or broken, I don't care — my scriveners can work with either. In exchange: full amnesty. Every one of you. The scholar walks free with her disgrace pardoned and her archive rights restored — yes, we know who she is, Vane was famous before she was foolish. The half-blood gets safe passage through every Order checkpoint from here to the coast, papers stamped, tolls waived. The Varrow girl —"

Bram's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

"— the Varrow girl," Serah continued, watching her with those patient grey eyes, "gets what she actually came for, which is her father's killers. We have names. Two of the bowmen are already in custody at Greywater. They'll hang, publicly, with her invited to watch, if that's the sort of closure that helps. And you, Ashgrove, get a clean slate and a contract of your choosing. Wardens are being reformed under charter. There would be a place for someone with your record and your — background."

"*Background,*" Bram repeated.

"Temple-raised. God-touched adjacent, the scriveners call it. It means you understand what we're fighting better than most of my knights ever will." Serah sat back. "Four lives made whole. One map surrendered. That's the arithmetic. I've done the sums on my side honestly, and I'll tell you the sum on yours: refuse, and within the week I take the map anyway, and then the amnesty is off the table, and then I am doing arithmetic of a different kind entirely. I don't say that as a boast. I say it because you deserve to hear it from the person who'll carry it out, not from some sergeant with a crossbow."

The wine sat untouched in front of Bram. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked; the owner, listening, or just a bad joint. Outside, Vell went on being Vell — a fishmonger shouting about mackerel, a child running past the window with a stick horse, ordinary life stacked up in layers like sediment.

And underneath it, in her chest, in the place where Kestrel used to answer prayers and lately answered with a pulse of warmth she hadn't told anyone about — underneath all of it, the map's weight, real or imagined, against her ribs.

Four lives. Rennick, who'd lost his family guarding this route and laughed like it cost him nothing. Odile, who'd spent ten years in a cellar being right and punished for it. Isa, who'd put a knife to Bram's throat six weeks ago and last night had fallen asleep sitting up, holding her father's compass, and looked about nine years old doing it.

One map. Which was, of course, the problem. Because the offer was generous, and generosity from the Hollow Order was a blade with a gift ribbon, and because Bram had already done this arithmetic herself, alone, more nights than she'd admit, and kept arriving at the same answer from the other direction.

"You said the bowmen are in custody," Bram said. "The ones who killed Varrow."

"At Greywater. Under warrant of the Order."

"Warrant signed by whom?"

Serah's cup paused. "The Order's writ is self-executing. That's rather the point of it."

"So the men who killed him answer to the same office that ordered him followed across half a country for carrying a piece of parchment." Bram set her cup down carefully, so she wouldn't set it down any other way. "You're offering me his murderers and his murderers' employer in the same handshake, Knight-Captain. That's not arithmetic. That's a shell game."

"The Order doesn't kill couriers." For the first time, something frayed at the edge of Serah's voice. "We contain relics. If arrows flew, they flew against orders, and I will see it corrected —"

"And if the writ itself is the arrow?" Bram leaned forward now, matching her. "You want to know what I've seen, since I left my valley? I've seen your Order burn a scholar's life's work to smoke her out. I've seen your patrols shake down river families for 'relic tithes.' I've seen a knight-captain execute a ferryman on a bridge for pointing the wrong road, and his own soldiers look at their boots while she did it. You think you're the dam against the flood. From where I stand, Captain, you're the flood wearing armor."

Something crossed Serah's face then — quick, quickly mastered, but Bram caught it, the way you catch a fish's shadow under brown water. Doubt, maybe. Or its cousin, recognition.

"You're good," Serah said quietly. "That speech. Where'd you learn to argue like a temple deacon?"

"Harrow Vale. Deacon Maren said I argued like a tax collector. Higher compliment, I always thought." Bram stood, unhurried, and pushed her chair in. "So here's my counter-offer, and I'll make it once, plainly, because I hate games too. There is no map."

Silence. The fishmonger outside had moved on to haddock.

"I burned it," Bram said. "Three weeks ago, after the ford. I read what I needed off it, memorized the rest, and put it in a cookfire, and I've regretted the haste ever since, because now nobody gets to study it — not your scriveners, not anyone. Whatever's at the end of it stays asleep, or wakes, or rots, and none of it goes through the Hollow Order. Four lives made whole, you said? They already are. They've got each other. Turns out that was the amnesty."

Serah looked at her for a long moment. The lamplight caught the fine scar along her jaw, old and silver.

"You understand I don't believe you."

"I'd think less of you if you did."

"But you understand," Serah said, rising too, gathering her coat with that same terrible courtesy, "that if you're lying, I will find out, and the finding-out will not be gentle, and it will not be only yours. I gave you the truth of my arithmetic because I respect you, Ashgrove. Don't mistake respect for mercy. Mercy is what I feel for the people your companions are going to get killed."

"Captain." Bram paused at the door. "Your sister —"

She saw it land like a hammer between the shoulder blades. Serah went absolutely still, the stillness of drawn steel, and Bram realized two things at once: that she'd said far too much, and that whatever Odile had pried out of that captured agent in the winter camp had been true, and that Serah Dain carried her dead the way Bram carried Kestrel — silently, everywhere, in the blood.

"Don't," Serah said. Just that. One word, and it had a whole cathedral inside it.

"They told us gods killed her," Bram said softly. "Ask yourself who told them to say so. Ask yourself who benefits from a world where every grief gets pointed at the divine, so no one ever looks at the men holding the torches." She opened the door onto the noise and onion-smell of Tanner's Row. "You're wrong about what we're carrying, Captain. And someday I hope you find out how wrong, while there's still time to be glad about it."

She walked. She did not run, though every nerve sang *run*. She bought the lamp oil she'd forgotten, haggled badly because her heart wasn't in it, and took the long way back to the lodgings, doubling twice through the wash-yards, and only let herself breathe when she was behind a door with Odile's candlelight leaking under it.

* * *

They were all awake, which was itself suspicious. Rennick sat up on his pallet looking entirely too healthy, Isa was pretending to oil a knife she'd oiled an hour ago, and Odile had the particular rigid posture of a woman who has been rehearsing a question.

"You're late," Odile said.

"There was a queue at the chandler's."

"Bram." Odile took off her spectacles, which she only did for matters of state. "A boy came by an hour ago. Nine, maybe ten, selling chestnuts. He asked for you by name. Then he left the chestnuts and ran." She nodded at the table, where a paper cone of roasted chestnuts sat, still warm.

Bram looked at them. Warm chestnuts, bought fresh, delivered fast. Somebody had paid a child to follow her through a market and report where she went, and somebody else had decided to send a message with the change.

Inside the cone, folded small, was a scrap of grey paper. She opened it with fingers that wanted very much not to tremble.

Two words, in a precise clerk's hand:

*Four days.*

"She knows we're here," Isa said, reading over her shoulder. Her voice had gone flat and cold, her father's voice. "How does she know we're here?"

Rennick was already swinging his legs off the pallet, wincing, reaching for his boots. "Doesn't matter how. Matters that the market gates close at dusk and there's a river gate that doesn't." He grinned at them, that bright coin-flip grin, and started counting their packs with his eyes. "Pack light, friends. We're leaving Vell tonight, and I know a road the Order's maps forgot."

Bram stood a moment longer, the note in her hand, the lie still warm on her tongue — *there is no map* — and understood, with the clarity of cold water, exactly what it had purchased. Not safety. Time. Four days of it, maybe less, and every hour of it bought with the only coin she had left: the truth, and the people who trusted her to spend it well.

She picked up her pack.

"Tonight, then," she said.
