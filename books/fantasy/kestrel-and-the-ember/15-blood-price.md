## 15. Blood Price

Rennick Cael had survived forty-one years in the smuggling trade by following three rules: never sleep in the same bed twice, never drink anything a priest poured, and never — *never* — fall in love with the people you were running with.

He was currently breaking rule three so thoroughly that it was less a broken rule than a shattered one, ground under his heel like a beetle.

It started small. It always did. First it was Odile Vane correcting his pronunciation of river-spirit names at two in the morning, and instead of telling her to shut her pretty mouth, he'd found himself asking her to say it again. Then it was Isa falling asleep against his shoulder on the Salt Road and him sitting perfectly still for three hours rather than wake her, which for a man whose left arm went numb if he sneezed wrong was practically martyrdom. Then Bram teaching Isa footwork in the frost, laughing — actually laughing, that rare rusty-hinge sound — and Rennick standing there like an idiot thinking, *ah. There it is. There's the family I swore off.*

So when they reached Vell and he'd said, "I know a quarter where nobody asks questions," what he'd really meant was: *I know a place where I can keep all of you safe, and I will burn every favor and forged seal I own to do it.*

The quarter was called Tallow Row, because of the chandlers, or possibly because of what happened to people who informed there — accounts differed. He'd run goods through it for twenty years. The landlady of the Drowned Bell owed him her second husband's freedom. The ferryman owed him a debt involving a customs officer, a barrel of pickled eels, and a night none of them spoke of again. Even the Order's informants in Vell owed him money, which was the most reliable protection in any city.

For six days, it worked.

Six days of Odile hunched over the map's seals in lamplight, muttering in dead languages like a woman arguing with old lovers. Six days of Isa getting better with a blade and worse at cards, which Rennick told her was the natural order of things. Six days of Bram watching the door with that look she had, the one that said she'd already counted the exits and decided which one she'd throw him through first if trouble came.

On the sixth night, trouble came through the door anyway, because trouble had never once respected Rennick's professional opinion.

* * *

It began as a smell. That was the thing about the Hollow Order nobody warned you about — not the armor, not the white-and-ash livery, but the smell. Cold iron and lamp oil and something underneath like a snuffed candle. Rennick had smelled it once before, on the river checkpoint where his family had died guarding this same cursed route, and his body remembered before his head did. He was out of his chair and moving before he understood why.

"Out," he said quietly. "Back room. Now."

Bram was already up, hand on her sword. "How many?"

"Smell says four. Maybe five." He tipped the table over with one shove — years of practice; you learned to make furniture into fortifications or you didn't last — and shoved Odile toward the back. "Go. Take the map."

"I'm not leaving my—"

"*Now*, scholar." He softened it with a grin he didn't feel. "I've been thrown out of nicer places than this."

The door came in without anyone knocking, which struck Rennick as rude even by Order standards. Two men in plain clothes — no livery, which meant deniable, which meant someone senior wanted this quiet — and behind them a third in half-armor who moved like a duelist. City blades, short and ugly, and the third man's had a greenish sheen near the edge that made Rennick's stomach drop.

Poison. Not the crude kind either. River-toad paste, the expensive sort, the sort you paid for when you wanted the target to have time to think about their mistakes.

"Well," Rennick said cheerfully, putting himself between the doorway and the back room, "if you're here about the eel debt, I want it noted I offered to settle in good coin."

The first man lunged. Rennick gave him the table leg across the knuckles and a knee to follow, and the man folded with a sound like wet laundry. The second was better — came in low, blade leading, and Rennick took a line across the forearm catching it, hissed, and headbutted him on principle. The man sat down hard and stayed down.

Which left the duelist.

He was good. Rennick could tell within two exchanges, and being able to tell was itself bad news, because it meant the man was better than Rennick, and Rennick was very good. They traded cuts in the narrow room, overturned furniture shrieking between them, and Rennick bought space with a thrown cup, a kicked ember from the hearth, and one outright lie about reinforcements coming up the stairs.

Then he heard it — boots in the alley behind, more of them, cutting off the back exit where Odile and the others had gone — and he understood the shape of the trap. Front to fix attention, back to harvest. And Odile would be in that alley with a satchel of papers and arms like a librarian's.

Rennick did the arithmetic without wanting to. Four against him here, three-plus in the alley, and Bram and Isa somewhere between. If he held this door, the others might circle back in time. If he ran for the alley now—

Odile screamed.

Not a long scream. A short one, bitten off, the worst kind.

Later, Rennick would try to reconstruct the decision and find there wasn't one. No weighing, no clever smuggler's calculus. Just his feet going, and the duelist's blade meeting him halfway, and the specific cold fish-belly slide of poisoned steel going into his side below the ribs — because he'd turned, deliberately, stupidly, gloriously turned his body into the wall between that blade and the back room.

He caught the man's wrist on the way in, though. Held it. Grinned through the white pain.

"Rule of the trade," Rennick wheezed. "Never touch the merchandise."

He broke the man's nose with his forehead, and then Bram arrived the way weather arrives, and after that it stopped being a fight and became an evacuation with violence in it.

* * *

The healer's name was Maren Quill, and she worked out of a tannery loft because poison-work was technically illegal in Vell and antivenom was technically medicine, and Vell had strong opinions about which mattered more.

She was sixty, built like a rain barrel, and she looked at Rennick's wound the way a jeweler looks at a flawed stone.

"River-toad," she said. "Who cut you, a banker?"

"A professional," Rennick managed. His voice sounded far away to himself. Everything did. The world had gone soft at the edges, warm and dim, like lying in shallow water. "Do you take payment in favors? I'm rich in those."

"I take payment in coin, and your friends are emptying their pockets in the other room, and it won't be enough, so let's skip to the part where I tell you the truth." She pressed something into the wound that made him reconsider several life choices. "The paste is in the blood already. I can draw most of it. Not all. What stays will sit in you like sediment. Some days you'll be fine. Other days your blood will remember it's poisoned and act accordingly. You'll know which days are which by how heavy your legs feel when you stand up."

"How long?" That was Bram's voice, from the doorway. Flat. Careful. The voice she used when feeling might spill.

Maren Quill shrugged, which was somehow worse than a number. "A season. Maybe two, if he rests. He won't rest. Men like him don't." She glanced at Rennick with something almost like respect. "Maybe less, if he keeps doing whatever it was he did to earn this."

"Shielded a friend," Rennick said.

"Foolish."

"The finest foolishness available," he agreed, and laughed, and the laugh hurt enough that he saw colors.

* * *

They carried him — he insisted on walking, lost the argument in eleven steps — to a safehouse deeper in Tallow Row, a chandler's storeroom that smelled of tallow and salt, with one window, two doors, and a lock Rennick personally guaranteed against everything except fire and honesty.

That first night, fever took him, and the family took watches.

He surfaced sometimes. Once to find Isa asleep in a chair pulled right up against his cot, her father's map spread across her knees like a blanket, her hand hovering near his wrist as though she'd been checking his pulse and fallen asleep mid-duty. Once to find Odile reading aloud — some dry archivist's text on river spirits, chosen, he suspected, because she thought the drone of it would soothe him, and she was right, gods help him, he found her voice better than any poppy draught. Once to find Bram sitting alone in the dark, sharpening a blade she had absolutely already sharpened, staring at nothing.

"You should sleep," he croaked at her.

"So should you."

"One of us is professionally unconscious. The other is being dramatic." He shifted, winced. "Bram. Come here."

She came. Sat on the crate beside the cot. In the dark her face was all edges and worry, and Rennick, who had spent his whole life reading faces for profit, read hers for free.

"You're doing the arithmetic," he said. "Don't. I can see the sums from here. One smuggler, mortgaged, against three of you still breathing. Favorable exchange rate."

"That's not—" Her jaw worked. "You took steel meant for Odile."

"Took it? I *invested* in it. There's a difference. Investments pay dividends." He grinned, and knew it was a gray-faced, sweat-slick grin, and did it anyway, because someone in this family had to keep the ledger of lightness, and apparently that job was his. "Besides. You'd have done the same. Isa would. Even the scholar would, though she'd cite a footnote while doing it."

Bram was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gravel in it. "The healer said a season."

"Maren Quill always rounds down. Pessimists live longer; it's documented."

"She said—"

"I heard what she said. I was there. Dramatic, remember." He reached out and gripped her wrist, and his grip was weaker than he wanted it to be, and they both pretended otherwise. "Listen to me, Ashgrove. I've spent forty-one years keeping everyone at arm's length because arm's length is where you survive. And then you lot came along with your maps and your dead fathers and your silent god, and somewhere on the Salt Road I looked up and realized I'd gone and broken my best rule." His throat tightened around the next part, so he made it a joke, because that was the only way he knew to carry heavy things. "You're the worst cargo I ever signed on to carry. High maintenance. Constantly pursued. Terrible at cards."

Bram laughed — that rusty hinge — and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand fast, like she thought he wouldn't notice.

He noticed.

"Get some sleep, captain," Rennick said. "I'll haunt you properly tomorrow."

* * *

Three days later, when the fever broke and his legs agreed — grudgingly, on unfavorable terms — to hold him upright, Rennick called a council of war from the dignity of his cot.

"We can't stay in Vell," he said. "The Order knows the quarter. My protections bought us six days, and six days is what we got. Whoever sent those men will send more, and next time they won't bother with deniable knives — they'll come with livery and warrants and the city watch holding their coats."

"Then we move," Bram said. "But the second seal's in the mountains. Three weeks' travel with you healthy. Longer now."

"I'll keep up."

"You'll *slow* us," Isa said, blunt as ever, and Rennick loved her for it even as it landed like a stone. "You know you will. Every day we carry you is a day Serah Dain gains."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

Odile spoke from her corner, where she'd barely slept in three days, ink-stained and hollow-eyed and ferocious. "There is another way. The river spirits. This stretch of the Vellwater is old water — older than the cities, older than the Order. The old bargemasters used to bargain with them. Passage, protection, in exchange for... payment." She hesitated. "The payment is always memory. The rivers take what you love and keep it."

"No," Bram said immediately.

"It wasn't offered to you," Rennick said.

"It's madness. You'd forget your own family—"

"My family's dead, Ashgrove." He said it lightly, and watched it fail to land lightly, and kept going anyway. "Dead twenty years, guarding this same cursed route your map follows. I keep their faces in a box in my head and open it maybe twice a year because it hurts too much to open it more. You're proposing I trade a box of grief for a season of breath." He looked around the room — at Bram's fury, at Isa's stricken face, at Odile's careful hope. "Let me think on it. That's all I ask. Let me price it out. Bargaining's my trade, after all."

Nobody liked it. Everybody saw the arithmetic. That was the thing about families, Rennick was learning — they argued with you, but they stood in the doorway while they did it, and stayed, and stayed.

That night, alone with the tallow-smell and the river murmuring somewhere below the floorboards, Rennick Cael opened the box in his head one last time. His mother's laugh. His brother's terrible singing. The little sister who'd named a barge after a cat that had never existed.

Then he closed it, gently, the way you close a door on sleeping children.

And he went down to the water to make his bargain.
