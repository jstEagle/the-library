## 16. The Second Seal

The pass had a name once. Odile said it did — something in Old Vellish that translated roughly to *the mountain's collarbone* — but the maps had worn the name away the way rain wears a carving, and now it was just the place where the trail narrowed and the wind learned to bite.

Bram walked point because she always walked point, and because walking point meant she didn't have to talk.

Behind her, the company made its small noises. Isa's boots crunching with the deliberate rhythm of someone who'd learned to march angry and hadn't yet unlearned it. Odile muttering to herself in three languages, two of them dead, cataloguing rock strata like a woman writing letters to a future that might not exist. And Rennick, at the rear, quiet in a way that was worse than his usual chatter. Three days since the river spirit took its price, and he still smiled at all the right moments. That was the horror of it. He smiled at all the right moments because he'd paid away whatever used to make them real.

"Your left boot's coming apart," Isa said, falling into step beside her. Not a question. Isa didn't ask questions so much as deliver findings.

"I'll mend it tonight."

"You said that about your cloak."

"And I mended my cloak."

"After I mended your cloak."

Bram grunted, which in her language was a concession. The trail bent around a shoulder of grey stone, and the wind came down off the icefields above with a smell like old metal. Somewhere behind them — a day back, maybe less — Serah Dain's column would be climbing the same road. You didn't outrun the Hollow Order. You only stayed ahead of it long enough to finish what you'd started.

The map rode against Bram's ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a sleeping bird. It had been warm for two days now. She hadn't told anyone that either.

*The list of things you haven't told anyone is getting long enough to publish,* she thought, and then hated herself for the joke, because Rennick would have made it better.

They made camp in the lee of a fallen pillar of rock just as the light went copper. Odile built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand and large enough to heat the barley, which was the eternal compromise of fugitive cooking. Isa sharpened knives. Rennick sat with his hands open on his knees, watching the flames, and when Bram caught his eye he winked and said, "I know this fire. We've met."

"You've met every fire," Isa said without looking up.

"Not this one." He tilted his head. "This one owes me money."

It should have been funny. It was almost funny. But there was nothing behind the wink except the shape of a joke he no longer remembered making, and Bram turned away before her face could do anything about it.

*Tell them,* said something in her chest that wasn't quite Kestrel and wasn't quite her own conscience. *Tell them what you felt at the first seal. Tell them what the warmth in the map means.*

She fed the fire a stick instead.

* * *

The drowned chapel had shown her things she still hadn't sorted into piles: gods and dragons burning together, a war so old its causes had rotted out of memory, and — underneath all of it, steady as a heartbeat under panic — a voice saying her name. *Bram.* Just that. As if the whole catastrophe of history had been leading up to a greeting.

And then the map had begun to glow, faintly, at night, and her old wounds — the arrow scar at her shoulder, the knife scar along her ribs, the burn from the magistrate's brazier — had begun to ache in time with it. Like they were keeping count of something.

She knew what the scholars' texts said about the second seal. Odile had recited it twice, the way she recited everything, as though repetition could substitute for certainty: *the second seal opens to blood of the line, in high cold places, where the god went quiet.* Blood of the line. In high cold places.

They were in a high cold place. And Bram Ashgrove, orphan of the valley temple, raised on broth and silence by priests who never explained why they'd taken her in, had been feeling warmer the higher they climbed.

She checked the knots on the picket line twice. Then a third time, because the horses were patient and she needed something to do with her hands that couldn't lie.

"Bram." Odile, at the edge of the firelight, holding the map case. "It's time, isn't it. The pass narrows ahead — there's a shelf of stone above the trail, exposed to the north sky. The texts say the seals answer to specific conditions. Altitude. Cold. Open sky." She paused. "And proximity."

"Proximity to what?"

Odile looked at her for a moment too long. "To whoever the seal is waiting for," she said, and handed over the case, and went to bed without another word, which from Odile was practically a shout.

* * *

They reached the narrowest throat of the pass an hour past dawn, with frost smoking off the rocks and their breath hanging in flags. The trail ran beneath a wall of stone fifty feet high, and at its top, exactly where Odile's texts promised, a shelf jutted out like a lower lip — bare, snow-dusted, open to a sky so blue it looked recently invented.

Bram stopped.

"What?" Isa said, hand going to her sword.

"It wants me up there."

Nobody argued, which was its own kind of terrible. A month ago they'd have argued. They'd have circled her with practical objections and alternate plans and Rennick would have proposed three scams involving the map. Now they just looked at her, four faces — three faces and one hollowed-out smile — waiting for her to decide, and Bram understood that somewhere between the ford and the pass she had stopped being the hired escort and become the thing they organized themselves around.

That was the burden nobody warned you about. Not the danger. The trust.

"I'll go alone," she said. "If anything happens—"

"Nothing will happen," Isa said flatly. "Because if it does, I'm coming up after you, and I'll be furious about the climb, and you'll have to live with that."

Rennick raised two fingers in a lazy salute. Odile simply nodded, already pulling out her notebook, because if the world insisted on ending, Odile intended to take notes.

The climb was easy, which felt like an insult. Handholds worn smooth by centuries of weather, or perhaps by centuries of other hands. At the top, the shelf was wide enough for one person kneeling, and the snow on it was undisturbed, and the silence up there was different from the valley's silence — the valley's silence was empty, but this was *attentive*. This was a silence leaning in.

Bram knelt. Unwrapped the map.

The scorched vellum lay across her palms, and the lines of ink were moving again, the way they had at the chapel — slow tectonic shifts, roads rearranging themselves into sentences. The warmth came up through the parchment into her skin, and her scars woke one by one, shoulder, ribs, palm, a litany of every wound she'd ever carried, and she thought, with a strange calm: *oh. It's not reading the map. The map is reading me.*

"Kestrel," she said aloud. Her breath didn't fog. "I don't know how to pray anymore. So I'll just talk."

The world folded.

Not darkness — the opposite. Every color arriving at once, too much, a flood, and then it sorted itself into a scene older than any nation whose name she knew:

A battlefield under a black sun. Dragons in their hundreds wheeling over a plain of glass where forests used to be, and against them, vast and dim and grieving, the shapes of gods — not the tidy statues from temple friezes but *weather*, gods as weather, gods as storms with intentions. Fire answering fire. The ground itself screaming.

And in the middle of it, smaller than everything, a figure standing on a ridge. A god in the shape of a bird — kestrel-brown, kestrel-small, absurd amid the apocalypse, the way a sparrow is absurd in a cathedral. Kestrel, who had fought longer than the others and burned less brightly and understood, before any of them, that the war had no winning side. Only sides that stopped existing.

She watched him turn away from the battle. Watched him do the thing that the Order called cowardice and the temples called mystery and the truth was neither: he *stopped*. He closed himself like a fist. He let his own fire gutter down to an ember, and he took that ember —

— and he poured it out.

Into bloodlines. Into mortal veins, scattered thin, generation after generation, diluted and hidden, so that no hunter — dragon or god or man — could ever find the whole of it in one place. The Ember's location wasn't written anywhere. It was *carried*. Distributed through ordinary people who farmed and quarreled and died without ever knowing what slept warm in their blood.

Kestrel went silent not because he died. Because hiding was the last duty left, and he hid so thoroughly that he hid inside his own worshippers.

Inside families. Inside lines. Inside—

The vision turned, gently, like a hand on her chin, and showed her a valley where magic could not enter, and a temple full of quiet priests, and a foundling child brought in from the snow, and she saw the priests look at the child with an expression she had spent thirty years misreading as pity.

It wasn't pity. It was *custody*.

"No," Bram said, in the vision, in the real world, in both. Her voice cracked the image like ice. "No, pick someone else, I didn't ask for—"

But the vision was already fading, folding up like a letter, and the last thing it gave her was not an image but a sensation: warmth spreading from her palms to her wrists to her arms, the exact warmth of standing too close to a hearth, the exact warmth of being *known*, and underneath it, faint as a pulse in a wrist, something that was not words but was unmistakably an apology.

*I'm sorry. I had no one else. You were enough.*

Then she was kneeling on a frozen shelf in the wind, alone, with the map glowing softly against her knees like a cat that had gotten what it wanted.

* * *

She didn't remember climbing down. That frightened her more than the vision had. She was suddenly at the bottom, boots on scree, and Isa was in front of her saying her name for the third time, and Odile was hovering with a water flask, and Rennick was watching her with an expression that had no joke in it at all.

"You were up there forty minutes," Isa said. "You didn't move. Your lips moved, but you didn't move."

"I'm fine."

"You're gray."

"I'm always gray. It's a complexion."

Isa didn't laugh. Isa grabbed her by the shoulders, hard, and searched her face with those fierce dark eyes, and Bram realized with a lurch that Isa Varrow — who had ambushed them, who had blamed them, who had every reason to keep her heart locked — was afraid for her. Genuinely, stupidly, generously afraid.

"What did it show you?" Isa asked.

And here was the moment. Here was the door, standing open, with everyone she loved on the other side of it. Forty minutes of vision pressing against her teeth. *Blood of the line. Kestrel's own blood can wake or quench the Ember. You were enough.*

Her father — some father, some man in the valley generations back — had carried a shard of a dying god in his blood and passed it down like a debt. Her mother. The priests who'd raised her had known. Had fed her broth and taught her blade-work and kept her safe and never, ever told her why, and now she stood in a mountain pass with the same secret swelling in her chest like a swallowed coal, and she understood, finally, why they'd kept it. Not to protect themselves.

To protect her from this exact moment. From four faces waiting. From the way love turns to arithmetic the instant people learn you're worth more than they are.

"The war," she heard herself say. "How it started. How the gods tried to end it. Kestrel hid the Ember — scattered it, hid it where no one could ever gather it." She let them lean in. She gave them ninety percent of the truth, which is the most dangerous fraction there is. "The third seal will show us where it rests. That's all this one was. History."

Odile's eyes narrowed, a fraction, a hair's width. The scholar had spent her life catching forgeries, and Bram watched her catch one now, watch her weigh it, watch her — deliberately, visibly — decide to set it down.

"History," Odile repeated. "Of course."

"We should move," Bram said. "Serah's a day behind us, maybe less. If we make the far side of the pass by dark—"

"Yes," Isa said. "Fine. Move. March. Be a wall." But she held Bram's arm a moment longer than marching required, and said, quietly, so the others couldn't hear: "When you're ready to tell the rest of it, I'll be here. I'm good at waiting. I waited twenty years to find out who killed my father."

Guilt is a strange animal. It doesn't bite. It sits down next to you and stays.

* * *

They crossed the spine of the pass as the afternoon light went long and gold, and the world on the far side opened below them — foothills, a brown ribbon of river, distances softened by haze. Somewhere down there, the third seal waited. Somewhere behind, dust on the horizon that might have been weather and might have been the Hollow Order.

Rennick fell into step beside her and for a while said nothing, which from Rennick was a eulogy.

"You lied well," he said at last, conversationally. "For the record. I used to lie for a living, before—" a small pause, a hand passing over a gap he couldn't see, "—before. You lied better than most professionals. Except for one thing."

"Oh?"

"You looked at Isa when you said it. People only look at the person they're lying to when the lie matters." He shrugged. "Anyway. Whatever it is — the thing you're carrying — it's heavy. I know heavy. I know what it costs to hold a thing alone because you think sharing it makes it everyone's problem." His smile came back, the borrowed one, the one that fit wrong. "It doesn't. Sharing it just means you're not the only one bleeding."

He dropped back to the rear before she could answer, which was probably mercy, since her answer would have been a lie too, and she was getting tired of the taste.

That night, camped in the first trees below the pass, Bram took the first watch and unwrapped the map by firelight. The ink-lines had settled into new configurations — a route, a destination, a shape in the mountains south of them that Odile would recognize by morning as Mount Sarn, though none of them knew it yet.

The warmth pulsed against her palms. Patient. Certain.

*You were enough,* the silence said.

"Don't," Bram whispered to it. "Don't say that like it's a comfort. Enough is just another word for *used up*."

The map glowed on, gentle and unbothered, and did not argue — and somehow that was worst of all, because she could feel that it agreed. That somewhere in the design of things, a dying god had chosen a family, and a family had chosen a valley, and a valley had chosen a foundling, and every choice down the long chain had been some version of the same sentence: *she won't refuse. She's the kind who doesn't refuse.*

Below her, the others slept in a heap of blankets like puppies. Isa's hand, even in sleep, rested near her sword. Odile's notebook had slid from her fingers, still open. Rennick breathed slow and even, dreaming, if he dreamed at all, of fires that owed him money.

Bram pulled her cloak tighter, looked south toward the mountain, and began — silently, in the old temple cadence, the first prayer she'd offered in years — to practice the sentence that was going to break her family's heart.

She got as far as *I have to tell you something* before she couldn't continue.

Down in the dark, the map glowed brighter.
