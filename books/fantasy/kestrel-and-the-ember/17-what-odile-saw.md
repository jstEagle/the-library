## 17. What Odile Saw

Odile Vane had spent thirty-one years learning to read things that did not wish to be read.

Censored manuscripts. Palimpsests scraped clean and written over. The marginalia of monks who had been ordered to burn what they could not bear to destroy. She had built a career — and then lost one — on the stubborn conviction that everything left a mark, that erasure was itself a kind of handwriting, and that the truth, however thoroughly suppressed, always bled through the page like ink through cheap vellum.

Which was why it was beginning to genuinely irritate her that she had not seen it sooner.

She sat in the lee of a granite outcrop while the others made camp below, ostensibly cataloguing her notes but in fact watching Bram Ashgrove across the firelight the way she might watch a document whose seal had been broken and re-waxed by someone hoping no one would notice.

The evidence, when she finally laid it all out in her head, was embarrassingly abundant.

Item: at the drowned chapel, when the first seal opened, every one of them had seen the vision — gods and dragons burning together, the sky coming apart like wet paper. But Isa had wept. Rennick had sworn. Odile herself had gripped a pillar until her fingers ached. And Bram? Bram had stood very still, the way one stands still when someone has just said one's name in a crowded room.

Item: afterward, Bram had asked — casually, too casually, in the tone of a woman asking about the weather — whether visions of that kind ever *spoke*. Whether they were only pictures, or whether there were words.

Odile had answered honestly: the archives described both kinds. And she had watched Bram nod once, satisfied, and file something away behind those grey eyes, and change the subject to salt rations.

Item: the map. The scorched map that had started all of this glowed faintly at night — a warmth without light, like a stone kept near a hearth. It glowed for all of them, faintly. But twice now, when Odile had taken her turn carrying it, she had noticed that its warmth deepened whenever Bram came within arm's reach. Like iron filings finding their lodestone. Like a dog hearing its master's step three streets away.

And item — the item that had kept her awake two nights running, staring at the canvas above her bunk in Rennick's riverboat cabin:

At the chapel, in the last moment before the vision released them, Odile had heard something. Not words. A voice like wind moving through a valley, vast and patient and terribly sad. And the voice had not said *Odile*, or *Isa*, or *Rennick*.

It had said — or she had felt it say, or she had dreamed she felt it say —

*Ashgrove.*

She had told herself it was nothing. She had told herself this for eleven days, which for Odile Vane constituted an act of heroic self-deception, and she was ashamed of it, and she was done with it.

Because here was the thing about secrets in a small company walking toward a mountain: they did not stay contained. They leaked. They soured the water they sat in. She had watched it happen in the archives — scholars who hoarded a discovery instead of sharing it, and how the hoarding curdled into paranoia, and the paranoia into ruin. Truth deferred did not keep. It bred distance, and distance bred suspicion, and suspicion, in a company being hunted by the Hollow Order, could get everyone killed.

Besides — and she admitted this to herself with a wince — she liked Bram Ashgrove. Genuinely. Against all her better judgment and considerable professional training, she had grown fond of the taciturn mercenary who pretended not to notice when Isa's boots wore through and "found" new ones, who fed Rennick's ridiculous river-gull every morning and claimed it was to keep it from following them, who prayed each night at whatever roadside shrine they passed with the devotion of a woman talking to a friend who had stopped writing back.

You did not let a friend walk into Mount Sarn carrying a secret that heavy without at least offering to help set it down.

So. Confrontation. Odile straightened her spectacles, closed her notebook, and went down the slope to make a nuisance of herself.

* * *

She found Bram apart from the camp, as she somehow always was — checking the picket lines, though the horses were calm and the night was quiet. The moon was a thin paring of bone over the mountain pass ahead. Somewhere below, Rennick was losing badly at cards to Isa and complaining about it with theatrical despair, his voice drifting up the hillside punctuated by Isa's flat, merciless call of *pay up.*

"Master Ashgrove."

"Bram." Bram didn't turn around. "Nobody calls me Master Ashgrove except people about to arrest me."

"A habit I'm trying to break." Odile came to stand beside her at the picket line. The nearest horse, a rangy bay named Turnip because Rennick had named him, lipped hopefully at her sleeve. She pushed his nose away. "I've been reviewing my notes from the chapel."

"Mm."

"The vision. The first seal." Odile kept her voice level, scholarly, conversational — the voice she used for delicate negotiations with archivists who did not want to admit what they possessed. "Fascinating material. The iconography matches nothing in the sanctioned canon, which is precisely why the Order burned every copy of the *Kestreline Fragments* they could find. Did you know that? The Order's founding charter doesn't mention dragons at all. It mentions *records*. Their first act, two hundred years ago, wasn't hunting fire. It was burning libraries."

"I didn't know that," Bram said, in the tone of someone who wanted very much for this conversation to be about libraries.

"It's relevant," Odile pressed on gently, "because it means the Order has always understood something most people haven't. That the war isn't just sealed behind the Ember. It's sealed behind *knowledge*. Information is the second lock. Which is why I've made a study of locks." She turned to look at Bram directly. "And why I've noticed that you, Bram Ashgrove, are behaving like a locked door."

Bram's hands went still on the bay's bridle.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Below, Rennick howled something about cheating and Isa's laugh cracked across the dark — a real laugh, unguarded, the first one Odile had heard from her since the bridge. The sound of it made what Odile was about to do feel worse and more necessary at once.

"At the chapel," Odile said quietly, "when the vision ended — I heard something. A voice. I don't think anyone else did, or if they did, they haven't said so. And I think the voice said your name."

The silence stretched so long that Turnip grew bored and dropped his head to crop at the sparse grass.

"You were dreaming," Bram said at last. "Visions do that. They spill over. Odile, you'd been half-drowned an hour before—"

"I know what I heard."

"Do you?" Bram turned then, and her face in the moonlight was composed, courteous, and utterly closed — a face Odile recognized, because she had worn it herself in front of the tribunal that stripped her title. The face of a person managing a conversation rather than having one. "You're a scholar. You know better than anyone how memory works under stress. You want the vision to mean something specific, so your mind gives it shape. It's not a failing. It's just how people are."

It was a good answer. It was, in fact, exactly the answer Odile would have given, six weeks ago, to a frightened witness insisting on something improbable. And that was the problem. Bram Ashgrove was many things — brave, loyal, maddeningly literal — but she was not a subtle liar, and this was the first time Odile had ever heard her lie well.

That frightened her more than the secret itself.

"The map warms when you're near it," Odile said. "I've carried it eleven nights. I've checked. I thought perhaps it was my imagination, so I started recording it. Temperature by touch, duration, proximity." She pulled her notebook from her coat and held it up. "I'm not proud of it. Testing a companion like a specimen. But I was trained to test things, Bram, and the map responds to you. Not to me. Not to Isa, whose father died carrying it. To *you*."

Something flickered across Bram's face — fast, quickly mastered, but Odile caught it, and it wasn't guilt. It was fear. Real fear, cold and old, the kind a person carries a long time before anyone sees it.

"And the questions you asked after the vision," Odile continued, softer now. "Whether visions speak. Whether they use words. You weren't curious, Bram. You were *confirming*. You already knew what you'd heard. You wanted to know if I'd heard it too."

"Stop."

It was barely a whisper. Bram's hand had come up — not threatening, just raised, like a man warding off a blow he'd been expecting for years.

"Or what?" Odile asked. There was no cruelty in it. She genuinely wanted to know. "What happens if I say it plainly? Shall I? I'll say it plainly, and then you can tell me I'm wrong, and I will believe you, because I want to believe you — that's the honest truth, I have staked my life on this company and I would prefer to go on doing it. So here it is, plainly." She drew a breath. "I think Kestrel did not fall silent. I think Kestrel hid. The second seal showed us gods pouring themselves into mortal bloodlines to keep the Ember's location from the enemy — you saw it same as I did. And I think the god of your valley, the mute god you've prayed to your whole life, poured itself into *yours*. I think the visions speak to you because they are speaking to what's already in you. I think, Bram Ashgrove, that you may be the reason any of this map works at all."

The words hung in the cold air between them.

Below, the card game had gone quiet; Rennick's voice rose again, softer, telling some story about a gull and a stolen herring. The ordinary sounds of people being people, and Odile clung to them.

Bram lowered her hand slowly. When she spoke, her voice was steady and flat as a frozen lake.

"No."

"No?"

"No. That's not—" A breath. "You've read too many fragments, Odile. You see patterns because patterns are your trade. I'm a mercenary from a valley where nothing magical has happened in four hundred years. My prayers have never been answered. Not once. If anything, that should prove the opposite — a god in my bloodline that won't even say hello?" She almost smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and Odile hated how much she wanted to trust it. "I carry the map because I took a contract. That's all this is. A contract that got out of hand."

Silence again.

And Odile — who had lost everything once already for refusing to say the true thing out loud, who had stood in front of seven robed censors and declared that dragons lived and been ruined for it, who had sworn on that day never, ever again to be the coward in the room — heard herself say:

"All right."

Bram blinked. "...All right?"

"If you say it isn't so, then it isn't so. I'll put the notes away." Odile tucked the notebook back into her coat, and her hands were not quite steady, and she hated that too. "I apologize. Truly. It was an intrusion. A scholar's worst habit — treating living people like texts."

"Odile—"

"Goodnight, Bram."

She walked back down toward the fire, spine straight, steps even, the picture of a woman entirely satisfied with a conversation. Behind her she felt Bram watching, and did not turn around, because turning around would have meant looking at Bram's face, and Odile was very much afraid that what she would find there was relief.

* * *

She did not sleep.

She lay in her bedroll listening to the camp breathe — Rennick's snore, which had a rhythmic, almost musical quality Isa had compared to a millwheel; Isa turning over restlessly, still sleeping with a knife in reach even after three weeks of truce; Bram, at the edge of the firelight, sitting up long past her watch, staring into the coals.

Odile stared at the stars and conducted the argument she should have conducted on the hillside.

*You should have pressed.* Yes. *You should have shown her the notes, the timings, the temperature readings.* Yes. *You folded like a first-year student afraid of offending her examiner.* Thank you, yes, I *know.*

But here was the thing she kept circling back to, the thing that had actually made her stop: Bram had lied *well*. And Bram did not lie well. Which meant Bram had practiced. Which meant this was not a secret born at the drowned chapel. This was an old secret. Possibly a lifelong one. Possibly one Bram had carried alone since childhood, since the temple, since whatever orphan upbringing had taught her to pray to a silent god every night of her life without ever once expecting an answer.

You could not force open a door like that. Odile knew — the tribunal had tried to force open hers, and all it had produced was thirty years of rubble. Doors opened from the inside, or they did not open at all.

But oh, it *ached*, the deferral of it. Because the lie hadn't just come between her and Bram. She could feel it already, a fine hairline crack running outward through everything — through the easy rhythm of the road, through the shared watches, through the small economy of glances that a traveling company develops. Every time Bram said something reassuring now, Odile would wonder. Every time Bram volunteered for the dangerous task, took point on the pass, offered to hold the map — and she always, always offered to hold the map — Odile would be counting degrees of warmth against proximity in her head like some pathetic human instrument.

Trust, once bent, did not spring back straight. Everyone learned that eventually. Most people learned it too late.

Near midnight, soft footsteps. Isa, wrapped in her blanket, settling beside Odile's bedroll with the wary courtesy the girl extended to exactly one member of the company.

"You're awake," Isa whispered.

"Scholars sleep like cats. Briefly, and suspiciously."

A ghost of a smile. Then, quieter: "Is it Bram? You two were arguing up on the picket line. I saw."

"We weren't arguing."

"You were standing like people argue." Isa pulled her knees up. In the ember-light her father's jaw looked suddenly very like her father's, and Odile's chest tightened. "My da used to say that about me. Said I argued with my whole body and my mouth just got there late." She was quiet a moment. "Whatever it is — she'll tell us. When she's ready. She's the sort who tells people things when she's ready, and not one breath before, and you just have to decide if you can live with that."

"Yes," Odile said. "That does sound like her."

"She carried me half a mile today when my ankle went. Didn't say a word the whole time. Just carried me." Isa rested her chin on her knees. "People like that don't lie about the big things. I'd bet my life on it."

Odile looked at the girl — at the dead man's daughter, betting her life, as they all were, on a woman who had looked Odile in the eye tonight and lied with the smoothness of long practice — and found she had absolutely nothing to say.

*She believes,* Odile thought. *They all believe. And I am the only one holding a notebook full of proof, and I put it away, and I called it an apology, and now the secret belongs to two people instead of one, and that is how cracks become faults, and faults become landslides.*

Across the fire, Bram finally lay down. Before closing her eyes, she glanced — just once, quickly — toward Odile's bedroll.

Their eyes met in the dark.

Neither of them looked away, and neither of them moved, and the space between them filled with everything unsaid, until at last Bram turned over and Odile shut her eyes and the camp settled into uneasy sleep.

In the morning, the pass waited — and with it, according to Odile's maps and her worsening dread, the second seal.

She only hoped that whatever it showed them, it would show all of them equally.

She was already afraid it wouldn't.
