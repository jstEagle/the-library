## 18. Winter Camp

The snow came down like the sky had finally given up pretending to be anything else.

Bram had found the camp by instinct more than planning — a hollow beneath a granite shelf where the wind broke and scattered, with deadfall enough for a fire and a view of the only approach for half a mile. In the valley she'd have called it a good camp. Here, past the veil, with Odile's charms humming faintly along the tent ropes like bees in a jar, it felt less like shelter and more like an agreement: the mountain would let them stay, for now, on sufferance.

Rennick was the reason they'd stopped at all.

He lay in the lee of the fire, wrapped in every blanket the company owned, and he was *grey*. Not the grey of exhaustion — Bram had worn that grey herself often enough to know its shape. This was the grey of something being slowly unmade, the poison from Vell still working through him even after the healer had cut and poulticed and prayed. The healer had also said, in the flat tone healers used when they wanted you to hear the words without hearing them, *a season, perhaps. If he rests.*

"Stop staring at me," Rennick said, without opening his eyes. "It's rude. And I can feel it. Like being licked by a very judgmental dog."

"You're supposed to be sleeping."

"I'm supposed to be dying, if we're being precise, and I find sleep cuts into my schedule." He opened one eye. It found her, sharp as ever despite everything. "You've got your thinking face on. The one where you decide things for other people."

"I don't have a thinking face."

"You have several faces. Thinking. Guilty. The one where you're about to do something stupid and noble and expect us all to be impressed." He settled back against his pack. "That last one's my least favorite."

Bram fed the fire instead of answering. Across the camp, Isa was cleaning her blades with the fierce concentration of someone who had learned, three weeks ago on a bridge, what steel actually did, and was determined to master it before it mastered her. Odile sat cross-legged over her books, hair escaping its braid in gray wisps, muttering translations under her breath.

A family, Bram thought. Gods help them all.

* * *

**Odile**

Odile had spent thirty years believing that scholarship was a solitary discipline. She had been wrong about many things — dragons, most famously, and the goodwill of university committees — but this error stung the most, because the correction kept insisting on feeding her soup.

"Drink," Isa said, pressing a tin cup into her hands. "You've been reading since dawn. Your eyes have gone pointy."

"My eyes are not—"

"They've gone *pointy*, Odile. Like a cat's. Drink the soup."

The soup was terrible. Isa made it the way she did everything now, with too much force, as though gentleness were a debt she could no longer afford. But it was hot, and there was salt in it, and Odile drank it while the girl hovered with the particular ferocity of someone who would accept no refusal.

It had been Isa's idea, the histories. Three nights ago, snowed in, bored past endurance, she had demanded: "Someone tell a story that isn't about seals or gods or how much everything costs. A real one. About *you*."

"I'm fascinating," Rennick had said from his blankets.

"You're a criminal."

"A *smuggler*. There's paperwork involved. It's practically a guild."

And so it had begun, awkwardly, in fits and starts — each of them trading off pieces of themselves the way travelers traded off watches through the night. Odile had gone first, because she was the oldest and claimed seniority, and had told them about the archive below the University of Corvane, and the day the provosts burned her life's work in the courtyard while students watched from the windows.

Tonight it was Isa's turn, though the girl didn't seem to know it yet. She was sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening.

"Your turn," Odile said gently. "Childhood. Rules are rules."

"There aren't any rules."

"We voted. You lost."

Isa scowled at the knife. Then, surprisingly, she put it down.

"I grew up in Tarrow's Reach," she said. "Salt marsh and sheep. My father fished the estuary in summer and ran cargo in winter — honest cargo," she added, with a glance at Rennick that dared him to comment. He raised both hands in surrender. "He was gone a lot. So it was me and my mother, mostly. She dyed wool. The whole house smelled of madder and vinegar. I used to think everyone's mother had hands stained red to the wrist."

She turned the cup in her fingers.

"She died when I was nine. Fever. After that it was just — waiting. For him to come home. He'd bring me things from his routes. A compass that didn't point north. A bird carved from ambergris. Once, a jar of fireflies from somewhere they glowed blue instead of gold, and I kept them alive for six days and cried for two more when they went out." Her mouth twisted. "I thought he was boring, honestly. A man who carried jars of fireflies across three borders. I thought he was small."

"He wasn't small," Odile said quietly.

"No." Isa looked into the fire. "No, he was carrying a map that gods and knights were willing to kill for, and he never once told me, because telling me would have put me in the path of it. That's not small. That's—" Her voice caught. She covered it with a shrug that fooled no one. "I keep redoing the arithmetic. All those winters he was away. He wasn't fishing. He was keeping it safe. And I was angry at him for missing my birthday."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a sound like slow breathing.

"He'd have liked this," Isa said at last, gesturing at the camp, the company, the absurdity of it. "Snowed in with a scholar, a smuggler, and a mercenary who pretends she isn't kind. He collected strays. I was the first one."

"You're not a stray," Odile said. "Strays don't make soup this bad."

Isa laughed — wetly, but she laughed, and threw a pinecone at Odile's head, which Odile permitted because it missed.

* * *

**Serah**

Serah Dain had not slept properly in eleven days, and the snow had decided the matter for her.

She sat in the Order's forward camp two ridges east, in a tent that leaked, listening to the prisoner.

They'd taken him at Greywater Ford — a local man, hired blade, the sort who sold his sword to whoever paid and his tongue to whoever asked twice. Serah had questioned him herself, briefly and professionally, and then left him tied by the cookfire while she read the dispatches. It was Corporal Vess — young, earnest, the kind of soldier who still believed the creed was a shield rather than a hammer — who'd gotten the story out of him. Not through force. Through tea.

"The big one," Vess reported, crouching beside her. "The smuggler. Poisoned at Vell. The healer gave him a season. They're moving slow because of it. And—" he hesitated.

"And."

"He talked about his family, sir. When the fever was on him. Before we took him." Vess looked at the fire. "He's got no one. That's why he does it, I think. The running. Nothing to go back to."

Serah said nothing. She knew about nothing to go back to. She had built a career on the floor of it.

"Anything else?"

"One thing." Vess lowered his voice. "He said the knight-captain's name. Said she'd understand. Said—" he swallowed. "Said her sister's name. Maerin."

The cold went through Serah then, clean as a blade finding the gap in armor.

Maerin. Dead eleven years. Dead in a barn outside Callowmere with forty other worshippers of a god who had promised them mercy and delivered ash, because a god-touched prophet had decided the town's granary was an offering. Serah had been nineteen. She had identified her sister by a locket, because there had been nothing else left to identify.

The Order had found her a week later, sweeping the ruins for survivors. *All dragon-fire must die,* they told her, *so that no god may ever again command men to burn.* And she had believed it, because the alternative was believing that Maerin had died for nothing, and Serah Dain had never in her life been able to carry a weight that shapeless.

"Sir?" Vess said. "Are you well?"

"I'm deciding whether the prisoner lives," Serah said.

Vess blinked. "He's cooperated fully, sir."

"Yes. He has." She rose, pulled her cloak tight. "Feed him. Cut him loose at first light, south road, with rations. We're not butchers."

"But the captain-general's orders say—"

"The captain-general is three hundred miles away and warm." Serah looked north, toward the black ridge line where the fugitives' fire would be burning, small and stubborn as a held breath. *She'll understand,* the prisoner had said. As if understanding were a door anyone could walk through.

Her sister had understood things too, right up until the end. That had never saved anyone.

"Double the watch," she said. "We move at dawn."

* * *

**Bram**

Back at the camp, the histories continued, and it was Bram's turn, and she would have traded her sword arm to get out of it.

"Temple orphan," she said, when there was no way around it. "Shrine of Kestrel, Ashford Vale. You know the one."

"The silent god," Odile said. "The god who doesn't answer."

"That's the one." Bram turned her hands toward the fire. "My mother left me on the steps in winter. Left a blanket and nothing else — no name, no token, nothing to trace. The priests named me themselves. Bram, for the thornbush I was found in. Ashgrove, for the vale." She shrugged. "I was raised on prayer and porridge. Learned that Kestrel hears everything and says nothing, and that this is not abandonment — it's trust. The brothers used to say the god went quiet so that we'd have to find our own voices."

"Were they right?" Isa asked.

"I liked the porridge better than the theology." Which was true, and also a dodge, and Bram let it stand. "I stayed until I was sixteen. Then a mercenary company wintered in the vale, and their sergeant — a woman called Dree Halloway — taught me staff work in the temple yard, between services. First person who ever looked at me like I might be dangerous instead of devout. I signed on before the thaw."

"And the shrine?" Odile asked. "Do you go back?"

"Every year." Bram kept her voice level. "There's a wall behind the altar where the brothers let the children carve marks. One for every year. Mine stop at sixteen." She smiled, and it cost her. "I keep meaning to add another."

It was almost entirely a lie, and the weight of it sat in her chest like a stone swallowed whole.

Because here was the truth she could not say: the shrine had answered her. On the road, weeks ago, at the wayside altar — warmth rising through her palms like water through cloth, matching the map's glow beat for beat. And since then, other things. Dreams of wings made of ember-light. Waking with her blood singing in a rhythm she recognized from visions sealed inside a drowned chapel. The growing, unbearable suspicion that Kestrel had never gone silent at all — that the silence was a hiding place, and she was inside it.

*Only Kestrel's own blood can wake or quench it.* The third seal had said so plainly that even Rennick, feverish, had lifted his head and looked at her.

She had said nothing. Every day she said nothing, the saying-nothing got heavier, and every day the family got warmer around her — Isa's terrible soup, Odile's dry corrections, Rennick's card-sharping from under his blankets — until Bram felt like a woman holding a coal in her bare hand and smiling so no one would ask why she didn't set it down.

"Your turn to feel sorry for me," she said aloud. "Go on. I can wait."

"Poor little thornbush," Rennick said fondly, from his nest of blankets. "Dealt a tragic hand. Orphaned, devout, and pretty. Some of us suffered."

"You were born rich."

"I was born *charming*, which is worse. Nobody pities you." He tried to sit up, failed, and settled for looking dignified lying down. "Deal the cards, mercenary. If I'm to die of poison, I'll do it holding a winning hand out of spite."

* * *

**Rennick**

Later, when the others slept, Rennick lay awake and counted what was left of himself.

The river spirit's bargain had taken the sharp edges of his memory — his mother's face gone soft-focus, his brothers' voices reduced to a rumor of voices. What remained was the shape of love without the heat of it, and he had thought, in the first days, that this was the cruelest possible arrangement.

He had revised his opinion tonight, watching Isa tell her firefly story. Because grief with edges cuts you; grief without edges just sits in the chest like ballast, and ballast has its uses. He could hold her father's map-route knowledge steady now without flinching. He could remember the toll-stations, the dead-drop cairns, the ford where his own family had bled for this same secret — and he could teach it all to these stubborn, reckless people without drowning.

He owed the spirit a season. He intended to spend every hour of it.

Movement at the edge of the firelight. Bram, on last watch, standing very still, head tilted toward the treeline.

Rennick didn't call out. He simply slid his knife free of its sheath, silently, and waited.

After a long moment Bram relaxed, and he heard her murmur something — an apology, maybe, to whatever animal had cracked a branch. She glanced back at the camp, at the sleepers, and Rennick saw her face in the firelight for one unguarded second.

He had spent thirty years reading faces across smuggling tables. He knew guilt when he saw it. He knew the specific guilt of a person carrying a secret that was going to hurt someone they loved.

*Well, little mercenary,* he thought, easing the knife back home. *Whatever it is you're not telling us — the mountain's long, and secrets weigh more with altitude.*

He closed his eyes. Above the shelf of stone, the snow went on falling, patient as a debt coming due — and far to the east, unseen, torches of the Hollow Order's column began to move through the pass at first light, following a trail bought from a guide who had sold their route for silver and a head start.
