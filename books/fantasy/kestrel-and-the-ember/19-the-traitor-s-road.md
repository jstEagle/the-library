## 19. The Traitor's Road

Isa Varrow had learned three things about Odile Vane in the weeks since they'd become reluctant traveling companions: she could read dead languages the way other people read street signs, she could sleep sitting upright against a tree like a folded letter, and she was incapable of walking past a mushroom without delivering a lecture.

"That one," Odile said now, pointing her walking stick at a shelf of pale fungi clinging to a rotting log, "is called widow's ear. Boiled and strained, it makes a tincture that will stop a man's heart in under a minute. Also it tastes terrible, which I've always felt was nature's way of apologizing."

"Why would you know that?" Isa asked.

"Because I read it in a book, obviously. I haven't *made* any." A pause. "Recently."

Ahead of them on the trail, Bram Ashgrove turned around and walked backward for a few strides, which she did often enough that Isa suspected she'd been born facing the wrong direction and had simply decided not to let it slow her down. "If you two are going to discuss poisons," Bram said, "at least do it quietly. We're paying our guide to get us through this stretch without anyone knowing we exist."

Their guide was a man named Corvin Hale, hired two days ago in the last market town before the deep forest — a broad-shouldered, easy-smiling fellow who claimed to have trapped these woods since boyhood and quoted a fair price without haggling, which Rennick had said afterward was either the mark of an honest man or a very good liar, and that in his experience the second kind smiled wider. Corvin smiled very wide indeed.

He was up ahead now, chatting easily with Rennick, who rode beside him on the mule and was, as far as Isa could tell, conducting a running negotiation over the ownership of a strip of dried venison. Rennick's color had come back since the healer's work at Vell — not all of it, never all of it, but enough. The poison still sat in him like a debt. He tired early. He'd stopped telling stories about his family, though nobody had remarked on it, because everyone understood some silences were load-bearing.

Isa adjusted the strap of her pack and thought about her father.

She thought about him every day, but differently now. In the first weeks he'd been a wound — the map pressed into a stranger's hands, the arrows, the empty road home she'd found too late. Now he was more like a compass needle. He had carried his secret toward the Ember, and she was carrying it the rest of the way, and when she got there she intended to ask the fire itself why it had cost what it had cost.

"You're doing the face," Odile said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're writing a letter to someone who wronged you and rehearsing the opening line." Odile smiled faintly. "I used to do it to the archivists' council. Very satisfying. They never once heard me out."

"I'm not—" Isa started, then gave it up. "Fine. Yes. I'm doing the face."

"Good. Keep it pointed away from me."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow ringed by old pines, and Corvin built a fire that burned clean and low, the way a man builds a fire when he has done it ten thousand times and thinks about it zero. He told a story about a bear he'd once chased out of a smokehouse by throwing a wheel of cheese at it, and Rennick laughed so hard he had to sit down, and even Bram's mouth did the thing where it almost became a smile before she caught it and put it away.

Isa watched the guide across the flames and felt nothing in particular, which should have been her first warning. She had learned on the salt road that her instincts spoke quietly — not a shout but a flatness, a missing note. And something about Corvin Hale was missing a note. He was friendly the way a merchant was friendly. His eyes went to their packs a half-second before his words did.

She said nothing. She was still new here. The family — and she was beginning, guiltily, to think of them as that — had its own weather, and she didn't yet trust herself to read the sky.

Later, when the others slept, she took the first watch beside Bram, and the two of them sat shoulder to shoulder in the dark while the forest ticked and breathed.

"Can I ask you something?" Isa said.

"You can ask. Whether there's an answer depends on the question."

"When we get to the mountain. When this is done. What happens to us?"

Bram was quiet long enough that Isa thought she wasn't going to answer. Then: "I've spent my whole life guarding things for money. Roads, caravans, people. You finish the contract and you walk away, and that's the whole shape of it." She fed a twig to the fire. "This doesn't feel like a contract."

"No?"

"No. Contracts end. This feels more like..." She searched for it. "Like a debt being paid forward. My mother prayed to Kestrel every morning of her life and the god never answered her once, and I used to be angry about that on her behalf. Now I think maybe the answer was coming, and it came through me instead, and the least I can do is see it delivered."

It was the longest speech Isa had ever heard her make. She turned it over. "You sound like you've already decided how it ends."

"I've decided how *I* end," Bram said. "The rest is negotiable."

Isa wanted to press — there was something underneath that sentence, something Bram had been carrying since the drowned chapel, some weight she set down carefully whenever anyone looked — but the watch turned, and the moment passed the way moments do, unremarked and unrecoverable.

* * *

Corvin Hale led them off the main track the next morning, onto a narrower path that wound down through fern-brakes toward the sound of water.

"There's a bridge," he explained cheerfully, "over the Greywash. Old logging span. Saves us two days of fording, and the ford's high with meltwater besides. Only soul who knows about it is me and the crows."

Rennick, riding behind him, caught Isa's eye and mouthed something that might have been *only soul who knows* with heavy irony attached. But the path was good and the day was fine and no one wanted to be the one to say *turn around* based on a feeling and a smile.

The trees opened. The river ran quick and white below, and the bridge stood across it — a genuine relic, iron-bolted timber gone silver with age, swaying gently in the wind like something asleep.

And on the far side of it, men were waiting.

Isa saw them the same instant Bram did. Six figures in grey-and-white, unhurried, arranged along the bridgehead with the casual confidence of people who had arrived early and expected their guests. At their center stood a woman in enameled plate, helm under one arm, hair cropped short — and Isa knew her by reputation before Bram said the name through her teeth.

"Dain."

Serah Dain looked across the water at them and inclined her head, almost courteously. Her voice carried over the river, clear as a bell in cold air.

"Bram Ashgrove. Scholar. Smuggler." Her gaze found Isa and stayed. "Daughter of Tomas Varrow. You are surrounded on three sides. Lay down your arms and the scholar and the girl walk free. That offer expires when my patience does, which is soon."

"Corvin," Rennick said softly, behind them. "Where's Corvin?"

But Isa had already seen it — the guide standing apart from them, twenty paces back up the trail, arms crossed, watching the bridge with the expression of a man watching a business transaction conclude. Not ashamed. Not frightened. Just *done*, the way a farmer is done at harvest.

"You sold us," she said. It came out flat. Strange. Her voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing slightly to her left.

Corvin shrugged. "Sold your route, girl, not your throats. Dain pays better than you do, and pays whether you live or die, which is more than I can say for most employers." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Nothing personal. You seem nice."

"Nothing personal," Rennick repeated, dismounting slowly, hand drifting toward his belt. "That's a lovely sentiment. I'll carve it on your headstone."

"Rennick." Bram's voice cut like a blade laid flat against skin. She hadn't taken her eyes off the bridge. "How many?"

Dain answered for the forest. "Twelve in the trees behind you. Six at the bridge. Two archers you won't find until they want finding." She stepped forward onto the span, boots ringing on the old timbers. "I am not my Order's masters, Ashgrove. I don't enjoy waste. Give me the map and this ends with everyone breathing."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then the bridge burns with you on it, or the trees give you up, or the river takes you. One way or another the day ends the same, and only the arithmetic changes."

Isa's heart was going hard now, finally, belatedly — her body catching up to what her mind had known since the moment she'd seen the grey coats. Twelve in the trees. Twenty men against four, and one of the four poisoned and one of the four a scholar whose deadliest weapon was citation.

Bram's hand found hers. Squeezed once. And Isa understood, without a word being spoken, that Bram was calculating exactly the same arithmetic and arriving at exactly the same answer, and that the answer was *no*.

"We refuse," Bram called back.

Dain sighed, genuinely regretful. "So be it. Loose—"

The world came apart.

* * *

Afterward, Isa could never assemble those minutes into order. Memory gave them back to her in pieces, sharp-edged and unrelated, like a dropped plate.

An arrow out of the fern-brake, taking Rennick through the meat of his shoulder as he spun — spinning anyway, already moving, dragging Odile down behind the mule. The mule screaming. Fire blooming in the dry bracken where Odile, hands shaking, had crushed something from her pouch and thrown it, and the green world lit orange. Bram wading into the treeline like weather, like a force with a name, her sword doing work Isa's eyes refused to follow.

And Corvin Hale, turning to run up the trail with his payment waiting somewhere beyond the ridge —

Isa went after him.

She didn't decide to. Later she would search that moment for a decision and find none, just her legs moving and her father's knife — his knife, the one she'd carried since the ford, since the beginning — suddenly in her hand as if it had always been there and her whole life had merely been the approach.

"Girl!" Corvin glanced back and his easy smile was gone, replaced by something older and more honest: fear. "Girl, wait — I never touched you! Nothing personal, remember? Nothing—"

He tripped on a root. She was on him before he finished falling, and everything after that happened very fast and also took years.

She remembered his weight. She remembered thinking, absurdly, that he smelled like woodsmoke and cheese, like campfires and the bear story, like two days of almost-family. She remembered the knife going in and how little resistance there was, how the body was just — a door, and the blade a key, and how no one had ever told her that. She remembered his hand scrabbling at her wrist, not fighting, just holding, the way you hold someone's arm at a funeral.

She remembered that his last word was not a curse or a plea but her own name, or the word *girl*, she couldn't be sure which, and that either way it sounded surprised.

Then it was done, and she was kneeling in the ferns with red on her hands and her breath coming in small, mechanical pieces, and the sounds of the battle behind her seemed to arrive from a great distance, through water, through years.

*Isa.*

Bram's voice. Bram's hand on her shoulder, warm and solid and real. There was blood on Bram's cheek that did not appear to be hers. Behind her, the bridge was burning — Odile's work, alchemical and thorough, cutting the Order's six off from crossing — and the twelve in the trees had broken, because soldiers will break when the ambush becomes a fight and the fight becomes a fire, and Serah Dain's voice was ringing commands through the smoke that grew fewer and farther between.

"Isa. Look at me."

She looked at Bram. Bram's eyes were steady and dark and full of something Isa recognized and hated: pity.

"He sold us," Isa said. Her voice worked. That was the horrible part. Everything worked. "He was going to sell the next ones too. He said nothing personal."

"I know."

"So why do I feel like this?"

Bram crouched down beside her, heedless of the blood, and took both of Isa's hands in hers — the knife-hand too, the red one — and held them.

"Because you were never supposed to learn this," Bram said. "Nobody is. Anyone who tells you it gets easier is selling something." A pause. "It doesn't get easier. It gets *smaller*. You carry it, and every year it weighs the same and you weigh more, and one day you look down and realize you can barely feel it. That's not healing. That's just... room."

Isa nodded, or her body did. Somewhere behind them Rennick was swearing with tremendous creativity about his shoulder, and Odile was answering him in the tone of a woman reading dosage instructions aloud, and the forest was settling back into its ordinary ticking and breathing as if twenty men and a burning bridge had been a passing shower.

"Can you stand?" Bram asked.

"Yes," Isa said, and discovered it was true.

She stood. She wiped her hands on the ferns, mechanically, uselessly. She picked up her father's knife, cleaned it the way he had taught her to clean it when she was nine years old and the blade had only ever seen fish, and sheathed it.

Something had closed in her. She could feel it — a door swinging shut on a room she couldn't live in anymore. The girl who had ambushed a company of strangers on the salt road demanding her father's map, all fury and grief and no blood on her hands — that girl had been standing on the far side of a line, shouting across it. Isa was on this side now. The air was different here. Colder. Clearer.

And underneath the numbness, quiet as banked coals, something else had hardened into place. Not rage. Rage was hot and stupid and spent itself. This was colder and would last longer.

Her father had died carrying a secret to a fire at the top of the world. The Order had killed him for it and called it righteousness. Well. She would carry the secret the rest of the way, and she would put it where it belonged, and anyone standing between her and that mountain — grey knights, smiling guides, gods themselves — was going to learn what a daughter with nothing left to lose weighed.

"Isa." Odile appeared at her elbow, pale, soot-streaked, holding a strip of cloth she'd evidently torn from her own hem for Rennick's shoulder and then forgotten to apply. "Are you hurt?"

"No," Isa said.

Odile studied her for a long moment with those archivist's eyes that catalogued everything and judged slowly. "No," she agreed softly. "You're not. That's rather the problem, isn't it."

* * *

They moved fast after that, deeper into the forest, away from the roads, because Dain would regroup and Dain did not strike Isa as a woman who lost gracefully twice. Rennick walked with an arrow-shaft snapped off short in his shoulder and complained about it in a continuous low mutter that Isa slowly realized was his way of keeping himself upright, and possibly of keeping all of them upright too.

By dusk they reached higher ground, a fold of the hills where the pines gave way to bare granite shoulders, and made a cold camp — no fire, not tonight — beneath an overhang that smelled of stone and old snow.

Bram sat apart, as she often did, staring north toward a horizon Isa couldn't read but Bram clearly could. Toward the mountain. Toward whatever she'd meant on the night watch, when she'd said *I've decided how I end*.

Isa went and sat beside her. Neither of them spoke for a while. The numbness was still there, a smooth cold place inside her, but Bram's shoulder against hers was warm, and warmth, she was learning, was a thing you could lean on even when you couldn't feel it.

"The third seal," Isa said at last. "The map showed a pass above the Greywash headwaters. We're close, aren't we."

Bram was silent a moment too long.

"Isa," she said, "when we open it — whatever it shows — I need you to remember that I didn't know. What's coming. I need you to believe that."

And Isa, whose hands still smelled of blood despite the ferns and the cold stream she'd scrubbed them in, looked at the woman she trusted more than anyone left alive, and heard the thing underneath the words — the weight Bram kept setting down whenever anyone looked — and felt the first thin crack run through the numbness, though she could not yet have said whether it was fear or foreknowledge.

"Why?" Isa asked. "What do you know?"

Above them, far to the north, past the black teeth of the ridgeline, the sky over Mount Sarn flickered — once, faintly, like a coal breathed upon — and went dark again.

Only Bram saw it. Only Bram went still.
