## 20. The Third Seal

The mountain pass had a name in the old survey maps — Odile called it the Throat of Sarn — but the people who lived in the valley below it had a better one: the Place Where the Wind Argues With Itself.

Bram could hear why by the time they made camp at the mouth of it. The wind came down off the heights in two currents, one warm and one cold, and where they met above the scree they fought like an old married couple, gusting and sulking and gusting again. It made a sound almost like speech. Almost like someone muttering just out of earshot, saying something important that you'd missed.

She'd been hearing things like that for three days now.

"You're doing the face," Isa said, dropping an armful of scrub-pine beside the fire ring. "The face where you're listening to something we can't hear."

"I'm listening to the wind."

"The wind doesn't make people look constipated."

"It's a very argumentative wind." Bram fed a branch to the flames and kept her eyes on it. Across the fire, Rennick lay propped against his saddle with a blanket around his shoulders like a disreputable king, watching her with the lazy attention of a man who noticed everything and pretended to notice nothing. The poison was still in him. The river spirit's bargain still held him up. But there were hours now — she'd started counting them — when his color went gray and his jokes came a half-beat late, as if he were translating them from somewhere far away.

"Odile," Rennick said mildly. "Your scholar's coming back. And she's got the map out already. In this wind. If it goes over the cliff I'm not chasing it."

"She won't drop it," Bram said.

"She dropped a whole jar of pickled eels at the last ford."

"That," said Odile's voice from the dark, "was sabotage, and we have all agreed never to speak of it again." She came into the firelight with her coat buttoned wrong and the map case hugged to her chest, and behind her the wind argued on, and Bram felt the thing under her sternum lean toward the case like a dog hearing its name.

The map had been glowing more often lately. Faintly, at first — a warmth in the scorched vellum that Odile swore up and down was her imagination, right up until the night it lit the inside of the tent like a paper lantern and Isa sat bolt upright yelling about fire. Now it glowed whenever Bram came within ten feet of it. Odile had taken to carrying it wrapped in three layers of oilcloth, which helped, which meant everyone knew exactly what the oilcloth was hiding, which helped not at all.

"Tonight," Odile said, without preamble, sitting down cross-legged and beginning to unwrap it. "The third seal. The pass is right. The map's been pulling north for two days; you've all felt it pull. And I would rather open this in the company of friends than have it open itself in the middle of the night and give Rennick another heart episode."

"That was one time—"

"Two times."

Bram looked at the fire. *Kestrel's own blood.* She'd known, since the second seal in the mountains, what the visions were building toward. She'd known the way you know a tooth is going bad — a low constant ache you can talk yourself around, right up until the moment you bite down.

Isa settled across from her, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them. Her father's knife rode her belt now instead of her boot; she'd moved it after Greywater, after Odile came back to them thin and burned and stubborn, and Bram understood that the knife had become a kind of promise. Isa caught her looking and didn't smile, but she didn't look away either. That was new. That was maybe everything.

"All right," Bram said. "Open it."

* * *

The drowned chapel had taught them the ritual: blood on the seal, and the map did the rest. Odile had refined it since — a single drop, pricked from the thumb with a clean needle, offered flat on the palm rather than smeared, because, as she put it, "the first time we essentially assaulted a piece of vellum and it responded accordingly."

Bram volunteered her thumb before anyone else could. Nobody commented. Everybody noticed.

The drop hit the wax seal and sank in like rain into drought-cracked earth, and the map woke.

It wasn't light, exactly. It was more like heat made visible — a slow bloom of amber spreading out from the third seal across the whole web of roads and rivers and dead gods' names, until the entire map hung in the air an inch above the oilcloth, burning gently, casting no smoke. The wind stopped arguing. All at once, both currents went still, and the silence rang worse than the noise.

Then the vision took them.

Bram had learned to recognize the feel of it — the lurch, like a stair that isn't where your foot expects — but knowing didn't soften it. The firelight snapped away and they stood somewhere else: a plain of black glass under a sky the color of a bruise, and on the horizon, a mountain. Not Mount Sarn as it must look now, green-shouldered or snow-capped or whatever it had become in the long centuries. This mountain was a wound. Its top was simply gone, sheared away, and from the ragged caldera rose a column of fire so vast it bent the sky around it, and the fire was not orange or gold but white, white as the center of a forge, white as the eye of a god.

Figures stood on the glass plain. Thousands of them. Armies, or what had been armies — ranks of soldiers facing ranks of things that might once have been dragons, except these were burned down to their essentials, wings like torn sails, throats still glowing. Between the two lines, walking alone, went a figure in gray, and though the vision gave no faces, Bram knew the walk. She'd seen it every morning of her childhood in the temple mirrors. She knew the set of those shoulders, the way the figure carried its hands slightly open at its sides, palms forward, the way you approach something you mean to greet and not fight.

*Kestrel,* she thought, and the word went through her like a bell through water.

The gray figure walked between the armies and raised its hands, and the fire on the mountain leaned toward it — actually leaned, the whole impossible column bending like wheat in wind — and then the vision compressed, fast-forwarded, centuries grinding past in heartbeats. The armies thinned. The dragons guttered. The mountain's fire shrank from a pillar to a torch to a lamp to — and here the vision slowed, tenderly, the way you slow down for the last page of a letter —

— to a coal. A single coal, cupped in gray hands, carried up into the high places of the world and set down in the deep of a caldera, and left there, and warded, and sealed, and the sealing was done by blood, and the words rose up out of the vision in a voice that was not a voice, that was warmth itself given grammar:

*Here lies the Ember. Here it sleeps, and sleeping, holds the war shut. Let none wake it, let none quench it, save Kestrel's own blood.*

The vision let go.

Bram came back to herself sitting in the dirt with her palm still open and four people staring at her, and the map lying quiet again, and the wind resuming its argument somewhere overhead as if embarrassed to have been caught eavesdropping.

"Well," said Rennick, into the silence. His voice was carefully light. "That's a mountain I never want to visit."

"That's Mount Sarn," Odile whispered. Her hands were shaking; she pressed them flat against her knees. "The caldera. The third seal gives us the place. Do you understand? Two hundred years of archivists, and I'm the first living soul to know where—" She stopped herself, breathed. "Sorry. Yes. The resting place. We have it."

"And the rest of it," Isa said quietly.

Nobody answered her right away. The fire crackled. Somewhere up the pass, loose stone clattered down a slope and went silent.

"*Kestrel's own blood,*" Isa repeated. She wasn't looking at the map. She was looking at Bram. "Wake or quench. Only that blood can do either. So whoever carries that blood — they're not just the key to the door. They're the only hand that can turn it. Either direction."

"Or it's symbolic," Rennick offered. "Old religions love symbolic blood. Blood of the lamb, blood of the covenant, blood of — whatever. Half the rites in the free cities are blood this and blood that, and nobody's ever bled anything but a chicken."

"Do you believe that?" Isa asked him.

Rennick was quiet for a moment. He pulled the blanket tighter around his shoulders, and when he spoke again the laziness was gone out of his voice. "No," he said. "I bargained with a river spirit for my life, Isa. I don't get to pretend the old words are decoration anymore." He looked at the map, then at Bram, and his gaze rested on her half a heartbeat too long, and Bram felt the floor of the evening tilt. "I think it means what it says."

"Then one of us," Odile said slowly, working it through the way she worked through anything, aloud and relentless, "is more important than the rest of us. To the Order, certainly — they'll want to kill anyone who could wake the Ember. To the Ember itself, possibly. To whatever happens at that mountain." She frowned. "We should think about who—"

"It could be any of us," Bram said.

Her voice came out flatter than she intended. Four faces turned to her.

"We don't know anything about the bloodlines involved," she went on, and heard herself building the sentence brick by brick, careful and reasonable, the way you build a wall. "The stranger who died at the ford — Isa's father — he carried the map for years, maybe generations back in his family. Rennick's family guarded the route. Odile's spent her life on dragon-lore. Any of us could carry it. That's the sensible reading."

It was a good speech. It was even mostly true. And she watched it land wrong anyway, watched Isa's eyes narrow a fraction, watched Odile go still in the particular way she went still when she was filing something away instead of arguing with it, and Bram understood that she had just lied to her family badly enough that the lie itself was now evidence.

"Sensible," Isa said. "Right."

"Bram." Odile's voice was gentle, which was somehow worse. "At the second seal, when the vision showed Kestrel pouring itself into mortal lines — you said afterward that you felt faint. You said it was the altitude. We were at eight hundred feet."

"The air was thin."

"Bram."

"I'm tired," Bram said, and stood up, and picked up her bedroll, and heard how it sounded — how it sounded exactly like a woman running — and did it anyway. "I'll take first watch. Sleep."

She walked to the edge of the camp where the scree began, and sat with her back to a boulder, and put her face in her hands.

* * *

The wind argued all through her watch. She barely heard it.

This was the trouble with secrets, she thought. She'd always imagined them as things you carried — heavy, yes, but portable, contained, yours. Nobody had told her that a secret grew. That it put down roots through the days until it wasn't a thing you carried anymore but a thing you *were*, a mass in the chest that shifted its weight when you laughed at Rennick's jokes, when you corrected Isa's grip on her blade, when Odile asked you a direct question and you chose the answer that kept the wall standing.

Because that was the other thing nobody told you about secrets: they weren't passive. Every day she kept this one, she made a hundred small choices to keep it, and each choice was a small theft. She'd stolen Isa's right to grieve honestly — because how could Isa mourn a father who died carrying a secret, when the woman he'd trusted it to had known its shape all along? She'd stolen Odile's scholarship, her one great truth, the vindication of her ruined career — dragons lived, the god endured, and the proof slept every night ten feet from her bedroll and wouldn't say so. She'd stolen Rennick's right to decide what his remaining season was *for*.

And underneath all of it, colder: she'd stolen her own choice too. Because if she said it aloud, she couldn't unsay it, and then she would have to be the thing the vision said she was, and some cowardly cellar of her heart still hoped that if she never named it, it might stay untrue.

The eastern sky hadn't even begun to think about graying when she heard footsteps on the scree. Deliberate ones. Isa walked like she'd been taught to walk quiet and had decided, lately, to stop bothering.

"Can't sleep either?" Bram said.

"I slept fine." Isa sat down on the rock beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and stared out at the dark bulk of the pass. For a while she said nothing. Then: "My father used to hum when he thought I was asleep. Some old tune from wherever our family came from. I used to lie there pretending to sleep so he'd keep humming." Her jaw tightened. "I keep thinking — he knew. Whatever the map was, whatever the blood was, he knew enough to die running with it. And he never told me. He sent me away and carried it alone, and I hated him a little for that, for deciding what I could bear. And now I'm sitting next to someone else who's decided what I can bear, and I hate it a little too, and I don't even know yet if I'm right to."

Bram's chest hurt. "Isa—"

"I'm not asking you to tell me anything." Isa held up a hand. "Whatever it is — if there's anything — it's yours to say and yours to keep. That's what I came to say. But I want you to know that I'll know eventually. One way or another, on that mountain, everybody finds out what they are. My father found out. You'll find out. And I'd rather hear it from you, ugly and early, than from a burning map, pretty and too late." She stood, brushed off her trousers, and paused. "Also, your watch ended twenty minutes ago and Odile's up, and she makes that tea that tastes like boiled chalk when she's worried, so you have about ninety seconds before the whole camp is awake and miserable."

She walked back toward the fire. Bram stayed where she was a moment longer, looking up at the pass — at the Throat of Sarn, black against the stars, the road they'd have to climb tomorrow, the road that led to a caldera and a coal and a choice.

*Kestrel's own blood.*

Under her sternum, the warmth pulsed once, patient as a heartbeat, like something that had waited a very long time and could wait a little longer — but not forever.

Behind her, she heard Odile put the kettle on.
