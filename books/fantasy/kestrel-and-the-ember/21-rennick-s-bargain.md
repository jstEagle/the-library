## 21. Rennick's Bargain

The poison was polite about it, at first.

Rennick Cael had been stabbed, shot with arrows, thrown from a barge, and once — a story he told only when very drunk and never the same way twice — bitten by a swan. He knew how bodies complained. This was different. This was a cold that started in the wound on his side and spread outward like frost climbing a window, and no amount of brandy, which Odile had stopped allowing him anyway, put it off.

"Nightshade-root," Odile said, on the third day, in the flat tone she used for facts she wished weren't. "Distilled. Whoever coated that blade wanted you dead twice over."

"And did they succeed?" Rennick asked, from the pile of blankets that had been, until recently, his hammock aboard the *Kettle-Witch*. They'd made camp in a riverbend two days south of Vell, because Isa had read the healer's face and Bram had read Isa's, and nobody had said the word *dying* out loud, which in Rennick's professional experience meant everyone was thinking it very loudly indeed.

"You'll live to see winter," the healer had said. "You won't live to see the thaw."

Rennick had thanked her, paid her double, and flirted with her on principle, and she'd laughed, which he counted as a victory, because a man ought to leave a room the same way he entered it.

Now he lay in the bend of the Greywater with the fever talking to him and the river talking back.

* * *

The Greywater ran slow there, brown-green and patient, the kind of river that had been going about its business since before anyone thought to name it. Rennick's mother had been riverborn — half-fae of the shallow folk, the kind who traded in small favors and smaller grudges — and she'd taught him the courtesies before she taught him letters. You never spat upstream. You thanked the water for carrying your boat. And if you ever needed something truly large, you went to the bend where the current doubled back on itself, because that was where the river kept its oldest self.

He crawled there on the third night, when the frost-cold reached his shoulder, and he did not tell the others, because Bram would have carried him back to camp over his objections and Isa would have cried, and Odile would have started composing a treatise, and he could not abide being a treatise.

The bend was quiet. The moon laid a silver road down the middle of the current, and Rennick, who had smuggled contraband past three navies and one very persistent customs wyrm, recognized a toll road when he saw one.

"Evening," he said to the water, and sat down on the mud like a man settling in at a card table, which cost him something, because the fever had made sitting a negotiation. "I'd like to open an account."

The river said nothing, the way rivers do, and then it said everything, the way rivers do when they decide to stop being polite.

The cold rose out of the water and stood before him. It had no shape he could hold onto — it was the memory of every drowned thing and every floated thing, every secret a river had carried and kept, and it regarded Rennick Cael with the mild interest of a banker reviewing a very small account.

*You are dying,* it observed. Not unkindly. Rivers are never unkind; they are simply accurate.

"Peritonitis of the personality," Rennick agreed. "The healers give me until thaw. I was hoping to negotiate an extension."

*On what collateral?*

"Ah." Rennick rubbed his hands together, which was a habit, and winced, which was the poison. "There's the rub. I'm a smuggler by trade. My assets are liquid, if you'll pardon the term, and currently being repossessed by nightshade-root. But I understand you deal in a different currency."

The cold waited. Rivers are good at waiting. It is practically their whole profession.

"Memories," Rennick said.

* * *

Here is what Rennick Cael knew about memory, and he knew more than most: it was the one cargo he'd never managed to smuggle. He could move anything across any border — stolen relics, fugitive scholars, half a tonne of black pepper once, and a bride, though that was a different story — but a memory went where it pleased and weighed exactly what it weighed and could not be bribed, because it had already been paid.

His family had died eleven years ago. The Order called it a cleansing; the survivors called it the Burning of the Ferrow; Rennick called it nothing, because he had learned that some names were too heavy to lift daily. His mother, who had taught him the river courtesies. His brother Tam, who cheated worse at cards than Rennick did and had the scars to prove other people noticed. His sister Wren, who was seven, and who had once spent an entire summer convincing the neighborhood that she could speak to ducks, and who had, in fairness, been right.

He carried them everywhere. That was the thing about the dead — they didn't take up room, exactly, but they took up *all* the room, in the way a single stone can fill an entire boot.

*One season,* the river said. *Spring, summer, autumn. The frost will take you when the frost takes the water.*

"And the price?"

*You know the price. You named it.*

"Be specific," Rennick said, because he'd haggled with spirits before and the cardinal rule was: never let them set the units. "Memories of them. All of them? Or —"

The cold tilted, considering. Rivers, he remembered his mother saying, are honest the way water is honest — it finds every crack.

*The price is what they weigh to you. The river takes the weight. What remains is what remains.*

"That's not a price, that's poetry."

*The river does not do arithmetic,* said the cold, with something that was almost, in a drowned and ancient way, a smile. *The river does tides.*

Rennick sat with that. Somewhere upstream an owl asked its one question and got no answer, which Rennick found relatable.

He thought about the arithmetic, though. That was the trap in it, and he saw the trap, and he walked toward it anyway, because that was also Rennick Cael: a man who could see a trap perfectly well and would go through it if the toll was right. If he paid everything, he'd live a season as a stranger to the only people he'd ever loved — a hollow man walking around with their names in his mouth and nothing behind them. If he paid too little, the river would take him at the first hard frost, and there were things he still needed to do. There was a map, and a mountain, and a girl named Isa Varrow whose father had died with Rennick's family's route in his hands, and a scholar who'd taken a poisoned blade that was meant for her, and a stubborn mercenary who prayed to a silent god like it could hear her.

He owed them. Not in the way smugglers owe each other, which is a ledger with numbers. In the other way. The heavy way.

"Counteroffer," Rennick said. "I keep their names. I keep their faces. I keep — " his voice did something he hadn't authorized — "I keep the duck thing. Wren and the ducks. You can have the rest. The burning, the grief, the — the nights. All the nights. Take the nights."

The cold was silent a long moment. The moon-road on the water shivered.

*You wish to keep the shape of them and surrender the weight.*

"I wish to keep the good bits. I'm a smuggler. That's the entire job."

*And you understand that the good bits and the weight are the same cargo? That the duck story is heavy because she is gone, and light because she lived, and you cannot cut one from the other without cutting both?*

"I understand," Rennick said, "that you're trying to talk me into a bigger sale, and I've been on the other side of that conversation my whole life, so save your breath." A pause. "Metaphorically."

The river laughed. It was a terrible sound — the sound water makes going over a fall in the dark, the sound of a thing vast and patient being amused — and Rennick's fever-addled bones understood that he had just done something either very brave or very stupid, and that the difference would be settled later, by someone else, in a season he was buying.

*Terms,* the river said. *You will keep their faces. You will keep their names. You will keep one story of your choosing, whole. The rest — the burning, the years, the weight of them — comes to the water. In exchange: one season. You will live until the first frost that locks the Greywater, and not one hour past. And Rennick Cael —* the cold drew close, and it smelled of deep places, of silt and sunken things — *you will not speak of this bargain. Not to your friends. Not to a priest. Not to the river itself, once it is done. A toll paid is a toll forgotten by the toll-keeper. That is the courtesy.*

"Mother's rules," Rennick said. His mouth was dry, which was funny, sitting where he was sitting. "Never spit upstream. Always thank the water."

*Then thank the water.*

Rennick looked at the moon-road a long time. He thought about the nights — the eleven years of nights, the waking at the hour his family had died, the way grief sat in a man like ballast and kept him steady and kept him sunk, both at once, forever. He thought about the season. One more season was not much. But it was a spring, and a summer, and an autumn, and Isa still didn't know how to hold a blade properly, and Odile still hadn't finished explaining the second seal, and Bram still prayed to a god that Rennick was beginning to suspect had been standing right next to her the whole time wearing her face.

"Take the nights," he said. "Keep the days."

*Done,* said the river.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the obscenity of it. He had expected tearing, a ripping-out, some grand agony worthy of the transaction. Instead the water simply rose — not over him, but *through* him, gentle as tide coming into a cave, and where it touched, things went quiet.

He felt the Burning of the Ferrow go first. Not the fact of it — the fact stayed, a line in a ledger, *the Ferrow burned and my family died in it* — but the weight of it, the eleven years of it, the nights. He felt them lift out of him like silt into current, and he watched them go, and he understood, in the last moment he could still understand it, that he was watching the heaviest part of himself float away, and he was grateful, and the gratitude went too.

Then Wren and the ducks — he held that one with both hands, the way you hold a lantern in wind, and the water passed around it, respectful, because a bargain is a bargain.

Then his mother's face. He kept the face. He kept the name — *Maren* — and he kept that she had been riverborn and that she had taught him the courtesies. The rest went: her voice, her temper, the way she'd hum when she mended nets, the smell of her, the particular shape of the hole she'd left. Tam's face stayed. Tam's laughter went. Wren's face stayed, and the ducks stayed, and everything else — the seven years of her, the whole seven years, the weight of a child — went into the Greywater, which received it the way the Greywater received everything, without comment, without cruelty, without giving it back.

The last thing to leave was not a memory at all. It was smaller than that. It was the knowledge of what he had just done — the *shape* of the bargain, the fact of the price. He felt the river take that too, gently, like a pickpocket lifting a man's own hand from his own pocket.

And then Rennick Cael sat on the mud in the moonlight, alive, fevered still but no longer dying, and he could not remember why he had come to the river, and he could not remember why he was crying, and the tears kept coming anyway, because the river takes the weight but the body keeps its habits, and his body had eleven years of habit in it and no idea anymore what the habit was for.

He thanked the water. He didn't know why. It seemed polite.

* * *

He woke in camp at midmorning, and the first thing he noticed was that the frost-cold in his side had stopped climbing. The second thing he noticed was Odile's face, which had the expression of a woman who had spent the night preparing a eulogy and was now deeply annoyed to have wasted it.

"You're," she said.

"Alive," Rennick agreed. "I know. I'm as surprised as you are. Delighted, obviously, but surprised."

"The healer said —"

"The healer," Rennick said, "underestimated me. It happens. I'm a complicated man." He sat up, and it cost him, but it cost him the way sitting up should cost a sick man, not the way it had been costing him, which was the way it costs a man walking into the sea.

Bram came over with that soldier's walk of hers, the one that pretended not to hurry. She looked at him the way she looked at terrain she didn't trust. "You're gray," she said.

"I'm always gray. It's the fae blood. Very fashionable."

"You were dying yesterday."

"And today I'm not. Bram. Light of my mercenary heart. Some men get better. It's in all the ballads." He grinned at her, and the grin worked, mostly, the way it always worked, and she let it go, the way she always let it go, and he noted with a strange floating feeling that he could not remember why he'd gone to the river, only that he had, only that there was mud on his knees and a smooth river-stone in his pocket that he had no memory of picking up, gray-green and water-worn, which he found himself holding onto the way a man holds a coin from a country that no longer exists.

Isa brought him soup. That was the thing about Isa — she'd spent two weeks being furious at the world, and somewhere on the salt road the fury had quietly turned into feeding people, which Rennick privately considered the most dangerous transformation of all, because you couldn't fight someone who kept handing you bread.

"Eat," she said. "You look terrible."

"I look *convalescent*. There's a difference. Convalescent men get soup brought to them. Terrible men have to forage."

"Then eat it and stop talking."

He ate it. And here was the thing he noticed, the thing that sat in him like the smooth stone in his pocket: the soup was good, and he was glad, and the gladness had nothing underneath it. It was gladness all the way down, with no cellar. When he looked at Isa's scowling, careful face, he felt warmth — and beneath the warmth, where a memory should have been, there was just a smooth place, like a stone worn blank by water. He knew her father had died. He knew he'd liked the man, or thought he had. He could not remember the man's face, or the night they'd met, or why Rennick had once, for reasons now gone, drunk himself sick over it.

The weight was gone. The river had been true to its word. And Rennick Cael, professional smuggler, discovered that a man can lose a cargo and still feel it gone — feel the absence of the absence, the way an amputated limb itches in the snow.

"You're staring at nothing," Isa said.

"Practicing," Rennick said. "It's a discipline. You wouldn't understand. You're young."

He laughed, and they laughed, and the camp settled into its evening shapes — Odile muttering over the map, Bram sharpening a blade that was already sharp, Isa pretending not to watch Bram sharpen it so she could learn. And Rennick sat by the fire feeling lighter than he had in eleven years, and hollowed to match, like a reed, like a boat with no ballast, riding high on the water and terribly easy to tip.

He reached into his pocket and found the smooth stone again, and held it, and did not know why his eyes burned, and decided — because he was still Rennick Cael, whatever the river had left of him — that some cargo you carry without asking what's inside.

It was three days later, at the ferry crossing below the gorge, when Odile came to him with her arms crossed and the map's second seal copied onto a scrap of paper, and said, in the tone she used for facts she wished weren't:

"Rennick. The river spirit at this bend — the old texts say the Greywater keeps one at every doubling of the current. And the texts also say what such spirits trade in." Her eyes were very sharp and very kind, which was the worst combination in a scholar. "What did you pay?"

Rennick opened his mouth to say *nothing*, and found — to his genuine astonishment, the first real surprise he'd felt since waking lighter — that he couldn't. The words wouldn't come. The courtesy held, even now, even from inside.

He smiled at her instead, that grin that worked mostly, and said, "Odile. Light of my scholarly heart. Ask me anything but that."

And behind her, at the edge of the firelight, Bram had gone very still, the way she went still when she'd heard something in the silence that other people couldn't — and her hand had drifted, not to her blade, but to the little scorched map at her belt, as if the map and the river and the man were all suddenly part of the same question.
