## 22. Serah Turns the Screw

Odile Vane had spent eleven years perfecting the art of being underestimated, and it had never once occurred to her that the skill would be tested quite like this.

They took her at Greywater Ford, in the space of four heartbeats. One moment she was haggling with a fishwife over the price of dried eel — an outrage at any figure, frankly — and the next there were hands on her arms, a hood over her head, and a voice saying *the scholar, take the scholar* as though she were a parcel of turnips.

She had time to think, absurdly, of Bram's face when Odile had insisted on going into town alone. *You're the most conspicuous person I've ever met,* Bram had said. *You argue with shopkeepers about marginalia.*

*I argue with shopkeepers about prices,* Odile had corrected. *Marginalia only when provoked.*

Well. She had been provoked now, hadn't she.

The hood smelled of lamp oil and old wool. Someone marched her forward by the elbow with a grip that suggested they'd been trained on livestock, and she counted her steps out of habit — forty-one to a cart, the rattle of wheels, the particular hollow sound of a bridge with loose planking that she'd noticed yesterday and mentioned, aloud, to no one who'd listened. She catalogued turns. Left, left, the long straight stretch along the water, right where the cobbles gave way to packed earth. It was not much. But a mind was the one thing no one could confiscate, and hers was very well stocked.

The cart stopped. The hood came off in a room that smelled of ink and cold iron, and Odile blinked against candlelight and thought: *of course.* A repurposed counting-house. Ledgers still stacked along one wall. A table bolted to the floor in the center, because someone here understood that furniture could be a weapon if you let it move.

And standing by the window with her gauntlets off and her hands folded like a woman at a funeral she respected, Serah Dain.

"Archivist," Serah said.

"Knight-captain." Odile brushed dust from her sleeve with great dignity. "Your men took my spectacles. Second pair this year. I want it noted that I hold the Hollow Order financially responsible."

"They'll be returned. Along with you, if this goes the way I'd prefer." Serah gestured to a chair — a plain one, set well back from the bolted table, which Odile noted with a scholar's precision. The table was for later. Or for someone else. "Sit. Please."

*Please.* The word sat strangely in the knight-captain's mouth, like a coin of foreign minting. Odile sat anyway. Her knees were less principled than the rest of her.

"I'll be direct," Serah said. "I don't enjoy what my order does to people who withhold from us. I've seen it done. I've ordered it done twice, and I remember both their faces." She said it without flourish, without apology, the way one reports weather. "So I'm offering you the version of this evening where none of that happens. You tell me where Ashgrove is taking the map, and you walk out of here tonight. Full pardon. Your archive restored. Your name struck from the exile rolls at Corveth."

For a moment — a small, shameful moment — Odile's whole body leaned toward it like a plant toward sun. Her name. Her *name*, restored. Eleven years of being *that woman*, the archivist who claimed dragons lived, the laughingstock of three faculties, the exile who'd watched her life's work burned in a courtyard while a provost read her errors aloud in a carrying voice.

Then she thought of Bram's scarred hands turning the map over, careful as a woman handling a sleeping animal. Of Isa's jaw set like her father's must have been. Of Rennick cheating at cards so badly it was practically charity, sliding aces to Isa under the table and thinking no one saw.

"No," Odile said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt. "But thank you for leading with the chair instead of the table. That was almost courteous."

Something flickered across Serah's face. Not anger. Something more tired than anger.

"The map is a death warrant," Serah said quietly. "Not just for you. For everyone who's touched it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? My order doesn't imprison map-bearers, Archivist. We erase them. Every hand that carried it, every eye that read it, every mouth that spoke its route. There are nine names on my ledger connected to that scrap of hide, and yours is the fourth I've been able to reach." She paused. "The first three are dead."

"By your order's hand."

"By necessity." And there it was — that terrible calm conviction, the thing that made Serah Dain more frightening than any zealot, because she did not *enjoy* it. "Gods are not kind, Archivist. They are old hungers wearing our prayers as clothing. Everything that feeds them must burn, or everything we love burns instead. I'm offering you the chance to be on the side of the fire that leaves something standing."

Odile folded her hands on her knee and made herself smile, because she had learned long ago that a smile was armor too. "You know, I gave a lecture once — my last before the exile — on the difference between evidence and belief. Belief feels exactly like knowledge from the inside. Indistinguishable. Warm, certain, complete." She tilted her head. "You have the look of a woman holding something warm and certain. I'd ask you to show me your sources, but I suspect your sources are dead men who can't correct you."

Serah looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded to the man by the door.

"Put her at the table," she said, and left.

* * *

Here is what they do not tell you about pain, Odile thought, in the white interval between one question and the next: it makes the mind *smaller*. All those years of hoarded learning, all the languages and ledgers and half-remembered marginalia — gone, in an instant, down to a single burning point, and everything outside it distant as stars.

They were efficient. She would give them that. No rage in it, no cruelty for its own sake; a woman named Brakka with forearms like ship cable worked methodically, pausing to offer water, which Odile found somehow worse than if she hadn't. The questions came in rotation from a thin clerk with a soft voice, always the same three, arranged differently, like a scholar rearranging a flawed argument hoping the structure would fix itself.

Where is Ashgrove taking the map. Who else has read it. What did the seals show you.

"I told you," Odile said, somewhere around the second hour, through teeth that no longer met properly. "I am an *archivist*. We don't skim. If you want my honest professional opinion, your interrogation technique lacks rigor. You should establish baseline responses before—"

Brakka adjusted something, and the sentence ended.

The strange thing — the thing she would carry home from this night like a stone in her shoe — was how much of her stayed *watching*. Even doubled over the bolted table, even with her own pulse roaring in her ears, some cold corner of her mind kept taking notes. *Iron restraints, padded, to avoid marking the wrists — they intend to return prisoners looking whole. Water offered regularly — they want me functional, not broken. The clerk flinches at the third application — new to this, or conscience not yet fully excised. Note: exploitable.*

It was, she realized dimly, the same muscle she used in the archives. The refusal to be only a body in a room. The insistence on being a *reader*.

Between rounds, when they left her slumped on the table's edge to sweat and shake, she thought about Bram.

Bram, who prayed to a silent god every morning of her life and never once asked for anything back. Bram, who had taken in a disgraced scholar because — what had she said? *You're insufferable and you're the only person alive who can read that thing, and I find I've gotten used to insufferable.* High praise, from Bram. Practically a sonnet.

Bram would come for her. That was the trouble. That was the *awful* trouble, because Odile had done the arithmetic already, hunched over the table while her ribs sang: they had taken her precisely because she was the bait most likely to work. Bram was loyal past the point of sense. She would walk into Greywater with that blade of hers and that stubborn face, and Serah Dain would be waiting, and—

No. So the answer was simple, wasn't it. The answer was to give them nothing worth coming for, and to give them nothing *fast*, before Bram ever heard.

"Again," said the clerk softly, and Odile lifted her head and grinned at him with blood on her teeth, because a smile was armor, and hers was holding.

* * *

They brought her water near midnight. She drank it because refusing was pride and pride was a luxury; she'd learned that in the exile years too. Brakka stood by the wall with her arms crossed, and after a while — Odile marked the time by the candle, which had burned down two finger-widths — Brakka said, without preamble:

"You're the one who wrote *On the Survival of Great Wyrms*."

Odile raised her head an inch. "You've read it?"

"My mother owned a copy. Banned, obviously. She kept it wrapped in oilcloth under the floorboards like it was contraband saints' bones." Brakka's face did nothing at all. "She believed it. Dragons living. Said the world was too big for all the fire to be dead."

"It is too big," Odile croaked. "That was rather the thesis."

"Hm." Brakka pushed off the wall, and Odile braced herself — but the big woman only unlocked one wrist restraint, flexing Odile's crushed fingers gently, matter-of-factly, the way a farrier handles a horse. "Circulation. Captain wants you coherent tomorrow." At the door she paused. "My mother died in the Vell purges. Order business. Before you ask why I stay — it's because the captain is the only one in grey who's ever said *wait* to a pyre. Once. For a boy in Saltmere." Her jaw worked. "Once isn't enough. But it's more than anyone else gave."

She left. Odile stared at the candle and turned the words over like a coin of unfamiliar minting.

*Wait.* Serah Dain had said wait. And then tonight she had walked out of this room rather than watch, and her clerk flinched at the third application, and her ledger had nine names on it heading toward nine graves, and she had told Odile the truth about all of it with the flat honesty of a woman who believed honesty was owed even to the condemned.

*She thinks she's saving us,* Odile thought, and the thought arrived with genuine horror. *She thinks she's offering mercy.*

That was the screw, wasn't it. Not the table. The table was only hardware. The screw was a woman who would grieve every name on her ledger and kill them anyway, because the alternative — in her ledger of the world — was worse.

Odile put her forehead against the cool wood and laughed, quietly, hysterically, until the clerk came back in and looked alarmed.

* * *

Near dawn they tried the last key they had.

The clerk laid it on the table between them like a card he was ashamed to play: a sheet of paper, written in a fair administrative hand. A list. Nine names. Hers fourth. Beside each, a single notation in the margin — not *execute*, nothing so crude. *Reclaim and render inert.*

"You see," the clerk said gently, "there is no version of this where your friends survive the map. Only versions where you do or don't die alongside them. The captain extended amnesty because she prefers clean solutions. I'm authorized to tell you the alternative is not imprisonment. It is erasure. All nine. Including the girl. Including the smuggler." He hesitated. "Including Ashgrove."

And there it was. The real screw, turned home at last.

Because Odile had imagined, in the white hours, every threat to herself and dismissed them one by one — she was, she now understood, genuinely brave about herself in a way that surprised her. But *Bram*. *Isa*. Rennick, who owed the group a death already and would spend it grinning. Rendered inert. Erased. Because of a scrap of hide Odile had deciphered in a cellar that burned.

Her silence was keeping them alive. Her silence was also — she could see it now, the trap's full geometry — the thing that would bring Bram riding into Greywater like a storybook hero, straight into nine crossbows.

Either way, her loyalty killed them. Unless.

Unless there was a third shape to the night that neither she nor Serah had drawn yet.

"May I ask you a question?" Odile said. Her voice came out wrecked and quiet. "Professional to professional."

The clerk blinked. "...Yes."

"Your captain said the Ember must die so no god can command men again. That's the creed. I've read your founder's writings — yes, all of them, including the ones your order suppressed; it's *what I do* — and here's what interests me." She leaned forward as far as her restraints allowed, and her eyes, behind the cracked spectacles Brakka had finally returned, were bright as a student's. "Your founder never once proved the gods *commanded* anyone. He asserted it. Every account in your own suppressed archives — I've read those too — describes mortals *choosing* gods. Choosing, following, refusing, betraying. Command implies compulsion. What your own records describe is *relationship*." She smiled, and it hurt her split lip, and she smiled wider. "You aren't afraid of tyranny, Knight-Captain's clerk. You're afraid of love. And love, I promise you, cannot be extinguished by killing every fire in the world. Believe me. I have been exiled from mine for eleven years and it has not dimmed one candle-width."

The clerk stared at her. Somewhere beyond the door, boots paused — a sentry, listening.

"And here is my counteroffer," Odile went on, softer now, the scholar surfacing through the prisoner like a reef through tide. "Tell your captain this: the archivist says — check your suppressed archives, folio ninety-one, the *Testimony of the Last Warden* — the Ember cannot be destroyed by force. It can only be refused, or shared. Burn it, bury it, collapse a mountain on it, and you will not end the covenant. You will only end the *keepers* — the only people alive who know how it was meant to be held. And then whatever wakes when the seal fails will wake unattended." She let that sit. "I'm not withholding to protect myself. I'm withholding because destroying the Ember doesn't save your sister's memory, clerk. It desecrates it."

He went pale at *sister* — so the ledger ran deeper than rank, so the wound beneath the crusade was known even down here — and gathered his papers with shaking hands and left, and did not come back.

* * *

An hour before dawn, the door opened again, and this time it was Serah.

She stood in the doorway a long moment, looking at Odile — at the ruin of her, the swelling, the blood-dried collar, the spectacles mended with wire that Odile had repaired herself using a splinter and sheer spite. Then she came and sat across the table, unbolted and immovable, and set something down between them.

Odile's own notebook. Taken at the ford. Unopened — the clasp still shut, her little wax seal unbroken.

"I didn't let them read it," Serah said. "It would have told us everything. Routes, names, the seals' contents — you write everything down, Archivist, it's your one discipline." She turned the book slowly. "I want you to understand what you're dealing with. Not the Order. Me. I lost someone to god-touched madness. I have spent nine years making sure it never happens to another family, and I have done things in that service that I will answer for, someday, to something." Her voice did not waver. "And in nine years, no one — no priest, no prisoner, no scholar — has ever talked to me the way you did tonight. Like the answer was a *citation*."

"It usually is," Odile rasped. "People just don't check."

A ghost of something moved at the corner of Serah's mouth. Gone instantly, but Odile had spent a lifetime reading marginalia, and she caught it.

"Here is what happens now," Serah said. "In one hour, my column rides east. There is a village called Greywater proper, a mill, and a bridge your friends will need if they come for you — and they will come for you, because Ashgrove collects strays and you are the strays' librarian." She rose. "I am leaving four men and a locked door. I am leaving because if I stay, I will do what my order requires, and I find—" the smallest pause, the closest thing to a crack the woman had shown all night, "—that I require less, lately. Tell your captain of strays that. When she comes."

She reached the door. Odile, heart hammering against bruised ribs, made herself say it:

"Folio ninety-one, Serah Dain. Read it before you light anything else. That's all I ask. It's what I'd want done with my work — *read*, not burned."

Serah stood with her hand on the latch. For three full breaths she didn't move.

"The first three names on my ledger," she said at last, without turning, "were a farmer, a ferryman, and a child of twelve. I signed the orders myself. I have never once checked whether the map needed to be destroyed — only whether it existed." The latch clicked. "Folio ninety-one. I'll read it."

The door closed. The lock turned.

Odile sat alone in the candle-stub dark, shaking now that there was no one to be brave in front of, and began — because it was what she did, because it was the only prayer she knew — to take notes. On the room. On Brakka's mother's banned book. On the pause in Serah's voice, *lately*, which meant the crack had started before tonight, which meant the screw could be turned back.

Four men and a locked door. An hour's ride east. And somewhere out in the hills, a stubborn mercenary who collected strays, probably already arguing with Rennick about which road was fastest.

Odile smiled through split lips, opened her notebook, and wrote, in a hand that trembled and did not stop:

*Bram — they're letting me be rescued. Try not to make it dramatic. Also: folio 91. Trust me.*

Outside, far off toward the eastern hills, the first grey of dawn touched the ridgeline — and with it, faint but unmistakable, came the sound she'd been listening for since midnight: hoofbeats, many, moving fast.
