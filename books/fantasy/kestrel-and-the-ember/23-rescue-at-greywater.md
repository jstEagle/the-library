## 23. Rescue at Greywater

The river at Greywater ran slow and brown, the color of old tea, and it smelled like everything the Order had ever done to this country.

Bram crouched in the reeds on the far bank and watched the fort through Rennick's borrowed spyglass. It wasn't much of a fort — a customs house once, squat and stone, with a new palisade thrown up around it and a watchtower that leaned like a drunk against his friend's shoulder. Lanterns burned behind the arrow slits. Somewhere inside, Odile Vane was sitting in a cell with her hands bound, and somewhere inside her was the stubborn certainty that Bram would come for her.

Bram intended to prove her right. She just hadn't decided yet whether to be flattered or furious about it.

"She'll be in the east room," Isa whispered beside her, flat on her belly in the mud with a knife between her teeth out of pure habit. She'd stopped biting it weeks ago but still carried it there, the way some people carried a coin for luck. "Interrogators don't use cells near the gate. Too much noise."

"You've been in a lot of interrogations?"

"I've *caused* a lot of interrogations." Isa spat into the water. "There's a difference."

Rennick lay on Bram's other side, propped on one elbow with the ease of a man watching a sunset instead of planning a raid. The poison had taken his color and most of his weight; his cheekbones now had opinions of their own. But his eyes were the same as ever, quick and amused, cataloguing sentries the way other men catalogued pretty faces.

"Four on the wall," he murmured. "Two at the gate, one walking the river path, one asleep in the tower if I'm any judge — and I am. The lantern rotation is lazy. They change the watch every third hour, not fourth, which means their captain is nervous." He lowered the glass. "Nervous captains post extra men where they're least useful."

"Where's Serah Dain?" Bram asked.

"Gone. Rode south two days ago with half the column." He smiled thinly. "Which is either very good news or very bad news, depending entirely on what she went south to do."

Bram passed the glass back and let herself breathe. The plan was simple, because plans made by four people and a borrowed mule could afford to be nothing else. Isa would cut the corral fence and start a fire among the Order's hay — nothing grand, just enough smoke to pull eyes eastward. Rennick would take the river path, quiet as only a half-fae could be quiet, and open the postern from inside. Bram would go through the front.

"Through the *front*," Isa had said, when they'd planned it.

"They'll expect us to come sneaking," Bram said. "So we won't sneak."

It was, she reflected now, the kind of plan that sounded brave around a campfire and stupid everywhere else. But it had one virtue: it was hers, and she'd learned long ago that a bad plan you believed in beat a good plan you didn't.

She touched the little wooden bird at her throat — Kestrel's sign, worn smooth by twenty years of her thumb — and felt nothing answer. That was usual. That had always been usual. And yet lately the silence felt less like absence and more like held breath.

"On my count," she said. "Isa — fire. Rennick — river. Me — door."

Isa grinned, and it was her father's grin, sudden and sharp, and Bram's chest did something complicated about it that she filed away for later.

"Count," Isa said.

* * *

Serah Dain stood in the command tent forty miles south and read the report twice, and both times her hands were perfectly steady, which was how she knew she was lying to herself.

*Prisoner secure. Greywater post. Twelve men.*

Twelve men, holding an archivist who knew nothing worth knowing — Vane had given them nothing in three days, not a name, not a route, not so much as a syllable, and Serah had read enough broken people to know the difference between silence and emptiness. Vane wasn't empty. Vane was *loyal*, which was worse, and rarer, and it sat in Serah's chest like a stone swallowed wrong.

Because loyalty was supposed to be theirs. The Order's whole creed was built on it: give us your fear and your faith and we will keep you safe from the age when gods walked men like puppets. Every knight in her column had lost someone to god-touched madness or dragon-cult cruelty or simply to the long grinding centuries of wars fought over things that breathed fire. Serah had lost a sister. She knew the price of faith better than any scripture.

And yet.

She set down the report and looked at the map pinned under her fingers, at the small inked mark of Greywater, and admitted what she'd been refusing to admit since the bridge ambush: Ashgrove had spared her. Twice now. Once at the ford when a crossbow bolt meant for Serah's throat had gone wide because the mercenary had shouted a warning — a warning! — to the woman she was fighting. And once more in the way she carried herself, the way her people followed her not out of terror but out of something warmer that Serah refused to name.

Mercenaries didn't warn their enemies. Believers didn't spare the heretics they were sworn to burn. Bram Ashgrove kept doing both, and every time she did, she made a small crack in a wall Serah had spent fifteen years building.

"Captain?" The young knight in the tent doorway — Pellam, barely nineteen, still polished his breastplate with vinegar like his mother taught him. "Do we ride back north?"

Serah looked at him. At his clean, earnest face. At the sister-shaped hole in her heart that had never once, in all these years, asked her whether what she filled it with was true.

"No," she said. "We hold here."

But she didn't sleep, and before dawn she wrote an order she might later burn: *If the prisoner is freed, no pursuit past the river. Report only.* She told herself it was tactics — a freed prisoner leads you to the others; a dead one leads nowhere.

She was getting very good at telling herself things.

* * *

The fire went up in the corral with a sound like a drawn breath, and the night split open.

Bram came through the gate in the middle of the confusion, walking, sword sheathed, hands visible, exactly as promised. A sentry turned toward her, saw a lone woman strolling into a burning fort, and hesitated for precisely the length of time it took Bram to reach him and put her pommel against his temple, gentle as a knock on a neighbor's door. He folded. She caught him and laid him down. Twenty years of escort work taught you how to drop a man without breaking anything that mattered.

Inside, the yard was chaos — horses screaming, hay-smoke rolling black and greasy, knights stumbling from barracks buckling armor over nightshirts. Nobody looked at the calm woman walking against the current of panic. Panic looks forward. Bram moved sideways through it, counting doors.

East room. Isa had been right, of course. The lock was new and expensive and utterly wasted on Bram, who had picked harder locks in worse moods. It gave with a click that sounded, in the roaring dark, almost polite.

Odile sat on the cell floor with her back against the wall, hands raw where the bindings had chewed them, spectacles cracked across one lens. She looked up, and for a moment — just a moment — the archivist's face did something young and unguarded, something that might have been relief or might have been fury, and then it settled into its usual dry architecture.

"You're late," Odile said. Her voice was sandpaper. "Three days. I had begun composing your eulogy. It was not flattering."

"I brought you a present." Bram worked at the wrist bindings with her belt knife. "Your spectacles are broken."

"My spectacles are the least of what's broken." Odile flexed her fingers, wincing. "They asked questions, Ashgrove. Interesting ones. Not about the map." She got her feet under her, swayed, and gripped Bram's arm hard enough to bruise. "About *you*. Your parentage. Your birth records in the valley temple. They know something, and whatever it is, they think it's worth more than the map."

The cold that went through Bram then had nothing to do with the river air.

"Later," she said.

"Now-ish," Odile said, but she was already moving, because whatever else she was, Odile Vane was a professional.

They made the yard together. Too easy. Bram knew it was too easy the way you know weather turning — a pressure behind the ears, a hush under the noise. Then the barracks door banged open and the hush explained itself.

Six knights, armored, unhurried, forming a line across the yard with the smooth economy of men who had drilled this exact formation a hundred times. And in front of them, stepping out of the smoke with her helm under her arm and her sword already drawn, Serah Dain.

Not gone south. Never gone south. Rennick's intelligence, for the first time in Bram's experience, had been beautifully, catastrophically wrong.

"Archivist," Serah said, without looking at Odile. Her eyes stayed on Bram. "Step aside. This doesn't concern you."

"She concerns me," Odile said. "I find I've developed opinions on the subject."

"Captain Dain." Bram drew her sword slowly, letting everyone see it, letting everyone see also that her stance was guard, not attack. "You're supposed to be forty miles south."

"And you're supposed to be a mercenary," Serah said. "Soldiers for hire don't raid forts for archivists. Mercenaries don't shout warnings to enemies. Mercenaries don't teach orphan girls blade-work around campfires like they're passing on an inheritance." She advanced a step, and the smoke curled between them. "What are you, Ashgrove? Because I've fought your kind before, and you aren't it."

Bram had an answer to that. She'd had it for weeks now, growing in her like a second heartbeat, heavy and secret and terrible. *I am the last vessel of a silent god, and the fire you want to kill may be sleeping in my blood.* She opened her mouth to lie —

— and what came out instead was: "Come and see."

It was, she thought distantly, the worst tactical decision of her life, and it felt wonderful.

Serah came.

The duel in the burning yard was short and vicious and utterly silent except for steel. Serah fought like her creed — disciplined, economical, every strike a sermon. Bram fought like her valley — patient, grounded, waiting for the mountain to make the first move. They broke apart and came together three times, four, five, and each exchange Bram gave ground she didn't have to give, and each exchange Serah noticed, because Serah Dain missed nothing, and each exchange the noticing cost her something.

On the sixth pass, Bram saw the opening. It was wide and clean and it would have ended the fight — a thrust under the raised guard, into the gap at the hip, crippling, maybe killing. Twenty years of muscle memory rose to take it.

Bram turned the thrust into a flat-of-the-blade slap against Serah's vambrace and stepped back, breathing hard, sword point dropping to the mud.

"Yield," she said.

Serah stared at her. Around them the yard had gone still — knights frozen mid-step, Isa emerging from the smoke with her knife out and murder in her face, Rennick at the postern waving them frantically toward escape. The fire crackled. The river ran on, brown and indifferent.

"You had me," Serah said quietly. "Twice in that exchange. You had me and you gave it away."

"Yes."

"Why?"

And here was the thing, the thing Bram would carry home afterward and turn over for many nights: she didn't have a strategy. She had only the truth, rising in her like water finding its level.

"Because you're wrong about what gods want," Bram said.

Serah's knuckles whitened on her grip. "You know nothing of—"

"I know what the visions showed me. I know what the war actually was." Bram's voice stayed low, steady, the voice she used on spooked horses. "Not glory. Not dominion. Two great powers bleeding each other dry for a hundred years while mortals starved underneath, until finally — *finally* — they stopped. Not because one won. Because they *promised*. The Ember isn't a weapon, Captain. It's a promise being kept. And the thing you're hunting—" she shook her head slowly, "—the thing your masters wear like a badge, the righteousness, the certainty, the *we alone know what's right* — that's not the cure for what killed your sister. That's the disease wearing new clothes."

The words landed. Bram watched them land. Watched them go into Serah Dain like arrows into deep water — no splash, no cry, but sinking, sinking, down to where the sister-shaped wound lived.

For a long moment the knight-captain of the Hollow Order stood in the firelight with her sword arm trembling, and Bram could not have said — would never have claimed to know — whether it trembled with rage or with something else.

"Pellam," Serah said at last, without turning her head. "Sound the retreat bell. The prisoner has escaped. We pursue at dawn." A pause. "*At dawn.*"

"Begging pardon, Captain," said young Pellam, "but the prisoners are escaping *right now*, and—"

"*At dawn,*" Serah said again, and this time there was something in it that made even the smoke seem to lean away.

Bram backed toward the postern, sword still bare, eyes still on Serah's. At the threshold she paused.

"The visions showed me names," she said. "Of the cultists who wore a god's name while they murdered. Mortals, Captain. Every one. When you're ready to hear them, come find me — not with a warrant. With a question."

Then the dark took her, and the river took them all — Odile leaning on Bram's shoulder, Isa rowing with her jaw set and tears she'd deny to her grave, Rennick steering with a strength he was renting at rates he couldn't afford.

Behind them, Greywater burned itself out politely, and Serah Dain stood on the bank a long time, watching the boat become a shadow become a rumor.

Pellam crept up beside her. "Captain? We could still—"

"No." She sheathed her sword. Her hand, she noticed, was not quite steady, and she looked at it as though it belonged to a stranger. "Get the men fed. Break camp at dawn."

"And the pursuit, Captain?"

Serah looked south, toward the Order's heart, toward the masters whose orders she had executed for fifteen years without once asking what they wanted with a dead god's fire. Then north, toward the mountains, toward Mount Sarn, toward whatever truth a mercenary with merciful hands was carrying like a wound.

"We ride north," Serah said. "Slowly."

Pellam blinked. "Slowly, Captain?"

"Slowly," Serah said, and heard, beneath her own voice, the first faint crack in a wall fifteen years wide. "Gods forgive me. *Slowly.*"

* * *

Dawn found them in the hills above the river, and Rennick paid for the night.

He'd held the postern open longer than any man should have, taking a spear-graze across the ribs and a blow to the head he laughed off at the time and did not laugh off now. He sat propped against a boulder, gray as the morning, while Odile stitched the graze with the brisk tenderness of a woman who had patched worse and grieved deeper.

"You held that door," Bram said, kneeling beside him.

"Somebody had to." His grin was a pale copy of itself. "Besides, I owed the Order a door. They owe me considerably more."

Isa pressed a cup of willow-bark tea into his hands and pretended, badly, that she wasn't scared. Odile tied off the stitch and sat back on her heels, and her cracked spectacles caught the sunrise, and she said, quietly, so only Bram could hear:

"They asked about your mother, Ashgrove. By name. Which means somewhere in their archives there's a record with your family in it." She looked up, and behind the broken lens her eyes were fierce and afraid. "Whatever you're carrying — the secret you think is protecting us — the Order already knows its shape. You're running out of time to tell us yourself."

Above them, far to the north, a smudge of ash rose from the shoulder of Mount Sarn, thin and gray against the brightening sky — like a candle seen from very far away, guttering.

Bram looked at it for a long time.

"Two days' ride," she said. "Then I'll tell you everything. I swear it on the flame."

And somewhere in her blood, warm and patient and terribly awake, something that had waited twenty years for her to say those words — *stirred*.
