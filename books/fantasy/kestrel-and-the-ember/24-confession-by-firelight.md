## 24. Confession by Firelight

The rain came at dusk and did not stop, so they made camp in the shell of a shepherd's cot that had lost its roof to some winter long past. Odile rigged a tarp from the cart canvas and pronounced it structurally sound in the way of a woman who had once slept three years under a collapsed archive shelf. Rennick built the fire with the particular ceremony he brought to all small comforts now, feeding it twig by twig as though each one were a favor being done him personally.

Bram sat apart, sharpening a knife she did not need sharpened.

She had been sitting apart for eleven days. She knew because Isa counted — Bram had heard her muttering the tally to herself on the road, not angrily, just the way you count anything that owes you something. Eleven days since the third seal opened in the mountain pass and the vision spoke of *Kestrel's own blood*, and everyone had looked at everyone else, and Bram had looked hardest at her own hands and said nothing.

Eleven days was long enough. She could feel the secret the way you feel a stone in your boot: not unbearable, exactly, but present in every step, and getting worse the longer you pretended it wasn't there.

"Supper," Rennick announced, "is barley again. I want it noted for posterity that I once ate oysters."

"You've never eaten an oyster," Odile said.

"I've *described* them beautifully. That's half the experience."

Isa laughed — a small laugh, quickly folded away, but a real one. She'd been doing that more lately. Folding things away. Bram watched her across the firelight and thought: *her father walked into my camp dying, and I have been keeping his secret like stolen goods.*

The knife made its soft shirring sound against the whetstone. Bram set both down.

"I need to tell you all something," she said, "and I'd rather do it before the barley, if that's all right. Bad news sits better on an empty stomach. You get it over with faster."

Rennick's eyebrows went up. Odile went very still, which for Odile was the equivalent of other people screaming.

Isa didn't move at all.

* * *

Bram told it plainly, because she had rehearsed it plainly on the road for eleven days and every fanciful version had died of embarrassment somewhere around the second mile. The shrine. The pulse of warmth answering her prayer. The first seal, and Kestrel's voice saying her name. The second seal, and the god pouring itself into mortal bloodlines to hide what it loved. The third seal's warning, and the shape of the arithmetic underneath it.

"The visions needed someone of Kestrel's blood to open them," she said. "They opened for me. All three. I think — I don't know, but I think — that I'm what's left of him. Not chosen. Made. Generations back, maybe further. My whole line." She turned her palms up, empty, ordinary, calloused from sword-work. "I should have said something after the second seal. I told myself I was waiting until I was sure. That was a lie I told myself so I wouldn't have to watch your faces while I said it."

The fire popped. Rain ticked on the canvas overhead.

Odile recovered first, because Odile always reached for structure when the world shook. "That explains the resonance readings. Well. It would explain them, if I still had instruments, which I don't, because the Order burned them, which is a grievance I will be raising formally at some point—"

"Odile." Bram said it gently. "It's all right to be angry too."

"I'm not angry, I'm *cataloguing*—" But her voice cracked on the last syllable, and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth, and for a moment the archivist was just a tired woman who had been lied to by someone she'd started, against all policy, to trust.

Rennick said nothing at all. He looked into the fire with an expression Bram couldn't read, and she thought of the river spirit, and the memories he'd paid, and how a man who had sold his griefs might be slower than most to forgive someone who'd hoarded hers.

And Isa stood up.

She didn't shout. Bram had expected shouting. What happened instead was worse: Isa picked up her bedroll, carried it to the far wall of the ruined cot, set it down with great care, and sat beside it with her back to all of them, and began, methodically, to braid her hair for sleep.

"Isa," Bram said.

"Don't." The word came out level. Almost kind. "I'm going to sleep now. We can talk tomorrow. I'll be reasonable tomorrow. I'm telling you this now so you know it's coming and can prepare whatever you're going to say, because I am *not* reasonable tonight, and I'd rather you got the version of me that thinks before she speaks."

"That seems fair," Rennick offered into the silence.

"It isn't fair," Isa said, still not turning around. "Nothing about this is fair. That's why I'm doing it this way."

* * *

Bram did not sleep.

She lay under the leaking corner of the tarp and listened to the rain and ran the evening back through her mind like a man checking his accounts for the error. She had confessed. She had confessed well, even — plainly, without excuses, owning the lie outright. And it had landed like a blade anyway, and she kept circling the wound trying to understand why, and somewhere past midnight she stopped pretending she didn't know.

Because it wasn't about the secret. It was about whose secret it had been.

She got up quietly, took her cloak, and stepped out into the rain.

The night was the soft grey-black of steady weather, no lightning, just water falling through dark air and the fire's glow behind her through the broken doorway. Isa was already there — of course she was; Bram should have known. She sat on the low stone wall of the sheep pen with her hood up, watching the wet dark, and she didn't turn when Bram came to stand beside her, but she didn't leave either, and Bram understood that this was permission of a very narrow and conditional kind.

"I've been counting," Isa said, before Bram could find a word. "Did you know that? Eleven days. I counted because I thought — if I ever found out you'd been hiding something, I wanted to know exactly how many days you'd let me walk beside you not knowing. So I could hold onto the number. Like evidence."

"And now you have it."

"Now I have it." Isa pulled her hood back. The rain beaded in her hair, and her face was bare in a way it hadn't been since the salt road, since the nights she'd laughed at Rennick's cheating and let Bram correct her sword-grip with gentle hands. "My father died in your arms."

"Yes."

"He carried that map across half the world. He knew what the Order does to people who carry it. He knew, and he went anyway, because he thought — I know what he thought, he wrote it in his letters — he thought the person the seals would open for was worth dying to reach. That was the whole plan, Bram. Find the vessel. Give her the map. Trust her with everything." Isa's voice stayed level, which took work; Bram could see it taking work, the muscles of her jaw standing out. "And he gets there, and he dies, and you take the map, and you take me in, and you teach me the blade, and you sit at my fire — and the whole time, you already knew. You knew what he died believing. You could have told me on the salt road. You could have told me at Vell. You could have told me *yesterday*, and instead I had to hear it with the barley waiting."

"I know."

"Do you?" And there it was, finally, the crack in the level voice, the thing underneath. "Do you actually know? Because here's what I keep coming back to. My father spent his last breath giving you the truth. His literal last breath. And you held it for eleven days like it was yours to ration." She was shaking now, fine tremors the rain almost hid. "He trusted you with everything, and you gave me back almost nothing, and I *liked* you. That's the part that makes me sick. I liked you. I thought we were — I thought this company was the first true thing since he died, and it turns out it was built on top of a hole where the truth should have been, and I was the only one standing on it."

"Isa—"

"No. You had your say inside. It was a good say. It was honest, and it was brave, and I'm sure it cost you." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at the tears or at herself for them. "But you don't get to decide when I'm done being hurt. Nobody decided that for you, did they? When you found out what you are?"

Bram had no answer. The rain filled the silence, patient and indifferent.

"I'm going to walk ahead tomorrow," Isa said at last, quieter. "Not away. Ahead. I still want the Ember found. I still want the Order answered for. But I need to be someplace where I'm not looking at you every hour and remembering that I trusted you before you earned it." She stood, and paused, and for one moment her voice dropped to something young and raw. "He said *the Ember remembers*. Did you ever wonder if what it remembers is everyone who lied to protect it?"

Then she climbed back over the wall and left Bram alone with the rain.

* * *

Morning came colorless and dripping. They broke camp in a silence with edges on it. Odile packed with unnecessary precision; Rennick whistled, badly and deliberately, the way he did when he was refusing to acknowledge a room's weather. Isa took point on the trail as promised, twenty paces ahead, her shoulders straight as fence posts.

By midday the country had changed — the forest thinning into moorland, heather and gorse and long views under scudding cloud. Bram walked at the rear and watched Isa's back and did her own arithmetic: two days to the old drover's road, four to the foothills of Sarn, and somewhere in between, a family bleeding out slowly on a wet hillside.

It was Rennick, of all people, who broke it.

He did it at the noon halt, with the timing of a man who had spent thirty years reading rooms he wasn't welcome in. He waited until Odile was occupied with her maps and Isa was checking the trail ahead, then lowered himself onto a stone beside Bram with the careful economy of a body running on borrowed time.

"When I bargained with the river," he said, conversationally, "I paid for my mother's face. Did I ever tell you that? First payment. The spirit asked for memories of the dead, and I gave it her face, and I remember everything else about her — her voice, her temper, the way she cheated at dice worse than I do — but the face is gone. Just a blank where it was." He turned a pebble over in his fingers. "For a week afterward I hated myself for paying it. Then I realized something useful. Grief isn't the memory. Grief is what the memory *does to you*. And what my mother did to me — the laughing, the cheating, the stubbornness — none of that lived in her face. It lived in me. The spirit took the picture and left the inheritance."

Bram looked at him. "You're saying that like it's supposed to help."

"I'm saying it like it's true, and you look like a woman short on true things." He flicked the pebble away. "You didn't lose the girl's trust, Ashgrove. You lost the *version* of her trust that didn't know what you'd done. Different thing. The first one's gone forever, yes. The second one you can rebuild, if you're willing to build it in front of her, ugly, where she can see every joint." He stood, wincing, and gathered his pack. "Take it from a smuggler. Hidden cargo always costs double at the border."

He wandered off to bother Odile about the route. Bram sat with the pebble-lesson turning over in her chest, and found — to her surprise — that it had a grain of comfort in it, rough and unpolished, but real.

* * *

The storm caught them at dusk again, as though it had been following, and they sheltered in the lee of a drystone wall that some dead farmer had curved like a cupped hand. The fire was small and mean. Supper was barley again; Rennick declined to describe any oysters.

And it was Isa who broke the silence this time.

She did it without preamble, from across the flames, in the flat voice of someone who had spent a full day walking ahead and thinking. "My father used to play a game with me. When I was small. He'd hide a coin in his fist and let me guess which hand, and no matter how many times I guessed right, he'd always swear the coin had been in the other one, and we'd end up laughing, and eventually he'd give me the coin anyway." She fed a stick to the fire. "I used to think it was a game about trust. Now I think it was a game about secrets — about how the people who love you are allowed a few, as long as the coin turns up in the end."

She looked up, and met Bram's eyes across the fire, and her gaze was red-rimmed and dry and absolutely steady.

"So here's my terms. Not forgiveness. Terms. One: no more secrets between us that touch the Ember, the seals, or what you are. If you dream it, I hear it. If you suspect it, I hear it. Two: when we reach Sarn, if it comes down to choosing — you choosing anything — I stand next to you when you choose. Not because I forgive you. Because my father died putting you there, and I intend to see what he bought." She swallowed. "Three. Three is harder."

"Tell me anyway."

"You tell me about him." Her voice finally frayed, just at the edge. "The ford. The last minutes. Everyone else has had pieces of my father — Odile has his letters, Rennick knew the routes. I got a grave dug in a hurry and a story with holes in it. You were *there*. You're the only one who was there. And I have been so angry at you that I never asked, and I think — I think part of me was afraid to ask, because as long as I didn't ask, he wasn't quite dead yet." She dashed a hand across her eyes, angry again, or past anger into something plainer. "So. Term three. Tonight, you tell me how he died. All of it. Even the parts that hurt. *Especially* those."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out on the moor, a curlew called, long and falling.

Bram set down her bowl. "All right," she said. "But come sit near. He deserved better than being shouted across a fire."

Isa hesitated — a long hesitation, the whole day balanced in it — and then rose, and came around the flames, and sat down beside Bram on the cold stone. Not close. But beside. Their shoulders a hand-width apart, the distance honest, neither pretending it was more nor insisting it was less.

"He stumbled into our camp at moonset," Bram began, "and I thought at first he was a drunk, because he was singing. Badly. Some marching song, half the words wrong..."

She talked. The others drew in by degrees — Odile first, folding her maps away with exaggerated care to disguise that she was listening; then Rennick, settling against the wall with his arms crossed and his eyes closed in a way that fooled no one. The fire burned down to embers and Bram fed it, and talked, and Isa listened with her hands clasped tight in her lap, and twice she laughed — small, startled laughs, at the bad singing and the complaint about the ford's mud — and both times the laugh seemed to surprise her more than anyone.

When Bram reached the end — the map pressed into her hands, the whisper, *the Ember remembers*, the arrows out of the dark and Dorn going down and the stranger's hand going slack — Isa was silent a long moment.

"He sang," she said finally. "At the end of everything, he sang."

"He did. Off-key."

"That tracks." A wet, broken huff that was mostly a sob wearing armor. She scrubbed her face. "Term three is satisfied. I'm going to sleep now, and tomorrow I'm still walking ahead, because I meant what I said about needing the space. But—" she paused at the edge of the firelight, not looking back, "—the coin turned up, Bram. Eventually. That counts for something. I don't know how much yet. But it counts."

She rolled into her blanket with her back to the fire, and this time it read less like exile and more like exhaustion.

Bram sat watching the embers. After a while Odile settled beside her, close enough that their sleeves touched.

"For the record," the scholar murmured, "I'm still cataloguing. Anger may arrive later. Possibly Thursday."

"I'll brace for Thursday."

"Mm." Odile glanced toward Isa's blanket, then at Bram, and her voice dropped to nearly nothing. "The fourth seal opens at the caldera rim. Whatever it shows — whoever it's *about* — you tell us first this time. Before the vision finishes, if you can manage it. Promise me."

Out on the dark moor, the wind shifted, and carried with it — faint, impossible, unmistakable — the smell of woodsmoke and hot iron, as though something far to the north had opened one eye.

Bram looked at Odile. "I promise," she said, and meant it, and wondered how much longer promises would matter — because far off across the moorland, where no traveler should have been, a light was moving. Small. Steady. Coming their way.
