## 25. The Long Ascent

The mountain had been visible for three days before it decided to notice them.

Bram felt the change first in her teeth — a low hum, like biting foil, that started at dawn and did not stop. By midmorning the sky over Mount Sarn had gone the color of a bruise going yellow, and the wind began to carry grit that was not sand and not snow but something finer, older, tasting faintly of struck flint.

"Ash," Odile said, holding out her palm to catch a few grains. They sat there glowing, very slightly, like embers that had forgotten to die. "Not from any fire I know. This is old ash. It's been circling this mountain for centuries."

"Circling?" Isa pulled her scarf up over her nose.

"Trapped. The mountain breathes it out and draws it back in." Odile let the wind take her hand's worth of history. "Like a lung."

"Wonderful," said Rennick. "A giant dying lung. And we're walking up its throat."

Bram checked the straps on her pack and said nothing, because what she was thinking — *it knows we're coming* — was not a thing you said aloud on a mountainside with four other people who all had their own private terrors. She'd gotten good at that lately: carrying a thought the way you carried a blade, edge turned inward so nobody bled until it was time.

Behind them, far below where the trail bent around a shoulder of black rock, the Order's column moved like a scar being drawn across the land. Banners too small to read at this distance, but Bram didn't need to read them. She'd spent enough nights listening to Serah Dain's soldiers march to know the sound of them even through wind. Boots. Always the boots. A thousand pairs of them, patient as erosion.

"They've stopped making camp at night," Isa reported, coming down from the ridge with her spyglass — her father's spyglass, which she handled now without flinching, which was its own kind of progress. "They just keep walking. Shifts. Half sleep while half climb."

"Serah's spending her men like coin," Bram said.

"She can afford to. We can't." Isa stowed the glass. "We have four legs between us that work properly and one that's running on a river spirit's charity."

Everyone looked at Rennick, because everyone always looked at Rennick now, and he hated it, and he covered the hating with a grin that had gotten thinner every day since Greywater.

"I'll have you know," he said, "this leg has climbed better mountains than this one. In worse weather. With better company, admittedly—"

"Rennick." Bram kept her voice level. "How is it, honestly?"

He considered lying. She watched him consider it — saw the whole performance assemble itself behind his eyes, the swagger, the joke, the smuggler's arithmetic of what a confession costs. Then he looked down at his own boot, and the performance collapsed quietly, like a tent with a snapped pole.

"It's like carrying water," he said. "You know how when you carry water uphill, you can feel it wanting to spill? Every step, it wants to spill. That's me. All the time now. Something borrowed wants to go back where it came from, and I'm the cup." He shrugged. "But cups hold. Cups are famous for it."

Nobody laughed. He hadn't really meant them to.

"We camp early," Bram said. "Full rest tonight. Tomorrow we hit the scree fields, and after that there's no shelter until the caldera rim. So tonight we eat everything heavy, sleep everything off, and start light."

"And if the Order doesn't stop for the night either?" Odile asked.

Bram looked back down the trail, at the distant wound of banners.

"Then tomorrow we find out how much mountain is worth," she said.

* * *

Odile could not sleep, which was normal now; her body had apparently concluded, somewhere on the salt road, that sleep was for people whose research wasn't trying to kill them. So she sat up by the fire with her journal and did what she always did: wrote down everything, in case the mountain ate them and someone else had to finish the sentence.

*Day thirty-one of the ascent,* she wrote. *The ash-fall thickens. Rennick's condition deteriorates in direct proportion to altitude — I have begun charting it. At sea level he limped charmingly. At two thousand feet he leaned on his staff. Today, at four, he leaned on Isa twice and pretended both times it was a joke.*

She paused, pen hovering, and then added, smaller:

*I know what he paid. He told me once, drunk on river-water and courage, that the spirit took his memories of his family — faces first, then names, then the shape of the grief itself. He said the strange part wasn't losing them. The strange part was that he still loved them. Love with no object. Grief with no grave. He said it was like reaching into your pocket for a key to a house that no longer exists and finding your hand already full of it.*

Odile looked across the fire at him. He was asleep sitting up, chin on chest, one hand curled loosely around his staff the way a child holds a stick they intend to be a sword tomorrow. His face in sleep was younger and emptier than his waking face, and it broke her heart in a way she had no theory for.

She was an archivist. Her whole life had been built on the principle that nothing should be lost — that every record, every name, every marginal note in a burned book was worth saving, because you never knew which scrap would turn out to be the load-bearing one. And here was a man who had traded away his entire archive to buy his friends a season, and he'd done it grinning, and the worst part — the truly unbearable part — was that she understood him completely.

*Note to self,* she wrote. *If we survive this, find some way to give him back something. Even one name. Even one wrong, blurry memory. A scholar owes a debt to every lost library, and Rennick Cael is a burned library that walks.*

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, the mountain exhaled its long gray breath, and the ash came down soft as apology.

Odile closed her journal and, for the first time in thirty years, prayed. Not to Kestrel — she'd never been sure Kestrel took dictation — but to the general office of whatever managed such things. *Keep him upright,* she asked. *Just upright. I'll handle the rest.*

She got no answer, obviously. But the fire burned a little steadier after that, and she chose to count it.

* * *

On the third day of the scree, Rennick's leg quit.

It didn't announce itself. There was no dramatic collapse, no cry, none of the theater he'd have chosen if he'd been consulted. He simply put his foot on a slope of loose black stone, and the stone accepted his weight, and his leg did not — the borrowed strength in it thinning all at once like broth stretched over too many guests — and he sat down harder than he meant to, and then found, to his considerable surprise, that he couldn't immediately get up.

"Rest stop," he announced to the group at large, with tremendous dignity. "I've called a rest stop. You're welcome."

Isa was beside him before he finished the sentence. She didn't make a fuss, which he appreciated; she just crouched, took his wrist, and held it the way you'd hold a rope you weren't willing to drop.

"How long?" she asked quietly.

"Since about the second hour," he admitted. "Maybe since the ford. Hard to say. Time gets slippery when you're mostly wishful thinking."

"Rennick—"

"Don't do the face. Any of you. I can feel all four of you doing the face." He patted the scree beside him. "Sit. Rest stop. I called it. It's official."

They sat. The ash fell. Below them, impossibly far below, the Order's column had reached the tree line and begun, with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the concept of time, to climb.

Bram crouched next to him and studied his leg with a professional eye — the same eye she used on horses and bridges and anything else that might fail under load. "Can you tell what's happening? Is it the bargain wearing out, or is it—"

"Spending faster because I'm spending it?" He smiled ruefully. "Both. Probably both. The spirit gave me a season, and I've been sprinting a season since Greywater. Turns out seasons don't care how you use them. Just that you do."

"There's another river," Odile said carefully. "The maps show meltwater streams off the southern face. If spirits run in the high water—"

"No." Rennick said it gently, which somehow made it more final. "No, love. I know what you're asking, and the answer's no. Not because I'm noble — gods know I'm not, ask anyone I've ever cheated — but because I've seen the ledger. I know what a second bargain costs, and it isn't memories anymore. First payment takes what you remember. Second payment takes what you are." He tapped his own chest. "And I've only got the one self left, and I'm using it. On this. On you lot. Best purchase of my life, actually, and I include the horse."

"What horse?" Isa asked.

"Exactly." He grinned, and for a moment the grin was the old grin, wide and crooked and entirely too pleased with itself, and everyone laughed despite everything, because that was the trick of Rennick Cael — he made laughter feel like a debt you were happy to owe.

But when they rose to climb again, and Isa put his arm across her shoulders, and Bram took the other side without being asked, and Odile walked ahead breaking the wind with her body like a small furious wall — when all that happened, Rennick looked up at the caldera rim, a thin bright line against the bruised sky, and thought: *well. If this is what the money bought, it was cheap at twice the price.*

He just wished, privately, in the place where his family used to live, that he could remember his mother's face to tell her so.

* * *

Serah Dain stood at the tree line and looked up at the mountain, and for the first time in her career, she hesitated.

Her quartermaster noticed. Vess noticed everything; it was why Serah kept her. "Captain?"

"The ash," Serah said. "Look at it."

Vess looked. Ash falling upward, in places. Ash hanging in the air in shapes that almost resembled script. Ash that glowed, faintly, like the coals of a fire that had gone out years ago and never quite committed to it.

"The men are frightened," Vess said. "They won't say it. But they're counting the banners that come back down, and the number keeps getting smaller."

"Superstition."

"Yes, Captain. Mine too, a little." Vess shifted. "There's talk. That the Ember is a god's heart. That burning it will burn us. Old stories — the ones the Order says aren't true."

"They aren't true," Serah said. "Gods are not hearts. Gods are debts. Every miracle ever performed was a loan taken out against mortal lives, and the interest always comes due, and it always comes due on people who never signed the contract." She touched the locket at her throat — Liss's locket, Liss's name worn smooth inside it by her thumb. "My sister signed nothing. She was ten. The cultists wore a god's name and called it holy, and the god said nothing, did nothing, corrected nothing. Silence is consent. I am going up that mountain to end the silence forever."

Vess saluted. But she lingered a moment longer than regulation required, and Serah knew what she wanted to ask — *and if the stories are true, Captain? What then?* — and Serah was grateful, in a tired way, that neither of them said it aloud.

"Double the pace," she said instead. "We reach the rim before they do. Whatever happens at that flame, it happens on our terms or not at all."

Above her, the mountain breathed its ash, and somewhere in the gray, Serah thought she heard — though this was impossible, and she filed it under fatigue — something vast and patient turning over in its sleep.

* * *

They climbed through the night of the fourth day, because the Order's torches below had become a river of them, and stopping had stopped being an option.

Bram led. She had led escorts through worse country than this — she kept telling herself that, anyway, though the truth was she'd never led anyone through a place where the ground itself seemed to be deciding, moment to moment, whether it approved of you. The scree shifted underfoot with opinions. The wind spoke in fragments. Twice she heard her own name in it, and both times it was Kestrel's cadence, and both times she gripped her staff tighter and climbed.

Near dawn, Isa caught up to walk beside her. The girl's face was gray with exhaustion, her hands split from the rock, and she was still — Bram noted this with something that wasn't quite surprise anymore — keeping watch on the others more than herself. Making sure Rennick's footing held. Making sure Odile drank. Making sure Bram didn't quietly carry more than her share, which was a losing battle, but Isa fought it anyway.

"When we get to the top," Isa said, "what happens?"

"You know what happens."

"I know what the seals showed. I want to know what *you're* planning." Isa's voice was steady, but her eyes weren't. "Because I've watched you for weeks now, Bram Ashgrove, and you plan like a mercenary. Exits first. Costs second. So — exits. Costs. Tell me."

Bram was quiet for three steps. Four. The ash drifted between them like a curtain neither wanted to pull aside.

"I'm planning to stand in front of that flame," she said finally, "and offer it everything I am. And if it takes the offer, the war stays sealed and none of you ever have to think about gods again. And if it doesn't—" She shook her head. "Then I don't know. That's the honest answer. The whole map, the whole covenant, and I don't know what comes after the offering."

"That's not a plan. That's a funeral."

"Sometimes those look alike from the outside."

"Bram." Isa stopped walking. The others drew ahead; the wind took their voices; for a moment it was just the two of them on a slope of broken world with a river of enemy fire climbing beneath them. "Your father — the man my father died getting the map to — whoever raised you in that temple, whoever taught you that your life is a tool to be spent — they were wrong. Do you hear me? Spending yourself isn't devotion. It's just spending. Devotion is *staying*. Staying is the hard part. Anyone can burn."

Bram looked at her — this furious, grieving, stubborn girl who had every right to hate her and had chosen kinship instead, badly and loudly and daily — and felt something in her chest shift, the way ice shifts on a river before it breaks.

"Isa—"

"Save it." Isa started walking again. "Tell me at the top. Tell me standing up. And Bram?" She glanced back, and in the pre-dawn gray her eyes were bright and hard as the mountain itself. "Whatever you're planning to do alone — don't. That's not how this family works. We've all been practicing."

Ahead of them, the caldera rim caught the first light and burned gold along its whole broken crown, and Bram saw, silhouetted against it, figures. Many figures. Ranked and waiting, banners snapping in the ash-wind, and at their center a single knight in white steel, standing very still, like a question the mountain had been waiting all morning to ask.

"They're here," Odile breathed, catching up. "They beat us to the rim."

Rennick leaned on his staff, leg trembling, and squinted up at the line of steel against the fire-colored sky.

"Well," he said. "Anyone else bring a plan? Because I brought a horse."

Bram tightened her grip on her staff and started climbing.
