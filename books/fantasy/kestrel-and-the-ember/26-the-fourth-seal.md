## 26. The Fourth Seal

The mountain had been trying to kill them for three days, and by the fourth it had settled for merely insulting them.

Ash fell like grey snow, fine and sour-tasting, coating everything in a film that made Odile look ten years older and Rennick look — well, Rennick had refused to let anyone comment on how he looked since the river spirit's bargain, so Bram kept her observations to herself. The scree slopes above the tree line rang faintly under their boots, a sound like distant bells made of bone. Somewhere below, half a day back down the switchbacks, the Order's column crawled after them like a patient serpent.

"Tell me again," Isa said, hauling herself over a lip of black rock, "why we couldn't have taken the *nice* volcano."

"This was the nice one," Rennick wheezed from behind her. "The other one has a village of fire-newts on it. Very territorial. Excellent soup, though."

"You've eaten newt soup?"

"I've eaten everything once. It's a smuggler's oath." He paused to breathe, one hand pressed flat against his ribs where the poison had done its worst work and the bargain its stranger work. His skin had gone translucent at the wrists, Bram had noticed. Veins showing through like rivers seen from very high up. She didn't say that either.

Odile climbed without speaking, as she had for most of the ascent, her satchel of seals and notes hugged to her chest like a child. Three seals opened. Three visions burned into all of them now: gods and dragons burning together; Kestrel choosing silence, pouring itself into mortal blood; the map's final warning — *only Kestrel's own blood may wake or quench the Ember.* Everyone knew what that meant. Everyone had been politely not looking at Bram for two weeks, which was somehow worse than accusations.

The rim appeared all at once, the way a cliff appears when you're walking toward it in fog. One moment there was slope and ash; the next, nothing but sky and a vast bowl of shadow dropping away beneath them, ringed in walls of fused black glass. The caldera of Mount Sarn. At its center, deep down, something glowed — not bright, not anymore, but steady. A coal wrapped in a dying hand.

Bram stood at the edge and felt the map in her pack go warm against her spine.

"It knows we're here," she said.

"Of course it does," Odile said. "It's been waiting longer than any of us have been alive. Longer than the Order. Longer than the war." She set down her satchel and began unpacking with the careful ritual Bram had watched three times now: chalk, iron stylus, the scorched map spread flat and weighted with stones at each corner. "Fourth seal. Last one. I'd like everyone to stand back, please, and if anything tries to climb out of the light, do *not* name it. Naming gives it purchase."

"That seems like an oddly specific rule," Isa said.

"The last seal taught me that rule." Odile knelt. Her hands were steady. Bram noticed they were only steady because she was holding them very deliberately still, the way you hold a cup when your whole body wants to shake. "Rennick, if you would. The western anchor point needs more weight than a stone."

Rennick shuffled over and put his boot on the chalk mark. "If this kills me, I want it noted that I died doing paperwork."

"Noted," Bram said. "In the ledger of heroic deaths, under 'administrative.'"

He grinned at her — the old grin, or close enough to it — and turned his face to the wind.

* * *

The seal did not open the way the others had. The first had cracked like ice. The second had unfolded like a flower made of memory. The third had simply *ended*, taking the mountain pass with it into vision.

The fourth seal asked a question.

Bram felt it before Odile's stylus touched the last line — a pressure behind her sternum, gentle as a finger tapping glass. *Why are you here?* Not words. Weight. The same weight she'd felt at the wayside shrine a lifetime ago, when Kestrel had answered her prayer for the first time in years with a pulse of warmth she'd hidden from Odile like a guilty thing.

She looked around. Isa had gone still, eyes wide. Rennick's boot hadn't moved from the chalk. Only Odile seemed unaware, bent over her work, muttering the old archivist's cadence — *anchor, anchor, witness, witness* — and Bram realized with a lurch that the question wasn't being asked of all of them.

It was being asked of her.

*I'm here,* she thought back, clumsy, a woman shouting across a canyon in a language she barely spoke. *I don't know why. I never knew why. You picked my family and I've just been — following.*

The warmth pulsed. It felt, absurdly, like laughter. Or like grief. With Kestrel the two had always lived in the same house.

"Bram." Odile's voice, sharp. "Your hands."

Bram looked down. Light was leaking between her fingers, thin gold seams glowing along the lines of her palms like cracks in a kiln door. It didn't hurt. That frightened her more than pain would have.

"Don't stop," she said.

"I wasn't going to—" But Odile hesitated, stylus hovering, and Bram saw the scholar's war cross her face: the hunger to understand against the fear of what understanding would cost. Two weeks ago Odile had stood in a snowbound camp and said, quietly, *You've known what you are since the second seal, haven't you?* And Bram had lied to her face, and they had both pretended the lie hadn't happened, and the pretending had gotten heavier every mile.

"No," Odile said finally, and touched stylus to map. "Whatever happens — no one stops me."

* * *

The world went white, then went *backwards*.

Bram had braced for another vision of the ancient war, another reel of fire and screaming gods. Instead she stood in a quiet place — a hall without walls, floored in something like water, lit from below. And she was not alone.

A dragon sat in the middle of it.

Not the vast horror of the first vision, not the winged catastrophe that had blotted out skies. This dragon was small. Old. Its scales had gone the color of banked coals, dull at the edges, and it lay curled around a flame no bigger than a candle — and Bram knew, with a certainty that arrived whole and unearned, that the flame was the Ember, and the dragon was what remained of the thing that had carried it, and both of them were tired past the reach of any word she owned.

"You're not a weapon," Bram said. Her voice came out strange in the water-floored hall, doubled, as if someone else were saying it with her.

The dragon lifted its head. Its eyes were the exact brown of her mother's — no. Of the temple matron who'd raised her. No. They were her own eyes, and she understood that too, and it nearly buckled her.

*No,* said the hall, in a voice that was every voice she had ever loved and none of them. *We were never weapons. We were a promise.*

And then it showed her.

Not the war's beginning — she'd had that, seared and awful. This was the end of it. The part no song recorded. Gods and dragons had not destroyed one another in glory. They had simply *worn out*. She saw the last battle, and it was pathetic — two exhausted armies hacking at each other in the rain, gods stumbling like drunkards, dragons too spent to burn. She saw the parley afterward, in a ruined temple, mortals and the few surviving powers sitting in the wreckage like refugees, because that was what they were. And she heard the terms spoken aloud:

*No power shall be hoarded. No power shall be destroyed. What remains of the fire shall be kept, freely given, freely shared, and passed hand to hand among those who choose to carry it — for a flame held by one belongs to no one, and a flame shared belongs to everyone, and only a shared flame can be trusted not to devour.*

The Ember was not a weapon. It was a *covenant*. It burned only because it was given. Hoard it — clutch it to one breast, one bloodline, one god — and it would gutter, because it was made of consent the way ordinary fire was made of air. Destroy it, and the seal on the old war died with it, and everything the Hollow Order feared came crawling home.

And Kestrel — Kestrel had understood first. Had seen the covenant's shape and known that a god could never keep it, because gods *were* hoarding by nature, vast and singular and unable to share themselves. So Kestrel had done the only faithful thing left. It had poured itself out. Into mortal bloodlines. Into smallness. Into silence. Into a valley where magic could not follow, so that the last vessel could grow up unwatched, untempted, *unhoarded* — free to choose.

Kestrel hadn't gone silent. Kestrel had been waiting, for centuries, to hear what its own heart would decide.

Bram came back to herself on her knees at the caldera rim, ash in her hair, tears freezing on her cheeks, and the light still seaming her palms.

"Well?" Rennick's voice, carefully light. "Do we run? Do we fight? Do we open a tavern? I feel like the answer should involve a tavern."

Bram laughed. It came out cracked and wet and real. She got up. Her legs held.

"It's not what any of us thought," she said. "It's not a weapon. It's not even a *god*, not really. It's a promise. A covenant. It burns only if it's shared — freely. If anyone hoards it, it dies. If anyone destroys it—" she glanced at Odile, who had gone pale with the scholar's particular ecstasy, the look she got when a theory became true, "—the old war comes back. Everything the Order is afraid of. Everything they're *causing*."

"So Serah Dain is marching five hundred men up a mountain," Isa said slowly, "to murder a promise."

"To murder the only thing keeping their nightmare sealed," Bram said. "Yes."

"And you?" Odile. Quiet. The question under the question. *And you, who lied to me in the snow?*

Bram looked at her hands. The glow had faded to a faint warmth, a coal's memory. She thought about the choice the vision had laid at her feet, plain as a road: take the Ember entire, become Kestrel whole, immortal and alone, the war sealed forever behind a wall made of herself. Or refuse, and trust the covenant to mortals — flawed, quarreling, card-cheating mortals — to keep it alive.

She found, examining herself the way you probe a bruise, that she already knew. She had known since the shrine. Maybe since the ford, since a dying man had pressed a burnt map into her hands and whispered *the Ember remembers.* The Ember remembered what it was *for*. So did she.

"I'm not going to become a god," she said. "That's not what it's asking. It's asking whether I'll *share*."

Isa stared at her. "Share it. Split a divine covenant four ways like — like dividing up a haul?"

"Five," Bram said. "Counting the mountain."

"That's insane," Isa said. There was a pause. Bram had learned to read Isa's pauses the way sailors read weather, and this one had sunlight in it. "Obviously. When do we start?"

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a ridge below the rim, because even revelations needed sleep, and Rennick announced that he refused to face destiny on an empty stomach. He produced — from some smuggler's pocket dimension Bram had never located — dried fish, a jar of pickled something, and half a bottle of plum brandy that had survived three countries and a poisoning.

"A toast," he said, pouring with a hand that trembled only slightly. "To promises. May ours be shorter than the last one."

"To Kestrel," Bram said, "who shut up for eight hundred years so I could grow up normal, and failed."

"To Odile Vane," Isa said, "who was right about dragons the whole time and is legally forbidden from saying so."

"I will be saying so," Odile said serenely, "for the rest of my life. Possibly loudly. Possibly to the Order, from the top of this mountain, while they're shooting at us."

"To Rennick Cael," Bram said, before he could deflect, "who paid more than any of us know, and gets to stop paying soon. Whatever's left of the bargain — we're finding a way to give it back."

Rennick looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes, in the hollowed places where his family's faces used to live. "Careful," he said at last, lightly. "Keep talking like that and I'll start believing I'm worth rescuing."

"You always were," Isa said. "You're just expensive."

The laughter that went around the fire was tired and genuine, and Bram sat in the middle of it and let it warm her the way the vision's warmth had, and thought: *this is what it burns for. Not thrones. Not seals. This.*

Then Odile, who had been staring east with her cup forgotten, said: "Lights."

Bram followed her gaze. Far below, snaking up the southern switchbacks through the ash-dark, torches. Hundreds of them. Orderly. Patient. A serpent of fire climbing toward the caldera, and at its head, somewhere, a woman in grey armor who believed with her whole broken heart that she was saving the world.

"They'll reach the lower vents by tomorrow night," Odile said. "Serah Dain doesn't climb mountains for a picnic. She'll have powder. She'll have engineers. She means to bring the whole caldera down."

Bram looked at the tiny coal-glow far below in the dark bowl of the mountain, and at the torch-serpent crawling toward it, and felt the cold-air clarity of the vision settle over her like armor.

"Then we go down and meet them," she said. "Tonight. Before she reaches the flame — and before she does something we can't undo."

Nobody argued. That, more than anything, told Bram how far they had come.

She banked the fire, took up her sword, and started down into the dark.
