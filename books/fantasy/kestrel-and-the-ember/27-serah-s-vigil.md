## 27. Serah's Vigil

The powder came up the mountain on the backs of mules, and the mules hated it.

Serah Dain stood at the eastern supply line and watched her soldiers coax the animals along the switchbacks, ash sifting down around them like grey snow that had forgotten how to be beautiful. The beasts balked at every turn. They could smell something in the rock, perhaps, or in the air above the caldera — something old and burning and patient. Mules were honest creatures. They had no creed to override their instincts.

She envied them a little.

"Third load's up, Captain," said Corporal Vess, jogging to her side with his helm under his arm. He was young — too young for what they'd come here to do, though Serah had long ago stopped believing there was an age at which a man became ready to end a god. "Quartermaster says we've got enough charge to bring the eastern vent down into the throat of the mountain. He says it twice more it'll start sounding like bragging."

"Tell him bragging is earned," Serah said. "After tomorrow he can brag until the ash buries him."

Vess grinned, then didn't. That was how it went with the young ones lately. The grin would start and then falter halfway, like a candle finding a draft.

She had been watching that faltering for three days now.

* * *

The camp sat in a bowl below the caldera rim, thirty tents and a command pavilion and a shrine — because the Hollow Order built shrines even here, especially here, small bare altars to the truth that no god deserved one. Serah passed it on her evening rounds out of habit. She never knelt. She had stopped kneeling at nineteen, when they buried her sister in ground that the god-touched had made holy and Serah had understood, with a clarity that had never since left her, that holiness was just a word men used to make murder sound like weather.

"Captain." Ser Ollen fell into step beside her. He was fifty, grey at the temples, steady as a wall, and tonight he was walking too casually, which meant he wanted to talk and didn't want to be seen wanting to talk. "A word?"

"You're having it."

"The men are asking questions."

"The men are always asking questions. It's why we give them orders instead of answers."

He smiled politely at that, the smile of a man who intended to keep talking anyway. "They're asking about the fugitives, Captain. About the Ashgrove woman. The story going round is that she fought you twice and spared you twice, and that she said—" he hesitated, then committed, as a good soldier commits, "—that you're wrong about what gods want."

"I know what I said she said. I was there."

"And are you?"

Serah stopped walking. Above them the caldera rim glowed faintly against the dark, a bruise-colored line where the mountain's heat bled through the stone. Somewhere beyond that rim, in the throat of the world, the last dragon-fire in creation sat guttering like a lamp in a sickroom, waiting for someone to come and either feed it or put it out.

She thought about all the careful answers she had given over twenty years. To recruits. To magistrates. To herself, in the long grey hours before dawn, when conviction needed feeding like any other animal.

"No," she said, and watched Ollen's face change. "I'm not wrong about what gods *do*. What they want is a mystery, and mysteries are luxuries. We are not here to guess at a god's heart, Ser Ollen. We're here to make sure no one ever has to again."

"That's not an answer either, Captain."

"It's the only kind I have left. Go see to the third watch."

He went. But she felt his doubt walking beside her longer than he did, the way you feel a coat after you've taken it off.

* * *

The quartermaster's tent smelled of tallow and blackpowder, and Quartermaster Brannoc — a round, fussy, indispensable man who had once told a knight-commander to his face that his logistics were an insult to arithmetic — was counting fuses by lamplight when Serah entered.

"Don't touch anything," he said, without looking up. "The numbering system is delicate."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

"You would. Everyone dreams of it. Captains especially." He finished his count, wrote the number down, wrote it again because he trusted no single version of any fact, and finally looked at her. His eyes were shrewd and tired. "You've come about the men."

"I've come about the charges."

"Mm. And the men will come up in the second minute of this conversation, so let's not pretend otherwise." He tapped the ledger. "Two hundred barrels, cut and bored, packed with the Order's best blackpowder and the Order's worst secrets. Set at the eastern vent, fired in sequence, and the whole gallery comes down into the magma throat. Nothing survives under that much stone. Not fire. Not whatever's keeping it alive." He paused. "Not anyone standing near it."

"I'm aware."

"Are you? Because I've done this arithmetic too, Captain, and my sums say that if the Ashgrove woman and her people reach the flame before we fire—"

"Then we fire anyway."

Brannoc set down his pen with great care, the way a man sets down something fragile he is resisting the urge to throw. "There are four of them. Four, and a handful of porters. And there are two hundred knights on this mountain who signed on to fight cultists and smugglers, not to bury four souls alive under a million tons of rock and call it piety."

"They won't be buried alive. They'll be given the chance to surrender first. I'll offer it myself."

"And if they refuse?"

"Then they chose the fire over mercy, and the mountain does what mountains do." She heard her own voice, flat and certain, and marveled distantly at it — at how easily certainty still came, like a well-worn sword hilt fitting a hand. "You have a concern, Quartermaster. Say it plainly. You always do."

Brannoc folded his hands. For a moment he looked less like a quartermaster and more like a priest, which was its own kind of joke, in the Hollow Order, where priests were forbidden and arithmetic was sacrament.

"My concern," he said, "is that we are about to do the exact thing we accuse the gods of doing. Deciding from very far away that some lives are kindling." He held up a hand before she could speak. "I'll set the charges. I'll set them beautifully; you know I will. But somebody on this mountain ought to say it out loud, and it seems the duty has fallen to me, as usual, because everyone else is busy being brave."

Serah stood in the lamplight and let the words land, because he had earned the right to land them.

"Noted," she said at last. "And answered. The Ember is not a life, Brannoc. It's a debt — an old one, kept open for centuries, collecting interest in blood. Every cult, every god-touched madman, every village burned because something whispered through a shrine — that's the interest. Tomorrow we close the account."

"And if closing it costs more than it owes?"

"Then mortals pay mortals' debts for once, instead of gods spending us like coin." She turned for the tent flap, then stopped. "Get some sleep, Quartermaster. Tomorrow you'll want your hands steady."

"They're always steady, Captain. It's everything else I worry about."

* * *

She walked the rim alone at midnight, as she had every night since they made the ascent, because a commander should see the ground she means to die on, or in this case the ground she means to drop onto other people.

The caldera opened below her like the mouth of something sleeping. Heat rose from it in slow breaths, and far down in the dark there was a glow — faint, deep, red-gold — that had not been visible from this distance a week ago. Or perhaps it had, and she hadn't let herself look. The Ember. The last living fragment of the dragon-fire that had sealed the ancient war, the covenant older than any kingdom, the thing her Order had sworn across nine generations to extinguish.

*So no god can ever command men again.* The creed was carved inside every Order chapel, and Serah had read it aloud at her sister's grave with such fervor that the presiding knight had wept.

She crouched at the rim and laid her palm flat against the stone. Warm. Warm as a living thing.

Her sister's name had been Liss. She had been fourteen. The cultists who took her had worn the sun-symbol of a dead god and sung while they did it, and afterward, when the Order hunted them down, the survivors had wept and sworn the god had made them do it, and the courts had argued for a year about whether a god could bear guilt, and Serah had sat outside those courtrooms understanding that the argument itself was the obscenity. As long as something existed that men could blame, men would use it. Kill the Ember, kill the last of the old fire, and the excuse died with it. Whatever else was true, that was true. She had built her whole life on that foundation, and a foundation does not ask whether the house upon it is comfortable.

"She spared me twice," Serah said to the dark below. Her voice sounded strange against the mountain's breathing. "Bram Ashgrove. Twice she had a blade at me and put it away. Do you know what that costs a soldier, Captain Ashgrove? It costs her the clean story. Now every time I close my eyes I have to decide whether mercy was a tactic or a belief, and I hate you for making me wonder."

The mountain, naturally, said nothing. Gods were good at silence. It was their oldest trick — let the faithful fill the quiet with their own voices, then take credit for the echo.

Except.

Except that the visions — the fragments her scouts had brought back from the drowned chapel, from prisoners, from the feverish testimony of a captured agent — except that the stories leaking out of that little company did not sound like a god commanding. They sounded like a woman arguing with one. Refusing one. Sharing one, if the wildest accounts were true, and Serah did not believe the wildest accounts, because believing them would mean the foundation was sand.

Below her, the glow pulsed. Once. Slowly. Like something turning over in its sleep.

Serah took her hand off the stone.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dirty copper, and with it came the work.

The charges went down the inner path in relays, barrel after barrel, Brannoc's beautiful terrible arithmetic taking physical form in the galleries above the vent. Serah supervised from the rim station, watching her knights descend into the mountain's throat, and it was there — watching the relay lines snake downward — that the doubts finally surfaced where she could count them.

Vess came to her first. Young Vess, whose grin kept dying. "Captain, the fourth relay's asking — formally asking, they made me write it down — whether the fugitives have been sighted inside the caldera."

"They haven't."

"And if they are? Inside, I mean. Below the firing line." He swallowed. "The order as written doesn't distinguish, Captain. I read it three times."

"The order as written stands. If they're below the line when we fire, they are casualties of the Ember's own stubbornness, and their names go in the record with honor." She said it evenly. She had practiced it evenly. Vess looked at her for a long moment with the particular nakedness of the young, then saluted and went, and Serah noted — in the ledger she kept behind her eyes, the one with two columns — that he had not written anything down.

Then Ser Ollen, at midday, quieter: "Captain, three of the fifth relay asked to be reassigned to the perimeter. They cite — " a pause, a soldier choosing words like steps across ice — "they cite fatigue."

"Granted. All three."

Ollen blinked. "That easily?"

"Doubt on a fuse-line kills more men than the enemy does. Let them guard the mules. The mules don't ask questions." She almost smiled. "Besides, Ollen. If I have to fire those charges over the objections of half my column, I'd rather know exactly who's left holding the torch. A small company of the certain is worth more than a large one of the unsure."

"And are we certain, Captain?" He said it gently, an old man's kindness wrapped around a young man's question. "You, I mean. Are you certain?"

Serah looked out over the caldera, at the ash-haze and the deep buried glow, at the relay lines and the mules and the small stubborn figures of her Order doing the work of ending wonder forever.

"I'm certain enough," she said. "Certainty enough is all anyone gets."

But that night, checking the fuse-lines by lantern with Brannoc, she found herself pausing at the master charge — the great heart of the arrangement, wired to the plunger she herself would throw — and laying her hand on it the way she'd laid her hand on the rim-stone. Cold now. Powder and wood and cord. Just machinery. Just a mortal decision, made by mortal hands, answerable to nothing but the record.

That was the whole point. That had always been the whole point.

"Captain," Brannoc said quietly, behind her. "You'll want to see this."

He led her to the rim station's eastern lookout, where a sentry stood with his spyglass fixed on the caldera's far side — the southern approach, the broken stair, the route any climber would take from the forest below.

At first she saw nothing but ash-dark and stone. Then the sentry handed her the glass, and she found it: a flicker, high on the southern rim. Not firelight — steadier than firelight, warmer, gold shot through with red, moving with purpose. Torchlight, if torches had heartbeats. And beneath it, tiny with distance, figures climbing. Four of them. Five, maybe, the glass trembling with her pulse.

"They came up the south face," the sentry murmured. "In the ash-storm. Nobody climbs the south face in an ash-storm."

Serah lowered the spyglass slowly.

Somewhere down in that glowing throat, the last god-fire in the world was waiting for the woman who had spared her twice. And somewhere on the eastern galleries, two hundred barrels of blackpowder sat wired and numbered and patient, and the plunger waited in its box like a verdict.

"Signal the galleries," Serah said. Her voice was calm. She noticed it the way you notice weather. "All teams to firing positions. And wake the perimeter — if Ashgrove's company reached the rim, then her friends may still be coming up behind her."

She raised the glass again. The gold light on the southern rim had gone still now, gathered, as if the mountain itself were holding its breath.

*They're inside,* she thought. *They're inside, and the clock is running.*

"Captain?" Brannock. "Your orders?"

Serah Dain set down the spyglass, and her hand — her famously steady hand — was not quite steady at all.

"Prepare to fire on my mark," she said.
