## 28. The Guttering

The tunnel down was older than language.

Bram knew that the way she knew a blade's balance — not by study but by the body's own arithmetic. The walls had been cut once, long ago, and then left to weep. Mineral streaks ran down the stone like old tears, and where Odile's charm-lantern passed, they glittered faintly, as if the mountain remembered being beautiful and resented being reminded.

"Mind your footing," Isa said behind her, for the fourth time in an hour.

"I heard you the first three times."

"And yet you keep walking like a woman who intends to fall."

Bram smiled despite herself. It was strange, how quickly a person could relearn how to be teased. For most of her life she'd been the one doing the quiet watching from the edge of other people's jokes, and now here she was, four days out from confession by firelight, four days out from Isa's cold shoulder and its slow, grudging thaw, and the teasing had come back like blood into a numb limb — painful and welcome and proof of something alive.

Isa hadn't forgiven her. Not entirely. But she had taken Bram's hand at the Ember's threshold, or she would soon; Bram could feel it gathering in the girl like weather. And whatever else happened in this mountain, that mattered more than Bram knew how to say.

Odile picked her way ahead, lantern held high, muttering translations under her breath. "This is pre-Imperial script. Older than anything I've published on. Do you understand what that means?"

"That you'll be insufferable about it for the rest of your life," Rennick said.

He was leaning on a borrowed spear-staff, gray-faced, moving with the careful economy of a man spending coins he couldn't replace. The river spirit's bargain still held him upright, but Bram had watched him all morning, and she could see the edges of him going thin. Like paper held too near a candle.

"Insufferable," Odile agreed cheerfully, "and correct, which is worse for everyone."

* * *

The tunnel opened without warning.

One moment there was stone, close and breathing-dark, and the next the passage simply ended, spilling them onto a ledge above a chamber so vast that Bram's first instinct was to step backward, as if she'd walked into someone's house uninvited.

The caldera's heart. Mount Sarn's hollow throat, a mile across and half again as deep, walled in black glass where ancient fire had cooled mid-flow. Stalactites hung from the distant ceiling like the teeth of something that had died with its mouth open. And at the center —

At the center, floating a hand's breadth above a shelf of obsidian, burned the Ember.

Bram had imagined it a hundred ways since the third seal. A furnace. A lake of fire. A dragon's heart the size of a temple bell, still beating after two thousand years.

It was none of those things.

It was small. Small as a candle flame — smaller, almost, a single tongue of gold-white light no taller than her thumb, burning alone in all that darkness, and the sight of it went through Bram like a spear through wet cloth. All the war. All the seals. All the dead men and burned cellars and poisoned blades and the Order's thousand-year crusade — all of it aimed at *that*. At a candle.

"Oh," Odile whispered. Her voice cracked in the middle of the word. She cleared her throat and tried again, archivist to the last: "Oh, it's *guttering*."

"It's what?" Isa asked.

"Guttering. Starving. Look at the air around it." Odile pointed with a trembling finger. "A flame that size should be nothing. Should have gone out millennia ago. It's burning on nothing. On will. On a promise." She pressed both hands over her mouth, and when she spoke again her words came out muffled and wet. "I spent thirty years telling them dragons lived. They laughed me out of every hall in the south. And it was true, and now it's *this*, and I don't know whether to weep or—"

"Weep later," Rennick said gently. "Climb first."

There was a stair. Of course there was a stair — the ancients had built everything twice, once for glory and once for practicality — spiraling down the inner wall in switchbacks of fused glass-stone, each step wide enough for two. Bram went first. Isa fell in beside her without asking, which was its own kind of answer, and Rennick took the rear because he refused, absolutely refused, to be carried or coddled or looked at with pity, and they had all learned not to try.

Halfway down, the warmth began.

Not heat — warmth. It rose to meet them like a host greeting guests at a door, and it touched Bram's face and hands and the backs of her closed eyelids, and it felt — she searched for the word and hated herself for finding it — it felt like being *known*. Like the shrine in the valley, the little moss-eaten altar where she'd prayed to Kestrel for twenty years and gotten silence, except now the silence had turned around and shown her its face.

"Kestrel," she said quietly. Just the name. An old habit, a key tried in a familiar lock.

The warmth pulsed.

And then the world folded.

* * *

She did not fall. That was the strangest part. Her boots stayed on the stair, her hand stayed on the wall, and her companions kept climbing beside her — she could hear Rennick's labored breathing, Odile's counting of steps — but some other part of her was elsewhere, standing in a memory that was not hers, watching a war end.

She saw a plain under a sky the color of a bruise.

Dragons filled it. Hundreds of them, maybe thousands, wheeling in slow ruin, their scales dull with ash, their wings torn like flags after a lost battle. Below them, gods walked — she knew them for gods the way you know a storm for a storm, by scale and pressure — vast figures of stone and river-light and one made entirely of falling snow, and they were fighting the dragons, or they had been fighting the dragons, and now—

Now they were just *stopping*.

That was the thing no song had ever told her. Wars were supposed to end in victory or defeat, in a field strewn with one side's banners. This ended in neither. It ended the way a fistfight between exhausted brothers ends: badly, breathlessly, with both parties kneeling in the mud too tired to land another blow, staring at each other with something worse than hatred.

Grief.

A dragon landed near her — near the memory-her, the ghost standing at the edge of the vision — and Bram saw that it was dying. Its flank rose and fell in long, laboring sweeps, and where its fire should have been there was only a flicker, a guttering gold-white light behind its ribs, small as a candle.

*We are done,* the dragon said. Its voice was not sound. It was the feeling of a hearth in winter, translated. *You are done. Look at us. Look at what we made of the world.*

And the god before it — snow-made, weary past all measure — answered: *Then let it end.*

*It will not end,* the dragon said. *Not truly. One of us will heal first. One of us will remember anger before the other remembers mercy. It is our nature, theirs and mine.* A pause, long as a season. *So we make a promise instead.*

*I am tired of promises,* said the god of snow.

*Keep this one anyway.* The dragon lowered its great head until its brow nearly touched the ground, and the small flame behind its ribs brightened, steadied, gathered itself like a soldier straightening before inspection. *I will give my fire to keeping. Not to winning. To keeping. Let it burn only if it is shared freely among many hands, so that no one keeper can hoard it into a weapon, and let it die if any hand tries. And your side — yours must also promise. No god will command men again. No throne built on divinity. Swear it, and my fire becomes a covenant instead of a crown.*

The snow-god was silent for a long time. In the vision's strange timelessness, Bram watched mountains erode. Watched rivers change their courses. Watched the dragon's breathing grow slower and slower.

*Sworn,* the god said at last. *But who will hold us to it? We are poor keepers of ourselves.*

*Then find better ones,* said the dragon, and smiled — Bram felt it smile, felt the enormous tenderness of it — *find mortals. They break every promise they make, and so they know exactly what a promise costs. That makes them honest in a way we never were.*

And the dragon reached into its own chest, into the place where its fire lived, and drew it out — small, and gold-white, and trembling — and set it upon the world like a candle set in a window.

*Let it gutter if it must,* the dragon said, and its voice was already fading, already becoming wind and stone and sleep. *A kept promise does not need to roar. It only needs to burn.*

The vision folded shut like a book.

* * *

Bram came back to herself on the last step, kneeling, one hand flat against the warm glass-stone. Isa had a grip on her shoulder. Odile was saying her name in the particular clipped tone she used when she'd been saying it for a while.

"I'm here." Bram's voice came out scraped. "I'm fine."

"You were gone ninety seconds," Odile said. "Your eyes were open and you were smiling, which was frankly unsettling, and then you weren't smiling anymore, which was worse."

"What did you see?" Isa asked. No accusation in it. Just the question, bare and brave.

Bram got her feet under her. She looked up the stair, back toward the dark mouth of the tunnel, and thought about Serah Dain somewhere below them with powder charges and a creed, thought about hollow men who believed fire was a leash and gods were tyrants, and understood suddenly, completely, why the truth of it would break Serah's heart rather than mend her purpose.

Because the war hadn't ended in triumph. It had ended in exhaustion and a handshake between two dying things, and everything after — the temples, the Orders, the crusades, the burning of Odile's cellar, the arrow through the stranger at the ford — all of it had been people fighting over the *terms of a promise*, without ever having heard the promise itself.

"They didn't win," Bram said. "Either side. There was no victory. There was—" She stopped. Started again. "Two armies knelt down in the mud together because they were too tired to hate anymore. And the dragon gave up its fire not as a weapon and not as a trophy. As a *promise*. Kept too long, maybe. Kept alone, certainly. That's why it's guttering." She looked down the last few steps, toward the small gold light waiting in the dark. "Promises aren't meant to be kept by one person. That's not keeping. That's just carrying."

Rennick made a low sound in his chest — not quite a laugh. "Sounds like a man I bargained with once. River spirit. Everything it owned, it had won, and it was the loneliest creature I ever met." He shifted his weight off the spear-staff, and Bram pretended not to see how much that cost him. "Well. Come on, then. Introductions are owed."

They descended the final steps together.

* * *

Up close, the Ember was smaller still, and more impossible.

It floated above the obsidian shelf without scorching it, a thumb-height flame of gold shot through with white, and it moved the way a living thing moves — leaning, listening, turning its attention. The warmth coming off it was gentle as a hand laid over a fevered brow. Around it, the air smelled of woodsmoke and, absurdly, of bread. Fresh bread. Bram's eyes stung.

"Somebody's grandmother," she murmured.

"What?" Isa said.

"The smell. It smells like bread baking. Like somebody's kitchen." She shook her head slowly. "Two thousand years of covenant, and it smells like Sunday morning."

Odile had produced a notebook from somewhere — the woman had notebooks hidden on her person the way other people hid knives — and was writing with one hand while wiping her eyes with the other sleeve, accomplishing neither task well. "The resonance patterns alone. If I could just — no. No, Vane, put the notebook away, you absolute pedant, you're standing in front of the last living dragon-fire in creation and taking *notes*—"

"You're crying on your notes," Isa observed.

"They're waterproof notes."

"They're visibly not."

"History is *damp*, Isa, that's what I keep telling people—"

Bram tuned them out, the way you tune out the voices of people you love when something important is happening, and stepped forward alone.

The Ember turned toward her.

There was no other way to describe it. The little flame inclined, the way a person inclines their head when a familiar name is spoken across a crowded room, and the warmth gathered itself and poured into her, and Bram Ashgrove stood in the caldera of Mount Sarn and finally, finally stopped being afraid of the answer to the question she'd been carrying since the second seal.

*Little vessel,* something said — not in words, in hearth-warmth, in the memory of a shrine and twenty years of unanswered prayers that had never actually gone unanswered, only *saved up*. *You came.*

"I came," Bram said aloud. Her voice didn't shake. She was proud of that, and slightly ashamed of being proud. "I brought people."

*I see them.* The flame flickered, and Bram would have sworn — would have testified under oath — that it sounded amused. *The scholar who argues with herself. The thief who is dying and has told no one how badly. The angry one who loves you anyway, which is the most expensive kind of love there is.*

Bram's throat tightened. "Yeah. Those are mine."

*And you have seen the ending. You know now what I am.*

"A promise," Bram said. "Kept too long. Kept alone."

*Yes.* And the sorrow that came off the flame then was so old and so patient that it made the vision's grief look like a child's tantrum. Two thousand years of standing watch in the dark, smelling of bread no one baked, warming a mountain no one visited. *I was meant to be shared. That was the whole of the covenant, and the whole of its flaw — I cannot share myself. I can only be taken up. Offered. Chosen. The dragon knew this and trusted mortals to choose, and mortals built an Order instead, and hid me deeper, and argued over my terms for forty generations while I burned down to this.* The flame guttered, just slightly, just for a heartbeat, and Bram's heart went with it. *I am so tired, little vessel.*

Behind her, she heard Isa step forward. Heard Odile stop writing. Heard Rennick's staff click against the glass-stone as he straightened, refusing, even now, to lean.

Bram held up one hand — *wait* — and the strange thing was, they waited. All of them. The found family, the fractured, furious, faithful lot of them, standing in the ash-light at the bottom of the world, giving her room to speak with a god.

"I know what you're going to offer me," she said. "The full mantle. Become Kestrel entire. Seal the war forever, alone, immortal." She swallowed. "And I know what I'm going to say. But I want you to hear it from me properly, because you've been listening to us for two thousand years and nobody's bothered to talk *back*."

The flame leaned closer. Listening. Always listening.

"So here's what I think," Bram said, and her voice did shake now, and she let it. "I think you're not a weapon, and you're not a god, not really, not anymore. You're a promise, and promises don't need one keeper with everything. They need many keepers with a little each. I think the dragon was right about mortals — we break everything, and that's exactly why we're honest about cost. And I think—" she laughed, wet and surprised, "—I think you smell like bread because you've been waiting all this time for somebody to sit down and eat with you. That's what sharing is. That's the whole rite. Nobody ever figured it out because everybody who found you wanted to *own* you or *kill* you, and nobody just thought to pull up a chair."

Silence. The good kind — the kind that meant something enormous was turning over.

Then, softly, the flame flared — not larger, but brighter, steadier, the way a face lights up — and the warmth rolled out through the chamber and up the stairs and into the bones of the mountain, and Bram heard Kestrel laugh.

It sounded like her mother might have sounded, if she'd ever known her. It sounded like home.

*Ask them,* the Ember said. *All of them. See who chooses.*

Bram turned around.

Her family stood in the gold light — Odile with ink on her fingers and tears on her waterproof notes, Isa with her jaw set and her eyes shining, Rennick upright through sheer spite and love, gray as ash and grinning like a smuggler who'd just spotted the customs boat sailing the wrong way.

"Well?" Rennick said. "What's it offering? Eternal life? Because I've priced that out recently and the exchange rate is terrible."

Bram wiped her face with her sleeve. "It's offering a choice," she said. "And I need to ask you all something, and I need you to really think before you—"

The mountain shuddered.

Dust sifted from the ceiling in long gray curtains. Somewhere far above — up through the switchback stairs, up through the sealed tunnels, up through a mile of stone — came a sound Bram knew from a lifetime of mercenary work and had hoped never to hear again in this life:

Powder charges. Plural. Being set.

And beneath the rumble, faint but unmistakable, drifting down the throat of the caldera, a voice she knew — level, disciplined, carrying the way a knight-captain's voice carries across a battlefield:

*"Seal the vents. Nothing comes out of that mountain alive."*

The Ember guttered.

Bram looked at her family, and they looked back at her, and there was no more time to ask anything politely.
