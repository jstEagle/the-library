## 29. Betrayal at the Rim

Rennick Cael had been counting porters the way other men counted coins, and he didn't like what the arithmetic was telling him.

There were six of them hired back in Vell — six strong backs to haul rope, powder-cakes of dried rations, and Odile's absurd collection of brass instruments up the ash-slopes of Mount Sarn. Six. He'd checked at every camp. Every morning, six faces in the grey dawn light, grumbling about their feet.

This morning there were five, and one of them was wearing a coat that hadn't been his yesterday.

"Morning, Tam," Rennick said pleasantly, falling into step beside the man whose name was not Tam. "Sleep well?"

"Well enough." The false Tam had a Vell accent laid over something harder underneath. A soldier's accent. Rennick knew it the way a fish knows water.

"Funny thing," Rennick went on, scratching under his jaw where three days of stubble lived. "I could've sworn we had a fellow named Doss with us. Big lad. Snores like a landslide. You wouldn't have seen him?"

"He left in the night."

"Did he now. Took his boots too, I suppose. And his face."

The false Tam looked at him then, just for a heartbeat too long, and Rennick smiled his best smuggler's smile — the one that said *I'm an idiot, please ignore me* — and dropped back to walk beside Isa instead.

He didn't tell Bram. Not yet. Not because he didn't trust her, but because he trusted her *too much*, in the specific sense that she would stop the column right there on the scree, four hours from the caldera rim, with the Order's full column a day behind and closing, and try to solve the problem with honesty. Bram Ashgrove solved everything with honesty. It was her most exhausting virtue.

No. This was smuggler's work. You watched. You waited. You found out what the rat was signaling, and to whom, and you chose your moment.

Besides — and here was the thought he kept turning over like a river-stone, smooth and cold — he wasn't sure how many moments he had left to choose from.

The bargain with the river spirit sat in him like a stone in a boot. One more season, it had said. He'd paid for it with his mother's laugh, his father's hands mending nets, the names of his sisters. He woke some mornings reaching for those memories the way a tongue finds the gap of a missing tooth, and found only a polished absence, a story someone had told him about himself: *you had a family; they died; you grieved.* The grief remained. The love remained, somehow, like heat lingering in a hearthstone after the fire's gone out. But the particulars were gone, and some mornings he felt less like a man than like a coat someone else had worn well.

One more season. And the season was spending itself fast, up this mountain, in this wind.

* * *

They made the rim by late afternoon, under a sky the color of old pewter. The caldera opened below them like the mouth of something sleeping — a vast bowl of black glass and grey ash, and at its heart, faint as a coal breathed upon, a glow.

Bram stood at the edge for a long time without speaking. Rennick watched her watch it, and thought: *she knows.* She'd known since the third seal, probably. Known what she was, what the flame would ask of her, and she'd carried it up this mountain alone because that was what Bram did — she picked up heavy things and never once asked anyone to help set them down.

Well. That was about to end, if Rennick had anything to say about it. And he usually did, loudly.

"There's a path down," Odile said, spectacles fogged, consulting her instruments. "The eastern stair. It descends into the inner chamber through the old vent-tunnel. The seals should—" she caught herself, glanced at Bram, softened. "The last seal opens at the threshold. Then the Ember itself."

"Then let's not keep a candle waiting," Rennick said. "Candles get nervous."

It was Isa who noticed first. She always noticed things — she had her father's eyes, though she'd have thrown anyone off the mountain for saying so. She touched Rennick's arm and nodded, very slightly, toward the false Tam.

Who had drifted apart from the line of porters. Who stood at the rim's edge with his hand raised, holding something small and dark against the grey sky.

A signal-glass. Catching the last light. Flashing it east, toward the saddle of the mountain where, if Rennick's instincts were worth the powder to blow them up, the Order's advance team was already climbing.

"Oh, you absolute turnip," Rennick murmured.

He was moving before he finished deciding to move — which, in fairness, described most of his life. Three long strides across the ash, and he had the false Tam by the collar, spinning him around, and the man's face changed from soldier-blank to something genuinely startled, which was flattering, because Rennick was half-fae and looked like a strong wind might finish him.

"You're wondering," Rennick said conversationally, twisting the man's wrist until the signal-glass dropped and skittered away over the rocks, "whether you can take me. Let me save us both the suspense. You can't. But here's the real question—"

The question arrived without being asked, in the form of a sound.

A *thump*. Deep. Felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard. Then another, and another, spaced like a patient heartbeat, coming from the eastern slope below them — and the whole rim shuddered, and a plume of ash jumped off the mountainside two hundred yards down, and Rennick understood, with the terrible clarity of a man who has spent his life around things that go bang, exactly what he was looking at.

"They're not attacking the camp," he said. "They're sealing the path."

"The stair," Odile breathed. "If they collapse the vent-tunnel entrance—"

Another thump. Closer. The ground rolled like a ship in weather. Somewhere below, rock the size of a house let go of the mountain and went bounding down into the caldera's throat.

Bram was already shouting orders — get the porters clear, get to the tunnel mouth, go, *go* — and the company broke into a stumbling run along the rim, ash kicking up behind them, and Rennick ran too, because running was the plan, except that the smuggler in him, the part that had spent thirty years reading rivers and reading men, was doing different arithmetic.

The tunnel mouth was a black arch in the caldera's inner wall, maybe sixty yards down a switchback trail. The charges were going off in sequence, working their way toward it. Someone competent had laid them. Someone who understood that you didn't need to bury the tunnel — you just needed to bring enough of the mountainside down to plug it like a cork in a bottle.

Sixty yards. The next charge would be close. Maybe thirty seconds' worth of close.

And here was the thing about corks: they could be pulled as well as pushed. The rubble wouldn't seal clean. There'd be gaps, cracks, a throat of broken stone — and somewhere in that throat, a mechanism, because the Order's sappers were thorough people and thorough people built their demolitions to be *adjustable*. Fuse-channels. Release-props. The little wooden wedges and iron cradles that held a charge in place until it was wanted elsewhere.

He'd grown up pulling cargo out of places cargo wasn't meant to go. Give him ten minutes and a crowbar and half a chance, and he could keep that tunnel breathing.

Give him ten minutes.

"How long does the spirit's season buy me?" he asked himself, and the honest answer was: nobody knew, least of all the spirit, which had taken its payment and gone back to sleep in its cold green water without so much as a receipt.

"Bram!" he called.

She turned, mid-stride, ash in her hair, and for one moment their eyes met, and he saw her start to understand — saw her do the same arithmetic he'd done, faster, because she was Bram — and saw her open her mouth to say *no*.

"Don't," he said. "Don't waste the breath. I'm faster than you downhill, I'm better at stones than any of you, and I'm the only one expendable, which is a sentence I've been waiting my whole charming life to use."

"You are not—" she began.

"Expendable? I absolutely am. It's my finest quality. Ask anyone." He grinned at her, and it cost him more than the climb had. "Get everyone inside. Get to the flame. I'll keep the door open."

"Rennick—"

"Ines wept, woman, argue on your own time." He shoved past her, took the switchback at a speed that would have appalled any physician, and heard her shout something after him that he pretended was *thank you* rather than *come back*, because both were true and only one was useful.

* * *

The tunnel mouth was an arch of fitted stone older than any kingdom, carved with dragons worn to suggestions of dragons. Above it, the slope groaned. Dust sifted down like flour.

Rennick found the first charge exactly where competence would put it: tucked into a fissure beside the arch, packed in clay, its fuse-channel running up-slope to a firing post hidden somewhere in the rocks. Beside it, propping the charge away from a natural drain-crack in the stone, a wooden wedge the size of his forearm.

He got his fingers on the wedge and hauled.

It moved a finger's width. The mountain moved back — another detonation, closer now, close enough to spit grit against his cheek — and somewhere above, voices shouted in the Order's clipped cadence. They'd seen him. Of course they'd seen him. A lone figure prying at their work was hard to miss even through ash-haze.

A crossbow bolt hit the stone a hand's width from his ear and burst into splinters.

"Rude," he told the slope, and hauled again.

The wedge came free with a shriek of wood, and the charge slumped, and he did the fastest worst job of demolition-disabling in the history of the trade: he dug the fuse-powder out with his knife, pissed on the rest, and kicked the whole arrangement into the drain-crack where no one would find it in time to matter. One charge down. The echo of the last big blast was still rolling when he counted the gaps between the next ones and did not like the number.

Two more charges along the approach trail. Then whatever they'd planted inside the tunnel throat itself.

He worked downslope, low and quick, using boulders the way he'd used crates in a hundred warehouses. Bolt after bolt chased him. One tore through his sleeve and stung a line of fire across his ribs — shallow, insulting, the mountain's way of charging toll. At the second charge he didn't bother with finesse; he simply threw himself onto it, clawed the clay packing loose with bare fingers, and hurled the charge as far down-scree as his arms allowed before diving flat. It went off in the open with a whump that deafened his left ear and showered him in pebbles, and he lay there laughing, actually laughing, wheezing in ash-thick air, because it was either laugh or think, and thinking was off the menu.

The third charge was inside the tunnel. Of course it was.

He reached the arch as the light behind him turned orange — they'd fired the slope charges in earnest now, a rolling thunder walking down the mountainside, and the eastern wall of the approach was beginning to come down in earnest, tons and tons of it, sliding toward the trail he'd just cleared.

Which meant the others were inside already. Which meant the door needed to stay open exactly long enough for the dust to settle behind them, and then—

And then it needed to close. Because the Order's knights were coming down that slope behind the falling rock, and if the tunnel stayed open, they'd follow the company straight into the heart of the last living fire in the world.

Rennick stood in the archway, deaf in one ear, bleeding from a dozen small places, and looked at the third charge sitting smugly in its niche forty paces down the tunnel, and understood the shape of the thing with perfect, horrible simplicity.

Someone had to be inside when it closed.

* * *

The tunnel was tall enough to stand in, mostly, and lit by the faint ember-glow seeping up from far below, a light like the memory of sunset. His boots rang on ancient paving. The dragon-carvings on the walls watched him pass with eyes rubbed featureless by centuries, and Rennick tipped an imaginary hat to each of them, because his mother — his mother, whoever she had been, whatever her laugh had sounded like — had taught him manners.

Forty paces in, he found the charge. Bigger than the others. Set into a side-gallery directly beneath a shelf of fractured rock that overhung the main passage. When it fired, the shelf would come down and the tunnel would be a tomb with a very exclusive guest list.

Beside it, the release-prop: an iron cradle on a screw-jack, the kind of elegant spiteful engineering the Order specialized in. Turn the screw, the prop retracts, the charge drops to a firing position against the prop-line, and the fuse burns from a slow-match that was even now hissing quietly in its little clay pot, patient as a spider.

He could jam it. He could jam it and run, and the tunnel would hold, and the knights would come pouring in behind the company, and Serah Dain's steel would reach the Ember, and everything everyone had climbed this mountain for would die in a cellar of collapsed stone.

Or.

Or he could wait for the company to get deep enough — deep enough that the collapse sealed *behind* them — and then pull the pin himself, on his own schedule, with the Order's vanguard halfway down the approach and nowhere near cover.

The math was ugly and it was also, unfortunately, the only sum on the page.

Rennick sat down on the cold paving beside the charge, took out the flask he'd been saving, and drank. It tasted like plums and bad decisions. He offered a toast to the carved dragons.

"You lot had a war," he told them. "Gods and fire and the end of the world, presumably. Very dramatic. And it all comes down to a half-fae smuggler with one working ear, sitting in your hallway, deciding whether to pull a lever." He drank again. "No pressure on me, then."

He thought about the season the river spirit had sold him. Thought about how he'd imagined spending it — some warm delta town, cards, cheap wine, a porch, the particular luxury of watching weather happen without being in it. A whole season. He'd been cheated, probably. Spirits always rounded down.

But here was the joke, the good one, the one he'd have told at any table in Vell if his ribs weren't cracked: he couldn't remember his family's faces anymore, but he could remember *this* — Isa's face the first time she won a hand off him and realized he'd let her, no, wait, that he absolutely had not let her, that she'd simply outdrawn him fair and square and gloated for a full day. Odile explaining, for the ninth time, why his smuggling routes proved her thesis about pre-war trade corridors, and getting visibly annoyed when he agreed with her. Bram standing in a doorway in the rain outside Greywater, counting heads like a shepherd, making sure every stray was in before she slept.

He couldn't remember the people who made him. But these people — these improbable, stubborn, infuriating people — had made themselves into the shape of a family while he wasn't paying attention, and that, he decided, was a better bargain than any spirit ever struck.

The slow-match hissed. Down the tunnel, faint through the stone, he could hear the others' voices, echoing deeper — Odile reciting seal-liturgies, Isa's sharp laugh, Bram's low steady answers. Going down. Getting further.

Above and behind, the Order's knights were picking their way through the settling ash of the approach. He could hear them too. Boots on scree. The creak of armor. A sergeant's voice: *"Careful — the smuggler may still be in the passage—"*

"May be?" Rennick called out, cheerful, his voice booming down the ancient stone. "I'm *definitely* in the passage! Come and get me! Mind the third step!"

A pause. Then, distantly, the unmistakable sound of several heavily armed men deciding whether an obviously insane person was a trap.

Rennick put his hand on the screw-jack.

"Not yet," he murmured to it. "Not yet. Let them get to the bottom. Let them find the door. Let them—" his voice did something embarrassing, and he let it, since there was no one to witness, which was rather the point of everything, "—let them have their hour."

He settled back against the cold stone, flask in hand, deaf ear to the mountain, and began to hum an old delta song about a girl and a riverboat, the melody intact even though every word of it had been sold away.

Below, the glow brightened. Above, the boots grew louder.

Rennick Cael smiled, spat on his palms, and gripped the screw.
