## 31. Two Hands on the Flame

The Ember was small.

That was the first thing Bram understood, standing on the black glass shelf above the caldera's throat, and it undid her more than any grandeur could have. She had carried a map scorched by dragon-fire across half a continent. She had buried friends, bled for seals, watched Rennick walk back into a collapsing tunnel with a smile he'd bought from a river spirit. She had expected — she didn't know what. A furnace. A sun in a stone cradle. Something that would justify all of it.

Instead: a flame no bigger than a candle, burning in a hollow of the rock like a bird cupped in two hands. It did not flicker. It did not waver. It simply *was*, and the air around it hummed with a patience so old that Bram's bones ached to hear it.

"It's dying," Isa said behind her. Her voice cracked on the word.

"No," Bram said softly. "It's *waiting*. There's a difference."

Odile stood at the edge of the shelf, one hand pressed flat against the glassy stone, her lips moving as she read something written there in characters older than any archive she'd been exiled over. "The covenant," she murmured. "Bram — the fourth seal wasn't a warning. It was instructions." She looked up, and in the ember-light her eyes were wet and fierce. "It burns if freely shared. It dies if hoarded or destroyed. Those are the only two rules. Everything else — the war, the silence, the bloodlines — everything else was just *people* deciding what to do around those rules."

"Then people decided badly," Isa said.

"People usually do," Odile said. "That's rather my field of study."

The tunnel behind them had gone quiet ten minutes ago. Quiet in the way that meant Rennick was no longer holding it, or no longer needed to, and neither Bram nor Isa nor Odile had said his name aloud since. Saying it would make it a thing that had already happened. Not saying it kept him, for a few minutes more, alive somewhere back in the dark with his knives and his cheating hands and his terrible singing voice.

Bram turned back to the flame.

She knew what she had come to do. She had known since the second seal, since the vision of Kestrel pouring itself into mortal blood like water into dry earth, hiding the Ember's location inside generations of women who never knew what they carried. She had known since the winter camp, since the confession by firelight, since Isa had looked at her with an expression Bram would carry to whatever grave she earned.

She stepped forward and knelt at the hollow's edge.

The heat was gentle. That surprised her most. After a lifetime in a valley where magic couldn't follow, after scars and arrows and a magistrate's brand burned into her forearm, she had braced for pain. Instead the warmth rose up to meet her like a dog that remembered being loved, and she heard — not with her ears, but somewhere behind her sternum, where Kestrel had lived silent for thirty years —

*Bram Ashgrove.*

"I know," she whispered. "I know what you are. I know what I am. I'm here."

*You may take all of me,* the voice said, and it was not a command and not a plea; it was the tone of someone setting the last of their possessions on a table and stepping back. *Every year of you. Every scar. You will be Kestrel entire. The war stays sealed. The valley sleeps safe. And you will be alone, Bram, for a very long time.*

"How long?"

*I stopped counting when your grandmother's grandmother was young.*

Bram closed her eyes.

She saw it clearly, the way she'd seen the visions: herself unchanging while Isa grew old and sharp-tongued and wonderful and then was gone. Odile's archives turning to dust in libraries she outlived. New faces, always new faces, because keeping people meant losing them, and she would have to keep choosing it, century after century, alone with a god's memories wearing a mercenary's face. The loneliness wouldn't come all at once. That was the trap of it. It would come the way rust comes — slowly, patiently, until one day the whole shape of her had quietly corroded through.

"You're offering me everything," Bram said aloud, "and the price is everyone."

*Yes.* A pause, and then, with something that in a mortal voice would have been rueful: *I have watched your kind for a long time, little vessel. I thought you might say yes. You have always been good at carrying things alone.*

"That's not a compliment," Bram said. "That's just what happens to orphans."

And then a hand closed around hers.

Not the hand she'd extended toward the flame — her other one, hanging loose at her side, forgotten. Fingers laced through hers, calloused from sword-work, trembling slightly, gripping hard enough to hurt.

Isa Varrow knelt beside her at the edge of the hollow, and her eyes were blazing, and she was crying without any apparent awareness of it.

"Don't," Isa said.

"Isa—"

"*Don't.*" Her voice broke and she let it. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to stand there doing the noble arithmetic again — one of me for all of them, that's a fair trade, I've done worse — no. No." She wiped her face with her free hand, furious at her own tears, which was so thoroughly Isa that Bram almost laughed. "My father died carrying your secret to you. He died believing the fire should be *kept*, guarded, hoarded in secret by one bloodline forever, and look where that got him. Look where it got all of us." She squeezed Bram's hand harder. "So no. If the fire needs hands, it gets both of ours. If it needs a life, it gets pieces of all of ours. We are not spending you, Bram Ashgrove. I spent four hundred miles learning to love you and I'll be damned before I spend you."

Bram stared at her.

Four hundred miles. Ambushes and salt flats and poisoned blades and a bridge where Isa had killed her first man and gone white and silent and let Bram hold her until the shaking stopped. Four hundred miles of rage curdling into kinship, of card games Rennick cheated at, of Odile lecturing them all about seal-craft while secretly mending their socks. Four hundred miles of Isa slowly, stubbornly deciding that the woman her father died reaching was worth reaching too.

"The flame has to go somewhere," Bram said quietly. "If not me—"

"Then *us*," Isa said. "All of us. That's what the seal says, isn't it?" She looked past Bram to Odile. "Freely shared. It doesn't say shared by one person. It says *freely*. So we share it. All of us who came. And the valley too — the valley that forgot magic. Let it remember."

Odile made a sound that was half sob and half scholarly triumph. "The covenant was never meant to be *held*," she breathed. "It was meant to be *passed*. Like a story. Like a hearth-fire. Oh, we fools — every keeper, every age, we all assumed the flame wanted a single guardian because *we* assume power wants a throne—" She pressed both hands to her mouth.

The Ember flared.

Not much. A candle becoming two candles' worth of light, no larger than a fist. But the hum changed pitch, and Bram felt it move through the stone floor and up through her knees and into the place where Kestrel lived, and the voice that spoke next was warmer than it had been in thirty years of silence.

*Two hands,* it said. *Yes. Yes, that is how it began.*

* * *

Isa hadn't believed in gods. That was the thing nobody in the company fully understood except maybe Rennick, who believed in everything and worshipped nothing. Isa's father had prayed to Kestrel every night of her childhood, kneeling in front of a chipped wooden icon, and Isa had knelt beside him and felt absolutely nothing, and hated herself for feeling nothing, and finally decided the gods were stories powerful men told to keep the rest of them kneeling.

Then her father died whispering "the Ember remembers" to a stranger, and Isa had followed a map instead of a god, and somehow the map had led her here, to a candle-flame in a mountain's throat, kneeling in the ash-light next to the woman she'd once put a knife to.

And the flame was looking at her.

She couldn't have explained how she knew that. It had no eyes. But attention settled on her the way sunlight comes through a window — sudden, specific, warm on exactly one patch of skin.

*Isa Varrow,* it said, and her father's voice had sounded nothing like this and exactly like this. *Your father carried me wrong. He carried me like a debt. You carry me like a question. Questions are better.*

"I'm not asking for anything," Isa said, and her voice shook. "I'm not praying. I don't — I don't know how to do this."

*Nobody does. That is why it must be given freely. If it required skill, only the skilled would hold it, and they would build towers and call themselves necessary.* The warmth deepened. *Take my hand, Isa Varrow. Both of yours, if you like. I have been held by one pair for too long.*

Isa looked at Bram.

Bram was looking at her with an expression Isa had seen only once before — on the salt road, the night Isa had finally told the story of her childhood, the night her father's letters had come out of her pack and been passed around the fire like relics. It was the expression of someone watching a door open that they'd believed was wall.

"If this goes wrong," Isa said, "if it burns us up, eats us, turns us into — into vessels, into things—"

"It won't," Bram said. "It's not taking. It never was. That's what I finally understand." Her thumb moved over Isa's knuckles. "Kestrel didn't pour itself into my bloodline to hide. It poured itself out because *that's what fire does*. It shares itself or it goes out. Everything else — the silence, the secrecy, the Order, all of it — is just three thousand years of people forgetting that."

"So we remind it," Isa said.

"We remind it."

They turned back to the flame together, shoulder to shoulder, and Isa reached out and laid her palm against the hollow's rim beside Bram's, and the world went white — not painfully, not blindingly, but *completely*, the way a chord is complete when the last note joins it.

She felt her father.

Not a memory of him — *him*, or the shape of him that had traveled inside the map, inside the mission, inside the ember of purpose that had kept her walking after grief should have stopped her. He was laughing. He was saying something she couldn't quite hear, but the tone of it was unmistakable, the tone he'd used when she'd finally beaten him at cards at fourteen: *There she is. There's my girl.*

The flame poured.

It did not pour into her. That was the miracle she'd spend the rest of her life failing to describe. It poured *through* her, the way light pours through a window and keeps going, and it left warmth behind in the glass but took nothing away. She felt it pass out of her other side and into Bram, and feel Bram's steadiness, Bram's long endurance, Bram's terrible habit of carrying things alone finally, *finally* set down. And beyond Bram — outward, downward, through the mountain's roots, seeking.

Seeking the valley.

"Oh," Isa heard herself say. "Oh, it's going *home*."

*Home is wherever the keepers are,* said the voice, and it was fading now, not dying but *distributing*, a single note becoming a chord becoming a music that no longer needed a musician. *I was never a god of fire. I was a god of hearths. Someone just wrote it down wrong.*

The light crested, held, and softened.

When Isa could see again, the hollow was empty. No candle. No flame. Just a ring of warm stone, and four people kneeling around it breathing like they'd run a great distance, and a glow — faint, unmistakable, *alive* — rising beneath each of their skins. Bram's forearms. Isa's palms. Odile's ink-stained fingers. And somewhere back in the dark of the tunnel, Isa realized with a jolt of grief and wonder, a fifth share waiting in a body that had bargained away its memories but not its hands.

Rennick. They'd have to bring Rennick his piece. The river spirit's bargain had taken his family's faces, but it hadn't taken his family's *fire*, and Isa found herself laughing through tears at the thought of his expression when they told him he was a keeper now. He'd want payment. He'd absolutely try to charge them.

"It worked," Odile whispered. She was staring at her own glowing fingertips like a woman reading the first page of a book no one had ever written. "It actually — three thousand years of theology and the answer was *pass it around*—"

Bram pulled Isa into her arms.

It was not graceful. Isa's elbow caught Bram's chin; Bram's knee ground into Isa's shin; neither of them cared even slightly. They held on in the ash-light, two women who had started this journey pointing weapons at each other, and Isa felt the new warmth under her own skin reach toward the warmth under Bram's and settle there, comfortable, like a cat finding its spot.

"I forgive you," Isa said into her shoulder. "For the secret. For letting my father die not knowing you already knew. I'm still angry. I'll probably be angry for years. But I forgive you."

"That's fair," Bram said thickly. "Angry and forgiven is more than I hoped for."

"It's what family is," Isa said, and then heard herself say it, and didn't take it back.

* * *

They were still on the floor of the caldera, still tangled together like children after a nightmare, when the light changed.

Not the warm light. A different kind — orange-white, stuttering, thrown from the eastern wall of the chamber. Odile saw it first. She went rigid.

"Bram." Her voice dropped to the flat, precise register she used for citations. "The eastern vent. Do you hear that?"

Bram listened. Beneath the mountain's slow heartbeat she caught it: footsteps. Many. The metallic song of gear being rigged. And threaded through it, faint but unmistakable, the smell of blackpowder.

"They're not coming down the main tunnel," Bram said, rising, her hand finding her sword. "Rennick sealed the main tunnel."

"Then they brought their own door." Odile was on her feet, pulling Isa up. "Serah Dain doesn't knock. She *demolishes*."

The first charge went off above them — muffled, distant, a cough of thunder through stone — and dust sifted down through the ember-glow like grey snow. Somewhere overhead, iron groaned. The mountain had held this covenant for three thousand years, and mortals had arrived with drills and powder and a creed, and the mountain, for the first time in its long patient life, sounded afraid.

Bram drew her blade, and the steel caught the new light under her skin and threw it back gold.

"Get behind me," she said. "Both of you. Whatever comes down that vent — nobody else dies today. Nobody."

Above them, through the smoking crack in the world, the first of the Hollow Order's knights began to descend.
