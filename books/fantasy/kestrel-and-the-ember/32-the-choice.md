## 32. The Choice

The Ember was small.

That was the first thing Bram understood, standing at the bottom of the world with ash drifting down around her like grey snow: all of this — the sealed war, the silent god, the map scorched into a dying man's hands, the miles of salt and stone and blood spent getting here — all of it had been guarding something no bigger than a candle flame.

It sat in a hollow of black glass at the caldera's heart, and it burned with a light that did not flicker. Not gold, not red. A color she had no name for, the color of the moment just before dawn when you can't say whether it's night or morning. It made no sound. It demanded nothing. And yet Bram's knees nearly buckled, because she knew it. The way you know your own heartbeat. The way you know the shape of a room you dreamed in as a child.

*Oh,* she thought, absurdly. *Hello.*

Isa stood at her shoulder. Odile behind them both, breathing hard, her spectacles fogged with the caldera's heat, one hand pressed to the wall of glass as if she needed to touch the world to believe it still existed. Rennick was above, holding a tunnel against the Hollow Order with a borrowed season of life and a smile he hadn't earned honestly. And somewhere up the slope, Serah Dain was doing arithmetic with powder charges.

But all of that was very far away just now.

The Ember pulsed. Once. Like a question.

"I know," Bram said aloud, and her voice sounded strange in the vast hush, too human, too small. "I know what you're going to ask."

"Bram." Odile's voice, careful. "What is it doing?"

"Talking." Bram took a step closer. The heat wasn't heat, exactly — it was more like standing in a doorway in winter, one side of you in the cold house and one side in the warm kitchen, and the doorway deciding which one you belonged to. "Not in words. It hasn't used words since the third seal. It just... shows you things."

"What things?" Isa asked. Her hand had found the hilt of her knife out of habit. Bram almost smiled. Isa Varrow, who had crossed a country to avenge a father, still reaching for steel in a room made of light.

"It shows you what it wants." Bram knelt at the edge of the black glass hollow. Up close, the flame was not still at all — it moved the way a sleeping animal breathes, slow and vast and trusting. "And it's showing me—"

She stopped. Because it was showing her, and the showing was not gentle.

* * *

It showed her a morning in the temple, forty years gone. She was five years old, maybe six, sitting on the steps of the shrine with her knees drawn up, watching the older acolytes sweep ash from the flagstones. Sister Maren had put a hand on her head and said, *You ask too many questions, little wren.* And Bram had thought, with the fierce private certainty of a child: *Good. Then I'll never run out of them.*

It showed her the valley. Every season of it. Frost on the fence rails in the month of Hollowing. The river going loud and brown with meltwater. The way the light came through the shrine's broken roof in the late afternoon and lay on the floor like something spilled. It showed her the taste of river water in summer, cold enough to hurt her teeth. The smell of the tannery she'd always hated. The sound of Odile laughing — really laughing, not the careful archivist's chuckle, but the snorting, undignified thing that had escaped her exactly twice on the salt road, both times at Rennick's expense.

It showed her Isa's face the first time Isa had laughed at something Bram said — surprised by it, almost angry about it, as if joy were a debt she hadn't agreed to owe.

It showed her Rennick's hands shuffling cards, quick and crooked.

It showed her hunger, and cold, and the burn scar the magistrate's men had left on her arm, and the arrow that had killed Tam at the ford, and Isa going numb and grey after killing her first man on the traitor's road. It showed her all of it — every wound, every grief, every hour of fear — and it held each one up like a jeweler holding a stone to the light, and she understood what it was telling her.

*This is what you would lose. All of it. Every piece. Even the pieces that hurt. Especially those.*

"You're offering me the whole thing," Bram said quietly. "Not a share. The whole mantle. Kestrel entire."

The flame breathed.

*Yes.*

"And if I take it, the war stays sealed. Forever. No one has to fight. No one has to die." She heard her own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when she was lying to herself about something. "That's the trade. I stop being Bram Ashgrove, and everyone else gets to keep being themselves."

*Yes.*

"Forever," she said. "How long is forever, exactly? Because from in here, I'd wager it looks like a very long time to be alone in a candle."

* * *

The Ember showed her.

It did not mean to be cruel. Bram understood that, the way you understand a dog isn't cruel when it brings you a dead bird — it simply didn't know any other way to show love. It showed her the shape of forever, and the shape was this:

A flame in a hollow of glass, burning without flicker, without hunger, without change. Around it, the world. Seasons wheeling past like pages turning faster and faster. The valley she'd been born in rising and falling — forests, then farms, then something she had no name for, towers of glass and light, then forests again. People coming to the caldera in ones and twos, pilgrims and scholars and the merely curious, kneeling before the flame, telling it their sorrows, and then going away. Always going away. That was the part the flame showed her most carefully, most honestly, the way you'd show someone the worst room in a house you were selling: they always go away.

Because that was what gods were for. You told them your sorrows and you went away, and you lived your brief bright mortal life, and you died, and the god remained. That was the whole arrangement. That had been the arrangement since the beginning of the war, when gods and dragons had burned the sky and mortals had learned to build their houses low and their prayers short.

It showed her the last of her companions' lives. Isa, grey-haired, sitting by a fire, telling a grandchild about the woman who had taught her blade-work — *she gave herself up for us, you know, she became the god so we could stay people* — and the grandchild nodding the way children nod at stories, and Isa looking into the fire with an expression Bram knew too well, the expression of someone keeping a vigil for a person who isn't dead but might as well be.

It showed her Odile, old, in a library, writing the definitive history of the Ember, and getting the ending wrong, because the only person who knew the ending had stopped being a person.

It showed her Rennick — and here the vision wavered, because Rennick's memories were half-spent, bargained away to a river spirit, and the flame could only show her what remained: Rennick at a card table, winning, and reaching to tell someone about it, and the seat beside him empty, and not remembering why it should be full.

*You would be worshipped,* the flame said. Not in words. In the way a hand is offered. *You would be safe. You would be eternal. The war stays sealed. No god commands men, because the last god will be a candle in a mountain, and the mountain is very deep, and forever is very long.*

"And you?" Bram asked, and her voice cracked on it. "What happens to you? If I take the mantle — do you rest? Do you end? Or do I just... wear you? Like a coat made of a god?"

The flame did something she had never felt it do before.

It hesitated.

And in that hesitation Bram saw the truth, the whole architecture of it, laid bare as a stripped beam: Kestrel had not gone silent. Kestrel had poured itself out — into a bloodline, into her bloodline, generation after generation, thinning itself like watered wine, hiding the Ember's location in the one place no god would think to look: inside a mortal who wanted nothing to do with gods. And now the pouring-out was nearly done. The bloodline had come down to one woman with a burn scar and a mercenary's contract book, and the god that remained was this: a candle. A candle and a question.

*Take all of me,* the flame said, *and there is no more me. There is only you, wearing the shape of what I was. That is the offer. That has always been the offer.*

"That's not an offer," Bram said. "That's a eulogy with extra steps."

* * *

"Bram." Isa's voice, low. "You've been kneeling there for a long time. Your lips have been moving."

"Has it offered you anything?" Odile asked. She had come closer, close enough that Bram could see the ash settling on her shoulders, grey on grey. "The visions at the seals — I've been cataloguing them. There's a pattern. The god didn't hide itself randomly. It chose bloodlines that wanted nothing from it. Farmers. Ferriers. One family of toll-keepers, if the third seal's imagery is literal." Her eyes were bright and fierce behind the fogged spectacles. "It chose people who would say no, Bram. It's been choosing people who would say no for two thousand years."

"Then it chose well," Bram said, and got to her feet.

Her knees cracked. She was thirty-four years old and her knees cracked, and she found she was absurdly grateful for that. Gods didn't have cracking knees. Gods didn't have the itch under the bandage of the burn scar, or the ache in the shoulder Tam's death had left her with, or the specific hunger of the hour before dinner when you'd been walking since dawn. Gods didn't have any of it, and she had spent her whole life in a valley that had forgotten magic, guarding a silent god, and she had thought what she wanted was an answer.

She had been wrong. What she wanted was this: the ache, the itch, the hunger, the people.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Bram said to the flame. "It's short. You'll like it."

The flame waited. Gods were patient. That was the trouble with them.

"When I was small, in the temple, Sister Maren told me about Kestrel. She said the god had gone silent out of grief, that it had watched the war and lost the will to speak. I believed that for twenty years. I prayed to a grieving god in a valley where nothing magical could follow me, and I thought I was keeping it company." Bram crouched, close to the flame, close enough that its light lay warm on her face. "You weren't grieving. You were hiding. And you hid so well you hid inside me, and I never knew, and you never told me, and do you know what I think?"

The flame breathed.

"I think you were lonely." She said it gently. There was no cruelty in it she could find. "I think two thousand years is a very long time to be a secret, even a holy one. I think you poured yourself out hoping that eventually you'd be small enough, quiet enough, mortal enough, that somebody would find you and *stay*." She swallowed. "And then a man died in my arms with a map in his hands, and I came all this way, and here you are. Small as a candle. And your great offer — the one you've been building toward for two millennia — is to make me into you. To make me the thing in the hollow that everyone tells their sorrows to and then goes away from."

*The war stays sealed,* the flame said. *No one dies. No one burns. Is that not worth one life? Is that not worth every life?*

"It would be," Bram said, "if it were true."

She felt Odile go very still behind her.

"Forever is long," Bram said. "Longer than seals. Longer than covenants. You've seen it — you showed it to me yourself. The war didn't end because someone won it. It ended because everyone got tired. And tired things wake up. Seals crack. Mountains erode. And when it happens — a hundred years, a thousand, ten thousand — there will be one god alone in a mountain who has forgotten what it's like to be afraid, or hungry, or *wrong*, because I will have forgotten. That's what the mantle costs. Not my name. My *fallibility*. And a god who can't be wrong is exactly the kind of god that started this war in the first place."

The flame flickered. Actually flickered, the first time since she'd entered the caldera, and Bram felt the whole chamber lean in.

"So here's my counter-offer," she said. "You've spent two thousand years learning how to be small. I've spent thirty-four years learning how to be a person. Between us, we might manage something neither of us could do alone." She held out her hand over the hollow, palm down, not touching, offering. "You don't get to be a god again. And I don't get to be a martyr. We split the difference. We share you out — thin, mortal, fallible, among people who will *argue with you*. People who will tell you when you're wrong. People who will go away and come *back*, which is the part you never understood. Going away isn't the wound. Never coming back is."

*It will not be enough,* the flame said, and for the first time it sounded not like a god but like something old and frightened. *A flame divided is a flame diminished. The seal will weaken. Things will wake.*

"Probably," Bram agreed. "And we'll deal with it. Badly, some of the time. We'll make mistakes and bury our mistakes and argue about whose fault they were for years. That's what mortals do instead of being eternal." She smiled, and it hurt, and she let it hurt. "You showed me Isa's face when she laughed at me. You showed me Odile's terrible laugh. You showed me Rennick's crooked hands. You showed me all of it like it was the price. Like it was what I'd be giving up."

She stood.

"It's not the price. It's the *point*. You had it backwards the whole time. You've been guarding the world from the war so long you forgot the world was the thing worth guarding."

* * *

The silence that followed was the longest silence of Bram's life, and she had grown up in a valley that had forgotten how to make noise.

The flame burned. The ash fell. Somewhere above them, faint through the rock, she thought she heard the boom of a charge — muffled, distant, a promise of trouble on its way — and knew the choice was about to stop being a choice and become a scramble.

And then the Ember did something no god had ever done, so far as she knew, in all the long history of gods and dragons and the mortals caught between them.

It laughed.

It was not a sound. It was a warmth that went through her like strong drink, and it carried an unmistakable flavor of *well said, little wren* — Sister Maren's words, from a morning forty years gone, and Bram realized with a jolt that the god had been there that day too, thin as watered wine in a child's blood, listening to a six-year-old ask too many questions, and had thought, even then: *this one.*

"You could have just said so," Bram muttered.

The flame rose from its hollow — rose, and did not go out, and did not grow — and hung in the air between her hands like a candle flame that had decided, after two thousand years, to take a walk.

And behind her, Isa Varrow drew a breath and stepped forward, and Odile Vane said, "Bram — the eastern vent — the Order is—" and the mountain began, very softly, to groan.

The flame trembled in the air between Bram's palms.

And down the tunnel, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots. Many boots. And a voice she knew, calling her name like a challenge and a prayer at once:

"Ashgrove. I'm coming down."
