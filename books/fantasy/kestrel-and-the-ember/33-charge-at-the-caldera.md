## 33. Charge at the Caldera

*Serah*

The ash fell like grey snow, and Serah Dain let it.

She stood at the caldera's eastern rim with forty-one knights behind her — forty-one, of the three hundred who had marched out of Greywater — and she did not brush the ash from her shoulders or her hair or the sigil of the Hollow Order stitched over her heart. Let it fall. Ash was honest. Ash was what fire left when it was finished lying.

Below her, through a wound in the world's skin, the Ember burned.

She could not see it from here. She did not need to. She had spent thirty years learning to feel dragon-fire the way other people felt weather in their joints — a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the teeth, a whisper that said *bow, kneel, worship, obey*. The whisper was louder here than it had ever been. It pressed against her skull like a thumb on a bruise.

"Captain." Lieutenant Harrow, young, ash in his eyelashes, his voice carefully level. "The powder teams are set along the western vent. On your word."

Serah looked down into the caldera one more time. Far below, small as candle flames, figures moved along the inner path. Five of them. The mercenary woman. The scholar. The smuggler — no, the smuggler was dead or dying; she'd read the report from the tunnel mouth. The girl. And one more shape that moved like someone carrying something precious.

They were going to wake it. Or feed it. Or kneel to it. It hardly mattered which. All of it ended the same way: men and women on their knees before a thing that breathed, because the thing breathed *fire*, and fire made kings of liars.

Her sister had died kneeling. That was the whole of it, really. Everything else was scaffolding.

"On my word," Serah said. "Not before. We go down first. If we can put steel between those five and the flame long enough for the charges to burn their fuses, we do it without burying them alive." She paused. "I have no quarrel with how they die. Only with how many."

Harrow blinked. "You want to *warn* them?"

"I want to kill the fire, Harrow. The rest is tidying." She drew her sword — plain steel, Order-forged, blessed by no one — and turned to face her forty-one. Ash settled on them all equally. That was the one mercy the mountain offered.

"The Hollow Order was founded on a single sentence," she said. "*No god shall command men again.* Not because we hate wonder. Because we have read what wonder costs." She let her gaze move down the line, face by face. Some of them were afraid. Good. Fear meant they still understood what they were walking toward. "Some of you have doubted me since Greywater. I know. Doubt is not treason. Follow me down anyway, and if you see something in that pit that makes you want to kneel — look at the man beside you instead. Kneel to *him* if you must kneel to anything. He bled for you. The fire didn't."

A ragged breath went through the line, half laugh, half prayer.

"Charge at the caldera," Serah said, and led them down into the throat of the world.

* * *

*Bram*

Bram heard them coming before she saw them — boots on scree, forty-odd sets, moving in the disciplined cadence of soldiers who had decided being afraid was a luxury.

"They're early," Odile said, breathless, hauling the last of the seal-satchels up the inner slope. Above them the Ember's chamber waited, a black mouth in the caldera wall, and Bram could feel it pulling at her like a tide. "The charges can't be laid yet, they'd never — they're not going to blow the vent, they're going to come down *here*—"

"They're going to buy time for the teams above," Bram said. Her hand found her sword hilt out of habit, and she hated the habit a little. "Which means every minute we keep them busy down here is a minute the fuses aren't burning."

"So we fight," Isa said. There was ash on her face and something harder underneath it, the thing the bridge had put there months ago. She'd stopped flinching at the word *kill* somewhere around the traitor's road. Bram grieved that and said nothing, because there had been no time for grief that wasn't load-bearing.

"We delay," Bram corrected. "We don't slaughter forty men who are mostly scared kids following a woman who believes every word she says." She rolled her shoulder where the old burn scar pulled. "Serah Dain isn't a butcher. I've fought her twice. She fights like someone reading a very long letter and hoping it ends well."

"That's the stupidest description of combat I've ever heard," Isa said.

"It's accurate," Rennick would have said, if Rennick were here. The thought arrived and then sat down heavily in Bram's chest and refused to leave. He was holding the tunnel. He was holding the tunnel alone, with a body he'd already sold most of the way to a river spirit, and every minute she stood here philosophizing was a minute off his bargain.

"Positions," she said. "Isa, left flank, rocks. Odile — can you still raise the light-charms?"

"In this much ash? Badly. They'll be the color of old porridge."

"Porridge is fine. Porridge is confusing." Bram climbed onto the ridge of fallen stone at the center of the path, alone, visible, sword still sheathed. Isa stared at her.

"What are you *doing*?"

"Having an argument," Bram said. "With a sword as a backup punctuation mark."

The first knights came around the bend of the inner slope in a wedge, and at its point, ash-grey and iron-straight, walked Serah Dain.

She saw Bram standing unarmed in her path and — to her credit — did not order a volley. She held up a fist, halted the wedge, and came forward alone until ten paces separated them. Up close, in the ember-light seeping from the chamber mouth, her face looked carved out of the same material as the mountain.

"Ashgrove."

"Dain."

"Step aside."

"No."

"You know what's down there."

"I know what's down there better than you do," Bram said. "That's rather the point."

Serah's jaw tightened. "It's a god's heart. A fragment of the thing that taught men to burn each other in its name for six hundred years. Whatever it told you in your visions — and I know it gave you visions, they always do — it lied. That's what gods *are*, Ashgrove. A lie old enough to have architecture."

"That's the second-stupidest description of divinity I've ever heard," Bram said, "and I once traveled with a man who thought the moon was a god's lost hat."

Something flickered at the corner of Serah's mouth. Died immediately. "Last chance. Stand aside, take your people, walk out of the mountains. I'll even let you keep the map. It'll be worth nothing by sundown."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I go through you."

Bram finally drew her sword. Slowly, so the whole line of knights could watch her do it — watch that she did it sadly, like opening a door to a room she'd hoped not to enter.

"Then here's my counteroffer," she said. "Fight me. Just me. Your forty against my one, but one at a time, and while we fight, you can tell me why. Not the creed. The *why*. And if anything I say ever lands — if even one sentence gets past your armor — you call your men off and we find out together what the Ember actually wants. Deal?"

"That's not how war works."

"It's how *this* one works," Bram said. "I've been having it with you since Vell."

Serah came at her.

* * *

*Serah*

The mercenary fought the way she talked, which was to say infuriatingly.

She didn't press. She didn't try to end it. She parried, gave ground along the ridge, and kept *talking*, ducking under Serah's blade to say things like "Your lieutenant's left side is open, by the way, you should really drill that" and "Did you know the fourth seal calls the Ember a covenant? Not a weapon. A covenant. Look it up — oh wait, your Order burns libraries."

Serah adjusted her grip and drove her back a step. "The fourth seal is a god's memoir. Of course it flatters itself."

"Covenants aren't flattering! Covenants are *chores*!" Bram caught her wrist, twisted, broke the engagement, hopped backward onto a flat stone with the nimbleness of a woman far too pleased with herself. "You've built your entire life on the idea that the fire wants worshippers. What if it wants *custodians*? What if it's been sitting down there for a thousand years doing the spiritual equivalent of holding a door shut, and it's tired, and it would give its — itself, whatever — to anyone willing to share the weight?"

"Then it should have asked nicely a thousand years ago, before my sister—" Serah stopped. Bit the word off clean.

Bram went still. Even mid-fight, the woman had a gift for stillness, for letting a silence open like a trapdoor. "Before your sister what?"

"En garde," Serah said, and attacked properly this time.

Steel rang along the ridge. Behind them both, the two forces had dissolved into scattered skirmishes — Harrow engaging the fae-blooded girl among the rocks, the scholar hurling porridge-colored light that did, admittedly, prove confusing — but Serah kept her focus narrowed to the woman in front of her, because the alternative was thinking about Maren, and she could not think about Maren and swing a sword at the same time.

Bram parried everything. That was the maddening part. Twice Serah had a killing line — twice — and both times the mercenary turned it into a graze, a disarm attempt, a shove that sent Serah stumbling on the scree and then, insultingly, a steadying hand.

"Stop *helping* me," Serah snarled.

"Then stop falling!"

They broke apart, circling. Ash drifted between them. Below, the chamber mouth pulsed its slow red light, and Serah felt the whisper press against her teeth again — *kneel, bow, worship* — and hated it, hated it, hated how familiar it was, how it sounded almost exactly like the cultists had sounded the night Maren died, joyful and certain and *sure*.

"There," Bram said quietly. She wasn't even looking at Serah's sword anymore. She was looking at her face. "There it is. That's not the fire whispering, Dain. That's memory. You're not hearing the Ember. You're hearing the night your sister died."

"You know *nothing* about—"

"I know what the visions showed me," Bram said. "All of them. Every seal. And one of them showed me a girl in a green dress kneeling in a barn, surrounded by people wearing a god's name like a costume, and I have spent four hundred miles wondering why the Hollow Order's captain flinches every time someone says the word *worship*." She lowered her sword. Actually lowered it, point-down, the way you'd plant a banner. "So here's my argument, knight-captain, and then I'm done arguing. You think the fire made those people kill your sister. I think people killed your sister and blamed the fire, because blaming the fire means you never have to blame yourselves. And right now, today, you're about to do the same thing. Wear righteousness like a costume. Kill in the name of a dead girl who never asked for it."

The words went in. Serah felt them go in, felt them slide between the plates the way a good blade does, and for one terrible moment she couldn't remember why her feet were planted on this slope or whose side the mountain was on.

Then Harrow screamed.

* * *

*Bram*

It happened fast, the way disasters borrow speed from somewhere.

One of the powder teams — impatient, or frightened, or simply following the letter of orders written for a different moment — lit the western fuses early. The crackle of burning cord ran along the vent like a lit fuse runs up a cartoon bomb, and every soldier on the slope heard it, and every soldier understood at the same instant that the ground beneath them was now on a schedule.

"Harrow, sound the retreat!" Serah's voice cracked across the caldera like a whip. For a heartbeat her whole attention was on her men — pulling them back from the vent, counting heads, forty-one becoming a number she refused to let shrink — and in that heartbeat, Bram saw the choice spread out in front of her like a map.

Serah Dain, back turned, shouting orders, sword low.

Every instinct Bram owned — the mercenary instincts, the ones that had kept her alive through twenty years of contracts — said *now*. One stroke. The war ends here. The Order breaks without its captain. Isa wouldn't have hesitated. Six months ago, Bram wouldn't have either.

Instead she ran forward and *shoved* Serah out of the way of a falling rock.

It wasn't even a large rock. That was the humiliating part. A chunk of caldera wall the size of a loaf of bread, shaken loose by the first deep thud of the charge, and it would have caught Serah square on the helm, and Bram knocked the knight-captain sprawling into the ash and took a glancing blow across the shoulder that would cost her a truly spectacular bruise.

Silence, except for the fuse hissing somewhere above and forty-one knights staring at their captain lying in the ash beneath the woman she'd sworn to kill.

"Why," Serah said. Flat. Dangerous. Not moving.

"Because you're wrong," Bram said, "but you're not *bad*, and I am so tired of confusing the two." She offered her hand.

Serah looked at it the way she might look at a snake offering to hold her umbrella.

Above them, the fuse hissed closer to the vent. Below them, the chamber mouth flared — the Ember feeling the tremor, Bram thought, feeling its own grave being dug — and the light that spilled out turned the falling ash briefly, horribly beautiful, like snow over a forge.

And then, faintly, up through the rock, from the direction of the sealed tunnel, came the sound of Rennick Cael laughing.

He was laughing because the charges had woken something on *his* side too — Bram knew it in her bones a half-second before Odile confirmed it, white-faced, pointing at the chamber mouth, where the Ember's guttering light had begun to pulse faster, faster, like a heart in panic.

"Bram," Odile called. "The collapse — it's not just collapsing the path. If the eastern vent goes, the whole chamber goes. We have minutes. Maybe less."

Serah got to her feet on her own, refusing the hand, ash sliding off her pauldrons. She looked at the flaring chamber. At her retreating men. At the fuse. At Bram.

"You're telling me," she said slowly, "that your plan and my plan are now both going to fail, together, in the next few minutes."

"Seems to be the shape of it, yes."

"Wonderful." Serah Dain closed her eyes for exactly one second — a woman filing her entire crisis away for later — and then opened them, and Bram watched thirty years of conviction do something no creed had prepared it for.

It started looking for a third option.

"The eastern vent team," Serah said. "My second powder team. Where are they?"

Bram pointed east. Serah was already running.
