## 34. The Sister's Name

The ash fell like gray snow, and Serah Dain had never been so tired of being right.

She came down the caldera slope with six knights at her back and a sword that had not failed her in nineteen years of service, and she told herself — as she had told herself on every road since Greywater — that what waited below was a fire to be put out. Not a person. Not a god. A fire. Fires did not have faces. Fires did not look at you across a field of smoking stone with an expression like apology.

Bram Ashgrove stood between her and the Ember's chamber, sword drawn, and she was not even guarding the door properly. She was standing slightly to the side of it, angled away, as if she'd rather be anywhere else.

"You're in my way," Serah said.

"I know," Bram said. "I'm sorry about that."

It was infuriating. Everything about the woman was infuriating — the way she fought like she was having a conversation, the way she had spared her at Greywater when a clean kill would have ended all of this, the way she kept saying *sorry* as though the Hollow Order were a spilled drink.

"Last chance," Serah said. "Walk away. The warrant dies with you either way. There's no shame in a mercenary choosing to live."

"There's some shame," Bram said. "I've got a reputation."

Serah attacked.

The duel was ugly ground — scree and slag, footing that shifted under every step, ash thick enough to taste. Serah had trained against a hundred styles and none of them had prepared her for Ashgrove, who fought like a valley woman who had learned her blade-work from farmers' brawls and temple drills and had never once cared about looking elegant. She took a cut along her forearm. She gave one across Bram's shoulder. They broke apart, circled, came together again, and somewhere in the third exchange Serah realized they had fallen into a rhythm, almost a courtesy, two people arguing in the only language left to them.

"You think I want this?" Bram gasped, parrying a thrust meant for her ribs. "You think I climbed a mountain to die on it?"

"You climbed it for the fire."

"I climbed it because a dying man put a map in my hands and wouldn't let go." She twisted inside Serah's guard, too close for swords, and shoved her back with a forearm. "I didn't ask for any of it. Neither did you, I'd guess."

"Do not," Serah said, and heard her own voice go flat and cold, "presume to know what I asked for."

They came together again. Steel rang. Somewhere above them, one of her knights shouted something about the eastern vent, and someone else shouted back, and Serah ignored both because the whole world had narrowed to this: the woman in front of her, and the door behind her, and the creed she had carried since she was sixteen years old with her sister's blood drying on her hands.

*All dragon-fire must die, so that no god may ever again command men.*

She believed it the way other people believed in rain. It simply fell. It had fallen through every doubt, every night she woke reaching for a name she could barely say anymore, every time some village elder wept at her feet and swore their gods were gentle. Gentle gods were how cults started. Gentle gods were how Maren died.

Maren.

The name slipped loose in the middle of a parry, the way it always did eventually, and her blade went wide, and Bram — curse her, *curse her* — did not take the opening. She stepped back instead, lowering her point into the ash.

"Enough," Bram said.

"We are not done."

"Yes, we are. This part of it, anyway." Bram planted her sword point-down and leaned on the pommel like a farmer leaning on a fence, which should have been comical and was instead the most disarming thing Serah had ever seen. "You're going to kill me or I'm going to stop you, and either way it'll take another hour and we don't have an hour. So let me say my piece first. Then, if you still want to fight, we fight."

"Words won't change anything."

"They changed things at Greywater. You hesitated. I saw it."

"I did not hesitate."

"You sheathed a killing stroke to check my eyes," Bram said. "Knights don't do that unless something's asking questions underneath."

Serah's jaw tightened. The ash drifted down between them, settling on their shoulders like a shroud they were sharing.

"Say your piece, then," she said. "Quickly."

Bram looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her speak more gently than Serah wanted to be spoken to.

"The visions showed me the war," she said. "All of it. The beginning, the middle, the end. Ninety years of gods and dragons burning the world down to its roots. And do you know how it actually finished? Not with victory. Nobody won. Both sides just... ran out. Ran out of fire, ran out of faithful, ran out of reasons. The Ember is the last coal of it, and Kestrel hid it, and went silent, and the world got to grow up without anyone commanding it." She paused. "That's the part you're fighting to protect. That silence. You just don't know it yet."

"I know what god-touched men did," Serah said. "I've buried them."

"I know you do." Bram's voice didn't waver. "And I know why."

"You know nothing—"

"Maren," Bram said.

The world stopped.

Not the mountain — the mountain went on groaning and breathing smoke, the knights went on shouting, the ash went on falling. But Serah Dain stopped, entirely, the way a clock stops, every gear locked at once.

"That name is not yours to say."

"No," Bram agreed quietly. "It isn't. But I'm going to say it anyway, because you deserve to hear it from someone who isn't using it as a leash." She straightened off her sword. "Your sister wasn't killed by a god. She was killed by men wearing one's name. I've seen it. In the visions — the Order's own founding, before it was the Order, when it was just a mob with a creed and a list. Your sister sheltered a family that had taken in a keeper's descendant. Ordinary people. Farmers. The mob came at dusk with torches and hammers, and they sang a hymn while they did it, and afterward they wrote in their ledger that the fire had been cleansed." Her voice caught, just slightly. "There was no god there, Serah. Gods had been silent for three hundred years by then. It was men. Men with a righteous name and a list, exactly like the one your captains carry now."

"You're lying." It came out very small. "You would say anything. You're standing in front of the thing you're trying to save."

"I am," Bram said. "And I could be lying. You can't know for certain, and that's the worst part, isn't it? You have to decide whether to believe me with nothing but my face to go on." She spread her empty hand. "But think. Think about what I'm actually claiming. Not that your crusade is cruel — you already know that, you execute collaborators and hate yourself after. I'm claiming something worse. I'm claiming that the wound you built your whole life around was made by your own kind, wearing your own righteousness. Why would I invent that? What does it buy me?"

"It buys you my sword arm stopping."

"It already stopped," Bram said. "You stopped it. Back at Greywater, before I ever said her name. Some part of you has been asking questions for years, and you've been drowning them in doctrine because the questions sound like her voice."

"*Stop.*"

Serah hadn't meant to shout. The word tore out of her raw, and her knights behind her went quiet, and the mountain seemed to lean in.

She stood there in the falling ash with her sword half-raised and her certainty — the thing she had polished for nineteen years until it shone like armor — sitting cracked down the middle.

Because here was the thing she had never said aloud, not to her chaplains, not to her confessors, not to herself in the dark hours: she had never actually seen the cultists' god. None of them ever had. The men who killed Maren had claimed a god's authority, and the Order had answered by swearing off all gods forever, and in nineteen years of hunting she had found embers and relics and desperate keepers — mortal, frightened, mortal — and never once a god giving orders. She had told herself that proved the danger was real and hidden. She had never once let herself consider that it might prove the danger had always been men.

"They sang," she heard herself say. It was not a question. It was a memory surfacing, one she had pushed down so deep she'd forgotten owning it. "When they burned the Harrow farm. My sister described it, in the letter she sent the week before. She said they sang while they worked, and she couldn't get the tune out of her head." Serah's voice went strange and distant. "We still sing that hymn. It's in the Order's morning office. *Cleanse the ember, cool the flame.*"

Bram said nothing. She didn't need to. The ash fell, and the hymn turned over in Serah's memory with its new words attached, and nineteen years rearranged themselves around a single terrible possibility: that she had spent her life avenging her sister by becoming the singers.

"My sister's name was Maren," Serah said, and it cost her everything, and she said it anyway. "She was twenty-two. She laughed at bad jokes. She was afraid of geese. She wrote letters full of gossip about people I'd never met because she knew I liked hearing about ordinary things." Her grip on the sword had gone white-knuckled, though the point had drifted down somewhere along the way; she couldn't remember when. "And men killed her. Men. With torches and a hymn and a ledger."

"Yes."

"And I—" The sentence wouldn't finish. There was no end to it. *And I joined them. And I became them. And right now, on this mountain, I am leading a column of soldiers to burn out a flame guarded by four mortals and a mountain spirit, and if any of them die, I will be the mob, I will be the hymn, I will be the ledger entry.*

"You can still put it down," Bram said softly. "Whatever you're holding. It doesn't have to be forever. Just for today. Just long enough to see what's actually down there before you break it."

"What's down there is a weapon."

"What's down there is a promise somebody kept too long," Bram said. "Come and look. That's all I'm asking. Look with your own eyes, then decide. If you still want to destroy it after — well." A ghost of a smile, tired and crooked. "You've seen how hard I am to kill. I'll make you earn it."

Behind Serah, her knights had gone uncertain. She could feel it — young Vess shifting her weight, old Coram lowering his shield a fraction, the whole line suddenly unsure of what they were following and why. Nineteen years of conviction, and it had taken one name to shake it, because conviction built on grief was never masonry. It was ice over water, and the water had been waiting the whole time.

"Captain?" Vess said carefully. "Orders?"

Serah opened her mouth to give them — *hold the line, form up, we press on* — and found that the words had a hymn's tune now, and she could not make herself sing them.

Before she could find others, the mountain answered for her.

It began as a tremor in the soles of her boots, deeper than any charge her sappers had set — a long, rolling groan from the eastern flank, where the second charge team had gone with their powder kegs an hour ago. Then a sound like the sky tearing, and a gout of orange light bloomed against the ash-dark, and the whole caldera shuddered as the eastern vent blew open.

The ground tilted. Bram staggered, caught herself, and her head snapped toward the chamber door with an expression Serah recognized instantly, because she had worn it once herself, at a farm at dusk: the look of someone realizing the thing they loved was already burning.

"No," Bram breathed. "No, no — they're early, they weren't supposed to—"

Dust and smoke boiled up from below. Through the ringing in her ears, Serah heard the Ember's chamber groan under tons of shifting stone, and heard, faintly beneath it, something else — a sound like a great heart stumbling, missing a beat, finding another.

Guttering.

The fire was guttering.

And Serah Dain stood on the broken slope with her sword finally, fully lowered, watching Bram Ashgrove run toward the collapse, and understood with terrible clarity that whatever she decided in the next hundred heartbeats would decide everything — and that she no longer knew, not even a little, whose side her sister would have wanted her on.
