## 35. Emberfall

The Ember was the size of a candle flame.

That was the thing no one had warned her about — not the map, not the seals, not Kestrel's voice threading through her dreams since the drowned chapel. Bram had climbed a mountain expecting a dragon's heart and found something small enough to cup in two hands, burning at the bottom of a chamber that smelled of old stone and older patience.

She knelt at its edge now, and it did not burn her. It warmed her the way a hearth warms a room you grew up in — familiar, unhurried, as if it had been waiting for her specifically and had all the time in the world to do it.

*You took your time,* it seemed to say, though it said nothing at all. The warmth simply arranged itself into meaning against her skin.

"I had errands," Bram murmured. Her voice came out rough. Behind her, Isa and Odile stood at the rim of the hollow where the flame sat, and neither of them spoke either. Some rooms you enter quietly or not at all.

The chamber itself was vast — a bubble of emptiness inside Mount Sarn's roots, walls veined with channels of cooled glass where fire had once run like water. Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of rock, they could hear the Order's column moving. Boots. Orders shouted in clipped voices. The ring of iron being stacked with terrible purpose.

"They're setting charges," Odile said for the third time. She said things three times when she was frightened; Bram had learned this somewhere on the salt road, between Rennick cheating at cards and Isa pretending not to laugh. "Bram. They are *setting charges*. We have perhaps an hour before—"

"Then we have an hour," Bram said. She did not take her eyes off the flame. "It waited ten thousand years. It can wait one more."

But even as she said it, she felt the wrongness. The warmth was steady, yes, but thin. A candle is steady too, right up until the wax runs out.

Isa crouched beside her. Up close, in the light of the thing her father had died carrying a map to, her face looked younger and older at once. "It's smaller than he thought," she said quietly. "Isn't it."

"Yes."

"He drew it like a sun." Her voice caught. "In his letters. *A sun under the mountain, little star.* He thought we'd find a god."

"It *is* a god," Odile said. "Or what's left of one. Or what was never one to begin with — the fourth seal was very clear, if anyone had let me finish my notes—"

"Odile." Bram said it gently. "Later."

Above them, the sounds changed. The boots stopped their aimless circling and began to move with intent, converging toward the eastern side of the mountain. Toward the vent. Bram felt it before she understood it — a cold thread down her spine, the same instinct that had kept her alive through twenty years of escort contracts: *they know where to hit.*

"They're not coming down," she said. "They're going to blow the vent and bury us."

*

Serah Dain believed she was saving the world.

Bram had fought her twice now, and both times she had come away with the same unsettled feeling — not that Serah was wrong about everything, but that she was wrong the way a well-built bridge is wrong when it spans the wrong river. Solid. Sincere. Carrying people somewhere terrible.

She could hear her voice through the stone, faintly, giving orders. *Seal the shafts. Nothing leaves this mountain carrying fire.* And beneath the words, the conviction, banked and patient as coals.

Bram had spared her at Greywater. Had told her, *you're wrong about what gods want*, and watched the words land like arrows into armor — not piercing, but *heard*. She had gambled that mercy would work on Serah Dain the way it worked on most people: slowly, uncomfortably, against their will.

Looking up at the ceiling of the world, listening to powder charges being placed with military precision, Bram understood she had lost that gamble. Or hadn't yet won it. There wasn't time to know which.

"Bram." Isa's hand closed over hers. "Whatever you're going to do — do it soon."

*

Here is what the fourth seal had shown her, alone at the caldera rim while the others slept:

The Ember was not a weapon. It was a covenant. Dragon-fire and god-light braided together in the last hour of a war that had ended not in victory but in exhaustion — two ancient powers bleeding out across the sky and choosing, in their final lucid moment, to make a promise instead of a grave. *We will keep the seal between worlds. But fire hoarded dies. Fire destroyed, dies. Only fire shared endures.*

Only fire shared endures.

And Bram Ashgrove — mercenary, orphan, vessel of a silent god who had never actually gone silent but had poured himself, generation by generation, into her bloodline like wine decanted from cask to cask until only the last cup remained — Bram had climbed down into the dark intending to give herself wholly. To become Kestrel entire. To burn her own name out of the world so the war stayed sealed and her friends went home safe.

She had seen the shape of that life in the flame's first touch. Immortality. Solitude. Centuries of watching Isa grow old, watching Odile's books crumble, watching Rennick's river-bargained season run out, and holding all of it alone behind eyes that no longer blinked like a woman's.

She had almost said yes. That was the shameful part. She had *almost* said yes, because it would have been easy, and because some stubborn mercenary corner of her soul still believed that the price of everything was paid alone or not at all.

Then Isa had stepped forward and taken her other hand, and the flame had flared toward both of them like a dog recognizing two voices at once, and Bram had understood that the covenant had never asked for a martyr. It had asked for a family.

They had been working out how — how to split a god five ways without killing anyone, Odile scribbling equations in the ash-dust, Isa arguing theology with a candle — when the mountain shook.

*

The first detonation was distant. A deep *whump* more felt than heard, rolling through the rock like a fist knocking on a door at the far end of a house.

Dust sifted from the ceiling. Fine, golden, glittering in the Ember's light.

"That," Odile said, "is not good."

The second detonation was closer. The eastern vent — Bram knew it now, could feel it the way she felt her own pulse, a thin place in the mountain's skin where fire had once breathed. The Order had found it. Of course they had. Serah Dain did not make the same mistake twice.

The floor jumped. Isa stumbled into her; Bram caught her arm and held on.

"The tunnel," Odile said, already moving, gathering her notes with the frantic precision of a woman who had lost one cellar to fire and refused to lose anything else. "If the vent goes, the whole eastern gallery comes down, and the gallery takes the inner path with it, and we are standing in a bottle with a lit fuse—"

"Rennick," Bram said.

Nobody answered. Nobody had to. Rennick Cael was somewhere above them, in the dark of the inner tunnel, holding the only road between them and the sky, because a hired porter had sold them all and the charges had already started falling. He'd grinned at them as they ran past him deeper — that crooked half-fae grin, one eyebrow up, like the whole thing was a card game he intended to win with a marked deck.

*"Go on,"* he'd said. *"I've held worse doors than this. Ask anyone. Don't ask anyone, actually, I cheated all of them."*

The third detonation was directly overhead.

*

The world turned white, then black, then sideways.

Bram had been in collapses before — mine shafts, a burned temple, once an entire watchtower that decided it preferred being rubble. She knew the grammar of it: the crack like the world clearing its throat, the long groan of stone reconsidering its opinions, then the fall.

She threw herself and Isa flat. Dust came down like snow, choking, hot. Somewhere Odile was shouting — words, Bram thought, actual scholarly words, probably citations — and then a section of ceiling the size of a barn came apart and the sound swallowed everything.

When it ended, the chamber still stood. Barely. A new fissure ran across the ceiling like a lightning scar, weeping dust. And through the fissure, from somewhere above, came light. Torchlight. Orange, flickering, human.

And smoke. Thin threads of it, curling down through the broken stone, tasting of powder and iron.

"No," Isa whispered.

The Ember guttered.

Bram saw it happen and felt it happen, both at once — felt it the way you feel your own heartbeat stutter. The little flame bent sideways, as if in wind, though there was no wind. Its gold dimmed toward orange, toward red, toward the sullen color of a coal remembering it used to be fire. The warmth against Bram's skin thinned. Thinned again.

*It's dying,* she thought, with a clarity like ice water. *Not in ten thousand years. Not in a hundred. Now. Tonight. Because the mountain that houses it is being pulled down around its ears, and a covenant needs a house.*

"*No,*" she said aloud, and put both hands into the hollow.

The heat that met her was feeble. A bird's heartbeat. A candle in a doorway with winter coming down the road.

"Bram—" Odile's hand on her shoulder. "What's happening? What do you feel?"

"It's going out." Her own voice sounded far away. "The collapse is cutting it off from — from whatever it draws on. The mountain's veins. It's being strangled." She laughed, and hated the sound. "Ten thousand years, and Serah Dain doesn't even need to touch it. She just needs to knock the house down."

"Then we carry it out," Isa said. "Take it. Run."

"We can't. The fourth seal—" Bram's throat closed. She made herself say it. "It can't be moved. It was never meant to be carried. It was meant to be *kept*. By keepers. From inside." She looked up at them, and the dust, and the torchlight leaking through the wound in the sky, and said the rest of it plainly, because there was no time left for anything but plain speech.

"If we don't share it now — tonight, in this room — it dies. And if it dies, the seal dies. And if the seal dies, everything the old war locked away comes home to find the door open." She swallowed. "There's no version of this where we walk away and try again tomorrow. Tomorrow is a word for people with time."

*

The chamber shuddered again. Smaller this time — an aftershock, the mountain settling into its ruin — but a slab the size of a cart came loose from the wall and crashed down thirty feet away, and the torchlight above multiplied. Voices. They were widening the breach. Coming down.

Odile stood very still amid the settling dust, and Bram watched her do the thing she did best: think. Fast, furious, unglamorous thinking, the kind that had cracked three seals' worth of dead languages while the rest of them slept.

"The covenant splits," Odile said slowly. "Fire shared endures. Five ways — you, me, Isa, and..." She stopped. They all stopped. The name hung there like the dust.

"She'll never take it," Isa said flatly. "She'll cut our hands off first."

"Then four ways," Odile said. "Four might hold. Four has to hold, because the alternative is—"

The light above brightened. A rope uncoiled through the breach, slow and deliberate, the way ropes descend when soldiers are at the end of them.

Bram looked at the flame. Small. Guttering. Ten thousand years old and smaller than a candle, and it had kept every promise it ever made, and it had never once been asked what *it* wanted.

*Well?* she thought at it. *Any last wisdom? Any godly counsel?*

The warmth stirred against her palms — faint, failing, but unmistakably amused. It felt, absurdly, like Rennick's grin. Like the shrug of someone holding a bad hand beautifully.

*Share me,* the warmth said, in the voice that was not a voice. *Before I am only a story. And Bram Ashgrove — tell the angry one with the sword that I remember her sister. Tell her I kept the door the night they died. Tell her—*

The mountain roared.

Not an explosion this time. Something deeper — a groan from the roots of the world, the sound of Mount Sarn deciding, all at once, that it was tired of standing. The fissure above yawned wider. Dust became a curtain became a weather. Through it, descending on the rope, came the first silhouette: armored, torchlit, helmeted, a knight of the Hollow Order lowering herself into the dying heart of the oldest promise in the world.

And behind her, faint through the thunder, from the direction of the eastern gallery — a sound Bram knew with her whole body, a sound she had heard across campfires and card games and one long salt road:

Rennick Cael, laughing.

"Bram." Isa's hand found hers in the dark. Steady. Terrified. There. "Tell me what to do."

Bram Ashgrove looked at the guttering flame, at the rope, at the two women who had crossed a continent and a war to stand in this exact spot, and felt desperation sharpen itself, whetstone-smooth, into something with an edge.

Decision.

"Now," she said. "Both of you. Hands in. *Now.*"

* * *

The rope touched the floor. A boot followed it. Through the dust, torchlight caught on pale armor, and a voice that Bram had heard offer amnesty and threats in equal measure rang out across the collapsing chamber:

"Ashgrove! Step away from the flame!"

Serah Dain landed in a crouch, sword drawn, and looked up — at the fissure, at the falling mountain, at the three fugitives kneeling around a candle-sized fire with their hands outstretched — and for one suspended moment, her certainty and Bram's desperation stared at each other across ten feet of shaking stone.

Bram did not step away.

She reached out her free hand instead — palm open, empty, offered — and said the only true thing left worth saying:

"Serah. It's dying. Help us save it, or help us die. But choose *now*."
