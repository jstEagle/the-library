## 36. The Sharing

**Bram**

The chamber was coming down around them in slow, grinding pieces.

Bram felt it first through her boots — a shudder that traveled up from the caldera's roots like the mountain clearing its throat. Dust sifted from the ceiling in gray curtains. Somewhere above, the eastern vent had opened its mouth, and the whole of Mount Sarn was beginning to decide whether it wanted to remain a mountain at all.

And still the Ember burned. Small as a candle. Small as a birthday wish.

It sat on a shelf of black glass at the chamber's heart, and it did not flicker, and it did not care about falling stone. It simply waited, the way Kestrel had always waited — patient, silent, certain that whatever came next would come honestly or not at all.

"Tell me again why we didn't take the other tunnel," Rennick had said, an hour ago, before he'd stayed behind to hold the pass alone. Bram could still hear him. She suspected she'd hear him for the rest of her life, which — if she had anything to say about it — would be a long one.

"Bram." Isa's hand closed on her arm. Her knuckles were white with ash. "The floor."

The floor was cracking. A seam ran from the black-glass shelf toward the far wall, widening by inches, breathing out heat that smelled of iron and old summers.

Odile stood at the edge of the light, her satchel clutched to her chest, her spectacles fogged with ash. She'd been crying, Bram realized. Not sobbing — Odile never sobbed — but tears had cut clean tracks down her dusty face, and she hadn't bothered to wipe them.

"It's guttering," Odile said. "Do you see? It's *guttering*. The covenant is failing because there is no one left to keep it. It has been waiting—" her voice cracked, "—it has been waiting for someone to *ask*."

"I'm asking," Bram said.

She stepped forward, into the warmth, and held out both hands over the little flame the way she had once held them over the shrine coals in the valley, back when prayer was simple and gods were quiet and she had believed silence meant absence.

*Take all of me,* she thought. *Take the name, the bloodline, the whole stubborn Ashgrove of it. I'll be your vessel. I'll be Kestrel entire. Just don't go out.*

The flame leaned toward her like a cat recognizing its person.

And then Isa stepped up beside her and took her hand.

"Not like that," Isa said. Her voice was rough, and her eyes were red-rimmed, and she was still angry — Bram could feel the anger in her grip, alive and unfinished — but she took Bram's hand anyway. "You promised you'd stop deciding things alone. This counts double."

"The fire doesn't split," Bram said. "That's not how—"

"You don't know that," Isa said. "Nobody knows that. Your god spent four thousand years being wrong about what people can carry. Let it be surprised."

The flame flickered. Once. Twice. And something moved through the chamber that was not wind and was not heat — something like attention, vast and old and suddenly, wonderfully uncertain.

"Oh," said Odile softly. "*Oh.* It's listening."

* * *

**Isa**

Isa had imagined this moment a hundred times since Greywater, and in none of those imaginings had she been holding hands with Bram Ashgrove while a mountain tried to die on top of them.

In most of them she'd been angrier, too. Cleaner-angry. The kind of anger where you say your piece and walk away and the story lets you be right.

But her father had carried his secret across half the world and died whispering it to a stranger, and Isa had spent months hating Bram for receiving what should have been hers. And then she'd watched Bram offer herself — wholly, gladly, *gratefully* — to a flame that would have eaten her name and left nothing behind but a god wearing her face, and Isa had understood, with terrible clarity, that her father would have done exactly the same thing. Had done exactly the same thing, in slower motion, his whole life.

She was not going to let another Varrow be consumed by a secret.

"So how does this work?" she asked. "Do we say words? Do we—"

"I think," Odile said, coming forward to stand at Bram's other side, "we simply mean it."

"That's the worst instruction I've ever received," Isa said, "and I was taught swordplay by a woman who communicates exclusively in grunts."

"That woman saved your life six times," Bram said.

"Seven," Isa said. "Don't undersell yourself now; it's unbecoming."

The crack in the floor reached the shelf. Black glass tilted. The Ember slid an inch toward the edge, and Isa's heart went with it, and there was no more time for instructions good or bad.

She gripped Bram's hand. She reached out with her free one and let her fingers hover over the flame — close enough to feel it press against her skin like a question.

*I'm not asking to be special,* she thought, because it seemed like the kind of thing you ought to say honestly. *I'm asking to help. My father died keeping you alive. Let me finish his work. Let me do it *with* her.*

The flame touched her fingertips.

Warmth went up her arm like dawn going up a valley — not burning, not even hot, just *filling*, the way sunlight fills a room you've been sitting in so long you'd forgotten it was dark. She heard something that was not a sound: a vast wingbeat, far away, unhurried. A promise older than language, renewed rather than spoken.

When she opened her eyes, she was still herself. That was the miracle of it. She was still Isa Varrow, twenty-three years old, bad at cards, worse at forgiveness, and inside her chest something burned that had been burning since before the first wall of the first city, and it fit. It *fit*, the way her father's coat had never quite fit her shoulders and yet she wore it anyway.

"It's in me," she whispered. "Bram. It's *in* me and I'm still—"

"Still you," Bram said. Her own eyes were shining, and her voice shook. "That's the whole point. That was always the whole point."

* * *

**Odile**

Odile Vane had been exiled from the Royal Archive at thirty-one for publishing a paper arguing that dragons survived. They had stripped her title, burned her books, and called her a fantasist in the official record, and she had spent eleven years proving them right in every way except the one that mattered.

Now she stood in the caldera of Mount Sarn with dragon-fire settling into her chest like a coal wrapped in wool, and she found — professionally, academically, with the last shred of her scholarly discipline — that she wanted very much to take notes.

"There's a *structure*," she breathed. "Do you feel it? It's not a possession, it's a *covenant* — reciprocal clauses, mutual obligations — oh, whoever wrote this was a lawyer before they were a mystic, I could *kiss* them—"

"Odile," Bram said.

"—the terms are beautifully plain, actually, that's the genius, no hidden clauses, no fine print, just *keep it lit and share the cost* — it's the most elegant legal instrument I've ever—"

"*Odile.*"

"I'm having a moment," Odile said with dignity. "Eleven years of being laughed at earns me a moment."

But her hands were shaking, and not only from wonder. Because she had deduced what Bram was months ago, in the winter camp, watching the girl pray at a shrine that shouldn't answer — and she had confronted her, and Bram had lied, and the lie had sat between them like a stone in a boot for a hundred miles. And now here they stood, sharing a god, and the lie was still there, and Odile discovered that she did not want it there anymore. Not today. Not on this day of all days.

"Bram Ashgrove," she said formally, "you lied to me."

"I know."

"You looked me in the eye in the snow and told me I was imagining things."

"I know."

"And I forgave you approximately forty minutes ago, when you offered to burn yourself alive rather than hoard this thing, which is the single stupidest and bravest act I have ever witnessed, and I have read the collected biographies of the Saint-Kings." Odile pushed her fogged spectacles up her nose. "I am telling you this now so that later, when you are insufferable, I can remind you that my forgiveness predates your heroism and therefore cannot be attributed to it."

Bram made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "Noted, archivist."

"Furthermore," Odile said, "I believe the covenant requires a fifth keeper."

The chamber went quiet. Even the mountain seemed to hold its breath.

They all turned toward the tunnel mouth, where Serah Dain stood in the ash-light with her sword drawn and her charges useless behind her, her knights dead or fled or kneeling, and her face wearing the expression of a woman whose entire architecture of belief had just been demolished with her still standing in the doorway.

"She tried to kill us," Isa said.

"Repeatedly," Odile agreed. "With escalating competence."

"She's *right there*," Isa hissed. "She can *hear* us."

"Good," Odile said. "Then she can hear the terms."

* * *

**Serah**

Serah Dain had come down into the caldera to end magic forever.

She had rehearsed this. In the long marches and the sleepless camps, she had rehearsed it the way other soldiers rehearsed battles: the charge set, the vent collapsed, the flame smothered under ten thousand tons of Sarn's heart, and the world delivered at last from the tyranny of things that burned brighter than men. Her sister's face had kept her company on that road. Marta, laughing, before the cultists came. Marta, dead, with a god's name in the killers' mouths like a license.

She had expected resistance. She had expected to die. She had not expected — and this was the unforgivable part, the part she would carry longer than any wound — she had not expected to *stand here*, sword lowered, watching a candle-sized flame refuse to be either weapon or tyrant, and feel her certainty come apart in her hands like wet rope.

The Ashgrove woman had spared her twice. Twice. And now the scholar — the little exiled archivist with the fogged spectacles — was offering her a place in the miracle.

"I killed your friends' countrymen," Serah said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears. Flat. Far away. "I executed villagers. I tortured the scholar. I came here to bury you all."

"Yes," Odile said. "We're aware. We were present."

"The Order taught me that fire like this always finds a master. Always. That it's the nature of power to climb into a throne." Serah looked at the Ember — small, patient, *waiting* — and then at the four people standing around it, holding hands like children crossing a river. "And you're doing the one thing it isn't supposed to be able to do."

"We're sharing it," Bram said.

"Why?" And there it was — the real question, the one underneath her sister's grave and her whole crusade, the question she had never dared ask because the answer might unmake her. "Why would you give away the only thing that makes you safe?"

Bram considered her for a long moment. Ash drifted between them. The mountain groaned overhead like an old man getting out of a chair.

"Because I've seen what keeping it alone costs," Bram said. "I grew up in a temple to a silent god, and I thought the silence was abandonment. It wasn't. It was *loneliness*. Four thousand years of it, poured into a bloodline, waiting for someone too stubborn to repeat the mistake." She held out her hand. "I'm not offering you absolution, Serah. I'm not sure you get that. I'm offering you a job."

Serah Dain, knight-captain of the Hollow Order, breaker of villages, widow of certainty, stood in the falling ash and did not move.

The ceiling chose that moment to shed a boulder the size of a horse. It struck the floor ten feet from the shelf, and the black glass lurched, and the Ember — the last living dragon-fire in the world, the covenant of an age — tipped toward the widening crack like a coin toward a drain.

Isa lunged. Missed. The flame dropped—

—and stopped. Hung in the air above the fissure, burning steadily, utterly indifferent to gravity, as though it had merely wished to demonstrate that it had opinions about being dropped.

"Well," said Odile faintly. "It's showing off."

"Decide faster," Isa snapped at Serah. "It's got a sense of humor and we're all about to be buried."

* * *

**Bram**

Later — much later, years later, on the nights when the shared flame woke her at odd hours with its slow warm pulse — Bram would try to remember what finally tipped Serah Dain's hand. A word? A look? The sight of the flame hanging in the air like a patient dog?

She never could. She only remembered the stillness: Serah standing apart in the ash-fall, sword-point resting on stone, her face doing arithmetic that had no honest sum. And then she remembered the knight-captain sheathing her blade — slowly, the way you close a door on a room you'll never enter again — and walking forward, and kneeling, not to the flame but beside it, taking Odile's hand on one side and Isa's on the other, her gauntlets off, her bare hands scarred and steady.

"If this burns me for what I've done," Serah said quietly, "I accept it."

The flame rose from the fissure. It hung between the five of them, small and bright and impossibly old, and Bram felt it look at each of them in turn — really *look*, the way Kestrel had once looked at her through a wayside shrine, the way her god had looked at her all her life without her knowing.

Then it divided.

There was no thunder. No pillar of light. It came apart the way a loaf comes apart — warmly, naturally, as though division were its native language — and five portions settled into five chests, and the remainder sank down into the rock itself, into the wounded mountain, threading through the broken stone like root through soil.

The shaking stopped.

The dust hung motionless in the air. Somewhere far below, Bram felt the caldera's bleeding vents knit closed, felt the mountain sigh and settle on its ancient foundations, and knew — with a keeper's new and unshakable knowledge — that Sarn would hold. That the war sealed beneath the world was sealed still, guarded now not by one hidden fire but by five stubborn hearts and one very large, very tired mountain.

No one became a god.

She checked, carefully, the way you check a wound. Reached inward to the ember in her chest and found — herself. Bram Ashgrove, mercenary, orphan, terrible at expressing affection. The flame sat inside her like a hearth sits inside a house: essential, warming, and entirely aware of who owned the property.

Beside her, Isa was laughing and crying at once, pressing her fist to her sternum. Odile had finally, finally taken out a notebook and was writing with a charred stick, narrating aloud ("reciprocal clause confirmed, *no ascension event*, take *that*, the entire Academy"). Serah knelt where she was, head bowed, silent, her shoulders shaking in a way that had nothing to do with fear.

And the Ember — the flame itself, the covenant, the last of the dragons' gift — was gone. Not extinguished. *Distributed*. Scattered so wide that no army could smother it, no zealot could steal it, no lonely god could ever again pour it into a single bloodline and wait four thousand years for rescue.

It was the triumph Bram had crossed the world to win.

It felt like a funeral.

Because Rennick Cael was not there to cheat at cards over it. Rennick, who had bargained his family's faces away for one more season, who had held a collapsing tunnel alone so they could reach this room, who had died smiling in the dark with no witness but the stone — Rennick should have been here, complaining about the ash, flirting disgracefully with Odile, insisting that if everyone got a piece of the fire he deserved two.

"He'd have hated missing this," Isa said thickly, reading her mind the way family does.

"He'd have claimed a larger share on account of his sacrifice," Odile said, sniffling.

"He'd have negotiated," Bram agreed. Her voice came out rough. "He'd have gotten it, too. He always did."

For a moment the five of them stood together in the settling ash — a mercenary, a smuggler's ghost in their hearts, a scholar, a daughter, and a knight — bound by a flame that asked nothing except that they never, ever carry it alone.

Then Odile looked up from her notebook, pushed her spectacles into place, and said, in the tone of a woman who has just remembered something inconvenient:

"I don't wish to alarm anyone. But the eastern vent is still open, the tunnels are collapsed, and unless I've misread the geometry — which I have not — our only way out of this caldera is straight through what remains of the Hollow Order's column."

Serah wiped her face, stood, and rolled her shoulders like a woman returning to work.

"Lucky for you," she said dryly, "I know the man who commands it. And lucky for him, he's about to meet the five keepers of the last dragon-fire in the world — and discover that his warrant is worth less than the paper it's written on."

Bram picked up her sword, felt the ember in her chest lean warm against her ribs like a friend taking her arm, and started for the stairs.

"Then let's go introduce ourselves," she said.
