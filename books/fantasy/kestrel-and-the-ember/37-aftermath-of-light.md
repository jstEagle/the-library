## 37. Aftermath of Light

Odile Vane had spent thirty years believing that the end of the world would arrive as a footnote — a dry line in some archive, *herein ends all things*, filed between a grain tariff and a bishop's complaint about cheese. She had not expected it to feel like climbing stairs.

The caldera's inner path was gone. The eastern vent had taken it, along with half the rim road and, she suspected, any hope Rennick had ever entertained of being paid for this expedition. What remained was a scramble of shattered stone and cooling ash, a slope that shrugged them upward in slow, grudging increments, and a sky going grey at its eastern edge with the first dishonest suggestion of dawn.

Bram went first. Of course Bram went first; Odile had known her for the better part of a season now and could count on one hand the number of times the woman had let anyone else break trail. But there was something different in the set of her shoulders this morning. Something lighter. When she reached back to haul Odile over a lip of broken rock, her grip was warm — actually warm, faintly glowing at the fingertips if you knew where to look, which Odile did, because looking was the entire profession.

"You're shedding," Odile said, nodding at the sparks drifting off Bram's sleeve like dandelion fluff.

"I'm *what*?"

"Sparks. Little ones. Very festive."

Bram looked down at herself, swore under her breath, and shook out her cloak, scattering embers into the dark like an offended hearth. "That's going to be a problem, isn't it."

"Almost certainly," Odile said cheerfully. "Everything worth doing is."

Behind them, Isa Varrow climbed in silence, ash to her elbows, her father's map — or what remained of it, four-fifths cinder now, its work done — folded against her heart. And behind Isa, moving with the deliberate care of a woman whose entire worldview had been detonated under her sometime in the last hour, came Serah Dain, knight-captain of the Hollow Order, her white-and-grey surcoat black with soot, her sword still sheathed because she had not been able to decide what to do with it.

Odile had watched Serah make that decision eleven times on the way up. Draw it, sheathe it, draw it, sheathe it. A woman trying to remember how to hold her own hands.

And below them all, far below, sealed behind a wall of collapsed stone: Rennick Cael.

Odile did not look back. Looking back would have meant stopping, and stopping would have meant thinking, and thinking about Rennick was a door she had nailed shut somewhere around the fourth switchback and did not intend to reopen until her legs gave out or the sun rose, whichever came first.

* * *

They came out onto the rim as the light did.

It was not a dramatic sunrise. That was the thing Odile would remember afterward, when she wrote it all down — and she would write it down, every word, even the parts that made her look foolish, *especially* those, because a scholar who edited herself was no scholar at all. It was not a dramatic sunrise. It was a grey, tired, ordinary dawn, the kind that happened over the valley every morning of Bram's magicless childhood, the kind that happened everywhere, indifferent and punctual, and it caught them standing in a ragged line on the broken edge of Mount Sarn with ash in their hair and a dead friend in the dark below them, and it did not care, and somehow that was the mercy of it. The world went on being the world. They were allowed to go on being in it.

Bram stood at the very edge, looking down into the caldera, where the chamber they had left was settling into itself with small geological sighs. The Ember was gone — not extinguished, never extinguished, but *distributed*, poured out five ways like wine too precious for one cup. Odile felt it in herself even now: a low warmth behind the sternum, patient as a banked fire, utterly unlike anything she had ever studied. Magic, she thought. Actual magic. In *her*. The disgraced archivist who had lost her chair, her library, and twenty years of reputation for insisting dragons still lived, and had been right in the most inconvenient way imaginable.

"It's quiet," Isa said. Her voice was hoarse. "In here. I expected it to be loud."

"Fire's only loud when it's hungry," Bram said. "This kind just... sits. Waits to be needed." She flexed her fingers. Sparks drifted again. She glared at them.

Odile peered past her into the caldera's throat, where — far below, at the ruined rim of the lower field — the surviving soldiers of the Hollow Order were emerging from their positions like ants from a flooded nest. Dozens of them. Maybe more. Some were staring up at the five figures silhouetted against the dawn. Some were staring at their own hands, because the powder charges had failed to kill the flame and something had to be blamed, and men blame their hands before they blame their gods.

"They'll come up," Serah said quietly. It was the first thing she'd said in an hour. "Some of them. The true believers."

"And the rest?" Isa asked.

Serah was silent a moment. "The rest will discover they're tired," she said. "Tiredness is more honest than faith. It usually wins."

* * *

The true believers did come up. There were nine of them, led by a lieutenant Odile didn't know — a broad young man with a fresh burn on his cheek and the particular rigid posture of someone who has decided that if he stops moving he will fall apart.

They crested the slope in formation, which was almost touching, given that the formation consisted of nine exhausted men on a slag heap at dawn. Their captain raised his sword.

Bram didn't move. Neither did anyone else. Odile, who had spent the last several minutes composing a suitably dignified opening statement (*Sirs, I would direct your attention to the historical record—*), watched with scholarly interest as the lieutenant took in the scene: the mercenary, the smuggler's friends, the archivist, and — worst of all, Odile could see it land — the knight-captain of the Hollow Order standing among them, unarmed, ash-covered, and *not fighting*.

"Lieutenant Hask," Serah said.

"Captain Dain." His voice cracked on the rank. "You're... they said you went down with the charges. To destroy it yourself."

"The charges failed." Serah turned her hand over. For a moment — deliberately, Odile realized; this was theater, and Serah Dain had always understood theater — she let the light show. A coal-glow beneath the skin of her palm, steady and small. "It can't be destroyed. I've seen that now. It was never a weapon. It was a promise, and we were the ones breaking it."

Hask stared at her hand the way Odile had once stared at a manuscript that proved everything she'd built her career on was wrong. Odile recognized the expression intimately. It was the face of a man watching his life reorganize itself without his permission.

"The Order teaches—" he started.

"The Order taught what I told it to teach," Serah said. "I wrote the doctrine, Lieutenant. Me. Out of grief, and I dressed the grief up as righteousness, and good people died carrying it. Including yours, I'd wager." She stepped forward, alone, empty-handed. "So here are your options. You can arrest me. I'll come quietly, and I'll testify to everything — the warrant, the masters in Corvane who ordered villages burned, all of it. Or you can put down that sword, walk down this mountain, and go home, and I will spend whatever years I have left making sure no one ever hands you another lie like the one I handed you."

The wind moved the ash. Somewhere below, a soldier laughed — one short, cracked, disbelieving note.

Hask looked at his sword. He looked at Serah. He looked, briefly and terribly, at his own hands.

Then he drove the sword point-first into the ash, where it stood upright, quivering, a grave marker for nothing at all, and turned, and walked away. Seven of his eight followed him. The ninth hesitated — a boy, really, sixteen at most — and then knelt. Not to Bram. Not to any of them. He knelt facing the caldera, the way men kneel at shrines, and after a moment he got up too, wiping his face with a filthy sleeve, and went.

Nobody spoke for a while.

"Well," Odile said at last, because someone had to, and she had built a career on being the someone. "I've attended fewer successful academic defenses."

Isa made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a sob and settled, as usual, on both.

* * *

They came down the mountain the slow way, because there was no other way left.

By midmorning they had reached the tree line, and by noon the first green things — actual green things, unburnt, absurdly cheerful — and it was there, in a meadow full of early crocuses, that Bram finally sat down and did not get up for two hours. Odile sat beside her. Isa collapsed across Bram's knees like a cat claiming territory, and to everyone's visible astonishment, including her own, Bram let her.

Rennick's absence sat among them like a fifth chair pulled up to the fire.

"He held the tunnel," Isa said, to no one, to the crocuses. "Alone. So we'd have time."

"He always cheated at cards," Odile said. "Did I ever tell you? Every game. Flagrant, unrepentant cheating. I once caught him with three aces up his sleeve and he tried to convince me aces were simply attracted to him. Magnetic personality, he said."

Bram huffed — half laugh, half something else. "He told me once that his family died guarding the map's route, and that he'd spent ten years making sure nobody else ever walked it, and that it was the stupidest way to honor anyone he could think of, and he was going to keep doing it anyway." She was quiet a moment. "Then he walked it himself. With us. On purpose."

"That's not stupid," Isa said fiercely. "That's—"

"That's exactly the kind of arithmetic Rennick would appreciate," Odile said gently. "A bad trade made gladly. He'd want us to argue about whether it was worth it for the next forty years. It's how he'd haunt us. Through the argument."

The warmth behind Odile's sternum shifted — not painfully, just a turn, like a sleeper rolling over. She wondered if the others felt it too. She suspected they did, because Isa suddenly pressed a hand flat to her own chest and frowned, and Bram's sparks chose that moment to return, drifting off her shoulders in a lazy golden haze, and Serah — sitting apart, as she had sat apart all day, close enough to guard them and too honest to pretend otherwise — looked down at her own hands and whispered something Odile was fairly sure was a prayer, the first one, perhaps, that Serah Dain had ever aimed at anything smaller than a god.

*Small wonders*, Odile thought. *Seeping in.* She had spent her life cataloguing the great ones — dragons, seals, wars — and had entirely missed that magic, real magic, might mostly consist of warmth in the chest and light on the shoulders and the unbearable, specific tenderness of being alive on a Tuesday.

She took out her journal. Her hands were shaking — exhaustion, grief, triumph, the usual cocktail — but she wrote anyway, because the record mattered, because *someone* had to write it down honestly, and because Rennick would have mocked her mercilessly for skipping a single day.

*Day ninety-one,* she wrote. *Mount Sarn, south meadow. The Ember lives. We all do, which is stranger. Distribution of the covenant as follows: B.A., I.V., S.D., myself, and the mountain itself, which declined to comment. Casualties: one porter (deserted), one guide (deserved it), and Rennick Cael, who was neither casualty nor loss but rather, I am coming to believe, the whole reason any of us arrived anywhere at all.*

*Note for future scholars: the fire does not command. This is the finding of my life. It waits. It warms. It asks nothing except to be shared, and it dies if hoarded — which means the Hollow Order was wrong in the worst possible way, and I was right in the best one, and I intend to be insufferable about both facts for the remainder of my natural span.*

She paused, pen hovering.

*Addendum:* she wrote. *Bram is still shedding sparks. Isa says it looks like fairydust. Bram says if one more person calls it fairydust she is walking into the sea.*

* * *

They buried Rennick's effects at the tree line, since his body stayed in the mountain and the mountain was not, in Odile's professional opinion, done arguing about it.

His cards. His knife. The river-spirit's token, a smooth grey stone that no longer hummed — the bargain spent, the season used up. Isa laid them out on a flat rock, and Bram built the cairn, and Serah — after a long, visible internal war — saluted it, crisp and formal, soldier's honors for a smuggler, which would have delighted him and appalled him in equal measure.

"He'd hate this," Isa said. "All this solemnity. He'd say we were wasting good drinking time."

"He'd steal the cairn stones," Bram agreed, "and sell them back to us as relics."

"*Genuine Rocks of the Fallen Hero,*" Odile said. "Five coppers each, ten for the authentic provenance, which he'd invent on the spot."

And then, because grief is a door that swings both ways, Isa started laughing, and then Bram, and then — helplessly, horribly, wonderfully — all of them, right there over the cairn, laughing until they cried and crying until they laughed, and Odile understood, with the clarity she usually reserved for footnotes, that this was the funeral Rennick would have wanted: loud, irreverent, and attended exclusively by people who loved him.

The warmth in her chest pulsed once, twice, like laughter of its own.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, you absolute cheat. You found a way in anyway.*

* * *

They reached the lowlands in three days, and the world had already begun to gossip.

Word ran ahead of them through the river towns the way news does — distorted, embellished, occasionally improved: *the dragon-fire of Mount Sarn has woken; no, it has died; no, it has flown north in the shape of a woman; the Order is dissolved; the Order has seized the capital; the Order's own captain has turned witness against it.* By the time they crossed the ford where a dying stranger had once stumbled into a mercenary's camp clutching a scorched map, there were already three ballads, all of them wrong, one of them featuring Odile as a witch with tentacles, which she intended to have framed.

At the ford itself, Bram stopped.

The others waited, sensing it. She stood where the man had fallen — where it had all begun, the map and the arrows and the unwilling inheritance — and the evening light came down the valley the same way it had that night, gold going to grey, and Odile watched her take off her glove and press her bare hand flat against the old shrine-stone at the water's edge. The wayside shrine. Kestrel's shrine. Cracked, weathered, silent for a hundred years.

For a long moment, nothing.

Then the stone warmed. Odile saw it happen — saw the frost of centuries lift off it in a soft exhalation, saw the moss brighten, saw a thread of gold light run up through the crack like sap rising in spring. Small. Quiet. Enough.

Bram took her hand back. Her eyes were wet and she pretended they weren't, and everyone politely pretended along with her, and that, Odile reflected, was also a kind of covenant.

"It's not him," Bram said quietly. "I keep reaching for him and it's not him. It's just... us. Echoing."

"Echoes are how the dead stay in the room," Isa said. "My mother used to say that."

"Your mother sounds like she'd have liked Rennick."

"Everyone liked Rennick. It was infuriating."

They made camp on the near bank, in sight of the shrine, and Odile sat up late with her journal while the others slept — Bram on watch because Bram would be on watch until the day she died, some habits being load-bearing — and wrote the last lines of the day's entry by the light of her own fingers, which, she was pleased to report, shed considerably more dignified sparks than Bram's did.

*Final observation, Day ninety-four:* she wrote. *Magic cannot be hunted now, because it hides in five ordinary hearts and one stubborn mountain. It cannot be hoarded, because it was given freely and free things cannot be stolen, only refused. And it cannot die, because — and here the archivist must set down her pen and admit the truth she once lost everything for saying —*

She stopped. Looked at the sleeping shapes around the dying fire. Bram, upright and vigilant, sparks drifting off her shoulders like patient snow. Isa curled against her pack with her hand resting on the burnt map. Serah apart but near, her sword finally, permanently sheathed, her face in sleep almost young.

*—because love, apparently, is a renewable resource. Someone check my citations. I don't believe it either.*

She closed the journal. Across the ford, in the dark, the shrine-stone glowed faintly, steadily, like a lamp left burning in a window for someone expected home.

And far to the east — though none of them knew it yet, though the news was even then riding hard through the night toward them — a rider carried a letter bearing the seal of the Order's masters in Corvane, addressed to one Serah Dain, containing three words that would change everything:

*We know where.*
