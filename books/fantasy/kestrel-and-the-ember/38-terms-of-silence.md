## 38. Terms of Silence

Serah Dain had signed her name to a great many documents in her life. Warrants. Requisitions. Death lists, though the Order had preferred the gentler phrase *censure rolls*. She had learned to write in a fine, narrow hand that took up as little space as possible, because paper was expensive and men were not.

The document on the table before her now was one page, and it had taken her three nights and four drafts to get it right.

She sat in what remained of the Order's field headquarters — a commandeered granary at the foot of Mount Sarn, its roof half caved from the tremors that had rolled down off the caldera two days past. Ash still sifted through the gaps in the beams, settling in a fine grey dust over everything: the map table, the abandoned ledgers, the warrant box with its broken seal. Her knights had cleared out most of the wounded to Greywater. The rest had gone quiet in the way soldiers go quiet after something they cannot yet name has happened to them.

Outside, dawn was coming up the colour of old pewter. Somewhere down the road, someone was singing — badly, cheerfully, a harvest song about a girl who outwitted a tax collector. It sounded like Odile Vane's doing. The scholar had appointed herself morale officer for the surviving Order column, apparently on the theory that no one could brood properly while she corrected their grammar.

Serah dipped her pen. Her hand did not shake. That surprised her a little; she had expected it to.

*By order of Serah Dain, Knight-Captain of the Hollow Order, acting under emergency authority vested in me by Article Nine following the destruction of the Order's command structure at Mount Sarn —*

Destruction. That was one word for it. *Rout* was another. *Miracle*, if you asked the porters who had survived the eastern vent, though most of them asked nothing and simply stared at their hands as if expecting them to glow.

She wrote on.

*— I hereby dissolve, in perpetuity, all warrants issued by this Order against Bram Ashgrove, Isa Varrow, Odile Vane, Rennick Cael (deceased), and any persons who aided or sheltered them.*

There. Four names living, one dead. She had argued with herself about Rennick Cael for an hour the first night. A smuggler, a liar, a man who had cheated her own quartermaster at cards during a parley and then had the gall to wink about it. And also a man who had held a collapsing tunnel alone so that strangers could live. Serah had spent twenty years teaching herself that people were simple — creed on one side, error on the other — and the dead smuggler kept refusing to fit into either column.

She let his name stand. It cost her less than she'd feared.

*Furthermore,* she wrote, *I offer my full testimony, given freely and under whatever binding the magistrates of the free cities see fit to impose, against the Masters of the Hollow Order: their financing of the Greywater massacre, their purchase of the traitor's road, their orders concerning the village of Fenlow, and the standing instruction — which I carried out, and which I will answer for — that all who touched the map were to be killed regardless of surrender.*

Her pen paused there. She made herself finish the sentence anyway. A confession that stopped short of your own crimes wasn't a confession; it was a eulogy for someone else.

She signed it. *Serah Dain.* The narrow hand, taking up little space. Then, after a moment, she added her rank beneath it and struck the rank through. Let the magistrates puzzle over that. Let them understand that the woman signing no longer claimed the title, but would not pretend she had never held it.

She was blotting the ink when the granary door opened without a knock, which narrowed the field of candidates to exactly one person in the world.

Bram Ashgrove came in the way she seemed to do everything — like the room should be grateful she'd bothered. Ash-grey cloak, sword worn honest at her hip, a fresh bandage on her forearm where the caldera's last convulsion had thrown her against the rock. Her hair was still singed at the ends. She looked, Serah thought, like a woman who had climbed out of a burning mountain and found it only mildly inconvenient.

"You're up early," Bram said.

"I didn't sleep."

"Neither did I." Bram hooked a stool with her boot, dragged it over, and sat down across the table without being invited, which was either rudeness or trust, and Serah had long since stopped being able to tell the difference with this woman. "Odile sent me. She says if you don't eat something, she's going to start reading her dissertation aloud in the mess tent, and she knows exactly how long it is."

"That's a threat?"

"It's the worst threat she has. She used it on Isa yesterday and Isa surrendered inside a minute." Bram glanced at the document, upside down from where she sat. She didn't reach for it. Serah noted that — noted it the way she'd once noted the angle of a shield wall. Restraint, offered plainly. "Is that what I think it is?"

"It depends what you think it is."

"I think it's the end of the hunt."

Serah turned the page around and pushed it across the table. If she was going to do this, she would do it looking at Bram Ashgrove's face when she read it. She owed the woman that much honesty, at least. Probably more than that, if she was going to be rigorous about the accounting, and Serah Dain was always rigorous about the accounting.

Bram read it slowly. Twice. Her thumb lingered near Rennick Cael's name.

"You spelled his name right," she said quietly.

"I checked with Odile. She claims he told her seven different spellings depending on who was chasing him."

"He did. The one with the extra L was for tax collectors." Bram set the page down. "Why?"

It was the question Serah had been turning over for three nights, and she had promised herself she would answer it without armor. So she took a breath, and she answered it.

"Because I was wrong," she said. "Not mistaken — wrong. There's a difference. Mistaken means you lacked information. Wrong means you had everything you needed and chose not to look at it." She folded her hands on the table, knight-captain posture even without the rank. "I stood at the rim of that caldera ready to bury the last living fire in the world because I believed fire was all it had ever been. And then I watched four people split it between them like bread, and nobody burned, and nobody ruled, and the mountain didn't make a god out of any of you. You said something to me once. In the fight at Greywater."

"'You're wrong about what gods want.'"

"That's the one." Serah's mouth twitched — not quite a smile, but a relative of one. "I've had two days to sit with that. Two days of sitting is more than I've allowed myself in twenty years. Do you know what I found at the bottom of it?"

"What?"

"That I never actually hated gods." She said it flatly, the way you'd report a supply shortfall. "I hated what men did wearing their names. And then I looked at the Order — really looked, the way you look at your own hand when you suspect it's gone numb — and I saw that we had become the thing we swore to stop. Men doing terrible things wearing righteousness instead of divinity. Same shape. Same result. My sister's killers wore a god's name. My Order wears a creed. I have spent my whole life hunting the costume and never once checking who was inside it."

Bram was quiet for a moment. Outside, the bad singing had stopped, replaced by the clatter of the camp waking — pots, hooves, Odile's voice rising in cheerful academic outrage about someone's improper storage of the powder stores.

"The visions showed me your sister," Bram said. "At the fourth seal. I never told you that."

"I know. You told me at the caldera. The mortals wearing a god's name." Serah nodded slowly. "I didn't believe you. Not fully. Part of me decided you'd seen what you needed me to hear." She met Bram's eyes. "I believe you now. That's part of why the page exists. The other part—" she exhaled, "—the other part is that my knights deserve better than to spend their lives chasing a family across the world for the crime of existing. They're good soldiers. Most of them. Some of them are thieves and one of them I'm fairly sure is a bigamist, but they are good soldiers, and they followed me into a mountain because I told them it was holy work. It wasn't. The least I can do is tell them it's over before they find out some worse way."

"How are they taking it?"

"About how you'd expect." Serah leaned back. "Brother Aldous cried, which shocked everyone including Brother Aldous. Sergeant Maren asked me, very politely, whether dissolving the warrant meant she had to give back the horse she requisitioned from you people at the pass. Half the column hasn't spoken since the mountain. The other half won't stop talking. And young Pell — the one who held the signal line — keeps asking whether the flame in you lot means the world's ending." She shrugged. "I told him the world ended once already, at Fenlow, and nobody noticed, and maybe this is what un-ending looks like. He seemed to find that either profound or deeply unhelpful. With Pell it's hard to say."

That got a real laugh out of Bram — short, rusty, genuine. Serah found she liked the sound of it, which was a strange and slightly alarming discovery, rather like finding a door in a wall you'd believed solid your whole life.

"There's a second matter," Serah said, and reached into her coat.

She laid the object on the table between them: a key. Iron, heavy, old — the master key to the Order's archive vaults at Corvane, the citadel where every record of every atrocity, every payment, every name, was kept behind three feet of stone and a garrison that answered to the Masters alone.

Bram looked at it. Looked at her. "That's not yours to give."

"No. It's the Order's. Which is precisely why I'm giving it." Serah pushed it across the table. "The Masters aren't dead, Ashgrove. They weren't on the mountain. They're in Corvane behind their walls, and when word reaches them that their knight-captain dissolved the warrant and testified against them, they will do what such men always do. They'll burn the records first, and then they'll burn the witnesses. Me, my knights, Odile, Isa. Possibly you, though you strike me as difficult to kill — you have a certain mule quality about you."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't entirely a compliment." She tapped the key. "Testimony needs evidence to stand on. The evidence is in that vault. I can't take it — anything I carry will be seized the moment I'm declared rogue, and I will be declared rogue within the month. But a scholar with a grievance and a reputation for digging, accompanied by a woman the warrant no longer applies to..." She spread her hands. "Corvane's archive opens for petitioners twice a year. Next petition day is in eleven days. After that, I suspect, never again."

Bram picked up the key. Turned it over. Serah watched her face do several things in quick succession — calculation, reluctance, and underneath both, something that might have been tiredness, the deep kind, the kind that comes from carrying a god's worth of decisions in one pair of shoulders.

"Odile will cry," Bram said finally. "Actual tears. She's wanted into those vaults since before I met her. She talks about Corvane's restricted stacks the way other people talk about lost loves."

"Then consider it an apology to her as well. I had her cellar burned."

"You had a lot of cellars burned."

"Yes." Serah didn't flinch from it. "The list is long. I've started writing it out. Names, dates, what was taken. It goes in front of the magistrates with my testimony. Whatever they decide I owe, I'll pay it. Prison, restitution, the rope, if it comes to that." She paused. "I'm not asking for absolution. I want to be clear about that. Absolution is a thing other people give you, and I don't trust anyone else's ledger but my own. What I'm asking for is simpler. Terms."

"Terms of silence," Bram said, glancing at the document's heading.

"I drafted it at midnight. It seemed apt." Serah almost smiled. "The Order's whole creed was silence — silence the gods, silence the fire, silence anyone who disagreed. I thought it fitting that the Order should end by choosing silence itself. No more warrants. No more hunts. We go quiet, and what we did stays written down where it can't be quieted."

Bram studied her for a long moment. Serah bore it. She had stood before tribunals and battle lines and her own reflection on bad nights; she could stand before the grey eyes of a woman she had hunted across half the world.

"You could come with us," Bram said. "To Corvane. Extra swords wouldn't hurt, and honestly, watching you and Odile argue about cataloguing systems would be worth the trip on its own."

For one treacherous heartbeat, Serah wanted to say yes. She examined the wanting carefully, the way she'd once examined enemy fortifications — and found the same flaw in both. The gates looked open, but the ground before them was mined.

"No," she said. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. My knights need mustering out — properly, with pay and papers and somewhere to go, not just scattered like a kicked anthill. And the Masters will move against the column the moment they hear. I won't lead the Order's ghosts into your company until I've settled my own." She rose, and Bram rose with her, and for a moment they stood across the scarred table with the ash sifting down between them like slow grey snow. "Besides. Your family has earned a road without me on it. Isa Varrow looks at me and sees the people who killed her father. Time is the only thing that fixes that, and time works best at a distance."

"She's coming around. Slowly. She made Maren give back the horse, if that helps."

"It does, actually. That horse was stolen disgracefully."

They walked out together into the morning. The camp was stirring — and it was a different camp than it had been a week ago, Serah realized. Order tents and fugitive tents no longer faced each other across a no-man's-land of suspicion. Someone had strung a wash line between a knight's pavilion and the smugglers' old wagon. Two of Serah's soldiers were letting Isa Varrow teach them a knife game that was almost certainly gambling. Odile stood on a crate lecturing a circle of bored-looking pikemen about the migratory habits of dragons, and — Serah slowed, listening — at least two of them were asking questions.

"Well," Bram said dryly. "Look at that. You've got scholars now."

"One. Temporarily. On loan." Serah stopped at the edge of the granary's shadow and turned. This was the part she had rehearsed, and like all rehearsals, it dissolved the moment it mattered. So she spoke plainly instead. "Ashgrove. I hunted you for a season. I burned, and killed, and lied in the Order's name, and I would have collapsed that mountain with you inside it, and I am not going to stand here and ask you to forgive any of it, because forgiveness isn't mine to request. But I will say this once, and then never again." She straightened, and gave the words the same care she'd have given an oath. "You were the better of us. From the ford onward, every time our paths crossed, you chose the harder mercy and I chose the easier cruelty. I saw it. I was too proud to name it. I'm naming it now."

Bram was silent a moment. Then she stuck out her hand — sword-callused, bandaged, steady.

"Terms accepted," she said.

Serah took it. The grip was firm and warm and utterly ordinary, and somehow that undid her more than any speech could have. Equals, shaking on it, in the ash-light, while a disgraced scholar explained dragon migration to a bemused pike square. Twenty years of creed, and it ended here, in a handshake with a mercenary who smelled faintly of woodsmoke.

"Eleven days," Serah said, releasing her hand. "Petition day at Corvane. Send Odile early — the queues are monstrous, and the archivists respect punctuality more than virtue."

"And you? After the testimony?"

Serah looked east, toward the low hills and the long road and whatever waited beyond them for a woman with no order, no rank, and a ledger of debts she intended to settle personally.

"After," she said, "I think I'll go find my sister's grave. I've never once visited it. I was always too busy avenging it." She picked up her helmet from the granary step and tucked it under her arm like a thing retired. "It occurs to me I never actually mourned her. Just the war I built on top of her. Perhaps it's time to meet the girl underneath."

Bram nodded slowly. "If you ever ride west," she said, "there's a valley where magic doesn't go. Didn't, anyway. There's a shrine there that needs rebuilding, and I'm told the company of ex-knights improves the mortar considerably."

"I'll keep it in mind." Serah managed the smile fully this time — small, crooked, real. "Fair warning: I'm told I brood."

"We'll put you next to Isa. You can brood competitively."

Serah laughed — an unfamiliar sound in her own throat, rough as an unoiled hinge — and turned to go. She had taken perhaps ten steps when Bram's voice caught her.

"Dain."

She looked back.

"The Masters at Corvane," Bram said, and her easy manner had dropped away, and beneath it was the woman who had stared down a dying god and bargained with it eye to eye. "When you testify — watch the tall one. Master Corvel. The visions showed him too. He wasn't afraid of the Ember because it might make gods." A pause. "He was afraid of it because it already made him one. He carries a splinter of the old fire himself. Has for years. That's why the Order wants the rest of it dead — not to end divinity. To be the only one left holding it."

The morning seemed to cool around Serah by several degrees. Twenty years of certainty, and the ground shifted under her one more time.

"Then the vault isn't just evidence," she said quietly.

"The vault is the battlefield," Bram said. "And you're the only one of us they'll let through the gate."

Serah Dain stood in the ash-fall with a dissolved warrant in her pocket, a dead god's warning in her ears, and — for the first time in her adult life — absolutely no idea what she believed. It felt, strangely, like freedom.

She squared her shoulders and went to muster her knights, moving faster now, because eleven days had just become the shortest span in the world, and somewhere behind three feet of stone in Corvane, a false god was waiting to learn that his hunter had changed sides.
