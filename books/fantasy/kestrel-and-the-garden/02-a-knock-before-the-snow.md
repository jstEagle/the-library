## 2. A Knock Before the Snow

The blizzard came early that year, and it came wrong.

Dominic Nystrom knew snow the way a shepherd knows his flock—by its moods, its weight, its habits. Forty winters in the Thornfell had taught him to read the sky above the pass like a ledger: which clouds meant flurries, which meant burial. This was neither. The clouds hung low and motionless, gray as grave-cloth, and the snow that fell from them fell straight down, without wind, without swirl, as if the mountain itself had decided to be buried and was doing it methodically, flake by flake.

He stood in the doorway of his workshop with a cup of barley tea going cold in his hand and watched it come.

*Wrong,* said the voice inside the bone at his throat.

He wore it on a leather cord, as he always did—a knuckle-bone of a woman named Sera Voss, dead three hundred years before his grandfather's grandfather, carved with a single spiral. She had been a warden of the old covenant, back when there was a covenant to ward. Most nights her voice was a murmur at the edge of sleep, half-dream, half-memory. But lately she had been waking him before dawn, saying *wrong* in a tone he'd never heard from her, not in eleven years of wearing her death against his skin.

"The snow's just snow," he told her, quietly, because talking to bones was a habit he'd stopped apologizing for years ago. "Cold is just cold."

*You know better,* Sera said. *You've always known better.*

He did know better. That was the whole curse of him.

Dominic closed the door and latched it, and went back to the bench where the day's work waited: a keepsake commission from the widow Hark in the valley town below, who wanted her husband's finger-bone carved with an anchor because Tam Hark had been a ferryman and had drowned in the Greyford weir last spring. Dominic had taken the bone in trade for salt and lamp-oil, and he had put off carving it for two weeks, because he knew what would happen when the knife touched it. He would hear Tam Hark's last moment—the cold water, the rope slipping, whatever prayer or curse or name the man had managed—and then he would carry that drowning around in his own chest for days, the way he carried all of them, the way the workshop itself had become a kind of crowded graveyard where every shelf whispered.

Eleven years ago, he had sworn he would carve only for coin and never listen. Eleven years ago, he had also sworn a great many things.

The knife found the bone's grain. The workshop's small sounds folded away—the tick of the stove, the drip off the eaves—and Tam Hark's voice rose up through the ivory like water rising in a well.

*—tell Marta the bell rang for me. Tell her I heard it under the water, clear as church, and it wasn't frightening, it was—*

The knife slipped. Dominic set it down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until the drowning receded to a dull ache behind his breastbone.

"Anchor," he said aloud, hoarse. "She asked for an anchor. Not your last thoughts."

But the dead didn't take commissions. They gave what they gave.

He finished the anchor in silence, working blind through the residue of another man's death, and when it was done he wrapped it in wool and set it on the shelf by the door for the next traveler fool enough to climb the pass. Then he banked the stove, checked the stores out of habit—flour, salt, dried apples, a ham bone for broth—and told himself, as he did every night, that this was enough. This was a life. A roof, a craft, a mountain that wanted nothing from him and got nothing. Invisibility as a discipline. Quiet as armor.

Outside, the snow kept falling straight down, and the light died early, and somewhere far below in the valley a dog began to bark and could not stop.

* * *

He woke past midnight to a sound he had not heard in six years.

A knock.

Three knocks, spaced evenly, soft but deliberate—not the frantic hammering of someone lost and freezing, but patient. Almost polite. As if whoever stood on his threshold understood exactly how unwelcome they were and had decided to knock anyway.

Dominic lay still in the dark and listened. The stove ticked. Snow hissed faintly against the shutters. The dog in the valley had gone silent, which was somehow worse than the barking.

*Answer it,* Sera murmured from the cord on the bedpost. Her voice had an odd texture tonight, stretched thin, like a thread about to snap. *Or don't. But decide quickly. Something is coming down the mountain, carver, and it isn't only weather.*

"That's helpful," he muttered. "Thank you."

*I am three hundred years dead. Helpfulness is all I have left.*

He dressed in the dark, pulled his boots on, and took the carving knife from the bench—not because he expected to use it, but because his hands wanted something honest to hold. The latch was stiff with cold. It took him two tries.

When he opened the door, the world was white and soundless and lit a strange pale blue by the snow's own reflection, and standing in it, not two paces from his step, was a child.

A girl, perhaps eight years old, maybe nine—it was hard to tell, because she was so caked in frost that she seemed carved rather than born, a small figure of rime with dark eyes sunk deep in a white face. Her hair was black and heavy with ice. She wore no coat. No boots. Just a thin shift of some fabric he didn't recognize, gray-green, patterned with veins like the underside of a leaf, and she stood barefoot in snow that should have been waist-deep by now, in drifts that reached his thigh, and the snow around her feet was—

He looked again. The snow around her feet was melted. A circle of bare black earth, steaming faintly, no wider than the child herself, as though she had been planted there.

Every instinct Dominic owned told him to close the door. The same instinct had kept him alive through the fall of the covenant, through the purges, through the long quiet years when the Warden's servants had gone up and down the Thornfell asking questions in pleasant voices. *Stay small. Stay silent. Stay stone.* He had built his whole life into a wall, and here was a hole knocked in it the size of a child.

The girl looked up at him. She didn't shiver. That was the detail that undid him—not the melted snow, not the impossible dress, but the stillness of her, the absolute absence of the violent shuddering that a frozen child's body could not refuse. She looked at him the way you look at a house you have been walking toward for a very long time.

Then she opened her mouth, and no sound came out.

She tried again. Her lips moved, shaped something, failed. Her small jaw tightened with frustration, and for the first time she looked frightened—not of him, not of the storm, but of the silence in her own throat, as if her voice were a room she had been locked out of.

Mute. Half-dead with cold and mute, and standing in a blizzard on the doorstep of the one man in the mountains least equipped to turn her away.

"No," Dominic said—to her, to himself, to Sera, to the whole arrangement of the universe. "No. Whatever you are, whatever sent you, I'm not part of this anymore. There's nothing here. Do you understand? There's nothing—"

The girl raised one hand. Small fingers, blue at the tips, trembling now at last—with effort, not cold. She pointed, first at her own mouth, then at the knuckle-bone hanging against his chest, visible in the gap of his collar.

And she spoke.

One word. One word in a cracked, rusted, almost-voice, a sound like a hinge opening after centuries, and the word was:

"*Sévaneth.*"

The world tilted.

Dominic had heard that language exactly once in his life—from bones. From thousand-year-old bones, from the deep archive-carvings of the pre-kingdom dead, the covenant-makers, the root-speakers whose voices came up through the ivory thick and strange and green, like sap. He had spent four years of his apprenticeship learning to shape his mouth around those ancient syllables, guided by a mentor who had laughed at his terrible accent and called him *little mole, digging through time.* He had not heard the tongue spoken by living air since the night the Garden closed. Since the night he helped close it.

*Sévaneth.*

*Open*, it meant. Or *bloom*. Or, in the oldest usages, the ones his mentor had loved best: *the door that opens from the inside.*

The girl swayed. The stillness that had held her upright finally broke, and she pitched forward, and Dominic caught her before he had decided to move—his body acting ten years ahead of his principles, his arms closing around a child made of ice and leaf-patterns and one impossible word.

She weighed almost nothing. She was burning hot, fever-hot, beneath the frost. And through his palms, where her skin touched his, he felt something he had felt only once before in his life, on the worst night of it: a hum. Deep, vegetal, patient. The hum of roots growing in the dark under a forest floor. The hum he had heard through the covenant-stones, the night they died.

"Oh," he said, uselessly. "Oh, no."

*Carver,* Sera said, and her voice was shaking now, actually shaking, three hundred years of composure cracking like river ice. *Carver, do you know what she is?*

"I know what she sounds like," he said, already moving, kicking the door shut behind him with his heel, carrying the girl toward the stove. "I know what she sounds like, and that's bad enough."

*That is not an answer.*

"It's the only one I have."

He laid her on the cot near the fire, wrapped her in every blanket he owned, chafed her hands, forced meltwater and honey between her lips. She swallowed obediently, eyes closed, breathing in long slow pulls like something dormant drinking rain. The frost melted out of her hair and left it black as loam. Under the grime and ice her skin was pale as birchwood, and along her left forearm, where the sleeve of that strange garment had ridden up, he saw a mark: a spiral, fine as a scratch, the same spiral he had carved into Sera Voss's bone without ever knowing why the shape had always felt right in his hand.

He sat back on his heels and stared at her, and the workshop crowded in around him—all those shelves of carved dead, hundreds of small voices sleeping in ivory—and he realized, with a sensation like the floor dropping out of a stairwell, that every one of them had gone silent. Not faded. Silent. Listening.

For the first time in eleven years, the dead in Dominic Nystrom's keeping were holding their breath.

* * *

Toward dawn the fever broke, or changed, or settled into something he decided not to examine too closely. The girl slept. The snow outside had stopped falling entirely, which was wrong in a different way now—the clouds still hung there, full and gray and waiting, a held breath over the whole range. On the doorstep, when he opened the door to check, the circle of bare earth remained, and in the center of it, impossibly, a single green shoot stood an inch tall, two leaves unfolded, glistening.

Dominic crouched over it for a long time. Nothing green survived up here past autumn. Nothing green survived *this*.

He didn't touch it. He went back inside, latched the door, and sat across from the sleeping child with the carving knife across his knees, and let himself think, properly, for the first time since the knock.

She had come down from the high passes. From the direction of the old covenant-road, the one that had sealed itself behind a wall of ice eleven years ago—the road he himself had helped seal, God forgive him, helped seal because she had told him it was mercy, because she had been his mentor and he had trusted her the way sons trust mothers. If the road was open again, if something had come *through*—

And the Warden's people still walked the valleys. He knew that. Every winter there were new stories from Greyford: travelers who came back with gaps in their memories, whole afternoons missing, faces of their own children momentarily unfamiliar. The Warden of Winter kept her peace, and her peace was made of forgetting.

If they learned a child had come down out of the sealed passes speaking the old tongue, wearing the seed-mark, melting snow with her bare feet—

They wouldn't ask questions. They would simply open her up like a drawer and take everything, the way the Warden took everything, and there would be nothing left in the girl's eyes afterward but white quiet.

Dominic looked at the child. She had turned toward the warmth of the stove in her sleep, one hand curled under her cheek, and she looked, in that moment, like any child anywhere—like a thing that deserved porridge and mended stockings and someone to teach her the names of birds. Like a thing that had no business being the most dangerous object in the Thornfell mountains.

"You can't stay," he told her softly. "You understand that. I can't— I don't *do* this anymore. I keep my head down. I carve my bones. I stay out of it. That's how everyone under this roof stays alive."

The girl's eyes opened.

Not gradually—all at once, the way a bird's eyes open, and they were dark and calm and fixed on him with an attention that made the back of his neck prickle. She looked at him for a long moment. Then she lifted her hand again, weakly, and pointed—not at him, not at herself, but at the shelves. At the bones. Hundreds of them, rank upon rank, sleeping in their spirals and anchors and bells.

And from the oldest shelf, the deep shelf, the one where he kept the fragments of pre-kingdom carving that he had never shown another living soul—the shelf he had promised himself he would burn a dozen times and never had—came a sound.

Faint. Green. Patient as roots.

A voice, in the old tongue, saying a single word. And though Dominic had not heard the language spoken aloud in eleven years, he understood it instantly, the way you understand thunder:

"*Kestrel*," the bone said. "*Sévaneth Kestrel.*"

The girl watched him. Waiting, it seemed, to see what he would do with a door that had just opened from the inside.

Dominic Nystrom sat very still, an old man alone with a child and a mountain of whispering dead, and understood that the quiet life he had built—stone by careful stone, silence by careful silence—had just come knocking at his own door, and it was eight years old, and it was hungry, and it knew his name.
