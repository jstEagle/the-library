## 3. What the Old Tongue Remembers

The workshop was not quiet.

That was the first thing Kestrel understood, before she understood anything else—before the fire's warmth finished thawing her fingers, before the man with the sad hands stopped watching her like she might be a wolf in a child's shape. The room was full. Not of things. Of *voices*.

She heard them the way other people heard rain: everywhere at once, layered, impossible to count. A low murmur under the floorboards. A thin bright thread running through the rafters. Something old and slow breathing inside the stone hearth itself, the way a sleeping animal breathes. And threading through all of it, dozens upon dozens of small voices, each one caught inside a piece of carved bone, humming to itself in the dark of the shelves.

Kestrel sat very still on the stool by the fire and listened.

She had been listening her whole life. It was what she did instead of speaking, because when she opened her mouth the wrong things came out—things that made grown men go pale and bolt doors—and so she had learned to keep everything behind her teeth, where it hummed and waited. But here, in this crooked little house above the snowline, the humming outside her matched the humming within, and for the first time she could remember, the noise in her chest felt less like a sickness and more like an echo.

The man—Dominic, though she didn't know his name yet—moved around the workshop pretending to do ordinary things. He stirred the fire. He set down a bowl of barley broth near her hand without meeting her eyes. He was good at pretending. She could tell he had been doing it for years, the way some people were good at whistling or mending nets. But his hands gave him away. Every time he passed the long shelf above his workbench, his fingers brushed one particular carving—a small bone bird, no bigger than her thumb, its wings folded—and every time he touched it, the whole room's murmur shifted, the way water shifts when you drop a stone in.

He couldn't hear what she heard. Not the way she heard it. His gift came through his hands, through the carving, through hours of patient work; hers came through her skin, her ears, the soft place at the top of her head where the cold got in even indoors. To him the bones spoke only when asked. To her they never stopped talking.

And right now, they were talking about *her*.

*Small thing,* said a voice from the shelf nearest the window—a woman's voice, dry as seed husks, coming from a carved comb with three broken teeth. *Small warm thing. Where has it come from? Nothing warm climbs this high anymore.*

*I smell the Garden on her,* answered another voice, deeper, from somewhere below the floor. This one made Kestrel's spine prickle, because it wasn't a dead person's voice at all. It was older. It sounded like roots grinding slowly through rock, like the mountain itself remembering. *Under the snow-smell. Under the fear-smell. Green. Green and waiting.*

*Hush,* said the comb-woman. *The carver will hear.*

*The carver hears only what he bleeds into us,* said the root-voice, unbothered. *But this one hears everything. Look how still she sits. Still as a stone that listens.*

Kestrel wrapped both hands around the bowl of broth and drank, mostly to give herself something to do. The broth was salty and good and tasted faintly of smoke, and the warmth of it spread out through her chest like a second, smaller fire. Above her, snow ticked against the shutters. Somewhere beyond the walls the blizzard went on howling, but in here the voices wove a kind of shelter of their own, and Kestrel found her shoulders lowering for the first time in—she tried to remember how long, and could not, and decided not to try.

Then the bone bird sang.

It happened when Dominic crossed the room again, reaching past the shelf for a jar of lamp oil. His sleeve dragged across the little folded-wing carving, barely a touch, less than a touch—and the bird woke. Not the way the others murmured. It *sang*, one clear rising note like a lark going up through morning air, and the note was in the old language, the language Kestrel carried sealed behind her teeth, the language the men in the low country had called cursed and the woman who had kept her last winter had called *the tongue that will get you killed, girl, bury it deeper*—

*Arise, arise, the root remembers spring.*

Kestrel's breath stopped.

Every carved bone in the workshop turned toward her. Not physically—they were only bone, only objects—but she felt them turn the way you feel eyes on your back. The comb. The beads strung along the wall like a frozen rain of knuckles. The great elk skull over the door with runes worn shallow by a century of thumbs. The drawerfuls, the boxes, the jar of uncarved splinters waiting by the bench. All of them listening. All of them *recognizing*.

*She knows the words,* whispered the comb-woman, and there was wonder in it, and underneath the wonder something that might have been hope, and underneath the hope something frightened. *After all this dark, she knows the words.*

*Say it back,* urged the root-voice, gentle now, coaxing, vast. *Little green one. Say it back and we will tell you everything. Say it back and we will tell you what you are.*

Kestrel set down the bowl. Her hands were shaking. She looked at the bone bird on its shelf, wings folded, patient as a held breath, and she opened her mouth—

And slammed it shut again.

Because she remembered. She would always remember. The village with the frozen well. The way the priest had gone white when she'd hummed a tuneless little song while drawing water, and how he'd said *where did you learn that*, and how the asking had become shouting had become torchlight, and how she had run barefoot up into the Thornfell passes with winter closing behind her like a door. The old tongue was not just a language. It was a summons. Things answered it. Things *woke*. And wherever she spoke it, sooner or later, something came hunting the sound.

Something was already hunting her. She could feel it the way you feel a storm two valleys off—not arrived, but coming, patient, certain. A pressure at the edge of the world. A cold that was not weather.

So instead of answering, Kestrel pressed her lips together and shook her head at the shelf, small and stubborn, the way she'd shaken her head at grown-ups her whole life.

The voices sighed—all together, a sound like wind through a winter field—and settled, disappointed, back into their long murmuring.

* * *

Dominic noticed.

Of course he noticed; he missed nothing in this room, which was his whole life arranged around him like a fortress. He had been watching her sidelong since the broth, and when her gaze fixed on the bone bird and her mouth moved around a word she refused to release, he went very still, the way hunters go still.

"You heard that," he said. Not a question.

Kestrel looked at him. She considered lying, which was difficult without a voice, and decided against it. She nodded once.

His jaw worked. He set down the oil jar with exaggerated care, as if his hands needed a task to keep from doing something else. "What did it say?"

Kestrel hesitated. Then she lifted one finger, pointed at the bird, and sang—wordlessly, breath only, no true syllables—the shape of the line. *A-rise, a-rise.* Her voice made almost no sound at all; it was a habit, making almost no sound. But the bone bird flared anyway, that single lark-note rising to meet her, and around the room every carving shivered on its shelf like leaves in a wind she couldn't feel.

Dominic sat down. Not gracefully. He simply lowered himself onto the bench as if his legs had resigned without notice, and he stared at the bird, and then at her, and then at the bird again.

"That carving is four hundred years old," he said quietly. "I found it in a barrow under the Greyfell scree. In all my years of working bone, it has never—" He stopped. Started again. "Bones answer me because I ask them. I carve the question into them. That's the craft. That's all it ever was." His eyes were bright and terrible with a hope he clearly hated carrying. "I have never heard one answer anyone else."

Kestrel shrugged. It was meant to be modest. It came out exhausted.

"Where did you come from?" he asked. "Who taught you—"

She shook her head before he finished. No one taught her. There was no one. There was the road, and the snow, and the thing behind her, and a word she had been born knowing the way she'd been born knowing how to be afraid.

Dominic studied her for a long moment. The fire cracked. Snow ticked at the shutters. And beneath it all, Kestrel heard the workshop holding its breath—every carved relic leaning in, every dead voice waiting to see what the living man would do with the strange green child the mountain had delivered to his door.

"The blizzard won't break till tomorrow," he said finally, gruff, looking away from her toward the black window. "You'll sleep by the fire. In the morning—" He rubbed his face with both hands. "In the morning I'll decide what to do with you."

Kestrel nodded. It was more than she'd been offered in a long time.

He spread a wool blanket near the hearth, rough and smelling of cedar smoke, and turned back to his workbench with the determined posture of a man refusing to think. Kestrel curled up on the blanket. The stones of the hearth breathed their slow hot breath against her cheek. Above her, the shelves murmured on, softer now, lullaby-soft:

*Sleep, little green one. We will watch. We have nothing but time; it is the one thing the dead have plenty of.*

*Sleep. And if the cold thing comes—*

*—we will make noise enough to wake the carver.*

Kestrel smiled into the blanket, a small crooked smile no one saw, and let the voices carry her down.

* * *

She dreamed of the Garden.

She had dreamed of it before—it was the only dream she'd ever had, really, the same one returning like a tide—but tonight it came closer than usual, close enough to hurt. She stood in a place that was not a place: light falling green-gold through leaves the size of sails, rivers running silver uphill, and everywhere the smell of growing things, thick and sweet as honey left in the sun. Everything there was alive in a way the waking world had forgotten how to be. The grass sang under her feet in the old tongue. The trees leaned down to look at her.

*There you are,* said the Garden, in a voice like ten thousand springs happening at once. *There you are. We thought we had lost you.*

*I'm coming,* Kestrel tried to say. *I'm trying.*

*They are closing the roads,* the Garden said, and its brightness flickered, shadows moving through the leaves though no wind blew. *Can you feel it, seedling? The Warden's hand on the passes. Stone sealing against stone. Root sealing against root. Soon there will be no way down to us, and no way up to you, and the stillness will take everything that remembers.*

*I don't know the way,* Kestrel said. *I don't know anything. I'm small.*

*You are a seed,* said the Garden. *Seeds are the smallest things there are. Seeds are also the whole point.*

The light began to dim at the edges, the dream fraying like cloth. Kestrel reached out—*wait, wait, tell me what I'm supposed to do*—and the Garden's voice came to her from very far away, fading, urgent:

*Find the stones. Four anchors, four sleeps. Wake them in the old words. Before the last snow falls—*

*—before the last snow—*

*—before—*

* * *

Kestrel woke to grey light and silence.

Not the good silence of the workshop-with-its-voices. A wrong silence. The fire had burned down to red eyes in white ash. The shutters had stopped ticking. Even the blizzard, which had howled all night, had stopped—and outside the window the world lay buried in snow so deep and so still it looked painted, every pine bowed under white, every sound smothered.

And the voices in the shelves were afraid.

She felt it before she was fully awake, the murmur pulled tight as wire. The comb-woman whispering, *it's early, it's too early, the frost should not come for weeks yet.* The root-voice below the floor, grave and slow: *the mountain is closing its gates. Feel it? Pass by pass. The high road is gone already. The smuggler stairs are gone. Something is folding the Thornfell shut like a book.*

Kestrel sat up, blanket sliding from her shoulders, breath fogging. Across the room, Dominic stood at the window with his back to her, and even without seeing his face she could read the set of his shoulders, the rigid stillness of his hands at his sides.

On the sill before him, in the space of a single night, the snow had climbed higher than the glass. And where his breath touched the pane, the frost did not melt.

It grew. Tiny feathers of ice spreading outward from each exhale, delicate and deliberate, like something on the other side of the window pressing its fingers against the glass.

*He knows,* whispered the comb-woman. *Look at him. He knows what it means.*

*Then say it plainly, sister,* rumbled the root-voice. *Say what we are all thinking. The Warden's winter has come down the mountains a season early. It has never done that before. Not in all the years of the dark covenant. Which means—*

*—which means it is looking for something,* the comb finished softly. *Or someone.*

Kestrel hugged her knees to her chest and did not make a sound.

Behind her, she heard Dominic draw a long breath, and turn from the window, and begin—quietly, methodically, with the grim efficiency of a man taking down a map he had sworn never to need again—to pull the boards off the wall above his workbench, revealing the faded ink lines of the Thornfell passes drawn in a smuggler's hand.

"We leave tomorrow," he said, to her or to the room or perhaps to the dead. "Before the roads finish closing."

The bones on the shelves rustled, though there was no wind, like a crowd murmuring assent.

Kestrel looked at the map—at the four small circles marked in faded ink, scattered across the mountains like seeds flung from a fist—and felt, deep beneath her breastbone, something green shift in its sleep and begin, very slowly, to wake.
