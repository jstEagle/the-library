## 4. Smuggler's Arithmetic

The map had hung on the back of Dominic's workshop door for eleven years, and in all that time he had never once needed to look at it.

He knew the Thornfells the way a man knows his own hands—by touch, by scar, by the places that ached when the weather turned. He had come over them half-dead with grief and a satchel of carving knives, following smugglers' roads that did not appear on any king's survey because they were paid for never to appear. He had learned which passes held snow until midsummer and which ones lied about it. He had memorized the way the light fell through the notch at Wolf's Jaw at dawn, so a traveler could cross the scree before it warmed and shifted. The mountains had been his wall against the world, and he had studied every stone of it.

Now he stood with a stub of charcoal in his hand and the map unrolled across his workbench, pinning down its curling corners with a whetstone, an inkpot, and Kestrel's abandoned cup of barley tea, gone cold.

She was asleep in the loft. He could hear her breathing through the floorboards—small, even, stubbornly alive. She had eaten three bowls of pottage without once looking up from her food, the way animals eat when they have learned that meals can be taken away. Then she had curled up in the corner of the loft farthest from the window, back to the wall, one hand closed around the river-stone she carried, and slept like something that expected to be woken by running feet.

Dominic told himself he would sleep too, after he checked the roads.

That was the lie he'd been telling himself since dusk: *just check the roads, just make sure nothing followed her up the switchbacks, then rest.* But checking the roads meant the map, and the map meant arithmetic, and arithmetic was the one discipline he had never been able to set down. A carver's trade was arithmetic of a kind—grain and stress and how much bone you could take away before the thing lost its strength. Smuggling was the same sums run in reverse: how much weight a mule could carry, how many hours of dark a pass allowed, how much silence a man could buy and for how long.

He began, as always, with the southern routes, because the south was where trouble came from if it came at all.

* * *

The Greyford road first. He drew it from memory rather than trusting the faded ink: south along the Vell, past the drowned quarry, down to the river town itself, where the water slowed wide and grey between shingle banks. In the old days—the covenant days, though no one had called them that aloud—you could buy passage out of Greyford on any of nine boats, and only two of the boatmen would cheat you. He marked the town with a small circle and felt the old familiar ache of names. Marta had loved Greyford. Marta had loved everything, that was the trouble with her; she had loved the world with both hands and no caution, and the world had—

He set the charcoal down. Pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes until the dark behind them bloomed red.

Not tonight. Tonight there was a child asleep above him who spoke a language older than kingdoms, and sentiment was a luxury priced beyond him.

He picked the charcoal back up and worked.

Greyford: open. Probably. River towns kept their own counsel and their own gates, and the Warden's reach, whatever it now was, would show itself slowest where the water moved fastest. He put a small mark beside the circle—a question, not a certainty. Honesty on a map was worth more than comfort.

Then the high passes. Here his charcoal slowed.

He had crossed the Sorrow Notch in autumn four years ago and found it exactly as it had always been: ugly, wind-scoured, survivable. Two years ago he had gone down as far as the lower switchbacks for salt and lamp oil, and the pass had still been a pass. But this morning, when he had stepped out to split kindling in the blue hour before dawn, he had noticed something he had filed away without examining, the way you file away a wrong note in a familiar song.

The wind had been blowing *out* of the mountains.

All of them. Every gap, every col, every notch along the southern skyline—the wind came down out of the high country in long, steady exhalations, like breath let slowly through teeth. That was backwards. Mountain winds ran uphill by day and downhill by night, breathing with the sun. This had been neither. It had been constant, and cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature—a dry cold, a *held* cold, the cold of air that has been shut in a room with something dead.

He had thought, at the time: early winter. The thought had been a coin he accepted without counting, and he was ashamed of himself for it. Eleven years hiding in these mountains had made him rich in excuses and poor in courage, and he knew it, and knowing it changed nothing.

He marked the Sorrow Notch with a line through it. Then, because honesty is habit, he wrote beside it: *why?*

* * *

By the second hour of the night the workshop had grown cold enough that his breath showed, and he lit the small stove and fed it one of the twisted root-billets the mountain pines shed after storms. The fire caught with a sound like a sigh. Above him, the child's breathing went on, even and small.

He worked northward next, and this was where the arithmetic stopped being comfortable.

The northern routes were the smugglers' roads proper—the deep ways, cut and maintained across centuries by people whose whole livelihood depended on the mountains staying porous. The Hollow Stair, switchbacking down the inside of a collapsed gorge. The Root-Road, which was less a road than a rumor the trees agreed to honor, running under the forest canopy from watershed to watershed. The Weepers' Gate, a slot canyon so narrow two men could not walk abreast, named for the springs that bled down its walls. None of these appeared on charts. All of them had moved goods and fugitives since before the kingdom had a name, because the covenant had made the border between realms a living thing, and living things have doors.

And every one of them, as far as he could reckon, was shut.

He could not prove it from a workbench. What he had was worse than proof: it was absence. No smoke from the shepherd steads in the northern valleys, where there should have been smoke—three households, hardy and stubborn, who traded with him in wool and cheese and asked no questions. No cart-tracks on the lower road when he'd walked to the spring two days past, though the season was right for the last wool runs. No birds. That was the detail that sat in him like a swallowed stone: no birds coming over the northern ridgeline. The Thornfells in autumn were a river of birds, thrushes and finches and the big black kites riding the thermals, all funneling south ahead of the snow. His feeders had been empty for six days.

He had told himself: late migration. Cold snap. Natural.

He looked at the word *natural* written nowhere on the map, and understood that he had been lying to himself in careful increments for a week, the way a man lies to himself about a pain in his chest.

Dominic set down the charcoal and went to the window.

* * *

The snow had begun again—not falling so much as *arriving*, sheet after sheet of it sliding down out of the north with a purposefulness that snow should not have. It filled the yard without drifting, level as poured wax. And beyond the fence, where the land dropped toward the valley, the air itself had changed texture. He had lived in cold country his whole life and he knew every variety of winter the sky could brew: the dry squeak of hard frost, the wet gray wool of sea-level storms, the glittering treachery of freezing rain. This was none of them. This was stillness given weight. The flakes fell straight, unhurried, evenly spaced, like ash from a fire that had burned something very large and very quiet.

And the wind was blowing out of the mountains again. Steady. Downward. Patient.

An exhalation. As if something vast had drawn breath weeks ago, somewhere beyond the northern peaks, and was letting it out slowly—and when the breath ended, Dominic suspected, the holding would begin.

He thought of the carved bones on their shelves, and of how, lately, the voices in them had grown fainter. He had blamed the cold. Bone carried voice poorly in deep frost; that was carver's lore, ordinary physics. But standing at the window watching the impossible snow, he let himself finish the thought he had been refusing: the dead in his carvings were not going quiet because the air was cold. They were going quiet because they were being *taken*. Something was eating memory out of the world, and memory was the only food the dead had.

Kestrel had arrived speaking the pre-kingdom tongue. Kestrel had come *down* out of the north, out of the direction the wind blew from, alone, mute, hunted-looking, carrying a river-stone she gripped like a talisman. He had let himself believe she was a stray. Strays were manageable. Strays could be fed and pointed elsewhere.

But strays did not make thousand-year-old bones sing.

Behind him, in the loft, the breathing stopped.

Dominic was moving before his mind finished the thought, taking the stairs in two strides—but the breathing resumed, a hitch, a dream-shift, steady again. He stood at the foot of the ladder with his heart slamming and made himself breathe slower. Fool. Old fool. You cannot guard a child from a direction the wind comes from by standing under her bed.

He went back to the bench. Back to the map. Because the map was the one place where fear became numbers, and numbers could be argued with.

* * *

Here is what the arithmetic said:

Route one, the southern low road through Greyford: possibly open, certainly watched, and it delivered whoever took it directly into the river towns where questions were already, he guessed, being asked. If the Warden's stillness was spreading—and the sealed passes said it was—then Greyford would feel it soon, and anyone traveling with a child who spoke the Garden's tongue would be a lantern in a dark field.

Route two, the high passes east: sealed by snow that was falling wrong, in wind that breathed backward, toward a horizon he did not want to name.

Route three, the northern deep ways: sealed by something that was not snow, unless snow had learned to eat birds.

Which left, if you added the columns honestly, a sum he did not like: there was no way *out* of the Thornfells anymore. The mountains were closing like a fist around whatever was inside them. And if the mountains were closing, then someone wanted what was inside them kept in—which meant the thing hunting Kestrel was not behind them. It was around them. It had been around them for days, tightening by degrees, patient as frost.

He looked at the map for a long time. The stove ticked. The snow arrived.

And then he saw it—or rather, he finally admitted he had seen it all along, because it was drawn on the map in his own younger handwriting, faded brown, in the margin beside the Root-Road: a note he'd made eleven years ago and never crossed out. *The old ways answer the old tongue.*

Smuggler's lore. Half superstition, all true: the deep roads predated the kingdom, predated the human realm's side of the covenant entirely. They had been cut in the time when the border was open and trafficked by both kinds of folk, and the roads themselves remembered it. Doors in rock that opened for words no living mouth used anymore. Bridges of root that bore weight only for those who greeted them properly. He had used such courtesies himself, once, guided by a mentor who had taught him that the world beneath the roots was not a metaphor but a neighbor—

His mentor. Her face rose, sharp as a knife-cut, and he put it down again. There would be time for that ledger later, or there would not.

The point, the terrible, inconvenient point, was this: the deep roads might be the only ways left open, because they answered not to weather or wardens but to the old tongue. And the only creature for a hundred leagues who spoke the old tongue was asleep in his loft, gripping a river-stone, three bowls of pottage heavier than she had been yesterday.

So the arithmetic came to this, and he wrote it plainly in the corner of the map, because writing things plainly was how he kept himself honest:

*She cannot stay. Everything that hunts her will find her here, and I am one old man with good knives and bad knees, and my invisibility has bought me eleven years and has just now come due.*

*She cannot be sent away. There is nowhere to send her. Every road out is closing, and the one set of roads that may yet stand open will open for her voice and no other.*

*Therefore: she goes forward, deeper, and I go with her, because sending a mute child alone down the Root-Road is not a plan, it is a eulogy.*

He stared at the words. Three lines of charcoal, and each one a door closing behind him—the quiet years, the safe years, the long self-burial he had called prudence. He had spent eleven years making himself a man the world could overlook, and he had been good at it, and now the bill had arrived with a child attached.

There was a fourth line he did not write, because his hand would not shape it: *and perhaps this is why she came to your door, old man. Perhaps the mountain sent her to the one soul in the Thornfells who owed the Garden a debt he had never once tried to pay.*

Below him—or above him, in the loft—the child stirred. He heard the ladder creak, and then small bare feet on the boards, and then she was there at the edge of the lamplight, wrapped in his spare blanket like a fledgling in a nest too big for it, looking at him with those dark, unblinking eyes.

She did not speak. Of course she did not speak. But her gaze went past him, to the map spread on the bench, to the charcoal lines and the crossed-out passes and the three plain sentences in the corner, and something in her face sharpened—the look of someone reading, though he would have sworn she could not read his letters.

Then she lifted her hand, the one that wasn't holding the stone, and pointed—not at the map, not at the door, but *north*. At the wall, and through the wall, at the sealed and silent deep ways, at whatever waited at the end of the backward wind.

And when Dominic shook his head slowly, not in refusal but in dread, she opened her mouth and gave him a single word in the old tongue—soft, clear, unmistakable in its meaning even to a man who had only ever heard it from the bones of the dead.

It was the word for *hurry*.
