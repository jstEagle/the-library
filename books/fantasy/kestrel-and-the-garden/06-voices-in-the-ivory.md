## 6. Voices in the Ivory

The child slept in my bed that night, and I sat at my workbench with a lamp turned low and a piece of rowan wood across my knees, whittling nothing in particular, because my hands needed to be busy or they would start shaking.

She had spoken one word before sleep took her. *Senneth.* I had turned it over all evening like a stone found on a beach, turning it to see what lived underneath. It was not a word from any tongue spoken in Thornfell, nor in the lowlands, nor in any of the smuggler cant I'd picked up in twenty years of keeping to the margins. But I knew it. I knew it the way you know a face in a dream — not from waking life but from somewhere older, somewhere the mind keeps locked and pays rent on anyway.

I had heard it from the bones.

I set down the knife. The workshop was quiet except for the wind working at the shutters and the small, wet sound of the child breathing. On the shelves around me, three hundred carvings sat in their places: birds and bells and hands and doors, each one cut from bone or antler, each one holding what was left of someone who had once been a person with a name and a voice and a last thing they wanted said.

Most nights I did not listen. That was the discipline of it, the whole architecture of my exile — I had built myself a house full of voices and then taught myself not to hear them, the way a man builds a wall against a river and then lives his whole life pretending not to hear the water.

But tonight the water was rising.

It started, as it always started, with the oldest carving. The others were quiet until I touched them; the old one never waited for permission. It hung on its iron hook above the bench where I could see it from anywhere in the room, and it was ugly, honestly — a slab of mammoth ivory no bigger than my palm, carved with a figure I had never been able to identify. Not quite a tree. Not quite a woman. Something that stood with its arms open and its roots showing, as if it could not decide whether it was growing upward or downward. I had bought it thirty years ago from a peddler who swore he'd pulled it from a barrow in the high passes, and the first time I held it, it spoke to me without my asking, which should have been my first warning about everything that came after.

The voice that came out of it now was thin as wire drawn through cold tallow.

*Dominic.*

Not a name it had ever used before. The dead who lived in my carvings knew me as *carver*, mostly, or *the man with the knife*, or sometimes — the ones who had died angry — *thief*. This voice knew my name, and knowing my name, it used it the way you'd use a key.

"I'm listening," I said, and hated how easily the words came, how much of me had been waiting years for exactly this.

*The ice is wrong,* the voice said. *You felt it. Say you felt it.*

The winter this year had come early and come crooked. Frost on the south-facing stones while the north stayed soft. Snow that fell upward in gusts. And yesterday, before the blizzard brought the child to my door, I had watched the stream below the workshop freeze in a single breath — not spreading from the edges inward the way water freezes, but all at once, top to bottom, like a thought being decided. I had told myself it was weather. Weather is what men tell themselves when the alternative is worse.

"I felt it," I said.

*Then listen properly. Put your hands on us. All of us.*

I looked at the shelves. Three hundred small deaths, arranged by year of acquisition, because organizing them any other way would have meant organizing them by grief, and I had not had the courage for that in a long time.

"You know I don't—"

*Dominic.* The voice frayed at the edges, and underneath the fraying I heard something I had never heard from the old carving before. Fear. *We do not have the time for your penances. She is awake. The seed is in your house, and the Warden's hounds are already on the passes, and if you will not put your hands on us we will scream, and you know what happens when we scream.*

I knew. When the carvings screamed, every dead thing within a mile heard them, and answered, and the mountains for a night became the kind of place where the living do not sleep. I had heard it happen twice. Once before the covenant broke, and once after, and both times I had sat up till dawn with my hands over my ears like a child, listening to the hills talk in a language made entirely of loss.

So I got up, and I took the carvings down off the shelves, and I laid them out on the bench in rows, and I put my hands flat among them the way a man lays hands on a sickbed.

The voices came up through my palms like water through roots.

---

They did not speak all at once, though I felt them all at once — a pressure, a crowd pressing at a door. They took turns. There was an etiquette among the dead that the living never managed, and it shamed me a little, every time, how courteous they were with the little I gave them.

A woman spoke first. I knew her carving — a small bone thrush, cut for a girl in Greyford who had drowned in the quarry flood eleven years back. The mother had paid me in salt fish and would not look at the finished bird. *The chapel froze,* the thrush-voice said. *In Greyford. Two nights past. Father Aldous went to ring the dawn bell and the rope snapped like it had been dipped in glass, and the frost came through the windows inward, and the icons wept ice instead of oil. He stood in the middle of it and forgot his own mother's face. Stood there moving his lips around a shape that wasn't anything anymore.*

"Greyford is four days' walk," I said. "Below the snowline."

*The frost does not walk,* said another voice — an old man in a boar's tusk, a smuggler I had known alive, whose last words had been a joke about the taxman. *The frost remembers where things are. It goes to them. Like a man going home.*

And a third voice, younger, rougher: *The passes are closing. We've watched them from the shelf, carver, we see out, you know we see out. The high road under Wolfjaw sealed itself Tuesday night. Ice grew across the cut like a scar. And the low ford at Greyford — the water stands still now. Not frozen. Still. The fish hang in it like things in amber.*

I stood there with my hands among the dead and let the picture assemble itself, because they were giving it to me in pieces, the way witnesses always do, and the pieces only made sense if you refused to flinch from the shape they made.

Something was drawing borders. Something was closing the mountain in — not with armies, not with walls, but with stillness. Roads sealing. Rivers standing. Chapels freezing from the inside out, and men forgetting their own mothers in the space of a bell-rope's snap.

"The Warden," I said. I hadn't spoken that name aloud in six years. It tasted like a tooth gone bad. "The Warden of Winter."

The silence that followed was the loudest thing in the room.

Then the old carving spoke again, alone, and the other voices drew back from it the way a congregation draws back from a bell about to strike.

*You say her name like a curse,* it said. *But you were her student. You loved her. And she loved you, in whatever way a thing like her can love.* A pause, long enough for the wind to fill. *That is why we chose you, Dominic. Not because you can hear us. Because she trusts the shape of your grief. She thinks she knows what you will do with it.*

"What am I going to do with it?"

*Listen. And remember. Those are the only two things left to you, and they are enough, if you are brave enough to be a map instead of a man.*

---

The old carving talked for most of the night, and the others filled in around it, and by the time the blizzard lightened toward grey morning I had learned more than I wanted to know.

There had been four anchors. Four covenant-stones, set into the bones of the Thornfell when the world was younger than kingdoms, when the human realm and the Garden beneath the roots had needed a way to stay open to each other without devouring each other. The stones held the boundary the way tent-pegs hold cloth — not shutting it out, but holding the shape of the opening. Memory flowed down into the Garden; dream and growth flowed up into the world. As long as the four stones sang to one another, both realms breathed.

I had known this the way everyone of the old covenant knew it — as lullaby, as liturgy, as the sort of truth you never examine because examining it might wake it. What I had not known, what the dead told me now in voices worn smooth by centuries of waiting, was where the stones had gone when the covenant broke.

They hadn't broken. That was the thing. Six years ago, when the sky over the Garden went dark and the root-roads closed and every door between the realms slammed like a lid, I had assumed — everyone had assumed — that the stones had shattered, that the catastrophe had been sudden and total. But the dead remembered differently. The stones had not broken. They had been *scattered* — sunk, buried, hidden, carried away by hands that meant either to save them or to make sure no one else could gather them again. Four anchors, pulled loose and strewn across the Thornfell like the pieces of a broken compass, each one still singing, faintly, to the others, each one still holding open a pinprick of the old passage whether anyone used it or not.

*The Drowned Bell,* the old carving said, *in the flooded quarry below Greyford. The Choir of Roots, deep in the forest where the rooted dead keep their court. The Teeth of the World, in the wind-canyon past the shepherd moors. And the fourth—*

It stopped.

The lamp flame leaned. Outside, the wind changed key — dropped from howl to something lower, steadier, almost patient.

*And the fourth,* I prompted.

*I cannot say it,* the old carving said, and for the first time since I had owned it, the voice sounded old. Truly old. Tired the way stone is tired. *Not because I do not know. Because saying it here, in this room, with her frost already tasting the air outside your shutters — saying it would be like striking a match in a room full of lamp-oil. She listens through stillness, carver. Every frozen thing is her ear. And there is a frozen thing closer to you than you think.*

I looked at the door of my bedroom, behind which the child slept.

"No," I said.

*Not her,* the carving said. *She is the opposite of frozen. She is the reason the frost is hurrying.* Another pause. *Look at your own hands, Dominic.*

I looked. In the lamplight, in the cold, my knuckles had gone white — ordinary white, winter white, the white of any man's hands in a mountain January. But as I watched, the whiteness crept a knuckle's width further up my finger, and it did not creep the way cold creeps. It crept the way ink spreads through wet paper. Deliberate. Reading me.

I flexed my hand hard, and the white retreated, sulking.

*Six years,* the old carving said softly, *you have kept yourself still. Still as a held breath. Stillness is her element, Dominic. You have been marinating in it. She has been able to reach into this valley for years — but there was nothing here worth taking. Now there is a seed sleeping in your bed, and four stones singing in her reach, and one man who knows the old roads and the old tongue and the shape of her heart. You have become worth taking.*

"So I run," I said. "Take the child and run south, out of the mountains, down to the lowlands—"

*And leave the stones in her grasp? She has three of four locations already — the frost walks, remember, the frost remembers where things are. If she takes the fourth and stills all four anchors, the boundary doesn't just close. It calcifies. Memory stops flowing into the Garden, and the Garden dies, and when the Garden dies, the dead stop going anywhere. They pool. They freeze. Everyone you ever carved, everyone you ever loved, held forever in a stillness with no spring on the other side of it.* The voice dropped to almost nothing. *Is that not what you have been doing to yourself these six years? Building your own small winter? Do you understand yet why we chose you? You are the only man in these mountains who already knows what she is offering, because you have been selling it to yourself by the hour.*

I sat down on the stool. My legs had decided the matter without consulting me.

Behind me, small feet on floorboards. I turned, and the child stood in the bedroom doorway wrapped in my spare blanket, hair wild, eyes enormous and dark and utterly awake. She looked at the carvings laid out in their rows on the bench — three hundred small faces, small birds, small doors — and something moved across her expression that I had no word for. Recognition, maybe. Or homesickness for a home that had never existed above ground.

She crossed the room without a sound. She looked at the old carving on the bench, and she reached out one finger, and before I could stop her she touched it.

The workshop filled with a sound like a struck bell heard through deep water. Every carving on the bench rang at once — not screaming, not speaking, *singing*, a chord of dead voices braided into one note that was older than any of them, and the child's eyes went wide and bright, and her mouth opened, and she said — clearly, deliberately, in the language of thousand-year-old bones —

*Senneth. Senneth, senneth, aru-vael.*

Four stones. Gather them. Open the door.

Then the chord faded, the carvings went silent, and the child swayed on her feet as if the singing had spent something out of her. I caught her shoulder. She looked up at me, and in her face was a question so naked it hurt to look at — the question of someone who has been carrying a message alone in the dark for longer than anyone should carry anything, asking at last: *did it arrive?*

"It arrived," I said hoarsely.

Outside, the wind stopped altogether. The silence that followed was total, and attentive, and I understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the stillness was listening too — and that from this moment on, everything we said aloud, we said to two audiences.

I began packing before the sun came up. Rope, salt, dried meat, the good knife and the backup knife, and — after a moment's argument with myself that lasted three seconds — the old carving, wrapped in wool and tucked against my ribs, where its voice could reach me and mine could reach it.

Grief makes a poor companion and an excellent map. I had spent six years using mine as a wall. It seemed I was going to spend the rest of it using it as a road — four stones, four graves of a sort, scattered across the mountains I had hidden in, each one a place I would have to go and a price I would probably have to pay.

The child watched me pack with her head tilted. When I shrugged into my coat, she was already at the door, blanket abandoned, hand on the latch, waiting with the patience of someone who has walked a very long way and is entirely prepared to walk further.

"Hold on," I told her. "Eat first. The dead don't march anyone anywhere on an empty stomach."

She considered this, decided it was wisdom, and sat down to bread and honey with the grave solemnity of a general reviewing troops.

I looked once around the workshop — three hundred empty hooks, six years of silence about to be traded for a road I'd sworn never to walk — and blew out the lamp.

By the time the sun cleared the ridge, we were on the smuggler's path heading west, toward Greyford, toward the drowned bell, and behind us, far up the pass, the snow had begun to fall again — straight down, unhurried, in perfect, terrible order, like something descending on purpose.
