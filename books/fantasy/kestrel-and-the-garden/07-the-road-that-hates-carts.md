## 7. The Road That Hates Carts

The smuggler's road began where the king's road ended, which was to say: at a deadfall of pine so deliberate it might have been architecture. Dominic stopped at its edge and let his pack slide from his shoulder, and for a long moment he simply looked at the gap beneath the fallen trees—barely wider than a coffin, black as a throat.

"You'll have to crawl," he said. Then, remembering, he said it again with his hands, pointing at the gap and then at himself, ducking his head.

Kestrel watched him with the flat patience of a heron watching water. She did not seem afraid of the dark. She did not seem afraid of anything, which was either courage or the particular fearlessness of someone who had already lost whatever there was to lose. She went down into the gap without hesitation, her small body folding like a wren's wing, and Dominic heard—not heard, felt—the old road take her weight.

It had been eleven years since he'd crawled under the deadfall. The passage smelled of resin and cold stone and, faintly, of something sweeter underneath, something that had no business surviving this far north after the covenant broke. He came out the other side scraping bark from his palms and found Kestrel standing very still in the hidden hollow beyond, her head tilted, listening.

"What is it?" he asked, and then made the gesture anyway, because he was learning that words were only half the conversation now.

She pointed down. At first he saw nothing but packed earth and needle-fall. Then he saw what she saw: a thread of green running through the dirt, thin as wire, bright as new spring. It hadn't been there when he'd built this hiding place. It hadn't been anywhere on Thornfell in eleven years.

*Roots,* said the voice in his pocket, faint and dry as paper turning. He carried three carved bones with him always—a knuckle of lamb for his grandmother, a sliver of elk antler for a smuggler named Brannoc who had died laughing, and the oldest piece, a finger-bone worn smooth, whose owner had never given him a name. *Roots, carver. Under the snow. Under everything.*

Dominic crouched and did not touch the green thread. Some things you touched and they woke; some things you touched and they remembered you were there. "That's not possible," he said quietly.

Kestrel reached out and laid one finger on it before he could stop her.

The thread brightened. Not flared—brightened, the way an ember answers breath, and along the hollow's walls, where roots had once grown through the mountain's skin and been cut or starved or frozen, something stirred. A tendril no thicker than a hair lifted out of the stone itself, quivered, and lay back down as if embarrassed to be seen.

Kestrel looked up at him. Her eyes were the grey-green of thaw-water, and in them was a question she had no way to ask except by pointing—at herself, at the root, at him—and then spreading her small hands open, empty.

*I don't know either,* he told her, and meant it more than he wanted to.

They walked. The smuggler's road was not a road so much as a sequence of arguments the mountain had lost—switchbacks cut by men who'd needed to move brandy and fugitives and worse over passes no cart could climb, marked by cairns that only looked like cairns. Dominic knew them the way he knew the lines of his own hands. Third cairn past the split birch, turn uphill. Where the scree field began, walk the shadow line, never the sun line; the sun line slid. At the notch below Raven's Tooth there was a shelf wide enough for two men to sleep back to back, and above it, if you knew where to look, a chimney of stone that went up easier than it looked and came out behind a waterfall that froze in winter into a door of blue glass.

He had planned to teach her all of it slowly, in gestures, over days.

The mountain taught her faster.

* * *

It happened at the rope bridge over Gallow's Cut—or where the rope bridge used to be. Eleven winters had rotted it; Dominic had checked two years ago and found the planks gone to punk and the cables frayed to whiskers, and had turned back rather than trust his weight to memory. Now they stood at the edge of the gorge, sixty feet of nothing between them and the far side, wind moaning up out of the dark like a choir that had forgotten its words.

"We go around," Dominic signed. *Three days around.* He pointed east along the rim, then held up three fingers.

Kestrel was already walking to the edge. She crouched there, balanced on her heels, and put both palms flat against the frost-hard ground, and Dominic felt it happen before he saw it—a pressure change, the way air moves before a door opens in a far room.

The roots came up out of the cliff face like slow green lightning.

They grew the way honey pours, unhurried and inevitable, thick as a man's wrist, weaving themselves out of bare granite in a lattice that arched across the gorge. Buds ran along them, tight and closed. Moss unfurled along the weave in soft grey-green ropes. In the space of forty heartbeats there was a bridge where no bridge had been, rooted into living rock on both sides, and it did not creak or sway. It hummed. A low note, almost below hearing, the note a tuning fork makes when you press it to bone.

Kestrel stood, swaying slightly, pale under her wind-chap. Whatever she'd spent, it had cost her. But she looked at the bridge the way other children looked at festival fires, and she looked back at Dominic, and held out her hand.

Not asking permission. Offering.

The voices in his pocket had gone silent, which was its own kind of testimony. The dead, who commented on everything, had nothing to say about a bridge growing out of a mountain. They were listening too.

Dominic took her hand. Her fingers were freezing and fierce, gripping his like she'd decided something about him and wasn't going to renegotiate. They crossed together, boots on living wood that gave slightly, warmly, like the flank of a sleeping animal, and halfway across Dominic made the mistake of looking down into the gorge and saw, far below in the blue gloom, the ghost of the old bridge still hanging—rotted cables, broken planks—and understood that the mountain had not built something new. It had *remembered*. It had pulled the bridge out of its own memory of being whole, and set it down again because a child had asked.

On the far side, Kestrel released his hand and immediately pretended she hadn't taken it, busying herself with her bootlaces, and Dominic found that his chest hurt in a way that had nothing to do with the cold.

*Careful,* murmured the elk-antler sliver, Brannoc's dry humor still alive in it after all these years. *That's how it starts, carver. First they hold your hand. Next thing you know you're carrying their pack and lying to yourself about why you smile.*

"Shut up," Dominic said fondly, and Kestrel glanced up sharply—she always caught it when he spoke to the bones—and he shook his head. Later. He would explain later. There was a whole vocabulary of the dead he'd have to teach her, and the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome: *there will be time.* He had spent eleven years assuming there would be no time for anything, and here was this child dismantling that assumption plank by plank, root by root.

They made camp that night in the lee of the split birch, and Kestrel slept with her back against the trunk, and in the morning there was a ring of new grass around her, green as June, in the middle of October.

* * *

The second door opened on the third day, and this one had been waiting for them.

Dominic knew the place. Everyone who'd run the smuggler's roads knew it: the Hollow Stair, a defile where the trail simply stopped, blocked by a wall of stone polished smooth as ice, thirty feet high, unclimbable. In the old days—before, in the covenant days—the wall had had a way through. The old runners spoke of it half-drunk and half-afraid: *you don't climb the Hollow Stair, you knock on it, and if the mountain likes your business it opens.* After the covenant broke, the wall had become just a wall. Dominic had come this way six years ago and beaten his fists against it until his knuckles bled, and it had given him nothing, and he had added three days to his route ever since, and hated it, and told himself the detour didn't matter.

Kestrel walked up to the wall and studied it the way she studied everything—head tilted, utterly present—and then she reached up and knocked. Twice. Small fist, small sound.

Nothing happened.

She knocked again, and this time she made a sound. It was barely a sound at all, a single syllable breathed more than spoken, in the language Dominic had heard exactly once before—from a thousand-year-old finger-bone, from a dead woman describing the Garden's gates. The word landed on the stone and sank into it like rain into drought.

The wall shivered.

Dust sifted down. And then, with a grinding sigh like a giant turning over in sleep, the stone drew apart—not cracking, not falling, but *parting*, the way curtains part, sliding into itself along seams that had been invisible for eleven years, revealing a corridor of raw rock lit by veins of the same green light that ran under the roots. Warm air breathed out of it, smelling of loam and growing things, impossibly sweet at this altitude, in this season.

Kestrel turned to look at him, and her expression was the first fully legible thing she'd ever shown him: *I didn't know I could do that either.*

"Eleven years," Dominic said. His voice came out rough. He cleared his throat. "Eleven years I've gone around." He gestured at the wall, at the closed years, at all of it, and didn't try to translate the gesture, because some things couldn't be translated.

She took his sleeve—two fingers, careful, the way you'd steady a bird—and tugged him gently toward the opening. *Come on, then, old man,* the tug said. *The mountain's waiting.*

Inside, the corridor was not entirely stone. Roots laced the ceiling in arches like the ribs of a cathedral built by patient hands, and here and there they had budded, tiny white flowers opening in the green light, and when Kestrel passed beneath them the flowers turned to follow her, slow as sunflowers tracking the sun. Dominic walked behind her and watched the mountain watch her, and thought about the word she'd spoken, and thought about the Warden of Winter, whom he had not permitted himself to think about in eleven years, and whose attention this flowering would draw like blood draws sharks.

*She'll feel it,* said the nameless finger-bone, and its voice was grave. *Whatever sleeps in the high fortress, it will feel every door this child opens. You are lighting candles in a house with a hungry owner, carver.*

"I know," Dominic said aloud, and Kestrel glanced back, and he shook his head: *later.* He was accumulating a great many laters. He was beginning to suspect that was what having a family was—just laters, all the way down, promises you intended to keep.

* * *

They camped that night inside the corridor itself, in a widening where the roots formed a natural shelter, and for the first time Dominic cooked a meal meant for two. He'd bought enough food in the valley town for one mouth plus margin, the arithmetic of a man who expected to eat alone until he died, and now he divided the dried venison and the hard bread and the last of the honey into portions, and watched Kestrel eat with the controlled ferocity of someone who had known real hunger, and pushed half of his own portion onto her side of the fire without comment.

She pushed it back.

He pushed it again, and made the gesture he'd begun using for *enough arguments*: palm flat, pressing down.

She considered him across the fire. Then she tore the venison in half and ate her share, and afterward, instead of curling up on her side of the flames as she usually did—always facing the entrance, always ready to run—she moved around the fire and settled against his side, shoulder to his arm, the way a foal leans on its mother, and was asleep within minutes.

Dominic sat very still.

The fire ticked. Somewhere above, wind moved through stone. Against his arm, the child breathed in the deep even rhythm of absolute trust, and Dominic stared into the flames and felt eleven years of armor develop its first crack, right down the middle, clean as ice splitting on a spring pond.

*You're doing it again,* Brannoc's voice said sleepily from his pocket, amused and kind. *Lying to yourself about why you're smiling.*

"I'm not smiling."

*You are. I can hear it. Dead men hear everything, we've got nothing else to do.*

He should have laughed. Instead he found his eyes burning, and he let them, because there was no one awake to see, and because grief and gratitude had always lived next door to each other in him, sharing a wall, and tonight the wall was thin.

He thought about the drowned bell at Greyford, three days south along the smuggler's roads—the first covenant-stone, if the oldest carving had spoken true. He thought about the chapel in Greyford with its windows already frosted in midsummer patterns last year, frost that wouldn't melt, frost that whispered. He thought about the Warden, who had taught him to carve, who had taught him to listen, who had smiled at him across a workbench while feeding him the lie that broke the world.

And he thought: *she will feel the doors opening. Every one.*

High above the corridor, far up in the still air of the pass, something answered the thought. Not a sound. A subtraction—the firelight guttering though there was no draft, the green veins in the rock dimming for the space of three heartbeats, as if the whole mountain had blinked. Kestrel stirred against his arm and made a small sound in her sleep, and the roots overhead curled inward, protective, and the light returned.

But in the ashes of the fire, when Dominic looked down, the coals had arranged themselves—no, they had *been arranged*, he was almost sure, though no hand had touched them—into a pattern he recognized from the oldest carving, from the warning the dead had given him:

A circle of four stones. One of them, cracked and dark.

And beside it, pressed sharp and deliberate into the ash, a single print. Small. Bare. Child-sized.

Going south, toward Greyford.

Going ahead of them.
