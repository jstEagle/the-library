## 8. The Toll at Greyford

The river told us before the town did.

Greyford's water ran slow and glassy where it should have run loud and brown with snowmelt, and where it passed beneath the first bridge it had frozen in mid-curl, a sculpture of its own motion, white foam turned to lace. Kestrel stopped on the bank and stared at it. Her breath came out in small silver ghosts, and she did not shiver, which worried me more than the ice did. A child should shiver.

"That's wrong," I said, mostly to myself. "The Ford runs free even in deepwinter. The smugglers used to time their crossings by it—*when the river forgets to speak, pay the ferryman double.*" Old arithmetic. Old roads. I hadn't spoken that rhyme aloud in nine years, and hearing my own voice say it made the past feel less like a locked room and more like a debt collector standing patiently behind me.

Kestrel touched my sleeve. She pointed at the ice, then at her own chest, then pressed her palm flat over her heart with an expression of such grave concentration that I almost laughed. Almost. Instead I crouched beside her and looked properly at what she was showing me: beneath the frozen surface, where the current should have been visible as a blur of movement, there was nothing. Not stillness—*absence*. The water under the ice wasn't flowing or stopped. It simply wasn't *there*, the way a word is not there after you've forgotten you ever knew it.

I'd seen this once before, at the breaking of the covenant, when whole valleys went quiet like snuffed candles. I had hoped never to see it again. Hopes are cheap in the Thornfell. They freeze like everything else.

"Come on," I said, and took her hand. "We keep our hoods up, we keep our mouths shut, and we find Maren Cobb before anyone finds us."

Greyford had been a smuggler's town when I was young enough to think smuggling was a trade rather than a survival. It sat where the river bent lazy around a shoulder of grey stone, all slate roofs and leaning chimneys, built by people who understood that the best way to hide something was to make the town itself look too poor to bother robbing. It still looked poor. That was the problem. It looked exactly as poor as it had nine years ago, and towns don't hold their decay that neatly unless something is holding them still.

The market square was half-empty. Vendors stood at their stalls with the particular motionlessness of people conserving warmth, and their goods were dusted with frost that hadn't melted even though the sun was up and pale but present. A woman sold black bread from a cart and never called out her wares. Two men argued over a bolt of wool in voices so low they might have been praying. Nobody laughed. In a river town, in a market square, on a trading morning, nobody laughed.

And above it all, at the end of the lane, stood the chapel of Greyford, wearing winter like a shroud.

Every chapel in the lowlands had been built in the century after the covenant, back when the realms talked to each other through dreams and the boundary-stones hummed at midsummer. They were built with open belfries and doorways wide enough for processions, because joy needs room. This one was sheathed in ice from spire to threshold—not snow, not rime, but ice grown in long clean blades, like the building had been dipped in glass. Through the frosted windows I could see the shapes of pews, of an altar, of figures kneeling. None of them moved. I counted eleven shapes and told myself they were statues, memorials, effigies. I told myself that very firmly, the way you tell yourself things when you already know better.

Kestrel made a sound. Small, cracked, the first noise I'd heard her make since the pass—a thin keening in the back of her throat, the sound a kettle makes just before it screams. Her grip on my hand went tight enough to grind bone.

"I know," I said. "I see it."

She pointed at the chapel, then shook her head hard, then pointed again, and I finally understood she wasn't pointing at the building. She was pointing at the ice itself, tracing its edges in the air, and her fingers drew a shape I recognized from carvings a thousand years dead: the sigil of the Still Air. The Warden's mark. Her mark, worn openly now, on a town chapel, in a border town, nine years after she was supposed to have gone to sleep with the rest of the broken covenant.

*She's awake,* I thought, and the thought landed in my stomach like a swallowed stone. *She's awake and she's working.*

We didn't go near the chapel. We went the other way, down Crooked Stair into the tannery quarter, where the smells were strong enough to cover a multitude of sins and the buildings leaned together like conspirators. At the bottom of the stair, behind a fence of antlers and a door painted the blue of bad luck, lived the woman who had once taught me that the past is currency—you spend it, you lend it, and sooner or later someone comes to collect.

Maren Cobb opened the door before I knocked. She'd always had ears like a hare and manners like a landslide.

"Well," she said, looking me up and down with eyes the color of ditchwater, "the mountain finally spat you out. Nine years, Nystrom. I'd heard you died twice and got rich once, and I only believed one of those."

"Which one?"

"The dying." She stepped aside to let us in. Then she saw Kestrel, and everything about her changed—the way a river changes when a log jams it. Surface calm. Deep current. "And who's this, then? Yours?"

"A foundling," I said. "Orphaned in the passes."

"Mm," said Maren Cobb, who had spent forty years detecting lies for a living, and whose face now told me she had detected mine.

* * *

Her house smelled of tallow and dried eel and the particular sourness of cheap brandy kept too near a fire. She sat us at her table, fed Kestrel bread and honey without being asked—an act of kindness she would have denied under torture—and poured two cups of something that tasted like it had been distilled from regret. Kestrel ate with the careful ferocity of a child who has known hunger and doesn't trust its absence. Maren watched her the whole time, and I watched Maren watching, and I felt the toll counter ticking in my chest.

"Say your piece, Nystrom," she said at last. "You didn't come down off your rock for honey cakes."

"Passage. Downriver, quiet, no names. You still have the flatboat?"

"I still have the flatboat."

"And the quarry landing?"

Something flickered across her face. "The quarry's flooded. Has been since the spring floods, four years back. Bell and all."

"The bell," I said carefully, "is why I'm asking."

Silence. Outside, someone walked past the shuttered window, boots crunching on frost that shouldn't have been there, and both Maren and I went still until the steps faded. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into the register she used for business that could hang a person.

"You're asking about the bell. The old bell. The one the grandmothers said rang by itself when the Garden was listening." She set down her cup with exaggerated care. "Dominic. What's in the quarry isn't a bell anymore. It's a *throat*. And something's been singing down there at night, and the fisherfolk won't go near the water, and last month Tam Wicker's boy went out on the ice on a dare and came back—" she stopped. Started again. "Came back walking wrong. Came back *quiet*. Sat down at his mother's table and didn't know her name. Didn't know his own. The hedgewife looked at him and crossed herself with the old sign, the pre-kingdom one, and told Tam to take the boy to the chapel, and Tam wouldn't, because—" her eyes flicked toward the window, toward the distant gleam of the iced spire, "—because of what's happened to folk who've gone to the chapel lately."

My mouth had gone dry. "How many?"

"Eleven." She said it flatly. "Eleven souls in that church since midwinter, kneeling, breathing, and not one of them remembers their own mother's face. The priest went in to fetch them and he's in there too now. Twelve." She drank, all at once, like medicine. "So you'll understand, Nystrom, when I ask you plain: what did you bring into my town?"

"I brought nothing. She came to me."

"She came to you." Maren leaned forward. Up close, age had carved her face into channels and ridges, a landscape of every deal she'd ever cut. "Then explain to me why the frost follows her like a dog. Why my shutters bloomed ice-flowers the size of my hand the moment she crossed my threshold. Why the eels in my barrel went still as stones." She reached across the table—slowly, giving me time to stop her, and I didn't—and turned Kestrel's chin gently toward the light. The girl let her, unafraid, chewing her bread, watching Maren with those dark patient eyes. "Look at her shadow, Nystrom."

I looked. Lamplight threw our shadows against the wall, mine wavering with the flame. Kestrel's shadow did not waver. It lay on the plaster sharp-edged and perfect, and along its outline, faint as breath on glass, ran a tracery of light—the pattern of roots, branching, the way a seed splits when it wakes.

Maren sat back. For a long moment the only sound was the tick of her mantel clock, counting seconds into a room that suddenly felt much smaller.

"Garden-blood," she whispered. "Saints and sinners. A seed-child. On my floor. Eating my honey." She laughed, one dry bark with no humor in it anywhere. "Do you know what they're paying for rumors of Garden-blood these days? Not coin. *Memories.* The white lady's people come through with lanterns of still air, and they take the memory of your worst day, your grief, your shame—all of it, clean out of you—and folk line up, Dominic. Folk *line up.* Because forgetting hurts less than remembering, and she knows it. She's built her whole winter on it."

"I know what she's built it on," I said. "I helped her build it."

That stopped her. Maren Cobb had seen me lie to magistrates, lie to revenants, lie to a dying man about the state of his soul. She knew all my faces. This one she'd never seen, and it unsettled her more than the seed-child had.

"So," she said finally, rubbing her jaw. "Bought or bound. That's the choice you've come to offer me, isn't it? That's why you came to me and not the ferryman. Because you need my boat and my silence, and you know my silence doesn't come cheap."

"It comes however you price it."

"Everything comes however I price it. That's my curse." She stood and paced to the window, peering through the shutter crack at the frozen street. "Here's my price, then. Not coin—I've got coin and nowhere safe to spend it. Not passage-rights—they're mine already. My price is a memory, bone-carver. One of yours. You'll carve it for me, the way you do, and I'll keep it, and when this is over—if anything of you is left over—you can buy it back or leave it with me. Fair warning: I'll choose which memory. A woman in my trade learns to spot the valuable ones."

The fire popped. Kestrel had stopped eating. She was looking between us, reading the shape of the bargain if not the words, and her small hands had curled closed around the crust of bread like a promise.

"Maren," I said slowly, "you don't know what you'd be taking. Those aren't trinkets. Some of them are load-bearing."

"Then you'd best hope I pick a wall and not a foundation." But her voice gentled, which in Maren was more alarming than shouting. "Dominic. Look outside. Twelve people in that church, and a boy who doesn't know his own name, and a river that's forgotten how to run. Whatever's coming, it's coming for *remembering* itself. You think I want to grow old in a town where I can't recall why I loved the people I loved?" She turned to face me. "Take the good ones out of my head while I can still choose to give them. That's not a toll. That's a rescue."

I looked at her for a long time. Forty years of debts ran between us, most of them hers to me, some of them mine to her, and underneath them all the older thing, the thing neither of us named: we had survived the same winters, and survivors recognize each other the way dogs do.

"All right," I said. "But not tonight. Tonight we sleep, and tomorrow you take us down to the quarry landing, and you'll get your carving on the water, where the dead can witness it. I don't cut bargains blind in a warm room. The bones deserve to hear the terms."

"The bones," Maren repeated, shaking her head. "Nine years and you're still talking to your carvings like they answer."

"They do answer," I said. "That's rather the point of me."

* * *

We slept in her loft, among bundles of dried herbs and coils of rope, and I woke once in the black hours to a sound below—the front door, easing open. I came down the ladder with a knife I didn't remember drawing and found Maren standing in her doorway, wrapped in a blanket, looking north.

I followed her gaze.

Above the rooftops, above the iced chapel with its twelve kneeling prisoners, the aurora had come early—out of season, out of place, green-white curtains hanging in the sky. But auroras move. This one didn't. It hung fixed over the northern peaks like a banner nailed to heaven, and as I watched, it pulsed, slow and rhythmic, and I realized with a lurch that it was pulsing in time with something. With a song. Far off, beneath the edge of hearing, beneath the frozen river, beneath the flooded quarry—

—the bell was ringing. Not sound. *Absence* of sound, a ringing shaped like silence, and every pane of frost on every window in Greyford lit briefly with the sigil of the Still Air, and then went dark.

Beside me, Kestrel appeared at the rail of the loft, drawn down by whatever calls children to trouble. She looked at the northern sky, and her shadow on the wall bloomed with root-light, and she lifted one small hand and pressed it flat against the air—as if answering. As if knocking on a door from the inside.

Far away, the silent bell rang once more. Closer, this time. Or maybe just louder.

Maren crossed herself with the old sign, the pre-kingdom one, and said, in a voice gone thin as paper:

"Tomorrow, then. Before it sings again. Because that, Nystrom—that wasn't calling to the town." She nodded at Kestrel, at the light bleeding softly from the girl's shadow. "That was calling to *her*. And things that call like that don't wait politely forever."
