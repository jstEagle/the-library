## 9. First Stone: The Drowned Bell

The quarry had been drowning for a hundred years, and it sang about it.

Kestrel heard the song before she saw the water—a low, patient hum that came up through her bare feet on the slate path, through the soles of her borrowed boots, into the long bones of her legs where it settled like a second heartbeat. *Drowned, drowned, drowned,* the water said to itself, over and over, the way lonely things repeat their own names so they don't forget them.

She stopped at the quarry's rim and looked down.

Greyford's flooded quarry was a bowl of green-black glass set into the mountain's shoulder, ringed by terraces of cut stone gone soft with moss. Rain moved across its surface in slow silver sheets. Somewhere beneath all that stillness lay the chapel bell of old Greyford, sunk when they flooded the workings, and beneath the bell—Dominic said—the first stone.

*It won't look like much,* he'd told her that morning, in his gruff way, not looking at her while he spoke because looking made him uncomfortable and being looked at made her worse. *A pillar of grey rock, maybe taller than you. Cracked. Ugly, even. Don't let that fool you. Everything worth anything looks like nothing from the outside.*

Kestrel knew this better than anyone. She had spent eight years looking like nothing—a mute child, a stray, a burden passed from doorway to doorway down the length of a valley that no longer remembered why it left offerings at the roots of trees. She had been handed along like a chipped cup, useful to no one, until the night the snow came early and wrong and a bone-carver with sad hands opened his door because something in his house was calling her name.

Now Dominic crouched beside her at the quarry's edge, unpacking rope from a canvas roll with the economy of a man who had done many dangerous things quietly. His breath smoked. The air above the water did not smoke at all, which was the first wrong thing Kestrel noticed, and then the second: the rain fell onto the quarry and made no sound. It struck the surface like snow striking snow, soft as memory, and the silence of it raised the hair on her arms.

*Cold down there,* she signed. Her hands had learned to speak fast in these weeks on the road; Dominic read them now almost as easily as he read the dead.

"Cold everywhere," Dominic said. "Question is whether it's sleeping cold or watching cold." He tested a knot, scowled at it, tied it again. "The bell will tell us. If the bell still rings when we wake the stone, we're ahead of the Warden. If it doesn't—" He didn't finish. He had a habit of leaving sentences open at the end, like doors he didn't want to walk through.

Kestrel looked at the water again and felt the song underneath it, deeper than the drowning-song, older. A thread of it ran up through the slate into her spine, and she recognized it the way you recognize your own name spoken in a crowd—the old tongue, the pre-kingdom speech, the language that lived in her mouth though she could not push it past her lips in any tongue men used. Down there. Under the bell. Something was saying words she knew.

She tugged Dominic's sleeve and pointed.

"I know," he said. "I hear it too. Or—I hear what the bones make of it." He touched the carving at his throat, a small grey thing like a bird's skull, one of the oldest he carried. "They're restless today. Haven't been this loud since the pass."

He rigged the rope to an iron mooring-ring rusted into the terrace edge, ancient smuggler's hardware, and went down the quarry wall first, boots finding holds he seemed to know without looking. Kestrel followed. The rock was cold and damp and kind to her fingers, full of small deep cracks, and she felt it noticing her as she descended—noticing the way soil notices a seed, a slow attention turning upward through the stone. By the time her feet touched the flooded lower gallery, ankle-deep in black water, the whole quarry was aware of her. She could feel its gaze the way you feel sunlight through a window.

The lower gallery was a corridor of drowned arches. The water stood waist-high and utterly clear, so that she could see the sunken world below: fallen scaffolding furred white with mineral bloom, a cart with its wheels still turning slowly in some current she couldn't feel, and there—at the far end, where the gallery opened into the great flooded pit—the bell.

It hung from a rotted beam that should not have held it, and it was enormous, green with age, its mouth open toward the surface as if it had been caught mid-cry a century ago and had never finished.

And behind the bell, faint as a shape seen through fogged glass, stood the stone.

* * *

Dominic waded out until the water reached his chest, holding the carved bird-skull above the surface. "Talk to me," he murmured—to the carving, or the dead in it, Kestrel couldn't tell. His face had gone tight and listening. Then he stopped moving altogether.

Kestrel watched the voices arrive. She couldn't hear them—she never could—but she saw how they took him: the small collapse of his shoulders, the way his eyes fixed on some middle distance where the living had no business. When he surfaced from it, he was pale.

"The drowned," he said. "There are more of them down here than I expected. Quarrymen. Smugglers. A priest." He swallowed. "They remember the bell. They say it rang the night the covenant broke—rang for three days without stopping, and then the water came, and it rang under the water for a while after that. They could hear it through the ground. Whole town lying awake listening to a bell underwater." His voice roughened. "Then it stopped, and everyone agreed to forget they'd ever heard it. Forgetting is easier. I've said that before myself."

He looked at her then, really looked, and Kestrel saw the question in him before he asked it.

"Can you do this?"

She thought about lying. She thought about how easy it would be to shrug, to sign *maybe*, to keep the door of herself half closed the way she always had. But the water around her waist was humming now, and the thread in her spine had become a cord, and somewhere below her feet the stone was saying her name—not *Kestrel*, which was a name given to her by strangers, but the older sound, the one the bones used, the one that meant *seed-that-waits*. Lying to the quarry would be like lying to her own blood.

So she nodded once, and began to unlace her boots.

"You don't have to go under," Dominic said quickly. "You can sing from here. The water carries it—that's the whole point of a drowned bell, the water carries everything—"

But Kestrel was already wading forward, and the water was already rising up her body like a hand drawing her down, gentle as sleep, and she understood suddenly that this was not a thing she could do from the shore. The stone was not asking for her voice. It was asking for *her*.

The last step dropped away and the quarry took her.

* * *

Down was quiet. Down was green-gold light falling in slanted columns from the world above, bending and wavering, and the cold closing over her skin like a second self. Her hair lifted around her face. Below her, the bell swung very slightly on its rotted beam, though nothing moved down here, nothing had moved in a hundred years except the slow work of water.

She sank toward it. Her lungs ached, and she let them ache, because the ache was part of it—she understood that now, in the wordless way she understood most things. The old tongue was not only a language of the mouth. It was a language of cost. Every true word in it was paid for with something the speaker missed.

She put her hand on the bell.

The metal was ice and it was also warm, both at once, the way a grave in summer is warm. And through her palm the bell poured itself into her: every ring it had ever rung, weddings and funerals and warnings of raiders coming down the pass, the christening bells and the plague bells, the last three-day peal when the covenant broke and the Garden drew shut beneath the roots like a wound closing—and after that, the ringing underwater, years of it, unheard, faithful, mad, a bell tolling to an audience of silt. The bell had kept time for two worlds. The bell was tired. The bell had been waiting for someone who could tell it the ringing had meant something.

Kestrel opened her mouth underwater, and sang.

No air came out. That was the miracle and the horror of it: the old tongue did not need breath. It needed *grief*, and grief she had in quantities beyond counting, eight years of it, pressed down into her silence like flowers in a book. It rose out of her now in a stream of silver, visible in the water, curling away from her lips in ribbons that glimmered and knotted themselves into shapes—she was singing letters, she realized; the language wrote itself as it left her—and the ribbons wrapped the bell, and the bell began to move.

It swung. Slowly at first, ponderous, a mountain learning to be a bell again. Then faster. And when it struck, the sound did not scatter—it *converged*, folding inward and downward, and the whole quarry lit from beneath.

Light came up out of the stone floor of the pit in veins, gold-green, the color of the first leaf she had ever seen unfurl, and Kestrel saw the covenant-stone clearly for the first time.

It was not ugly. Dominic had been wrong, or kind, or both. It was a pillar of grey rock, yes, cracked, crusted with a century of silt—but the cracks were not damage. They were *doors*. As the bell tolled and the light climbed, the cracks filled with growing things: rootlets uncoiling like the fingers of waking sleepers, moss blooming into fern, fern into small white flowers that had no business existing underwater and existed anyway, defiantly, beautifully, breathing light instead of air. The stone was not a marker. It was a stump that had refused to stop being a tree.

And the stone *answered her*.

It answered in the old tongue, and for the first time since the snow, since the workshop, since the bones had first turned toward her whispering, Kestrel heard the language not as a feeling in her chest but as sound—rich, layered, many-voiced, a chord sung by roots and rain and stone and the long patience of things that grow. The words were simple. The words undid her.

*Little seed. You came back. We kept your place.*

Her place. There was a place. There had always been a place, under everything, under the handing-along and the shut doors and the winters of being no one's—there had been a place keeping itself ready the way a bed keeps the shape of its sleeper. Kestrel hung in the green-lit water with her hand on the bell and wept, and her tears joined the quarry, and the quarry accepted them, and she understood that she was crying into something that counted every drop, the way a granary counts grain, the way a mother counts breaths.

The bell struck again, and the two worlds overlapped.

* * *

For one breath—one long breath, longer than any breath she had ever held—Kestrel stood in both realms at once.

Above the waterline, Greyford remained: the grey sky, the terraces, Dominic's face turned up toward her, shouting her name soundlessly, his hand outstretched over the surface. But through it, translucent as breath on glass, she saw the other place. Trees the height of hills, their canopies full of slow golden weather. Rivers that flowed uphill and sang while they climbed. Doors standing open in the sides of living trees, and figures in the doorways—tall, rooted, crowned with blossom—watching her with expressions she had spent eight years starving for. Recognition. Welcome. *Home.*

The Garden. Not the story of it, not the lullaby version the shepherd children still half-sang. The real one, vast and dreaming, built from everything mortals had ever remembered lovingly—every garden ever tended, every orchard, every grave planted with roses, every tree a child had climbed and named. It was all of it, all at once, and it was looking at her, and it was *glad*.

For that one breath, Kestrel belonged. Utterly, specifically, irreversibly. The feeling was so large that it frightened her more than the cold ever had, because she knew—with the terrible clarity of the old tongue, which does not permit lies even in the heart—that belonging could be taken away again. Had been taken away. Was being taken away right now, somewhere, by someone.

Because in the same breath, in the far distance of the overlapped worlds, she felt the answering cold.

It came from the north of the north, from a fortress of still air she had never seen and knew instantly, the way you know a nightmare you've had before. It was vast and patient and *hungry*, and it had felt the bell ring. It had felt the stone wake. It turned toward Greyford the way a head turns toward a sound in the dark, and though it had no face, Kestrel felt it smile.

And it said her name. Not *Kestrel*. The older sound. *Seed-that-waits.*

*I have been waiting for you too.*

The breath ended. The overlap folded shut like a book closing, and the Garden was gone, and the cold was gone, and Kestrel was alone in the green water with a tolling bell and lungs full of fire.

She kicked for the surface.

* * *

She came up gasping into grey ordinary air, and Dominic hauled her against the quarry wall and held her while she coughed the quarry out of her lungs. Behind them, the bell rang on—audible now above the water, rolling across the terraces, down into the town, and Kestrel heard the town answer: dogs barking, doors opening, and voices, human voices, calling out in confusion and something close to joy. People who had never heard the bell were hearing it. People who had forgotten they'd forgotten were remembering.

"It rang," Dominic was saying, half laughing, half weeping, wringing water out of his beard. "By the roots, it rang, and the stone—the flowers, did you see—the first stone is *awake*, Kestrel, do you understand? One of four. One of four, and the road just got shorter and the danger just got—" He stopped. He had finally looked at her face.

"What?" he said. "What happened down there?"

Kestrel gripped his wrist. Her hands were shaking too hard to sign clearly, so she pressed her palm flat against the carved bird-skull at his throat, and the dead in the bone carried what she could not say:

*A garden woke. It knows me. It wants me home.*

*And something in the north heard the bell—and it knows me too—and it is coming.*
