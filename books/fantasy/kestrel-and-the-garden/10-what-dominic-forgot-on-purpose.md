## 10. What Dominic Forgot on Purpose

The snow had stopped sometime in the night. Dominic woke before dawn with Kestrel's breathing steady in the loft above and the workshop silent in the way he had spent nine years teaching it to be silent — no voices rising from the drying rack, no whispers from the finished pieces wrapped in wool and ash-cloth. He had learned to quiet them. He had learned, mostly, not to listen.

He lay in the dark and did the thing he did on nights when sleep would not hold him. He went back.

Not to the fall itself. He never went straight to the fall. He went first to the spring before it, because that was where the wound began, and a bone-carver knew better than anyone that you could not read a break without finding its origin.

* * *

Nine years ago, in the last spring of the old world, Maren Vael had come down from the high fortress for the first time in four years.

She arrived at dusk, alone, wearing traveling grey instead of the white-and-silver of her office, and she stood in the doorway of his workshop in Lowtham with snowmelt dripping off her cloak — snowmelt in spring, which should have told him something even then — and she said, "Dominic. You've been hiding."

"I've been working," he said.

"Show me."

He had been twenty-six then, and she had been the finest mind the Wardens of the Boundary had ever produced, and he had loved her the way an apprentice loves the person who first tells him *you have a gift*. Not softly. Structurally. The way a wall loves its cornerstone. She had found him at fourteen, carving a sparrow from his grandfather's knucklebone in a churchyard because grief needed somewhere to go, and she had put her hand over his and said, *Do you hear it? Do you hear what you're holding?* And when he wept, she had not comforted him. She had said, *Good. That's the material. Now learn to shape it.*

So when she said *show me*, he showed her.

He brought out the piece he'd been working all winter: a reliquary bead carved from the fingerbone of a woman named Aldith Crane, who had died in the winter floods and whose family wanted one word from her — just one — before they let her go into the ground. The bead held her last word the way amber holds a fly. When Dominic closed his hand around it, Aldith spoke through him, through the bone, one syllable of gratitude, and then silence, because he had shaped the carving so that silence came after. That was the craft. Anyone with the gift could open a door to the dead. Only a carver could close it again.

Maren turned the bead in her long fingers for a long time. Her hands were beautiful hands, precise as instruments, and he remembered noticing — the way you notice weather changing — that they were colder than they used to be.

"You close them now," she said.

"Mostly."

"Mostly." She smiled, and it was the old smile, dry and warm at once. "Do you know what I envy about you, Dominic? Everyone else in my order guards the boundary between the living and the dead like soldiers at a gate. You're the only one who treats it as a *craft*. Something with technique. Something that can be improved."

He should have heard it then. *Improved.* He would spend nine years hearing it afterward, in the dark, in every register her voice owned. But that evening it sounded only like praise, and praise from Maren Vael was rarer than sun on the Thornfell, and he drank it down like a man who has been cold for years.

"Why are you here?" he asked.

"Because the covenant is failing," she said, "and the Council won't believe me, and I need someone who works in bone."

* * *

The story she told him took three evenings, told across his own table, over his own bread, in his own house, and it was built — he understood this much later, turning each timber of it over in memory, testing it, finding the places where a lie had been joined seamlessly to truth — it was built almost entirely of true things.

The covenant was real. It had stood since before the kingdoms, an agreement older than writing: the human realm above and the Garden below the roots, the dreaming world where everything mortals had ever remembered lovingly went on living after its mortal life ended. Grandmothers' kitchens. Childhood dogs. First loves, dead friends, the smell of a house burned down two hundred years ago. All of it tended below, alive in the Garden, and in exchange the Garden drew its strength from being remembered. Memory flowed down like rain. Love flowed up like springwater. Four covenant-stones anchored the border — the Drowned Bell beneath Greyford, the Choir of Roots in the deep forest, the Teeth of the World in the wind-canyon, and the Threshold Stone at the Garden's heart — and as long as the stones sang to one another, both realms breathed.

All true. Every child in the mountain villages still knew fragments of it in lullabies.

And this was true too: the flow was thinning. Maren showed him the records. Tribute-memories arriving at the boundary thinner each year, paler, fewer. The Garden's seasons stuttering. Stones going quiet one by one, like bells with cracked throats.

"The mortals are forgetting," Dominic said. "It happens. Kingdoms rise and people get busy—"

"People get busy." She laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Yes. And while they're busy, the Garden starves. And when the Garden starves, Dominic, do you know what happens? Everything your gift depends on goes with it. Every voice you've ever raised from bone. The dead don't survive in *our* realm. They survive down there, in the remembering. Cut the flow and within a generation the bones are just bones. Your whole craft dies of neglect, and worse—" she leaned forward, and her eyes were the grey of ice under pressure, "—worse, everything ever loved is simply *gone*. Not dead. Dead things can be visited. Erased. That's what a failed covenant means."

He believed her. That was the part he could never forgive, nine years on, lying awake under Kestrel's sleeping weight in the loft above. Not that he'd been deceived — deceivers deceive; that was their nature and no shame belonged to the deceived — but that he had believed her *so easily*, so gratefully, because she had offered him a version of the world in which his loneliness was actually vigilance, his exile was actually duty, and his gift mattered to the architecture of everything.

"What do you need?" he asked.

"A key," said Maren Vael. "The stones are locked to one another. To test whether they can be re-tuned — strengthened, renewed — I need a carver to make an instrument that can speak to all four at once. A masterwork. The kind of thing only you can do."

* * *

She brought him the material herself.

That was the detail that undid him, later. Any other bone, any relic of any dead soul, and he would have listened first — would have held it to his ear the way he'd taught himself since childhood, hearing the grain of the life inside it, the pitch of the death. But Maren brought it wrapped in Warden-grey silk, sealed with the sigil of the Boundary Order, and told him it was the femur of a Warden who had died in service two centuries past, released from the order's reliquary by special dispensation, *because only a Warden's bone has stood at the boundary long enough to remember all four stones.*

"It's called the Meridian Key," she said. "Carve it so it can sound all four anchors together. Not open anything. Sound them. Like tuning forks struck side by side. If the covenant can be saved, this will tell us how."

"And if it can't?"

Her face did something he had never seen it do. It softened. "Then at least we'll know," she said, "before the end. You of all people know what it costs to die unheard, Dominic. Give the Garden the mercy of a diagnosis."

So he carved it.

He carved it through the summer, in the workshop in Lowtham, with the windows open to the bees. It was the finest work of his life — he knew that even as he did it, felt it in his hands the way a musician knows a performance has passed beyond technique into something else. The femur yielded to him like it had been waiting. He shaped it into a slender rod banded with four rings, each ring tuned to one stone, each cut made while listening to the ghost-echo of the anchor it addressed. He slept four hours a night. He forgot to eat. When the last facet closed and he lifted the finished key, all four rings sang at once, faintly, harmonically, a chord he felt in his teeth, and he sat back with his hands shaking and thought: *I have made the instrument that saves the world.*

Maren came for it at the turn of autumn. She held it the way she had once held Aldith Crane's bead, turning it in those cold precise fingers, and she said, "You've outdone everything I dreamed when I found you," and he glowed like a boy.

"Come with me," she said. "To the Threshold. You should hear it sound at the heart. Carvers never get to see what their work does. Come and know."

* * *

He went. Of course he went.

They traveled down the root-roads, the living ways beneath the mountains that opened for Warden's silver, and he saw the Garden for the first and last time: the orchards grown from remembered weddings, the rivers that ran with the color of childhood summers, houses that were not copies of houses but the *idea* of houses, every one warmed by the love that had imagined it. The dead walked there, or the shapes of the dead, tended and dreaming, and some of them waved to him because they could feel the bone-dust on his hands and knew him for a friend.

At the heart of the Garden stood the fourth stone, the Threshold, a pillar of pale stone veined with gold, singing low and constant like a held breath. Maren set the Meridian Key against it.

"Stand back," she said. "When it sounds, the resonance will be strong."

He stepped back. He watched her fit the key to a socket he had not known was there — a socket shaped, he realized with a slow cold drop, exactly to the key's base, exactly, as though the socket had been waiting for this instrument for two hundred years, as though the specifications had been passed down, Warden to Warden, until the one carver gifted enough to make the key could be found and guided—

"Maren," he said. "What does it do?"

She looked at him over her shoulder, and there was no softness left anywhere in her face. There was, he would remember always, something worse than anger. There was *relief*. The relief of a woman setting down a weight carried so long it had grown into her spine.

"What it was made to do," she said. "What it was always going to do. I'm sorry, Dominic. Truly. You were the only one who could have carved it, and you were the easiest of the ones who could."

She turned the key.

The Threshold Stone did not shatter. That was the horror of it — he had expected shattering, violence, some crack of doom he could have thrown himself against. Instead the stone *stopped*. The song didn't break; it ended, cleanly, the way a note ends when a hand is laid on the string. And the silence spread outward through the Garden like frost through water, and he heard — he would hear it for the rest of his life — the sound of ten thousand remembered things going quiet at once. Orchards stilling. Rivers slowing toward glass. The dead turning their faces up toward a warmth that was withdrawing.

"No," he said, and lunged, and Maren caught his wrist with those instrument-precise fingers, and for one moment they stood face to face, teacher and student, at the exact center of the breaking world.

"Listen to me," she said, fast and low, and there was desperation in it, real desperation, which was somehow the worst discovery of all — that she believed her own madness. "The flow was killing us both. Don't you understand? Memory flows down, but *forgetting* flows up. Every year the Garden drinks our remembering and gives back erosion. Why do you think your villages lose their dead so fast? Why do you think you hoard voices in bone like a miser? Because everything erodes, Dominic. Everything loved slides away. I am ending the leak. Stillness isn't death. Stillness is *preservation*. In a hundred years, when both realms are frozen perfect, nothing will ever be lost again—"

"You're killing it," he said. "You're killing everything—"

"I'm *saving* it," said the Warden of Winter, in the voice of the woman who had once put her hand over a grieving child's knucklebone and told him his grief was a material, "the only way it can be saved. Completely. Forever."

Then the Garden's dying surge threw them apart, and the root-roads sealed themselves behind him as he fled, and he came up out of the earth alone into a world whose winters had begun to arrive early.

* * *

He tried, in the first year, to undo it. Of course he did. He went back to the Threshold's surface-mark above Thornfell and dug, and the mountain had already forgotten the way down. He sought the other Wardens and found them scattered, silenced, or converted — Maren had spent decades, he understood then, positioning her order for this, and he had been the last lock in a mechanism assembled long before he was born. He sought the three remaining stones and found them guarded by distance, by grief, by rooted dead who turned their faces from the man whose key had silenced the fourth.

And somewhere in the second year, in a rented room in Greyford with his hands around a cup of tea gone cold, Dominic Nystrom had done the thing that gave this night-memory its name.

He had begun to forget on purpose.

Not with magic. With discipline. He took the memory of the Garden — the orchards, the waving dead, the gold-veined stone — and he folded it small, and he buried it deep, and he built over it the only structure that would hold: guilt. Guilt as foundation, guilt as walls, guilt as roof against the sky. Because the guilt was *true*, and true things make good load-bearing walls. He deserved exile. He had carved the key. Whatever penance the mountains demanded, he would pay it in solitude, and he would not carve for the dead again except for coin, and he would not listen unless paid to, and he would certainly, certainly never again trust the sound of his own devotion.

It worked. It worked for nine years. The wound stayed buried under its masonry, and the workshop stayed quiet, and the winters came earlier each year and he told himself it was weather.

* * *

Now he lay in the dark beneath the loft and heard Kestrel turn over in her sleep, and the masonry inside him had cracks in it wide enough to walk through. Because a child had knocked on his door speaking the pre-kingdom tongue, and the first covenant-stone had risen out of a flooded quarry at her song, and the mountains were sealing their passes like a body shutting down around a wound — and because he knew, now, with the terrible clarity of a man reading his own old carving, what Maren Vael would do next.

She had spent nine years completing the stillness. Three stones remained outside her grasp, and a seed of the Garden had surfaced, and she would be hunting it with everything her frozen order had left.

And Dominic had helped build the instrument of her will. Not just the key. *Himself.* His craft, his credibility, his guilt-shaped exile keeping him blind for nine years while she worked.

He got up quietly, banked the fire, and took down the map of the Thornfell passes that hung behind the door. His finger found Greyford, and the drowned quarry, and moved east along the smuggler's roads toward the deep forest, where the second stone waited among the rooted dead — the dead who knew his face, and knew what he had done, and would demand a toll for passage that no amount of coin could cover.

Behind him, up in the loft, Kestrel murmured something in the old tongue in her sleep. One word. He didn't recognize it, and yet some buried part of him flinched at it, the way a bone flinches before the break shows on the surface.

Outside, far off in the high passes, something gave a single cry — not bird, not wolf, thin and clear as air moving through a keyhole — and then the night went still again, the particular patient stillness of a thing that has found a track and does not need to hurry.

Dominic stood with his hand on the map a long moment. Then he went to wake the child. They had a road to beat, and for the first time in nine years, he knew exactly what he was running toward — and exactly what was running after them.
