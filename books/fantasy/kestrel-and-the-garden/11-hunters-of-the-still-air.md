## 11. Hunters of the Still Air

The pass had a name once, before the maps forgot it. The smugglers called it the Needle's Eye, and they called it that with a certain grim affection, because it was narrow enough to hide in and mean enough to keep honest people out.

Dominic did not feel hidden. He felt watched.

He had felt it since Greyford, since the drowned bell sang beneath the quarry and both worlds overlapped for one impossible breath. Awe like that does not go unnoticed. It rings out across the roots of things, across the still places where something patient is listening.

"Keep moving," he said, for the fourth time that hour.

Kestrel glanced back at him. She had taken to walking ahead on the narrow stretches, her small feet finding purchase on ice-slick stone as though the mountain were politely rearranging itself beneath her. It probably was. He tried not to think about what that meant, or about how the frost on the rocks near her bloomed into fern-patterns instead of flat white sheets—frost with intention in it, frost that looked almost like growing things.

The wind came down the pass in long, low moans, threading through the rock spires on either side of them. Dominic pulled his collar up and counted his fears the way he'd once counted knots in rope: one, the cold was wrong, too dry, too even; two, no birds; three, the silence between gusts had a texture to it, a held-breath quality, as if the air itself had been taught to stand at attention.

Something had passed through here recently. Something that ate.

He touched the carving at his throat—a small fox skull, worn smooth by years of handling—and felt only static. The dead who lived in his bones had gone quiet since Greyford. Not absent. *Listening.*

*They know too,* he thought. *Whatever's coming, the dead can hear it better than I can.*

Kestrel stopped so suddenly he nearly walked into her.

She stood rigid on the trail, head tilted, listening to something below the wind. Then she turned and looked up—not along the pass, but up, at the cliff wall above them, at a shelf of shadowed rock where nothing moved.

Her hand found his sleeve. She didn't point. She didn't need to. Her fingers said everything: *There. There. There.*

Dominic looked up and saw, at first, nothing but stone and dusk. Then the dusk shifted. Three shapes detached from the shelf without hurry, without sound, and began to descend the cliff face the way water descends—smoothly, inevitably, taking the shortest path because obstacles meant nothing to them.

They had been men once. That was the horror of it. Even at this distance, even in failing light, Dominic could see the remnants: boots, belt buckles, the cut of courier's coats. The Warden's servants were made from people who had carried messages, guarded doors, loved their children. She took the memories out of them one by one until all that remained was obedience wearing a body like an empty coat.

Still air clung to them. He could see it now—the faint distortion around each shape, like heat-haze in reverse, like the world refusing to touch them.

"Run," Dominic said.

Kestrel was already running.

* * *

The Warden's servant had been a man named Aldous Ferrin, and then he had been a courier of the Winter Court, and now he was neither. Names were among the first things she took. What remained was purpose, honed to a single edge: *the seed lives; find the seed; bring the seed or end it.*

He descended the cliff face without thought, because thought was a room long emptied of furniture. Below him, two small figures fled along the pass toward the old rope bridge that crossed the Gorge of Whistling Bones. His instructions were precise. The bridge was good. Bridges were good. Narrow places funneled prey.

Behind him came the second servant, and behind that, the third. They did not speak. Speech required memory of language, and language required memory of faces, and faces required memory of love, and all of that had been eaten months ago, swallowed in slow patient mouthfuls by a woman who wept while she fed.

Aldous did not remember weeping. He remembered only the cold, which was not a sensation but an instruction: *go.*

Below, the fugitives reached the bridge.

* * *

The bridge was older than the smuggling roads, older than the kingdoms, maybe older than the covenant. Ropes of braided root-fiber, planks of pale wood that might have been grown rather than cut—it swayed over a gorge so deep the bottom was a rumor of darkness and distant water. Wind screamed up out of it, moaning through holes in the canyon walls, giving the place its name.

Dominic got three planks onto the bridge before he understood they weren't going to make the far side.

The servants had stopped running. They stood at the near anchorage, three still shapes against the darkening sky, and they were waiting, and the waiting was worse than pursuit. They knew something he didn't. Then he saw it—one of them raised a hand, casually, the way a man waves away a fly, and the nearest anchor-rope *froze*. Not frayed. Froze. Ice crawled up the braided root-fiber, stiffening it, making it brittle, making it ready to snap under weight.

"They're going to drop us," Dominic breathed. "They're going to let us get halfway and drop us."

Kestrel tugged his sleeve. She pointed at the far side of the gorge—at a seam in the rock face, barely visible, a vertical line of darker stone. And from that seam, faint as breath on glass, came a glow. Green-gold. Warm.

*A door.* One of the mountain's old doors, waking for her. But forty feet of rotten bridge lay between them and it.

Dominic looked at the frozen anchor. Looked at the servants, standing patient as winter itself. Made the arithmetic smugglers live and die by.

"We cross fast," he said. "Don't look down. Don't stop. If I fall—" He caught her eyes. "If I fall, you keep going. You hear me? You are more important than me. That's not kindness, that's strategy."

Her jaw set in a way that was answer enough: she heard him, and she rejected him, and she would do both silently forever.

They stepped onto the bridge.

* * *

The first ten planks were sound. The next five groaned. By the twentieth, the whole span was swaying in long sick arcs, and the wind from the gorge was trying its best to peel them off the world. Dominic kept his eyes fixed on the green-gold seam ahead and his hand on the rope, and he did not look down, and he did not look back, until Kestrel made a sound—not a word, she never made words, but a sharp intake of breath that meant *look*.

He looked back.

The servants were on the bridge.

All three of them, spaced evenly, walking without haste, and the bridge did not sway beneath them. That was the detail that turned Dominic's blood to slush. A living bridge moves; it answers every footstep with a shiver. Under these three it hung dead still, as if it had already been told it was going to die and saw no reason to argue.

And the middle one—he could see its face now, in the last grey light. A man's face, weathered, maybe fifty, with the deep lines of someone who had spent years squinting into distance. Courier's lines. Road-lines. The face was calm the way a frozen lake is calm, and where his eyes should have been there was only a pale frost-glaze, and yet somehow he was looking directly at Kestrel.

His mouth opened. What came out wasn't words. It was a long exhalation, and the air around it crystallized into drifting motes that hung motionless even in the screaming wind.

*Still air,* Dominic thought. *They carry her stillness with them. Everything they breathe on stops.*

The lead servant raised one hand toward the anchor-rope ahead of them—the far anchor, the one attached to the door-side of the gorge—and ice bloomed along it with a crack like a rifle shot.

Thirty feet to the door. A freezing rope. Three dead men closing at a walk, because they knew, with the patience of the grave, that they did not need to hurry.

Dominic did the only thing left. He drew his knife and began hacking at the rope behind them.

* * *

Kestrel felt the power come up through her feet.

It had been doing that more and more since the bell—rising unasked, answering fear before she could decide whether to let it. She felt it now as a great green pressure behind her ribs, like spring packed too tight into a seed casing, like every growing thing that had ever slept through winter and woken furious.

The servant was twenty feet away. Fifteen. Its frost-glazed eyes were fixed on her with something that was not hunger, because hunger was a feeling and feelings had been eaten out of it—but was the *shape* of hunger, the outline hunger leaves behind, the way a nest holds the shape of the bird long after the bird is gone.

*Bring the seed,* it had been told. *Or end it.*

It leapt.

Later, Kestrel would try to reconstruct those half-seconds and fail. She remembered the servant airborne above the gorge, coat flapping, arms outstretched, close enough that she could see the stitching on its cuff, the faded ink stain on one finger—a courier's ink stain, a life's small residue. She remembered Dominic shouting something. She remembered the power rising through her like a struck bell.

And she remembered choosing.

That was the part that frightened her most, afterward. It hadn't been reflex. It hadn't been panic. Some cold, clear part of her—some part that spoke in the old tongue even when she didn't—had looked at the leaping shape and *decided*, the way a gardener decides which branch to prune.

Winter answered her.

Not the Warden's winter—hers. It went out of her in a silent ring, a sphere of absolute stillness, and inside that sphere the servant *stopped*. Mid-leap. Mid-air. Over four hundred feet of whistling dark.

Its coat settled. Its outstretched hands froze in the act of reaching. Frost climbed it in ferns and feathers and patterns like growing things, and the still air around it thickened into crystal, and there it hung—suspended above the abyss, a man-shaped absence, reaching forever for a child it had been ordered to kill.

The other two servants halted at the edge of the ring. They stood at its boundary like men at the edge of a lit room, and then, without expression, without anything, they turned and walked back off the bridge into the dark, because their instructions had not accounted for this, and they had nothing left inside them to improvise with.

The silence rang.

Then the frozen rope Dominic had been cutting gave way, the whole bridge lurched and swung, and the spell of horror broke into simple animal urgency. They ran. Planks cracked behind them. The far anchor shrieked. Dominic grabbed Kestrel by the back of her coat and threw them both the last six feet through the green-gold seam in the rock—

—and the mountain closed behind them like a held breath released.

* * *

Inside was a smuggler's cache, ancient and dry: a hollow in the living rock lit by veins of the same green-gold light, shelves of rotted sacking, a rusted lantern, air that smelled of stone and old resin. Safe. For now.

Dominic slid down the wall until he was sitting, and discovered his hands were shaking so badly he had to press them flat against the floor.

"That," he said, when he could speak, "was not supposed to be possible."

Kestrel stood in the middle of the chamber, very still, hugging her elbows. In the green light her face was pale and her eyes were enormous.

He knew what she was seeing. He'd seen it too: the moment of decision. The cold clarity. The ease.

"It saved us," he said gently. "What you did—it saved us. Both of us."

She shook her head. Small, fierce, miserable. She pressed her fist to her sternum, then opened the fist slowly, fingers unfurling like something blooming, and looked at her own hand as if it belonged to a stranger. Then she pointed back through the sealed stone—back toward the bridge, the gorge, the thing hanging in the air—and made a sound low in her throat. Not a word. A grief-noise. The noise of someone who has just learned that they contain a winter of their own.

Dominic crossed the chamber and knelt in front of her, careful, the way you approach a deer or a lit fuse.

"Listen to me." He waited until her eyes came to his. "I have spent years listening to the dead tell me what they regretted. Do you know what none of them ever regretted? Protecting someone. Not one. Not once." He took her frozen hand and folded her fingers closed again, over the power, keeping it warm. "You'll want to hate yourself for this. Don't spend the coin yet. Save it for a choice that deserves it."

But even as he said it, he was thinking of the servant's face. The ink-stained finger. The courier's coat. Someone had loved that man. Someone had waited for his footsteps on the stairs. And now he hung over a gorge in a cage of a child's making, reaching, reaching, and would hang there until the mountain wore away.

*This is what she is,* Dominic thought, and the thought tasted like iron. *This is what the Garden makes. Power that answers the heart before the heart can speak.*

And underneath that, quieter, colder, a question he refused to ask aloud:

*What did the Warden freeze first, I wonder? Her enemies—or her own grief?*

Above them, muffled by tons of stone, the wind changed. The long moan of the gorge faltered, steadied, and went utterly, perfectly still.

Somewhere in the dark of the pass, the remaining hunters had stopped walking.

They were waiting for morning.

So, Dominic realized with a lurch, was something else—for far below them, through the living rock, through the sleeping roots of the world, he felt a tremor pass, faint as a heartbeat: the second covenant-stone, deep in the forest, calling to the seed.

Calling to her. And something else calling back—cold, patient, and much, much closer than it had been yesterday.
