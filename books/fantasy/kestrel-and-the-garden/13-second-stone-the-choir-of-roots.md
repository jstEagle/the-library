## 13. Second Stone: The Choir of Roots

The forest began where the smuggler's road gave up pretending to be a road.

Dominic felt the change before he saw it—a pressure in his ears, like descending into water, like the moment before a carved bone speaks. He stopped at the treeline and let his pack slide from his shoulder. Behind him, Kestrel did the same without being told. Three weeks on the mountain paths had taught her his silences: which ones meant *wait*, which meant *danger*, and which—rarest—meant *I am listening to something you cannot hear*.

This was that last kind.

The trees were wrong here. Not sick, not dead—wrong the way a held breath is wrong. They stood too straight, too close together, and their bark had grown in long vertical ridges that looked, when the light moved, almost like the seams of closed eyes. Birch and black pine and one species Dominic had no name for, pale as candle-wax, that grew only in the old maps' blank spaces.

"The Choir," he said quietly. Not because Kestrel needed words anymore—she read his hands now as easily as her own thoughts—but because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be prepared for.

Kestrel touched his sleeve. When he turned, she pointed into the trees, then pressed both palms flat over her heart, then opened them outward: *the voices are singing.*

He knew. Even standing at the edge, he could feel them—not through any carving, not through bone or ivory, but raw, the way he'd felt things before he'd learned to armor himself. A hum beneath the soil. Hundreds of voices, maybe thousands, all holding one long note the way a congregation holds its breath before the hymn breaks open.

"They're the rooted dead," he said. "People who chose to stay. After the covenant broke, some of the Garden's faithful didn't run north or hide in the valleys. They lay down in this forest and asked the roots to take them. Standing up." He swallowed. "The old stories say they're still in there. Still tending something."

Kestrel's brow furrowed. She made a shape with her fingers—small, curled—and looked at him with a question.

"A seed," Dominic translated. "Yes. I think so too."

They went in.

* * *

The light changed within fifty paces. It came down green and slow, sifting through needles, and it fell in columns rather than shafts, as if the forest itself were deciding where illumination was permitted. The ground was soft as a mattress of moss, and every footstep sank an inch and then rose again behind them, erasing their passage. No tracks. No broken branches. The forest kept itself tidy the way a good housekeeper keeps a parlor—for guests, or for judgment.

Kestrel walked differently here than she did anywhere else. In towns she hunched, made herself small, watched doorways. On the high passes she moved like a climber, economical and wary. But under these trees she lifted her chin. Her steps lengthened. Twice Dominic caught her trailing her fingers along the pale bark of the nameless trees, and twice the bark seemed to lean very slightly toward her touch, the way a cat leans into a known hand.

She's coming home, Dominic thought, and the thought hurt in a place he'd thought long scarred over. She's coming home and I'm leading her deeper into the thing I helped break.

By midday—or what he judged was midday; the canopy made its own weather—the humming had resolved into something with structure. Not melody exactly. More like many voices reading the same long text at different speeds, overlapping, agreeing. And threaded through it, unmistakable now even to his unaided ear, was the old tongue—the pre-kingdom speech he knew only from thousand-year-old bones and from one mute child who had spoken it at his door in a blizzard.

Kestrel had gone still. Her lips moved. Nothing came out—they never did—but her mouth shaped the syllables, following along, and Dominic realized with a chill that walked down his spine that she wasn't translating. She was *reading*. The way a literate man reads a sign by the roadside. This language that existed nowhere in the living world except inside her was, to her, simply *text*.

"What does it say?" he asked. His voice sounded rude in the green light, loud as a dropped plate.

Kestrel considered. Then she knelt, drew in the moss with one finger—a spiral, and lines radiating out from it like sun-rays—and pointed to each line, then to herself, then spread her arms wide to take in the whole forest.

*Everything comes back to the center,* Dominic guessed. Or: *everything grows from one root.* He nodded slowly. "And the center is the stone."

Her expression said: obviously.

They found the clearing an hour later, though *found* was generous. The forest simply ended around it, the trees drawing back in a perfect circle forty yards across, and in the circle's heart stood the second covenant-stone.

It was nothing like the drowned bell they'd raised beneath Greyford's quarry. That stone had been small enough to hold, dense as grief, singing one clear note. This one was a pillar—twice Dominic's height, grown rather than carved, its surface a braid of wood and mineral and something glassy that caught the green light and bent it. Roots thicker than his waist coiled around its base and ran outward into the earth like the guy-ropes of a tent, and Dominic understood suddenly that the entire forest was anchored to this point. Every tree. Every rooted dead soul sleeping upright in the dark. All of it tethered here, to this one braided spine of the old covenant.

And the stone was dying.

Not shattered, like the first had been. *Starving.* Its glassy surfaces had gone cloudy, its braid unraveling at the top into splinters, and the great roots around its base were gray and brittle as old rope. The choir's long note wavered around them, and now that he listened properly, Dominic could hear what he should have heard from the treeline: the song was fraying. Voices dropping out one by one, gaps opening in the harmony like missing teeth.

Kestrel made a sound. Small, involuntary, wounded—the first noise Dominic had ever heard her make that wasn't breath. She took three steps toward the stone before his hand on her arm stopped her.

"Wait." His mouth had gone dry. "Look."

Between them and the stone, the ground was moving.

It was subtle at first—moss shifting, ferns folding flat—but then the soil itself began to rise in shapes. Shoulders. Heads. Figures pulling themselves up out of the earth the way reeds rise out of a river, unhurried, inevitable. They were human-shaped but not human-colored: bark-skinned, leaf-haired, their faces smooth as uncarved wood with only the faintest suggestion of features, like faces remembered by someone who hadn't seen them in years. There were dozens of them. Then more. They rose in a slow ring around the clearing's edge, facing inward, and the choir's note deepened beneath them until Dominic felt it in his sternum, in his teeth, in the fillings of his jaw.

One figure stepped forward. Taller than the rest. Its bark-face bore actual features—shallow-carved eyes, a mouth-line—and when it spoke, the voice came not from its mouth but from everywhere, from the roots, from the ground, from the trees ringing the clearing.

**WHO COMES TO THE CHOIR?**

Dominic's knees wanted to bend. Old instinct, older than training: kneel before the dead who kept faith. He mastered it and bowed instead, shallow and deliberate, the way one bows to an equal who has earned respect.

"Dominic Nystrom," he said. "Bone-carver. Late of the covenant's keeping." Beside him, Kestrel stepped forward, and he felt the whole clearing's attention swing to her like a lens focusing. "And Kestrel."

The rooted dead did not move. But every tree in the forest leaned—one degree, two degrees—inward, toward the child, and the green light thickened, and the choir's wavering note steadied for one impossible moment into something clean and whole, the way a fire flares when a door opens somewhere in the house.

Then it guttered again, and the tall one spoke.

**THE SEED RETURNS. AND IT BRINGS A BONE-SPEAKER.** A pause, long as a season. **WE KNOW YOUR KIND, DOMINIC NYSTROM. WE KNOW WHAT YOUR HANDS DID WHEN THE COVENANT FELL.**

"I know what my hands did," Dominic said. His voice came out level, which cost him. "That's why we're here. The stones are failing. All four. Something is eating the memory out of the world, and if the last anchor goes, there won't be anything left to remember either realm alive. I need to raise this stone. We need to raise it. Together."

**THE STONE CANNOT BE RAISED BY WILL ALONE.** The tall figure's carved eyes fixed on him. **IT IS ANCHORED IN REMEMBRANCE. IT FEEDS ON WHAT THE LIVING GIVE UP FREELY AND FOREVER. THIS WAS ALWAYS ITS PRICE. YOU KNOW THIS.**

Dominic knew. He'd known since Greyford, since the drowned bell had risen to Kestrel's song and for one breath he'd smelled river-water and chapel incense from a day forty years gone. The covenant-stones weren't mechanisms. They were *wounds that had been made holy*—each one fed on sacrifice, on something given up that could never be taken back.

At Greyford, Kestrel's own nature had been payment enough. Here, the rooted dead would want more.

"What do you ask?" he said.

**A MEMORY,** said the choir, all of them, the ground itself speaking. **ONE MEMORY, FREELY GIVEN, NEVER RETURNED. NOT A STORY. NOT A NAME. THE THING ITSELF, ROOT AND BRANCH, AS A TREE GIVES ITS WHOLE SELF TO THE SOIL.**

* * *

They sat at the clearing's edge while Dominic thought, because the rooted dead permitted retreat but not departure—if they turned back now, the ring of figures would simply be trees again, and the path out would be the path in, and no harm done except the slow death of everything. Kestrel sat close against his side, her shoulder pressed to his arm, watching him with those dark eyes that missed nothing.

He laid out his memories the way he'd once laid out tools on a workbench, turning each over, weighing it.

His father's face—he couldn't. That face was already half-gone, worn smooth by years of deliberate not-looking, and what remained was load-bearing. His master Aldric's laugh, the smell of the workshop shavings, the particular way the old man had said *measure twice, carve once, and listen always*—no. Those were the bones of his craft. Take them and he became a man who carved shapes without meaning, and a meaningless carver was useless to Kestrel, to the stones, to all of it.

The night the covenant fell. He flinched away from that one so fast it was almost comic—the rooted dead would likely refuse it anyway, poisoned as it was with the Warden's deception. Guilt was not remembrance. Guilt was just guilt wearing remembrance's coat.

And then, unbidden, the way the true things always come unbidden:

His mother, singing.

Not her face—he could barely hold her face anymore, it had been thirty years and grief had done its smoothing work. But her voice. Her voice was intact. Her voice was the first voice he had ever heard speak the old tongue, back when the covenant lived and border-villages still sang the planting songs without shame, before the purges, before the burning of the songbooks. She'd sung while she cooked, while she mended, while she walked him through the winter market with his mittens tied to his sleeves by string so he couldn't lose them. She'd sung the lullaby about the garden under the world, the one that began *sleep, small root, the dark is kind*—

He could hear it now, note-perfect, the exact grain of her voice, low and a little smoky, the way it caught on the third line every single time because she always smiled there and smiling changed the sound.

If he gave it up, he would not remember that he had given it up. That was the cruelty in the arithmetic. The rooted dead took the memory *and the knowledge of the taking*. He would walk out of this forest with a hole he couldn't see, shaped exactly like his mother's voice, and he would never know why certain songs made him leave rooms.

Beside him, Kestrel had gone rigid. He glanced down and found her staring at him, and her eyes were wet, and her hands were moving fast, urgent, angry.

*No. Not that one. Choose another. I've seen it—I see it when you sleep sometimes, your lips move, you're hearing someone sing—don't give me that look, Dominic, don't you dare—*

"It has to be worth the price," he said quietly. "That's how the toll works. If I pay cheap, the stone rises cheap, and a cheap anchor falls to the first hard frost. You know it's true. You felt the bell at Greyford—you know what full strength feels like." He looked back at the dying pillar, at the gray roots, at the ring of patient dead. "My mother's voice sang the old tongue. It's the most covenant-soaked thing I own. It's the only payment worthy of the asking."

Kestrel grabbed his hand. Her grip was shockingly strong for her size, and her fingers were cold. She pressed his palm flat against her own chest, over her heart, then pointed at him, then at his chest, then she mimed—clumsy, furious, desperate—singing. Mouth open, hand rising and falling in the shape of a melody.

*I will keep it,* she was saying. *Give it to me. Give it to me and I will carry it.*

"You can't carry what doesn't exist anymore," Dominic said gently. "That's not how giving works. That's not how *loss* works."

But she was shaking her head before he finished, and there was something in her face he'd never seen there before—something fierce and absolute, the same thing he imagined lived in the hearts of people who walked into burning houses. She pulled his knife from his belt—she'd learned to handle it on the road, cutting cord, whittling kindling—and before he understood what she intended she had pressed the blade's flat side against her own forearm, hard enough to blanch the skin, and pointed at the faint mark, and then at him.

*A mark fades. A scar stays. Let me be your scar.*

Dominic stared at her. The choir waited. The green light held its breath.

And he understood, finally, what he had been refusing to understand since a frozen child knocked on his door: that love was not a debt he owed the dead alone. That the living could inherit. That a memory given up was not necessarily a memory destroyed—it could be *entrusted*, passed hand to hand like flame from candle to candle, and the rooted dead might take the original from his mind but they could not reach into hers, not without her consent, and she was the seed of the Garden itself, and what the Garden's seed promised to hold, perhaps the whole dreaming world would help her hold.

"It might not work," he warned her. "You might lose it too. Memory isn't a purse you can divide."

Kestrel lifted her chin in exactly the way she'd lifted it under the trees that morning. *Home* in her posture. *Certainty.*

"All right," Dominic said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he let it. "All right."

* * *

They rose together and walked to the center of the clearing, to the base of the dying stone, and the rooted dead closed around them—not threateningly, but the way water closes around a wader, total and calm. The tall one stood before them, and the ground beneath Dominic's feet hummed with the attention of every buried faithful soul in the forest.

**SPEAK THE GIVING,** the choir said. **NAME IT, AND MEAN IT, AND LET IT GO.**

Dominic knelt. He put one palm flat against the stone's braided surface, and it was warm—faintly, desperately warm, like a body fighting fever—and through his hand he felt the stone feel *him*, felt it cataloguing what he carried, searching.

"I give the voice of my mother," he said. "Maren Nystrom, of Hollowbrook, who sang the old tongue to her son every night of his childhood. I give the sound of it. The songs. The lullaby about the garden under the world. I give it freely, forever, never to be returned to me." His throat closed. He forced the last of it out. "And I ask—" his eyes found Kestrel's, standing beside him with her fists clenched at her sides— "I ask that what is taken from me be given to *her*. To keep. Not as mine. As hers. As a trust."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats. Four.

Then the tall rooted one inclined its head, slowly, like a tree bending in wind, and the choir's note shifted into a chord Dominic had never heard but recognized instantly, the way you recognize a relative's footsteps on a stair.

**SO BE IT. THE VOICE OF MAREN OF HOLLOWBROOK, GIVEN BY HER SON, ENTRUSTED TO THE SEED. THE STONE ACCEPTS.**

The roots around the pillar's base flushed gray to brown to a deep, living red-brown, color flooding back into them like dawn coming up over a valley. The glassy surfaces cleared. And the memory—

It went the way a tide goes. No pain. That was the terrible part. One moment the lullaby was there, whole, *sleep, small root, the dark is kind*, his mother's smoke-and-honey voice catching on the third line because she always smiled there—and the next moment there was a space in him where it had been, a room-shaped absence, and he knew only that something had been in it, and that Kestrel's hand was gripping his so hard her nails left crescents in his skin, and that he was crying without knowing why.

He would not know why ever again. He made himself accept that in the same breath the memory left, made himself nod at the tall figure, made himself say, "It's done," in a voice that sounded like someone else's.

The stone woke.

Light ran up the braid like fire up a fuse, and the choir *broke open*—the long held note resolving at last into the hymn it had been holding for a hundred years, hundreds of voices, thousands, pouring out of the ground and the trees and the air itself, and the rooted dead raised their smooth faces and the carved mouths opened and they sang in the old tongue, and Kestrel sang with them, silent-lipped, tears streaming, her free hand pressed to the stone as the second anchor of the covenant blazed back to life beneath her palm.

Dominic knelt in the middle of it, hollowed and full at once, weeping for a reason he would spend the rest of his life not remembering.

Later—much later, when the light had gone amber and the rooted dead had sunk back into the earth with something like peace settling over the clearing—Kestrel sat beside him against the warm flank of the risen stone. She took his hand again, gentler now, and turned it over, and with his own knife she scratched two small marks into the skin of his palm, shallow, careful, the kind of cut that scars.

He looked at them. Two lines, side by side. A mystery.

"Forget-me-not," she mouthed, and tapped the marks, and tapped her own chest, and her eyes said the rest: *I have it. I have all of it. Sleep, small root. The dark is kind.*

Dominic closed his fist around the marks and, for the first time since Hollowbrook burned, slept without dreams.

He woke at moonrise to Kestrel shaking his shoulder, her face lit silver and stricken, pointing east through the trees—where, above the far ridgeline, the stars were going out in a slow spreading wave, as if something vast and cold were drinking the sky.

And on the wind, faint but unmistakable, came the smell of a chapel after snowfall.
