## 14. The Bargain at the Ice Chapel

The chapel had been a smuggler's waystation once, back when the border roads still breathed. Dominic remembered it from the years before the fall—a low stone building tucked into the shoulder of Mount Cael, its walls furred with moss, its bell-porch crowded with travelers' offerings: ribbons, buttons, the occasional carved bird left in thanks for safe passage. He had slept there twice himself, decades ago, with his pack under his head and the wind worrying at the shutters like an old dog.

Now it was made of ice.

Not snowed upon, not glazed by storm—*made* of it. Every wall stood smooth and translucent as river glass, thick enough that the moonlight coming through came down green and blue and slow, so that walking inside felt like wading into deep water. The rafters were icicles grown straight as beams. The altar was a single block of frozen spring water, and inside it, suspended like a fly in amber, hung the old waystation's iron bell—still, silent, holding its clapper mid-swing as if the frost had caught it one heartbeat before ringing.

Kestrel stopped on the threshold and would not go further.

She didn't need to speak. She hadn't spoken more than a handful of words since Greyford, and those had been in the old tongue, syllables that sat in the ear like seeds in soil. But her silence had a grammar all its own now, and what it said here was simple: *wrong place, wrong cold, turn around.*

"We're not turning around," Dominic said quietly. "The second stone's road runs through this valley. There's no other crossing before the pass closes." He said it because it was true, and he kept his eyes on the frozen bell while he said it, because if he looked at her he would see her trust sitting there like an open hand, and his hands wanted very badly to be somewhere else.

They had been three days out of the Choir of Roots. Three days of Kestrel humming his mother's melody under her breath—not perfectly, not the way his mother had sung it, but faithfully, the way you carry something breakable—and three days of Dominic feeling the shape of the hole where that voice used to live. It was strange grief, grieving a memory he'd given away willingly, for a good reason, to rooted dead who guarded their door jealously. Stranger still that the grief was lighter than he'd expected. A debt paid is not the same as a debt forgiven, but it rhymes.

He'd told her about the toll, of course. He wasn't a coward about that much. *They took my mother's voice,* he'd said, and she had taken his hand without looking at him, which was somehow worse and better than any words.

What he had not told her—what he could not yet find a shape for—was why the rooted dead had let them pass so easily after. Why the eldest of them, a woman grown into birchbark, had leaned close to Dominic's ear and whispered: *She knows where you are, carver. She has always known. Go carefully at the ice chapel.*

He had assumed it was a warning against the Warden's servants.

It was not a warning against her servants.

* * *

"She's waiting," Kestrel signed—or rather, she made the small gesture they had built between them over the weeks, two fingers walking across an open palm, *someone ahead*. Then she touched her own throat and shook her head once. *Not for me. For you.*

"I know," Dominic said.

And he did know, the way you know weather in your bones before the clouds arrive. The chapel's cold was not the dumb cold of altitude. It was attentive. It had been waiting for him specifically, the way a snare waits for a particular foot.

"Stay by the door," he told her. "Whatever happens in there, whatever you hear—stay by the door."

Her chin came up. She pointed at him, then at herself, then pressed both fists together: *together*, the sign they'd made on the rope bridge, after the freezing, after the night neither of them had spoken of since. His chest did something painful and complicated.

"Together," he agreed. "But I go first."

* * *

Inside, the ice sang.

That was the only word for it. As he crossed the threshold the whole structure began to resonate, a low harmonic hum that rose through his boot-soles and settled behind his teeth, and Dominic understood that the chapel was not a building at all. It was an instrument. She had built it the way he built carvings—with intent, with craft, with love, if you could call what she loved by that name anymore.

"You always did come when called," said the Warden of Winter. "Even as a boy. I only had to whistle."

She stood at the altar with her back to him, hands folded, watching the frozen bell as though it might finish its swing if she were patient enough. She wore white—not the white of snow but the white of bone, of paper, of things bleached past saving—and her hair had gone the same color since the last time he'd seen her, which had been through a closing door, eleven years ago, on the worst day of his life.

"Master," he said, because the word was in his mouth before he could stop it, worn into the grooves of him like a cart-track.

"Don't." She turned. Her face was still hers—the sharp cheekbones, the gray eyes that had measured every apprentice's work and found most of it wanting—but there was frost in the creases now, actual frost, fine as sugar, and when she moved the light bent strangely around her, as if the air itself had learned caution. "You're not my apprentice anymore, Dominic. You haven't been since you ran. Call me Sera, or call me nothing."

"Sera." It tasted like a name from someone else's story. "You killed the covenant."

"I ended a disease," she said mildly. "But we'll get to theology later. Sit. Please." She gestured, and a bench of clear ice slid from the wall, polished, waiting, exactly the height he liked to work at. Of course she knew the height. She had taught him to carve. "You must be tired. You've been walking my mountains for weeks, sleeping rough, eating whatever the shepherds' charity provides. And carrying—" her gaze flicked toward the doorway, toward the small dark shape of the girl silhouetted against green moonlight, "—so much more than a pack."

Dominic did not sit.

"The rooted dead say hello," he said. "They also say you've been listening."

"I listen to everything now. It's the privilege of stillness." She drifted along the altar, fingertips trailing the ice, leaving no mark. "Do you know what I was doing, the day you helped me? Do you remember what I told you?"

He remembered everything. That was the cruelty of his gift—he remembered even the things he wished to amputate. "You said the Garden was bleeding into the world. That the boundary had gone septic. That dreams were getting out that shouldn't be out." He swallowed. "You showed me the hollowed villages. The people standing in fields, empty, smiling at nothing. You said the Garden was eating them."

"And you believed me."

"You were my teacher. You had never lied to me."

"No," she agreed, and something moved behind her face, brief and glacial, that might have been regret in a warmer creature. "I hadn't. That was the last honest season of my life, I think. I did believe it, then. The Garden was sick. It *is* sick—it has been dying for a century, slowly, the way an orchard dies when no one tends it. And I thought the cure was surgery." She lifted her hand, and between her fingers a single snowflake formed, perfect, six-armed, rotating slowly. "I have refined my diagnosis since."

"The villages weren't eaten by the Garden."

"They were eaten by forgetting. Which is the same hunger wearing different clothes." The snowflake fell; before it reached the floor it became a memory—Dominic saw it happen, saw the air itself remember: a kitchen, bread, a woman laughing, then the image graying, thinning, going out like a coal. "Every soul that crosses into stillness forgets first what hurt it, then what comforted it, then finally that it ever loved anything at all. I have simply... accelerated the process. Chosen who forgets, and what, and when. Order instead of rot. Is that so monstrous?"

"Yes," Dominic said.

She smiled, and it was the smile he had trusted across twenty years of apprenticeship, and that was the most frightening thing in the room. "You always were quick to judge and slow to count the cost. Very well. No theology. Let's talk trade."

* * *

She walked him, then, through what she had built.

With a touch she woke the chapel's ice, and it became a window—not onto the present, but onto possibility. Dominic saw himself young again, or rather saw the workshop as it had been before: the shelves full of voices, the kettle singing, the particular amber light of Thornfell autumn through south-facing glass. He heard them. Saints preserve him, he heard them—his mother's laugh layered under the melody Kestrel had been humming for three days; old Tam Voss complaining about the price of tallow; the drowned ferryman of Greyford singing his slow river song; every dead man and woman whose bones had passed through his hands, whose last words he carried like stones in a sack.

All of it restored. Not remembered—*restored*. Living voices, living warmth, the covenant unbroken and the Garden breathing and the border roads open and the world as it had been before the winter, before the fall, before the girl.

"All you have to do," Sera said softly, "is bring me the seed."

The ice-window held the image steady. His mother turned, in it, and looked directly at him, and said his name.

"That's not her," he said. His voice cracked on the second word. "That's a carving you made of her. You're doing to me what I do to bones."

"Yes," she said, without shame. "I learned it from you, after all. Or you from me—the lineage hardly matters now. The question, carver, is whether the copy is worth less than the original. You've spent your life telling mourners that the voice in the bone is real. That the dead persist in what remembers them. Was that a lie too? Because if it was a lie, then your whole life's work was theft. And if it was true—" she spread her hands, and the workshop in the ice went on glowing, warm, eternal, "—then I am offering you the truth, forever, and all it costs is one child's delivery."

"One child," Dominic repeated.

"A seed," she corrected. "A thing that wants, more than anything, to be planted. I'm offering it a garden of stillness. Perfect preservation. Nothing it loves will ever die, because nothing will ever change. Isn't that what growing things want? Permanence?"

"No," Dominic said. "It isn't. Nothing that grows wants a glass case."

"Then it can refuse," she said, and shrugged, elegant, terrible. "After it's delivered. After the fourth stone is frozen and the rite is done and the choice is academic. Bring me the seed, Dominic. In exchange: your mother's voice. Your workshop. Your dead, alive in your ears until the sun burns out. Everything the winter took from you, returned with interest." She stepped close, close enough that the cold coming off her raised the hair on his arms, and her gray eyes were wet, actually wet, and he realized with a lurch that she was not entirely lying either—that some ruined corner of her still meant this as a gift. "You think I don't know what exile costs. I have been exiled from everything, boy. From time itself. Take the bargain. Be warm again. Let an old woman carry the cold alone."

For a long moment—one heartbeat, two, ten—Dominic Nystrom stood in a chapel of ice and let himself want it.

That was the shame of it, afterward. Not that he refused. That he *wanted*, openly, greedily, the way a starving man wants bread he knows is poisoned. He wanted his mother's voice so badly his teeth ached. He wanted to sit at his bench with the kettle singing and the shelves full of the beloved dead. He wanted the eleven wasted years back, the loneliness unworn, the armor set aside.

And beneath all of it, quieter, meaner: he wanted his guilt returned too. If the covenant was restored by her hand, then his part in breaking it was erased. No more nights staring at the ceiling cataloguing his betrayals. Sera was offering absolution bundled with resurrection, and both together nearly undid him.

What broke the spell was small. It was the sound of Kestrel shifting her weight on the threshold—just leather on stone, just a child tired of standing—but it came through the ice-hum clearly, and it reminded him what the delivery would cost. Not him. Her. A seed planted in a glass case. A girl who had crossed a mountain range mute and hunted, who had sung a drowned bell up out of black water, who had promised to carry his mother's melody so he wouldn't have to grieve it alone—who trusted him with fists pressed together, *together*—

"No," Dominic said.

Sera closed her eyes briefly, as if he had confirmed something she'd already known. "No," she echoed. "Of course no. You were always stubborn past the point of wisdom." She opened her eyes, and the warmth in them was gone, packed away like tools at dusk. "Then understand what remains. The rite proceeds regardless. The fourth stone falls within the month, and when it does, the boundary between memory and death freezes solid, and every soul in both realms begins losing its dead—not gradually, carver. All at once. Mothers will look at their children's graves and feel nothing. Husbands will forget they were ever married. Your gift will become a curse, because the voices in your bones will fade to static, and you will sit in your workshop surrounded by relics of people you cannot recall loving." She smiled again, thinner now. "Unless you bring her to me. The offer stands until the rite completes. I am patient. Stillness is my whole nature."

"Why tell me all this?" Dominic asked. "Why show me the workshop, the voices? You must know it changes nothing."

"It changes one thing." She turned back to the frozen bell, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Now you'll spend every day wondering. Now the temptation lives in you, and it will grow, because I put it there and I grow things well. That, Dominic, is not a bargain. That is a seed of my own." She glanced back at him, one last gray glance. "Go. Take the north road. The rooted dead will let you pass—I've asked them to. Consider it a courtesy between colleagues."

* * *

He walked out of the chapel on legs that felt borrowed, and the cold of the ordinary night hit him like mercy.

Kestrel was waiting exactly where he'd left her, and the moment he emerged she was on her feet, hands moving fast: *What did she say? What did she want? Are you hurt?*

"She talked," Dominic said. "Old women talk. She wanted to know if I'd reconsider my loyalties. I told her no. We should move—the north road's open, and I don't want to be in this valley when she decides to stop being courteous."

Kestrel studied him. She had gotten good at studying him; weeks of silence will do that, will make a person read faces the way he read grain and marrow. Her eyes went from his eyes to his hands—they were shaking, he realized, and shoved them in his pockets—to the chapel door behind him, where the green light still pulsed faintly, patiently, like a heart under glass.

Then she signed, slowly: *What did she offer you?*

And Dominic Nystrom, who had spent his life listening to the dead because the living were too hard to face, looked at the child who trusted him and told his first lie.

"Nothing I wanted," he said.

Something flickered across her face—hurt, or doubt, or the beginning of the arithmetic children do when the adults around them start keeping secrets—and then she let it go, or seemed to, and fell into step beside him as they climbed out of the valley toward the north road and the third stone beyond it.

Behind them, in the chapel of ice, the frozen bell hung motionless in its block of still water, one heartbeat from ringing, and waited—as she waited, as the winter waited—for the seed to be delivered, or the world to end, whichever came first.

Dominic did not look back. But all that night, as they climbed, he caught himself humming his mother's melody without meaning to, testing the silence where her voice had been, measuring how much of him the temptation had already filled—and he did not notice that Kestrel, walking beside him in the dark, had begun to watch him the way she watched the mountains before a storm.
