## 15. Kestrel Unbound

The village had no name anymore, because names were the first thing the frost took.

Kestrel felt it as they came down the last slope—a silence with weight to it, like a hand pressed flat over a mouth. Below them, in the cup of a valley where a river should have been singing, forty-odd houses huddled under snow that had not fallen so much as *arrived*, settling over rooftops in perfect, unbroken sheets. Smoke stood up from the chimneys in straight grey columns that did not waver. The river itself was frozen mid-curl, a wave of white glass holding the shape of its own laughter.

"They're alive," Dominic said beside her. His breath did not fog. That was wrong too—she was learning to read the wrongness now, the way she'd once read faces before her voice went away. "The Warden doesn't kill what she freezes. Killing is a kind of change. She just... stops it."

Kestrel looked at him. He'd been quieter since the ice chapel, since whatever he wasn't telling her had started living behind his eyes like a splinter. She could feel it when their shoulders brushed on the road: a small cold knot in him, kept turned away from her on purpose.

*You're hiding something,* she thought at him, and, as always, said nothing.

He glanced at her anyway, the way people did lately, as if her silence had gotten louder.

They went down into the village together.

* * *

The stillness inside the houses was worse than outside.

The first door opened at Kestrel's touch—not locked, just *unattended*, the way things are left when everyone has stepped out of the world for a moment and expects to step back in. Inside: a kitchen. A pot of porridge gone to grey glue on a stove burned down to coals that neither died nor warmed. A woman sat at the table with a wooden spoon halfway to her mouth, and a boy of maybe six sat across from her, mid-giggle, his face caught in the amber of some joke Kestrel would never hear.

She knew them. Not their names—but she knew the *shape* of them, the way you know a song from three notes. The Garden hummed under everything, and these two were part of its music, and the music had been stopped with a palm laid across the strings.

"Careful," Dominic murmured behind her. "Don't touch—"

She touched.

It wasn't a decision. It was more like flinching toward warmth. Her fingers closed around the woman's wrist, and the power that lived in Kestrel's chest—the seed-thing, the growing thing, the thing that had frozen a man mid-leap off a rope bridge and frightened her more than any hunter—rose up through her arm like sap in spring.

And the grief came out first.

That was the thing she hadn't understood until that moment, standing in a frozen kitchen with a stranger's pulse stuttering awake under her thumb: grief was the frost's favorite food. The Warden didn't freeze villages because she hated them. She froze them because frozen things couldn't hurt, and every stillness here was built on top of an ache. The woman at the table had lost someone—Kestrel could feel it, a hollow in her the size of a person, worn smooth by years—and the frost had come for her the way water comes for a crack, filling the wound and making it solid.

So Kestrel melted the wound instead.

It poured out of the woman like dark water finding a drain. Kestrel felt it pass through her own chest on the way—every grief she'd ever swallowed, hers and borrowed, rising to meet it—and then it was simply *gone*. Unmade. Not healed, not carried, not set down somewhere safe. Erased, the way frost erases a footprint.

The woman blinked. The spoon completed its journey to her mouth. She chewed, swallowed, frowned at the porridge as if she couldn't remember why she'd been sad about anything, ever.

"Mama!" The boy was moving, laughing, alive, the giggle finishing itself across years. He threw his arms around her and she caught him and spun him and her laugh was bright and whole and *hollow*—

Because Kestrel could see it now, from the inside, where the grief used to be. The hollow hadn't filled. It had been *scraped clean*, down past the ache, down past the memory of the ache, and the woman's love had been sitting at the bottom of that well the whole time, tangled in the roots of her sorrow like a flower grown through a fence. When the sorrow went, it took the flower.

The woman loved her son. Kestrel could feel that, faintly, the way you feel sun through a window. But it was distant now. Polite. The love of someone remembering a story about love rather than feeling it.

"Oh," Kestrel said. Or tried to. Her throat made no sound at all, which was somehow right, because there was nothing in her worth saying.

"What did you do?" Dominic's voice, very quiet, very careful.

She let go of the woman's wrist. The woman smiled at them both, pleasant as weather, and asked if they'd come about the room upstairs, they let it cheap since her husband passed—

She stopped. Frowned. Tilted her head like a bird hearing a far-off sound.

"Passed," she repeated. "Did he? How strange. I can't seem to feel badly about it."

* * *

They walked out of that house and into the next one, and the next, because Kestrel could not stop.

That was the terrible truth of it, the thing she would carry afterward like a stone in her shoe: it felt *good*. The power moved through her like a thaw after a long winter, and every frozen grief she touched was a knot of pain begging to be undone, and who was she to refuse? A man frozen at a graveside with tears stopped on his cheeks—undone, and then he blinked at the grave-marker with mild curiosity, as if visiting a stranger. An old woman stopped mid-prayer—undone, and her prayers afterward were recitations, correct and empty, words with the music boiled out of them.

House by house. Person by person. Kestrel moved through the village like a lamp through a dark room, and everywhere her light fell, the shadows of sorrow fled, and the love that lived inside the sorrow fled with them.

She told herself she was saving them.

She told herself the Warden had done this, not her—that the frost had already taken these people, and she was only giving them back.

But the frost had left the love intact. Frozen, yes. Stopped, yes. But *there*, waiting under the ice like seeds under snow, ready to wake whole whenever spring came. What Kestrel was doing was not spring. Spring melts. Kestrel *burned*—a clean, white, rootless fire that consumed the wound and the treasure both, leaving the villagers upright and breathing and gently, terribly vacant, smiling at their children the way you smile at a neighbor's cat.

Dominic followed her. She was distantly aware of that—of him moving door to door behind her, saying her name, then not saying it, then simply watching with an expression she refused to turn around and look at. At some point he began pulling people gently away from her reach, steering them into side rooms, and she hated him a little for it, and hated herself more for the hatred, because she knew—he was right, he was *right*—and the power in her chest did not care about right. The power only cared that there was still frost in the world, and frost was pain, and pain was *food*.

By the time she reached the well at the center of the village square, the sun had moved. Hours. She had been at this for hours.

The well was frozen solid, and around it stood eleven villagers in a loose ring, and Kestrel realized, with a lurch, that they had gathered to mourn. There was a small grave beside the well, new-turned even under the snow, marked with a wreath of woven straw. A child's grave. And the eleven had come here, day after day, to stand together and grieve—to hold each other up under the weight of it, because that is what people do, that is the oldest covenant there is, older than stones, older than the Garden: *your sorrow is heavy, so I will carry one end.*

Kestrel felt all eleven griefs at once, braided together, and the thing inside her rose like a flood against a door.

*No,* she thought. *No, not this one. This one is theirs. This one is how they—*

Her hand was already reaching.

* * *

What stopped her was not Dominic's voice, though he was shouting now, or his hands, though they were on her shoulders, dragging her back. What stopped her was a bone.

His oldest carving hung at his neck on a leather cord, and as he pulled her around, it swung free of his collar and struck her collarbone—and the dead spoke through it, straight into the seed-place where her power lived, in that language older than kingdoms:

*Some things must be carried, little root. Some weights ARE the shape of the soul. Melt this, and there is nothing left for the spring to wake.*

Kestrel gasped—as close to a scream as her silenced throat allowed—and wrenched herself backward, out of Dominic's grip, out of reach of the well, out of reach of the eleven grieving villagers who stood frozen (by frost, only frost, *only frost*) around their child's grave.

She fell to her knees in the snow.

The power in her chest thrashed, starving, furious, lunging toward all that beautiful sorrow—and she held it the only way she knew how. She thought of the shepherd family's hearth. Of the lullaby the grandmother sang, half in the old tongue, about a garden sleeping under winter until someone brave enough to dream woke it. Of bread torn into four pieces and the smallest piece saved for her without being asked. Of being *counted*. Of belonging somewhere, to someone, for the first time since the frost took her voice and her mother and her name.

*If I burn their grief, I burn what grief holds. If I burn what grief holds, I am the Warden. I am exactly what she is, wearing a smaller coat.*

She knelt in the snow and shook, and did not touch anyone, and the power screamed in her like a kettle, and slowly, slowly, like a storm spending itself against a cliff, it quieted.

When she could finally lift her head, Dominic was crouched an arm's length away, having learned, apparently, not to touch her. His face was grey. Not with cold.

"You have to learn to stop," he said. No anger in it. Something worse. Fear. "Kestrel. Before we go another mile, you have to learn to stop."

She looked past him at the village. At the windows where people moved now, alive but thinned, loving each other at a polite distance, across a gap she had carved. At the woman in the first house, who would spend the rest of her life unable to remember why her husband's chair being empty felt like nothing at all.

At the small grave by the well, which she had spared, and which was the only thing in this valley she had not broken.

Her hands found snow and squeezed it, as if she could hold the cold accountable, as if she could give it back to the people she'd emptied. It melted between her fingers. Everything she touched melted, or burned, or woke, or hollowed. She was the last seed of the Garden, and she was beginning to understand what the Garden had never told anyone, what perhaps even the Garden did not know about itself:

Life, unchecked, does not preserve. Life *consumes*. Winter only wins because spring cannot help devouring everything in its path, given enough spring.

The Warden wasn't her opposite.

The Warden was what happened when someone saw this truth and chose to end the cycle—by ending everything.

And Kestrel, kneeling in the ruined quiet of a village she had unmade with kindness, finally understood why the frost had come for her first, before any other seed, before any other threat.

Not because she was the Garden's hope.

Because she was the same hunger, wearing green.

She looked up at Dominic, and her mouth shaped the old tongue word for *help*, soundless, and his face changed—because he heard it anyway, the way he heard the dead, the way he heard her—and behind the fear in his eyes she saw something else take root.

Resolve. The kind that comes before sacrifice.

He reached for the carved bone at his throat, and began, quietly, to speak to it.
