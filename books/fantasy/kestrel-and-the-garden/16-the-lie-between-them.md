## 16. The Lie Between Them

*Kestrel*

The moor above the tree line was the color of old iron, and the wind came off it in long gray sheets that smelled of snow that hadn't fallen yet. Kestrel walked with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves, and she did not look at Dominic.

She had been not-looking at him for two days.

It had started small, the way rot starts small—inside the wood, invisible, until the beam gives way. Two nights ago at the shepherd's hearth, while the family slept and the peat banked itself down to a red breathing glow, she had woken to voices. Not the dead. Dominic. He stood at the window with his back to the room, speaking low and fast into the dark as though the dark were listening, and she had caught only fragments through the fog of half-sleep: *I know what you took from me. I know what you're offering. Don't ask me again.*

She had thought it was grief talking. He spoke to his carved bones sometimes; she'd seen him do it, holding them to his ear like a man listening for rain. She had folded the words away and told herself they meant nothing.

But then there was the chapel at the ice-bound village, where he had gone alone and come out with frost on his shoulders that didn't melt for an hour. And the night on the moor's edge when she woke and his bedroll was empty and cold, and when he returned he said *checking the passes* without meeting her eyes, and the wind carried to her, faint as a thread, the smell of a place that should not exist above the snowline—the green smell of growing things, of roots, of the Garden.

And last night, in the ruined bothy where they sheltered, she had finally heard it whole.

He thought she was asleep. He sat by the fire with his oldest carving—the ivory bird, the one whose voice was older than kingdoms—and he held it the way a man holds a hand he is afraid to take, and he said, quietly, so quietly:

"She offered me everything back. My mother's voice. Marta. All of them, every soul I've ever carved, alive again behind my eyes. All I have to do is bring her the seed." A long silence, firelight crawling over his face. "And God help me, some part of me wants to say yes."

Kestrel had lain still as stone in the dark and felt something inside her go very quiet, the way a forest goes quiet before it falls.

The seed. Her.

* * *

Now the moor opened ahead of them like a gray sea, and the storm was building somewhere past its far edge—a bruise-colored wall of cloud stacked against the sky, swallowing the peaks one by one. They had perhaps three hours of light. Perhaps less.

Dominic walked a few paces ahead, choosing the path between the peat hags, his staff finding the firm ground with the ease of forty years in these mountains. He had been kind this morning. That was the worst part. He had made tea without being asked, had checked the straps of her pack twice, had smiled at her across the fire with a warmth that now looked, in the light of what she knew, like a man paying a debt he intended to default on.

"Storm's coming faster than I'd like," he called back. "There's a hollow past the ridge—we can shelter if it breaks early."

Kestrel stopped walking.

The wind pulled at her hood. Somewhere under the moor, deep beneath the peat and the stone, she could feel the faint pulse of the second covenant-stone they had raised together, the choir of roots singing its slow note of *held, held, held*. It steadied her. It reminded her what she was walking toward, and what she was.

"Dominic." Her voice came out rough from disuse. She saved her words the way he saved his memories—spending only what mattered. "At the bothy. Last night."

He turned. The wind flattened his gray hair against his skull. "What about it?"

"You talked to the bird." She watched his face the way she had learned to watch weather. "You talked about an offer."

For a moment—just a moment—his expression did nothing at all. It went smooth and closed as river ice, and she understood, with a sick lurch, that this was the face he wore when he was deciding whether to lie to her again.

"It's nothing," he said. "Old ghosts. Old grief. You know how the bones talk."

"The bones don't talk about seeds."

The word landed between them like a dropped stone. She saw it hit. She saw his knuckles whiten on the staff, saw the breath go out of him slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door seals.

"Kestrel—"

"How long?" She took a step toward him, and the wind took her hair out of the hood and whipped it wild. "How long has she been offering? How long have you known what she wants, and how long have you been—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, hated the smallness of it. "Was Greyford real? Was the bridge? Was any of it?"

"Every word of it was real." His voice came hard, and underneath the hardness something was shaking. "You don't get to decide what was real for me."

"Then tell me the truth!" It burst out of her louder than she'd meant, loud enough that the wind seemed to flinch around it, and beneath her feet she felt the moor answer—a tremor in the peat, a shiver in the heather, the old magic waking to her anger the way it always did now, unasked, unwanted. Frost bloomed in a ring around her boots, delicate ferns of ice spreading through the brown grass. "Tell me you didn't hide it. Tell me you weren't going to walk me to her and choose."

"I wasn't going to choose anything," he snarled, and now he was coming toward her, staff striking the ground, and his face had lost all its careful kindness and shown the raw thing beneath. "That's the whole point, girl. That's why I didn't tell you—because I knew you'd look at me exactly the way you're looking at me now!"

"So it's my fault you lied?"

"It's my fault I lied," he said, "and it's your fault I couldn't stop lying, because every time I look at you I see her offer standing behind you like a door, and I am *tired*, Kestrel. Do you understand tired? Forty years of quiet bones and empty rooms and every voice I ever loved locked in ivory, and she walks up to me in a frozen chapel and says *say the word and you'll never be lonely again*—" His voice broke clean in half. He turned his face away, jaw working, and when he spoke again it was barely above the wind. "I said no. I've said no every time. But I'm not going to pretend the wanting isn't there, because pretending is how she got me the first time."

The first time.

Kestrel stood very still. The frost ring spread another finger-length. Somewhere in her chest, beneath the fury, a colder thing was assembling itself out of pieces she hadn't wanted to hold: *he helped break the covenant.* The rooted dead had taken his mother's voice as toll and he had paid it without hesitation, guilt moving through him like water through a channel worn deep by years. She had felt the shape of that channel and never asked where it led.

"What did you do?" she whispered. "Before. With her. What did you do, Dominic?"

His silence lasted three heartbeats. Four. Long enough.

"That's not—"

"Don't." The word came out of her flat and final, and the storm chose that moment to arrive—not gradually, not politely, but all at once, the cloud-wall breaking over the moor like a wave, sleet driving sideways, the world contracting to ten feet of gray. "Don't tell me it's complicated. Don't tell me you'll explain later. Everyone who lies to me says those things."

"Because the truth takes longer than a storm!" he shouted over the wind. "Because if I tell you wrong—if I give you the shape of it before you're ready—you'll do what you're doing right now, which is stand in the open with winter coming down and your power climbing up your spine like fire, and you'll walk off this moor and straight into something that will eat you alive! I have hidden you, fed you, bled for you, and taught you everything I know, and I am allowed one secret that keeps you safe—"

"I'm not a secret you get to keep!" And here it was, the true wound, the one under all the others, and it came out of her in the old tongue because there were no words in the new one strong enough—*she heard herself say it in the language of roots and bells,* the syllables ringing off the stones, making the sleet shiver midair: **I am not yours to guard or spend.**

The moor heard her.

The ground bucked. Peat split along a seam twenty feet long, and through the crack came a smell of deep earth and green, a thread of root pushing up through the broken turf like a vein opening, curling toward her feet, hungry for her, answering her. The temperature fell so fast their breath froze into falling crystals. And in the storm, distantly, she felt the other thing too—the answering cold from the north, the Warden's attention turning toward this flare of power like a head turning toward a shout.

Dominic saw it. She watched him see it—watched the fear move across his face, not fear *of* her but fear *for* her, and that was almost worse, because it would have been easier to hate him if he were simply a traitor instead of a frightened old man who loved her badly.

"Kestrel," he said, gentler now, hands out, staff abandoned in the heather. "Listen to me. Whatever you're feeling right now, the mountain is feeling it too, and she will feel it from fifty miles. We need to get below the ridge. We need to—"

"No."

"We need to *move*—"

"*No.*" She stepped back from him, and the root withdrew with her, sliding back into the earth like a tongue into a mouth. "You want to keep me safe. You want to keep me *hidden*. You hid her offer, and you hid what you did, and you'd have hidden the third thing and the fourth until we reached the Garden's heart and you made your choice with my life as the coin." Her eyes were burning—from the sleet, she told herself, only the sleet. "So I'll make it easy for you. No more choices to make."

"Kestrel, the storm—"

"Will keep me company." She pulled her pack strap tight with fingers that shook and would not stop shaking. "You taught me the smuggler's roads. You taught me the signs. You said I was ready for the third stone." She backed away, one step, two, the sleet needling her face. "Maybe you lied about that too. We'll find out."

"Kestrel—*wait*—"

She turned and walked into the white.

* * *

*Dominic*

He lost her in ninety seconds.

Ninety seconds of shouting her name into a gale that ate sound whole, of stumbling through sleet-blind whiteness, of finding her footprints filling even as he watched them fill, and then nothing—no prints, no shape, no flicker of her gray cloak. Just the moor, and the storm, and himself.

He stood in the roaring white with his heart slamming and did the arithmetic he had spent two days refusing to do. She was heading north-northwest. North-northwest lay the Teeth of the World, the third stone, the canyon of wind—and also lay the Warden's reach, thickening by the day, the still-air servants moving through the high passes like frost moving through a wall. Alone, untrained in her own surges, furious enough to crack the ground open—she was a beacon lit in a country full of hunters.

And he had done this. Not the Warden. Him. He had hoarded his secrets like a miser because honesty cost more than he could pay, and now the bill had come due on a moor in a blizzard, and the currency was her.

"Marta," he said aloud, to no one, to the ivory bird in his pocket, to whatever dead might still be listening. "Marta, I've done it again. I've traded someone I love for something I was afraid to lose."

The wind answered with nothing.

He bent, stiff-kneed, and found her trail by feel rather than sight—the faint wrongness in the sleet where she had passed, the way the ice ferns she left behind melted slower than they should. He followed it north-northwest, into the teeth of the storm, and as he walked he made himself say the thing he had refused to say on the moor, out loud, to the empty white, practicing it like a man rehearsing a confession:

"I broke the covenant. She asked and I trusted her and I broke it, and everything since—the fall, the freezing, the forgetting—all of it grew from a seed I planted. And I let you walk into the storm rather than say it to your face."

The words vanished into the wind. That was the nature of confession in Thornfell; the mountains kept everything and forgave nothing.

Ahead, through a tearing in the sleet, he glimpsed the ridge line—black and jagged, and beyond it, impossibly far, the pale glint of the canyon the maps called the Teeth of the World. And on the ridge line, brief as a struck match, a figure: small, gray-cloaked, upright against the gale, walking with the terrible steady purpose of someone who has decided that alone is safer than deceived.

Then the storm closed, and she was gone.

Dominic Nystrom pulled his collar up, set his face north, and followed—knowing that the storm was the least of what hunted her, knowing that the Warden's servants moved fastest through exactly this kind of weather, knowing that if he reached her too late he would deserve it.

Behind him, far to the south, the second covenant-stone's song faltered—one note dropped from the choir, a hole in the harmony—and in the frozen fortress above the world, something patient opened its eyes.
