## 17. Third Stone: The Teeth of the World

The canyon had a voice before Kestrel reached it.

She heard it for half a day as she climbed the switchback trail out of the moor—wind funneling between two walls of black stone, a long low note that never quite resolved into silence. The shepherds on the high pasture had called it the Teeth of the World, and had refused to walk her even to the mouth of the pass. *The wind there remembers every name ever shouted into it,* the grandmother had said, pressing a heel of bread into Kestrel's hands. *It does not give them back.*

Kestrel went anyway. She had no name worth keeping, and nothing left to lose that the storm hadn't already taken.

Three days since the moor. Three days since Dominic's face when she'd found the folded paper in his pack—the Warden's offer written in frost-script that melted even as she read it. *Everything you have lost, restored. Bring me the seed.* Three days since he'd called her name and she had kept walking, because if she stopped, if she turned around, she would see in his eyes the same hunger the Warden wore like a crown, and she could not bear for him to be that.

The wind took her the moment she passed between the first pillars of stone.

It was not cold the way winter was cold. It was cold the way a held breath is cold—patient, expectant, full of everything that had ever been said here and never answered. Kestrel felt it move through her like fingers through water, tasting her, reading her. She pressed her palm against the canyon wall and listened with the part of herself that was older than kingdoms.

*Names,* the stone said. *Ten thousand years of names.*

And beneath them, deeper, wrapped in root and mineral and patient dark:

*A seed. Come home.*

She followed the sound. The canyon narrowed until she walked with both shoulders brushing rock, the sky above her a ribbon of bruised grey, and at the bottom of the defile, where the wind could not reach, the third covenant-stone waited.

It did not look like the drowned bell or the choir of roots. It looked like a tooth—a fang of white stone driven point-down into the earth, taller than a man, its surface carved so densely with script that the carvings blurred together like feathers. Frost crawled up its base in perfect spirals. Someone had been here. Someone had tried to still it.

But it was singing. Faintly. Stubbornly. A thread of the old tongue rising through the ice like breath through a keyhole.

Kestrel knelt and put both hands flat against the frozen ground.

*I'm here,* she said, without words, the way the Garden spoke. *I came.*

The stone's song stuttered. Strengthened. And then—

The wind stopped.

Not died. *Stopped*, all at once, the way a door closes, and in the ringing silence Kestrel heard footsteps on the ledge above her. Many feet. All of them landing at exactly the same interval, exactly the same weight, like the ticking of one enormous clock.

She knew what stood up there. She had seen them on the rope bridge, seen the man Dominic had frozen mid-leap, his face caught between fury and relief. The Warden's servants. People who had once grieved, once loved, once remembered—and had let the stillness take the grief because the stillness asked nothing of them.

They came down the canyon wall without hurry. Six of them, dropping from ledge to ledge, their boots finding holds no living climber would trust. Their faces were smooth as river stones. Frost bloomed from their mouths when they breathed, though they did not breathe often.

Kestrel backed away from the stone until the canyon wall met her spine.

One of them stepped forward—a woman, or what had been a woman, her hair rimed white, her eyes the grey of a lake under ice. When she spoke, her voice was many voices speaking in unison, flattened into one.

"The Warden offers mercy. Stillness is not death. Stillness is only sleep without dreams."

"I don't want to sleep," Kestrel said. Her voice cracked in the middle of it, and she hated how young she sounded. "I want to *grow*."

"Growing hurts."

"Yes," Kestrel said. "So does being forgotten."

The woman tilted her head, considering this with the slow gravity of something that had forgotten how to be quick. Then she raised her hand, and the six of them moved together, and the canyon filled with the sound of frost spreading through stone.

Kestrel did the only thing she knew how to do.

She sang.

It came out of her raw and wordless at first—not the careful syllables Dominic had taught her from the bones, but the language underneath the language, the one that lived in her chest like a second heartbeat. The old tongue. The tongue of seeds splitting open, of roots shouldering through clay, of green things insisting upward through snow. She sang at the frozen ground and the frost recoiled; she sang at the stone and moss burst from cracks that had been dry for centuries; she sang at the servants and—

Nothing.

The woman simply kept coming, frost sealing each wound Kestrel's song opened, and Kestrel understood with a lurching horror that her power could wake the sleeping but could not fight the walking dead. They were already past waking. There was nothing in them left to grow.

A hand closed around her wrist. Cold enough to burn.

"The Warden thanks you," the woman said, "for delivering yourself."

Kestrel screamed—not words, just sound, rage and terror and three days of walking away from the only person who had ever— and the scream went down through her palms, through the frozen earth, into the deep places where roots remember, and somewhere far below, something enormous turned over in its sleep.

The canyon floor split.

Roots came up through the crack like whales breaching—thick as oaks, pale as bone, writhing with a slowness that was somehow more terrifying than speed. They did not strike the servants. They simply *took hold*—coiling around arms and legs, lifting the six of them off the ground, holding them spread against the canyon walls like specimens pinned to a board. The frost-woman's face did not change. But her hand opened. Kestrel tore free and ran, and behind her she heard the servants begin, in unison, to speak the Warden's stillness into the roots themselves, and she heard the roots beginning to freeze, and she knew she had bought minutes, not hours.

She reached the stone. Put her hands on it. Sang the opening phrase of the raising-rite that the drowned bell had taught her—

And the frost took her feet.

It climbed her ankles in spirals, precise as calligraphy, and where it touched her the world went quiet—not numb, *quiet*, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters. She looked down and saw her own legs going grey and still, and understood, distantly, that this was how it ended. Not with a battle. With a slowing. The Warden didn't kill anything. She just made it stop wanting to move.

*Stillness is not death,* said the frost, in the woman's layered voice, right against her ear. *Stop fighting. You are so tired. Everyone you love has already stopped.*

"That's not true," Kestrel whispered.

"It will be easier if you stop."

"That's not *true*—"

Boots on stone. Fast ones. Uneven ones—one foot dragging slightly, the gait of a man who had walked too many miles on an old injury and refused to admit it.

Kestrel knew that limp the way she knew her own heartbeat.

Dominic came around the canyon bend at a dead run, coat torn at the shoulder, blood on his temple, three days of storm written across his whole exhausted body. He did not stop to speak. He did not stop to explain. He crossed the distance between them, dropped to his knees in the frost, and put his bare hands against the ice climbing her legs—and began to carve.

No knife. No tools. His fingernails, his fingertips, dragging lines through the frost faster than any blade could cut, and Kestrel realized with a jolt that he wasn't carving the ice at all. He was carving *words*. Old-tongue words, the script from his oldest bones, cutting them into the frost the way he cut them into ivory—and the frost, which had never in ten thousand years been *read* instead of obeyed, hesitated.

"You're supposed to be on the moor," Kestrel gasped.

"I know."

"You were supposed to go back for your mother's voice. The bargain. She offered you—" Her throat closed. "*Everything.*"

"I know." He didn't look up. The carving was reaching her knees now, and beneath his fingers the ice was softening, loosening, remembering how to be water. "I sat with it for a day and a night. Everything I've lost. My mother's voice. Marta's laugh. Every dead soul I ever carried." His hands were shaking—from cold, from effort, Kestrel couldn't tell. "And then I thought about what I'd actually be getting back. Voices. Echoes. Bones that talk." He finally raised his head, and his eyes were red-rimmed and furious and terrified and absolutely certain. "You're not an echo. You eat my terrible cooking and you hum when you think I can't hear and you get angry at me in complete sentences now. That's not memory. That's *alive*. And I chose wrong once before, Kestrel. I spent nine years paying for it. I am not choosing wrong twice."

The frost around her calves shattered.

She fell forward into him and he caught her, and for one moment neither of them said anything, because there was nothing either of them could say that his hands on the ice hadn't already said better.

Then the roots on the canyon walls finished freezing, and the six servants dropped lightly to the floor, and there was no more time.

"They'll keep coming," Dominic said, hauling her upright. "We can't outrun them and we can't fight them. So we do the only thing left." He nodded at the great white fang of the stone, its song nearly smothered now under spiraling frost. "We raise it. Now. Together."

"Together? Your gift hears the dead. Mine wakes the growing things. The rite needs—"

"—both," he finished. "That's why the covenant worked. That's why it broke. It was never meant to be held by one kind of voice." He grimaced. "The Warden knows that better than anyone. It's why she wants you alone."

Kestrel set her hands on the stone. Dominic set his beside hers, his scarred carver's fingers next to her small cold ones, and the stone's buried song rose to meet them like a dog hearing its name.

"On three," he said. "In the old tongue. I'll give you the anchor-words—you carry them up, I hold them down."

"How do you know the anchor-words?"

His jaw tightened. "Because I helped bury them," he said. "Nine years ago. I helped her seal this very stone. I remember every word of the closing-rite." He met her eyes, and did not flinch. "Every word I used to close a door, I can use to open one. That's the one good thing about what I did. Let's finish it."

He spoke first—low, rough, the closing-rite run backward, each syllable a chisel-blow—and the frost on the stone shivered. Kestrel caught the words as they left him and lifted them, transposed them, turned closure into opening the way a seed turns darkness into stem, and sang them back into the stone's heart.

The fang rang.

Not a bell's ring—a *tooth's* ring, a vibration that came up through the bedrock and out through the canyon walls, and the wind that had been holding its breath for ten thousand years finally exhaled. Names came pouring off the walls in streams of glittering air: every shout, every prayer, every last word ever given to the Teeth of the World, released all at once. The frost spirals peeled away like bark. The stone blazed white, then gold, then a green so deep it was almost black, and through the canyon floor Kestrel felt the second stone answer from the distant forest, and the drowned bell answer from beneath Greyford, and all three notes rise together into a chord that made her teeth ache and her eyes flood and her chest feel too small for what was happening inside it.

The servants froze—but not by their mistress's will. The chord passed through them, and whatever the stillness had built in them cracked along old fault lines, and for one moment Kestrel saw their faces as they must have been: frightened people, grieving people, people who had once been loved. The frost-woman looked directly at Kestrel, and her layered voice collapsed into a single human voice, small and hoarse.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, I had a *daughter*—"

Then the chord faded, and the stillness rushed back into them, and they turned as one and fled up the canyon walls, faster than they had come, carrying that single broken word back to their mistress like a splinter working toward the heart.

Silence, after. Real silence, warm-edged, alive.

Kestrel slid down against the stone and sat in the wet grass that hadn't existed an hour ago. Dominic lowered himself beside her with a groan, and for a while they just breathed.

"You followed me," she said finally.

"You walked into the worst canyon in Thornfell alone," he said. "Someone had to follow you. It's practically my job."

"I'm not sorry." She picked at the grass. "For reading the letter. For leaving. I'm not sorry I was angry."

"I know." He was quiet a moment. "You shouldn't be. I hid it because I was ashamed, and shame is just fear wearing a coat, and I've got nine years of practice at dressing fear up as prudence." He exhaled. "I won't hide things from you again. Even ugly ones. *Especially* ugly ones."

Kestrel considered this. Found it insufficient. Leaned her head against his shoulder anyway, because forgiveness and satisfaction were different animals, and one of them could wait.

Above them, the third stone hummed its new note into the mountains, and the wind carried it outward in widening circles—over the moor, over Greyford, over the shepherd pastures where lullabies still remembered the old covenant. Three stones awake. One remaining.

And Kestrel felt it before she heard it, the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm: a cold answering from the east, vast and patient, where the Garden's heart lay buried beneath the roots. The fourth stone. The boundary between memory and death itself.

The Warden had stopped waiting.

Far away, so faint it might have been imagination, Kestrel heard the first note of a rite beginning—and heard, underneath it, across both realms at once, thousands of voices forgetting.

Dominic heard it too. She knew by the way his hand closed over hers, hard, as if the wind might take her.

"She's started," he said. "We're out of time. We have to go *down*."
