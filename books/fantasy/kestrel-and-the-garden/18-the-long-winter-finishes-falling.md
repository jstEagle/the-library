## 18. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

The Garden's heart had once been a place of unbearable green.

The Warden of Winter remembered it that way—remembered it the way a woman remembers her own wedding day, or the face of a child before the fever took him: too clearly, and not at all accurately. Memory polished everything to a shine that was also a lie. The heart of the Garden had been green, yes, but it had been loud with it. Vines whispering in a language older than roots. Rivers that sang their own names. The great tree at the center of everything, whose bark was carved—by whom, by what, no one knew—with every name ever spoken with love.

Now she stood beneath that tree, and it was hers.

Frost climbed its trunk in slow silver rivers. The carvings were still there, thousands upon thousands of names, but they had gone quiet under the ice, the way a bell goes quiet when you fill it with snow. She ran one gloved finger along a groove of letters and felt nothing. That was the point. That was always the point. Feeling nothing was the only mercy she had ever trusted.

"Begin," she said.

Her servants moved through the clearing like figures in a music box—beautiful, precise, empty. There were nine of them now. Once there had been more; some had crumbled on the rope bridges and frozen passes of the world above, spent like coins. She did not mourn them. Mourning was a tax paid to memory, and she had abolished taxes.

They carried the fourth covenant-stone between them on a bier of black ice, and even now, even half-frozen, it fought them. The stone was small—a fist-sized knot of amber shot through with a single green thread, like a vein in a sleeping wrist—but it *pushed*. It pushed against the cold the way a drowning man pushes against still water. Where it passed, frost sublimated off the ground in little ghosts of steam. One of the servants carrying it had lost three fingers to its warmth already. He had not noticed. He would not notice anything again until she told him to.

They set it at the base of the great tree, in the socket that had waited for it since the beginning—the fourth anchor, the last lock, the final hinge between the world of breath and the world of dream.

Three stones remained unclaimed, somewhere out in the white noise of the mountains. She could feel them faintly, the way an old woman feels missing teeth: absences with shapes. The drowned bell. The choir of roots. The teeth of the world. Two of them had *moved* recently—or rather, had been raised, sung awake, by hands she could almost see if she squinted into the future the way other people squinted into fog.

A child. And a bone-carver who should have stayed buried in his mountain with his dead.

The Warden let herself feel, for exactly four seconds, something adjacent to disappointment. Then she folded it away, as she folded everything away, into the vaults of herself that no longer opened.

"It doesn't matter," she said aloud, to the tree, to the ice, to the nine empty things listening. "Two stones raised is two stones I know the location of. And the fourth—" she looked down at the amber knot straining against its bier, "—the fourth comes to me willingly. They always bring me the last one themselves. Hope is such an obedient courier."

She knelt beside the stone.

Beneath her gloves, her hands were very old. Older than the covenant. Older than the kingdoms that had signed it. She had been young once in this clearing—she could almost find the shape of that girl in the frost patterns, a smudge of warmth long since rendered into geometry—and the girl she had been had sworn, over the body of someone whose name she had paid dearly to stop remembering, that no one would ever be erased again. Not by death. Not by forgetting. Not by time's slow indifferent tide.

The world had called her mad for it. The world had called her Warden instead, which amounted to the same thing, and gave her a title.

"Now," she said.

* * *

The rite was not complicated. That was the horror of it, if horror had been a thing she permitted herself to perceive. Everything terrible she had ever done had been simple. Freezing a river was simple. Freezing a border was simple. Freezing the boundary between memory and death required only that she do to the fourth stone what she had done to everything else: hold it still.

She pressed both palms flat against the amber.

It burned. Of course it burned. Warmth was the last language the living things spoke, and the stone screamed it at her through her gloves, up her arms, into the hollow cathedral of her chest—a wordless shout of *no, no, we are not finished, nothing is finished, the roots are still drinking, the mothers are still singing—*

"Hush," said the Warden, in the tone one uses with a frightened animal. Or a frightened child. "Nothing ends. That is rather the entire point."

She pushed.

Cold went out of her in a wave she could actually see—a ring of white light flattening the snow-grass, bending the smaller trees backward, racing outward through the root-roads of the Garden faster than grief travels, faster than news. She felt it pass through the dreaming world like a hand smoothing a wrinkled sheet. She felt it reach the boundary itself, that gossamer membrane where the Garden touched the mortal realm everywhere at once—in every grave, every hearth, every lullaby—and she felt it *seize*.

The boundary stiffened.

For a thousand years, the covenant had kept that membrane supple. A grandmother could dream her way down to her dead husband's garden and walk with him among the tomato rows. A dying man could cross over holding his daughter's name in his mouth and be caught on the other side by everyone he'd ever loved. Grief flowed across it both directions, and so did comfort, and so did memory—the dead lending their strength back to the living in dreams, the living keeping the dead alive by telling their stories into the dark.

All of that was stillness now. A frozen lake where there had been a river.

The Warden rose, and watched the frost take the great tree's crown, branch by branch, each carved name going silent in its turn. Somewhere far below, in the warm mud of the world's first spring, roots stopped growing mid-drink. Somewhere in the mortal realm, a candle flame in a shrine window bent sideways as though a door had slammed.

"There," she said softly. "No one else will be lost. No one else will be forgotten. Everyone stays exactly where they are, forever."

It sounded, even to her, less like a promise than a diagnosis.

* * *

She felt the forgetting begin the way you feel weather change—not in one place but everywhere, a pressure drop across the whole sky of the mind.

Because that was the cost, and she had known the cost from the first night she conceived of this, kneeling in the ruins of the old covenant with blood on her hands and her mentor's voice in her ears: a boundary frozen does not merely stop passage. It stops *exchange*. And memory was exchange. Every remembered face was a little bridge built between the living and the dead, and bridges need traffic or they fall.

Across both realms, people began to forget their dead.

Not all at once. She was not a butcher. It began gently, mercifully even, which was the cruelest thing about it. In a fishing village on the western coast, a widow reached for her husband's voice and found she could recall the words he used to say but not the sound of him saying them. In a city of bells, a boy who visited his mother's grave every morning stood at the gate and realized he could not remember whether her eyes had been brown or gray, and the not-knowing did not hurt—it *itched*, like a scar from a wound he couldn't locate. In a shepherd's hut high in the Thornfells, an old woman humming a lullaby to her granddaughter lost a line of it mid-verse, hummed through the gap without noticing, and the granddaughter, who had never known the song was missing anything, learned it incomplete.

In the Garden, it was worse, because the Garden *was* memory. The whole realm was mortared from everything mortals had ever remembered lovingly—every remembered summer, every treasured kindness, every face held in the mind's lantern-light. As the forgetting spread, whole districts of the dreaming world dimmed. A street made of someone's childhood went dark house by house. A forest grown from ten thousand grandmothers' stories thinned at its edges, trees fading like breath off glass.

The Warden walked through the dimming streets and did not look at them. Looking was a form of feeling, and feeling was a debt, and she had declared bankruptcy centuries ago.

"You are saving them," said one of her servants, walking at her shoulder. Its voice was a borrowed voice, taken from a messenger months ago along with everything else it had been. "You are saving them from loss."

"I am," she said.

"They will thank you."

"No," she said. "They won't. No one has ever thanked the person who took the knife out slowly." She paused at a window where a family scene—carved from some forgotten child's memory of a harvest supper—was dissolving into pale mist, the faces going first, then the hands, then the laughter that had hung in the air like bunting. "But gratitude is for the living, and the living are precisely what I am trying to spare. Let them hate me in perfect safety. Let them hate me with intact hearts, never having lost anyone, never having to learn the weight of a name they can no longer say."

The servant considered this with its borrowed face.

"And the seed?" it asked.

The Warden stopped walking.

The seed. The Garden's last seed, wearing a mute child's skin, somewhere in the white teeth of the Thornfell mountains with a traitor bone-carver who owed her everything and understood nothing. Even now, even with the boundary frozen, the seed was a problem—because the seed was not a bridge. The seed was a *root*. Bridges freeze. Roots grow toward water no matter how hard the frost, and the seed's water was the covenant itself, the old deep current the stones anchored. Raised stones meant living anchors. Living anchors meant the membrane could thaw.

And there was another matter, one she kept in the deepest vault, behind the door she never opened: the seed could remember. That was what seeds did. They carried the past forward inside them and broke it open into the future. If the child reached the Garden's heart—if the child stood where the Warden stood now, under the frozen tree with its thousand thousand silent names—then everything the Warden had locked away might find a key.

Including the girl she had been.

"Increase the watch on the passes," she said. "Spend the servants freely. Spend the cold freely. I want the mountains closed like a fist." She turned back toward the heart of the Garden, where the fourth stone sat in its socket, amber gone quiet at last, the green thread in it dimmed to almost nothing. Almost. Not entirely. Some stubborn filament of warmth still pulsed there, once every few breaths, like a heart deciding whether to keep arguing.

"And send word to my hunters," she added. "If the bone-carver reaches the third stone, he will have a road straight to me. Let him come. But the seed—" her voice did not change, and that was what made it terrible, the absolute evenness of it, the temperature of still air, "—the seed must not arrive remembering anything. If she arrives at all, she should arrive as I arrived: hollowed, obedient, grateful for the silence."

She laid her palm once more against the frozen stone, and felt, far away, faint as a pulse through a wall, two of the remaining stones answer each other across the dark—singing to each other in the old tongue, gathering courage.

Let them sing, she thought. Choirs are loudest just before the freeze takes the throat.

Above her, snow began to fall inside the Garden for the first time in the history of the world—slow, enormous flakes, each one a small forgotten thing coming down out of the dark. On the great tree, one name near the top of the highest branch, carved by hands no one remembered, finally went silent under the ice.

The Warden watched it go, and felt nothing, and counted that as victory.

Far to the north, in a canyon where the wind lived, a third stone woke singing.
