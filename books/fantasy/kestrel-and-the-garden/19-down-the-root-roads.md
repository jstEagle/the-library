## 19. Down the Root-Roads

The mouth of the root-road opened in the hillside like a throat.

Dominic had known it was there for years—had walked past the tangle of hawthorn a hundred times on his way to the high pastures, never once seeing what he now could not unsee: the way the branches arched inward instead of outward, the way no bird ever nested there, the way the dark beneath them breathed. Slowly. Patiently. Like something asleep that had been waiting a very long time to be asked.

Kestrel stood at the threshold with her hand pressed flat against the first great root, and the root—gray-barked, thick as a mill wheel—shifted under her palm. Not much. Just enough. The way a dog leans into a hand it has missed.

"It knows you," Dominic said quietly.

She looked back at him. In the three days since the Teeth of the World, since they had raised the third stone together with their hands overlapping on wind-scoured granite and the canyon roaring around them like an organ played by a mad god, she had not spoken—not that she ever did—but her face had changed. There was less of the hunted animal in it now. More of something older. When she turned to look at him, he sometimes caught himself bowing his head slightly, some instinct older than manners insisting that he was in the presence of a queen in exile.

She signed to him, quick fingers in the cold air: *It's warm.*

He hadn't told her yet what waited below. He wasn't sure words would survive the telling anyway. "Then let's not keep it waiting."

They went down together into the breathing dark.

* * *

Kestrel felt the road before she saw it.

It began as a hum in her sternum, the same note she had carried since Greyford's drowned bell, but deeper here, layered, many-voiced—a chord rather than a single tone. The tunnel of roots closed over their heads and the last gray light of Thornfell's dying afternoon fell away behind them, and still she could see. Not with her eyes. With something else. The roots themselves were faintly luminous, veined with a gold-green light that moved like sap, like blood, like thought, and where the light pooled it took shapes—

A cradle, rocking though no hand touched it.

A kitchen table scarred with knife-marks, set with bread no one would ever eat.

A pair of boots, worn through at the heel, standing by a door that opened onto nothing.

She stopped walking. Dominic bumped gently into her, and she felt him go still too, felt his breath catch as he saw what she saw.

"Memories," he said hoarsely. "The road is made of memories."

Not made of them—she corrected him silently, though she had no way to say it—*built* from them. Every plank of light was something someone had loved hard enough to remember perfectly. She reached out and touched the rocking cradle, and it was warm, and for half a heartbeat she heard a woman humming, off-key, patient, the song looping because the woman who sang it had sung it ten thousand times and the memory had worn a groove deep enough to walk in.

The cradle rocked faster when Kestrel touched it. The humming grew brighter.

Dominic pulled her hand back, gentle but urgent. "Careful. You're—you're louder down here than anything else. Everything can hear you."

She understood then why the road hummed so loudly around her. It wasn't just the Garden welcoming its own. It was the Garden *leaning toward* her—the way the cradle leaned, the way every remembered thing strained in its niche like flowers toward a window. She was the last seed. This whole place, this cathedral of kept love, was built from the same substance she was.

She was walking through the memory of everything she might one day become.

Her chest went tight. She took Dominic's sleeve instead of his hand—he had offered his hand once, on the rope bridge, and she had taken it, and neither of them had mentioned it since—and they descended.

* * *

The road was long in the way dreams are long.

Dominic lost track of hours. There was no sky to mark them, only the slow brightening of the root-veins as they went deeper, gold giving way to colors that had no names above ground—a green like the moment after rain, a blue like the pause before forgiveness. And everywhere, everywhere, the memories.

He passed a wedding ring spinning slowly in midair, catching light from nowhere, and heard—faintly, the way you hear surf from far inland—a man laughing. He passed a child's drawing of a house with smoke curling from the chimney, crayon-wax and impossible, pinned to the living wood like a treasure. He passed a field of wheat that had been someone's whole livelihood and someone else's whole joy, golden stalks swaying in a wind that belonged to another world, and he understood suddenly, viscerally, what the Warden was trying to kill.

This place was not built of power. It was built of *attention*. Of mortals, in their short loud lives, stopping to love things hard enough to hold them forever. Every remembered kindness was a stone in these walls. Every lullaby was mortar.

And the Warden wanted it all frozen. Perfect. Unchanging. Safe.

He thought of her face in the ice chapel—that terrible gentleness—and for the first time he had understood her completely, and hated himself for it. She had loved this place too. That was the horror of her. She loved it so much she would embalm it.

"Dominic." He realized Kestrel had stopped again, was tugging his sleeve, pointing.

Ahead, the road forked. Three ways split off into the dark, and each fork was marked by a figure of woven root: a woman holding a lantern, a soldier with a broken sword, a small hooded shape whose face was blank wood. Guardian-shapes. Old covenant-work. And carved into the trunk above them, in the pre-kingdom tongue that Dominic knew only from bone and Kestrel knew the way she knew her own pulse:

*Which of you remembers?*

Kestrel stepped forward without hesitation. She pressed her hand to the hooded figure's blank face—and the face changed. Became hers. Became, briefly, a hundred faces at once, every child who had ever come down this road carrying grief or hope or both.

The fork chose itself. The middle path lit up like a struck match, gold-green fire running down its length into the depths.

"She didn't even hesitate," Dominic murmured, mostly to himself. Then, aloud, dry as his old carving-knife: "I've spent six years avoiding decisions. You make them look like breathing."

Kestrel glanced back at him, and in the root-light he saw her almost-smile—the small crooked thing that was all she ever gave, and all he ever needed.

They took the lit road down.

* * *

Deeper still, the memories changed.

Kestrel noticed it first, because noticing was what she did—had always done, even mute, even hunted, even freezing in a blizzard at a stranger's door. The memories near the surface had been simple: bread, boots, cradles, songs. But the further down they went, the larger they grew, the more they held. A whole harbor town, complete with gulls and fish-smell and the sound of a bell buoy, folded into a single alcove of root. A grandmother's garden spanning a cavern wall, every flower blooming at once because the woman who remembered it had never been able to choose a favorite season.

People did not remember small things deeply, she realized. They remembered *whole worlds*. A person was not one memory but a country of them, and when they died, if they were loved rightly, their country came here.

And if they were forgotten—

She touched a hollow in the wall. An alcove shaped like a room, but empty. Dust of light lay in it, undisturbed. Someone had been remembered here once, fiercely, for decades—and then the rememberer had died, and no one after had cared enough, and the room had gone vacant. The emptiness ached worse than any wound. It ached like the chapel at Greyford, like the frozen villages, like everything the Warden had already taken.

She made a sound. Small. Involuntary. The first noise she had made since the third stone.

Dominic's head came around fast. "What is it?"

She pointed at the empty alcove. Tried to explain with her hands and failed, her signs collapsing into a fist pressed against her sternum, over and over.

He looked at the vacancy for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "That's what forgetting looks like. Down here." He exhaled. "That's what she's doing to everything. Up there, people are losing their dead. I've felt it—the bones have been going quiet, one voice at a time, like lamps going out. And every voice that goes quiet up there leaves a hole like this one down here." He put his hand flat against the empty alcove, and his jaw tightened. "My mother's voice is probably already gone. I paid it at the Choir of Roots, and I told myself it was given, not taken. But if the fourth stone falls—" He stopped. Started again. "If the fourth stone falls, it won't matter what anyone paid. All of it goes. Every voice. Every room."

Kestrel took his hand off the empty alcove and held it, both of hers around his one, the way you hold a wound closed.

He looked down at her hands, then at her face, and something passed between them that needed no language at all—which was fitting, she thought, because language was all she had ever lacked and somehow never the thing that mattered.

*Hurry,* she signed against his palm, letter by letter, so he would feel it as much as read it.

"Yes," he said. "Hurry."

* * *

The last stretch of road was a staircase of roots spiraling down through darkness shot with light, and as they descended, Dominic became aware that they were being watched.

Not threatened. Watched. Shapes gathered in the periphery of the glow—figures made of the same gold-green luminescence as the veins, translucent, shifting. Some were human-shaped. Some were animals. One was unmistakably a horse with a mane of flowing script, as if someone had loved a horse and a story in equal measure and the memory had refused to choose.

"The dead," he whispered. "Or... the remembered. They're coming to see her."

They were. Dozens of them, then hundreds, lining the spiral stair above and below, silent, radiant, and every one of them inclined toward Kestrel as she passed—an inclination so slight it might have been imagination, except that it happened in perfect unison, wave upon wave, like wheat before wind, like the bowing of a court.

Kestrel walked through their reverence with her chin up and her fists clenched, and Dominic saw that she was terrified. Not of them. Of what they seemed to believe she was. He reached out and took her hand properly this time, no pretense of sleeves, and her grip crushed his fingers white, and she did not let go until the stair ended.

It ended at a door.

There was no other word for it, though it was grown, not built: two vast root-halves parted around a threshold of silver bark, and beyond it light poured out—not the veined gold of the road but true daylight, impossible daylight, the kind that meant morning and open sky and a world with weather in it. Warm air flowed through, smelling of cut grass and river water and bread baking somewhere out of sight, and beneath all of it ran a sound like distant singing, thousands of voices braided together, wordless and glad.

The Garden. The actual Garden, the dreaming world beneath the roots, the place the covenant had been built to keep open, the place Dominic had helped close and had spent six years pretending he'd done it to protect people rather than to escape his own guilt.

Kestrel stepped to the threshold and stopped. Her hand in his trembled.

"What's wrong?" he asked.

She signed one-handed, slow, deliberate: *Once we go through, we can't come back this way. Can we.*

He considered lying to her. He had hidden the Warden's bargain from her; he was carrying that lie like a stone in his boot, and it ground against him with every step. He was done adding weight.

"No," he said. "The roads are closing behind us—I can feel it. Whatever happens next, we go forward. To the heart. To her."

Kestrel nodded. Then she did something he did not expect. She turned to face the gathered dead on the spiral stair—all those luminous figures, all that patient love—and she bowed to them. Deeply. The seed bowing to the soil.

The light of the whole cavern flared, once, like a heart beating.

And then she pulled him through the door, into the morning that had no sun, and the root-road sealed itself behind them with a sound like a book closing.

* * *

The Garden spread out before them, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

It was a valley without end, terraced and blooming, built from everything mortals had ever remembered lovingly—and it showed. Rivers ran with light that was partly water and partly music. Orchards bore fruit shaped like moments: a first snowfall, a returned letter, a hand grasped in the dark. Houses stood in clusters that no architect would have approved and every heart would have recognized, because they were the houses people remembered, idealized and imperfect, doors always open. On the far hills, forests of memory climbed toward a sky that shifted like silk, and everywhere, threaded through everything, ran the four great lines of covenant-light—three of them burning steady and strong from the stones Kestrel had raised, converging toward a point at the valley's center.

Where the fourth line should have been, there was frost.

Even from this distance Dominic could see it: a stain of white spreading across the heart of the Garden like ink dropped in milk, a fortress of still air rising where the fourth anchor should have burned, and above it the sky was graying, the silk-sky stiffening, color draining out of the orchards nearest the cold in slow, widening rings.

"She's already begun the rite," Dominic said. His voice sounded very small in all that beauty. "The fourth stone. She's freezing it from her side, and every hour she works, more of this place dies and more of the living world forgets." He turned to Kestrel. "We have maybe a day. Maybe less."

Kestrel was staring at the frost-stain, and her face had gone still in a way he had learned to fear. Then she raised her hands and signed, and her signs were sharp, certain, final:

*Then we stop walking and start running.*

She was right, and they ran—down through orchards of remembered summers, past rivers that sang, toward the cold at the heart of everything beautiful. And as they ran, Dominic saw the frost ahead shudder and reach, and heard, thin across the distance, a voice he knew better than any carved bone, calling his name with all the tenderness of a mentor and all the patience of winter.

She knew they were coming.

She had been waiting for him all along.
