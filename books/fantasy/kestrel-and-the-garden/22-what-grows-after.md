## 22. What Grows After

The thaw came to Thornfell the way forgiveness comes—slowly, and then all at once.

Dominic stood in his doorway on the first morning of true spring and watched the snow let go of the mountain. It slid from the pines in soft white avalanches, pooled in the wheel-ruts of the smuggler's road, and ran down toward Greyford in a hundred bright threads that would become rivers by noon. The air smelled of wet stone and green things waking. Somewhere below the workshop, a bird was making an argument about territory, and losing, and starting over.

He had forgotten what birds sounded like in the old days. Not forgotten-forgotten—the way he'd lost his mother's voice, or the last words of the drowned bell-keeper, or every dead voice he had ever carried in carved bone. That kind of forgetting was clean, surgical, paid for at the Garden's heart with hands that shook. This was simpler: he simply hadn't heard birdsong in years, because for years the winters had come wrong and stayed wrong, and the birds had gone quiet, and so had everything else.

Now the mountain was loud again. Water talking to itself in the gullies. Wind moving through branches that were no longer brittle. And beneath it all—this was the part that made him stand very still, one hand on the doorframe—a hum, faint as a plucked string three rooms away, running through the roots of the world.

The covenant held. He could feel it the way you feel a healed bone: not the absence of injury, exactly, but the knowledge of it, weather coming before rain.

Behind him, inside the workshop, Kestrel was arguing with a seedling.

He turned and watched her through the doorway. She had claimed the south-facing bench where he used to cure bone, and she had filled it with clay pots in rows, each one holding something green and improbable. The seedling in question—a sapling no taller than her forearm, its leaves silver on the underside like fish scales—had grown four inches overnight and now leaned out of its pot at an angle that could only be called insolent. Kestrel frowned at it. She tapped the rim of the pot twice, sharply, the way a schoolteacher taps a desk.

The sapling straightened. Mostly.

"You can't just grow wherever you want," Dominic said, coming back inside. "There are rules."

Kestrel looked up at him. She still didn't speak—not in any tongue mortals used—but she didn't need to anymore. Her face did the work of a whole choir. *Rules,* her expression said, with the flat disdain only a child can manage, *are for things that aren't me.*

She was taller than when she'd arrived on his doorstep two winters ago, half-frozen and mute and carrying a word in her mouth older than kingdoms. Her hair had gone from winter-brown to something darker, shot through with strands of living green that moved when there was no wind. When she slept, moss crept a careful, polite distance from her pillow. When she laughed—which was more often now, though never carelessly—the room got warmer. Literally. The kettle had learned to come to a boil whenever she was happy, which made tea in their house a kind of emotional barometer.

She was the Garden's last seed. She had chosen, at the heart of everything, to grow here instead—in the mortal world, among short-lived people who forgot, because forgetting, she had decided, was not the same as loss. Forgetting was the soil loss grew in. And things grew in soil.

Dominic had argued with her about it once, in the first raw weeks after the reforging, when his head was still full of holes shaped like voices. *You could go home,* he'd said. *You could be whole.* She had taken his hand, pressed it flat against the floorboards of the workshop—against the wood, the stone beneath it, the roots beneath the stone—and shown him what she felt: the whole mountain humming, the covenant singing through it, both realms breathing together again. *This is home too,* she'd said without saying it. *I'm not leaving half of myself behind.*

So they stayed. He mended his workshop; she grew hers into it.

* * *

The bones were different now.

That was the thing he was still learning, on quiet mornings like this one. Before, every carving had been a window—he'd hold a keepsake bone and hear its owner's voice pour through, the last words, the unfinished sentences, the griefs and jokes and small domestic prayers of everyone who had ever been loved enough to be remembered in ivory. His gift had been a curse worn as armor, loneliness dressed up as purpose. He'd hidden in Thornfell because every bone he touched talked, and none of them ever stopped, and silence was the one luxury the dead never granted him.

Now he picked up the oldest carving on his shelf—a sparrow-sized bird he'd made for a girl in Greyford whose name he could no longer retrieve, along with everything else the covenant had taken as its price—and heard nothing.

Not nothing-nothing. He pressed his thumb into the wing, into the little chiseled feathers, and felt... warmth. A faint pulse, like a word remembered rather than heard. The girl had liked to run along the quarry wall. She had been afraid of deep water and proud of being afraid of it anyway. These weren't voices. They were stories—his own stories, the ones he'd told himself so many times they'd worn grooves, the way water wears stone. The dead were silent now. But the shape of them remained, the way a footprint remains after the foot has gone.

He set the sparrow down carefully.

It should have broken him more than it did. In the Garden's heart, when Kestrel had asked him—asked him with her whole luminous terrible self—to pay the covenant's price, he had known exactly what he was giving away. Every voice. His mother's, telling him the names of trees. The Warden's, from before she was the Warden, when she was Maerin and she'd laughed at his first clumsy carving and said, *You listen better than you carve, boy. Don't ever lose that.* All of them. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Forty years of the dead speaking through his hands, gone in a single breath, traded so that the living could keep their own memories, so that mothers would remember their children and lovers their lovers and no one anywhere would wake to find a beloved face gone smooth and strange.

He had done it gladly. That was the strange part. Grief, he understood now, had been his engine for forty years—it had driven him up the mountain, kept the door barred, kept the bones talking. Paying it away hadn't emptied him. It had left room.

He took up his knife instead of a bone. There was a fresh length of rowan on the bench, cut that morning from the tree Kestrel had coaxed up through the workshop's foundation—her tree, silver-leaved, already taller than the chimney. He wasn't sure yet what lived in the wood. That was new too. He used to know before the first cut; the dead announced themselves. Now he carved blind, and whatever came out came out, and mostly it was just beautiful, and that was enough.

* * *

Visitors started coming when the passes opened.

They came up the smuggler's road—the road that hated carts, the road that had unsealed itself the day the fourth stone was reforged, root-doors swinging open in rock faces all across the Thornfell like eyes blinking awake. They came quietly, in twos and threes, the way people come to a place they've heard about from someone who heard it from someone else, and they stood awkwardly in his yard holding bundles wrapped in cloth.

Bone. Always bone. A grandmother's knuckle. A father's finger joint. A lock of hair braided around a sliver of rib. Things people had kept in drawers for years, unable to bury them, unable to explain why.

"I heard," they always began, "that you used to—"

"I don't hear them anymore," Dominic told the first one, a stooped shepherd from the high moor whose wife had died before the long winter. "No one does. That's what we bought."

The shepherd had looked at the bundle in his hands as if it had suddenly become heavy in a new way. "Then what do I do with her?"

And Kestrel, who had been listening from the garden gate with dirt on her knees, had walked over, taken the bundle, and unwrapped it with the gravity of a priest. She'd held the small bone up to the light. Then she'd looked at the shepherd and signed—she'd invented a whole language of signs that spring, quick and expressive, and Dominic was fluent in it before he knew he was learning—and pointed at the shepherd's own mouth.

*Tell me about her,* the sign said.

The shepherd had blinked. Then, haltingly, standing in a stranger's yard with the meltwater running off his roof, he had begun to talk. How she sang off-key on purpose to make the children laugh. How she could predict rain in her knees. How she'd once fought off a wolf with a fire poker and then fed its mate all winter out of guilt.

Kestrel listened the way she listened to everything—with her whole body, leaning in, green-streaked hair falling forward—and when he finished, she pressed the bone back into his hands, closed his fingers over it, and pointed at his chest. *Keep it. Keep her. You're the remembering now.*

That was how it went, all that spring. They came with bones and left with stories. Dominic built a shelf by the door for the ones people chose to leave behind—not relics anymore, just objects, beautiful and quiet—and beside the shelf he hung a slate, and on the slate, in his carpenter's square hand, he wrote the stories down as people told them, because paper remembered differently than bone did, and differently than hearts did, and there was no harm in all three.

Some evenings he read the slate aloud to Kestrel while she worked her pots, and she would correct him—*no, the wolf was smaller than that, the man exaggerates*—and he would amend the record, and somewhere in the amending he understood that this was what the covenant had always meant. Not stones under mountains. Not magic sealing borders between worlds. Just this: people, telling each other who they had loved, until the telling became a kind of keeping, and the keeping became a kind of growing.

* * *

On the evening of the year's first real warm rain, Kestrel came to him with her hands behind her back and the particular stiffness of someone concealing something.

She led him out to the cottage garden on the south slope—the one they'd built together over the winter, walled in dry stone, facing the valley where Greyford's lights would come on at dusk. In the middle of the garden there was a patch of bare earth she had guarded jealously all season, turning away even Dominic's questions with a look that brooked no negotiation.

In the middle of the bare earth, a hole.

"Should I be worried?" Dominic asked.

She rolled her eyes. Then she brought her hands from behind her back, and in them was a pot—the clay pot from the workshop bench, cracked now down one side where the roots had pushed—and in the pot stood the silver-leaved sapling, her first, the insolent one, the one that had grown four inches overnight and argued with her about rules.

She looked at him. She looked at the hole. She looked at him again, and her expression was doing something complicated—asking permission, offering something, bracing for refusal—all at once, the way only her face could.

"You want to plant it here," Dominic said slowly. "Outside."

She nodded. Then she shook her head. Then she crouched, pressed her palm flat against the soil, and showed him.

He saw it the way he'd once seen through her touch on the workshop floor: the Garden, vast and dreaming beneath the roots of the world, rebuilt now, breathing, its halls full of everything mortals had ever remembered lovingly. And he saw the thread that ran from that place up through the mountain, up through the bedrock, up through the soil of this small walled garden, and ended—ended, deliberately, chosen—at the roots of a stubborn silver sapling in a cracked clay pot.

She wasn't asking to plant a tree. She was asking to plant herself. To put down roots in mortal ground, in a mortal garden, in a life measured in decades instead of ages. To grow the way people grow: crooked, weathered, pruned by loss, blooming anyway.

"Kestrel," Dominic said, and his voice caught on her name the way it always did now, because he had given away every other name he loved and hers was the one he got to keep. "You don't have to ask me. This is your ground too."

Her eyes went bright—not with Garden-light, just with ordinary tears, which he suspected she'd had to learn, and learning them had cost her more than any rite. She tipped the pot. The sapling slid out, roots wrapped in dark earth, and settled into the hole like it had been waiting for it all along. She packed the soil down with her small fierce hands. Dominic knelt beside her and helped, his old carver's fingers clumsy next to hers, and the rain came down warm around them, and the tree—no, the girl, the girl-tree, the seed who had chosen—shivered once from crown to root and began, unmistakably, to grow.

By full dark it stood taller than both of them. By morning, he knew, it would be taller than the cottage. It would grow the way she grew: fast, willful, silver underneath, leaning always toward whoever needed the shade.

They sat against the garden wall afterward, shoulder to shoulder, sharing a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke, watching the first stars come out over the pass where the Warden's fortress had been. The fortress was gone. Where it had stood there was a waterfall now, loud and cheerful, throwing spray that caught the starlight. Falling snow had unmade her; falling water remembered her. Dominic found he could think of Maerin now without flinching—not her voice, that was gone forever, paid fair and square, but the story of her: the mentor who had feared forgetting so much that she froze the whole world trying to escape it, and who had, in the end, been freed by a child and a coward and a handful of old stones. He kept that story on the slate too. Even villains deserved remembering. Especially the ones who'd taught you to carve.

Kestrel yawned and leaned her head against his shoulder. Above the garden wall, her tree's new leaves stirred, and the wind coming down off the mountain carried the smell of green growth and far-off smoke from some shepherd's hearth, and beneath it all, faint and constant, the hum of the covenant, singing through the roots of the world like a song someone was finally bothering to sing all the way through.

"Spring," Dominic said to no one, to everyone, to the girl asleep against his shoulder and the tree growing in the dark and the dead who could no longer answer and the living who finally could. "About time."

In the morning there would be visitors on the road, with bundles in their arms and stories in their mouths. There would be tea boiling before anyone was happy, because the kettle had gotten sentimental. There would be a slate to update, a garden to tend, a tree to grow around, a life to build in the space where grief used to live.

But tonight there was only rain, and roots taking hold, and a family—strange, small, planted, permanent—growing toward the light.
