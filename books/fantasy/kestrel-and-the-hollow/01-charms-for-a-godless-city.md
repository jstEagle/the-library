## 1. Charms for a Godless City

The rain in Vharrow fell the way it always did — sideways, gray, and with the particular spite of a city that had outlawed wonder and settled for weather instead. Paloma Sterling pulled his collar up and arranged his tray of trinkets on the corner of Saltmarket Row with the practiced ease of a man who had done this ten thousand mornings and expected to do it ten thousand more.

"Charms!" he called, voice pitched to carry over the fishwives and the cart wheels. "Blessed charms! Protection from ill fortune, bad debts, unfaithful lovers, and — for a modest surcharge — moderately faithful ones!"

A woman with a basket of eels paused, squinted at him, then moved on. A boy of perhaps seven stopped dead in front of the tray, eyes wide at the little glass vials that caught what light the clouds permitted.

"What do they do?" the boy asked.

"They almost work," Paloma said solemnly. "That's the honest truth of it. This one—" he tapped a vial of river-glass and knotted thread, "—will keep nightmares off you about four nights out of five. The fifth night, it'll keep them *mostly* off. You'll dream of a dog instead of whatever was coming."

"That's not magic."

"No," Paloma agreed. "It's better than magic. Magic's illegal here. Almost-working is perfectly lawful." He pressed the vial into the boy's hand and closed the small fingers around it. "For you? Free. Tell your mother it cost you a kiss on the cheek, and consider us even."

The boy ran off clutching it like treasure, and Paloma watched him go with something in his chest that had no name he was willing to use.

Under his left sleeve, against the inside of his forearm, the oath-mark sat cold as ever. He didn't look at it. He never looked at it anymore. Looking invited hope, and hope in Vharrow was a luxury taxed at rates no charm-seller could afford.

* * *

He had sworn himself to Kestrel at nineteen, kneeling in a shrine that smelled of juniper smoke and old stone, while an old priestess with laugh lines carved deep as riverbeds pressed her thumb to his arm and spoke the words of binding. *Kestrel of the quiet hours. Kestrel who walks beside, not before. Kestrel who asks nothing but that you notice the small things: the sparrow's fall, the stranger's hunger, the child asleep against a wall.*

The god had been there. Paloma remembered it — or thought he remembered it — the way you remember warmth from a fire that has since gone out. A presence like a hand resting between his shoulder blades. Not commanding. Never commanding. Just *there*, the way a friend is there when you walk home in the dark.

Then, one morning ten years ago, Paloma had woken and the hand was gone.

No thunderclap. No omen. Just silence where presence had been, the way a room is silent after someone leaves it. He had waited. He had prayed until his knees ached and his voice went raw. He had climbed to every ruined shrine in three counties and lit candles until his fingers were wax-burned.

Nothing. Ten years of nothing.

So he had come to Vharrow, where gods were contraband and faith was a misdemeanor, and sold trinkets that almost worked, and told himself it was enough. Told himself Kestrel was a quiet god, and maybe this was just a very long quiet. Told himself the hollow feeling under his ribs was hunger, or age, or the damp.

He was very good at telling himself things. It was, he sometimes thought, the only miracle he had left.

* * *

The job came at midday, wrapped in saffron dust and desperation.

Mistress Anselma Okonkwo ran the largest spice concern on Saltmarket Row, which meant she paid her taxes, bribed her inspectors, and still — somehow — kept getting robbed. Three times this season her warehouse locks had been found open and her ledgers rifled, though nothing of value taken except, oddly, her correspondence.

"Wardens say I'm imagining it," she said, arms crossed in her office above the shop, cinnamon and clove thick enough to chew. "Wardens say a lot of things. What they don't say is who's doing it. So I'm hiring outside help."

"I sell charms," Paloma said. "I don't investigate."

"You sell charms that almost work. I want one that works all the way. Name your price."

Paloma looked at her. She was sixty-odd, built like a doorframe, with hands stained yellow to the wrist from turmeric and eyes that missed nothing. He liked her immediately, which was dangerous. Liking people was dangerous in his line of — well, in any line, in Vharrow.

"Locks aren't my trade," he said carefully. "But I can ward the doors. Old craft. Nothing the Wardens would flag." *Because it isn't really anything,* he did not add. *Because it's thread and glass and intention, and intention hasn't held weight in ten years.*

"Fine. Ward the doors. And if whoever's reading my letters comes back—"

"They won't get past me," Paloma said, and heard the oath-mark hum faintly against his skin, cold and empty as a struck bell that makes no sound.

He should have known better than to make promises. Promises were how everything had gone wrong the first time.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon, and it was pleasant work, the kind that let his hands be busy while his mind wandered the old paths. Knotting thread. Setting glass. Whispering the old words under his breath — not prayers exactly, more like letters to someone who'd moved away without leaving a forwarding address. *If you can hear this. If you're anywhere. The eel woman looked tired today. The boy took the nightmare-charm free of charge. I'm still noticing the small things. I'm still here.*

Silence, as always. But he said them anyway. That was the whole of his religion now: saying things into silence, and meaning them.

By dusk the warehouse doors were strung with his work — subtle, ugly-beautiful knots of gray thread and smoked glass, tucked into the iron hinges where no inspector would think to look. Anselma watched him finish, arms crossed, and paid him in coin counted twice and a bowl of lamb stew that made him nearly weep with gratitude.

"Come back in a week," she said. "Tell me if it held."

"It'll hold," Paloma said. "Almost certainly."

She snorted. "You're the only honest merchant on this street. It's unsettling."

He was three streets away, thinking about the stew and whether he could afford a second bowl tomorrow, when the world behind him turned white.

Not bright — *white*, a clean ringing absence of color, like the moment before lightning strikes. Paloma spun. Down at the warehouse, through the gap between buildings, he could see the doors he'd warded blazing with pale light, thread burning like fuse-cord, glass singing a note he felt in his teeth.

His charms were igniting. All of them. At once.

They had never done that. In ten years of selling almost-magic, not one of his trinkets had ever done more than warm slightly, glow faintly, nudge luck by a hair's breadth. They were placebos with good craftsmanship. And now they were burning like signal fires, and the note they sang was climbing, climbing—

And somewhere beneath the fear, in the hollow place where a god used to live, Paloma felt something turn over. Like a sleeper shifting in bed. Like attention.

*Oh,* he thought, dizzy. *Oh, please.*

Then the Wardens' horns sounded from the Wardenhouse on Candler's Green, and the light died mid-note, and the something-that-turned-over went still again, and Paloma Sterling did what he had spent ten years learning to do.

He ran.

* * *

He knew the back ways of Vharrow the way other men knew their own names. Down the tannery steps, along the drainage channel, through the gap in the wall behind the chandler's where the mortar had crumbled years ago and nobody official had ever bothered to look. His heart hammered. His lungs burned. Behind him, boots rang on cobbles — two pairs, maybe three, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew the city belonged to them.

Wardens didn't chase charm-sellers. Charm-sellers sold glass and string; everyone knew it, including the Wardens, who found them useful the way cats find mice useful. Wardens chased *magic*. Real magic, working magic, the kind that lit up their detection-horns from across the city.

And his charms had lit those horns like festival bonfires.

Which meant either his craft had finally, impossibly, begun to work —

— or something else had poured itself through his knots and threads and intentions, something vast and desperate and familiar, using his shabby little wards as a doorway because it had no other way to reach him.

Paloma crouched in the dark behind the chandler's wall, rain dripping off his nose, and pressed his palm flat against the cold stone.

"If that was you," he whispered, "you have *terrible* timing."

Silence. Of course. Always silence.

But the oath-mark on his arm wasn't cold anymore. It was warm — barely, faintly, the way a hearthstone stays warm hours after the fire dies — and it was pulsing. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like footsteps. Like someone walking toward him through the dark, one careful step at a time, from very far away.

Boots splashed in the alley mouth behind him. A lantern beam swept the drainage channel, close enough that Paloma could hear the Warden muttering to his partner: *"—source was the spice warehouse, sure as salt. Working craft, real craft. Find the charmer. Captain wants him breathing."*

*Breathing.* How generous.

Paloma pressed deeper into the shadows, mind racing faster than his feet had run. Whoever was robbing Anselma hadn't come for spices or letters. They'd come for *him* — or for whatever answered him tonight. Someone had known. Someone had set a trap baited with a kind old woman's warehouse, and his stupid, starving, hopeful heart had walked straight into it.

He had one name in Vharrow he trusted with anything, and she owed him a favor besides, and she lived on the far side of the river in a house full of books she wasn't supposed to have read.

Odile Vane would call him a fool. Odile Vane would be right.

Paloma pulled his sleeve down over the warm, pulsing mark, stood up out of the shadows, and began to run again — toward the river, toward the bridge, toward the one door in Vharrow that might still open for him.

Behind him, the lanterns multiplied. Ahead, the rain kept falling, sideways, gray, and patient.

And somewhere — far below the city, far below the world, in a dark he couldn't yet imagine — something that had bought his name ten years ago opened its ledger, found his entry, and smiled.
