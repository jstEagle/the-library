## 2. The Dying Courier

The rain came down on Vharrow like a debt collector — patient, thorough, and entirely without mercy.

Paloma Sterling watched it from the doorway of his shop, which was generous to call a shop and kinder to call a closet with pretensions. Three shelves of charms that almost worked. A jar of river-stones polished into something resembling luck. A sign he'd painted himself in his second year, when optimism was still a habit: *Sterling's Small Protections — No Refunds, No Miracles.*

The no-miracles part was honest, at least. In Vharrow, honesty about the limits of your merchandise was the only kind the Wardens permitted. Magic was outlawed here, had been for a generation, and so Paloma sold trinkets: knotted cords to keep headaches at bay, copper coins stamped with a bird that might have been a kestrel if you squinted and wanted very much to believe. They worked the way a mother's hum worked — not at all, technically, and yet somehow they did.

He touched his left wrist through his sleeve, where the oath-mark sat beneath the skin like a splinter of cold light. Ten years, and the god it bound him to had not spoken. Ten years of prayers poured down a well with no bottom and no echo.

"Still nothing?" said the rain, or possibly the silence. It was hard to tell the difference some days.

He was reaching for the shutter when the man fell into his doorway.

Not walked. *Fell* — a tall, rain-soaked shape that hit the frame shoulder-first and slid down it like a puppet with its strings cut, ending in a wet heap across the threshold. Paloma caught a whiff of blood and horse sweat and something else beneath it, something mineral and wrong, like the air inside a cave that has never seen weather.

"Sir?" Paloma crouched. "Friend? You've picked a bad door to die against. I haven't the floor space."

The stranger looked up. He was young under the grime, maybe thirty, with the grey complexion of a man who had been bleeding internally for hours and had simply decided to keep going anyway out of spite. His lips were dark with blood. One hand was clenched around something pressed to his chest as though it were the only warm thing left in Vharrow.

"You're—" The man's voice cracked like dry kindling. "You're the charm-seller. The one on Candle Lane."

"I'm the one on Candle Lane," Paloma agreed. "There are four others. If you're shopping, I'd recommend Marta two doors down; her knots are better than mine."

"Not shopping." A wet laugh, quickly converted to coughing. Blood speckled the doorstep. "I need — you don't understand, I ran the whole way from the Hollow Road — they'll burn it, they burn everything—"

"Burn what?"

But the man wasn't listening anymore. He was fumbling at his coat with fingers that had forgotten how fingers worked, and Paloma, who had spent ten years learning exactly how much trouble curiosity bought in this city, found himself helping anyway — peeling back the soaked wool, prying loose the thing the courier had guarded across half the mountain country at the cost of his life.

It was a map. Folded small, sealed in wax, the wax stamped with a sigil Paloma didn't recognize: a circle broken by a vertical line, like an eye that had been closed by force.

"The court," the courier whispered. "Under the mountain. You have to — someone has to see it. Someone who can read it." His eyes, fever-bright, fixed on Paloma's face with terrible focus. "You look like a man who reads."

"I look like a man who sells knots," Paloma said. "Take it to the archivists' quarter—"

"They burned the archivists' quarter in the spring." The courier's laugh again, awful and thin. "You really don't know anything, do you? You live in this city like a man asleep."

"I sleep very well, thank you."

Something in Paloma's tone must have reached the man, because he stopped struggling to rise and simply lay there in the doorway, rain running off the eaves behind him in silver threads, and studied Paloma's face with the strange calm of the nearly dead.

"What's your name?" the courier asked.

"Sterling. Paloma Sterling."

"No." The word came out soft and certain, and it landed in Paloma's chest like a stone dropped down that same godless well. "That's the name you wear. Like the charms. Almost right." The courier's bloody lips curved. "I carried papers for the Undercourt for six years. I know what names cost there. And yours—" he lifted the map between them, wax seal glinting, "—yours is written in the margin. Dated ten years ago. Paid in full."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere down Candle Lane a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"That's nonsense," Paloma said, and heard how unsteady his own voice was. "I've lived here ten years. Same shop. Same shelf. Same—"

"Same hollow mark under your sleeve." The courier's eyes flicked to Paloma's wrist, and Paloma's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cellar, through whatever lay beneath Vharrow, which apparently went considerably deeper than he'd ever suspected. "Kestrel-bearer. That's what the ledger calls you. *Soul intact, reserved.*"

The world did a slow, sick half-turn. Paloma gripped the doorframe.

He had worn the oath-mark since before he could remember — which, he realized now, with a vertigo like standing at the edge of a stair that isn't there, was itself strange. Everyone remembered their oathing. It was the one ceremony the silent god still permitted, the binding of a servant's soul to Kestrel's service. Paloma had told curious customers a hundred comfortable lies about it: sworn young, sworn far away, sworn in a village whose name he preferred not to discuss. The lies had always sat easily in his mouth.

Too easily. The way things sit in a pocket you never check.

"I don't remember," he said aloud, and hated how small it sounded. "Swearing. I don't remember swearing."

"No," the courier said gently, as if consoling a child. "You wouldn't. That was the price. That was the whole *point*." He tried to push himself up, failed, and settled for pressing the map into Paloma's hands with the last of his strength. His skin was ice. "They didn't just buy you, charm-seller. They bought the *god*. Silenced him as part of the contract — collateral, the magistrates called it. Ten years of a god's voice, locked in a vault under Mount Hollow, and nobody below even remembers there was ever a sound."

Paloma stared at him. Rain. Blood. The little bell above his door, which had not rung because the door was blocked by a dying man, hanging silent as everything else in his life.

"Why me?" he asked. "Why bring it here?"

"Because you're in the margins." The courier smiled, and there was blood on his teeth, and somehow it was a kind smile, the smile of a man handing off a weight he could finally put down. "Because whoever they bought you from sold you cheap, and the ledger hates a fraud. Because—" his breath hitched, "—because you were the nearest door, and I am done running."

His grip loosened. The map stayed in Paloma's hands.

"Listen," the courier whispered. His eyes were going distant, fixing on something over Paloma's shoulder — the rain, the dark, the road back toward the mountain. "Listen. The Hollow keeps what it buys. Whatever you think you are — the shop, the name, the quiet little life — none of it is yours. It's all *collateral*. And courts come to collect."

Then, with the last air in his ruined chest, he said a name.

It was not *Paloma*.

It was a long name, a ringing name, a name with the shape of bells in it and the taste of cold mountain air, and it went into Paloma's ears and straight down to the oath-mark on his wrist, which flared — actually *flared*, first time in ten years, a pulse of light like a heartbeat remembered — and somewhere impossibly far away, something turned over in its sleep.

Then the light died. Then the courier's eyes did too, still smiling, still looking at the road home.

Paloma knelt in his doorway holding a sealed map and a stranger's name, and felt the ground of his entire self crack open like river ice in spring.

* * *

He did not remember closing the man's eyes. He must have; they were closed. He did not remember moving the body inside, laying it on the floor beside the shelf of almost-luck, covering it with the good cloth he saved for festival days. But he had, evidently, because that was where the courier lay now, peaceful as a customer waiting out the rain.

Paloma sat on his stool and held the map and shook.

Not dramatically. He was a composed man; composure was the load-bearing wall of his whole personality. He shook the way old houses shake in wind — quietly, continuously, in places no one could see.

*Paid in full. Soul intact, reserved. The Hollow keeps what it buys.*

Ten years. He counted backward through them the way you count stairs in the dark, testing each one. The shop. The charms. The first winter, when he'd woken in a rented room with an oath-mark on his wrist and a purse of coin he could not account for and a memory like a room after a fire — walls intact, contents gone. He had built a life in that empty room. He had built it carefully and called it choice.

Had he ever once, in ten years, wondered why he never wrote letters? Why he kept no portrait of family? Why the question *where are you from* produced in him a smooth, practiced, utterly contentless answer?

He had. Once. Early on. And then the wondering had slid off him like rain off wax, and he had gone back to knotting cord, and he understood now — with a horror that arrived not as a shout but as a very quiet, very clear voice — that the sliding-off had not been his doing.

Someone had paid for that smoothness. Someone had purchased his forgetting, itemized and dated, and filed it in a margin.

And the god. His god. The silence he had mourned every night for a decade, kneeling over his little tin icon of the kestrel, whispering *are you there, are you there, have I failed you* —

He hadn't failed. He'd been *sold*. Both of them. Master and servant, bundled together like a lot at auction, and Paloma had spent ten years grieving an abandonment that was actually a theft.

A laugh came out of him, cracked and disbelieving. "Well," he said to the corpse, to the rain, to the silent god. "That's embarrassing for both of us."

The map sat in his lap. The wax seal gleamed. Beneath his sleeve, the oath-mark pulsed faintly, arrhythmically, like a heart remembering its work — or like a voice on the far side of a wall, tapping.

He should burn it. That was the sensible move. The Wardens burned archives; the Wardens burned people who read archives. A man with a shop full of harmless trinkets had exactly one talent worth cultivating, and it was being forgettable. Take the map to the hearth. Mourn the courier. Go back to sleep.

Paloma looked at the dead man's face, at the smile still on it, and knew he wasn't going to burn it. Whoever the courier had been, he had run himself to death across half a country to put this in the hands of the one man whose name was written in its margins. That wasn't delivery. That was trust, placed blind, in a person the courier had never met — in the person Paloma had been before he was emptied out.

Somebody, somewhere, had thought that person was worth saving.

Paloma wanted to meet him.

He picked at the edge of the wax seal — and froze.

Boots. On Candle Lane. Not the scattered splash of passersby but the coordinated, deliberate tread of men who had trained together, six or more, coming up the lane at a walk that meant *we already know which door*.

Through the gap in his shutters he saw the lantern-light: grey cloaks, iron badges, the long staves the Wardens carried instead of swords because in Vharrow the law preferred its violence deniable.

They stopped outside his shop.

For one absurd moment Paloma considered the window display — *No Refunds, No Miracles* — as if the sign might protect him, as if ten years of honest mediocrity might be entered as evidence. Then the first blow landed on his door, and the little bell above it jumped on its hook, and the voice that came through the wood was polite the way winter is polite.

"Open in the name of the city. We know what was delivered to you."

Paloma stood. The map went inside his coat, against his chest, where a dying man had kept it warm. The oath-mark pulsed against his wrist like a second pulse, urgent now, almost frightened — or almost *hopeful*, and he couldn't tell the difference, and he suspected he was going to spend the rest of his life failing to tell the difference.

There was exactly one person in Vharrow who could read a document like this and had survived the spring's burning. Odile Vane. The archivist they'd exiled. The woman who drank.

The door shuddered under the second blow.

Paloma blew out his lamp, slipped through the back into the rain, and ran.
