## 3. The Archivist Who Burned Her Library

The Wardens were still hammering on his door when Paloma went out the window.

He had lived above his charm shop for ten years, and in all that time he had never once used the window as an exit. It was a narrow thing, painted shut twice over, and it opened onto the gutter of Tanner's Row with all the grace of a bad idea. He went through it anyway, elbow-first, taking a strip of paint and most of his dignity with him.

Below, Vharrow smelled of rain and frying onions and the particular sourness of a city pretending it wasn't afraid. Somewhere behind him, wood splintered — the door, not the window frame, thank every silent god — and a voice like wet gravel said, "Search the shop. He'll have bolt-holes. They always have bolt-holes."

Paloma did not have bolt-holes. He had one hole, and he was in it.

He ran.

* * *

Odile Vane lived at the top of Candlemaker's Stair, in a garret that had once been a dovecote and still smelled faintly of pigeon and old paper, which Odile insisted were the two great smells of civilization. Getting there meant crossing half the Lower City with a wax-sealed map pressed against his ribs and his heart going like a drummer who'd been paid by the hour.

Twice he ducked into doorways for Warden patrols. Once he knocked over a fishmonger's tray and apologized so profusely that the man gave him a herring out of sheer confusion. He carried the herring the rest of the way. It seemed rude to refuse.

The stair up to Odile's was forty-one steps. He counted them the way you count things when your mind wants something to hold onto that isn't *a dead man knew my name*. At the top, a green door with a brass knocker shaped like an owl, one eye worn smooth by decades of nervous hands.

He knocked.

"Closed," said a voice through the wood. "Permanently. Indefinitely. Go away."

"It's Paloma."

A pause. "The charm-seller?"

"The charm-seller."

Another pause, longer. "You're the only customer I've had in three years who wasn't a rat. Come in before the rats change their minds."

* * *

The garret was a library that had survived a fire and then decided to keep living there anyway. Books lined every wall, but they were wrong books — ledgers, almanacs, tax rolls, a complete annotated history of Vharrow's sewer commission in nine volumes. Nothing forbidden. Nothing worth burning.

In the middle of it all sat Odile Vane, gray-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be two pencils and a bone stylus she probably shouldn't have owned, spectacles pushed up on her forehead, a cup of tea gone cold beside her. She was fifty-ish and built like a question mark, and she looked at Paloma the way she looked at everything: as though checking him against a catalog.

"You're bleeding," she said.

"I'm fine."

"You're bleeding and carrying a herring. That's either a very good story or a very bad one." She took the herring from him, set it on a plate with the gravity of a museum acquisition, and pointed at a chair. "Sit. Talk."

So he talked. The courier collapsing in his doorway. The blood on his lips. The name — *his* name, spoken by a stranger with his last breath, a name Paloma had no memory of ever owning. The map. The pounding on the door. The window.

Odile listened without interrupting, which he suspected was her rarest skill. When he finished, she held out her hand.

"The map."

He gave it to her. She unrolled it across the table, weighted the corners with teacups and a shoe, and leaned close.

It was a map of the court beneath Mount Hollow. Paloma had looked at it a dozen times since dawn and understood nothing: corridors drawn in ink that shifted color when the light moved, doors marked with symbols like teeth, a great vaulted chamber at the heart rendered in something that wasn't quite silver and wasn't quite black. Around its edges crawled annotations in a spidery hand.

Odile made a sound. Not a word. A sound, low in her throat, the sound of a woman meeting an old enemy across a crowded room.

"You know it," Paloma said.

"I know *of* it." Her finger hovered over the central chamber without touching it, the way you hover near a stove you've been burned by. "I read about the Undercourt eleven years ago. In a text called the *Ledger of Small Surrenders*. Do you know what happened to me for reading it?"

"They burned your library."

"I burned my library," Odile said, with terrible precision. "They gave me the choice. Burn it myself and keep my hands, or let the Wardens burn it and lose those too." She flexed her fingers, looking at them. "I chose my hands. Some nights I'm proud of that. Most nights I'm not."

"What's the Undercourt?"

"A market." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *plague* or *war*. "But not of goods. Of souls. The magistrates below Mount Hollow buy and sell them — whole souls, fragments of souls, memories, oaths, names. Everything a person is can be weighed, priced, and traded down there. And the currency—" she tapped the shifting-ink corridors, "—the currency is other souls. That's how they keep it running. Everyone who buys has first to sell."

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. "And the map. Why would a courier die in my doorway carrying this?"

"I was getting to that." Odile pulled a lamp closer, bent over the margins, and went quiet in the way of someone reading something she wished she weren't. Her lips moved. Then stopped moving.

"Odile?"

She didn't answer. She turned the map ninety degrees, then back, tracing the spider-hand script with one finger, and Paloma watched her face do something complicated — surprise folding into pity folding into a kind of anger that had nowhere to go.

"Paloma Sterling," she said quietly. "That's the name the courier spoke?"

"Yes."

"It's written here. In the margin." She rotated the map toward him. "Alongside a date."

He read it. The letters swam, then steadied, then became the worst thing he'd ever read:

*P. Sterling — Kestrel-bearer — soul intact — purchased in full — 10 yrs past.*

Ten years past. Ten years and four days. The night the god went silent.

"That's impossible," Paloma heard himself say. His voice sounded very far away, like someone else speaking through a wall. "I still have my soul. I'm standing here. I'm — I'm *me*."

"You're standing here," Odile agreed gently. "But look at the entry. *Soul intact.* Not consumed. Not spent. Purchased and *held*." She pulled her spectacles down onto her nose and looked at him over them, and her eyes behind the glass were kind and furious at once. "Paloma. What did you feel, ten years ago? The night Kestrel went silent?"

"Nothing. That's the point. I woke up and the god was just — gone. The oath-mark went cold. I thought he'd abandoned me. I thought I'd failed some test I never knew I was taking." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Ten years. Ten years I've been selling charms that almost work and praying to a god who wouldn't spit on me if I was on fire, and the whole time—"

"The whole time he wasn't ignoring you." Odile finished it for him, softly. "He couldn't hear you. Your silence wasn't abandonment. It was theft."

The garret was very quiet. Somewhere below, a cart rattled past on Candlemaker's Stair, and a vendor cried the price of chestnuts, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary while Paloma sat in a chair and felt the floor of his life tilt.

Because here was the thing — here was the thing he had never told anyone, the thing he barely told himself: when Kestrel went silent, it hadn't felt like a door closing. It had felt like a *room* missing. Like walking into your own house and finding a wall gone where a wall should be, and everyone insisting the house had always been built that way. He had grieved the god. But underneath the grief, all these years, there had been this smaller, colder suspicion — that the shape of the loss was wrong. That more had been taken than a voice.

"They took my memories," he said. "Whatever I remembered of him. Of the oath. Of — of who I was when I swore it."

"And kept them as part of the purchase." Odile took off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, a fussy, human gesture that anchored the moment. "Souls are currency down there, but intact souls are collateral. They don't spend you. They *hold* you. Which means somewhere under that mountain is a vault with your name on it, and inside that vault is every piece of yourself you've been missing for ten years."

"Then I'll get it back."

"Paloma—"

"No." He stood up. His legs held, which surprised him. "I don't know how yet. I don't know anything except that a man died in my doorway to put this map in my hands, and that the Wardens want it badly enough to break down a charm-seller's door at dawn. Someone down there bought me, Odile. Bought me like a barrel of wine. And I am going to go down that mountain and take myself back."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Then she sighed — the sigh of a woman watching herself make a decision she'd already made.

"You'll need someone who can read that margin script," she said. "It's Hollow-court hand. There are perhaps four people in Vharrow who can, and I'm the only one who isn't dead or employed by the people who killed the other two." She was already pulling books off shelves, stacking them with the speed of long habit. "Which means you're stuck with me, and I'm stuck with you, and we're both probably going to die, and I want it noted that I said this was a bad idea."

"Noted."

"Good." She paused, a ledger in each hand, and something flickered across her face — old grief, quickly shelved. "There's one more thing. If we're doing this properly, we'll need more than a disgraced archivist and a bleeding charm-seller with a talent for windows. We'll need—" she waved a hand vaguely, "—assets. Resources. People who know things."

"People like who?"

Odile set the books down and smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile.

"There's a girl," she said, "who remembers everything she reads. And Paloma — the Wardens have burned a great many things. Everything they've burned, she's kept."
