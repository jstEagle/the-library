## 4. A Girl Who Remembers Everything

The orphanage at Candlewick Row had burned down twice, and both times the Wardens had signed the fire permits themselves. Odile Vane knew this because she had read the permits — before they were burned, along with everything else she'd ever loved.

She stood now in the alley behind the rebuilt building, in the gray hour between night and morning, and knocked on a door that was not really a door. It was a section of wall that looked like a door, which in Vharrow was often safer than being one.

"Password," said a voice from inside. Small. Unimpressed.

"Custard," Odile said.

"That was last week's password."

"It's always been custard. You change it every week to custard."

A pause, in which Odile could feel the child on the other side of the wall considering whether this was worth admitting. "It could have changed."

"Wren. Open the door."

The wall swung inward on greased hinges. The girl who stood there was small even for eleven, with ink-stained fingers and hair cropped short in the way of children who cut it themselves and would not be told otherwise. She looked up at Odile with the flat, patient expression of someone who has already read every book you own, including the ones you're embarrassed about.

"You smell like smoke and old paper," Wren said. "You've been to the Ash Market. You bought something wrapped in oilcloth because you were afraid of rain, but it didn't rain, so you wasted two coppers on the wrapping." She stepped aside. "Come in before someone sees you. Matron thinks I'm asleep. Matron is wrong about most things before dawn."

* * *

The room Wren kept was a closet that had once held brooms and now held books — or rather, had once held books, until the Wardens took them, and now held nothing but a pallet, a candle stub, and a girl who had read all of them anyway.

That was the thing about Wren. That was the whole impossible thing about her.

Odile had found her three years ago, shelving stolen volumes in the archive's restricted stacks — a child too small to reach the top shelves, reading them anyway from atop a ladder, lips moving. Odile had tested her, half as a joke. Handed her a treatise on pre-Prohibition warding law, four hundred pages of the driest prose ever set to type, and asked her to recite page ninety-one.

Wren had recited page ninety-one. Then pages one through four hundred, in order, without drawing breath, until Odile made her stop because her voice was going hoarse.

Perfect recall. Not cleverness — plenty of children were clever. This was something else, something the old texts called *mnemonic grace*, a gift so rare that the Hollow's magistrates had once paid handsomely for such minds. Before the Prohibition. Before the burning.

Now Wren sat cross-legged on her pallet while Odile unwrapped the oilcloth bundle on the floor between them: Paloma Sterling's map, and the courier's blood still dried brown in one corner like a signature.

"I need you to look at something," Odile said. "And then I need you to forget it."

Wren snorted. "I don't do that anymore."

"I know. That's why I said it. It's polite."

* * *

The map spread out under the candle stub's weak light. Odile watched the girl's eyes move across it — not scanning, *reading*, the way other people breathed. The court beneath Mount Hollow. The vault levels. The magistrates' circuit. And there, in the margins, in a hand Odile recognized from a thousand burned ledgers: the entry dates, the purchase codes, and one name written twice.

Paloma Sterling. Dated ten years ago. The same night Kestrel went silent.

"The Kestrel-bearer," Wren read aloud, softly. "'Soul intact, reserved.'" She looked up. "What does 'reserved' mean? Reserved like a table?"

"Reserved like a coat check," Odile said. "They took his memories when he wasn't looking and kept the receipt."

Wren was quiet for a moment, which for Wren was an event of some geological significance. Then she said, "I've seen that code before."

Odile's heart did something complicated. "Where."

"In the ledger fragment. The one Master Ellery made me memorize the night before they came for him." Her voice stayed level, but her fingers found the hem of her sleeve and began working it, fold and unfold, fold and unfold. "He said if he couldn't get out, then it should live somewhere they couldn't burn it. He said paper burns but—" she tapped her own temple, once, "—this doesn't. Mostly. I haven't died yet to check."

"Wren—"

"Page forty-one of the purchase ledger, fiscal year of the Silence." She closed her eyes. When she spoke again, it was in a different voice — flatter, older, Master Ellery's cadence worn into hers like a stone worn smooth by water. "'Item: one oath-bearer of the god Kestrel, acquired through covenant instrument, soul intact, reserved against future settlement. Note: bearer's memory of the covenant to be collected at acquisition. God's voice held as collateral pending full consumption.' End of entry." She opened her eyes. "There's more after it. Do you want the rest?"

Odile found she had to swallow before she could speak. "Yes."

"'Settlement scheduled upon completion of the decade term. Bearer to present himself or be collected. Magistrate Vess assures the court the subject will come willingly; subjects always come for their names.'"

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"He'll come willingly," Wren repeated, in her own voice now, and there was something almost gentle in it. "Because of us, probably. Because you'll tell him what I just told you, and he'll go down that mountain to get himself back, and the magistrates will be waiting with the receipt." She shrugged, small shoulders under a patched shirt. "That's how traps work. I've read four hundred and six books about traps. Well — three hundred and eighty. Some of them were about pies."

Despite everything — despite the dead courier, the burning city behind her, the ten-year-old crime unfolding in a child's borrowed voice — Odile laughed. It came out cracked, but it came.

"You're going to be insufferable when you grow up."

"I'm already insufferable. Ask anyone. Ask Matron. Actually don't ask Matron, she'll say worse things than insufferable, and two of them are true."

* * *

Later — much later, when the candle had been replaced by daylight leaking thin through the wall-door — Odile sat with her back against the broom-closet wall and let herself think the thought she'd been avoiding since the courier fell into Paloma's doorway.

She had lost people to the Hollow. Not to its magistrates directly, not dramatically, not in any way that made a good story. She had lost them the way the court preferred: slowly, bureaucratically. Her husband had sold a memory first — just one, he'd said, just the funeral of his father, to pay a debt — and had come home lighter, easier, wrong. He hadn't remembered why he wept at certain songs. Within two years he'd sold enough of himself that he'd sold his marriage too, cheerfully, unable to recall what it had weighed. Her daughter had gone the other way: taken whole, purchased by a magistrate whose bone mask Odile had seen only once, across a courtroom, and never stopped seeing since.

So Odile had done the only thing left. She had read. Everything. The forbidden ledgers, the sealed contracts, the court's entire architecture of theft — and when they finally came for her library, she had stood in the doorway of her own archive and refused to move until they burned it around her, because a library that can be taken was never safe, and she would rather watch it die free than live caged.

She had thought, for ten years, that she was finished. That grief was simply the room she lived in now.

And then a hollow-eyed charm-seller had turned up on her step with a map and a name in the margins, and within a day there was talk of a thief with no shadow, and here was a child who was a living record of everything the Wardens had ever burned, and Odile had caught herself — caught herself actually counting them, actually thinking the word *we*.

It was a trap. She knew it was a trap. The magistrates had built it that way, patient as interest accruing.

But sitting in a broom closet with a girl who remembered everything, watching dawn come up over a city that had outlawed wonder, Odile found she did not care. Let it be a trap. Traps could be sprung from the inside. She'd read four hundred books about that too — well, she'd read everything about everything, that was rather the point of her.

"Wren," she said. "If I asked you to come with us — down the mountain, into the court — what would you say?"

Wren considered this with the seriousness of a magistrate reviewing a contract. "I'd say you need me. Your map is incomplete — the servant tunnels aren't marked, and neither are the memory-toll gates, and if you walk into a gate without knowing what it takes, it takes whatever it likes." She paused. "Also I'd say yes. Obviously. But it's nice that you asked like I had a choice."

"You do have a choice."

"No," Wren said, matter-of-fact, folding her sleeve hem one last time and letting it go. "I memorized the ledger. Which means the ledger lives in me. Which means they'll come for me eventually, the way they came for Master Ellery, the way they came for your—" She stopped herself, having read enough of Odile's face over three years to know where that sentence ended. "So I'd rather be walking toward them than waiting for them. Waiting is just walking very slowly."

Odile reached out and straightened the girl's crooked collar, because someone should, and because her hands needed to do something that wasn't shaking.

"Then we go tonight," she said. "There's one more person we need. A thief, apparently. Paloma swears he knows the tunnels."

"A thief with no shadow," Wren said. "Yes. I know about him too."

Odile blinked. "How could you possibly—"

"Matron talks in her sleep," Wren said, "and I sleep lightly, and I never forget anything." She was already pulling on her boots, already grinning, quick and bright as a struck match. "Also, Odile? He owes money to everyone on Candlewick Row, and last month he tried to sell Matron a map of the Undercourt that I'm fairly certain he drew himself, because it gets the river wrong."

She hopped off the pallet and pulled open the wall-door onto the waking street.

"So if we're going to recruit him," Wren said, "we should hurry. Before he sells our names to somebody first."
