## 5. The Thief Without a Shadow

The thief without a shadow was drinking Paloma's wine.

He sat in Odile's kitchen with his boots on the table, a lean man in a coat the color of wet slate, and where the lamplight fell across him it simply... stopped. It pooled on the floorboards around his chair, touched the edges of his sleeves, and refused to go further. He cast nothing. He was a hole in the shape of a man, and he drank like a man who had never once been offered anything for free.

"More," said Sef.

"That's my third bottle," Paloma said.

"Your cellar. Your hospitality." Sef tipped the bottle over his glass again. "Besides, you'll want me agreeable when I tell you what this is going to cost."

Odile stood by the stove with her arms crossed, radiating the particular patience of a woman who had spent thirty years cataloguing other people's nonsense. Wren sat on the counter beside her, legs swinging, watching the place where Sef's shadow should have been with the frank hunger of a girl who memorized everything she saw and wanted to know why this one thing refused to be recorded.

"You're standing in your own light," Wren said. "Did you know that? The lamp's behind you and there's no shadow in front of you at all. That's not possible."

"Nothing about me is possible, little scribe. That's rather the point of me." Sef set down the glass and looked at Paloma across the table. "So. You want into Mount Hollow. You want the servant tunnels, the ones that don't appear on any map the magistrates let exist. I can take you there. I've walked them since I was small enough to fit inside a flour barrel."

"And the price?" Paloma asked.

Sef smiled. It was not a comforting smile. "You've heard how the Undercourt works, yes? Nothing is taken. Everything is *bought*. And the gate doesn't take coin." He turned his glass slowly, watching the wine turn. "Every traveler who passes through surrenders one memory. One. Chosen by the gate, not by you. It reaches into your head and plucks out whatever it likes best, and you walk through knowing something is gone but never what. Like waking with a word on the tip of your tongue that will stay there forever."

The kitchen went quiet. Outside, Vharrow's evening bells rang the hour — the ones dedicated to gods nobody believed in anymore, rung out of habit, out of stubbornness, out of the city's long habit of pretending its hollow places weren't hollow.

"I sold mine already," Sef said, before anyone could ask. "Not a memory. My shadow. Ten years ago, same as your ledger date, as it happens. I owed money to men who collect debts in ways you don't want described after supper. The Undercourt bought my shadow off me for passage out, clean and final. A man without a shadow can't be tracked by certain kinds of hunters. Can't be bound by certain kinds of oaths." He flexed his fingers. "Can't be *held*, if you understand me. It seemed a fair trade at the time."

"And now?" Odile asked.

"Now I walk under noon suns and feel the light pass through me like I'm made of smoke." He shrugged, and the gesture had no weight behind it, none at all. "Now children point. Now dogs won't sleep near me. Now I stand in front of mirrors and half expect to see straight through." Another pull of wine. "It was a fair trade. I say that every morning. Some mornings I almost believe it."

Wren slid off the counter. She crossed the kitchen and stood beside Sef's chair, studying the floor where his shadow ought to have lain.

"When the gate takes our memories," she said, "will we know? Afterward?"

"You'll feel the shape of the absence," Sef said. "Like a tooth pulled. You'll keep touching the gap."

"Then I'll write down everything important first," she said, matter-of-fact. "I'll make lists. Names, dates, faces. If the gate takes something, I'll read my own handwriting and know what's missing even if I can't remember it."

Paloma felt something twist gently in his chest. "That won't work, little one. If they take the memory, the words won't mean anything—"

"They took your memories ten years ago," Wren said. "All of them. And you still came looking. You still knew something was missing. Didn't you?"

He had no answer for that. Ten years of selling charms that almost worked, ten years of praying to a god who didn't answer, ten years of waking some mornings with grief so large and sourceless it felt like weather — and he had never once thought to ask *why*. Because there was nothing to ask about. The wound had been closed so neatly he'd mistaken it for skin.

"The gate will be disappointed with me, then," Paloma said finally. "I'm told I have almost nothing left to give."

Sef looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, warm and rough. "Oh, that's beautiful. That's genuinely beautiful. The Hollow took payment from you *in advance*." He wiped his eyes. "Do you know what that makes you?"

"A fraud?"

"A regular," Sef said. "A valued customer. They'll wave you through, friend. 'Ah, the Kestrel-bearer, back again, no charge, please enjoy your visit.'" His grin faded into something more careful. "Which means the rest of us are paying full price so you can walk in free. I'd like everyone to understand that going in. I'd like everyone to choose it with their eyes open."

"We have," Odile said. "I burned my library for less than this."

"You burned your library for exactly this," Sef said. "For the principle that people shouldn't be erased. I read about you, Archivist. Before they stripped your credentials, your name was in three of the books I stole."

"Then you're better read than most thieves."

"I'm better read than most archivists. I just prefer taking books to keeping them." He raised his glass to her. To Wren. To Paloma last, and longest. "So here's my terms, and they're simple. I get you through the tunnels — three days underground, cold, dark, and smelling of things I won't describe. Nobody touches my share of whatever we find. And when this is done—" he paused, and for the first time the flippancy dropped away entirely, leaving something raw underneath, "—when this is done, I want my shadow back. Whatever vault they keep it in. Whatever jar, whatever ledger line. I want it."

"You'll have it," Paloma said. "We'll all take back what's ours. That's the whole point."

"See, that's the part I don't believe," Sef said cheerfully. "Nobody gets anything back from the Hollow. But I like the way you lie. It's an honest sort of lying. You almost believe it yourself."

* * *

They left before dawn, because Sef insisted the tunnels' upper mouths were only unwatched in the hour when even Wardens dreamed. Odile carried her satchel of tools and her father's compass, which pointed not north but toward whatever the holder most feared — a useless heirloom, she'd always said, except on days like this. Wren carried paper, ink, and three days of memorized maps recited twice nightly like prayers. Paloma carried the courier's map against his chest and the weight of a name he couldn't remember owning.

And Sef carried nothing at all, which was, he explained, the entire secret of carrying things well.

The tunnel mouth opened behind a tannery on Vharrow's eastern edge, beneath a grate furred with ten years of rust. Sef lifted it as though it weighed nothing, dropped into the dark, and caught Paloma's arm when he stumbled on the ladder.

"Careful," Sef murmured. "First rule of the underways: the dark down here isn't empty. It's *full*. Of old air, old sounds, old bargains. Walk politely."

They walked politely. For hours. The passages narrowed and widened like the throat of some enormous patient animal, and somewhere in the blackness water dripped in rhythms too regular to be natural. Wren recited turns and landmarks in a whisper. Odile marked their path with chalk in a cipher of her own devising. And Paloma found himself walking beside a man who cast no light-shape at all, so that in the lantern glow Sef seemed to flicker at the edges, less present than the stone around him.

"Does it hurt?" Paloma asked quietly. "Being without it."

Sef was silent long enough that Paloma thought he wouldn't answer.

"You know what a shadow is?" he said at last. "It's proof. Proof you're solid, that you take up room, that the world has to go around you. Mine's gone, and some days I swear I could put my hand through my own chest." He glanced sideways, and in the lantern light his eyes were very tired and very young. "But here's the joke of it, Kestrel-bearer. Down here, in the dark, nobody has shadows anyway. Down here I'm just like everyone else. First time in ten years I've felt normal is walking toward the place that robbed me."

Paloma laughed despite himself, a small startled sound in the dark. "That's the bleakest thing anyone's ever said to me."

"I have worse. I save them for emergencies."

* * *

They camped in a widening of the tunnel where someone long ago had built a hearth, its stones blackened by fires older than the city above. While Wren slept with her head on Odile's knee and Odile pretended she wasn't stroking the girl's hair, Sef produced a battered tin of tea leaves from nowhere and brewed a pot over embers.

"To family," he said, handing out chipped cups. "Since apparently that's what we are now. The damaged, the disgraced, the dis-shadowed, and the dis-souled. We should get a crest."

"A banner," Odile agreed dryly. "A hollow circle on a field of ash."

"I'll embroider it," said Wren, without opening her eyes. "I remember every stitch pattern I've ever seen."

Paloma held his cup and watched the firelight move across these three strangers who were no longer quite strangers, and felt the strange ache in his chest shift — not the old sourceless grief, but something newer, warmer, more frightening. He had spent ten years alone without knowing he was mourning company. Now here it was, offered over bad tea in a hole in the world: a woman who burned libraries to keep truth alive, a child who was herself a library, a thief who had sold his proof of existence and joked about it so he wouldn't scream about it.

"Tomorrow we reach the gate," Sef said. His voice had lost its humor entirely. "So tonight, each of you — think about what you can't bear to lose. Name it to yourselves. Hold onto it tight. The gate takes what it likes, but it likes best what you clutch hardest. So hold loosely, friends. Hold everything loosely from here on."

He looked at Paloma then, and something in his face was almost gentle.

"All except you," Sef said softly. "You hold on to whatever's left. Even if it's only the ache. Especially if it's only the ache. That ache is yours, Kestrel-bearer. Don't let them have the last piece."

The fire settled. Somewhere far below them, deeper than any tunnel Sef had mapped, something vast turned over in its sleep — and Paloma felt the oath-mark beneath his sleeve grow warm for the first time in ten years, like a hand remembering how to grip.

At dawn they would reach the Gate of Small Surrenders, and Paloma would learn what it meant to arrive at a door with nothing left to pay — and what the door did with people like that.
