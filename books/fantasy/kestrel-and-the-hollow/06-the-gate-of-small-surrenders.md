## 6. The Gate of Small Surrenders

Wren had memorized the servant tunnels before they ever set foot in them, which was fortunate, because Sef's directions consisted mostly of "left here, probably" and "don't touch that, it bites."

She had read the tunnel maps three years ago, in the weeks before the Wardens came for the archive on Candlemaker's Row. She'd been nine. The archivists had let her sit in the map room because she was quiet and small and could recite any page she'd seen exactly once, and so when the fires came she had carried out more books than anyone — not in her arms, but in her head. Eleven hundred pages, give or take. She kept them all in order, like beads on a string.

Now she walked at the front of the line with a lantern held high, reciting turns under her breath.

"Passage narrows after the third arch. There's a false wall on the left — don't bother with it, it leads to a cistern full of dead pigeons. Sef, stop humming."

"I'm not humming," said Sef.

"You're always humming. It's how you think. Odile says so."

Odile, behind her with the second lantern, made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a cough. She had been quiet since they'd left Vharrow's last lamp behind them. Wren had noticed that adults went quiet in two ways: the way before crying, and the way before deciding something. She hadn't yet worked out which this was.

Paloma brought up the rear. He had barely spoken since the gate question was first raised, two nights ago around a fire, when Sef had explained it in his cheerful, terrible way.

"Every traveler pays," Sef had said, turning a stolen spoon over his knuckles. "One memory at the gate. That's the toll. Small ones pass through easy. Big ones cost you your footing for a while."

"What counts as big?" Paloma had asked.

Sef had looked at him then — really looked, the way he almost never did — and said, "You'll know when you're standing in front of it."

Wren had spent the whole walk down trying to decide what she would pay. This was harder than it sounded. She remembered everything, which meant every memory she owned was load-bearing. Take one out and things collapsed around it. Her memory of learning letters was tied to the memory of Odile teaching her, which was tied to the memory of the archive burning, which was tied to the promise she'd made over the ashes. Pull one thread and the whole garment came apart in her hands.

So she had chosen carefully. And she had chosen the snow.

* * *

The gate was not what she expected. She had read descriptions of it — there were three in the burned books, and one in a magistrate's confession she'd memorized off a confiscated letter — and none of them agreed. In truth it was simply a door, set into the living rock where the tunnel widened into a rough chamber. Grey wood, grey stone, no ornament. Above the lintel, carved in letters worn nearly smooth:

*ALL WHO ENTER LEAVE SOMETHING OF THEMSELVES.*

"Well, that's friendly," said Sef.

"It's honest," said Odile. "That's worse."

The door had no handle. As they approached, a seam opened in its center, and from the seam drifted a light — pale, patient, neither warm nor cold. Wren felt it settle against her mind the way a librarian's hand settles on a book spine: measuring, cataloguing, waiting.

"It knows we're here," she whispered.

"It always knows," said Sef. He stepped forward first, chin up, wearing his thief's grin like armor. "Ladies first is for parlors. Tunnels are mine." But his hand, when he raised it to the seam, shook slightly, and Wren pretended not to see, because pretending not to see was a kindness she'd learned from Odile.

The light touched him.

Wren watched it happen — watched the pale glow sink into Sef's palm and then up his arm, searching, sorting. His face went slack. For a moment he looked younger, and lost, and very far away.

Then he gasped, staggered back, and pressed both hands to his chest as though checking that everything was still inside.

"Gone?" Odile asked quietly.

"My mother's face." He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but the lightness had cracks in it. "I remember that I had one. I remember she sang. I remember her hands were strong. But the face itself—" He laughed, short and awful. "It's just a blank now. A shape where a face should be. Ten years I've carried that face through every dark place in this city, and I've gone and left it at the door like a coat."

"Sef—" Wren began.

"Don't." He held up a finger. "Don't be kind to me right now. I'll fall apart, and I'm the one who knows the way from here." He turned to the door, squared his shoulders, and added, softer, almost to himself: "Worth it, though. If it works. Worth anything, if it works."

He pushed the door, and it swung open onto darkness and cold air smelling of deep stone.

* * *

Odile went next.

She stood before the seam for a long moment, lantern lowered, and Wren saw her lips move — not words, exactly. A rehearsal. A goodbye.

"Odile," Wren said. "You don't have to tell me what you're giving. You don't have to say it out loud."

"I know, little wren." Odile smiled down at her, and her eyes were wet and steady at once. "But you'll want to know later. When I can't tell you. So I'll say it now, while it's still mine to say." She drew a breath. "My wedding day."

Wren knew about the wedding day. Odile had told her once, by firelight — the rain on the awning, the borrowed ring, her husband Tomas laughing so hard during the vows that the officiant had to start over twice. Tomas who had died in the court's purges. Tomas whose name Odile never said anymore except on that one day, which she kept folded up inside herself like a letter she reread.

And now she would keep the fact of it and lose the living thing. She would know she had been married and feel nothing where the rain had been.

"Will you still know you loved him?" Wren asked. It was the most important question she had ever asked anyone.

Odile considered it honestly, which was the only way she ever considered anything. "I'll know it the way you know a sum," she said. "Two plus two. True, but cold." She touched Wren's cheek. "Guard it for me, will you? You remember everything. Somewhere in that head of yours, let my wedding stay warm."

"I will," Wren said fiercely. "Rain on the awning. Borrowed ring. Laughing twice through the vows. I have it. I'll keep it exact."

Odile kissed the top of her head, turned, and gave her wedding day to the door.

She didn't cry. She stood very straight while the light searched her, and when it withdrew she blinked twice, slowly, like someone surfacing from deep water, and said, "How strange. I feel lighter and robbed at the same time." Then, frowning faintly: "Who was Tomas?"

And Wren's heart broke cleanly in two, because she had promised, and so she said: "Your husband. Rain on the awning. He laughed through the vows and you made the officiant start over twice, and you loved him so much you kept the day folded up like a letter."

Odile stared at her. Something moved behind her eyes — grief arriving without its address, knocking at a door that no longer opened inward. "Did I," she said softly. "Well. Keep it safe, little wren. It sounds like it mattered."

Then she walked through the gate with her back straight, and Wren memorized the sound of her footsteps so she could describe them to her someday, if the memories ever found their way home.

* * *

Her own turn came last, before Paloma's.

She had rehearsed this too. She stood before the seam and held out her hand, and the light poured over her fingers, gentle as milk.

*First snowfall,* she thought at it, deliberately, the way you'd hand over a coin you'd counted twice. *Take the first snowfall.*

She was four in that memory. The archive courtyard, fat flakes coming down through the smoke of the city, and the old archivist Master Corvin lifting her up so she could catch flakes on her tongue, and everyone laughing because she'd sneezed. It was the oldest memory she had. It was the first page of her.

The light took it.

She felt the page lift out of her — felt the exact place where it had been bound into her, the way you feel a tooth missing with your tongue. She still knew that snow existed. She still knew she'd seen it. But the seeing itself was gone; there was only a smooth white blank where a four-year-old's wonder used to live.

She walked through the gate on legs that felt borrowed, and Paloma caught her elbow steadying her, and she heard him say, low and rough, "Thank you. Both of you. Whatever happens past this door—"

Then it was his turn.

* * *

Paloma faced the seam the way a man faces a debt collector.

"I don't have much," Wren heard him say. Not to them — to the door. "Ten years of selling charms that almost work. Bad lodgings. Cheap stew. A few friends I don't deserve. Take one of those. Take the taste of the stew, even. I won't miss it."

The light flowed over his hand.

And stopped.

It pooled in his palm, brightened, dimmed, brightened again — searching, Wren realized, the way she searched a shelf for a book she knew wasn't there. It sank up his arm, across his chest, and Paloma's breath went ragged. The light pressed deeper, harder, glowing through his skin like a lamp behind parchment, and Wren saw his face change — saw horror arrive in stages, the way dawn arrives, gray and then grayer.

"No," he whispered. "No, take it. Take anything. Take the stew—"

The light withdrew. It hung in the seam, flickering, and Wren — who remembered everything, who had read the ledger fragment forty times until the letters lived behind her eyelids — understood one heartbeat before anyone said it aloud.

*The Kestrel-bearer, soul intact, reserved.*

"He has nothing to give," she said. Her voice came out very small in the stone chamber. "The gate can't find anything. Because—" She looked at Paloma, at the hollow oath-mark hidden under his sleeve, at ten years of a man praying to a god who never answered. "Because they already took payment. Ten years ago. At the purchase. He paid at the gate before he ever knew there was a gate."

The chamber was silent except for the flicker of the seam-light.

"They took everything worth taking," Odile said slowly, "and left him enough to walk and work and grieve. They hollowed him in advance and called it a sale." Her voice, stripped of its own wedding day, was flat as slate — but her hand found Paloma's shoulder and gripped it hard. "You're not empty, Paloma. You're robbed. There's a difference, and I intend to make the court learn it."

Paloma stared at the door. Wren watched him do arithmetic with his face — the same arithmetic she'd done on the walk down, threads and garments, what holds up what. Ten years of silence. Ten years of a god who seemed to have abandoned him. And it had never been abandonment. It had been theft, committed against him while he slept, priced into a contract he couldn't remember signing.

"The gate wants payment," he said at last. His voice was hoarse. "Fine. Then it can have an IOU. Tell it—" he almost laughed, and it was a terrible, wonderful sound, "—tell it the Hollow owes me. I'm collecting, not paying."

The seam-light flickered — and, Wren would have sworn afterward, flickered *amused* — and the door swung wide.

Beyond it, stairs descended into blue-dark, and the air rising up from below smelled of candlewax, old paper, and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going soft. Somewhere far beneath them, faint as a pulse, something vast shifted its weight.

Wren took Paloma's hand. It was cold.

"Whatever's down there," she said, "I'll remember all of it. Every detail. Even if they take more from us — even if they take me — somewhere in the world there'll be a true record. That's what I'm for."

Paloma looked down at her, and for the first time since the courier died in his doorway, he smiled like it meant it.

"Then let's go give them something to write down."

They descended together, four small debts walking into the mountain that had bought them — and far below, in a vault lined with jars, something that had once been a god's voice stirred in its glass, and waited.
