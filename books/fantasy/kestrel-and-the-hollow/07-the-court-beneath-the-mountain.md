## 7. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The tunnels ended the way all bad ideas end: suddenly, and in a room far too grand for what they'd done to deserve it.

Paloma stepped out of Sef's servant passage — a crawl of stone that had smelled, for the last three hours, like old candles and older regret — and into light. Not torchlight. Not the gray seep of the mountain. Light that came from nowhere at all, falling evenly on marble floors and pillars carved to look like trees that had never grown anywhere honest.

"Right," Sef said behind him, casting no shadow on the marble, which somehow made the floor look *more* shadowed, not less. "When I said 'the Undercourt,' I may have undersold the ceilings."

The ceilings were the problem, Paloma decided. They were high enough to be a weather system. Somewhere up in that darkness, water dripped in slow, deliberate rhythm, like the mountain itself counting.

Wren pressed close to Odile's side, her eyes moving constantly — reading the walls, the pillars, the faint script inlaid in silver along the baseboards. "It's a ledger," she whispered. "The whole room. The carvings are entries."

"What kind of entries?" Odile asked, though Paloma could hear in her voice that she already knew. Archivists always knew. It was their curse.

"The kind where the currency column is people."

They stood in an antechamber of some kind — a waiting room, Paloma realized with a lurch, *for souls*. Benches lined the walls, polished smooth by centuries of the seated. At the far end, doors of black wood stood open onto a hall beyond, and from that hall came a sound he felt in his teeth before he understood it with his ears: voices. Many voices. All speaking at once, none interrupting each other, the way a market sounds from two streets away.

Except no market sounded like this. This was the sound of *appraisal*.

"Last chance to develop a sudden passion for surface living," Sef offered. "I hear Vharrow's lovely this time of year. Oppressive, but lovely."

"We've come ten years too late to turn around," Paloma said. "I just didn't know it until now."

He touched his sleeve, where the oath-mark sat beneath the cloth — the pale scar of Kestrel's silence, the thing he'd spent a decade hiding from Wardens and explaining away to customers. *Old burn,* he told them. *Candle accident.* Ten years of small lies, and every one of them had been true in a way he hadn't known: he'd been hiding evidence.

Odile adjusted the satchel of forged credentials at her hip. Wren took a breath and squared her small shoulders like a soldier reviewing her one order: *remember everything.* Sef checked knives Paloma sincerely hoped were decorative.

They walked through the black doors together.

* * *

The hall of the Undercourt was built for a congregation of thousands and held perhaps forty, and somehow that was worse. Magistrates moved between benches of bone-white masks — long-faced, eyeless, serene — conducting business in murmurs. And everywhere, everywhere, were the jars.

They sat in niches along the walls, hundreds of them, glowing faintly with a light like memory of light. Some pulsed slow as sleeping breath. Some flickered, frantic. One near the entrance glowed a steady, stubborn gold, and when Paloma passed it he felt something brush against him — familiar, brief, gone — like a stranger wearing a friend's coat.

"Don't touch anything," Odile breathed. "Don't *look* at anything too long. In a place like this, attention is a transaction."

"I've been looking at you for two days," Sef said. "What do I owe?"

"Everything you have, probably."

"That's reassuringly cheap."

Paloma barely heard them. Because ahead, at the center of the hall, on a dais of dark stone, stood a magistrate who was not moving among the others but waiting — still as furniture, mask tilted slightly, as though it had been watching the doors since before they opened.

And beneath the bone face, Paloma felt it: recognition. Cold and patient and horribly *proprietary*, like a hand closing over something that had been set aside for safekeeping.

"You," the magistrate said. Its voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. It had the warmth of a shopkeeper greeting a regular. "You're punctual. We did note that about you, in the margins. Punctual. Honest in small transactions. A tidy soul." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Intact."

"I want it back," Paloma said.

Around them, the murmur of commerce faltered — not stopped, nothing so dramatic, but thinned, the way a room quiets when someone says something either very foolish or very interesting. Masks turned. Jars flickered.

"Back," the magistrate repeated, tasting the word. "That presumes it was ever yours to begin with. Come. Walk with me. Negotiation is so much more civil than demands, don't you find?"

It descended from the dais and drifted toward a side gallery, and Paloma — because his legs apparently held their own opinions now, and those opinions were *ten years late* — followed. He heard Odile shift behind him, heard Wren's quick breath, and raised one hand slightly: *stay.* If this went wrong, someone needed to be free to run, and it wasn't going to be the man whose soul was already in escrow.

The gallery overlooked the vault.

Paloma stopped walking.

Below, through a floor of iron lattice, lay a chamber lit by no light he could name, and in its center, on a pedestal of black glass, sat a jar larger than the others. Larger, and brighter, and *wrong* — because the light inside it didn't glow so much as *listen*. It shifted when he moved. It leaned, if light could lean, toward the lattice, toward him, the way a dog leans at a door it has waited behind for years.

His knees nearly went.

"That," the magistrate said gently, "is collateral. The voice of your god, held against your account. You understand how lending works, I hope? Nothing is given freely down here. When we purchased you — your service, your silence, your little charms that almost work — payment was required. And you had nothing, then. No coin. No name worth taking; names are cheap. So you paid with the only thing of value you carried." The mask tilted. "Your oath. Your god's voice in you. We took the contract's price from the only place it lived."

"You're lying," Paloma said. His voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice.

"Am I? Recall the night. Ten years ago. You knelt. You swore yourself to Kestrel — aloud, witnessed, binding. And within the hour, you woke on the mountainside with a mark and a silence and a decade of forgetting ahead of you. You thought the god abandoned you." The magistrate's tone softened into something almost kind, which was its own obscenity. "Gods don't abandon, little bearer. *Contracts* foreclose."

The rage arrived slowly, which surprised him. He'd expected it to come like fire. Instead it came like water rising in a sealed room — quiet, total, pressing against his ribs from the inside. Ten years. Ten years of lighting candles to a silence. Ten years of believing himself unworthy of an answer, of praying into a well and hearing only his own heart fall. He had built his whole life — the stall, the harmless charms, the careful small goodness of it — on the foundation of *he does not answer me*, and the foundation had been a receipt.

"You silenced him," Paloma said. "To buy *me*."

"To buy your *oath*, which is to say, to buy you. The god's voice was simply where it was kept." The magistrate spread its hands, a merchant displaying fairness. "And consider what you received in exchange! Ten years of safety. Of obscurity. The Wardens never marked you — we saw to that. You wanted a quiet life selling trinkets in a godless city. We provided the godless city." A pause, and the mask seemed almost to smile. "You have been our most satisfied customer."

The sound Paloma made was not a word. Several nearby jars flickered hard, as if flinching.

"There it is," the magistrate observed, with scholarly interest. "Rage. We rarely get to see it in person. Most sellers are numb by collection day." It turned, robes whispering, and gestured at the vault below, at the listening light. "But let us speak practically, since you came all this way through my mountain's teeth. The account stands. The collateral stands. But accounts can be settled, bearer. There are terms by which a soul returns to its—"

"Its owner?" Paloma said. "Which one?"

"—its origin," the magistrate continued smoothly, "and I am authorized to offer one. Renounce the oath. Formally, here, before the court. The contract voids, the collateral releases, and you walk out of my mountain whole. Memory, soul, silence lifted — everything restored." The pleasant voice dropped half a register, conspiratorial. "You would be free of him, at last. Truly free. Has it occurred to you that you have never once been free *of* Kestrel? Even silent, even stolen, he has been the shape of your whole life. The mark. The hiding. The fear. Give him back his voice and take back your self, and see what remains. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps that frightens you more than us."

And there — there was the intimate corruption of it, Paloma thought, standing in the beautiful cold light. Not cruelty. Cruelty he knew how to face. This was *care*. This was power offering to solve your life for you, kindly, reasonably, at the low low price of everything you actually were.

Because the magistrate was right about one thing, and Paloma hated it: Kestrel had been the shape of his life. The hollow ache had been a compass. Every charm he'd sold honestly, every small mercy he'd done in a city that outlawed mercy's cousins — he'd done them *toward* that silence, aiming at a god who wouldn't answer, because answering or not, the aim was the prayer.

Take away the silence, and what remained?

A man named Paloma Sterling, who sold trinkets.

"No," he said.

The word fell into the gallery and rang there.

"No?" The magistrate's serenity cracked, just at one edge, like ice under a boot. "You would refuse wholeness? Refuse your own soul, returned, restored, *yours again* — for a voice that left you nothing for ten years?"

"He didn't leave me nothing." Paloma looked down through the lattice at the leaning light, and his voice steadied the way it never did at his stall, the way it only did when it wasn't only his. "He left me the wanting. That was the whole message. That was enough."

Below, the light in the great jar flared — once, hard, like a struck bell made visible — and every jar in the hall answered it. Hundreds of them, gold and green and grieving, pulsing in a single wave that rolled through the Undercourt like a shudder through a body.

The magistrate went very still.

"That," it said quietly, all the pleasantness gone, "was not supposed to be possible."

From across the hall, Wren's voice rang out, high and clear and absolutely delighted, the voice of a girl who had just read the last line of a very good book:

"Odile! The ledger! The fraud entry — it's *here*, it's carved into the dais, and the record-keeper is standing right next to it!"

The magistrate turned its bone face toward the sound.

Paloma ran for the stairs.
