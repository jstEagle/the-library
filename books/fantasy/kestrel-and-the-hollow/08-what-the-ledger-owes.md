## 8. What the Ledger Owes

Odile Vane had spent thirty years among records, and she knew the way a room full of paper breathed. The Hall of Accounts beneath Mount Hollow did not breathe at all. It *held its breath* — ten thousand soul-jars racked in niches like wine bottles, each one faintly luminous, each one labeled in a hand so precise it made her fingers itch with professional envy.

She hated it here. She also, shamefully, admired it.

"Stop fidgeting," she murmured to Paloma.

"I'm not fidgeting."

"You're vibrating. You vibrate when you're frightened. You've been doing it since the gate." She adjusted her spectacles — a prop, down here, where no one needed spectacles, but they gave her something to do with her hands that wasn't wringing them. "Stand still and look like merchandise. Merchandise doesn't vibrate."

Paloma stood still. Mostly.

They had been waiting an hour in the antechamber of the Record-Keep, on benches of black stone polished smooth by centuries of anxious petitioners. Somewhere below, Sef and Wren were supposed to be slipping into the vault through a maintenance shaft Sef swore he remembered from his shadow-selling days. Odile was trying very hard not to think about an eleven-year-old crawling through the dark toward the most dangerous room in the Undercourt, because if she thought about it she would go after her, and if she went after her the whole plan would collapse like a badly shelved archive.

Instead she recited Wren's ledger fragment in her head, for the hundredth time. The girl had memorized it off a burning page three years ago, one of thousands the Wardens fed to their pyres, and had carried it since the way other children carry a stone in their pocket.

*Entry 4,417. One soul, bearer-marked, sold by oath-holder to the Undercourt. Consideration: silence of the god Kestrel, in perpetuity. Entered by order of Magistrate Corvane. Witnessed: none.*

Witnessed: none. That was the crack in the wall. Every legitimate entry in the court's books required two witnesses. Odile had spent her exile learning the court's own rules the way a locksmith learns locks — lovingly, obsessively, with the eventual aim of picking them.

The doors opened without anyone opening them.

"Petitioners," said a voice like dry leaves being sorted.

The Record-Keep was not what Odile expected, though she should have known better than to expect anything monstrous. It was a small man. Small and neat, with ink-stained fingers and half-moon spectacles exactly like hers, and he looked at her the way she had once looked at a first edition: with hunger held carefully behind courtesy.

"An archivist," he said. "Exiled. I know your work, Madame Vane. Your cataloguing of the Warden burn lists was *elegant*. A waste, what happened to you."

"A great many things are a waste," Odile said. "That's rather why I'm here."

* * *

The negotiation happened over tea, which Odile found either reassuring or deeply sinister, and she never quite settled which.

The Record-Keep's office was lined floor to ceiling with ledgers, and he poured for her himself, and for one dangerous moment they were simply two people who loved books talking about books. He asked about her marginalia system. He complimented her cross-referencing. She caught herself enjoying it and put a stop to it firmly, the way you'd step on your own foot.

"You have something to sell," he said at last.

"I have something to *correct*," Odile said. "There's a difference, and the difference is the entire point." She set Wren's fragment on the desk between them — copied out that morning in her own careful hand, the original living only in a child's memory where no magistrate could burn it. "Entry 4,417. Read the witness line."

He read it. His face did nothing at all, which told her everything.

"Fraudulent entries void the authority of the entering magistrate," she said. "Your own codicil, section nine. I checked. Twice. If this entry is fraudulent — and it is, because it has no witnesses — then Magistrate Corvane's purchase of this man's soul was never lawful. Which means every ruling Corvane has signed for ten years rests on a corrupted foundation. Which means—" she sipped her tea, letting him arrive there himself, because the pleasure of watching someone reach your conclusion was one of the few she'd kept through the exile— "the court must annul the purchase. Publicly. Formally. In writing."

"And if we simply destroy your little copy?"

"Then I'll dictate another from memory before your guards finish crossing the room. And the girl who taught me it has it written somewhere even I don't know about. She's eleven and she frightens me more than you do."

Something flickered across the Record-Keep's face that might have been respect. Or indigestion. With bureaucrats the two were often indistinguishable.

"You want the Kestrel-bearer freed," he said slowly. "For a technicality."

"For a *procedural error*, yes. The law is the only god left in this mountain that still answers prayers. I intend to pray loudly."

* * *

It was going well. That was the problem. Odile had negotiated enough to know that when things go smoothly in a place like this, someone upstairs is changing the terms.

The doors opened again, and Magistrate Corvane came in wearing his bone mask, and the temperature of the room dropped in a way Odile suspected was theatrical but effective anyway.

"The archivist argues procedure," Corvane said. His voice resonated oddly inside the mask, like wind through a ribcage. "Charming. But the matter resolves itself more simply." He turned to Paloma, who had been standing very straight and very quiet in the corner, vibrating only slightly. "Kestrel-bearer. We return your soul. Whole. Today. All you must do is renounce your god and enter our service. Ten years of wages, owed and unpaid. A position of honor. You would want for nothing."

Odile watched Paloma's face, and her heart went out to him, because she knew exactly what Corvane was doing. She'd read the manuals. Power's cruelest trick isn't cruelty — cruelty just makes people fight. Power's cruelest trick is *comfort*. It offers you back everything you lost and asks only for the part of you that was already bleeding.

"You'd give him his memories," she said flatly. "And take his future."

"We'd give him his past," Corvane corrected gently. "We'd offer him a future. There is a difference, madame, and the difference is the entire point."

Oh, she disliked him immensely.

Paloma was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "I don't remember him," he said. "Kestrel. Ten years, and I prayed into a hole in the world, and I thought — I thought maybe I'd imagined him. Maybe I'd invented a god because I couldn't stand being alone." He looked at his sleeve, where the hollow oath-mark sat beneath the cloth. "You're offering to make that true. To make it official."

"I'm offering to make it *painless*," said Corvane.

"That's what worries me," Paloma said.

And Odile — who had lost a husband and a daughter to this court, who had burned her own library rather than let the Wardens have it, who had every reason in the world to grab any bargain that reduced the sum of suffering — felt something warm and fierce rise in her chest, because she recognized the shape of what was happening. This ragged, charm-selling, quietly stubborn man was standing in the belly of the machine that had eaten his life, being offered all of it back, and he was worried about the price.

"Don't answer yet," she said quickly, to both of them. "Procedural point, Magistrate. Your offer presumes the purchase stands. My evidence says otherwise. Shall we let the record decide?"

Corvane's mask tilted toward her. "The record," he said, "is in my keeping."

"Yes," Odile agreed pleasantly. "That has always been the trouble with records, hasn't it? They're only as honest as the people who keep them."

Somewhere far above them — or perhaps far below; directions were unreliable in the Hollow — there came a sound. Faint. Metallic. The particular clatter of a maintenance shaft grate being lifted by someone who had practiced lifting grates for a living, followed by a smaller sound that Odile knew absolutely, the way mothers know footsteps: a child whispering, excitedly, *Sef, look, there are SO many jars—*

The Record-Keep's teacup paused halfway to his mouth.

Corvane went very still.

Odile folded her hands in her lap and smiled the smile she'd once used on Wardens, the one that said *I am a harmless middle-aged woman with a filing system,* and prayed to a silent god that Wren would have the sense to be quiet.

She did not, of course. Eleven-year-olds with perfect memories and no fear whatsoever rarely did.

From the direction of the vault came a clear, carrying, delighted voice:

"Sef! SEF! I found it — I found the Kestrel-bearer's jar — oh, and there's a whole shelf labeled *voice, divine, collateral* — Sef, is that a *god* in a *jar*?"

Corvane moved for the door faster than Odile would have believed bone masks could move.

Paloma looked at her. She looked at Paloma.

"Well," she said, setting down her teacup with a small, decisive click. "I believe the record has decided."
