## 9. The Shattering

The magistrate's offer hung in the air like smoke that refused to rise.

"Your soul," it said again, its bone mask tilted with something almost like kindness. "Whole. Returned. All you must do is kneel, renounce the silent god, and serve the Court for a term of — oh, let us say a century. You've already spent ten years as our property, Kestrel-bearer. What is a hundred more to a man who has learned to live without himself?"

Paloma looked at the soul-jar.

It sat on a shelf of black glass in the vault behind the magistrate, one jar among thousands, each glowing faintly with the color of the life inside. His was small. Dim. A stub of candlelight in a warehouse of suns. But it was *his* — he could feel it the way you feel a hand gone numb, an ache that proved the limb still belonged to you.

And beside it, larger than any other vessel in the vault, wrapped in chains of written law, was the voice of Kestrel.

It didn't glow so much as *breathe*. The jar pulsed slow and vast, like the chest of something sleeping at the bottom of the sea. Ten years of silence, bottled. Every prayer Paloma had whispered into his sleeve and heard answered by nothing — it had all been pouring into this room the whole time.

"You're staring at it," the magistrate observed. "Sentimental. I'd expected better from a man who survived Vharrow."

"I'm not being sentimental," Paloma said. "I'm doing arithmetic."

Behind him, somewhere above, Odile's voice rang through the court — sharp, furious, glorious: *"Entry four hundred and eleven is fraudulent and every one of you knows it!"* Sef's laugh followed, shadowless and bright, from a direction it had no business coming from. Wren was reciting something in a child's clear voice, and the bone-masked clerks were flinching at every word.

His family was tearing the Undercourt apart with nothing but paper and nerve.

"Arithmetic," the magistrate repeated.

"Yes." Paloma turned back to it. "You're offering me my soul if I give up the god. But here's what your own ledger says, and I have an eleven-year-old who can recite it backward: the oath was the purchase price. My oath. Which means the moment I renounce him, the contract voids, the sale reverses, and this whole mountain of yours comes down on your heads. You're not offering me a bargain. You're offering me a trapdoor."

The mask did not move. But the courtroom went very quiet, the way a forest goes quiet when something large stops moving through it.

"That," said the magistrate, "is a dangerously literate reading."

"I know an archivist."

Somewhere in the dark of the vault, the great jar breathed out. And Paloma felt it — faint as a draft under a door, but unmistakable. A voice that was not a voice, saying his name. Not the name he'd used for ten years. Not even the true name the dying courier had gasped into his doorway.

Something older. Something that sounded like *home*.

"Oh," Paloma said softly. "You absolute bastards. He's been awake in there."

* * *

He moved before the magistrates finished moving, which was the only advantage he would ever have over them.

The vault's wards were built to stop souls going *out*, not hands reaching *in* — the Court had never imagined anyone would want to touch the merchandise. His fingers closed around the soul-jar, and it was warm, warm as a stone left in summer sun, warm as a hand held too long, and it *knew* him. It pressed against his palm like a cat remembering its person.

"There," the lead magistrate said, and its voice had lost all pretense of kindness. "Seize him."

Bone masks turned. Chains of law uncoiled from the ceiling like iron serpents. And every magistrate in the vault turned toward the largest jar in the room — toward Kestrel — with their hands raised and their mouths open, and Paloma understood in one cold flash:

They weren't going to catch him.

They were going to *drink*.

Consume the god's voice, and the contract sealed itself forever. Consume the voice, and Paloma stayed hollow until the mountains wore down. It wasn't spite. It was bookkeeping.

"No," Paloma said.

It came out quiet. It came out the way he'd said *no* to Wardens, to slumlords, to every hard thing in ten years of selling charms that almost worked. Quiet was the loudest thing he knew.

He held the soul-jar in both hands, and he raised it, and he understood — with the strange calm of a man standing at the top of a long fall — exactly what he was about to do.

A soul-jar shattered releases what's inside. That was the first law of the Court's own craft. Release a soul into the body that made it, and the body remembers. Release a soul into a room full of stolen things—

Release a soul into a room where a god has been breathing against the glass for ten years—

"Kestrel-bearer," the magistrate said, and for the first time, the bone mask sounded afraid. "Think. You will lose everything. You will lose *yourself*."

"Yeah," Paloma said. "That's rather the point. You can't keep what won't hold together."

He thought of Odile, who burned her library rather than let them have it, and who had never once regretted the warmth. He thought of Wren, carrying a thousand burned books in her head because someone had to remember. He thought of Sef, who had sold his shadow and walked lighter for it, laughing in the dark.

Nobody keeps what matters by holding it tight. You keep it by letting it go where it needs to go.

"Sorry," Paloma told the jar — told himself — "for keeping you on a shelf."

And he threw it down, and it broke.

* * *

There was no light, at first. There was *remembering*.

Ten years came home all at once. Rain on the temple roof at Hollow's Edge. The smell of ink and Odile's terrible tea. His mother's hands — he had a mother, he had *hands that taught him knots*, he had a name before the name, a birthday, a dog, a winter he'd cried over a broken kite — all of it flooding back into a man who had been living in a house with half the rooms bricked over, and the walls came down and the rooms were *full*, they were full of everything, and it hurt, it hurt like being born, and he laughed while it hurt because —

Because the god's voice went with it.

When the soul-jar burst, the shockwave hit Kestrel's prison like a hammer on a bell, and the chains of written law — every clause, every seal, every fraudulent entry four hundred and eleven — cracked in a single ringing note. The great jar split from crown to base.

And Kestrel spoke.

Not in words. In weather. In the pressure drop before a storm, in the hush after snowfall, in the feeling of a hand on your shoulder when you are certain you are alone. The voice filled the vault, filled the court, filled the *mountain*, and every soul-jar on every shelf lit up like dawn hitting a field of windows.

Then the jars began to break on their own.

Ten years of purchases, ten years of small surrenders, ten years of memories siphoned from everyone who ever passed the gate — the flood found every crack, and the cracks were everywhere now, and the Court's own foundations groaned like a ship in ice. Somewhere above, Paloma heard the magistrates screaming in voices that had forgotten how to be afraid. Somewhere below, a clerk tore off his bone mask and wept at the return of his daughter's face.

Every soul the Court ever bought got its change back that night, with interest.

Paloma stood in the middle of it, empty-handed, hollowed clean through — and free. Whatever was left of him, whatever the shattering hadn't scattered, felt lighter than he had since he was twenty-three years old and knelt before an altar and gave away more than he knew.

A presence settled beside him. Not above. Beside.

*Well done,* said Kestrel, in the voice of a friend leaning close. *Now run.*

The mountain was coming down.

* * *

He ran. Of course he ran. He ran through corridors that had been tunnels and tunnels that were now open sky, past magistrates clawing at their own masks, past shelves of empty glass glittering like frost. Behind him the Undercourt folded inward, ten years of stolen weight collapsing into the space it had been taken from, and the sound it made was not thunder.

It was a city-sized exhale. Ten years of held breath, let go at once.

He burst out of the servant tunnel into snow and starlight, staggered, fell to his knees in a drift, and started laughing — great, ugly, sobbing laughter — because he could not remember most of what he'd just set free, and it didn't matter, it *didn't matter*, because it was out there now. Loose in the world. Unowned.

Hands grabbed him. Odile, soot-streaked, hauling him upright by the collar. Wren attached to his side like a burr, chattering a list of everything she'd seen, which was apparently everything. Sef strolled out last, unhurried, and stopped at the tunnel mouth to watch the mountain settle.

"Huh," Sef said. "I think my shadow's coming back. There's a bit of it. Look. Like a puddle."

"It'll grow," Wren said confidently. "Shadows regrow at about a finger-width a week. I read it in *On Limbs and Their Losses*."

"Why were you reading that?"

"In case."

Above them, Mount Hollow exhaled a last plume of dust, and the stars came out over it, sharper than Paloma had ever seen them. He searched the sky without meaning to — the old habit, the prayer habit, the reach toward a god who never answered.

This time, something reached back.

Not a command. Not a claim. Just a warmth at his shoulder, familiar as a hand, patient as morning — and beneath it, unmistakably, a question. An actual question, asked of an equal:

*So. What do we do now?*

Paloma looked at his family — the archivist, the girl, the thief, the god — silhouetted against the burning ruin of everything that had owned them.

"Now?" he said. "Now we go home. And then—" He grinned, and it felt like a door opening. "Then I think somebody owes me ten years of catching up. All of us do. Starting with breakfast."

Far below, in the settling dark of the Hollow, something ancient shifted in the rubble — something the magistrates had kept deeper than souls, older than ledgers, and very much awake.

But that, as Sef liked to say, was tomorrow's problem.
