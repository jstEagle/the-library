## 10. Kestrel and the Hollow

Paloma woke to the taste of stone and the smell of dawn.

He was lying on a slope of scree above the mountain's western shoulder, and the first thing he noticed — before the ache in his bones, before the ringing in his ears that sounded almost like singing — was his own bare forearm, pale in the early light.

The oath-mark was gone.

For ten years it had sat beneath his skin like a brand: the silent god's sigil, a circle with a single vertical line through it, the mark of Kestrel's service. He had hidden it under his sleeve in Vharrow because marks were evidence and evidence was death. He had touched it every night like a man touching a locked door. And now there was nothing there at all — just skin, ordinary and unclaimed, rising and falling with his breath.

*Unbound,* he thought. *Unowned.*

The word arrived without an owner too. It was one of the memories coming back to him in pieces, drifting up like silt after a riverbed is stirred: fragments of a childhood he'd half-forgotten, a mother's kitchen, a teacher's voice, the day he'd sworn himself to a god who never spoke. They came slowly, gently, as if someone were handing them back one at a time and waiting to see if he could carry each one.

Some of them hurt. Most of them didn't. All of them were *his.*

"Good," said a voice. "You're keeping them."

Paloma did not startle. He was past startling; he had spent the last week underground among magistrates wearing bone masks, and whatever remained of his capacity for fear had been spent in the vault. He turned his head, and there — sitting on a boulder beside him with the casual ease of someone who had been waiting a long while — was a man.

Or something shaped like a man. He was grey-eyed and weathered, dressed in travel-worn clothes the color of dry grass, and he looked tired in the way mountains look tired: anciently, patiently, without complaint.

"You're not what I expected," Paloma said. His voice came out rough.

"No one ever expects their god to look like this." The man smiled faintly. "They expect fire. Or silence. I gave them silence for ten years, so I suppose I earned the reputation."

"You didn't *give* it." The anger came up fast and hot, older than the memory of it. "It was taken. You were—"

"Bottled. Yes. I know." The god — Kestrel, it had to be Kestrel, though nothing about him demanded worship — looked out over the valley below, where Vharrow's rooftops were just beginning to catch the light. "Do you know what I heard, all those years? Everything. Every prayer you said over those worthless charms of yours. Every night you asked me why I'd abandoned you."

"I wasn't praying. I was complaining."

"That too counts." Kestrel's smile deepened. "Complaints are just prayers with better honesty."

Paloma sat up slowly. His body reported its grievances one by one — bruised ribs, scraped palms, a headache like a bell being struck behind his eyes — and he let it, because pain meant he was whole enough to feel it. "The others," he said. "Odile. Wren. Sef. Are they—"

"Alive. Waiting. Arguing about who gets to wake you." Kestrel tilted his head toward a cluster of rocks further down the slope, where three figures huddled around a small fire that was mostly smoke. "The girl has been reciting the magistrates' entire ledger from memory since sunrise. She says she doesn't want to lose any of it now that it finally matters."

"She won't. She remembers everything."

"Yes," Kestrel said softly. "I know. She prayed to me once, you know. Two winters ago, alone in a burned archive, holding a book that was still warm. She asked me to help her keep what everyone else wanted destroyed." He paused. "I couldn't answer her. But I heard. I heard all of it. Ten years of hearing and no voice to answer with — do you understand now why your soul mattered so much to them? Yours was the jar's stopper. As long as they held what bound me, they held my silence too."

Paloma looked at his bare arm again. "And now?"

"Now you're free of me entirely." Kestrel spread his hands. "The shattering broke every contract in the Hollow. Your oath died with the court that held its paper. You owe me nothing, Paloma Sterling. No service. No silence. No mark."

Something in Paloma's chest went tight and strange. For ten years the mark had been the only proof that his devotion meant anything — the one thread connecting him to a god who never answered, the thing he'd clung to on the worst nights in Vharrow when selling charms that almost worked to people who almost believed. Losing it felt like losing a scar. Like losing proof that he had once been brave enough to promise something.

"What am I, then?" he asked. "If not yours?"

Kestrel considered him for a long moment. Then he stood, brushed the scree dust from his knees, and offered his hand — an ordinary hand, calloused at the palm, exactly the kind of hand a traveling man would have.

"A friend, if you'll have one," he said. "I've had ten thousand servants and not one companion. Servants kneel. Companions walk beside you and tell you when you're being a fool. I'm told I need that. I have been, historically, quite foolish."

Paloma took the hand. It was warm, and solid, and it pulled him to his feet like any stranger's might.

"Fair warning," Paloma said. "I complain a lot."

"So I've heard. Ten years' worth." Kestrel's grey eyes crinkled. "I'm looking forward to hearing it out loud."

* * *

The fire was more smoke than flame, but Wren had found a tin kettle somewhere and was presiding over it with the gravity of a general. She looked up when Paloma limped into the circle of stones, and her face did something complicated — relief and accusation fighting for space.

"You were asleep for eleven hours," she said.

"He needed it," Odile murmured, not looking up from the leather satchel she was packing with the tenderness most people reserved for infants. It was stuffed full now — loose pages, copied fragments, charcoal rubbings of the Hollow's ledgers. The court's entire rotten accounting, carried out into the light. "Souls don't shatter cleanly. He held the pieces together long enough to get us out. That costs."

"It was worth it," Sef said from where he lay sprawled against a rock, hat over his eyes. "Mostly. I mean, I lost my shadow for six years and got it back overnight, so honestly, best deal I've ever made, and I once traded a forged deed for a boat."

Paloma looked at him properly for the first time. In the growing light, Sef cast a shadow again — thin and wavering, like a candle flame learning to stand, but unmistakably there. It stretched out behind him across the scree, and every few moments it flickered, thickened, grew a little darker.

"It itches," Sef said, catching his look. "Like a limb waking up. Do you know how strange it is to feel your own shadow regrowing? It's like being a house someone is furnishing while you sleep."

"Does it remember how to be sneaky?" Wren asked.

"Absolutely not. It keeps bumping into things." He lifted the brim of his hat and grinned at Paloma. "There he is. The man who broke a mountain. You look terrible."

"I feel wonderful," Paloma said, and discovered it was true.

He lowered himself onto a flat stone near the fire, and Odile handed him a tin cup of something hot and terrible that was probably tea in the way a rock is probably a chair. He drank it anyway. Around him, his family arranged itself without anyone asking them to — Wren scooting close so she could keep talking, Odile settling on his other side with the satchel between her feet like a sleeping animal, Sef tipping his chair of air back against the boulder.

"We should talk about what happens next," Odile said. "The ledgers prove the Undercourt's authority was fraudulent from the start — every soul-purchase traced back to a contract the magistrates themselves forged. When this reaches the right hands in Vharrow—"

"The Wardens will burn it," Wren said.

"The Wardens will *try.*" Odile's mouth curved, sharp and unrepentant. "I've been burned once. I learned where to hide the embers."

"And me?" Paloma asked. "What does a man do after he shatters his own soul and breaks a god out of prison?"

"Breakfast," said Wren. "Then breakfast. Then we walk down the mountain, and then breakfast in Vharrow, at that place with the honey bread, and you're paying, because you're famous now and famous people are rich."

"I sold charms for a living. I am famously poor."

"Then we'll rob someone," Sef said comfortably. "I know people."

Paloma laughed — really laughed, the sound surprising him, coming up from somewhere deep and long-unused. Above them the last stars were fading, and below them the valley was turning gold, and somewhere in the middle distance a bird was making an absolute fool of itself with enthusiasm.

Kestrel had settled a little apart from the fire, close enough to listen, far enough to make clear he wasn't owed a place in the circle. But Wren, without pausing in her recitation of the fourth magistrate's accounts, simply scooted over and patted the ground beside her. The god of the silent oath — the voice beneath the Hollow, the collateral, the prisoner — looked at that patch of dirt for a moment as if it were the strangest thing he'd encountered in ten thousand years.

Then he sat down in the circle, next to a child who remembered everything, and accepted a cup of terrible tea.

"So," Kestrel said quietly, to Paloma, under the noise of the argument that had broken out over whether honey bread counted as breakfast or dessert. "Vharrow. What will you sell there now? Your charms almost worked. Mine never did."

Paloma turned his cup in his hands. Below, the city where he'd spent ten years hiding was waking up, unaware that the law of its silence had cracked overnight.

"I don't know yet," he said. "Maybe I'll stop selling almost and start selling actually. There's a market for it, I think. People are tired of pretending magic isn't real."

"Mm. Dangerous business."

"I've done dangerous business before." He glanced sideways at the god beside him — not master, not servant, just a tired traveler sharing bad tea on a cold morning. "Besides. I hear I've got powerful friends."

"One," Kestrel corrected. "Plus a girl who never forgets, a woman who never forgives, and a thief whose shadow is still finding its feet."

"That's four."

"That's family," Kestrel said. "I counted generously."

The sun cleared the ridge then, and the whole mountainside lit up at once — five figures and one regrowing shadow around a dying fire, a satchel full of crimes, a head full of truth, and a road going down into the gold. Somewhere far below, in the dark under the mountain, the Hollow was quiet for the first time in a thousand years.

Paloma finished his tea, stood up, and started walking.
