## 1. The Ledger of Small Thefts

The candle had burned down to its last honest inch when Marguerite Ravensworth found the lullaby.

She sat in the counting room of the Vellum Guild with her sleeves pushed back and her ink-stained fingers resting on a ledger bound in calfskin so old it had gone the color of weak tea. Around her, the guildhouse slept—or pretended to. The building never truly slept; it breathed. Floorboards settled like old men into chairs. The vials in the vault below ticked and chimed as memories shifted against their glass, faint as insects against a window. Somewhere on the third floor, an apprentice dreamed loudly enough that Marguerite could almost hear the shape of it.

But here, in this pool of candlelight, there was only the ledger.

*Entry 447,* she read. *One memory, minor: a lullaby, sung in a kitchen in Lowmarsh, winter. Seller: unnamed. Buyer: M—, widow of the ferryman Aldous Crane. Price: three crowns and one favor, unspecified.*

Marguerite's pen hovered above the audit column.

Three lines of ink. That was all a person's grief amounted to, once the Guild got its hands on it. The widow Crane had come to them in the gray weeks after her husband drowned beneath the ice of the Marrow, and she had not asked for a memory to be taken. She had asked for one to be *given back*—the lullaby Aldous used to hum while he mended his nets, off-key, patient, the way he did everything. He'd learned it from his mother, who'd learned it from hers, and somewhere between the drowning and the funeral, the widow's grief had pressed down on the song so hard it went silent in her. She could remember that he used to sing it. She could not remember how it went.

That was the cruelty grief performed on its own victims. It didn't take things away. It just folded them under, like linen at the bottom of a chest.

The Guild could fix that—for a price. Memory-work was not charity. Master Corvane said it often enough, saying grace over the strongbox: *We are merchants, girl, not saints. What we trade is the only currency that never rots.* And the ledgers agreed with him. Every entry neat as stitches. Every price exact.

Except that Marguerite had made an error.

No—not an error. She had committed a small theft, which was the only kind the Vellum Guild ever punished, because it was the only kind it ever noticed. Three nights ago, when the widow Crane had counted out her three crowns with hands that shook too much to argue, Marguerite had written *three crowns* in the ledger and accepted only two. The third crown sat now in the false bottom of her writing desk, wrapped in a scrap of blue cloth, waiting for morning, when she would walk it to Lowmarsh herself under the pretense of a delivery errand no one would question. The ledger would balance. The Guild would never know. And a woman who had forgotten how her husband sounded when he was happy would wake tomorrow remembering.

It was such a small rebellion. That was what made it bearable—and what made it shameful, if she let herself think about it too long, which she tried not to. A third crown. A lullaby. Against the weight of everything else in these books, it was nothing. It was also everything she had.

Marguerite dipped her pen and wrote, in the tidy hand Corvane praised her for: *Audit complete. Entry 447 verified.*

Then she closed the ledger, and in the dark behind her eyes she heard, very faintly, a man humming off-key over fishing nets, and wondered whether the widow would recognize the tune when it came home to her, or whether it would feel like something new.

* * *

The vault smelled of cold stone and beeswax and, underneath both, something harder to name—a sweetness like fruit going soft, which was the smell of memory aging in glass. Marguerite carried the candle down the spiral stair with her free hand trailing the wall, past shelves that rose into darkness taller than any ladder could reach.

Each vial held a small theft. That was the honest word for it, whatever euphemisms the sales catalogs preferred. *Acquisitions. Consignments. Voluntary deposits.* But every vial down here had been pried loose from a living head, and Marguerite knew it the way a locksmith knows locks: intimately, and without illusion.

She walked the aisles slowly, reading labels by candlelight, because tonight was audit night and the audit required her to verify the oldest stock—the memories that had sat unsold long enough to fade. Faded stock was dangerous stock. A memory left too long in glass began to leak its edges, and a leaking memory could seep into whoever stood too near. There were apprentices who swore the west aisle whispered in a dead woman's voice. Marguerite had worked late enough times to half believe them.

*Entry 12. First snowfall, age six. Seller deceased. Unsold forty years.*

She lifted the vial and held it to the flame. Inside, the memory had gone thin as breath on glass—a white shimmer, a child's delight worn down to its outline. Once, some boy or girl had stood at a window watching the world go soft and new, and had felt the feeling so large they'd sold it rather than carry it. Now it was nearly nothing. In another decade it would be nothing at all, and the Guild would pour it out in the yard like sour milk.

Marguerite's chest did the thing it always did in the vault—that slow, quiet ache, like a door opening onto a room she wasn't allowed to enter. Because here was the secret she kept even from herself, mostly: she didn't hate the work. She hated what it cost other people, yes. But when she held a vial up to the light and let the faded edges wash over her, she felt—recognized. Understood. As though each little theft were a letter addressed to someone, and she were the only person alive who bothered to read them.

Loneliness will do that. It will make a warehouse of stolen childhoods feel like company.

She set Entry 12 back on its shelf, marked it *degraded—dispose*, and moved on. Past the love affairs sold out of embarrassment. Past the guilt a merchant had paid handsomely to be rid of, still heavy in its glass after thirty years, refusing to fade, as though guilt knew it was being stored and disapproved. Past a whole shelf of names—people who had sold their own names to escape debts, or families, or warrants, walking out of the guildhouse afterward as blank slates, free and hollow, blinking at the sun like newborn calves.

At the end of the oldest aisle stood the ledger shelf itself: the first ledger, the one Corvane called the Foundation Book, older than the guildhouse, older than the city walls, bound in leather gone black with handling. No one audited the Foundation Book. Corvane kept its key on a cord around his neck, next to his heart, and had told Marguerite once, in a voice she'd never heard him use before or since: *Some accounts are not yours to settle, girl.*

She paused in front of it anyway. She always did. The candle flame leaned toward the black cover as if it, too, wanted to read.

"Marguerite."

She turned, heart knocking. Corvane stood at the foot of the stairs, silver-haired, straight-backed, his dressing gown buttoned to the throat as though formality were armor he wore even to sleep in. His eyes went from her face to the Foundation Book and back with a smoothness that pretended not to notice anything at all.

"Audit night," he said. "You're thorough. I'll grant you that."

"The old stock is degrading faster than the catalogs predict," she said. "Entry 12 should be disposed of by spring."

"Mm." He came down the aisle toward her, and the candlelight made his shadow climb the shelves like something climbing a cliff. "And the Crane account? Widow, ferryman's wife, Lowmarsh?"

Marguerite kept her face still. This was the discipline of her life: stillness. "Balanced," she said. "Three crowns, received in full."

Something flickered behind Corvane's eyes—too quick to name, gone before it arrived. "Good," he said. "Sentiment is a leak in a roof, Marguerite. It never stays small." He touched the Foundation Book's spine once, lightly, the way a man touches a scar. "Finish your counts. And sleep. Tomorrow the herald comes through the square, and I want this house looking respectable."

"The herald? What proclamation?"

He was already climbing the stairs. "The kind that makes respectable houses glad they're respectable," he said, and was gone.

Marguerite stood alone in the dark among forty years of stolen yesterdays, and the sweetness of aging memory seemed suddenly less like fruit and more like something rotting.

* * *

She did not sleep. Instead she sat at her desk until the candle drowned in its own wax, and then lit another, and took out the blue cloth with the third crown inside it, and turned it over and over in her fingers like a coin of a country that didn't exist.

The trouble with stealing mercy, she thought, is that you can't stop at one theft.

It had started small, as these things do. A widow's fee shorted by a crown. Before that, a soldier who'd sold the memory of his brother's face to stop himself deserting—Marguerite had copied the description from the intake record and later, "by accident," left it where the man's sister could find him. Before that, a girl who'd sold her first kiss and cried about it in the corridor, and Marguerite had knocked a full vial off a shelf and reported it broken when really she'd poured it down the disposal chute and told the girl to run.

Seven small thefts in four years. Seven entries in a private ledger she kept sewn into the lining of her coat, written in a cipher of her own devising, because a girl who audits thieves learns to keep her own books carefully.

She knew what Corvane would say. She could hear his voice exactly, that dry patience he wore like the dressing gown: *You think you're giving them gifts. You're giving them debts, girl. Every kindness unpriced becomes a chain.* And maybe he was right. Maybe the widow Crane would wake tomorrow with her husband's lullaby restored and never know it had been bought twice, and maybe that was a gift, and maybe it was a chain, and maybe Marguerite would never be permitted to know the difference, because knowing would require staying, and staying would require becoming what the Guild was.

She sewed the crown deeper into the blue cloth. Outside, the city of Vespergate lay under a sky the color of dirty pewter, and the river Marrow ran black between its banks, carrying ice toward the sea, carrying, somewhere under that ice, a drowned ferryman's silence.

Tomorrow she would deliver a lullaby. Tomorrow the herald would come. She fell asleep at her desk with the private ledger open on her knee and her cheek on the page, and if she dreamed, she dreamed of feathers—which was strange, because she had never owned anything with feathers, and would not, until the world ended and began again.

* * *

Morning came gray and early, smelling of coal smoke and river fog. Marguerite wrapped the blue cloth bundle in her shawl, pinned her plainest bonnet over her unmistakable hair—Corvane called it *unfortunate*, that copper-red, called it a liability in a business built on forgettability—and slipped out through the counting room before the apprentices stirred.

Lowmarsh huddled along the south bank where the Marrow bent, a district of net-menders and eel-catchers, its lanes so narrow the rooftops leaned together like conspirators. The widow Crane's cottage sat at the lane's end, its door painted the green of river weed, its windows fogged with the warmth of a fire kept burning for no one.

Marguerite knocked. Waited. Knocked again.

Footsteps, slow, uneven. The door opened on a small woman wrapped in a man's coat far too large for her—Aldous's coat, still worn, still smelling of tar and rope—and eyes that had the particular rawness of someone who has cried past crying.

"You're the Guild girl," the widow said. Not unkindly. Weary, the way the whole district was weary. "Come to count my pennies again?"

"I've come about your account," Marguerite said, and heard her own voice do the thing it did: flatten, professional, safe. "There was an error in the entry. An overpayment. The Guild refunds its errors." She held out the blue cloth. "With apologies."

The widow looked at the bundle without touching it. "I paid what I owed."

"There was a surplus."

For a long moment the two women regarded each other across the threshold, and Marguerite understood that the widow understood—understood everything, the way poor people always understand everything, having no luxury of illusion—and chose, graciously, to play the game.

"Well," the widow said, taking the bundle at last. Her fingers trembled around it. "The Guild is generous after all."

"The Guild is precise," Marguerite said. "I'm the generous one."

She meant it as a joke. It came out sounding like a confession, and the widow's eyes filled, and before Marguerite could retreat, the old woman caught her hand and pressed it between both of hers—hands rough as net-hemp, warm as bread.

"He used to sing it while he mended," the widow said. "Off-key. Our girl used to laugh at him. She's married to a cooper in Harrow Fen now, and she doesn't remember it either, and I couldn't—" Her voice failed, gathered itself. "Thank you. Whatever your name is. Thank you."

"Marguerite," she said, and immediately wished she hadn't. Names were liabilities. Corvane's first lesson. *Never give them a handle to hold you by.*

But the widow only smiled, wet-eyed, and squeezed her hand once more, and said, "Marguerite. A good name. I'll keep it," and shut the door softly, and through the fogged window Marguerite saw her silhouette sink into a chair and unfold the blue cloth, and heard—faint, muffled, breaking in the middle—the sound of a woman humming a lullaby she had forgotten, finding the tune again note by note like a path through snow.

Marguerite stood in the lane with the fog thickening around her and let herself feel it: the whole warm weight of it, the smallness and the enormity. One lullaby returned. One grief lightened by a single crown's worth. It was nothing. It was the only thing.

She was halfway back across the bridge into the city proper when she saw the crowd.

It gathered in Herald's Square like water finding a drain—shopkeepers with their aprons still on, fishwives, children hoisted onto shoulders, a knot of guildsmen from the Assay House looking grim. At the square's center, beside the great iron post where proclamations were nailed, stood a royal herald in the king's colors, flanked by two soldiers whose spears caught the flat morning light.

Marguerite should have walked on. She told herself to walk on. Instead she drifted to the crowd's edge, the way a moth drifts to a shuttered lamp, and heard the herald's trained voice ring out over the square:

"—by order of His Majesty, and sealed under the Privy Seal: whereas the traffic in memories corrupts the loyalty of subjects and steals the lawful inheritance of the mind, let it be known that all persons gifted in the taking, trading, or altering of memory are hereby declared *traitors to the crown*. Let them surrender themselves to the Inquisitor's office within seven days, or be hunted—"

The rest of it blurred. Marguerite heard her own heartbeat instead, loud as oars in a lock. Around her the crowd murmured, shifted, began already to look at its neighbors with new arithmetic—*who, nearby, who?*—and somewhere in that murmur she caught the words *Vellum Guild* spoken once, twice, three times, each time a little louder.

She pulled her bonnet low and turned for the guildhouse, walking quickly, then not running, then running.

Behind her, nailed to the iron post, the Proclamation of Severance fluttered in the river wind like something newly alive—and hungry.
