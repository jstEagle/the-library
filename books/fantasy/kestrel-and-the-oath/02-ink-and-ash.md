## 2. Ink and Ash

The herald came at the hour when Vellum Street was still half-asleep, when the lamplighters had gone home and the bakers had not yet begun to argue with their ovens. Marguerite heard him before she saw him: the flat percussion of a horse's hooves on cobbles worn smooth by a century of trade, and beneath it, the sound that made her spine go cold before her mind understood why—the wet slap of a hammer striking nails into wood.

She was standing at the guildhouse window with a cup of chicory going cold in her hand, watching frost feather across the glass. Below, in the grey light, a man in royal livery—crimson and bone-white, the colors of the Crown's Office of Inquiry—dismounted before the door she had walked through every day for eleven years.

He was young. That was the first thing she noticed, and the thing she would remember longest afterward. Young enough that his jaw had not yet learned to harden around the things he read aloud. He unrolled a parchment sealed with wax the color of dried blood, nailed it to the guildhouse door with four strokes—each one ringing through the street like a small, official apocalypse—and then mounted his horse again without looking up at the windows where faces were beginning to gather like moths.

Marguerite set down her cup. Her hands were steady. She noted this with a strange, detached pride, the way one notes the behavior of someone else's hands.

*All memory-gifted are hereby declared traitors to the realm.*

She could not yet read the words from three floors up, but she knew them anyway. You did not keep the ledgers of the Vellum Guild for a decade without learning to hear the shape of a sentence before you saw its ink.

* * *

By the time she reached the ground floor, Corvane was already there.

Guildmaster Aldous Corvane stood before the proclamation with his back to the room, reading it the way he read everything—slowly, thoroughly, as though the words might change if he gave them time. He was a tall man gone slightly stooped, silver at the temples, with the patient hands of someone who had spent forty years handling fragile things: rare vellum, bottled oaths, other people's pasts. He had raised Marguerite from the age of nine, plucked her out of the Foundling Row winter with warm gloves and a warmer voice, and taught her that memory was the only currency that never debased.

He did not turn when she entered. "Read it," he said quietly.

She crossed the floor. The herald's ink was still fresh enough to shine, and the seal above it bore the crest of the Inquisitor's office—a closed eye wreathed in feathers. She read:

*By decree of His Majesty, and pursuant to the findings of the Royal Commission on Unnatural Arts: all persons gifted or practiced in the taking, trading, alteration, or holding of the memories of others are declared traitors to the crown and enemies of the common good. Such arts are anathema to the natural order of the mind, which is the sole property of its bearer and of God. All practitioners shall surrender themselves for examination within seven days. Those who harbor them, shelter them, or trade with them shall share their sentence.*

*The sentence is severance.*

Below that, in smaller script, the definition—the one that made the widow's lullaby flash behind Marguerite's eyes like a struck match. *Severance: the lawful and permanent extraction of all memory, given or taken, held or hoarded, until the subject stands empty before God and the crown.*

"They're calling us traitors," Marguerite said. Her voice sounded very far away. "For giving people back what they asked to be rid of."

"For existing," Corvane said. He turned then, and his face was composed in the particular way that frightened her more than any rage could have—not blank, but *arranged*, every feature set deliberately, like type locked into a frame. "The commission has been sitting for two years. I told the council this day would come. I told them to slow the trade, to bury the dangerous stock." A muscle moved in his jaw. "They called me cautious. Cautious men survive, Marguerite. Remember that."

"Where is everyone?"

"Gone to ground, those with sense. Tam went before dawn—he'd heard rumors from the customs house. The apprentices I sent home to their families." He paused, and something flickered behind his eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "Most of them."

The word sat between them. Marguerite thought of Nettie, twelve years old, who swept the counting room and memorized ledger columns for fun and asked endless questions about where memories went after they were bottled. *Most of them.* She decided, for the moment, not to ask which most.

"What do we do?" she asked.

Corvane looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "We burn."

* * *

The archive room of the Vellum Guild occupied the whole of the second floor, and it smelled the way Marguerite imagined heaven might smell if heaven kept accounts: beeswax and cedar, old paper and sealing wax, the faint mineral tang of bottled memory. Three hundred years of trade lined its shelves. Jars of preserved recollection glowed faintly along the north wall, each labeled in the careful hand of some long-dead registrar—a child's first snowfall, 1809; a soldier's last letter home, unsent; the taste of a mother's bread, seller unknown, price paid in full.

Corvane worked methodically, and that was the horror of it. He did not rage or fling things. He sorted.

"The client registers go first," he said, feeding pages into the iron stove piece by piece. "Names, addresses, descriptions of goods. Then the acquisition logs. Then anything touching the Court." He said the last word lightly, but Marguerite saw his hand pause over a slim volume bound in green leather before consigning it to the flames.

"The Court?" she said. "I've never seen anything about the Kestrel Court in the ledgers. There's no such section."

"There is no such section now," Corvane said.

The fire ate the paper with a soft, greedy whisper. Marguerite stood among the shelves with her arms wrapped around herself, watching three hundred years curl and blacken, and felt something in her chest she could not name—grief, yes, but also a strange, cold clarity, as though she were standing outside herself taking inventory. *One guildhouse. One master. One girl who knows too much.* She had spent her life cataloguing other people's losses; it was disorienting to feel one being itemized inside her own skull.

"Why burn the client registers?" she asked. "If we surrender—if we comply—"

"We are not complying." Corvane did not look up from the stove. "Compliance means examination. Examination means severance. I have watched what they do in the capital, girl. They do not empty you cleanly. They take everything, and they keep it, and they use it." Now he looked at her, and his eyes were the grey of slate in rain. "Every memory they pull from your head becomes a weapon in their hand. Your mother's face. Your first kindness. Everything you have ever loved, turned against you by men with warrants."

Marguerite's throat tightened. "Then what—"

"You will prove your loyalty," Corvane said. "Today. Before the seven days run out. Before they come looking."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the street door groaned in the wind.

"How?" she asked, though some part of her—some deep, ledger-keeping part that had always balanced every column—already knew.

* * *

The client's name was Master Ellery Vance, and he was waiting in the front parlor when Marguerite came down, because Corvane had sent for him an hour before dawn.

Vance was a tax assessor, a soft-bodied man with ink-stained fingers and the anxious courtesy of someone who had done business with the guild for six years. He sat in the good chair with his hat on his knees, and when Marguerite entered he rose halfway and then seemed to forget whether to finish standing, so that he ended in a kind of apologetic crouch.

"Mistress Ravensworth." He wet his lips. "Master Corvane said—you could help me. With the edict. He said there was a way to make certain I'm... clean. That nothing in my head could be construed as—" He swallowed. "As association."

Marguerite looked at Corvane, who stood by the cold fireplace with his hands folded behind his back, wearing the expression of a man observing a routine transaction.

"Explain to her what you sold us, Master Vance," Corvane said pleasantly.

Vance's gaze dropped to his hat. "Memories. Four purchases over six years. Nothing criminal—I want that understood—nothing seditious. A holiday in the south of Ardenne, when my wife was alive. My wedding day. The birth of my son." His voice thinned. "And last spring, the day my wife died. I couldn't bear it. I asked you to take it. I've regretted it every day since, but I *asked*, it was consensual, there are papers—"

"There were papers," Corvane agreed. "There were papers for everything. And the papers are ash now, and the crown does not recognize consent, only practice. If the Inquisitor examines you, Master Vance, and finds guild-work in your head, you hang beside us. Unless." He let the word hang. "Unless there is nothing left to find. Unless a licensed practitioner of this house removes all trace of our trade from your mind, freely and completely, and swears before witnesses that the Vellum Guild has never touched you."

"And you'll swear it," Vance said, turning to Marguerite with sudden, desperate hope. "You'll write it down. A certificate. Signed."

Marguerite's mouth was dry. "That isn't how it works," she said. "What I take doesn't vanish. It comes to me. Every memory I've ever drawn out of anyone, I carry. If they examine *me*—"

"Which is why you will draw them out and destroy them," Corvane said smoothly, "in the presence of witnesses, using the rite of dissolution. Which you know."

She did know it. It was in the oldest manuals, the ones kept under lock: a method for unmaking a held memory rather than absorbing it, burning it out of both minds at once. It was forbidden within the guild—not by law, but by ethics, by the first rule written inside the cover of every ledger Marguerite had ever kept: *what is given into our keeping is sacred; we do not destroy what we hold, for a memory once lost cannot be regrown.* She had copied that rule out in her own hand at age ten, sitting on a stool too tall for her, while Corvane dictated.

She looked at him now. "You taught me never to dissolve."

"I taught you many things," Corvane said. "This morning requires others." He gestured toward the parlor window, where the proclamation hung on the door outside, its fresh ink facing the street like an accusation. "Inquisitor Vale arrives in this city within the week. I have seen her work, Marguerite. In Callowmere she emptied a convent of forty women in a fortnight, and she kept what she took—all of it, stacked inside her like coins in a vault—and when she was finished she could recite the prayers of women she had never met, weep for children she had never borne. That is what waits for us. For you especially." His voice gentled, and that gentleness was the cruelest thing in the room. "You are the finest taker I have ever trained. Do you imagine they will simply hang you? No. They will open you like a cabinet and inventory everything inside. Every secret of this house. Every name. Every client. And then they will have *you*, walking around wearing your face, carrying their warrant, doing their work forever."

The fire crackled upstairs. Vance sat motionless, hat crushed in his fists, watching them like a man watching a verdict be deliberated over his own body.

"It's one afternoon of your life," Corvane said softly. "Four memories. A dead woman's funeral, a wedding, a beach, a birth. You will draw them, dissolve them, and Master Vance walks away clean, and when Vale comes, this house can show her a practitioner who refused the trade—who destroyed contraband and turned a client away. Proof of loyalty. Proof that we are what we have always claimed to be: custodians, not thieves." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice to the register he used only for her, the one that had soothed her through fevers and nightmares and the first terrible years after Foundling Row. "I am asking you to save us. Both of us. All of us."

Marguerite looked at Vance. Vance looked at his hat.

And underneath the fear, underneath the arithmetic of survival, something in her went very quiet and very clear, the way water goes clear when it freezes.

Because she had heard this voice before. Not from Corvane—from clients, over eleven years, a hundred times: *it's just one memory, it's a small thing, it's better this way.* The voice of people persuading themselves at someone else's expense. And she had spent eleven years keeping the ledgers of those transactions, recording each small theft in her neat hand, and telling herself the trade was mercy because most of the time it was, because the widow got her lullaby back, because the soldier slept at night.

But this was not mercy. This was a man asking her to reach into a grieving widower's skull and burn out the last of his wife—the wedding, the birth, the death itself, the whole shape of a love—to furnish a receipt for the Inquisitor. To spend another person's past buying their own safety.

"No," Marguerite said.

The word came out small. She said it again, louder, and it steadied as it grew. "No."

Corvane's expression did not change, but the temperature of the room did. "Marguerite."

"He came here in good faith. Six years of good faith. We hold what people give us—that's the covenant of the house, *your* rule, the first thing you ever taught me. What is given into our keeping is sacred." Her heart was slamming against her ribs, and her voice shook, and she found she did not care. "If you want proof of loyalty for the Inquisitor, burn the ledgers, bar the doors, scatter the stock. But you don't get to spend his dead wife to buy our lives. That's not caution. That's just theft with a warrant."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—she thought he would strike her. She had never seen Corvane strike anyone; she had never even seen him raise his voice. But something moved behind his arranged face then, something old and cold and disappointed, the look of a man watching a fine instrument fail him at the worst possible hour.

"You always were sentimental," he said. "It's why I kept you close. Sentiment makes people predictable." He turned to Vance. "Go home, Master Vance. Forget you came here today—as much as you're able. The guild regrets it cannot serve you further."

Vance fled. The street door banged behind him.

Corvane straightened his cuffs. When he spoke again, his voice was mild as milk, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "The Inquisitor will be here within the week. When she comes, she will find either a loyal servant or a traitor, and you will decide which, in the next few days, one way or the other, whether you mean to or not." He paused at the foot of the stairs. "Burn the rest of the second floor before nightfall. That is not a request, Marguerite. That is the last order I will ever give you as your master."

He climbed the stairs. The archive door closed. A minute later, through the floorboards, she heard the stove door clang, and the fire begin to eat again.

Marguerite stood alone in the parlor, in the grey morning light, with the proclamation hanging on the door behind her and ash already sifting down through the house like black snow. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had finally begun to shake.

On the hall table lay the day's post, delivered before dawn by a boy who had not known what the day would bring. On top of the stack, in a spidery hand she almost recognized, was a letter addressed to *the Registrar, Vellum Guild*—and beneath the direction, where a seal should have been, someone had pressed a single grey feather into the wax.

She had never seen a feather used as a seal. She had certainly never seen one like this: barred in brown and blue, sharp-tipped, the kind of feather that belonged to a bird that did not live anywhere near the city. The kind of feather, said a half-remembered fragment of some burned book, that kestrels carried across the veil.

Marguerite picked it up. The wax cracked under her thumbnail.

Inside, in that same spidery hand, were only five words:

*They are coming for you.*
