## 3. What the Widow Knew

The knock came at dawn, and Marguerite knew before she opened the door that it was for her.

Not a knock, precisely. A hammering—three blows of an iron-shod fist against the guildhouse's lower door, each one landing in her chest like a dropped stone. She had been awake all night anyway, sitting cross-legged on her narrow bed with the oldest ledger open across her knees, tracing entries by the grey light that seeped under her shutters. Sleep had been impossible since the Proclamation. The words kept rearranging themselves behind her eyes: *all who traffic in the memories of others are declared traitors to the crown, their goods forfeit, their persons subject to the Inquisition.* She had read enough edicts in Corvane's ledgers to know what *subject to* meant. It meant rooms without windows. It meant people coming out of those rooms with their eyes gone smooth as river stones.

The hammering stopped. Voices rose and fell below—the porter's sleep-thick protests, then the crisp authority of someone accustomed to being obeyed. Boots on stairs.

Marguerite closed the ledger and did not move. Her room sat at the top of the guildhouse, three floors above the counting room, above the vault where the bottled oaths slept in their velvet cradles, above everything she had spent eleven years learning to be invisible inside. She knew every creak of these stairs the way other people knew their own pulse. She knew which boards sang under weight and which held their silence, which doors needed lifting on their hinges, which windows opened onto the drainage gutter that ran along the roofline like a spine of cold lead.

She had planned this escape without ever letting herself call it planning. That was the trick of survival in the Vellum Guild: you never admitted what you were doing until it was done.

The boots reached the second floor. A voice—female, level, carrying the particular patience of a cat at a mousehole—said something she couldn't make out. Then Corvane's voice answered, and Marguerite's blood went strange and cold. He sounded calm. He sounded *reasonable*. He was using the voice he saved for clients who haggled over prices, the voice that made you feel foolish for wanting what you wanted.

"—found in the possession of one Hester Marlowe, widow, of Candlemaker's Row," the woman was saying now, louder, each word placed like a coin on a table. "A lullaby. Sung complete, note for note, in a tavern on Tuesday last. A song the woman had not sung in nine years, by her own sworn account, because the memory of it was sold." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down beside a plate. "Sold through your house, Guildmaster. And returned to her by—someone."

Marguerite's hands went numb around the ledger.

*Hester.*

The widow from Candlemaker's Row, with her chapped hands and her kitchen that smelled of tallow and dried lavender, who had wept so quietly when she described selling the lullaby—not the memory of singing it, but the memory of her daughter's small voice harmonizing with hers, the two of them tangled together in one song before the fever took the girl. Nine years Hester had lived with a hole shaped exactly like that evening. Nine years of humming fragments that meant nothing, of turning toward a doorway expecting a duet and finding only her own thin voice.

And Marguerite had given it back. Had copied the memory out of the guild's deep archive where it lay filed under *MISCELLANEOUS VOCAL, DECEASED MINOR*, had carried it through the winter streets in a phial warmed against her breastbone, had poured it into Hester's cupped hands like water into cracked earth—and had told herself, walking home through the snow, that mercy left no trace.

But mercy left traces everywhere. Mercy was a signature. The memory had been logged out of the archive—or rather, it hadn't; she had forged the entry, but forgeries were her craft too, and somewhere in the copying, in the warming, in the giving, she had left a thread loose. The tavern. Tuesday. A widow singing whole what she should only have been able to hum.

Someone had listened. Someone had counted the notes.

Downstairs, Corvane said, "I assure you, Inquisitor, the Vellum Guild has complied fully with the Proclamation. Our registers are open to you."

"Our registers," the Inquisitor repeated, tasting the possessive. "Then you will not object to my reading them. All of them. Including the older volumes."

A silence stretched long enough for Marguerite to count her own heartbeats. Eleven years, and she had never once heard Corvane hesitate. He was the man who could price a dead man's childhood to the copper, who could look a grieving mother in the eye and name a figure. He did not hesitate.

"The oldest volumes are damaged," he said finally. "Water. Fire. The ordinary attrition of—" 

"I will read them anyway."

Another silence. Then Corvane, smoother than glass: "Of course, Inquisitor Vale. The guild has nothing to hide."

*He is going to give me to her.* The thought arrived with terrible clarity, unaccompanied by surprise. Marguerite had watched Corvane burn pages two nights ago—had stood in the counting room watching the Proclamation's ink dry on the door while he fed the archive's most recent ledgers into the stove, his face lit orange and utterly unreadable, ordering her to erase a client's memory as proof of loyalty while the paper curled and blackened. She had thought: *he is saving us.* Now, listening to him offer up the old volumes with the ease of a man surrendering another man's purse, she understood. He was saving himself. He was saving what he chose to save, and Marguerite had just heard him decide that she was not among the chosen.

Or worse—he had decided long ago. Perhaps the hesitation had been theater, performed for the Inquisitor's benefit, a small reluctance to sweeten the betrayal. Corvane had raised her from nothing. He had found her at thirteen, thieving memories off strangers in the fish market just to feel less empty, and he had taught her that the gift was a trade, that feeling was a commodity, that the ledger was law. Every kindness he had ever shown her had been entered somewhere, she realized now, in some ledger of his own. Debts. He had been keeping accounts on her since the day he took her in, and tonight the balance had come due.

Boots again. Moving toward the stairs. Toward the third floor. Toward her.

Marguerite moved.

She did not think about it—thinking was a luxury for people whose names weren't already in someone's book. She pulled on her boots, wrapped the oldest ledger in the oilcloth she kept for preserving vellum, and slung it across her body beneath her cloak. The ledger was heavier than any book had a right to be, its covers scarred leather gone soft as skin, its pages filled with handwriting that predated the guild itself. Corvane had told her once, drunk on plum brandy, that it was the first ledger, the one from which all others descended. *Everything we are is in there,* he'd said. *Guard it with your life.* She had thought he meant it as trust. Now she wondered if he had simply been telling her where the evidence lived.

The window stuck, as she knew it would. She lifted it on its hinge, swung out onto the sill, and let the February air cut through her cloak. Below, the city lay blue and frozen, chimneys breathing their morning smoke straight up into windless cold. Somewhere down in Candlemaker's Row, Hester Marlowe was in a cell, or would be by nightfall, and it was Marguerite's doing. Not the arrest—no, the arrest was the crown's—but the *evidence*. She had handed the Inquisitor a song.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, knowing the widow would never hear it, knowing apology was another thing that left traces. *I'm sorry I was kind. I'm sorry kindness was the death of both of us.*

The gutter was slick with rime. She went along it on her toes, the way she'd learned in her first year at the guild, when invisibility had still felt like safety instead of a slow starvation. Rooftop to rooftop, slate to slate, past sleeping dormers and frost-feathered chimney pots. The city unfolded beneath her, gray and gold at its edges, and for the first time in eleven years Marguerite saw it clearly: not a place to hide in, but a trap closing. Every street she had ever walked was a line in some ledger. Every client she had ever served was a witness. The Proclamation had turned the whole city into an archive of her crimes, and somewhere in it, reading that archive page by page, came the Inquisitor.

She paused in the shadow of a bell tower to get her bearings, breath smoking, fingers aching around the ledger strap. From here she could see the guildhouse roof behind her, and—she pressed herself flat—a figure emerging onto it. Small, quick, wrapped in a watchman's oilskin that fit badly. Not a watchman. One of theirs. One of the guild's runners, sent up after her? No—the figure moved wrong for a runner, moved like someone who knew roofs but not *these* roofs, hesitating at the junction where the gutter split.

Hunting her already. Or hunting the ledger.

Marguerite slid down the far side of the bell tower, dropped the last six feet into a drift of coal-dust snow behind a dyer's shed, and came up running. Her plan—if the shapeless instinct driving her deserved the word—was simple to the point of stupidity: get off the guild's streets, get out of her own patterns, become no one. There was a warren of tenements south of the Tanner's Canal where a person could dissolve into the crowd like salt into water. She'd audit nothing, restore nothing, touch no one's grief. She would bury the gift alive and let the ledger grow cold in whatever cellar she found.

She was three streets into the flight, lungs burning, when she passed the notice board outside the weighhouse and made the mistake of stopping.

The Proclamation had been copied out fair and nailed up fresh, the royal seal dripping red wax like a wound. Beneath it, newer, the ink barely dry, hung a bill of particulars:

*WANTED FOR QUESTIONING IN THE MATTER OF TREASON AND MEMORY-THEFT: a woman, middle years, plain dress, known to frequent the Vellum Guild. Also sought: any person having knowledge of the unlawful restoration of memories. Reward of fifty crowns for information leading to arrest.*

*Any person having knowledge of the unlawful restoration of memories.*

Not *theft*. *Restoration.* They knew. Not everything—they didn't have her name, or they'd have printed it—but they knew the direction of her crime, and that word, *restoration*, told her something worse than any name could have. Theft was common. The guild was full of thieves; the crown had always tolerated theft at a price. But restoration—giving back what was taken—that was supposed to be impossible. Corvane had said so a hundred times. *We take, girl. We do not give. What's given back unravels the trade.* He had said it like physics, like weather. And now the crown had written the impossible word on a public board, which meant someone had told them it was possible, which meant—

Which meant Corvane had talked. Corvane, who burned pages and opened registers and used the word *we* like a weapon. Corvane, who had taught her that the gift was a trade, had apparently appraised her gift, found it priceless, and sold the appraisal.

The cold that went through her then had nothing to do with February. She stood in front of the notice board with the crowd flowing around her—an old woman squinting at the reward, a boy stealing apples from a barrow, a pair of soldiers laughing at some joke—and understood that she had never been invisible at all. She had been *inventory*. Filed, valued, and now listed for sale. Fifty crowns. Her whole secret life, priced to the copper, the way Corvane had taught her to price everything.

She walked on. Not running now. Running drew eyes, and eyes were the new currency of the city, and she was done leaving traces she didn't choose. She crossed the Tanner's Canal on the ice-slick footbridge, turned into the tenement warren, and let the crooked streets swallow her.

By full dark she had found a room: fourth floor of a leaning house on Ropewalk Lane, rented from a landlady who asked no questions because questions cost extra. The room contained a bed, a stool, a candle stub, and a window that looked out on a wall close enough to touch. Marguerite locked the door, set the oldest ledger on the stool, and unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that would not quite steady.

In the candlelight, the first ledger looked exactly as it always had: battered, patient, indifferent. She opened it to the beginning, as she had a hundred times, and read the opening lines in that old, spidery hand:

*Let it be recorded that the covenant holds, and that the price is paid willingly, and that what is remembered cannot be unremembered, only returned.*

Covenant. The word had always seemed like guild cant, some archaic flourish the founders had liked. Tonight, alone in a stranger's room with a warrant out for a crime the crown called impossible, the word sat differently. It sat like a keyhole she'd been walking past for eleven years.

*What is remembered cannot be unremembered, only returned.*

She thought of Hester's hands cupped around a lullaby. She thought of the Inquisitor's voice downstairs, precise as a scalpel: *a song the woman had not sung in nine years.* She thought of the word on the notice board—*restoration*—and of Corvane's smooth voice offering registers he claimed were damaged, and of the figure on the roof moving like someone who knew roofs but not hers.

Somewhere out there, hunters were following a trail she had left out of love. Somewhere in a cell, a widow was paying for a song. And here, in Marguerite's hands, a book older than the guild whispered that everything she had ever been told about her gift was a lie—or half of one.

She turned the page. The candle gutted. And in the margin beside the next entry, in ink faded to the color of dried blood, she found a single line she had never seen before, though she had read this page a dozen times:

*The oathbearer comes when the veil grows thin. Guard her, for she is the last.*

Marguerite looked up at the wall, at the candle, at the small dark window, and felt the city holding its breath around her.

Footsteps on the stairs below—slow, unhurried, climbing.

* * *

They stopped at the third floor. A door opened and closed. Muffled voices, a woman's laugh, the ordinary sounds of neighbors she would never meet. Marguerite let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding and pressed her forehead against the cold window glass.

Below, on Ropewalk Lane, a lamplighter worked his way along the street, and in the halo of each new flame she watched the shadows shift and settle. Somewhere among those shadows, she knew, were eyes. Fifty crowns bought a lot of eyes in a city where half the population went to bed hungry. By morning, someone would remember a woman in a plain cloak crossing the canal footbridge. By tomorrow, the description would sharpen. Plain dress, middle years, frequenting the Vellum Guild. The net was drawing in, thread by thread, and every hour she sat still was an hour someone else spent remembering her.

She could not stay. She had known it since the stairs, since the laugh behind the thin wall, since the moment she'd understood that hiding was just another word for waiting to be found. But where does a ghost go when the whole city has learned to see ghosts?

The answer arrived the way answers always came to her—not as a thought but as a memory, unbidden. Tobias. The boy from the fish market, years ago—no. Not years. Three days ago, before the Proclamation, before the burning pages, before any of it. A boy of maybe fifteen with quick brown eyes and a smile like a knife thrown carelessly, who had caught her wrist in the crowd and said, *You're one of them, aren't you? The memory-people. I sold mine. My name. I want it back.*

She had brushed him off. She'd had a delivery to make, a client waiting, a life to keep invisible. But he had known what she was on sight, the way animals know weather, and he had named his price without flinching: *my name, the memory of it, the day I gave it away.* She had told him the guild didn't deal in returns. She had lied, and he had known she lied, and he had grinned as if being lied to were the most entertaining thing that had happened to him all week.

*Find me when you're ready to stop lying,* he'd said. *I'll be at the sign of the Crooked Kestrel. You'll know it by the bird with the broken wing.*

The Crooked Kestrel. A tavern she had walked past a hundred times without seeing, in a part of the city where the law came rarely and asked fewer questions. A boy who had sold his own name and wanted it back—which was impossible, which was the very crime the crown had invented a new word for, which was the thing the first ledger whispered about in margins faded like dried blood.

*The oathbearer comes when the veil grows thin.*

Marguerite snuffed the candle and sat in the dark, listening to the house settle around her, the pipes knocking, the wind testing the shutters. On the stool, the oldest ledger waited, patient as a stone. She thought of Hester in her cell, singing to no one. She thought of the word *restoration* nailed to a public board, and of how a lie repeated often enough becomes architecture, and of how she had lived inside that architecture for eleven years believing it was the world.

Tomorrow she would find the Crooked Kestrel. Tomorrow she would stop lying—to the boy, to herself, to the dead hand that wrote in margins. Tonight she allowed herself one minute of grief for the life she'd had: the candlelit audits, the quiet rebellion, the small mercies smuggled out of the archive like contraband tenderness. It had been a small life. It had been hers. And she had traded it for a widow's song without hesitation, and she found, examining herself honestly in the dark, that she could not regret it. Regret required wishing she had acted otherwise, and some stubborn core of her refused. Let the crown call it treason. Let Corvane sell her name to the highest bidder. She had put a lullaby back into the world, and the world was measurably less broken for it, and no ledger could unwrite that.

But regret's absence was not the same as peace. Fear sat in her chest like swallowed river water, cold and rising. She was hunted now, truly hunted, and the hunter had a name—Vale—and a reputation that preceded her like a smell. They said Seraphine Vale could take a memory out of a living head the way you'd draw a splinter, clean and total, no consent asked, no payment offered. They said the people she finished with forgot their own children's faces. If Vale caught her, Marguerite would not even know she had lost anything. She would simply wake up smooth.

She would not be caught. She folded the ledger back into its oilcloth, tied the straps, and slung it across her body where it lay against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Then she lay down on the stranger's bed, boots on, cloak bunched beneath her head, and stared at the ceiling until the sounds of the house softened into sleep-sounds and the footsteps overhead ceased and the last lamp on Ropewalk Lane guttered out.

Sleep took her eventually, shallow and salted with dreams. She dreamed of feathers, hundreds of them, drifting down through a green light like snow falling upward, and a voice moving through them like wind through a forest—saying something urgent, something meant for her alone, something she strained toward and could not catch. She woke once, certain she had heard her name spoken in that feathered voice, certain it had sounded like an accusation and an invitation at once. But the room was dark and silent, and the voice was gone, and only the ledger against her ribs remained, warm from her body, heavy with its secrets.

Morning came gray and thin. Marguerite rose, counted her coins—nine coppers and a clipped silver, not enough for a week anywhere decent, enough for three days anywhere honest—and went down into the waking city wearing her invisibility like a garment she no longer trusted.

The Crooked Kestrel occupied the ground floor of a building that leaned drunkenly over Cutpurse Alley, its sign swinging on a rusted bracket: a wooden kestrel with one wing snapped mid-flight, painted and repainted so many times the bird had gone featureless, an anonymous brown shape against the sky. Inside smelled of tallow smoke, spilled ale, and frying onions. The morning crowd was the usual congregation of the city's discarded: dockers between ships, a fortune-teller with dead eyes shuffling cards no one asked her to cut, two old men arguing over dice with the passion of men who had nothing else left to argue about.

And in the corner, in the shadow of the broken-winged sign's reflection in the window, sat the boy.

He had seen her come in—of course he had; he'd probably seen her three streets away—and he didn't wave or call out. He simply turned his mug a quarter-turn on the table, the way a merchant turns a scale, and waited. When she sat down across from him, he pushed the second mug toward her without asking, and his grin was exactly as she remembered: careless, sharp, entirely unsorry.

"You stopped lying," he said. "Good. Took you long enough."

Marguerite wrapped her hands around the mug and did not drink. "How did you know I'd come?"

"Didn't." He shrugged. "Hoped. There's a difference. Hope's cheaper." His eyes flicked to the lump beneath her cloak where the ledger rode against her ribs, then back to her face, and something in them sharpened. "You're in trouble."

"I'm in trouble," she agreed.

"Big trouble. Crown trouble." He leaned forward, dropping his voice beneath the din. "There's a bill with your shape on it. Fifty crowns. I could eat for a year on fifty crowns." He let that sit between them, grinning, and then, softly, with the grin suddenly gone: "I'm not going to. Before you ask. I don't sell people who bring me what I asked for." He tapped the table twice. "You brought it?"

"I brought the possibility of it," Marguerite said carefully. "I need to understand something first. About what happens when a memory is returned."

Something flickered across his face—hope trying not to be hope, hope embarrassed to be seen. "And if I don't like the answer?"

"Then you walk away, and I walk away, and neither of us mentions this table again."

He considered her for a long moment, this boy with no name and a knife-smile, and then he nodded slowly, and the bargain was struck without another word, sealed in tallow smoke and bad ale, between two people who had nothing left to lose but each other's company.

"What's your name?" he asked. "Your real one. Since we're trading true things."

"Marguerite."

"Marguerite." He tried it out, rolling it like a stolen apple. "Pretty. Wasted on a fugitive." He stuck out his hand across the table, grimy and quick. "You can call me Toby. Everyone does. It's not my name—it's the shape of the hole where my name used to be. But it fits well enough." His grin came back, softer at the edges now. "So. Memory-restorer. Fugitive. Woman with a book that makes her walk like she's carrying a baby. Tell me why a guild thief crossed the whole city to find a nameless boy in a den of thieves."

Marguerite looked at him—at the quickness in him, the loneliness dressed up as swagger, the hole shaped like a name—and felt the first unfamiliar thing she had felt in days: not fear, not guilt, but the faint, terrifying warmth of not being alone.

"Because," she said, "I think you're the only person in this city who wants what I can give. And I think—" she touched the ledger through the oilcloth, feeling the old vellum breathe against her palm, "—I think this book says I'm supposed to."

Outside, the wooden kestrel swung on its rusted bracket, one wing forever broken, and somewhere across the frozen city a cell door clanged shut on a widow who would sing her daughter's song to walls that could not listen. The hunt was on. The veil, wherever and whatever it was, was thinning. And in a corner of the Crooked Kestrel, over mugs of terrible ale, a fugitive and a nameless boy shook hands on a bargain that would carry them both—though neither of them knew it yet—all the way to the Standing Stones.
