## 4. The Boy Who Sold His Name

Tobias Quill had three rules for surviving Ashfall's rooftops, and he'd carved them into his own skull so thoroughly that even he couldn't steal them back: never be seen, never be owed, and never sleep in the same chimney twice.

He was breaking the third rule when he found her.

The garret above the tannery on Cordwainer's Row was his because nobody else wanted it. The smell alone kept away the watch, the beggars, and every rat with functioning nostrils, which meant it was the safest square of roof in the whole crooked city. He'd wedged himself into the space between the chimney stack and the slates where the heat from the curing vats below rose up through the shingles like a fever, and he was halfway to a dream about nothing in particular—dreams were a luxury he rationed like sugar—when a sound woke him.

Not a footstep. Footsteps he knew by weight and rhythm; he could name a man by the way his heel struck tile. This was something smaller. The creak of a slate taking weight it wasn't used to, badly. Someone climbing who didn't climb.

Tobias opened one eye.

The moon had gone behind the smoke-haze that hung over Ashfall most nights, but there was enough light to see her: a woman crouched at the ridge of the roof opposite, gripping the tiles with both hands as though the city might shake her loose. She wore a clerk's grey cloak, mud-hemmed, and she moved like someone who had spent her whole life indoors and had recently been evicted from it. Her hair had escaped its pins in dark ropes. And she was looking down into the street below with an expression Tobias recognized, because he'd worn it himself once, years ago, before he learned to wear nothing at all.

It was the look of a person listening for boots.

He should have rolled over and gone back to sleep. Rule one: never be seen. But rule one assumed the thing being seen was worth seeing, and this woman was a spectacle—a guild-clerk type, soft-handed, perched on a rooftop like a cat that had been taught to read. Curiosity was the one theft Tobias had never managed to quit.

He watched her try to stand, watch her slip, watch her catch the gutter with a scrape that rang out over the Row like a dropped bell.

Below, in the mouth of Cordwainer's Lane, a lantern stopped moving.

*Hunters.* It had to be. The Proclamation had gone up on the guildhouse doors three days past, nailed there by a herald with a voice like a trumpet falling downstairs—*all who traffic in memory are traitors to the crown, all who bear the gift shall be taken, all who shelter them shall share their chains.* Tobias had heard it secondhand, from a baker's boy who'd heard it wrong, which was how Tobias heard everything important. But the shape of it was clear enough. The crown had decided that people who could take a memory out of your head were dangerous, and the crown was not famous for its patience with dangerous things.

The woman on the roof had frozen. The lantern below began to move again, slower now, the way hunters moved when they'd heard something too.

Tobias sighed at himself. He could already feel the shape of the mistake he was about to make, the way you feel rain coming in your knees.

He whistled—two notes, low—and when her head snapped toward him, he beckoned once with two fingers and slid backward off the chimney stack into the dark gap between the tannery and the dyer's next door, down the ladder of leaning boards he'd nailed there himself, into the garret that smelled like a dead animal's ambitions.

Then he waited, arms crossed, to find out what kind of fool he'd just invited home.

* * *

She came through the window badly. There was no graceful way into his garret—the frame had warped years ago and only opened if you lifted and shoved and cursed in the right order—and she did none of these things correctly, so she arrived in a clatter of elbows and cloak, landing on his floor pallet in a heap of grey wool and held breath.

For a moment neither of them spoke. She knelt there among his possessions—which consisted of a blanket, a knife, a chipped cup, four books stolen for their covers rather than their contents, and a tin whistle he couldn't play—and looked around the room as though cataloguing it. He saw her eyes catch on the books, then the knife, then him, and he watched her do the arithmetic everyone did when they first got a good look at Tobias Quill: *young, thin, sharp-faced, harmless-looking, probably not.*

"You're bleeding," he said, because her palm was, from the gutter-scrape, a thin dark line across it.

"It's nothing." Her voice was low and precise, the voice of someone who read aloud in quiet rooms. "I'll go. I'm sorry—I thought—" She glanced at the window. Below, faintly, the lantern light swept the mouth of the lane and moved on. Her shoulders came down half an inch. "Thank you."

"Didn't do anything yet." He stayed by the wall, between her and the door, out of habit. "Question is whether I did something stupid. You're one of them, aren't you. Memory-folk."

Something went very still in her face. Not fear exactly—or fear, but wearing the uniform of dignity over it. "And if I am?"

"Then the whole city's supposed to hand you to the Inquisitor, and instead I hauled you into my house." He said it flatly, without accusation, mostly because he was still deciding whether it *was* an accusation. "So either I'm a traitor now, or you're about to tell me you're a clerk who likes climbing roofs at midnight, and we both pretend the last five minutes happened."

The woman rose slowly to her feet. Up close she was younger than the cloak suggested, mid-twenties maybe, with ink stains on the fingers of her right hand and a smudge of candle-soot along her jaw. She looked tired in the deep way, the way that doesn't wash off.

"My name is Marguerite," she said. "I keep ledgers."

"For the Vellum Guild."

Her chin lifted. "You've heard of us."

"Everyone's heard of the Vellum Guild. Half of Ashfall's sold them something they can't remember selling." He let that sit. "I know because I'm the other half."

Understanding moved through her face slowly, like frost crossing a window. "You're a thief."

"I'm a *collector*," Tobias said, with the particular dignity he reserved for the word. "Thieves take things people want to keep. I take things people want gone. Mostly. Sometimes the line gets blurry." He tilted his head at her. "Which is why I know what your guild pays for memories, and why I know you're not just a ledger-keeper, because ledger-keepers don't run across rooftops with hunters under them. Ledger-keepers get burned with the rest of the paper. So. What are you?"

For a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. She stood in the middle of his miserable garret with the tannery stink seeping through the floorboards and the moonlight laying a pale stripe across her, and she looked at him the way he imagined she looked at accounts that refused to balance—like a problem she intended, patiently, to solve.

"I could erase tonight from your mind," she said quietly. "Before I leave. You'd wake tomorrow and never know I existed."

Cold walked up Tobias's spine, but he kept his face still. Rule two: never be owed. Rule two had a corollary he'd never written down: *never give anyone power over what you remember*, because a man's memories were the only property he truly owned, and Tobias had built his whole life on owning almost nothing else.

"You could try," he said.

"Yes," she agreed, and there was something in her voice—not quite a threat, more like an honest weighing—that told him she knew exactly how it would go between them, and it would not go quickly or cleanly for either of them. "I could. I won't. That's not—" She stopped. Started again. "That's not what my gift is for. Or it shouldn't be. I don't take things, Mr.—?"

"Quill. Tobias Quill."

"Mr. Quill." A pause, and then, so dry it was nearly funny: "You stole your own name from somewhere. Nobody's born sounding like a debt collector's fiction."

He grinned despite himself. "Bought it off a drunk archivist for two coppers and a kiss he doesn't remember giving. Best trade I ever made." Then the grin faded, because the truth underneath it was less funny, and it had been sitting in his chest all night like a stone he'd swallowed years ago and never passed. "Actually, that's a lie. Worst trade I ever made. But it's the one I can't stop thinking about."

Marguerite waited. She was good at waiting; he could tell. It was probably a professional skill.

"I sold a memory," Tobias said. "When I was eleven. To your guild, or whatever it was before it was your guild—one of the little offices off Ropewalk, the ones that fed into the Vellum House. One memory. That was the deal. They took one and paid me enough to eat for a winter, and I told myself it was nothing, some dull afternoon, who'd miss it." His jaw tightened. "Except it wasn't nothing. I know it wasn't, because of the hole. You spend your whole life walking around a hole in yourself, you learn its shape even if you can't see what filled it. Mine has edges like—" He gestured vaguely, frustrated. "Like a name. Like somebody saying a name, and it matters, and I can't hear it anymore. I sold my name, lady. Whatever my mother called me before I was Tobias Quill, whoever gave it to me—it's in one of your guild's bottles, sitting on a shelf gathering dust, and I have spent nine years trying to buy it back, and the guild doesn't sell *back*. They only ever sell forward."

Silence. The tannery vats gurgled below like something digesting.

"They don't sell back," Marguerite repeated softly. Something crossed her face—guilt, he'd have called it, if he hadn't known better than to trust faces. "No. They don't."

"So here's my offer." He stepped away from the wall, and the knife stayed where it was; this wasn't going to be a knife conversation, he could feel it, this was going to be worse than that. "You're hunted. You need a roof, food, streets you don't know, and I know every chimney, cellar, and culvert in Ashfall better than the rats do. I can keep you alive until this Proclamation blows over or doesn't. In exchange—" his heart was hammering, which annoyed him, because he'd sworn never to want anything badly enough to hammer over it— "you go back into that guildhouse of yours, or wherever the stock went, and you find my bottle. My memory. And you give it back."

"That's theft," she said.

"It's *return*," Tobias said. "There's a difference. Ask any fence."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you can climb back out that window and take your chances with the lanterns, and I'll have lost nothing but a night's sleep." He held her gaze. It cost him something to add the next part, but lies were bad currency with people like her—he could see she weighed words the way he weighed purses. "And if you're caught anyway, and dragged in front of the Inquisitor, and she cracks your head open like a walnut to see what's inside—" he'd heard the stories, everyone had, of Seraphine Vale and the husks she left behind— "then I suppose I lose the only person who knows where my name is kept. So I'd rather you lived. Call it self-interest."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, haunting the premises.

"Self-interest," she echoed. "Of course."

"I'm a thief. We're famous for it."

* * *

They negotiated until the sky went grey at the edges, sitting on opposite ends of his blanket like two merchants dividing a corpse. She was harder than he'd expected. He'd assumed guild-folk would be soft, all candlelight and quills, but Marguerite Ravensworth bargained like someone who had audited a thousand deals and trusted none of them.

She would not promise to break into the guildhouse while the hunters watched it—reasonable, he hated it. She would not promise to restore the memory herself, only to find it, because—and here she hesitated in a way that snagged his attention like a loose thread—*restoration was not what her gift was supposed to do, and she didn't know if she could.* He turned that over and found it strange. What use was a memory-taker who couldn't put things back? Wasn't that like a locksmith who could only break locks? But she said it with such careful blankness that he filed it away among the things he'd steal later, when he knew her better.

In exchange she wanted: passage out of the city within three days, to somewhere south, somewhere with trees; silence about her face and name; and one condition stated so plainly it startled him.

"If I find your memory," she said, "and I give it back to you—you may discover you were better off without it. Names come attached to people. People come attached to grief. I've seen what happens when a widow gets back a lullaby she traded away to stop hearing it." Her eyes went distant, fixed on something he couldn't see. "Grief is the price of remembering. I need you to understand that before you agree. If I put it back and it breaks you, that breaking belongs to you. Not to me."

Tobias sat with that. Outside, the first carts were rattling over the cobbles, the bakers' boys were shouting, the city was doing its morning impression of a place where nothing terrible had ever happened.

"Somebody gave me a name," he said finally. "Somebody sang it over me, or shouted it, or wrote it on a birth-roll somewhere. Nine years I've walked around the hole where that should be, and everybody in Ashfall calls me a made-up word I bought off a drunk." He shrugged, and tried to make the shrug careless, and knew he failed. "Maybe it breaks me. Maybe it's worth it. Either way I'd rather be broken by the truth than comfortable in a lie. That's not self-interest. That's just—" he searched for the word and landed, embarrassed, on the true one— "that's just wanting to be *real*."

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment. Then she extended her ink-stained right hand across the blanket, formal as a treaty.

"Tobias Quill," she said. "You have a bargain."

He shook it. Her grip was stronger than her hands looked.

* * *

He slept in the chimney-corner that day with the knife unsheathed beside him, which was not trust, exactly, but was the closest thing to trust Tobias Quill had offered another human being since he was small enough to still believe in mothers. When he woke at dusk, she was still there—she hadn't robbed him blind and vanished, which raised her considerably in his estimation—and she had found his tin whistle and was turning it over with a puzzled expression.

"It's out of tune," she said.

"It's a whistle. It's supposed to be out of tune. That's how you know it's honest."

And she laughed—short, surprised, rusty, like a hinge that hadn't swung in years—and the sound did something strange to the little room, filled up corners that had been empty so long Tobias had stopped noticing they were empty.

Down in the street, a bell began to ring. Not the hour-bell. The other one, the fast one, the one the churches only rang for fire or treason. Voices rose along Cordwainer's Row, and running footsteps, and someone shouting a word that came up through the floorboards muffled but unmistakable:

"*—taken a widow, they've taken the widow on Bellows Lane—*"

Marguerite went white. She stood very still, the whistle forgotten in her hand, and Tobias watched the color drain out of her face and understood, with the instinct of a boy who had survived by reading rooms, that she knew the widow on Bellows Lane.

"What is it?" he asked. "Who is she?"

Marguerite set the whistle down carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"Get your coat," she said. "I'll explain on the way. And Tobias—" her voice had changed, gone hard and quick, the ledger-keeper replaced by something with an edge— "the guildhouse keeps its stock in the vault under the counting room. Third shelf, blue glass, the oldest bottles. If anything happens to me, that's where your name is."

The bell kept ringing. Somewhere across the city, a crowd was gathering around a scaffold or a pyre or a cage, and a woman was about to be made an example of, and Marguerite Ravensworth was pulling on her ruined grey cloak with shaking hands.

Tobias Quill had three rules, and he was about to break all of them at once.

He grabbed his coat and followed her out the window.
