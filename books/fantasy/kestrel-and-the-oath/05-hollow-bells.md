## 5. Hollow Bells

The bells of Saint Verity's rang wrong that morning.

Marguerite heard it from the doorway of the dye-house where Tobias had stashed her—a flat, cracked note, like a coin bitten by a counterfeiter. The carillon had rung true her whole life, marking hours and weddings and funerals with the same indifferent silver voice. Today it sounded as though something had been scooped out of it, some essential resonance taken and replaced with the shape of sound but not its soul.

She stood in the shadow of the archway with the old ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest and understood, with the cold certainty of a woman who has spent her life reading other people's losses, that the bells were not broken.

They were grieving.

* * *

The square before Saint Verity's filled early. Marguerite let the crowd carry her, hood up, shoulders bent into the posture of a laundress or a fishwife—anyone forgettable. She had learned invisibility the way other children learned prayers, and she wore it now like armor that had grown too thin.

A scaffold had been raised on the cathedral steps. Not a gallows—something stranger. A single iron chair sat at its center, ringed with bands of pale metal that caught the light like fish scales. Candles burned at each corner, white ones, unbleached tallow, the kind the Guild used for work that required no warmth.

*Memory-candles,* she thought, and her stomach turned over. *They've been buying from us. Or we've been selling to them without knowing who held the purse.*

On the scaffold's edge stood a woman in dove-grey, hands folded, still as a saint in a niche. She wore no insignia of office, only a thin chain at her throat bearing a single feather worked in silver—and yet the soldiers around her arranged themselves like iron filings around a lodestone. Even at forty paces, even through the murmur of the crowd, Marguerite felt the pull of her.

Inquisitor Seraphine Vale. The name had come down through the Guild's whisper-network for three days now, always in the same hushed register people used for plague and weather. *She doesn't need instruments,* the whispers said. *She doesn't need consent.*

And there, between two soldiers whose pikes trembled just slightly—soldiers were afraid of her too—came Maren Aldous.

The widow.

Marguerite's breath went out of her in a long, silent ache. Three days since the rooftops, since she'd crouched behind a chimney pot and watched red-coats kick in the widow's door, and she had told herself a hundred merciful lies: they'd only question her, they'd release her, an old woman who'd done nothing but grieve could not be made to matter to an empire. Now here was the lie made flesh, walking up the scaffold steps in a nightcap and house-slippers because they hadn't given her time to dress.

Maren Aldous had been a client of the Guild for eleven years. She had sold the Guild her wedding day—every kiss, every candle, every promise—in the winter after her husband died, because grief was heavier than hunger and she'd needed coin more than she needed joy. And eleven days ago, Marguerite had opened the vault, found that small glass vial with its ribbon of gold light, and poured the lullaby back into the widow's sleeping mind for free. A stolen song returned. A kindness.

A signature written in a hand the crown could read.

*They traced it.* The thought arrived with terrible clarity. *The lullaby came back to her from nowhere. Someone asked where. Someone answered.* Marguerite did not know who had talked—the Guild's ledgers were sealed, Corvane burned what he could—but someone had, and now Maren Aldous stood blinking in the morning light, confused and small, holding the crime of having remembered something beautiful.

"Good people of Vellamarch." Vale's voice carried without effort, low and clear, the way water carries under ice. "You have heard the Proclamation of Severance. Memory-gifted persons are declared traitors to the crown. Their art is theft. Their trade is poison. This woman—" she gestured, gentle as a hostess presenting a guest, "—is not your enemy. She is a victim. And I am going to show you what was done to her."

Two soldiers guided the widow into the iron chair. The pale bands closed over her wrists with a sound like a struck bell, and Marguerite flinched, because she knew that sound. Resonance-iron. It didn't bind the body. It tuned the mind, drew every memory to the surface like fish rising to feed.

Vale knelt beside the chair so that her face was level with the widow's. From this distance Marguerite could not hear what she said. But she saw Maren Aldous's expression change—saw fear soften into something worse, into trust, into the dazed gratitude of a lonely woman being listened to at last.

*Oh,* Marguerite thought. *Oh, that's how she does it.*

* * *

What happened next, the crowd would describe afterward in a dozen different ways, and all of them would be wrong.

Marguerite alone, standing in the press of bodies with her gift straining against her ribs like a bird against a window, saw truly.

Vale did not cut. There was no blade, no vial, no needle, none of the Guild's careful apparatus of consent and compensation. She simply leaned close, laid two fingers against the widow's temple, and *breathed in*.

Marguerite felt it across the square the way you feel a door open in winter. The widow's memories rose out of her—not offered, not traded, not paid for—ripped loose whole, roots and all. Marguerite had spent her life handling memories the way a midwife handles newborns: gently, reverently, leaving the living tissue intact, taking only what was freely given and sealing the wound behind her. What Vale did was not taking. It was *excision*. She pulled the lullaby out of Maren Aldous and she pulled everything tangled around it—the husband's voice singing it, the kitchen where he'd sung, the twenty years of mornings that had ripened around that song—and she left nothing.

The widow's face emptied.

It happened slowly enough to be deniable, which was the horror of it. First the eyes went vague, then the mouth forgot its habitual line of worry, then the whole architecture of the person sagged like wet paper. When Vale straightened and stepped back, Maren Aldous sat in the iron chair looking at the crowd with the mild, vacant interest of an infant. Her lips moved. No words came.

"She was made to carry a stolen thing," Vale announced to the square, and her voice was sorrow itself, beautifully performed. "The thieves put a song in her head that was never hers, and the song was rotting her from within. I have removed it. She is clean now." She turned to the widow with a smile of infinite compassion. "You are safe, mother. Tell them how you feel."

Maren Aldous looked at her, at the crowd, at the sky, with the open blankness of a page before ink.

"I feel," the widow said slowly, tasting each word like a foreign coin, "like a room after the furniture's been sold."

A ripple went through the crowd—unease, quickly smothered. People wanted to believe. Believing was easier than the alternative, which was that any one of them could be next, that the things they treasured could be pulled out through their temples in front of their neighbors while a woman in dove-grey smiled.

Marguerite did not want to believe. Marguerite wanted to be sick.

Because she recognized what she was seeing. Strip away the cruelty, strip away the consent, and underneath Vale's gift was *hers*. The same current. The same touch. She could feel the extraction happening as if it were happening through her own hands, could feel the shape of Vale's power the way a harpist feels another harp played in the next room. Every gift in the Guild was a variation on a theme—Corvane could bottle oaths, little Ferro could taste lies, Odile had once been able to walk through doors that weren't there. But theft of memory, true theft, was rare. Two practitioners in a generation, perhaps.

And one of them was doing *this* with it.

*I could become that.* The thought arrived unbidden, unwelcome, precise as a scalpel. *If I stopped asking permission. If I decided my need outweighed theirs. If I got hungry enough, or frightened enough, or righteous enough.* The distance between Marguerite's careful, guilty, candlelit mercy and the thing on the scaffold was not a chasm. It was a decision. Made once, and then made easier each time.

Her hands shook inside her sleeves. She pressed them flat against the ledger beneath the oilcloth, feeling its familiar weight, its record of every small theft done honestly, and hated it and clung to it in the same breath.

* * *

Vale was not finished.

"You will ask," she said, pacing the scaffold now, addressing the crowd like a congregation, "why the crown concerns itself with songs and wedding days. You will say: these are small things. Who is harmed?" She paused, letting the question hang, and Marguerite realized with a lurch that Vale was good—genuinely, terrifyingly good, that she believed her own sermon down to the marrow. "I will tell you who is harmed. Every stolen memory weakens the wall between what is and what should not be. The Guild trades in the substance of souls, and souls are the mortar of this realm. They have been pulling mortar from the wall for a hundred years." Her hand rose to the silver feather at her throat, an unconscious gesture, almost tender. "The wall is nearly down, good people. And when it falls, what comes through will not trade fairly."

*She knows.* Marguerite's blood went cold. *Not the edict's language—she knows something real. Something older.* The words were wrong, garbled, a covenant translated into a threat, but the shape beneath them was unmistakable. The veil. The fading. Odile's maps and Wren's—no, she hadn't met Wren yet, wouldn't for weeks, but the Kestrel Court was already a half-heard whisper in the oldest pages of the ledger she carried against her heart.

Vale knew about the veil. And she was tearing it further with every memory she ripped loose, and she either didn't know that part or didn't care.

Then the Inquisitor did something that made the crowd gasp and made Marguerite's knees go soft.

She reached into her own coat and drew out a memory-vial—one of the Guild's own make, stoppered with wax stamped with the raven sigil—and held it up to the light.

"Recovered from the guildhouse of Corvane," she said. "He cooperates with the crown fully. He has given me his inventory, his clients, and his methods." She smiled, and for one flickering instant the compassion fell away and Marguerite saw what lived underneath: a vast, cold, orderly hunger, a library where a heart should be. "This vial contains a memory of a girl. An apprentice. The Guild's auditor. The one who kept the books." Vale's gaze lifted from the vial and swept the crowd, unhurried, thorough, a lighthouse beam turning. "She has fled. She is hiding somewhere in this city with a ledger that names every memory-thief in Vellamarch—including, I suspect, several of you."

The crowd stirred. Neighbors looked at neighbors. Marguerite felt the ancient arithmetic of fear begin its work: *better her than me, better I point than be pointed at.*

"The crown pays for names," Vale said softly. "And for the ledger, it pays a great deal more."

* * *

Marguerite did not remember deciding to move. One moment she was stone in the crowd; the next she was drifting backward through the press of bodies, one step at a time, invisible, her heart hammering so hard she was certain the whole square must hear it. Behind her, the soldiers were lifting Maren Aldous from the chair—or what remained of Maren Aldous, a woman who no longer knew her own name, who would spend the rest of her life in the crown's charitable houses being fed, clothed, and never once knowing why the world felt so empty.

*I did that.* The guilt was a knife turning slowly. *My mercy. My one free gift. I wrote her name in the book of the ruined.* And beneath the guilt, colder and quieter, the other truth coiled: *Vale has a memory of me. Corvane gave it to her. He knows I ran. He knows what I carry. He is selling me the way he sold everything else.*

She reached the mouth of Tanner's Lane before the first shout went up behind her—someone had seen a face, or thought they had, or simply wanted coin. Boots on cobbles. A whistle, sharp as a kestrel's cry.

Marguerite ran.

She ran the way she'd run across the rooftops three nights ago, ledger clutched to her chest, past the dye-house where Tobias waited with his sharp grin and his dangerous bargain, past safety, because the bells were still ringing their hollow cracked note overhead and she finally understood what they were mourning.

Not the widow. Not the law.

They were mourning the wall coming down—and somewhere in the crowd below the scaffold, in a dove-grey coat with a stolen girl's memory in her pocket, the woman who would bring it down was already listening for the sound of Marguerite's name.
