## 6. The Cartographer of Doors

Tobias had never trusted a door he couldn't kick open.

It was a principle learned young, in the years after he'd sold his name—doors that opened politely were doors that wanted something from you, and doors that required knocking were doors with people behind them, and people were the whole problem with the world. So when Marguerite stopped before a shuttered chandlery at the crooked end of Candlemakers' Row and said, "Here," Tobias looked at the blank wall beside it and felt his stomach drop.

"Here," she said again, and pressed her palm flat against the bricks.

Nothing happened. Rain dripped off the eaves into the collar of his coat. Somewhere behind them, a crier was still shouting the Proclamation of Severance in a voice gone hoarse with repetition—*traitors, traitors, all who traffic in memory*—and Tobias kept one eye on the street's empty length and the other on the woman who had, three days ago, traded him sanctuary for a bargain neither of them fully understood.

"You're certain this is the place?" he asked.

"I'm certain of what Odile wrote." Marguerite's fingers moved along the mortar lines like she was reading them. "Third house past the bell-foundry. *Knock where there is no door.*"

"That's not instructions. That's a riddle."

"It's a map," she said. "Everything Odile makes is a map."

The brick under her palm was warmer than the others. Tobias saw her feel it too—a flicker across her face, quick as a fish turning—and then the wall exhaled. There was no other word for it. The bricks sighed apart along a seam that hadn't existed a moment before, dust sifting down like flour, and beyond them lay a passage lit by lamps that burned without oil, their flames a soft greenish white, the color of foxfire or deep water.

Tobias did not move.

"Problem?" Marguerite asked.

"Doors that open themselves are worse than doors that don't," he said. "I have a whole philosophy about it."

She almost smiled. It was the first time he'd seen anything close to a smile on her—the ledger-woman, the memory-thief with ink-stained fingers and a way of looking at him as if she were cataloguing him for some private archive. Three days of rooftops and back alleys and sleeping in shifts, and he still didn't know what to make of her. She'd bought his silence with a promise to find his name, and then the world had caught fire around them both, and now here they were, walking into a wall because a dead woman's handwriting told them to.

Not dead. He corrected himself. Exiled. There was a difference, probably.

"After you," he said, because if something in that passage ate people, it could start with someone who deserved it less.

* * *

Inside was a shop that was larger than the building containing it, which Tobias decided not to think about too hard, on the grounds that thinking about it would cost him sleep he couldn't afford.

Maps covered every surface. Not maps of any city he knew—or rather, maps of cities he half-knew, drawn slightly wrong, streets bending where they shouldn't, rivers running uphill in confident blue ink. Maps hung from the ceiling on wires, rotating slowly in a draft he couldn't feel. Maps lay rolled in barrels labeled in a script that hurt to look at directly, letters that seemed to rearrange themselves when he blinked. And everywhere, everywhere, the smell: cedar oil and old paper and something underneath it, something cold and mineral, like the air inside a cave that has never seen weather.

"She's real," Marguerite breathed. "Odile Voss. The Cartographer of Doors."

"Don't say that name loud," said a voice from the back of the shop. "Names are how they find you."

The woman who emerged was small and sharp-faced, sixty if she was a day, with white hair cropped close as a hedge and spectacles pushed up into it. Her hands were stained every color ink comes in. She carried a long brass instrument Tobias didn't recognize, something between a sextant and a lockpick, and she looked at the two of them the way a jeweler looks at paste stones.

"You're bleeding on my threshold," she said to Tobias.

He looked down. His boot had left a dark print on the pale floorboards. "Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be precise." She turned to Marguerite, and something in her face shifted—not softer, exactly, but more careful, the way a hand is careful around a blade. "You're Corvane's girl. The auditor."

"I was never his girl," Marguerite said. "And I'm no one's anything now."

"Mm." Odile set the brass instrument down on a table crowded with compasses. "That's what I said, forty years ago. Come in properly, both of you, and mind the third step. It goes down further than it looks. Most things do."

* * *

The third step did go down further than it looked. Beyond it was a room with no windows and a table made of a single slab of river stone, and on that table, under a glass dome, lay a map that made Tobias forget, for a moment, everything he was afraid of.

It showed two worlds.

One was his own—he recognized the coastline, the capital, the river, though rendered in fine gold line as if drawn by someone who loved it. And threaded through it, over it, under it, ran a second country: a realm drawn in silver, its borders not lines but feathers, thousands upon thousands of tiny silver feathers, each one inscribed with writing too small for any human eye. Where the two realms touched, the gold and silver overlapped, and in those places the mapmaker had used an ink that shimmered even now, even in lamplight, like oil on water.

"The Kestrel Court," Marguerite whispered.

"You know the stories," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"Everyone knows the stories. The falcon-folk. The ones who taught us the old crafts—how to bind a book so it never breaks, how to sing a roof onto a house. My grandmother swore her mother had seen one, at a fair, buying ribbon." Marguerite's hand hovered over the glass without touching it. "Everyone knows the stories. Nobody believes them."

"Nobody believes them because nobody remembers them properly," Odile said. "Which is the point. Which was always the point." She lifted the dome, and the smell of the map rose up—cold and green, like the moment before snow. "Sit down, girl. You especially. This is a longer story than your legs want to hear standing."

* * *

What Odile told them, in a voice dry as autumn paper, was this:

Before the crowns, before the guilds, before the city had a name, the human realm and the Kestrel Court had been one country with two peoples. The falcon-folk lived in the high thin places—cliff eyries, cloud meadows, the tops of storms—and humans lived in the valleys, and between them ran trade the way blood runs through a body. The kestrels gave craft and clear sight and songs that could mend broken things. Humans gave weight and warmth and *memory*, which the falcon-folk had little of, being creatures of the present tense. A kestrel could hold a moment perfectly but not a lifetime. They needed humans to remember for them. That was the first covenant: *we will carry your past, if you will lend us our future.*

"And to seal it," Odile said, "the two peoples made an oath together at the Standing Stones, on an equinox, when the veil between the realms wears thin as spring ice. An oath spoken by an oathbearer—someone gifted to hold memory whole, to give it and take it back without breaking either side. There were always a few such. Rare. Precious." Her eyes, small and bright behind her smudged spectacles, settled on Marguerite like a bird settling on a branch. "The guild used to call them restorers, back when the guild remembered what it was for."

Tobias watched Marguerite go very still. He'd seen her still before—on the rooftop, when the bells had started ringing for the arrests—but this was different. This was the stillness of someone hearing their own name spoken in a language they didn't know they spoke.

"The covenant held for six hundred years," Odile went on. "Then, forty years ago, it broke. The oathbearer of that generation refused to pay the covenant's price—you'll learn what that price is soon enough, and I hope it doesn't come out of you—and fled his duty. The oath unraveled. The veil began to thicken. And the Kestrel Court began to fade." She tapped the silver feathers on the map, one by one. "They're not dying the way things die. They're being *forgotten*. Every year fewer humans remember them, and every year the Court grows thinner, more transparent, more like a story and less like a people. In another generation or two, there'll be nothing left but the stories, and badly told ones at that."

"The Proclamation," Marguerite said slowly. "Severance. Why now? Why would the crown declare war on memory-gifted if the covenant's already broken?"

"Because the crown knows what's coming." Odile's mouth twisted. "There are those at court who understand the veil is failing, and they've decided the answer is to sever cleanly—to burn every bridge, arrest every gift-bearer, end even the possibility of reforging. A kingdom can survive losing its magic, girl. What it cannot survive is owing a debt. And the covenant is a debt, written into the bones of the land. Reforge it, and the price must be paid. Sever it forever, and the debt dies unclaimed." She shrugged, a small bitter motion. "Kings prefer bankruptcy to payment. Always have."

Tobias found his voice somewhere near his boots. "And the equinox? You said the veil thins each equinox."

"Twice a year, the veil wears thin enough to work with. Autumn equinox is the stronger of the two—the year exhaling, letting go. On that night, at the Standing Stones, an oathbearer could speak the covenant again. Could reforge it." Odile took off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, which gave her face a strange nakedness. "The next autumn equinox is in thirty-one days."

The number landed in the room like a stone in a well. Thirty-one days. Tobias counted without meaning to—thirty-one days, with hunters in the city, with the widow already in chains, with his own name still missing from inside him like a tooth pulled from his jaw.

"There's a problem with your arithmetic," he said. "You're describing an oathbearer like she's a key everyone's been looking for. But the only person in this room who might be one is—" He stopped. Looked at Marguerite. Looked away. "No," he said. "Absolutely not. We came here to hide. To get her out of the city. That was the plan. Small plan. Good plan. Plans where you survive."

"Tobias." Marguerite's voice was quiet. "Show her the ledger."

He'd forgotten she carried it—the oldest ledger, pulled from the guildhouse the night they fled, wrapped in oilcloth against his chest ever since because Marguerite said her coat pockets were too shallow and he'd believed her because arguing took energy. Now she unwrapped it on the stone table, and Odile leaned over it, and her breath caught audibly.

"This is a covenant ledger," she said. "Pre-guild. Do you know how rare—" Her fingers hovered above the vellum without touching, the way people hover over relics. "Where did Corvane get this?"

"He keeps it locked. Kept. I stole it the night we ran." Marguerite turned pages until she reached the first entry, and Tobias, reading upside down, felt the hairs rise on his arms. The ink was brown with age, but the hand was strong, and the words were words he'd heard once before, in a dream he couldn't quite keep hold of:

*We will carry your past, if you will lend us our future.*

"The original oath," Odile whispered. "Written by the last oathbearer's own hand. Child, do you understand what you're carrying? This isn't a record. This is the *instrument*. The covenant can't be spoken from memory alone—it has to be read aloud from its own witness, at the Stones, by a gifted tongue." She looked up, and for the first time her sharpness cracked into something rawer. "Forty years I've charted doors, waiting for someone to walk through mine carrying this. Forty years. I'd begun to think the equinoxes would just keep passing, one after another, until there was nothing left on either side worth saving."

Outside, far off, the crier's hoarse voice had faded. The rain had stopped. In the green lamplight, the map of two worlds shimmered, and the silver feathers seemed almost to stir, like a great bird shifting in its sleep.

"So that's it, then," Tobias said, and hated how young his voice sounded. "Thirty-one days. The Standing Stones. And her—" he nodded at Marguerite, at the ledger, at the whole impossible shape of it—"standing in front of an army with a book."

"Not an army," Odile said, already pulling fresh vellum toward her, her pen moving, the cartographer waking in her hands. "Not yet. First, roads. If we're going to the Stones, we go by ways the crown doesn't know exist, and I am the only person alive who charts them." She glanced up, and there was something wild and bright in her face now, something that had clearly been waiting decades for permission to burn. "But understand this, both of you. Once you start down this road, there's no door back to the life you had. The covenant doesn't ask twice, and it doesn't accept partial payment."

Marguerite laid her hand flat on the old ledger, over the faded oath, and Tobias saw her steady herself the way she'd steadied herself on the rooftop—as if some wind only she could feel were trying to take her.

"I've spent my whole life handling other people's memories," she said. "Taking them in, keeping them safe, giving them back. Maybe that was never theft. Maybe it was practice."

Tobias wanted to argue. He was good at arguing; it was most of what he had. But looking at her—at the ink-stained fingers resting on the covenant like they belonged there, at the fear in her face that wasn't going to stop her—he understood, with a sinking certainty, that the small plan was dead. It had died the moment a wall had opened for her. Doors didn't open like that for people with small plans.

"Fine," he said. "The Stones. Thirty-one days." He pointed a finger at Odile. "But I want it on the record that I objected. Formally. In writing, if you've got a spare page."

Odile snorted. "Boy, everything in this shop is on the record. That's rather the trouble with it." She was already sketching—lines blooming under her pen, a road taking shape on the vellum, running out of the city, under the river, north through country Tobias had no name for. "Now. Before we go any further, there's something you should see. Something I've kept for forty years, waiting for the right eyes." She crossed to a cabinet at the back of the room, unlocked it with a key from around her neck, and drew out a flat packet wrapped in oilcloth, older and more brittle than the kind that had protected the ledger.

She set it on the table but did not unwrap it. Her hands, Tobias noticed, were not entirely steady.

"When the covenant broke," Odile said quietly, "the fleeing oathbearer passed through my family's house. He left something behind. Or perhaps he left it deliberately—I've wondered my whole life. Either way, it's the only piece of the original ceremony that exists outside the ledger itself." She looked at Marguerite. "And I think, child, that it may be meant for yours."

Her fingers found the knot of the oilcloth.

And from somewhere very far away—farther than the city, farther than the river, farther than any distance a map could measure—Tobias heard, faint and unmistakable, the sound of wings.
