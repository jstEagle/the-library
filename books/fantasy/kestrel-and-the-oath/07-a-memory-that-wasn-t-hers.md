## 7. A Memory That Wasn't Hers

The mapmaker's attic smelled of cedar shavings and old rain.

Odile had gone down to the harbor at dusk to bargain for lamp oil and bread that wasn't green with mold, leaving Marguerite alone with the boy who had sold his name. Tobias sat on a crate turned on its side, knees drawn up, watching her with the wary patience of a stray cat deciding whether the hand extended toward it held food or a snare. His fingers drummed against his shin in a rhythm he probably didn't know he kept—*tap, tap-tap, tap*—and Marguerite had begun, without meaning to, to think of it as his heartbeat made audible.

"You don't have to do this tonight," she said. "You've waited six years. You could wait one more."

"No." He said it too fast, then slowed himself with visible effort, the way he did everything—grabbing first, thinking second, apologizing never. "No. If I sleep on it I'll find a reason. I always find reasons. Reasons are how I ended up nameless in the first place."

Marguerite set the candle between them on the floorboards. Its flame stood perfectly still in the windless attic, throwing their shadows up the slanted wall where they bent and stretched like two figures in a fever dream. She had laid out her tools the way she'd done a thousand times in the guildhouse: the shallow copper dish of saltwater, the strip of clean linen, the small vial of tallow-and-ash salve that Odile had sworn by and that Marguerite suspected was mostly superstition. The oldest ledger lay closed beside her knee. She would not need it. But its weight near her hip had become a kind of courage.

"Tell me what you remember about the sale," she said. "Not the memory itself—you can't have that. Tell me about the *edges*. What was around it."

Tobias frowned. He was better at questions than he liked to admit; she'd noticed that over three days of shared bread and shared silence in Odile's attic. Street children learned to be observant or they learned to be dead, and Tobias had chosen observation with the same ferocity he chose everything.

"It was winter," he said slowly. "I remember it was winter because my hands were cracked and bleeding, and the broker said cold hands take cleaner impressions. Which is a lie. They say things like that so you'll stop asking questions." His drumming fingers stilled. "There was a room behind a chandler's shop. Smell of tallow. A man with soft white hands who asked me to think of my name while he—" He made an ambiguous gesture near his own temple, fingers curling like smoke. "It didn't hurt. That's the worst part. It should have hurt. He put his thumb here—" he touched the hollow beneath his ear, "—and it was like falling asleep in a warm room while someone reads aloud. And then I woke up on the street and there was a hole in me shaped like two syllables, and everyone I'd ever known looked through me like I was weather."

"What were the two syllables?"

"I don't know. That's rather the point."

"But you know it was two."

He blinked. "I—yes. Yes, I know that. How do I know that?"

"Because they took the sound but not the shape of it." Marguerite felt something turn over inside her, slow and cold and bright, like a fish glimpsed under ice. "A theft takes the whole cloth. But a name isn't cloth. It's a knot tied in you, and even when someone cuts the cord, the place where the knot was keeps its print. I can read the print. I can tie it again."

"And if you can't?"

"Then you're no worse off than you are now." She met his eyes. "I won't lie to you, Tobias. I've only ever taken memories before. Putting one back is—" She searched for the honest word and found it. "It's like walking backward through a house I've only ever walked forward through. I know the rooms. I don't know what's behind each door until I open it."

He was quiet a moment. Outside, gulls argued over the harbor, and somewhere below Odile's window a fishwife sang a song about drowned husbands that managed to be both mournful and cheerful, which seemed to Marguerite the most accurate song ever written about this city.

"The widow," Tobias said abruptly. "The one whose lullaby you gave back. Did you walk backward through her house?"

"Yes."

"And what was behind her doors?"

"Grief," Marguerite said. "And a garden she hadn't thought of in thirty years. And the sound of her daughter laughing before the fever took her." She wrapped the linen strip around two fingers of her right hand, the working hand, as Corvane had taught her—or as Corvane had *used* her, though the distinction had grown thinner every day since the Proclamation went up in ink and ash. "Memory isn't a single thing, whatever the ledgers say. It's a house with ten thousand doors, and every door opens onto every other door. When I give someone back one room, all the other rooms shift. Furniture moves. Light comes in windows that were painted shut. Sometimes that's mercy. Sometimes it's a flood."

"And you want to do this anyway." It wasn't quite a question. He was looking at her the way he'd looked at the lock on the guildhouse strongroom, back when she'd caught him picking it: assessing, hungry, already halfway committed.

"I want to give you back your name," she said. "Yes."

"Then do it before I'm clever enough to be frightened."

* * *

She had him lie back on the pallet Odile kept for clients who fainted, which Odile admitted was most of them. The candlelight made a small gold country around them, and beyond its borders the attic went dark and enormous, rafters lost in shadow like the ribs of some beached and ancient whale.

"Close your eyes," Marguerite said. "Think of the hole. Don't reach into it. Just hold your attention at its edge, the way you'd rest your tongue against the gap where a tooth was."

"That's a horrible comparison."

"It's an accurate one. Hold still."

She pressed her linen-wrapped fingertips to the hollow beneath his ear, and closed her eyes, and let herself fall.

Falling was the word Corvane had used, years ago, crouched beside a nine-year-old girl with a gift she didn't yet have words for. *You fall into them*, he'd said. *Like diving into deep water. Don't fight the current. Follow the silver.* And she had followed the silver ever since—the bright thread of a memory's substance, the glimmering filament along which recollection lived—and she had pulled threads out with a delicacy that had made her famous in certain unlit rooms of the city, and she had never once, not once in eleven years, tried to push one back.

Now she fell into Tobias, and the current took her.

His mind was nothing like the ledgers described. The Guild taught that memories were inventory—that a mind was a warehouse and a gifted thief a clerk with keys. But Tobias's interior was a city at night, lit sparse and stubborn, streets of it, alleys of it, and everywhere the same quality of careful motion: a boy who had learned to move quietly through places that might turn on him. She passed shuttered windows behind which she sensed hunger, cold, a dog he'd loved, a dog he'd lost. She passed a door that radiated warmth and heard, muffled through it, Odile's voice saying *you can stay until the rain stops* and knew she was brushing the memory of the last three days, the memory that contained herself, and she moved past it quickly, embarrassed, the way one looks away from a letter not addressed to you.

And then she found the gap.

It was exactly as he'd described: a hole shaped like two syllables. Not darkness—darkness would have been something. This was an absence with edges, a socket in the world, and around its rim the print of the knot remained, fine as engraving. She knelt at its edge in the silver dark of him and read the print the way she read ledger entries: patiently, line by line, letting the shape tell her what had been carved away.

Two syllables. Short first, open. Second one falling, like a stone dropped into a well. A name given by someone who—she traced the impression deeper, feeling the grooves of it—a mother? No. The hand that tied this knot had been rough, hurried, loving in the desperate way of people who love things they expect to lose. An aunt, perhaps. A grandmother. Someone who had held an infant in a cold room and spoken this name over him like a charm against the dark.

*I can rebuild it,* she thought. *I have the shape. I have the—*

But where would the substance come from?

That was the question she'd been circling for three days and refusing to look at directly. Taking was easy; the memory came out whole, self-contained, a pearl drawn from its shell. But restoring—she couldn't fabricate what had been stolen. She couldn't invent his name. The knot-shape told her the form but not the content, the vessel but not the wine. Unless—

Unless the wine was still somewhere.

She pressed closer to the absence, and the absence pressed back, and for one vertiginous moment Marguerite understood that she was not looking at an empty socket at all. She was looking at a doorway. The theft had cut the memory out of Tobias—but a thing removed must go somewhere, and the broker's soft white hands had carried it out of this city of his mind and down some road she couldn't see, and the road was still there, faint as a scar, running out from the wound like a root.

Every instinct she had as a thief said *don't follow the scar*. Thieves did not follow. Thieves extracted and withdrew; that was the discipline; that was how you kept your own mind your own.

Marguerite thought of the widow singing to an empty chair. She thought of Tobias saying *everyone I'd ever known looked through me like I was weather.*

She followed the scar.

It ran through the silver dark faster than she could properly travel, and she stopped trying to walk and simply poured herself along it, a ribbon of attention unspooling, and the dark around her changed texture—became colder, older, less like a mind and more like a night sky with the stars picked out. And far ahead, at the end of the scar, there was light.

Not the warm gold of candlelight. Something paler. Feathery.

She came to the end of the thread and found—not the stolen name, not at first—found a voice.

It was speaking. It had been speaking, she realized, for longer than she had been listening; it filled the starless space the way water fills a bell, and it spoke in a language she did not know and understood completely, the way one understands grief or birdsong, without translation, straight into the marrow:

*By feather and by fur, by root and by rain,
the Court of Kestrel and the Children of Clay—*

A covenant. The word arrived in her whole at once, huge and ancient, and with it a vertigo of scale: this voice was old, older than the guild, older than the crown, older than the city stacked in its crooked centuries below Odile's attic. It spoke an oath, and the oath was being *kept* somewhere, stored somewhere, tended like a flame in a lamp—and the voice was fraying as it spoke. She could hear the fray. Words dropped out of it like feathers molting, and the spaces where words had been were the same quality of absence as the hole in Tobias's mind, the same socketed nothing, and Marguerite understood with a lurch that she was hearing something dying by inches.

*—one shall stand between and speak the binding:
a keeper of the taken, who gives back what was given—*

The voice paused. In the pause, Marguerite felt it notice her.

There was no eyes-open shock, no drama. Just a vast, weary attention turning toward her like a lighthouse beam, and within it something that might have been hope, worn thin as altar cloth.

*A restorer,* the voice said, and now it was not chanting; it was addressing her directly, and the sound of it was wind moving through a thousand feathers, through a wing the size of the sky. *Oh, little clerk of small thefts. Do you know what you are?*

"I take memories," Marguerite said, and her voice sounded absurdly small, a mouse squeaking in a cathedral. "I'm a thief. That's all the guild ever—"

*Thief.* The word came back to her transformed, gently mocked, the way an adult repeats a child's fierce declaration. *Child. Look at your hands.*

She looked down. In the feathery light she saw them—her own hands, the linen strips, and around her fingertips the silver filament of the thread she'd followed, except now she saw what it truly was. Not a thread. A stitch. Her passage through Tobias's mind had left a seam of light behind it, closing as it went, knitting the scarred dark together. Where she had walked, the absence was filling. Not with anything she carried. With something the dark itself provided, rising to meet her touch like water rising to meet a lifted gate.

*We do not take,* the voice said. *We never took. The taking is the wound others make. You—* and here the great tired attention gathered itself, focused, burned, *—you are the needle. You are the sewing. You are the last of the line that ties the knot again.*

"I restore," Marguerite whispered. The words rearranged eleven years of her life as they left her mouth. Every extraction, every careful theft, every entry in every ledger—and underneath all of it, unnoticed, the seams she'd left behind, the minds she'd touched knitting themselves quieter, easier, the widows and debtors and grieving lovers who had come back to her afterward saying strange things like *I feel more myself than I did before you came.* She had thought it flattery. She had entered it in the ledgers as client satisfaction.

*The covenant requires an oathbearer,* the voice said, and now urgency threaded through the weariness, a bell tolling under the hymn. *One who gives back. One who stands between the Court and the Children of Clay and speaks the binding at the Stones before the veil seals. The last who held the office broke faith forty years ago, and we have been fading since, feather by feather, name by name. Find the Stones. Before the equinox. Before—*

The voice tore.

Not faded—tore, like cloth catching on a nail, and through the tear Marguerite saw a flash of something impossible: a court of pale towers built among living trees, wings numberless against a green-gold sky, and then the image guttered and the tear sealed itself and the voice was gone, and the feathery light collapsed inward like a fist closing.

And in the sudden dark, small and ordinary and unmistakable, she heard two syllables.

*Tobias.*

They rose up out of the absence like a bubble finding the surface, and she caught them—caught the sound and the shape and the weight of them, the rough loving hand that had tied the knot forty—no, sixteen years ago, an aunt's kitchen, tallow smell, a boy answering to his name for the first time—and she poured them into the socket and stitched the seam closed behind them, quick and sure, a needle pulling thread.

* * *

Tobias gasped like a man surfacing from deep water.

He sat bolt upright on the pallet, one hand flying to the hollow beneath his ear, and his eyes were wide and wet and fixed on the middle distance, seeing something that wasn't the attic.

"Tobias," Marguerite said, testing it, offering it.

He flinched. Then he made a sound she had never heard him make—not a joke, not a boast, not a street-smart deflection, but a raw broken half-syllable, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his mouth and shook. His shoulders shook. His whole narrow frame shook, and the candleflame stood witness, unmoving.

"My aunt," he got out, when he could. "Her name was Bess. She called me Toby. Nobody's called me—" He stopped. Started again. "I had a bed. I had a *bed*, with a blanket she'd patched with blue cloth, and I forgot I ever had a bed, I thought I was born on the street, I thought—" He laughed, wet and wild. "I thought I was nobody from nowhere. I was somebody's nephew. I was somebody's *nephew*, Marguerite."

"Yes," she said softly.

He wiped his face with both palms, briskly, the way boys do, erasing evidence. "What did you see in there?" he asked, and his eyes, red-rimmed, were sharp again. "You went somewhere. Your face went somewhere. You were gone a long time."

How could she possibly tell him? *I fell through the hole in your head and found the dying god of another realm, and it called me a needle, and it begged me to save it before the equinox.* She heard the sentence assembled in her own mind and watched it refuse to be spoken.

"I found your name," she said. "The rest was—deep water. Currents. Things that weren't mine to see."

He studied her for a long moment, and she braced for him to push, because Tobias pushed at everything, pried at every lock. But perhaps the name had softened something in him, or perhaps he recognized the look on her face, because he only nodded slowly and said, "When you're ready. Whatever it was. I'll listen."

Outside, the fishwife's song had ended. The harbor bells rang the hour—hollow, as they always did since the Proclamation, since the crown had ordered the memory-guilds' quarter silenced and the bells had never quite recovered their full voice. Nine strokes. Three days until the equinox.

Three days. The number landed in her like a stone dropped down a well, falling toward something she couldn't yet see the bottom of.

"Tobias," she said, and marveled at it, at the ordinary miracle of a name restored, at the boy sitting up straighter on the pallet as if the word itself had bones. "I need to find Odile. There's something I have to ask her about the Standing Stones."

He frowned. "The old ring on the moor? Why? Nobody goes out there. Even the sheep avoid it."

"I know," Marguerite said, reaching for the ledger at her hip and holding it like a compass. "I know why the sheep avoid it. And I think—I think I'm the reason they should start coming back."
