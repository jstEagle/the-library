## 8. Corvane's Offer

The guildhouse had never been quiet at this hour. For thirty years Aldous Corvane had fallen asleep to the sounds of it—the scratch of apprentices copying sigils in the scriptorium below, the soft clink of memory-vials being racked like wine bottles in the cellar, Marguerite's candle burning past midnight in the ledger room because that girl had never once trusted a number she hadn't counted twice. The building breathed around its people the way a ship breathes around its crew, all creak and settle and small human noise.

Now it was silent, and the silence had teeth.

Corvane stood at the window of his study and watched the smoke rise from the quarter where the widow's house had been. Not her house anymore—her house was ash by noon, and the woman herself was in an Inquisitor's cell, and somewhere between those two facts a lullaby had died. He had taught Marguerite the trade. He had not taught her mercy, because mercy could not be taught; it had to be caught, like a fever, from someone who already had it. She had caught hers from no one he knew of. It had simply grown in her, wild and inconvenient, the way ivy grows through mortar.

*You left evidence,* he thought at her, wherever she was hiding. *You always leave evidence. That is what kindness is.*

The fire in the grate had burned down to a red eye. On his desk lay the things he would need tonight: a writ of amnesty bearing the crown's seal—he had paid dearly for that seal, in coin and in worse—and beside it, wrapped in oilcloth, the oldest ledger. Her ledger. She had taken it when she fled, which meant she understood what it was, which meant she had read further into it than he had ever permitted anyone.

He poured himself a measure of plum brandy and did not drink it. He had not drunk anything in forty years that he could not set down instantly if the door opened. Old habits. Old fears. They were the same thing, in him.

Forty years ago tonight—or near enough; the equinoxes blurred together after a while, each one another year the veil had thinned and he had done nothing about it—he had stood among the Standing Stones with an oath in his mouth and a price in his hands, and he had refused to pay it.

That was the whole of it. That was the story he told himself on the bad nights, the plain version, stripped of ornament: *I refused.* Everything else—the guild, the ledgers, the thirty years of trading in other people's yesterdays—was scaffolding built around that one refusal, load-bearing and elaborate, designed to keep the truth from crushing him.

He had been twenty-three. He had been chosen, as oathbearers were always chosen—not for power but for *keeping*, for the particular stubbornness of a mind that holds a thing and will not let go. The Kestrel Court had shown him what the covenant required. A memory, freely given. Not any memory. The dearest one. The one you would fight death itself to keep. You walked to the center stone, you spoke the old words, and you opened your hands and let them take it, and in exchange the veil held, and the two realms breathed together, and the world stayed whole.

And Corvane—who had been brave in every way that didn't matter, who had crossed the frozen river alone at fourteen, who had stared down bailiffs and bullies and once a wolf—had stood before the stone with his mother's face in his mind, her voice singing in the kitchen of his childhood, the smell of bread and tallow, the single most precious room in all his memory—

—and he had closed his hands.

He had run. He had told himself, running, that there must be another way, that a covenant demanding so much was a covenant worth breaking, that the Court was cruel to ask it. By the time he reached the city his legs had stopped shaking and the lie had set like plaster, smooth and solid, and he had lived inside it ever since. *There is another way. I will find another way. I am finding it still.*

Thirty years of guild work had been the finding. Every stolen memory catalogued, every bottled oath racked in the cellar—he had told himself he was studying the architecture of remembering, learning how memories were made and unmade, searching for the loophole, the side door, the way to reforge the covenant without paying its price. And perhaps some part of him had believed that, once. But the larger part, the honest part he drowned nightly under ledgers and ledgers of other people's grief, knew the truth: he had built an empire of forgetting because he could not bear to be the only man in the realm who remembered what he'd refused.

And now the edict had come down, and Inquisitor Vale was pulling the city apart thread by thread, and the girl he had raised—the girl whose gift was not theft at all, he had known that since the night he'd watched her press a vial to a client's temple and seen the memory flow *backward*, restored instead of taken—was out there in the dark with the oldest ledger and no idea what she was carrying.

No. That was not true either. She had some idea. She was Marguerite. She always knew more than she said, and said less than she knew, and loved people in secret the way other people committed crimes.

A knock at the study door. Two knocks, then a pause, then one—the old signal, though the old signals were worthless now; half the household had scattered and the ones who remained were frightened enough to sell their own mothers' names for passage out of the city.

"Enter."

Brannick, the doorwarden, filled the doorway with his usual apologetic bulk. "Beg pardon, Guildmaster. The runner came back from the Tanners' Row safehouses. No sign of her. But—" he hesitated, and Corvane watched the man decide whether to say the next thing, and hated himself for knowing exactly what fear looked like on a subordinate's face because he had put it there, "—the Inquisitor's people were asking after her by name. Not just 'memory-gifted.' Her name."

"Of course they were." Corvane turned back to the window. "Vale doesn't waste writs on nobodies. Thank you, Brannick. Lock up. No one in or out until dawn."

The door closed. Corvane looked at his reflection in the dark glass—gray-bearded, hollow-eyed, a man wearing authority the way a scarecrow wears a coat—and thought: *She will hate me for what I'm about to do.*

Good, said the coward in him. Hate keeps people alive. Love gets them burned.

* * *

He found her where he knew he would find her, because he had raised her and some things a father knows even when he has no right to the word.

The rookery behind the Chandler Street chapel was a ruin of a place, all collapsed beams and pigeon filth, and Marguerite sat in the lee of the broken wall with the oldest ledger open across her knees, reading by the light of a candle stub she must have bought with her last coins. She had a blanket over her shoulders that had once, he noted with a strange pang, been one of the guild's—guild green, guild stitching, cut down and resewn. She had taken more than the ledger when she fled. She had taken pieces of home without meaning to.

He did not announce himself. There was no need. She went still over the page—a particular stillness, prey-stillness—and then said, without looking up:

"You walk loudly for a man who taught me to walk silently."

"I walk loudly," Corvane said, "because I want you to know I'm coming. If I wanted to surprise you, Marguerite, you'd never have heard me at all." He lowered himself onto an overturned crate nearby, joints cracking, and let the silence sit between them. Above them the pigeons muttered and shifted. Somewhere across the rooftops a bell rang the hour—hollow, cracked, the city's bells had sounded wrong for days, everyone said so, though no one could say why. He knew why. The veil was thinning faster than it should. Forty years of his cowardice, come due with interest.

"You shouldn't be here," she said. "If they're watching the guildhouse—"

"They're watching everything. Which is why I came myself." He studied her profile in the guttering light. Thinner. Shadows under her eyes. But the spine straight, the chin level. Frightened and refusing to be, which was the bravest condition a person could occupy. "The widow is alive. I want you to know that first. Vale hasn't executed her. She's keeping her. Extracting slowly. It's a message, and we both know who it's addressed to."

Marguerite's hand flattened against the ledger page. "Then I'll turn myself in."

"You will do no such thing."

"She's suffering because of me. Because I gave her back a lullaby. Four bars of a song, Corvane, and they burned her house for it."

"And if you give yourself up, she suffers anyway, and so does every soul whose name is written in that book." He leaned forward. "Look at me, girl."

She looked at him. Green eyes, her mother's coloring, no relation whatsoever—it had always amused him, the fiction that he'd found her rather than that she'd found him—but looking at her now he felt the full weight of the word he had no right to. *Daughter.* Not by blood. By thirty years of suppers and scoldings and lessons in penmanship and the thousand small surrenders of raising a child who was never yours.

"I did not come here to drag you back," he said. "I came to offer you a way out. A real one."

Her eyes narrowed. He watched her do the arithmetic—she was always doing arithmetic, weighing cost against cost—and he pressed on before she finished it.

"A writ of amnesty. Crown-sealed. Your name struck from the lists, your gift registered as... a licensed curiosity, nothing more. You keep your freedom. You keep your life." He drew the parchment from his coat and laid it on the rubble between them, the wax seal catching the candlelight like a drop of blood. "In exchange: the ledger. All of it. And your gift, surrendered—bound, sealed, whatever euphemism the crown prefers. No more restoring. No more taking. You live as an ordinary woman, and no one hunts you, and the widow lives, and this ends."

Silence. The pigeons resettled overhead. Marguerite looked at the writ for a long moment, and then at him, and something in her expression made his chest ache, because it wasn't suspicion. It was sorrow.

"That's not your offer," she said quietly. "That's theirs. What's yours?"

Ah. There it was. Thirty years of teaching her to read the fine print of human hearts, and she turned the skill on him without hesitation.

"The guild is finished," he said. "You know that. Whatever happens with the edict, the old trade is dead—there's no future in stolen memories, not with hunters on every corner. So yes, the crown wants the ledger destroyed and your gift leashed, and I have agreed to deliver both. But I agreed because the alternative is Vale, and Vale does not offer writs, Marguerite. Vale offers cells and slow extraction and a public example made of your bones." He heard his own voice sharpen and forced it flat again. "I am not asking you to trust the crown. I am asking you to trust that I have spent forty years learning exactly how much a person can lose, and I am telling you: do not lose everything for a principle. Principles don't hold your hand when they burn you."

"The ledger isn't a principle." She pulled it closer to her, arms wrapping it, and he saw that she was trembling—not with fear. With something hotter. "It's people. Three hundred years of people who trusted the guild with the worst nights of their lives. You want me to hand their grief to the men who are burning houses over four bars of a song."

"Their grief is *paper*, girl—"

"It was never paper to you." Her voice cracked and steadied. "I've read the early entries. Your hand, Corvane. Before you were guildmaster. You catalogued every memory like it was scripture. Like each one mattered. What happened to that man?"

The question went through him like a blade through wet canvas. He sat very still and let it pass through, because answering it honestly was the one price he had never learned to pay.

"That man," he said at last, "learned that mattering is how they get you. Mattering is the hook. You care about a thing, and then someone can threaten it, and then you are owned." He rose, joints protesting, and looked down at her—at the blanket stitched from guild cloth, at the ledger held like a child, at the candle stub burning itself out for her sake. "Take the writ. Burn the ledger yourself, if it helps—I'll tell them you did. Live, Marguerite. Grow old somewhere green and quiet and forget any of this ever touched you. That is the only mercy left to offer, and I am offering it while I still can."

"And if I refuse?"

He had hoped she wouldn't ask. He had known she would.

"Then I can't protect you," he said, "from what comes next. And what comes next has already been sent."

Her head came up sharply. "What does that mean? Corvane. *What does that mean?*"

But he was already turning away, because a man could pay any price except the one that required him to watch her face while he answered. Behind him the candle guttered, flared, held. He climbed down through the rookery's broken stairwell into the dark, each step loud on purpose, giving her every chance to call him back.

She did not call him back. She was her father's daughter in that way too, whoever her father had been.

At the bottom of the stair, in the cold and the pigeon-smell and the distant ringing of the cracked bell, Aldous Corvane finally drank the brandy he had carried in his pocket all evening, and it tasted like forty years ago, and like bread and tallow, and like nothing at all.

By dawn, three of Seraphine Vale's hunters had picked up a trail at the Chandler Street chapel: a girl, a ledger, and a second set of footprints leading away from her, toward the river road.

They followed the first set.
