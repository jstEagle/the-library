## 10. The Road Under the River

The river froze in the third week of Frostmoon, and Odile said that was when it was safest to walk beneath it.

Tobias had laughed at her when she first said it — a short, disbelieving bark that came out of him before he could stop it, the way laughter always did around Odile, because she said impossible things in the flat voice other people used for bread prices. She hadn't laughed with him. She'd simply looked up from her maps with those pale, river-colored eyes and waited until his laugh died of embarrassment.

"There's an old road," she said. "Older than the city. Older than the crown. It runs under the Greywash from the Tanner's Stair to the willow bank at Corrow's Ford. The kings paved over it when they built the bridge, and then they forgot it, which is the way of kings. The water remembers, though. Water remembers everything." She tapped the map with one ink-stained finger. "And in deep winter, when the ice is thick enough to hold a cart, you can walk under it as dry as walking through a cellar."

"Under the *ice*," Tobias said.

"Under the river," Odile corrected, "which is not the same thing."

He'd spent four days deciding whether he believed her, which was three days longer than he'd needed to decide whether he trusted Marguerite, and about two days less than he'd needed for Wren, who was half-faded and glowed faintly in the dark like a lamp left burning in an empty house, and who had once caught a falling icicle without looking at it. Trust, Tobias had learned, was not one decision but a hundred small ones, made daily, mostly against your own better judgment.

On the fifth day they went down through the Tanner's Stair, past the tannery sheds where the hides hung like flayed ghosts, down stairs cut into the living rock and slick with old frost, and Tobias stopped believing and started knowing, because there it was: a road.

It ran beneath the frozen river like a vein of amber under skin. The ceiling overhead was the underside of the ice itself — thick, green-gold, shot through with trapped bubbles like frozen breath — and the light that came down through it was strange and underwater-colored, so that everything wore a faint drowned shimmer. The road surface was old paving stones, fitted so precisely that no weed grew between them, and along both edges ran low walls carved with things Tobias couldn't look at too long: birds with human hands, rivers with faces, a long procession of small figures holding something between them, hand to hand to hand, all the way into the dark.

"The covenant road," Wren said quietly. The knight's voice echoed strangely, as if the ice above were repeating it in a whisper. "The Kestrels helped build this, before the Severance. It was meant to be a meeting place. Neutral ground, where either realm could come to the other safely." A pause, and then, softer: "No one has walked it in forty years."

"Then we'll be the first," Marguerite said.

That was the thing about her, Tobias thought, watching her stand at the head of that drowned-lit road with the oldest ledger strapped across her back like a shield. She didn't say brave things. She just said true ones, plainly, as if courage were simply another kind of bookkeeping — a debt to be paid, an entry to be made. He'd known her barely a season and already he could read her ledgers better than anyone alive. He knew what her silences cost. He knew that when she touched the widow's lullaby-bottle she'd kept hidden in her coat, she did it the way other people touched relics.

He knew, though he would have bitten off his own tongue before saying it, that he watched her more than was strictly necessary for survival.

* * *

The road under the river was cold in a way Tobias had never been cold before.

It wasn't the biting cold of the streets in winter, which came at you like a knife and could be dodged or endured. This cold rose out of the stones themselves and seeped upward through boot leather and wool and flesh, patient as arithmetic. It was the cold of deep places, of things that had been still for a very long time. Nettie, seven years old and stubborn as a burr, rode on Odile's back wrapped in every blanket they owned, and even so her teeth chattered in little bursts she tried to hide, because Nettie had decided somewhere along the road that complaining was for babies and she was done being a baby forever, having seen a guildhouse burn.

"We should sing," Odile suggested, after an hour. "To keep the blood moving."

"If you sing, I'm leaving you here," Tobias said.

"I know eleven songs about rivers."

"That's ten too many."

But Marguerite laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, bright as a struck match in all that green-gold gloom — and Tobias felt it land somewhere under his ribs and stay there, warm, like a coal carried in a cupped hand. He catalogued the sound carefully, the way he catalogued everything about her now. Guarded Marguerite. Ledger-keeper Marguerite, whose face closed like a door whenever anyone asked about her past. That laugh was a room in her house he hadn't known existed, and he wanted, with a ferocity that frightened him a little, to be let inside.

They walked. The road bent with the river's bend. Once, far behind them, something boomed — a vast, muffled concussion, like a giant knocking on a door made of glass — and everyone stopped, and the ice ceiling shivered, and a fine snow of frost sifted down through the colored light.

"Just the ice settling," Odile said. "Or the river turning over in its sleep. Or—" she considered, "—something else. Fifty-fifty."

"You're a comfort," Tobias muttered.

"I'm a mapmaker. Comfort isn't on my charts."

Wren walked at the rear, sword drawn, and did not speak again for hours. The knight's glow dimmed and brightened with some tide Tobias couldn't see, and once, passing a stretch of wall where the carved procession figures had been deliberately defaced — chiseled smooth by human hands, long ago — Wren stopped and laid one fading palm against the stone and stood there so long that Marguerite went back and stood beside them, silent, until Wren was ready to move.

Tobias carried the second pack, the heavy one, with the food and the fire-oil and Odile's rolled maps. The oldest ledger rode against Marguerite's spine. He'd offered twice to carry it. She'd refused twice, politely, the way you refuse someone offering to carry your heart.

* * *

It happened at Corrow's Bend, where the river made its great slow curve and the road dipped, following the bedrock down, and the ice overhead thinned from cart-thick to window-thick in the space of twenty paces.

Odile saw it first. She always saw terrain first; it was the one sense she had sharper than anyone Tobias had ever met. "Stop," she said, and her voice had changed — flat, quick, professional. "The ice is thin ahead. We go single file, soft steps, no talking. The road rises again past the bend."

They were halfway through the thin stretch when the cracking started.

It began as a sound like someone tearing cloth, very slowly, very far away. Then another tear, closer. Tobias looked up and saw the cracks spreading through the green-gold ceiling like black lightning, branching, reaching, and understood with the sudden total clarity of disaster that the river above them was not frozen all the way through — that there was moving water up there, dark and patient, held back by a hand's width of winter.

"Run," Odile said. "Don't run— *walk fast*, don't—"

The ice gave.

Not all of it. One pane, maybe six feet across, directly over the center of their line, directly over the place where Marguerite stood. It dropped away whole, a great lid lifting, and the river came down through the hole like night falling — black, roaring, impossibly loud after all those hours of silence — and took her.

Tobias did not remember deciding to move. Later, he would piece it together from evidence: the pack straps torn off his shoulders (he found them afterward, still buckled), the ledger-bottle in his inner pocket cracked from the impact of his own dive, the state of his knuckles. What remained in his memory was only sensation: cold like a fist closing around his whole body, darkness, the roar filling his ears, and Marguerite — a pale shape tumbling in the black water, the ledger's strap tangled around her arm, dragging her down, down, because the oldest ledger was bound in oiled vellum and lead-edged covers and it sank like sin.

He got a fist into her collar. The current fought him for her. It occurred to him, distantly, absurdly, that the river wanted the ledger — that water remembers everything, Odile had said, and perhaps it had come to collect. He got his other arm around her waist and kicked for the grey ghost-light of the hole above, and the cold was in his bones now, in his teeth, in the space behind his eyes, and his legs were slowing, and the surface seemed very far away and getting farther—

Hands. Hands seizing his collar, his hair, his wrist. Wren's glow cutting through the murk like a lantern through fog, and Wren's grip — a grip that had caught a falling icicle without looking — hauling them both upward through the black.

Then air. Air like knives, air like mercy. They dragged him out onto the stones and he lay there coughing up the river while someone — Odile, brisk and terrified — stripped his coat and wrapped him in blankets, and Marguerite knelt beside him with the oldest ledger clutched to her chest, soaked, shaking, her lips going blue.

"You *idiot,*" she said.

Her voice broke on the word. That was how he knew. Not from the words themselves — people called him idiot all the time, often correctly — but from the crack running through them, the same fracture he'd heard in her laugh on the road, the sound of a door coming open whether she meant it to or not.

"You could have drowned," she said. "For a book."

"Not for a book," Tobias said, through chattering teeth. His whole body was shaking so hard the words came out in pieces. "You're welcome."

She stared at him. And Tobias, half-dead on the covenant road with river water freezing in his hair, watched something happen in her face that he suspected she would deny under torture: a settling, a surrender, a door swinging wide. She pressed her forehead briefly, hard, against his wet one — the touch of someone checking that a thing is real — and then pulled back and became brisk and bossy and ordered everyone around, and never said anything else about it at all.

But he knew. You spend your life reading marks and seals and forged signatures, you learn to recognize the genuine article. She loved him. She didn't know it yet, maybe, or wouldn't say it, but it was written on her plain as a royal seal, and Tobias Kestrel-smuggler-street-thief lay in the blankets and grinned like a fool at the green-gold ceiling of the world.

* * *

They made camp that night in a side chamber off the main road — a hollow in the rock, Odile said, where covenant-keepers had once rested, its walls carved with more of the processional figures, its ceiling high enough for smoke. Wren stood the first watch at the entrance, a fading silhouette against the drowned light, and Odile slept curled around Nettie like a comma around a period, and Tobias sat as close to the fire as the fire allowed and steamed gently, drying, aching, alive.

Marguerite brought him tea. Made it herself, strong enough to trot a horse across, and handed it over without meeting his eyes.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't do it again."

"Dive into rivers?"

"Save me." She said it lightly. It wasn't light. "Next time, save yourself. I'm not worth drowning for."

There it was — the ledger-keeper's arithmetic, the old wound talking, the girl raised by Corvane to believe she was a tool that happened to have a heartbeat. Tobias blew on his tea and considered several replies and discarded them all, and finally said, "Too late. Already decided you're worth it. I don't renegotiate terms."

Something flickered across her face — fear, hope, exasperation, all three wearing the same mask. She sat down beside him, close, their shoulders almost touching, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the fire popped, and above them, through however many feet of stone and frozen river, the winter stars would have been wheeling, unseen.

"Tell me something true," she said at last. Quietly. "About you. Anything. I've told you—" she gestured vaguely, taking in the whole ruined landscape of her history, the guild, Corvane, the widow, the flight across rooftops, "—more than I've ever told anyone. And I realize I don't know your favorite color. Or your mother's name. Or why a boy sells his name in the first place."

Tobias looked into the fire. The honest answer was that he'd sold his name because names could be traced, and a traceable thief was a caught thief, and he'd been nine years old and hungry and clever enough to think himself invincible. But that wasn't the true answer. The true answer was underneath.

"My mother sang," he said. "When I was very small. Before she died. She had a song she sang only to me — not a lullaby exactly, more a naming-song. It had my name woven all through it, my real one, the one I sold. When I gave the memory up, I lost the song too. I've spent eleven years trying to remember a tune I can't hear anymore." He shrugged, aiming for carelessness, missing by a mile. "So. That's why. Not strategy. Sentiment. Worst trade I ever made, and I once traded a coat for a hat in a hailstorm."

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the way it was careful when she handled the fragile bottles.

"When we restore it — your memory — I'll hear it. The song. For a moment, I'll be inside it." Her hands tightened around her own cup. "I want you to know that I understand what I'm carrying. It's not just information. It's your mother's voice. I'll hold it like it's made of glass."

And there it was again, Tobias thought — the thing that had made him offer her sanctuary in a doorway half a lifetime ago, back when they were strangers negotiating trust in the dark. Everyone else in the world who dealt in memories treated them as goods. Coin. Leverage. Weapons. Only Marguerite treated them as what they were: the people we love, kept in glass.

"Green," he said.

She blinked. "What?"

"My favorite color. Green-gold. Like—" he nodded upward, at the stone, at the river sleeping over their heads, "—like this. Apparently I've had a favorite color for years and only just found out tonight."

She laughed — the match-strike laugh, the rare one — and leaned, very slightly, so that her shoulder rested against his. Just barely. Just enough to feel. Neither of them acknowledged it. Both of them stayed exactly where they were until the fire burned low.

Across the chamber, Odile opened one eye, observed them with the expression of a cartographer confirming a landmark, and closed it again.

* * *

Near dawn, Tobias woke to voices — low, urgent, at the chamber's mouth. Wren stood rigid, and beyond the knight, out on the main road, the drowned light had changed. It pulsed now, faintly, rhythmically, like a heartbeat in the ice.

"What is it?" Marguerite whispered, coming to stand beside him, her shoulder brushing his again, unhidden this time.

Wren turned, and the knight's faded face wore an expression Tobias had never seen on it: awe braided with dread.

"The veil," Wren said. "I can feel it from here. It's thinning faster than it should — faster than any equinox in the records. Something on the other side is pressing against it. Or something on *this* side is pulling." The glowing eyes fixed on Marguerite, and then, reluctantly, on the oldest ledger strapped to her back. "We have less time than Odile's maps promised. Days, not weeks. And we are not the only ones traveling toward the Stones."

"How do you know?" Tobias asked.

"Because," Wren said, "the road remembers. Look."

And on the ancient paving stones outside the chamber, faint as breath on glass, new footprints were appearing in the frost — dozens of them, marching in perfect formation, heading the same direction they were headed. Behind them, printed deeper, deliberate, came the mark of a single pair of boots that Tobias recognized from posters nailed to every wall in the city.

Seraphine Vale was already on the road under the river.
