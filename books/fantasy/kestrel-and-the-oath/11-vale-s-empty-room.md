## 11. Vale's Empty Room

The Inquisitor's house had no ghosts in it, because Seraphine Vale had never allowed anything to die there.

It stood on Candlewick Row among the townhouses of minor magistrates and retired colonels, three stories of grey stone with black shutters, indistinguishable from its neighbours except for one detail: no smoke ever rose from its chimneys. Fires were a comfort, and comfort was a door left unlocked. She kept the hearths swept and cold and did her thinking wrapped in a wool cloak, her breath ghosting silver in the parlour, because a mind that shivered stayed sharp.

The house was empty the way a reliquary is empty—stripped, deliberate, every surface polished to a shine that reflected nothing back. No portraits. No letters in the writing desk. The bookshelves held law codes and anatomies and nothing that could be called beloved. When she had taken possession of it seven years ago, the previous tenant's wife had wept over a vase of dried lavender she'd been forced to leave behind, and Seraphine had stood in the doorway watching the petals scatter across the cobbles and thought: *that* is what I am paid to prevent. Attachment. The soft rot of holding on.

She had believed it then. She was beginning to have trouble believing it now, and the trouble had a shape, and the shape sat in a locked cabinet upstairs, and it hummed to her at night like a struck tuning fork buried under floorboards.

But first—the day's work. There was always the day's work.

* * *

The widow's name was Anselma Croft, and she had sung.

That was the whole of the crime, when you stripped the legal language away. A woman of sixty-one years, widowed twice over, arthritic hands that had wrung other people's linens for forty years, had stood at her window on the night of her second husband's funeral and sung a lullaby she had no right to remember—a lullaby that appeared in no registry, belonged to no licensed memory, and had therefore been *given* to her by someone outside the law. Someone who traded in the stolen property of the crown.

Seraphine had read the transcript four times. The arresting officers reported that when they came through the door, the widow had not run, had not denied anything. She had simply looked up from her mending and said, "Oh. I wondered how long the song would keep me safe." As if the lullaby were a candle burning down. As if she'd known all along that light attracts exactly what it attracts.

Now the widow sat in the interrogation room beneath the Assize Hall, across a table scarred by thirty years of confessions, and Seraphine studied her the way a jeweller studies a stone before cutting.

"You sang at your window," Seraphine said. "The fourteenth of Frostmonth."

"I did." Anselma Croft's voice was dry as old paper, unafraid in a way that irritated Seraphine more than screaming would have. "My husband liked it. He'd forgotten it, you see. They all forget. That's what dying does, or grief does—I've never worked out which. And then one morning I woke up and there it was again, whole, like bread rising. So I sang it for him even though he was already in the ground. It seemed rude not to."

"Where did you obtain it?"

"Obtain." The widow smiled with too few teeth. "You make it sound like fish."

"The memory is registered property of persons unknown. Its presence in your mind constitutes theft."

"Its presence in my mind constitutes *my mother's voice*, girl." For a moment the dryness cracked and something older showed through, something with roots. "She sang it to me before I could walk. Then the fever took it out of me when I was six—the physicians said the sickness burned memories out like it burned the hearing out of my left ear. Sixty years I couldn't remember her face or her voice or one single thing about her except that I loved her, and loving without remembering is like eating without tasting. Then someone gave it back. Whoever they are, whatever the law says they are, they handed me my mother across sixty years of dark, and you want me to call that theft." She folded her cracked hands. "Call it what you like. I'll call it grace."

Seraphine wrote none of this down. She had stopped writing things down years ago, because paper could be seized, and because some part of her had learned—without ever deciding to learn it—that the widow's words would stay where they landed regardless.

"Give me the name of your benefactor," she said quietly, "and I will recommend leniency. A fine. Perhaps nothing at all."

"I don't know the name."

"Describe them."

"I never saw them." And here the widow hesitated, and the hesitation was a door swinging open an inch, and Seraphine stepped through it the way she stepped through everything, without knocking.

"But someone saw *them*. You know this because you've thought about it obsessively since the arrest. You lie awake composing testimony. Tell me what you've composed."

Anselma Croft went very still. "How do you do that?"

"Do what?"

"Walk into my head like it's a room with the door off its hinges." The widow's eyes, milky with age, fixed on her with terrible clarity. "You didn't ask. You just took. I felt it go past like a cold draft." Her voice dropped. "What did they give *you*, Inquisitor, that you'd spend it doing this?"

Seraphine rose, straightened her coat, and ended the interview. Protocol required a formal extraction of the relevant memories within the week—the lullaby itself, catalogued and shelved, the widow released with a warning and a hole where her mother's voice had been. She signed the order with a hand that did not shake, because her hand never shook, and walked home through streets going blue with dusk, and told herself the cold feeling in her chest was the weather.

She knew better. Everything in her was weather now. Everything in her was weather that never cleared.

* * *

Here is what the city did not understand about the Inquisitor's gift, and what Seraphine understood all too well: taking is not the same as having, and having is not the same as being rid of.

The guild-thieves bottled what they lifted. They decanted a memory into glass and sold it, and the seller walked away lighter, and the buyer wore the memory like a borrowed coat and returned it when the wearing palled. Clean commerce. Abominable, according to the edict, but clean.

Seraphine had no bottles. What she took, she kept—not by choice, but by architecture. Her gift built no vessels. Every memory she extracted settled into her the way silt settles into a riverbed, layer upon layer, year upon year, until the river ran shallow over a drowned country. Twenty-two years of service. Four hundred and eleven formal extractions, each logged in the crown registries, each still *in here*, filed nowhere, going nowhere, hers forever whether she wanted them or not.

She did not want them. God knew she did not want them.

They sat in her like stones in a sack carried up a mountain that never ended. A smuggler's first kiss, taken as evidence in her third year—he had wept when it left him, and now she kissed a woman whose name she'd never learned, tasting salt that wasn't hers, every time it surfaced, which was often, because first kisses are stubborn things. A child's terror of the river flood, pulled from a boy who'd watched his sister drown; she carried his drowning too, had carried it so long she sometimes woke gasping with riverwater in her throat that wasn't there. A baker's pride in his risen loaves. A traitor's love for a country not his own. A dying man's last sight of his daughter's face, which Seraphine had extracted as evidence in a murder case and which visited her nightly for a decade, patient as a creditor, until she had learned to greet it by name—*good evening, Elenna*—because fighting it was worse than hosting it.

Four hundred and eleven rooms in her, furnished with other people's lives. She kept her own house bare because there was no room left in her that was only hers. That was the truth of the empty house on Candlewick Row, though she had never once said it aloud: she had made her dwelling match her interior. A husk outside for a husk inside. The thieves at least sold their takings onward. Seraphine was a warehouse that no cart ever came for.

And in the deepest room of the warehouse—one she had never opened on purpose, because it had no lock to open, only a weight she felt when she stood very still in the dark—there was a memory that did not belong to any of her cases.

* * *

It came for her that night, as it came perhaps once a season, always uninvited, always the same.

She had banked no fire because there was no fire to bank. She sat in the cold parlour with a case ledger open and unread, and outside the wind came down off the hills carrying the first smell of the thaw, and somewhere in the city a bell rang the hour—hollow, the way bells sounded in late winter, as if the bronze had caught the season's emptiness—and the memory rose in her like a fish rising toward a hole in the ice.

Snow. She was small. Snow falling through the branches of a hawthorn, and she was standing—no, hiding—behind a standing stone taller than her father's house, and her breath came out in white flags, and she was afraid, but it was the good kind of afraid, the kind you feel in a church.

There were voices. Many voices, layered, human and otherwise. The otherwise ones she had no words for, then or now—they sounded like wind moving through feathers, like a flock turning all at once above a field, and they were speaking with the humans in a rhythm that was not quite language but that her small body understood as *promise*. People stood in a ring around the stones, holding candles that burned without flickering in the wind, and their faces were lit gold from below, and they were saying words together, words that sank into the frozen ground like seeds—

*We remember, and are remembered. We hold, and are held. What we give freely will not be taken—*

And then the feather-voices answered, and the answer passed through her chest like a bell struck against her spine, and every hair on her arms lifted, and she knew—even at five years old, even before she knew the word—that she was witnessing something the world would spend the rest of her lifetime trying to forget.

Then: running. Her father's hand hard around her wrist, too tight. His face white as the snow. *Don't look back. Don't ever say you saw.* And the last thing, over her shoulder despite everything: the ring of candles guttering all at once, as if snuffed by a single breath, and the great stones standing alone in the dark, and one feather-shape wheeling up above them, alone, alone, alone, into a sky suddenly vast and vacant as a stripped bed.

The memory closed. It always closed there. It never gave her more.

Seraphine sat in the cold parlour with her heart going hard and slow, the way it did after this happened, and performed the ritual she had performed for twenty years: she reached for the case files in her mind, the four hundred and eleven, looking for the drawer this belonged in.

It fit in none of them.

Every stolen memory she carried bore the fingerprints of its taking—she remembered the room, the subject, the warrant, the moment of extraction, the specific flavour of loss. This one had no such fingerprints. It had simply always been there, the way a birthmark is there, older than her service, older than her training. Which meant it was not taken at all.

Which meant it was *hers*.

She was perhaps six years old in it. The Standing Stones. The covenant—she knew the word now; she had spent three nights in the restricted archive reading the fragments the crown permitted to exist, cross-referencing, and the word the scholars used for what she had witnessed was *covenant*, and the court it bound was called Kestrel, and the last recorded reforging was dated forty-one years ago and marked with a single word in the margin of the oldest folio: *failed*.

Forty-one years. She was thirty-six.

She had been a child at the last attempt. Her father had been there—her father, the magistrate, the rigid, frightened man who had forbidden her gift from the moment it first showed, who had beaten her at nine years old for telling a neighbour what he'd dreamed, who had delivered her to the Inquisition himself at seventeen with the words *"it's better she be the knife than the wound"*—her father had stood in that ring of candles, and had fled it, and had spent the rest of his life making sure his daughter never learned why.

Seraphine pressed her palms flat against the cold table and breathed, and the breath shook, which it never did, which frightened her more than the memory did.

Because here was the arithmetic she could no longer avoid, laid out plain as a merchant's sums: the covenant had failed. The veil between the human realm and the Kestrel Court had been thinning for forty years—she had read the reports herself, dismissed them herself, the crop failures along the old borders, the birds vanishing from the western skies, the children born in those districts who dreamed in feathers. She had signed orders calling such things superstition. She had hunted the guild that traded in memory while the whole architecture of memory and oath and promise rotted underneath the realm like a foundation giving way.

And she had known. Not with her mind—with the room in her that had no lock. Some part of her had been standing behind that stone for thirty years, waiting for the rest of her to catch up.

The widow's question came back to her across the cold: *what did they give you, Inquisitor, that you'd spend it doing this?*

Nothing. They had given her nothing. That was the horror of it. She had been given nothing and had taken everything, four hundred and eleven lives stacked in her like cordwood, and the one memory that was truly hers—the one warm thing, the one golden-lit thing, the sound of promises made in feather-voices—she had spent twenty years treating as contamination. Evidence of weakness. Something to be suppressed, filed, survived.

She got up. She lit no lamp. She climbed the stairs to the locked cabinet in the study, and stood before it a long moment in the dark, listening to the humming she had pretended for months not to hear.

Inside, under seal, lay the evidence from the widow's arrest: a scrap of sheet music recovered from the windowsill, the lullaby transcribed by a court musician. Routine evidence. Due to be catalogued and destroyed with the extraction.

Seraphine broke her own seal, which was a crime, and unfolded the scrap, and read the notes without playing them, and heard them anyway—heard them in a voice sixty years dead singing to a child in a kitchen—and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that mercy had passed through that window on the fourteenth of Frostmonth. Someone had broken a dozen laws to hand an old woman her mother's voice. Someone with a gift like hers, turned inside out. Not a knife. The opposite of a knife.

The guild registers listed one auditor of unusual talent. One keeper of ledgers whose entries never balanced, whose mercies never appeared in the books at all. Marguerite Ravensworth. The name had crossed her desk a hundred times and she had let it pass, because Corvane's reports described a functionary, a clerk, a nobody.

Corvane lied. She had read enough extracted confessions to know the texture of a lie when it arrived wearing an honest face, and the guildmaster's reports had that texture, had had it for years, and she had ignored it because ignoring things was how she survived her own head.

No longer.

Seraphine folded the lullaby along its original creases, returned it to the cabinet—but did not relock it; she left the key sitting in the lock like a door left open an inch—and went to the window. Below, the city lay under the last snows, rooftops silvered, chimney-smoke standing straight up in the still air. Somewhere out there was a woman who restored instead of took. Somewhere out there were the Standing Stones, and a failed oath, and a feathered shape wheeling alone in a vacant sky.

Somewhere out there was the answer to the only question that had ever mattered, the one her father had beaten out of her and the crown had trained out of her and she had locked in the deepest room of herself for thirty years:

*What was promised? And who broke it?*

In the morning, she would request the Ravensworth file. In the morning, she would begin the hunt properly, thoroughly, with all the terrifying competence the crown expected of her.

But tonight, standing alone in her empty room with four hundred and eleven strangers' lives pressing at her walls and one small golden memory humming in the dark like a struck fork, Inquisitor Seraphine Vale admitted the thing she had never said aloud, the thing that would unmake her career and remake everything else:

She was not hunting the thief.

She was following her.
