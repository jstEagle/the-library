## 12. The Guildhouse Burns

We came back to the city the way smoke leaves a room—slowly, and against every instinct.

Odile's maps were the reason. She had said it plainly on the road beneath the river, her voice flat as a drawn line: *Without my charts of the veil-thinnings, you'll walk into the Standing Stones blind, and blind oathbearers die there.* The originals had been taken from her years ago, seized in her exile, and she knew exactly where they had gone. Not to a vault. Not to a private collector.

To Corvane. To the guildhouse. Filed, no doubt, under *Confiscated — Unsalable*, because Odile's charts showed passages between realms, and nothing frightened buyers quite so much as proof that other realms existed at all.

So we went back. Tobias grumbled the whole way through the drainage culvert that let us inside the walls without passing the gate-wardens' new scrutiny; Wren moved beside him like a shadow learning to be a person again, half-faded feathers rippling along one arm whenever the torchlight caught it wrong. Nettie was not with us. Nettie was safe with Odile, three miles out, mending nets and asking questions no one wanted to answer.

I told myself that mattered. I told myself a great many things mattered, standing in the culvert's dark with river water seeping through my boots, listening for the sound of the city I had grown up in.

The first thing wrong was the bells.

Ashfall had always had bells—the Vellum Guild paid for two of them, generations back, so that the hours would ring honest across the merchant quarter. But tonight they rang hollow, cracked-sounding, and then they did not ring at all. Silence where the ninth hour should have been. A city holding its breath.

"The quarter's dark," Tobias whispered, easing the culvert grate aside. "Look."

I looked. The merchant quarter should have been a spill of lamplight, late traders, the smell of frying onions and tallow from the supper stalls. Instead the streets lay empty, shuttered, patrolled by pairs of soldiers carrying lanterns hooded with tin shutters—light let down in slivers only, like eyes narrowed against something.

"They're not hunting us tonight," Wren murmured. His voice had a strange doubled quality when he spoke quietly, as though wind were repeating him a half-beat behind. "They're guarding something. Or waiting for something to finish."

We went up. Rooftops had saved me once before, the night I fled the guildhouse with the oldest ledger strapped against my heart, and I trusted them more than streets now. We crossed the tiles above Cooper's Row, above the silent chandlers, above the shuttered premises of every licensed memory-broker who had sworn the new loyalty oaths and hung the crown's white ribbons from their doors like bandages over wounds.

And then we crested the roofline of Tanner's Stair, and saw the guildhouse burning.

* * *

Fire is honest in a way people are not. It does not pretend. It does not file things under categories or weigh their market value. It simply takes what is flammable and makes it light.

The Vellum Guild burned from the top down. The archive floor—the long gallery where I had spent eleven years of my life cataloguing bottled oaths and stolen first kisses and the last words of fathers to sons—was an open wound of orange, its leaded windows blowing outward in gouts of glass and ember. Smoke stood over the whole quarter like a black tree, taller than any bell tower, bending eastward on the night wind. Sparks rode that wind in slow spirals, settling on rooftops three streets away, and everywhere the hooded lanterns of the soldiers turned toward it, watching, *letting* it burn.

"No," I heard myself say. It came out very small. "No, he wouldn't—he wouldn't burn the ledgers. He'd sooner cut off his own—"

"Corvane would burn his own hand if it stopped shaking," Tobias said grimly. "Question is whose orders those are." He nodded at the soldiers. Crown grey, crown badges. They weren't fighting the fire. There wasn't even a bucket line.

Wren had gone very still. In the ember-light his faded side looked almost solid, which somehow made the rest of him look less real. "The fire started at the archive," he said. "Not the kitchens, not the lamp stores. Someone opened the ledgers to the flame deliberately. Page by page, I'd guess. Slowly." His bird-bright eyes found mine. "That is not destruction, Marguerite. That is a *ritual*. Or a confession."

I didn't answer. I was already moving, down the fire-escape stair of the chandler's shop, across an alley hot as an open oven mouth, into the crowd of neighbors who had come out into the street despite the curfew—drawn the way mourners are drawn to graves, forbidden and helpless. An old cooper stood with his cap in his hands. A woman held a child too young to understand why everyone was crying. Nobody shouted. Nobody threw water. The guild had never been loved by this quarter—we took memories, after all, even kindly—but it had been *known*, and there is a grief in losing a known thing that has nothing to do with love.

A soldier tried to push the crowd back and the crowd simply absorbed him, staring, until he stopped pushing.

I got within thirty feet of the door. Heat rolled off the stone facade in waves, and through the great arched windows I could see the interior of my life collapsing: shelves going up like festival candles, the brass catalogue drawers sagging, and—God help me—the memory-vials themselves beginning to burst.

You have never heard anything like a vial of stored memory breaking in fire. It does not shatter like glass. It *releases*. Each one went off with a soft whump of displaced air, and whatever it held spilled out into the heat—a laugh, a name, a wedding morning, a lullaby—and for one impossible moment the smoke itself seemed to shape around these things, ghosts made of other people's yesterdays rising off the flames and dissolving. A woman's voice called someone's childhood nickname into the night. A dog barked, joyful, decades dead. Somewhere in the roar I heard rain on a roof I had never stood under, and I pressed my hands over my ears and it did not help at all.

"They're all still in there," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "Every memory the guild ever held that wasn't sold. Forty years of them. He's burning forty years of—"

"Marguerite." Tobias caught my arm. "Marguerite, look at the door."

The guildhouse door—black oak, iron-banded, older than Corvane himself—had been nailed shut. Fresh nails, bright heads glinting in the firelight. And pinned to it at eye level, already browning at the edges from the heat, hung a proclamation in the crown's formal hand:

*By order of the Inquisitor Seraphine Vale, acting under the Proclamation of Severance: the premises of the so-called Vellum Guild are condemned. Its records are heresy. Its keepers are traitors. Let the faithful witness the purging.*

Below it, in smaller script, almost an afterthought:

*The master of the house has given full testimony and is granted the crown's protection.*

Tobias read it twice. Then he looked at me, and I will be grateful until I die that he did not say *I told you so*, because I could not have borne it. He just said, quietly, "Corvane sold everything. The maps, the client lists, the vault locations. And when the crown was done taking, they burned the rest—and gave him a ribbon for it."

"He raised me," I said. The words felt absurd, childish, true. "He taught me my letters. He taught me the ledger-hand. He—" My voice cracked clean through. "He used to leave honey-cakes on my desk when I worked past midnight. He never once said they were from him. I just *knew*."

"I know," Tobias said. And then, because he was Tobias, and because grief makes him practical the way it makes other people stupid: "Is anyone else in there?"

The question went through me like cold water.

The apprentices. God in heaven, the apprentices. Corvane kept six children in the guildhouse—foundlings mostly, cheap hands for the vial-work, taught to hold a memory-glass steady before they could reach the high shelves. When I fled, they had been asleep in the attic dormitory. That was nine days ago. Nine days, and I had not thought of them once, because fear is a greedy thing and eats all the room in your head.

"The attic," I said. "Tobias, the dormitory's directly above the archive gallery—"

"The stairs are gone." Wren was already scanning the facade, his gaze moving with a soldier's arithmetic. "The rear service stair might hold. The front is a furnace. But—" his feathered arm lifted, pointing, "—the old ivy wall. The drain-spout corner. If the roof hasn't fallen there yet, a climber could reach the dormitory window from the chandler's roofline. It's how an apprentice would sneak out to see the fair, yes? Every guildhouse has such a route. The children will know it better than we do."

It was suicide dressed in arithmetic, and all three of us knew it, and none of us hesitated, and that is the moment—I mark it now, looking back—that they stopped being people I traveled with and became something else entirely. Something with a name I didn't have yet.

* * *

The chandler's roof was soft with heat. The tiles gave slightly underfoot, like skin over fever, and the air tasted of copper and burnt sugar—the guild's stock of candied ginger, some absurd corner of my mind noted, the storeroom must have caught. Tobias went first across the gap to the drain-spout corner, testing each foothold, swearing softly and creatively. Wren followed, and where he passed, strangely, the heat seemed to relent, as though the fire recognized something older than itself in him and drew back like a dog remembering a former master.

I came last, because I was the lightest and because someone had to carry what we found.

The dormitory window was open six inches. Of course it was. Children.

Inside, the attic was a nightmare lit orange through the floorboards. Heat pressed up from below like a living thing. The little beds stood in their neat row, coverlets thrown back, and the room was empty—

—except for one small shape curled in the corner behind the wardrobe, knees to chest, arms over her head.

Nettie.

She was eight years old, the youngest of the six, all elbows and soot and stubbornness, the one who used to follow me through the galleries asking why the ledgers smelled like lavender (preservative ash, I'd tell her, and she'd say *no, it's because the memories miss their gardens*) . She was supposed to be with Odile. She was supposed to be three miles away, safe, mending nets. Instead she was here, in a burning building, alone.

Her face came up out of her arms, streaked black with tear-tracks through soot, and when she saw me she made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life—not a word, not a scream, just a small animal noise of a child who had decided no one was coming and been proven wrong.

"Nettie—sweetheart—how—"

"Marta got us out." Her voice was a wreck, scraped raw by smoke. "Marta and Fen and the others, down the ivy, they went to the temple, but I went back, I went back for—" she held up a small fist, and opened it, and in her palm, blackened but whole, sat a single memory-vial, "—for yours. The lullaby one. The widow's. You gave it back free and Master Corvane said mercy was theft and he put it in the punishment case and I thought—if the guild burns, nobody gets it back ever, so—"

She had gone back into a burning building for a stolen lullaby. For a mercy of mine she had witnessed once and carried like a coal in her chest ever since.

I gathered her up and she weighed nothing and everything. Through the window, Tobias was braced on the spout-corner, reaching; Wren's wings—half-real, half-hope, spread wider than his body had any right to allow—broke the worst of the heat as we crossed. Behind us the floorboards of the dormitory began to glow at their seams like the seams of the world itself coming undone.

We dropped to the chandler's roof. We slid down to the alley. We ran.

Behind us, with a sound like a giant sighing, the guildhouse roof fell in, and forty years of stolen and saved and sold and smuggled human yesterday rose up in one last vast plume of sparks, and scattered east on the wind, over the sleeping city, over the river, toward the hills where the Standing Stones waited—every spark a memory finding, at last, the only freedom the guild had never managed to give them.

* * *

We did not stop until the tannery ponds, where the stink hid us and the water reflected the fire like a second, inverted burning. Nettie drank from Tobias's cupped hands, coughed, drank again. Wren stood watch, feathers flat, eyes on the road. And I sat on the bank with a child in my lap and rage in my mouth like hot coins.

"He knew they were in there." My voice came out low and level, which frightened me more than screaming would have. "Six children, Tobias. He signed testimony knowing Vale would seal the doors. He probably *told* her about the dormitory. 'Full testimony.' Full betrayal, more like—"

"You don't know that."

"Don't I?" I laughed, and it came out broken. "He burned the ledgers rather than let the crown read them—or rather than let *me* read them, maybe, maybe there are pages in that archive that say what he really did forty years ago, maybe that's the confession Wren smelled in the fire. Either way he chose the flame over the truth, and he chose it with children sleeping upstairs, and I sat in Odile's kitchen eating bread while it happened, because I needed *maps*."

"Hey." Tobias crouched in front of me, soot-striped, singed, alive. "Listen to me. You didn't know. And if you'd known, you'd have walked into that fire with your bare hands and died useful and dead, and then who reforges the covenant, hm? Who unbreaks the sky?" He tapped my sternum, gently, once. "The guilt can ride along. It always does. But it doesn't get to drive."

From the darkness by the ponds, Nettie spoke.

"Are you really her?" she asked Wren. "A knight of the Kestrel Court?"

"I am," Wren said.

"And the Kestrel Court—is it really real? Under the world? With the feather roads and the lantern orchards?"

"It is real, and fading, and it will die within a season unless the oath is remade." Wren knelt so his strange, doubled shadow fell across her. "Why do you ask, small one?"

Nettie looked down at the scorched vial in her fist. Then up at me. Then at the fire, still staining the clouds red over the distant city.

"Because I don't have anywhere to go now," she said, in the matter-of-fact voice children use for the largest facts of their lives. "The guild was where I went. So." She squared her small shoulders. "I'm coming with you. To the Stones. I can carry things. I'm good at carrying things. And I know how to be quiet, and I know how to count, and Marguerite says I ask good questions."

I opened my mouth to say something sensible—*it's dangerous, you'll be safer with Odile, the road ahead is no place for a child*—and found that every sensible word had burned with the guildhouse.

What came out instead was: "Yes. You come with us. You carry things. You ask your questions." I pulled her close against my shoulder, soot and all, and felt her go boneless with relief, eight years old and orphaned twice over in one night, trusting me absolutely, and I thought: *this is what Corvane never understood. This is the thing he broke forty years ago and has been paying interest on ever since.*

Family is not what keeps you. Family is what you refuse to leave behind in a burning building.

Tobias watched us, and something moved across his face—tenderness and terror sharing the same expression—and I pretended not to see it, because I was not ready, because the price of the Stones was a secret I was still hiding behind my ribs like contraband.

But that is another ledger entry. That cost comes due later.

For now there was only this: a fire dying behind us, a child asleep against my heartbeat, a scorched vial holding a widow's lullaby warm in Nettie's fist, and three miles of dark road between us and Odile's door.

Wren lifted his head suddenly, nostrils flaring, feathers rising along his arms like hackles.

"Riders," he said softly. "On the north road. Crown grey. Moving fast." His eyes found mine, and in them was the reflection of the burning city, and something colder underneath it. "They've found the temple where Marta and the others ran. And they are asking, by name, for the girl who keeps the oldest ledger."
