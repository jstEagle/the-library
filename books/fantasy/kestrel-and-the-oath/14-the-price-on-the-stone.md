## 14. The Price on the Stone

Wren had not stood among the Standing Stones in forty years, and the stones remembered.

He felt it as he crested the last hill—the old recognition moving through him like a hand through water, the monoliths humming at a frequency only the oathbound could hear. Once it had been a welcome. Now it was an accusation. Nine stones in a broken ring, each taller than a cathedral spire, their surfaces veined with silver light that pulsed slow as a sleeping heart. Snow lay thick upon them, and where it touched the veins it did not melt but *glowed*, so that the whole circle seemed to breathe frost and fire at once.

Behind him, his companions climbed the frozen road with the stumbling gait of the exhausted. Odile leaned on her staff, her maps wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a swaddled child. Nettie rode Tobias's shoulders, half-asleep, her small fists knotted in his hair. And Marguerite—

Wren watched her come up the hill last, and felt the old dread settle into his bones like snowmelt into stone.

She had stopped at the ring's edge, her breath steaming, her dark eyes lifted to the monoliths with an expression Wren knew too well. He had seen it once before, on another face, on another night forty years gone: the look of someone standing before a door they already knew they must walk through.

*Corvane wore that same face,* he thought. *And Corvane walked away.*

"Is it true," Marguerite said quietly, "that they sing?"

"When the veil is whole, they sang." Wren stepped forward, his boots crunching through the crust. "Now they only hum. A song with its heart cut out." He laid his palm against the nearest stone. The silver vein flared beneath his touch, then dimmed, sulking. Even the stones knew what he was now—half a knight of a court that no longer fully existed, his edges frayed like cloth worn thin. Some mornings he woke and could not remember the color of the Kestrel Court's sky. Some evenings his reflection came late to the glass, a heartbeat behind, as though it had to be summoned from somewhere very far away.

"They know me," he said. "That's more than I can say for myself some days."

Marguerite came to stand beside him. Close enough that he could see the exhaustion carved beneath her eyes, the chapped split in her lip from the cold road. She looked so *small* against the monolith. So human. That was the cruelty of it—that the fate of two realms should rest on a woman made of nothing sturdier than ink-stained fingers and stubborn mercy.

"The others are making camp," she said. "Odile found a hollow beneath the eastern stone, out of the wind. Tobias is arguing with Nettie about who carries the kindling." A ghost of a smile touched her mouth. "Nettie is winning."

"They usually do."

"Wren." Her voice dropped, and there it was—the question underneath all her other questions, the one she had been carrying since the river road, since the ambush, since the night he first told her what she was. "Tell me again. All of it. What the reforging asks. Don't soften it this time. I want every tooth of it."

Wren looked up at the stones. The silver light pulsed. Somewhere far above, beyond the grey ceiling of cloud, the veil waited—a membrane thinner than memory, stretched between the human realm and the Kestrel Court, torn forty years ago by a man's cowardice and fraying further with every season. At the equinox, three days hence, it would tear past mending forever. Unless.

"Come," he said. "Walk the ring with me. It's easier said standing inside it."

* * *

They walked the circle slowly, snow squeaking beneath their boots, and Wren told it as it had been told to him when he was young and newly sworn, kneeling on these same stones while the court's oldest singers draped him in feathers that weighed more than iron.

"The covenant is not a spell," he said. "It is a promise with a body. When the world was younger, your realm and mine were not separate—they were one country sung in two keys. But mortals multiplied and forgot, and the fading folk withdrew behind the veil rather than be trampled or worshipped. The covenant held the door between us—not shut, but *latched*. Passage for passage. Gift for gift. Each realm kept what it was, and neither starved the other."

"And the oathbearers kept the latch oiled," Marguerite murmured.

"Yes. One in each generation, born with the deep gift—memory given and returned whole. Not theft. Restoration. They walked between, carried messages, mended breaches. And because the covenant is alive, it demanded a price of its keepers. Everything living demands a price. That is how you know it's alive."

They reached the northern stone, the tallest, its silver veins running dense as frost ferns. Wren stopped and turned to face her, and made himself say the rest without flinching, though every word tasted of ash.

"To reforge what was broken, the covenant requires an oathbearer to speak the old words at the equinox, willingly, with full knowledge of what they surrender. The words will call the veil back together. But a latch needs a hand, and a hand needs flesh, and flesh—" He exhaled. "The price is memory. Not just any memory. The most treasured ones. The ones that hold the most love. The covenant takes the dearest thing you carry, because only something truly dear has the weight to hold a door closed against eternity."

Marguerite said nothing. The wind moved her hair against her cheek and she did not brush it away.

"It cannot be tricked," Wren went on, softer. "It cannot be substituted. We tried, in the early years after the breaking—oathbearers offered bitter memories, painful ones, memories they were glad to lose. The stones refused them. The covenant knows the difference between a sacrifice and a transaction. It will take only what you love most, and it will know if you offer less, and it will simply... wait. Until you are honest, or until the veil fails, whichever comes first."

"And if it fails?"

"You have seen the fading. You have seen what happened to my court—our colors going grey at the edges, our children born lighter each generation, our borders shrinking like ice in spring. Your realm fared better, but you've felt it too. The long winters. The forgetting that spreads through the lowlands like rot—whole villages losing the names of their dead, mothers unable to recall their children's faces. The veil doesn't just separate us, Marguerite. It *bleeds*. And when it goes entirely, both realms go with it."

Still she said nothing. But Wren saw her hands curl at her sides, saw the small motion of her throat as she swallowed, and thought—with a tenderness that surprised him—that she was braver than any knight he had ever served beside. Braver because she was asking the right questions instead of running from them.

"There is one more thing," he said. "And I would spare you it if I could, but you deserve the whole truth. The price is not only paid. It is *witnessed*. The covenant does not take silently. When the moment comes, everything you surrender will pass through you one final time—all of it, every treasured hour, burning bright as it goes—and those who stand within the ring will see it. See *you*, in those moments. Your loves laid bare before strangers and stones alike. There is no privacy in a covenant. Only truth."

The wind gusted, sharp with ice. Above them the clouds tore briefly apart, and for a single breath Wren saw the sky beyond—not the grey of the mortal realm nor the amber of his own, but a seam of impossible color where the two bled together, thin as a scar. Then the clouds closed, and the vision was gone.

"Three days," Marguerite said at last. Her voice was steady. Too steady, the way a bridge is steady right up until it isn't. "Then the equinox."

"At moonrise."

She nodded slowly. Then she turned to him, and smiled—a real smile, warm and terrible, and Wren felt dread move through him like cold water finding every crack.

"Thank you," she said. "For not softening it."

And she walked away toward the campfire's glow, her spine straight, her steps even, leaving Wren alone in the ring of humming stones with the terrible arithmetic turning over in his mind.

* * *

He did not sleep. None of his kind slept well so close to the veil; the thinning tugged at them like a tide. Instead Wren sat apart from the camp, on a fallen slab of granite, and watched the firelight play across the faces of the family Marguerite had gathered around herself in a handful of desperate weeks.

Tobias sat sharpening his knives with the restless diligence of a boy who had learned that idle hands invited disaster. Every few strokes he glanced up—at Marguerite, always at Marguerite—as though confirming she still existed. Odile had spread one of her rescued maps across her knees despite the cold, tracing routes with a cracked fingernail, muttering about passages that might no longer exist. Nettie slept against Odile's side, her thumb in her mouth, her other hand curled around a scrap of feather Wren had given her weeks ago—one of his own, shed without pain, which the girl treated like a holy relic.

*A found family,* Wren thought. *Built in flight, in fear, in the narrow spaces between disasters.*

And then he saw it.

He saw Marguerite watching them. All of them. The way her gaze moved from Tobias's bent head to Odile's maps to the sleeping child, lingering on each face with an attention so fierce it was almost physical—like a woman memorizing a room she knew she was about to leave. He saw her lips move faintly, counting them perhaps, or naming them. He saw her hand rise halfway toward Tobias and then fall, unfinished.

And Wren understood.

The dread that settled over him then was not the sharp dread of battle but the slow, glacial dread of certainty. He had spent forty years telling himself that Corvane's failure was inexplicable—that no soul could stand before the price of the covenant and choose the world's slow death over one man's grief. Now, watching Marguerite watch her people, he understood it completely. That was the horror of it. Cowardice was not a mystery. It was the most human thing in the world, wearing devotion's face.

Because Marguerite Ravensworth had been alone her entire life. He had read it in her ledger-hand, in the careful distance she kept even from kindness. And in the span of one hunted season she had finally, *finally* built the thing she had never had—a family, chosen and fierce and hers. And the covenant required precisely that. The dearest memories. The ones that hold the most love.

Hers were all of *them*.

Every treasured hour she possessed had been made in these last months—in Tobias's crooked grin, in Odile's dry laughter, in Nettie's small weight falling asleep against her shoulder. She had no childhood sweetheart to surrender, no mother's lullaby, no golden summer to give the stones. The covenant would reach into her and find only *them*, glowing like embers, and take every one.

She knew. He could see that she knew. And she was sitting there by the fire letting Tobias drape a blanket over her shoulders, letting Nettie burrow against her in her sleep, letting Odile pour her tea—hoarding the final hours of her treasures the way a miser hoards gold she intends to burn.

*Say something,* Wren told himself. *Warn her. Tell her there may be another way—*

But there was no other way. He had searched forty years for one. And if he spoke aloud what he suspected, she would only deny it, and the others would spend their last days trying to dissuade her, and the veil would fail while they argued over the arithmetic of her heart.

So Wren said nothing. He sat in the dark with the stones humming their broken song, and he hated himself a little, and he loved her a great deal, and he understood at last why the old songs called the oathbearers *the willing wounds*.

Later—much later, when the fire had burned to orange coals and the others slept in a huddle against the cold—Marguerite rose quietly and came to sit beside him on the granite slab. For a long time they watched the stars wheel above the tear in the sky.

"Tobias asked me today what I'll do afterward," she said. "When it's done. Where I'll go." Her voice was light, conversational, and utterly false. "I didn't know what to tell him."

Wren kept his eyes on the sky. "What did you want to tell him?"

"That I'd open a shop. That I'd learn to bake bread badly. That I'd be there." A pause. The coals crackled. "Wren. If a person gave up everything dear to them—if the covenant took it all—would anything be left? Would they still be... a person who could begin again?"

He heard the question beneath the question, and it broke his heart with its precision. She wasn't asking permission. She was asking whether the sacrifice would *work*—whether the realms would truly mend, whether her emptiness would buy anything real. Whether it would be worth it.

"The oldest texts say the oathbearer forgets," Wren said carefully, "but is not unmade. What the covenant takes, it takes wholly—but it leaves the vessel. And vessels can be filled again. New memories. New..." He hesitated. "*New family.*"

"Chosen," Marguerite whispered, "not remembered."

"If they're willing to choose you twice."

She laughed then, softly, wetly, and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and did not explain the tears, and he did not ask.

"Go sleep, Marguerite," he said gently. "You have three days. Spend them well."

She rose. Paused. Looked down at him with an expression he would carry to whatever end awaited his fading kind—an expression of gratitude and apology braided so tightly together they could not be separated.

"I intend to," she said.

And she went back to the fire, and lay down among her sleeping family, and drew Nettie close, and closed her eyes as though she could hold the moment inside her forever—which, Wren thought, his heart a stone in his chest, was exactly what she intended to do.

Above the ring, through the tearing clouds, the seam of impossible color widened by a fraction. Two days, now, until moonrise. And somewhere down in the dark of the world, on roads only the guilty traveled, Corvane was coming with crown troops at his back—and Seraphine Vale, with her hoard of stolen stones, was riding toward the Stones with something waking in her that even she could not yet name.
