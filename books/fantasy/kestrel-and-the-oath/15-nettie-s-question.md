## 15. Nettie's Question

The camp below the Standing Stones had grown into something like a village in the three days since we arrived—a village of six souls and one fading knight, huddled in the lee of a granite shoulder while the wind came down off the moor carrying the smell of frost and old stone.

I had claimed the task of mending, because mending was the only work my hands could do without trembling. Nettie's coat had a tear at the elbow where she'd caught it scrambling up the fallen king-stone, and I sat now with the coat across my knees, needle flashing, thread pulled from the emergency kit Odile had packed with the foresight of a woman who had been fleeing things for thirty years. The wool smelled of woodsmoke and of Nettie herself—chalk dust and scorched linen, the smell of a child who had lived her whole short life above a burning archive.

She sat beside me on the flat stone, legs swinging, watching the needle with the fierce attention she gave to everything. She was nine years old and had already survived more than most soldiers, and she bore it the way children bear everything: as though survival were simply what happened between breakfast and supper.

"Your stitches are crooked," she said.

"They're strong."

"Crooked *and* strong." She considered this, apparently finding it acceptable. "Like Wren."

I glanced up. Wren stood at the edge of the camp where the ground began to rise toward the Stones, half in shadow, half in the last copper light of the afternoon. Even now, even knowing him, my eye did strange things when it found him—the light seemed to pass through his edges, as though he were a drawing done in ink that was slowly drying out of the world. He had taken to standing watch at dusk, every dusk, facing the monoliths as if he expected them to answer him.

"Like Wren," I agreed quietly.

Nettie was quiet for a moment. In Nettie, quiet was never empty; it was a held breath before a question. I had learned to dread her questions the way sailors dread a change in wind—not because they were cruel, but because they always came from a direction you hadn't braced for.

"Marge," she said—she had shortened my name within an hour of meeting me, and no one, not even Tobias, had been permitted to correct her—"when we do the thing at the Stones."

"The reforging," I said. "Yes."

"When you pay the price." She picked at a loose thread near the coat's cuff, not looking at me. "Wren told Tobias. Tobias thinks I don't listen, but I listen to *everything*."

"I know you do."

"You have to give up your most treasured memories. That's the price. Willingly given or it doesn't count." She recited it like a lesson, like the multiplication tables Corvane used to make us chant in the guildhouse schoolroom. Then she looked up at me, and her eyes were too old, the way they always were when she forgot to be a child. "Does forgetting someone mean they stop mattering?"

The needle stopped in my hand.

The wind moved through the gorse behind us with a sound like whispering, like a thousand small voices saying nothing at all. Somewhere beyond the ridge, Tobias's voice rose and fell as he argued amiably with Odile about the placement of the warding chalk—I could hear the smile in it even from here, that easy warmth he carried like a lantern. And I sat with a child's question in my lap heavier than the coat, heavier than the ledger in my pack, heavier than anything I had carried in twenty-six years of careful, invisible living.

Because here is what I knew, and what I could not say:

Tomorrow night was the equinox eve. Tomorrow night the veil would tear, and I would stand among the Stones and speak the covenant, and I would pay its price. And my most treasured memories—every single one that rose unbidden when I searched myself, the way Wren had taught me to search, palms open, asking *what would I refuse to lose*—were not of my childhood, which had been gray and orderly. Not of Corvane, who had raised me and betrayed me and whose face I had already begun to stop grieving. They were of them. Of Tobias laughing in the dark under the frozen river, water streaming off his jaw, still grinning because I was safe. Of Odile pressing a cup of chicory into my hands our first morning on the road and saying nothing, which from Odile was a speech. Of Nettie asleep against my shoulder in the cart, her thumb in her mouth, her other fist closed around a scrap of vellum she'd rescued from the fire.

If I gave those up, they would walk out of my head and leave no footprints. I would look at Tobias tomorrow-after and see a stranger with kind eyes. I would look at this child and feel nothing but the generic tenderness one feels for any child, which is a real feeling but not *this* feeling, not this fierce specific ache that had a name and a smell and a weight.

Would they stop mattering?

No. God, no—they would matter more than ever, because the covenant would hold because of them, because of what their memory in me had purchased. But mattering is not the same as being mattered. And I would not know the difference, because I would not remember there had ever been a difference.

That was the honest answer. And I could not give it to her.

"Nettie," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me, "mattering isn't something that lives in memories. It lives in—" I gestured vaguely, uselessly, at the world. "In what's true. You matter whether anyone remembers you or not. The lullaby I gave Mrs. Halloway back—she'd forgotten it for eleven years, and her daughter wasn't any less loved during those eleven years. She was just less *known*. Do you see? Forgetting isn't erasing. It's—" I hunted for it. "It's a room with the door shut. Everything's still inside."

Nettie frowned at the gorse. "But if the door's shut forever, and nobody opens it, does it count that everything's still inside?"

"That's—" I laughed, a small broken sound. "That's a philosopher's question, and philosophers get paid better than I ever did."

"I'm serious."

"I know you are." I set down the needle and turned to face her fully, because she deserved that much, at least. "Here's what I can tell you honestly. What matters stays mattered. It's written into things deeper than remembering. The Stones have stood for two thousand years and no one remembers who raised them, and they're still holding up the sky. Does that answer—"

"But *you'll* forget *us*." Her voice cracked on it, went thin and high, and suddenly she was not a philosopher at all, just a nine-year-old with ash still under her fingernails from the night her whole world burned. "You won't know my name. You won't know I'm yours. You'll look at me like—" Her chin wobbled. "Like the lady at the orphanage door looked at me. Like I was somebody's problem."

"Oh, Nettie." I reached for her and she let me, folding into my side with the desperate gracelessness of a child trying to be brave about it, her face pressed into my shoulder, her fists knotted in my sleeve. The coat slid to the ground between us, forgotten, its crooked strong stitches half-finished. "Listen to me. Listen. Whatever happens tomorrow—whatever I remember or don't—you will never be somebody's problem again. Not to me. Whatever's left of me after tomorrow, whatever shape it takes, it will take one look at you and choose you. I promise you that. Memories are just the record. The choosing is the thing itself, and the choosing doesn't live in my head. It lives—" I pressed my hand flat over her heart, then, hesitating, over my own. "It lives here. In both places. And doors can be reopened, little bird. Doors can be rebuilt. Even if I forget the house, someone can walk me back to it and say *this is where you lived, this is who you loved*, and I will believe them, because some part of me will recognize the truth of it the way stone recognizes rain."

She sniffled against my shoulder. "That's a very fancy way of not answering."

I laughed despite everything, despite the hot pressure behind my own eyes. "Yes. It is."

"Do you want to know the truth?" she said into my sleeve. "I think you're scared."

"I am absolutely terrified."

"Good." She pulled back and wiped her nose with the back of her hand, a gesture Odile had tried for weeks to civilize and failed. "Tobias says being scared and doing it anyway is the whole definition of brave. He says it's the *only* definition. Everything else is just stories people tell afterward."

"Tobias," I said, "is occasionally wise."

"He's mostly annoying."

"Also true."

She picked the coat back up and handed it to me, businesslike again, the storm passed through her the way children's storms pass—completely, leaving the air washed clean. "Finish my sleeve," she commanded. "And Marge? When you forget me—" She paused, and I saw her choose her words with the care of someone handling something breakable. "When you forget me, I'll remember enough for both of us. That's how it works, right? That's how it worked with Mama. Papa forgot her when the fever took him, but I remembered, so she stayed."

My throat closed. I bent back over the stitching so she wouldn't see my face fail. "That's exactly how it works," I managed.

It was a lie. It was the worst lie I had ever told, and I had spent my life telling merciful lies in ledgers full of stolen things. Because Nettie's remembering would keep *her* memory of me alive, yes—but I would wake in a world where a child loved me and I could not love her back, not properly, not with the whole of me, and no amount of her remembering could carry my half of the bridge. Someone would have to hold it alone. She had already held too much alone.

I finished the sleeve. My stitches were crooked. They were strong.

* * *

Supper was barley and salt pork and the last of Odile's dried apples, eaten around the fire with the particular ceremony Odile insisted upon, as though a shared meal were a warding in itself. Maybe it was. We had all lost our tables; we had built this one out of habit and defiance.

Tobias told a story about the winter he was eleven and had lived three months inside the bell tower of Saint Vessa's, stealing candles from the votive rack and selling them back to the same mourners at a markup. He did the voices—the fat verger, the suspicious widow, the priest with the foghorn cough—and Nettie shrieked with laughter until she hiccupped, and Odile smiled her rare crooked smile into her cup, and Wren watched from the fire's edge with an expression I was beginning to learn how to read: hunger and grief braided together, a man memorizing a family he had never had, because soon he would return to a Court where such things were legend.

I laughed with them. I laughed and I watched each of their faces the way a drowning woman watches the shore, cataloguing: the gap in Tobias's teeth, the ink stain on Odile's left ring finger that never washed out, the cowlick at Nettie's crown that stuck up like a feather no matter what we did to it. *Remember this,* I told myself, and then hated myself for it, because hoarding them felt like the guild's sin all over again—taking without consent, keeping without permission. But I couldn't stop. If these were the last hours I would know them, then by every god named and unnamed, I would *know* them.

Later, when Nettie had gone down into sleep in the tent with Odile, and Wren had taken the watch, and the fire had burned down to a red heart breathing under its ash, Tobias came and sat beside me on my bedroll with the wordless ease he had, the ease of someone who had learned that presence asks less than questions.

"You're doing the thing," he said.

"What thing?"

"The face. The ledger face. Where you're writing everything down behind your eyes." He poked the fire with a stick, sending up a constellation of sparks. "You've been doing it for two days."

"I don't have a ledger face."

"Marge. I've watched you audit accounts for a living. You have a *very* distinctive ledger face."

I almost told him then. The words climbed my throat—*the price is you, all of you, I am going to stand at those Stones tomorrow and trade every memory I have of your laugh and your terrible voices and the way you say my name like it's a joke we share*—and they climbed, and they crested, and I swallowed them back down like a stone.

Because if I told him, he would try to stop me. Or worse—he wouldn't try to stop me, he would simply go quiet in that way of his, and carry it, and spend his last day with me performing cheerfulness through a crack in his chest. I had seen him carry things before. He carried his sold name for seven years without once letting it make him bitter. He deserved better than to spend his final hours as my co-conspirator in grief.

"It's nothing," I said. "Just nerves. Tomorrow's a big day."

"Mm." He looked at the fire. The light moved on his face, gold and shadow, gold and shadow. "Funny thing about nerves," he said lightly, too lightly. "They make people practice goodbyes. I've seen it. On the streets, when someone's decided to run, or decided to die—they start looking at everything like it's the last time. Memorizing." He turned to me, and his eyes were gentle and terribly awake. "You've been memorizing us for two days, Marge."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the moor, a nightjar called its churring note, once, twice.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

And it was a bad lie. It was the second-worst lie I had ever told, and he heard it land badly—I saw it in the small shift of his mouth, the way his gaze held mine a beat too long, weighing, not pushing. He was a thief; he knew what it looked like when someone hid something valuable. He didn't call it out. He just nodded slowly, and reached over, and tucked a loose strand of hair behind my ear with a touch so careful it hurt worse than any accusation.

"All right," he said softly. "Whatever it is—you don't have to carry it alone. That's the whole point of a family, in case nobody told you. The weight gets divided. That's the arithmetic."

"There's no arithmetic that divides this one."

"Then we'll invent it." He lay back on his bedroll beside mine, hands laced behind his head, and looked up at the stars beginning to burn through the cold. "Get some sleep, oathbearer. Tomorrow you've got a sky to hold up."

I lay down beside him and did not sleep. I listened to his breathing slow toward dreams, and I stared at the stars, and I practiced, silently, over and over, the opening words of the covenant that Wren had taught me—*kestrel and kestrel, wing to wing*—until they wore smooth as river stones in my mouth.

Beside me, in his sleep, Tobias murmured something I couldn't catch. His hand found mine in the dark and held on.

And far off across the moor, past the black line of the ridge, a light was moving. Small and steady and wrong—no shepherd carried a lantern in a line that straight, that purposeful, toward the Stones on equinox eve.

I sat up. The light did not waver.

It was coming for us, and dawn was still hours away.
