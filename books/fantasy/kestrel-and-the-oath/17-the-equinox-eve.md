## 17. The Equinox Eve

**MARGUERITE**

The Stones sang before she could see them.

She had thought it a figure of speech when Wren said the monoliths *humm* on the eve of an equinox—some old knight's poetry, some embellishment to make the road feel holy. But as they crested the last hill above the Vale of Standing Stones, with Nettie's small hand cold in hers and Tobias a half-step ahead with his knife already drawn out of habit, Marguerite felt the sound enter her through the soles of her boots and climb her spine like water finding its level.

It was not music. It was memory itself, she realized—the land remembering, groaning under the weight of everything it had been made to hold. The covenant was a ledger too, in its way. And ledgers, left unbalanced long enough, began to scream.

"By all the drowned gods," Odile whispered beside her. The old mapmaker had gone gray around the mouth, her ink-stained fingers gripping her walking staff like a rail in a storm. "I charted this place forty years ago from rumor alone. I never believed I'd stand here."

The Stones rose from the frost-white grass like the ribs of some god that had lain down and died there—or like pillars holding up a roof only they could see. There were nine of them, each taller than the guildhouse bell tower, each furred with lichen and older than any kingdom whose edicts had ever hunted her. Between them hung the air itself, and the air was *wrong*: shimmering faintly, like heat off summer cobbles, except the night was bitter cold and the shimmer bled colors that had no names.

"The veil," Wren said.

Marguerite turned. She still was not used to looking at the knight without flinching. Wren stood at the edge of the firelight they'd allowed themselves, and where the light touched him he looked almost whole—a tall figure in tarnished half-armor, a face like a weathered door. But where the light failed, he came apart. His edges frayed into feathers. Sometimes, when he spoke, his voice arrived a heartbeat after his lips moved, as though it had to travel a great distance to reach the world.

"It's thinning faster than it should." He said it flatly, but Marguerite heard what lived underneath: fear. A creature centuries old, afraid. "Corvane's breaking of the oath starved it for decades. But tonight—" He lifted one hand, and a feather drifted loose from his wrist and dissolved before it touched the ground. "Tonight it is being torn. From both sides."

"Both sides?" Nettie asked. Her voice was very small. She had not let go of Marguerite's hand since the river road.

Wren looked at the girl for a long moment, and something in his ruined face gentled. "The Court is dying, little one. Dying things claw."

Marguerite felt it then, properly felt it: the pull. It was the same sensation she knew from her work—the moment a memory came loose, the soft tearing of a thread—but scaled up until it was weather, until it was gravity. The veil wanted memories. All of them. Hers most of all; she could feel herself being *read*, the way she read ledgers, line by line, by something vast and starving on the other side.

She tightened her grip on the oldest ledger, bound in cracked leather, carried across rooftops and under rivers and through fire. Inside it were the covenant's terms, copied in a hand four hundred years dead. She had read them so many times she could recite them in her sleep, and lately she did.

*An oathbearer stands among the Stones at equinox. She speaks the words. She pays the price.*

*The price is what she treasures most.*

Tobias caught her looking at him. He grinned—that crooked, street-born grin that had gotten them past three checkpoints and one very confused toll-keeper—and something in Marguerite's chest folded in on itself like paper in flame.

*Not yet,* she told the feeling. *Not yet. First the oath. Then you may break me.*

"We should make camp below the ridge," Odile said. "And pray your Inquisitor's maps are half as good as mine."

"She doesn't need maps," Marguerite said quietly. "She has me. Or she had me. Every road we've taken since the guildhouse burned, I chose by instinct—and instinct can be followed."

No one answered that. The wind moved through the Stones, and the singing changed key, and somewhere far behind them, down the dark throat of the valley, torches bloomed like a disease.

**TOBIAS**

Tobias had stolen a great many things in his seventeen years: bread, boots, a silver candlestick once, a name (his own, briefly, back), and lately—a thing he hadn't meant to steal and couldn't give back—Marguerite's habit of humming when she thought no one was listening.

He was stealing time now. That was the truth of the watch he'd volunteered for. Everyone else slept—Nettie curled against Odile's side, Wren standing his endless vigil at the Stones' edge, Marguerite asleep sitting up with the ledger in her lap like a cat she didn't trust anyone else to hold. Tobias sat on a fallen stone and watched the torches in the valley multiply and told himself the tightness in his chest was just fear of soldiers.

It wasn't. He'd been afraid of soldiers since he was six years old and knew exactly how fear fit. This was different. This was arithmetic.

Because he'd overheard. Two nights ago, at the fire, Nettie asking her question—*does forgetting someone mean they stop mattering?*—and Marguerite lying so badly that Tobias, who had built his whole life on reading lies, had felt the floor drop out of the world. And then Wren's voice, low, patient, terrible: *the price is what the oathbearer treasures most.* And Marguerite's face doing nothing at all, which was how she looked when she was hiding the largest things.

He'd done the sums since. He couldn't stop doing them.

What did Marguerite Ravensworth treasure most?

Not the ledger—he'd seen her ready to burn it twice if it bought Nettie's safety. Not her gift; she talked about it like a debt. Not the guild, not Corvane, not revenge.

Him. Odile. Nettie. The family assembled out of strays in the space of one desperate season. She treasured *them*. She treasured—

Tobias pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until sparks bloomed.

"No," he said aloud, softly, because saying it aloud made it real and real things could be fought. "No, you don't get to."

"You don't get to what?"

He nearly fell off the stone. Marguerite stood behind him, awake, wrapped in her threadbare cloak, watching him with those ledger-keeper's eyes that missed nothing and forgave less.

"Nothing," he said. "Talking to myself. Street habit."

"Mm." She sat down beside him. Close. Closer than she needed to. The cold was vicious but he barely noticed it anymore, which was its own kind of alarming. Below them the torches had become a river of orange, flowing up the valley road. "They'll be here by midnight. Maybe sooner. The veil tears fully at moonset."

"I know. Wren said."

"Wren says a lot of things." She was quiet a moment. Then, in a voice carefully sanded smooth: "Tobias. If something happens tonight—if I have to do something you don't understand—"

"Don't." The word came out harder than he intended. "Don't do that thing where you say goodbye without saying it. I've watched you do it to everyone else all week. You're bad at it. You're worse at it than you are at lying, and you're *terrible* at lying."

Something flickered across her face—hurt, quickly banked, quickly replaced by the small stubborn smile he'd have walked into a fire for. Had walked into a river for. Would walk into anything for, apparently, including this conversation.

"Tobias—"

"When this is over," he said, "you owe me a story. A proper one. About why a ledger-keeper taught a thief to read, and why she cried at a campfire in the river-road when I told her about my mother's kitchen, and—" His voice cracked and he hated it and kept going anyway, because if he stopped he would say the other thing, the true thing, and she would look at him the way she was looking at him now, like he was something precious and breakable and *already being said goodbye to*. "You owe me that story. So don't spend it tonight. Whatever you're planning to pay with—don't."

For a long moment Marguerite said nothing. The Stones sang. The torch-river climbed.

Then she reached over and took his hand, and her fingers were freezing, and she held on like someone memorizing.

"Deal," she said.

He didn't believe her. He held on anyway.

**SERAPHINE**

Seraphine Vale rode at the head of two hundred soldiers, and every stone of memory inside her was screaming.

That was the only word for it. For years her hoard had sat quiet—her taken memories stacked within her like coins in a vault, heavy but inert, each one labeled and shelved by her own iron discipline. Tonight, as the valley opened before them and the Standing Stones rose against the moon like the fingers of a buried hand, every coin began to rattle.

The soldiers felt it too. She heard them muttering. Horses shied at nothing. One man wept openly and claimed he didn't know why, and Seraphine believed him, because she didn't know why either, and Seraphine Vale always knew why.

Except for the one. The oath. The fragment she'd carried since childhood, unplaceable, unlabeled—a voice like wind through feathers, speaking words she could not forget and had never learned. It had grown louder with every mile. Now, in sight of the Stones, it was not a whisper. It was a bell.

*Your Majesty's edict is just,* she reminded herself, the way she reminded herself each morning, each interrogation, each husk-eyed prisoner led away from her table. *Memory-thieves are traitors. What I do is justice.*

But the children she'd released last week had run into the dark with their faces full of something she had not seen in a mirror for twenty years. Hope. And she had stood in the empty room afterward, surrounded by the stones of her hoard, and understood with sudden, nauseating clarity that she could not remember her mother's face—that somewhere in her, filed and labeled, was the memory of her mother's face, taken by her own hand or another's, and she could not find it, and she had torn her own mind apart searching, and the oath-voice had sung louder, and she had finally, finally begun to ask the question that unraveled everything:

*If I cannot remember why I began, what exactly am I continuing?*

"Inquisitor." Captain Drewe reined up beside her, visor down, voice carefully neutral. "Scouts report fires on the ridge. Small party. Six, maybe seven. And—" he hesitated, and Seraphine marked the hesitation the way she marked all hesitations, "—there's something wrong with the air up there, ma'am. Men are saying it's haunted."

"It's not haunted, Captain. It's *occupied*." She lifted her gaze to the Stones, to the shimmer between them that hurt to look at directly, like staring at a wound in the sky. Somewhere up there was the Ravensworth woman, and the ledger, and whatever madness had brought her here. Somewhere up there was the answer to the question in her vault. "Form the lines. Torch discipline. No one approaches the monoliths until I give the order—I want the traitors alive. Especially the ledger-keeper."

"And if they resist?"

Seraphine touched her saddlebag, where a sealed writ from the Crown lay against her hip, and beneath the writ, wrapped in oilcloth where no soldier could see it, a single kestrel feather she could not explain and could not throw away.

"Then they resist," she said, and put her heels to her horse.

**CORVANE**

Guildmaster Aldous Corvane had spent forty years telling himself a story, and tonight, riding at the rear of the Crown's column in a borrowed officer's coat, the story finally collapsed.

The story had been simple and it had been good: *I saved us. I saved the Guild. If I had paid the covenant's price—if I had given up her memory, given up Elowen, given up the one true thing I ever loved—then who would have kept the ledgers? Who would have kept the trade alive, kept our people fed, kept the gift from dying out entirely? Someone must be practical. Someone must survive. Better one broken oath than a hundred broken lives.*

Forty years of that. Forty years of watching the veil rot at the edges of the world—crops failing in rings, birds forgetting their migrations, children born with hollows where their gifts should be—and telling himself it was slow, it was manageable, it was the price of pragmatism.

And now he rode with soldiers to arrest the girl he had raised, because she had found the courage he had buried, and the practical story had rotted through at last, and underneath it was the truth he had known since the night he fled these very Stones with his oath unpaid:

*I was a coward. Everything since has been cowardice wearing my father's coat.*

He saw the campfires on the ridge. He saw, briefly, impossibly, a shape at the edge of the Stones that flickered between man and bird, and his heart seized—not with fear but with recognition. A knight of the Court. Still bound. Still waiting. Still keeping faith after forty years while Corvane had eaten well and slept warm and called it wisdom.

"Guildmaster." Captain Drewe's second, a young lieutenant with sharp eyes, fell in beside his horse. "Inquisitor Vale's orders—you'll make the offer personally. She says the traitor trusts you."

*Trusted,* Corvane did not correct. *Past tense. I burned that bridge myself, page by page, the night I sold the archive.*

"Very well," he said.

His hands, he noticed, were shaking. He had told himself for forty years that he felt nothing about the covenant, that the old life was ash. But now, in sight of the Stones, with the veil's tear visible even from the valley floor—a wound of light between the monoliths, widening as the moon slid westward—his body remembered what his mind had spent decades forgetting.

Elowen's voice. Wind through feathers. *Will you pay, Aldous? Will you pay?*

He had said no. He had ridden away. And the world had begun, very slowly, to die.

Tonight, he told himself, he would fix it. Whatever it cost the girl. Amnesty, the Crown promised her—walk away whole, surrender the ledger, surrender the gift, live. It was even generous, in its way. It was the same bargain he had wished someone had offered him.

It was also, he knew, a lie. Because he had seen Marguerite Ravensworth slip a lullaby back to a grieving widow for free, and he had known even then, even furious, even afraid, that she was everything he had failed to be.

The column halted below the ridge. Torches everywhere. Above, the Stones sang a note that made his teeth ache.

Corvane dismounted, straightened his borrowed coat, and began to climb.

* * *

**MARGUERITE**

They came at moonrise, and Corvane came first.

He climbed the ridge alone, hands open and empty, an old man in a borrowed uniform with snow in his hair, and Marguerite met him at the edge of the firelight with Tobias at her shoulder and Wren a shadow at her back, half-feathered and fully armed.

"Marguerite." His voice was the voice from her childhood—warm, worn, the voice that had taught her double-entry bookkeeping and which drawer held the bottled oaths and how to tell a lie from a memory by the weight of it. Hearing it now was like hearing a song from a house that had burned down. "You still keep the ledger."

"I keep what matters." She did not step forward. "You sold the archive. You sold *us*. Nettie nearly died in that fire."

"I know." And there—it was not nothing, the flinch that crossed his face. Grief, maybe. Or guilt wearing grief's clothes. She had spent her life distinguishing the two and could not, tonight. "I have come to make it right, as much as it can be made right. Listen to me, girl. The Crown knows where you are. Two hundred soldiers, and Vale—and Vale is not a woman who leaves tasks unfinished. But the edict offers amnesty to any memory-gifted who renounces the art and surrenders forbidden texts. I've secured it for you. Written pardon. Walk away now, whole, with your gift intact and your people unmolested, and I swear on what's left of my name that no harm comes to you."

"And the covenant?" Marguerite asked. "And the Court? And the world going hollow at the edges, year by year, while men like you call it weather?"

Behind Corvane, down the slope, the torch-lines were moving. Forming up. The singing of the Stones had risen until she felt it in her teeth, in her ribs, in the ledger against her heart.

"Marguerite." Corvane stepped closer, and for one unguarded moment she saw him truly: not the guildmaster, not the betrayer, but an old man standing in front of the grave of his own courage. "I stood where you stand. Forty years ago. I heard the price named, and I ran, and I have *drowned* in the running ever since. Don't do what I did. Don't pay. Whatever it costs—whatever you think you must give up—there is always another way. There has to be. I have to believe there is, or none of it meant anything."

For a moment—one treacherous, human moment—she almost pitied him enough to waver.

Then she thought of the widow's face when the lullaby came home. Of Nettie's hand in hers on the river road. Of Tobias humming her own hum, badly, on purpose, to make her laugh.

"There isn't," she said gently. "There's only the right way and the survived way. You taught me that, actually. You just never believed it applied to you."

She opened the ledger. Behind her, Wren drew his blade, and it rang like a bell answering the Stones.

Corvane closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and he stepped back down the slope, and raised his arm, and dropped it.

"Take her," he said, in a voice like a door closing. "Alive."

The valley erupted. Two hundred soldiers came up the ridge with torches and steel, and above them the veil tore wider, spilling light that was not light, and Marguerite Ravensworth turned from the man who raised her toward the Stones that had waited four hundred years, and began to walk.

*Tobias shouted her name. She did not stop.*

Steel met feather-fire among the monoliths. The battle for the covenant had begun.
