## 19. Strangers at a Fire

The world did not end when the veil mended. That was the first thing Tobias learned, and it was somehow the cruelest thing he learned all morning.

He had expected—had braced himself for—some great shudder of the earth, a tearing of the sky, the Standing Stones toppling like drunkards into the frost-burned grass. Instead there had been light, and wind that smelled of feathers and rain on old stone, and then a silence so complete that his own heartbeat sounded obscene in it. The battle simply stopped. Crown soldiers stood among the monoliths with their swords half-raised, blinking like men waking from a shared dream. Somewhere beyond the ridge, Corvane's voice had risen once—a raw, cracked sound, half a name and half a curse—and then nothing.

And Marguerite lay in the trampled snow with her eyes open and her hands folded, as if she were only resting between entries in her ledger.

Tobias had run to her. He remembered running—the stones blurring past, Wren's fading shape dissolving into a storm of small birds, Nettie screaming somewhere behind him—and then he was on his knees in the slush, gathering her up, saying her name over and over because saying it was the only prayer he knew.

Her eyes found his face. And they were kind. That was the worst part. They were warm and curious and entirely, perfectly kind, and they held nothing of him at all.

"Are you hurt?" she'd asked. Her voice was steady, a little hoarse from the cold. "You're bleeding. Your hands—you should bind them."

She meant the cuts from the ice, from the road under the river, from clawing through the veil's last resistance to reach her side. She looked at those wounds with the mild concern one gives a stranger's injury, and Tobias understood, in the space of a single breath, exactly what she had paid, and what it had bought, and that the bill had been collected in full while he was still fighting his way across the battlefield.

"Marguerite," he said. "It's me. It's Tobias."

She frowned—not unkindly, the way one frowns at a word in a language almost recognized. "I'm sorry," she said. "Should I know you?"

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow beneath the largest stone, because no one could bear to leave, and no one could bear to go back either. Odile built the fire with the economy of a woman who had lit fires in places where smoke meant death, stacking the wood so it burned low and clean. Nettie sat pressed against Odile's side, wrapped in the mapmaker's coat, too exhausted for tears but not yet past them. Seraphine Vale kept apart from the rest, sitting on a fallen stone with her back straight as a blade, watching the dark for soldiers who were no longer coming—or perhaps watching herself, Tobias thought, for the woman she had stopped being sometime in the last two days.

And Marguerite sat by the fire with her hands around a tin cup of broth, looking at each of them in turn with polite, patient attention, like an auditor reviewing unfamiliar accounts.

"I keep trying," she said quietly, when the silence had gone on too long. "There are—shapes. Feelings without names attached. When the big one speaks"—she nodded toward Wren's empty place among the stones—"I feel something in my chest, like a door opening onto warmth. I don't know why." She turned the cup in her hands. "When I look at any of you, I feel... almost something. Like a word on the tip of the tongue. But it won't come."

"You don't have to try," Odile said. Her voice was rough; she had been crying earlier, Tobias suspected, though she would deny it under torture. "Not tonight. Not ever, if you don't want to."

"I want to." Marguerite said it simply, without drama, the way she must once have said *the ledger doesn't balance* or *we don't sell children's memories, whatever the client pays*. "Whatever I gave, I gave it on purpose. I'd like to know what I bought with it."

Tobias looked at the fire. The flames had a way of rearranging themselves into shapes he didn't want to see: her face in the cartographer's workshop, lit gold by candlelight, laughing at something Odile had said. Her hand on Nettie's hair as they walked the road under the river. Her mouth against his ear in the dark of the frozen tunnel, whispering that if they survived this, she wanted—

He closed his eyes. Opened them.

"Then let me start," he said.

* * *

"My name is Tobias," he said. "I was born in Saltrow, in the tanners' quarter, which smells exactly as bad as you'd imagine. My mother dyed leather until her hands were blue to the wrist. I never knew my father. When I was nine I ran away because a man offered me bread and a warm corner, and by the time I understood what he actually wanted, I'd already sold him the only thing I owned worth anything."

He paused. Marguerite watched him with that patient attention, and he made himself keep going.

"He took my name. That's what memory-thieves do with children—they take the smallest, brightest thing first, because a child's name is tied to everything: their mother's voice saying it, their own reflection answering to it. Afterward I couldn't remember being called anything. People would shout in the street and I'd never turn around, because none of the sounds belonged to me." He laughed, and hated how thin it came out. "I called myself Toby for years. Just a sound I liked. A stray dog's name for a stray boy."

"That's terrible," Marguerite said softly.

"It gets better. Or worse. Depends how you count." He fed a stick to the fire. "Years later, a girl fell off a rooftop practically into my arms. She was hiding from guild hunters, clutching this enormous old ledger like it was a baby, and she looked at me—this stranger, some gutter rat with stolen goods in his pockets—and she made me a bargain. Sanctuary for a memory. Except the memory she gave me back wasn't a payment. It was a gift. She reached into my head and found the hole where my name used to be, and she filled it."

Nettie had gone very still against Odile's side. Vale had stopped pretending not to listen.

"She put my name back," Tobias said. "My real one. The one my mother used to call across the yard at supper. And when it settled into place, I cried like a child in front of a total stranger, and instead of laughing, she sat down next to me on the rooftop and waited until I was done, and then she said—" His voice caught. He pushed through it. "She said, *a name is just the handle. What matters is what people carry by it.*"

Marguerite's cup had stopped turning in her hands. She was staring at him, and behind her eyes something moved—like a fish passing under ice, there and gone.

"Tobias," she repeated. Testing it.

"Yeah," he said, and smiled, and felt his heart crack neatly down the middle. "That's me. You named me twice, in a way. Once when you gave it back, and once every day after, whenever you'd catch me stealing extra rations and say *Tobias Kestrel's-cousin, put that back*—"

"I did not call you that."

"No," he agreed gently. "You called me worse. You called me *a liability with good intentions*, usually in front of Odile, who never let me forget it."

Odile huffed something that might have been a laugh. Nettie giggled wetly. And Marguerite—Marguerite's mouth curved, just slightly, in a way that suggested her body remembered humor even if her mind had misplaced its owner.

* * *

Later, when Nettie had finally surrendered to sleep and Odile had taken the first watch, Tobias walked the perimeter of the hollow alone, because sleep was a country whose border he couldn't cross. Frost furred the grass. Above the Stones, the night sky had changed—he could feel it more than see it, a rightness in the positions of the stars, as if the whole heavens had exhaled after forty years of holding its breath. Far off, a kestrel called once, high and thin, and was answered.

Wren was out there somewhere, scattered into feather and wind, keeping the court's long watch again. Tobias hoped the knight could see this. He hoped it counted for something.

He heard footsteps behind him and turned, hand going to the knife he no longer needed to reach for, and found Marguerite standing at the edge of the firelight with her cloak pulled close.

"I can't sleep either," she said. "Everything's too quiet. Where I lived before—wherever that was—I think quiet meant danger."

"It usually did," Tobias said. "Around us."

She came to stand beside him, a careful arm's length away, and they watched the Stones together. Moonlight slid down the monoliths like water down old throats.

"The others look at me like I'm a ghost," she said. "Or a grave. The little one—Nettie—she keeps starting to say things to me and stopping halfway. And the soldier, the Inquisitor, watches me like she's cataloguing me for evidence." She hugged the cloak tighter. "But you talk to me like I'm a person. So. Thank you."

"You are a person," Tobias said. "The best one I've ever known. That's not flattery, it's testimony. I have witnesses."

She glanced at him, and in the moonlight he saw the exact angle of her skepticism, the one that used to make guild clients wither. "You loved her," she said. It wasn't quite a question. "The woman I was. It's written all over you. You look at me the way people look at a house that burned down."

Tobias breathed out slowly, watching the fog of it rise and unravel.

"Yes," he said. "I did."

"And now?"

"Now I'm learning the difference between loving someone and loving your memories of them." He said it steadily, though it cost him. "Because here's the thing nobody tells you about grief—it wants to be the whole story. It wants to sit by the fire and tell you that everything good is over, that the person you needed is gone even though she's standing right there. And grief is a liar, Marguerite. I've been poor my whole life. I know a con when I hear one."

She was silent a moment. "What do you hear instead?"

"I hear a beginning." He turned to face her fully. "Look—I'm not going to pretend this isn't the worst thing that's ever happened to me, and I include the time I nearly drowned under a frozen river saving a book. It is. But you gave up everything you loved to save a world, and the world was saved, and you're still here. That's not an ending. That's a ledger with the first page torn out. And I—" His voice betrayed him, cracked down the middle like river ice. He steadied. "I spent nine years without a name, thinking I was nobody because I couldn't remember who I was. Then someone taught me that you don't find out who you are by digging backward. You build forward. Choice by choice. Day by day. So that's what I'm offering. Not the past. The forward part."

Marguerite studied him for a long moment. The kestrel called again, closer now, somewhere above the Stones.

"You talk like someone who's rehearsed this," she said.

"I've rehearsed it since sundown."

A breath escaped her that might have been a laugh. "Honest, at least."

"Honesty's cheap compared to what you paid. Consider it a discount."

She looked away, back at the Stones, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quiet and careful. "There's something I haven't told anyone. When I woke up—when everything was empty—I wasn't frightened. Everyone keeps expecting me to be devastated, and I should be, I know I should, but underneath all the blankness there's this..." She searched for the word. "Certainty. Like a stone at the bottom of a well. Whatever I lost, I lost it for something true. I can't remember the people. But I remember *that it was worth it*. Does that make sense?"

Tobias thought about the road under the river, her hand gripping his in the black water. Thought about her voice saying *whatever happens, finish it.*

"Yeah," he said. "It makes sense. It's the most you thing you've ever said to me."

"But I'm not her anymore."

"No," he agreed. "You're someone new. Someone who woke up this morning owing nothing to anybody, with a clean slate and a whole world that owes *her*." He shrugged, aiming for lightness and mostly missing. "Frankly, most people would kill for that deal."

This time she did laugh—short, startled, quickly smothered, but real. And Tobias stood in the cold with his heart breaking and mending in the same motion, and thought: *there. There she is. Not the woman I remember. A new one. Also hers. Also worth knowing.*

* * *

Dawn came gray and gentle, the frost steaming where the first light touched it. Odile spread a chart on a flat stone—the old maps half-burned, redrawn overnight in fresh ink, new passages marked where the veil had healed. Vale stood at the edge of the group like a sword someone hadn't decided where to hang. Nettie hovered near Marguerite, working up courage, and finally blurted: "Did I have a family? Before? Did you—did we—"

Marguerite crouched to the girl's height. "I don't remember," she said honestly. "But you stayed. Through all of it. That tells me everything I need to know about you, and everything I need to know about me, because I don't leave people behind. Apparently that's just who I am." She tucked a strand of hair behind Nettie's ear. "So we'll start there, you and I. Family's not a memory. It's a practice."

Nettie threw her arms around her neck and held on, and Marguerite held her back, and over the child's shoulder her eyes met Tobias's—uncertain, asking.

He nodded. *Practice,* he mouthed. *We'll practice.*

They broke camp as the sun cleared the ridge. Odile led them south along a path that hadn't existed a week ago, toward the city, toward the burned guildhouse and the ledger that might still be salvageable, toward whatever came next. Tobias walked beside Marguerite, matching her stride without thinking, and when she noticed and raised an eyebrow, he grinned.

"Habit," he said. "Give me a season. We'll make new ones."

Ahead, the road unspooled through the brightening morning, and somewhere overhead a kestrel rode the wind, keeping watch over everything they had bought and everyone they were about to become.
