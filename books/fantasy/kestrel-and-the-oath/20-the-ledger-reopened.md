## 20. The Ledger Reopened

The guildhouse had been rebuilt on the bones of the old one.

That was how Odile put it, anyway, standing in the doorway with her charts rolled under one arm and soot still ghosting the mortar between the new bricks. "Everything worth keeping grows back crooked," she said. "Straight walls are for people with nothing to hide."

Marguerite ran her fingers along the doorframe where the Proclamation of Severance had once been nailed. The wood was fresh pine, pale as an unlined page. Somewhere beneath it—or perhaps not; perhaps the fire had taken even that—the old oak had held the marks of a hundred years of small thefts and smaller mercies. She could not remember them. That was the shape of her life now: a house full of echoes, and herself standing in the middle of it, listening.

She had been Marguerite Ravensworth for three months, and she knew almost nothing about the woman who had chosen that name.

She knew what people told her. Tobias told her the most, though he had a way of stopping mid-sentence, his jaw tightening around some memory he seemed to think would wound her if she swallowed it whole. Nettie told her everything else—Nettie, who had appointed herself Marguerite's shadow and answered questions before they were asked, as though silence were a kind of drowning and she the only one who remembered to throw rope.

And sometimes, when the light came through the new windows at a certain angle, amber and thick as honey, Marguerite felt something move through her that had no name attached. A warmth with no address. Grief with no grave. She had learned to let those feelings pass through her like weather instead of clutching at them until they tore.

Mostly.

* * *

The new guild opened on a morning of thin rain, the kind that did not fall so much as hang in the air, silvering everything it touched.

There was no sign above the door. Odile had suggested THE HOUSE OF RESTORATION, and Nettie had suggested THE PLACE WHERE MARGUERITE LIVES, and both had been overruled by Marguerite herself, who said that a guild which traded honestly needed no advertisement beyond its open shutters. Word traveled anyway. It always had, in this city of narrow streets and narrower secrets.

The first client arrived before the ink on the new ledger's first page had dried.

She was a ferryman's daughter, no older than twenty, with river-mud dried in the creases of her knuckles and a look Marguerite recognized without knowing why she recognized it—that particular hollowness of someone carrying a hole where something used to be.

"They said you give things back," the girl said. Her voice cracked on *back*, like ice underfoot. "Not take. Give."

"That's the trade now," Marguerite said. "Sit. Tell me what's missing."

"It's my mother." The girl sat on the stool by the window, hands knotted in her lap. "She died two winters ago, and I—I sold the memory of her voice. Just the voice. Everything else stayed, but I can't hear her anymore. Not in my head. I sold it to buy medicine for my brother, and he died anyway, and now I've got nothing." She looked up, and her eyes were dry in the terrible way of eyes that have cried past their own capacity. "Can you give me her voice?"

Marguerite opened the ledger to its second page. The first page held only five words, written in her own hand in a script she'd been told was hers but looked like a stranger's careful forgery:

*We were strangers once.*

"I can try," she said. "Restoration isn't like taking. Taking is easy—it's just opening a door. Giving back is..." She searched for the image. "It's like rekindling a fire from smoke. The smoke remembers being flame. But you have to want it enough to hold your hands in the cold while it catches."

"I've been cold two years," the girl said. "I can wait."

So Marguerite took the girl's hands, and closed her eyes, and reached into that hollow place the way Wren had taught her—not grasping, not pulling, but listening, the way you listen for a bird you know is in the room because the air feels occupied.

And there it was. Faint. A thread of sound woven into the girl's grief so tightly the two had become nearly inseparable—a mother's voice, low and laughing, saying something about gulls, about bread, about *come away from the water, love, the water keeps what it takes*. Marguerite gathered the thread gently, the way you lift a spider from a sleeve, and fed it back into the girl's memory the way you feed a wick into a lamp.

The girl made a sound Marguerite had never heard before and somehow knew: the sound of someone hearing a dead beloved speak after years of silence. She wept with her whole body, rocking, laughing, saying *Mama, Mama* to a voice only she could hear.

When it passed, she pressed a few coppers into Marguerite's hand, and Marguerite pushed them back.

"First restoration is free," she said. "House rule."

"You just opened."

"Then it's tradition," Marguerite said, and found herself smiling at her own joke as though someone else had made it—as though somewhere inside her, a woman who had spent years quietly rebelling against the old guild's cruelties was nodding approval.

After the girl left, Marguerite sat alone in the empty room and wrote the entry in the ledger, her hand moving in that borrowed handwriting:

*Entry the First. Restored: a mother's voice, to a ferryman's daughter. Taken: nothing. Price paid: none. Witnessed: the rain.*

She stared at the words a long time. There was a rightness to them that felt less like invention than recognition, like finding a path already worn into grass.

*Someone kept ledgers like this,* she thought. *Someone kept mercy in the margins.*

She didn't know how she knew. She wrote it down anyway.

* * *

Seraphine Vale came at dusk, as she always did, materializing out of the grey like a debt come due.

She stood in the doorway without entering—she never entered uninvited, a courtesy so foreign to everything Marguerite had been told about the Inquisitor that it circled back around to being unsettling—and her eyes went over the room with the methodical hunger of a woman cataloguing exits, threats, and valuables in a single sweep.

"You're open late," Vale said.

"We're open always. That's rather the point."

"Hm." Vale stepped inside at last, and the lamplight caught the hollows of her face. She had grown thinner since the equinox, since the Stones, since whatever had happened inside her when Marguerite spoke the covenant and the sky filled with feathers. There were stones behind her eyes still—Marguerite could see them, the stolen memories Vale carried like ballast—but some of them had shifted, settled, begun to erode. "I'm not here on crown business. There is no crown business anymore. The edicts are ash. Half the Inquisition resigned when they learned what we—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "I'm here because there are men in the Low Quarter selling false restorations. They take coin, whisper nonsense into people's ears, and call it memory. Grieving families are being robbed twice."

Marguerite set down her pen. "You want me to do something about it."

"I want you to *know* about it," Vale said. "What you do with knowing is your affair." She paused at the door on her way out, and something moved across her face that on any other person might have been called uncertainty. "The widow. From the old guild. The one whose arrest started all of this."

Marguerite's chest tightened around a feeling she couldn't source. "What about her?"

"She's alive. In the country, north of the city. When the edicts burned, I saw to her release myself." Vale's jaw worked. "I extracted her memories during interrogation. All of them. I have them still—they don't leave me, they never leave—and I find I cannot return them, because I don't know how, and I cannot keep them, because they weigh—" Her hand rose halfway to her sternum and stopped. "Bring her voice back to her someday. If you can. Consider it the first debt of the new ledger."

Then she was gone, into the rain, her grey cloak drinking the lamplight, and Marguerite sat looking at the space where she'd stood and understood, dimly, that she had just watched a woman try to say *I'm sorry* in the only language she had left.

*Reluctant protector,* Tobias called her. *Like a dog that bit everyone and now guards the yard out of guilt.* Marguerite thought that was unfair to dogs.

* * *

Odile arrived three days later with a crate of maps and an argument.

"The crossings should be charted," she said, spreading vellum across Marguerite's worktable with the flourish of a general deploying troops. "Officially. Under law. The veil is mended but it breathes, Marguerite—it thins at every equinox, it always has, and right now half the realm's thresholds are unmarked. People wander through by accident. Last month a shepherd followed a stray lamb through a fold in the air near Harrow Fell and came back three days older and speaking fluent Kestrel. He thinks it was a dream. It was not a dream."

"And you want to map the folds."

"I want to map the folds, register them, and post signs." Odile tapped the largest chart, where the human realm lay rendered in careful ink beside the shimmering suggestion of the Kestrel Court—drawn, Marguerite noticed, in colors that didn't quite sit still. "A border nobody can see is not a border. It's an accident waiting in a field."

Marguerite studied the maps. Something in her responded to them the way a tuning fork responds to its note—a hum of rightness deep in the ribs. "You've done this before," she said. "Charted doors."

Odile's hands went still on the vellum. For a moment her face did something complicated. "Once," she said. "For friends. Before it was illegal." She smoothed the map flat with unnecessary force. "The point is, the new law needs a Cartographer of Crossings, and I need a guildhouse to work from, and you need someone who knows which doors bite. So. Do we have terms?"

Marguerite looked at this sharp-eyed, exiled woman who spoke of exile the way other people spoke of weather, and felt again that unmetered warmth, that grief with no grave.

"We have terms," she said. "First chart is free."

"Your house runs on charity."

"My house runs on debts," Marguerite said, "the good kind. The kind that bind people together instead of bleeding them." She heard the words come out of her mouth and wondered, not for the first time, whose conviction she was borrowing.

Odile smiled—rare, crooked, real. "Terms accepted."

* * *

Winter deepened. The guild grew.

They came in ones and twos at first, then in streams: the soldier who'd sold his courage before a battle he survived too easily; the old man who wanted back one afternoon in an orchard, umber light and wasps and a girl whose name he'd traded away decades ago; a Kestrel knight, barely more substantial than heat-shimmer, who crossed legally through one of Odile's registered doors to ask whether the human realm truly remembered the covenant now, and left weeping silently, the way the faded wept, without wetness, only weight.

Marguerite restored and restored and restored. Her gift grew surer with each working—she could feel the shape of a missing memory now before she touched it, could tell a sold memory from a stolen one by the texture of its edges, could sense the difference between a thing given up willingly and a thing torn out. Stolen memories came back angry. Sold ones came back shy.

And every night, Tobias was there.

He had taken to sitting in the corner of the guildroom during working hours, ostensibly keeping the accounts—Nettie had taught him his letters, laboriously, and he guarded his new literacy like contraband—but really he watched. Marguerite caught him watching often. He never looked away when caught; he just smiled, that crooked, patient smile, as though he were waiting for something he was willing to wait a very long time for.

"Do I know you?" she asked him once, late, after the last client had gone. "From before? Really know you?"

The lamplight did something to his face. "Everyone says you don't remember anything," he said carefully. "Is that true? Nothing at all?"

"Nothing before the Stones. Feelings, sometimes. Shadows of feelings." She turned her cup in her hands. "But you—you're not a shadow. You're the opposite. When you walk into a room it's like remembering something I never knew. Is that strange?"

"No," Tobias said, and his voice was rough. "No, I don't think it's strange at all."

He walked her home along the river most nights. He told her stories—about the city, about the old days, about a boy who'd sold his name for bread and spent years answering to whatever strangers called him. He never claimed the boy was himself. He never had to.

* * *

It happened on an evening in late winter, when snow was falling past the windows in fat, unhurried flakes and the guildroom smelled of tallow and cedar and the faint green scent that clung to everything Wren touched.

Nettie had gone to bed. Odile was bent over her charts, murmuring coordinates to herself in three languages, two of them possibly not languages at all. And from the street below, drifting up through the gap in the shutters, came a woman's voice singing.

A lullaby. Old, half-familiar in the way street songs are—everyone knows the tune, no one knows the words. The singer's voice was rough and warm, a mother's voice or a grandmother's, rising through the snowfall:

*Sleep, my kestrel, wing and bone,*
*the dark is long but not alone...*

Marguerite's pen stopped mid-stroke.

Something broke open in her chest.

She did not know the song. She was certain she did not know the song—she knew nothing, that was the whole architecture of her life now, a cathedral built on an emptied floor—and yet the melody went through her like a blade going through water, meeting no resistance, finding the heart anyway. Tears arrived without permission, without grief she could name, hot and bewildering, and she sat in the lamplight with her hand pressed to her mouth while a stranger's lullaby undid her.

*Marguerite?* Odile was beside her. When had Odile crossed the room? *Hey. Hey, look at me. What is it?*

"I don't know," Marguerite managed. "I don't know the song. I've never heard it. Why am I—"

The voice outside finished the verse and faded down the street, and the crying eased as it went, leaving her wrung out and shaking and utterly unable to explain.

Odile crouched in front of her, took her hands. Her grip was strong and ink-stained and steady. "Listen to me," she said. "What you gave up at the Stones—it wasn't erased. It was *paid*. There's a difference. A payment leaves a mark where the thing used to be, the way a ring leaves a pale band on a finger. You don't remember us. But the shape of us is still on you." Her voice caught, very slightly, and steadied. "That song found one of the shapes."

"A song," Marguerite whispered. "Why a song? Whose—"

She stopped. Because she almost had it. Because for one vertiginous instant there was something at the edge of the pale band—an image, a candlelit room, a ledger open on a desk, her own hands copying a lullaby onto a scrap of paper meant for a grieving widow—and then it slid away like a fish into deep water, leaving only the ache, only the certainty that it had mattered, that *she* had mattered once in some small secret merciful way she could no longer touch.

"Marguerite?" Odile said softly.

"Nothing." She wiped her face with her sleeve and laughed, wet and unsteady. "Gods. I cry at lullabies now. The clients will talk."

"The clients will think the guildmistress has a heart," Odile said. "Terrible for business."

But later, much later, when the lamps were out and the snow ticked softly against the glass, Marguerite sat up in bed with the new ledger open on her knees, and by the light of a single stub of candle she turned to the margins of Entry the First and wrote, small, in the space where the old guild had once hidden its mercies:

*A lullaby I do not know made me weep tonight. Whoever taught it to me: thank you. Whoever I sang it for: I hope you sleep well, wherever you are, whoever you were to me.*

She blew out the candle. In the dark, she laid her hand flat over her sternum, over the pale band where something used to be, and felt it there—the shape, the absence shaped exactly like a presence.

Down the hall, Tobias's door was closed, but she could hear him, very faintly, talking to himself the way he sometimes did when he thought no one listened. Telling a story. His voice rose and fell, patient and warm, and though she could make out no words, she found she did not want him to stop.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would ask him what he was always telling stories about.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would start writing them down.
