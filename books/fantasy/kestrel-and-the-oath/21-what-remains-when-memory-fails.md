## 21. What Remains When Memory Fails

The first snow of the new year came down over Vellum Street like ash that had repented, soft and slow and white instead of grey. Tobias watched it from the window of the guildhouse—*their* guildhouse now, though the sign still needed painting—and counted the things Marguerite did not know.

She did not know that the third stair from the bottom creaked in the key of a mourning dove, and that he had learned to skip it so she could sleep past dawn. She did not know that she took her tea bitter enough to strip varnish, or that she hummed when she worked, a tuneless little thread of sound that filled the archive room the way lamplight fills a jar. She did not know his name—not truly, not the way you know a thing you have carried for years—and every morning when she looked at him across the ledgers, her eyes were polite and curious and utterly empty of him.

It was the emptiness that undid him. Not grief. Grief would have been simpler. This was grief with its face washed and its hair combed, sitting down to breakfast and asking him to pass the marmalade.

"Your turn," she said that first snowy night, not looking up from her column of figures. "You promised."

"I promised nothing of the kind. I said I'd tell it *sometimes*."

"You said nightly." She glanced up, and there—the ghost of a smile, the one he had spent whole seasons learning to coax out of hiding. "I keep records, Tobias. I'm told I'm very good at it."

He was. She was. And so he told it.

---

He had learned early that she could not bear to hear it all at once. The first week after the Stones, when she woke knowing no one's name, he had made the mistake of pouring everything out at once—the guildhouse, the rooftops, Odile's road beneath the frozen river, the battle among the monoliths where the sky tore open like wet paper—and she had gone white as the snow on the sill and pressed both hands flat against the table as though the world were tilting.

"Stop," she'd whispered. "Stop. It's like being handed a chest of someone else's letters. I can see they matter. I can't feel why."

So he learned to ration it. One piece a night, the way you feed a fire that must not flare but must never die. He started small: the smell of the old archive, tallow and vellum and Corvane's bitter incense. The way the widow Marta had wept when her lullaby came home to her. Nettie's habit of stealing apples and hiding them in her boots, where they went soft and fragrant and were always, somehow, still eaten.

And Marguerite listened the way she did everything—with her whole attention, her chin propped on her laced fingers, her eyes following his mouth as though the words might be caught if one only watched closely enough.

Tonight he gave her the river.

"The road under the ice," he began, settling onto the stool across from her desk. Outside, the snow ticked against the glass. "Odile called it the Underway. A cartographer's door beneath the frozen river—you stepped through a seam in the bank and suddenly you were walking *under* the water, above your head, whole barges going by like clouds."

"That isn't possible."

"No," he agreed comfortably. "But it was true."

She wrote that down. He had learned not to flinch when she did it—she kept a private ledger now, *Tobias's Stories*, in her own careful hand, because written things stayed when memory would not. He had read it once when she was out. Her transcription of his voice was so exact it had frightened him; he had sounded, on those pages, like a man worth listening to.

"Tell me about the ice breaking," she said. She always asked for the hard parts. He had stopped trying to protect her from them; she had told him once, in a rare flash of the woman she must have been, *I'd rather be cut than coddled.*

"The ice broke," he said, "because I am an idiot who thought a ledger was worth more than my life."

"Was it?"

He looked at her—at this woman who held his whole history in a notebook and felt none of it, who had given away every memory of him to mend the sky, who sat here night after night asking a stranger to make her stranger into something familiar.

"It was worth *your* life," he said. "You'd have drowned going back for it. So yes. Worth mine too."

Her pen stilled. For a moment the only sound was the snow and the small pop of the stove, and then she closed the ledger gently, as though closing a door on something sleeping.

"Tobias," she said. "Tell me what happened after. Not the story. After."

"After the river?"

"After I forgot you."

* * *

Spring came up the street in green increments, ivy unrolling along the guildhouse wall, and with it the season of open windows and Nettie's laughter ricocheting off the rooftops where she trained with Wren. The knight was less faded now—there was color in Wren's cheeks where there had been translucence, weight in Wren's stride—and some evenings the two of them could be heard arguing cheerfully about sword-forms until Odile threw a map-weight out the window at them both.

Marguerite rebuilt the guild the way a gardener rebuilds a burned orchard: patiently, without illusion, one graft at a time. Clients came hesitantly at first—a man who wanted to give away a memory of a war rather than lose it to drink; a mother who had sold her daughter's first steps to buy bread and now, under the new law, could buy them back at cost. Marguerite restored each one with hands that shook afterward, every time, as though the giving-back cost her something the taking never had.

Tobias ran the front counter. He had discovered, in the seasons since, that he was good at it—that a boy who had once sold his own name knew exactly how to price another person's grief fairly, and how to talk them down when they offered too much.

And every night, the telling.

He varied it now. Some nights he gave her the funny parts—Nettie's boots full of rotting apples, Odile charging a toll of one riddle per crossing, Wren's horror at discovering human ale. Some nights he gave her the terrible ones, because she asked, because she said a story with the pain sanded off was a lie wearing a costume. And some nights—more often, as the spring thickened toward summer—he found himself telling her things that had never happened at all, embellishments, inventions, the natural embroidery of any tale told often enough.

She caught him the first time. Of course she did. She was Marguerite Ravensworth, even hollowed out; she audited everything.

"That isn't how you told it in March," she said, tapping the page. "In March, the guard dropped the lantern because he slipped on the ice. Now you say I threw a bottle at him."

"...Artistic license."

"Liar." But she was smiling, and she wrote down *both versions*, and something in his chest turned over and lay quiet.

That was the thing he could not explain to anyone—not to Odile, who watched him with her cartographer's squint and said nothing; not to Wren, who had once said, with the blunt kindness of knights, *you know she may never come back to you*, and Tobias had answered *I know*, and meant it, and kept setting the table for two anyway.

Because here was what he had learned, watching her across the seasons: she was not coming back. The woman who had walked into the Standing Stones with their whole family folded inside her heart like letters in a coat pocket—she was gone, paid out into the veil, spent on the sky. No retelling would raise her. He had understood that by midwinter, and grieved it properly, alone, in the stairwell where the third step sang.

But the woman who remained was not a stranger. That was the miracle he tripped over daily. She was *made of the same material*. She stole apples from the bowl and hid them in her sleeve and looked baffled when Nettie accused her of it. She hummed while she worked. She could not walk past a locked drawer without wanting to pick it, purely on principle. Once, hearing a street musician fumble through Marta's lullaby, she had stopped dead in the doorway and wept without knowing why, and Tobias had stood beside her and wept too, and neither of them had explained anything, because there was nothing to explain. Grief had outlived its reasons. Love, apparently, intended to do the same.

* * *

Midsummer. The archive windows stood open to the smell of warm stone and cut grass, and Marguerite sat cross-legged on the floor amid a drift of returned memories, sorting bottles by client, her hair coming loose from its braid in a way that would have scandalized the woman she used to be and clearly delighted the woman she was.

"Tell me the part about the bargain," she said, without looking up.

He knew which bargain. There had been several—his sanctuary for his stolen name, struck on a rooftop with a fugitive he'd half expected to rob. But she always asked for that one, and he had long since stopped wondering why.

"You were hiding behind a chimney," he said, lying back against a shelf. "Covered in soot, clutching a ledger like a shield, glaring at me like I was the one trespassing. I offered you my roof. You offered me payment. I said I didn't want coin—I wanted my name back. The one I sold when I was nine."

"And I said—" she prompted.

"You said, *names are heavier than money. Are you sure you can carry yours?*" He watched her mouth move faintly around the words, half a syllable ahead of him, the way she did now. She had heard it enough times to know its shape. "And I said I'd been carrying the lack of it for eleven years, so how much heavier could the thing itself be."

"And then?"

"And then you shook my hand, and your grip was filthy and strong, and I thought—" He paused. This was the part he had never told her straight, in all the seasons of telling. "I thought, *oh no.*"

"Oh no?" She looked up at last, grass-stained and skeptical.

"Oh no, *her*. The oh-no of recognizing trouble you're going to walk into anyway. The oh-no of—" He gestured vaguely at the air between them, at the sunlit room, at her. "You know."

"I don't know," she said. "Tell me."

He sat up. Somewhere below, Nettie shouted triumphantly and Wren groaned; a cart rattled past on Vellum Street; the world went on being ordinary, which was itself the extraordinary thing, the peace they had bought at the Standing Stones holding firm as mended sky.

"When you look at me," Tobias said slowly, "what do you feel? Honestly. Not the ledger answer."

She considered him with the seriousness she brought to everything, and he braced himself for kindness, for the gentle arithmetic of *I am fond of you the way one is fond of a good storyteller*—

"I feel like I've forgotten something important," she said. "Every day. Like walking out of a house and being sure I've left the stove lit. Except the stove is a person, and the person is you, and there is no way to check." She picked up a bottle of somebody's recovered wedding day and turned it in the light, amber and slow. "I don't remember loving you, Tobias. I want to be honest about that. But I keep *choosing* things that don't make sense unless I did. I choose the seat facing you. I choose your stories over sleep. Last week I caught myself buying honeycakes because you lingered at the baker's window, and I don't even like honeycakes." She set the bottle down with a small decisive click. "So either I'm a fool, or whatever I felt survived the paying of it. And I've never much enjoyed being a fool."

The room was very bright. Tobias found that his throat had closed entirely.

"So," Marguerite said, and now she was smiling, that smile, the one he had spent seasons learning to coax and now recognized arriving on its own, wild and unscheduled, "I've decided to stop waiting for the memory to come back. I've decided to simply—do it again. Court me, Tobias. Properly. I want walks and arguments and flowers I pretend not to like. I want to fall in love with you on purpose, from the front, where I can watch myself doing it." She tilted her head. "Unless you'd rather keep telling me stories forever."

"Both," he managed. "I'll do both."

"Good." She returned to her bottles, serene, as though she had not just rearranged the architecture of his heart between one inventory line and the next. Then, without looking up: "For the record. Tonight's entry. Tell me the ending again—the one at the Stones. The true version. I want to write it down before we begin."

"Why?"

"Because," she said softly, "if I'm going to love you twice, I want to know what it cost the first time. All of it. Even the parts that hurt you to tell."

Outside, the summer light lay gold along the shelves, and in the newest ledger, under her steady hand, waited a blank page.
