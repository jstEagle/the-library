## 22. First Entry, New Hand

The new ledger arrived on the first morning of spring, wrapped in brown paper and tied with a string that smelled faintly of beeswax.

Marguerite set it on the counter of the guildhouse—*her* guildhouse now, though the word still fit strangely, like a coat cut for someone taller—and unwrapped it with the care one gives to something that might still be deciding what it wants to be. The cover was calfskin, pale as birch bark, unmarked by any guild sigil. She had asked the binder in Cooper's Lane to leave it blank. Whatever this book was going to hold, it would not be the Vellum Guild's old trade. It would not be theft at all.

She opened it. The first page gleamed, empty and patient.

Outside the window, Ashfall was waking. The city had changed its sound since the Severance was repealed—since the King's own herald, red-faced and stammering, had nailed the *Proclamation of Restoration* to the very same door where the other proclamation had once gone up like a curse. There were more voices on the streets now. More laughter that didn't check itself. Children ran errands through the market without flinching from the black-and-silver coats of the Inquisitors' guard, because those coats were fewer now, and because Seraphine Vale had spent the winter making certain the ones that remained remembered how to be gentle.

Marguerite dipped her pen. The ink caught the early light, dark as river water.

She had thought about this first line for weeks. She had drafted it in her head while restoring an old soldier's memory of his mother's kitchen, while walking Nettie home from her lessons, while sitting across from Odile in the map room listening to her chart the legal crossings—the *sanctioned* crossings, she said with relish, tapping each one—between the human realm and the Kestrel Court. She had drafted it lying awake at night, staring at the ceiling of her small room above the shop, trying to understand why sleep kept sliding away from her like water through fingers.

Every draft came back to the same five words.

She wrote them slowly, in her best hand, the hand Corvane had taught her at nine years old when he'd set her on a stool at this very counter and told her that a ledger was a promise made of paper:

*We were strangers once.*

She looked at the line for a long time. It seemed too small to hold everything it held. But then, she had learned that most true things were like that—a lullaby was only seven notes until it was the last thing a widow had of her daughter; an oath was only words until the world's whole weather turned on whether they were kept.

The bell above the door chimed.

"I smell fresh ink," Tobias announced from the doorway, "which means either you've started the new ledger or you've finally snapped and begun writing ransom notes."

"Good morning to you too."

He came in carrying two paper cones of roasted chestnuts and the particular expression he wore when he had been awake longer than he wanted anyone to know. His hair was doing something unruly in the damp spring air. He set the chestnuts on the counter, stole a glance at the open ledger, and went very quiet.

Marguerite watched him read the line. Watched something move through his face—something that arrived and departed too quickly for her to name, leaving behind only a kind of careful brightness, the way a sky looks after rain it has decided not to cry.

"It's good," he said.

"You haven't read anything but the first line."

"That's how I know." He turned a chestnut over in his fingers, peeling it with his thumbnail, not looking at her. "First lines are the honest ones. Everything after is just... keeping the promise they made." He glanced up. "You're keeping it, then? The guild. Under the new law."

"Odile filed the charter yesterday. Vale countersigned it, which took three hours and cost me a bottle of the good plum wine." Marguerite closed the ledger gently. "We restore now. We don't take. Every entry witnessed, every gift consented, every price paid openly or not at all."

"And the ones who can't pay?"

"The Kestrel Court pays for them." She smiled. "Wren delivered the first stipend last week. Forty years of back interest, apparently. The Court keeps accounts too, it turns out. Very old ones."

Tobias laughed—that laugh she knew the way she knew the weight of her own hands, without any memory of learning it—and the sound filled the shop the way light fills a room with the shutters newly opened.

* * *

They were strangers once. That was the part she could not get past, no matter how many times she circled it.

Tobias had told her the story so many times that it had worn smooth as a river stone: the rooftops, the bargain, the boy who sold his name and wanted it back. He told it plainly, without drama, the way he told all his stories, as if the remarkable thing about them was merely that they had happened to him and not to somebody more deserving. He told her about Odile's map room full of doors that weren't doors. About Wren materializing out of the snow with feathers in his hair and half his body gone translucent, swearing fealty to a girl holding nothing but a ledger. About the road under the frozen river, and the moment the ice groaned and he went into the black water after a book.

"Why the book?" she always asked. "Why not save yourself?"

"Because the book was yours," he always said, "and I hadn't finished needing you yet."

And then there was the rest of it—the part he told more slowly, watching her face the whole way, as though checking each word against some original he carried and she did not. The Standing Stones. The equinox. The battle among the monoliths, crown troops and hunters and Corvane's cold voice offering her wholeness if she would only walk away. And Marguerite—herself, but a version of herself she could not remember being—standing before the keystone and speaking the covenant aloud, and paying its price.

Her memories. All of them. Of him, of Odile, of Nettie, of Wren, of the found family she had gathered out of the wreckage of her old life. She had known the price in advance. She had hidden it from all of them. And she had paid it anyway, willingly, because that was the covenant's terms and because—this was the detail that undid her every time—because she had judged that people she loved were worth more than loving them.

"You could have chosen differently," Marguerite said once, late, both of them tired, the fire down to embers. "I could have. Whoever I was. She could have refused."

"She almost did," Tobias said. "Not because she wanted to keep the memories. Because she wanted to keep *you*—" he tapped his own chest, "—the having of you. She was selfish in exactly one direction and it was inward." He looked at the fire. "Then Nettie asked her question. Whether forgetting someone means they stop mattering. And whatever answer she gave herself, it wasn't the coward's one."

Marguerite turned her cup in her hands. "Do you miss her? The one who remembered?"

Tobias was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Every day. But here's the thing nobody tells you about grief like that." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his eyes when they found hers were steady and unbearably kind. "You're not her replacement. You're not the copy left over after the original burned. You're the same person, Marguerite. The memory was never where you lived. You live *here*—" again the tap against his chest, and then, softer, reaching over to tap hers, right over the heartbeat, "—and there. Everything else is just the record. And records can be rewritten."

* * *

Spring deepened. The guildhouse filled.

Nettie claimed the corner desk by the window and covered it with practice ledgers in her enormous, earnest handwriting, and Marguerite corrected her figures and marveled daily at how quickly the girl was growing, how her silences had healed into something less like fear and more like thought. Nettie had been the child pulled from the burning archive; she did not speak of it, but sometimes, when the wind rattled the shutters, she would go still as a held breath, and Marguerite would cross the room and put a hand on her shoulder and stay until the breath released.

Odile arrived twice a week with maps rolled in oilcloth and opinions rolled in nothing at all, free-floating and abundant. She had hung a great chart of the veil-crossings behind the counter, annotated in four colors of ink, and she lectured any client who would stand still long enough about the difference between a door and a passage and a *threshold*, which she insisted was a moral category. On Thursdays she brought seed cake and gossip from the Court, where the Kestrels were rebuilding in colors the human eye could barely hold—Wren described the palace as "a cathedral made of weather"—and where the Queen of Feathers had sent, via knightly courier, a single message for Marguerite that Odile had memorized exactly:

*Tell the oathbearer the debt is paid and the door stands open. Tell her also that the Court remembers what she chose to forget, and will keep the remembering safe until she wants it back.*

Marguerite had stood very still when she heard it. "And do I want it back?"

"That," Odile said, folding her hands, "is the only question in the world I won't chart for you."

Seraphine Vale came on the first of the month, punctual as a tax, in plain grey now rather than black-and-silver, with her sword left at home and her face doing the difficult work of learning openness at forty years of age. She inspected the consent registers with genuine rigor, argued with Odile about the ethics of threshold-charting, drank one cup of tea without ever appearing to enjoy it, and stayed an extra hour every visit without ever explaining why. Once, near closing, she paused at the door and said, without turning around: "There's a memory I've carried since I was six. An oath, spoken somewhere green, in a voice like wind through feathers. I've been trying to give it back. I can't find where it belongs." A pause. "If you ever learn where it belongs, owl-girl, send word."

Then she was gone, coat collar up, into the street.

And Tobias—Tobias was simply there, the way weather is there, the way the river is there. He had taken the loft room above the shop "temporarily," eight months ago, and had unpacked with the shameless thoroughness of a man who had never intended temporary to mean anything. He ran deliveries. He charmed clients. He sat on the counter when Marguerite was working and peeled apples with a knife in long unbroken spirals and talked, endlessly, wonderfully, about nothing and everything, and every night he told her their story, or a piece of it, whichever piece the evening asked for.

Some nights she cried at the lullaby.

It happened without warning—a client humming it in the waiting chair while Marguerite prepared a restoration, or Nettie singing it absently over her sums, or once, unbearably, Tobias himself, tuneless and off-key, washing dishes. Grief would arrive from nowhere, huge and specific, aimed at a song she did not recognize, and she would have to set down her pen and press her knuckles to her mouth until it passed. She had searched every archive. The lullaby appeared in no ledger, belonged to no client, matched no song in the city's whole repertoire.

"It's hers," Tobias said quietly, the first time it happened. He had crossed the room and stood beside her, not touching, just near. "The one she gave away. It's the shape of the hole, Marguerite. That's all a memory leaves behind when it goes—the shape of itself. And shapes are stubborn. They wait."

"For what?"

"For whatever comes along that fits."

* * *

On the evening the new ledger's first entries were complete—eleven restorations, all consented, all witnessed, all paid openly—Marguerite locked the front door and turned the sign to CLOSED and found Tobias sitting on the counter in the lamplight, as he did, peeling an apple, as he did, in one long unbroken spiral that coiled onto a saucer below.

"You're staring," he said, pleased.

"I'm trying to catch you at it."

"At what?"

"Whatever it is you're not telling me. You have a face for it. You've had it for weeks."

The knife paused. He looked at the apple, at the perfect ribbon of peel, and something in his jaw worked, the way it did when he was choosing between the funny answer and the true one. He chose true. He had gotten better at that, she'd noticed; or perhaps he had always been better at it than he let on, and she was only now fluent enough in him to see it.

"There's a thing I've been waiting to ask you," he said. "Since the fire, really. Since before the fire. Since the river, if I'm honest, which I'm trying to be, these days." He set down the knife. "But I made myself a rule. I don't get to ask you anything until you've had a fair chance to fall for me *now*. Not because of the story. Not because some girl who isn't—you, except that she is—paid a price that bought us a future. I wanted you to choose me with a clean slate. No debt. No ghost of a decision you can't remember making."

"Tobias."

"So I waited." He slid off the counter and stood in front of her, close enough that she could see the lamplight doubled in his eyes, and his voice went low and rough at the edges. "And you fell. I watched it happen. Slow, the way real things happen. And I've been carrying this around like a hot coal for a month, waiting for the slate to be clean enough, and tonight I read your first line and I thought—*we were strangers once.* Past tense. You wrote it yourself. So."

He got down on one knee on the floorboards of the guildhouse, in the lamplight, amid the smell of ink and apples and beeswax, and Marguerite's heart performed a maneuver she had no name for.

"Marry me," Tobias said. "Not because we were. Because we are. Because every night I tell you our story and every night you listen like it's the first time, and maybe it is, and maybe that's the most romantic thing that has ever happened to anyone anywhere. Because I loved you when you knew me and I love you now that you don't, and both loves turned out to be the same love wearing different clothes. Because you taught me that family is made by choice, and I am—" his voice cracked, and he grinned through it, wrecked and radiant, "—I am *choosing*, Marguerite Ravensworth, with my whole ridiculous heart, for the rest of my life. If you'll have me. Again. Or for the first time. Whichever this is."

The silence rang like a struck bell.

Marguerite looked down at him—at this stranger she was certain of, this man whose name felt like her own name in her mouth, whose laugh she had known forever in a way that had nothing to do with years—and she understood, suddenly and completely, what the shape of the lullaby had been waiting for. What all the stubborn shapes inside her had been waiting for. Not the memories back. Not the record restored.

This. Whatever came along that fits.

"Yes," she said, and then, because she was a ledger-keeper to the bone and precision mattered: "Yes. Both times. Every time."

He surged up and caught her face in his hands and kissed her, and outside the window the first swifts of spring came screaming joyfully over the rooftops of Ashfall, black scythes against a sky going rose and gold, and somewhere far beyond the veil—in a court built of weather—a queen of feathers lifted her head and smiled at a sound only she could hear: the small, bright click of a covenant renewing itself in a form its makers had never imagined.

When they broke apart, laughing, foreheads together, Marguerite reached back without looking and drew the new ledger toward her across the counter. She opened it past the first line, past the eleven careful entries, to a clean page.

"What are you doing?" Tobias murmured.

"Second entry." She dipped the pen. Her hand was steady. Her heart was not, and she suspected it never would be again, and she found she hoped it never would. Beneath *We were strangers once*, in her best hand, the hand that had been taught to make promises of paper, she wrote:

*We are not anymore.*

She blotted it, closed the book, and turned the lamp down to a glow—and neither of them saw, because the lamp was low and they were otherwise occupied, the faint shimmer that passed across the fresh ink, like wind moving through feathers, like a door standing open, like the world quietly writing back.
