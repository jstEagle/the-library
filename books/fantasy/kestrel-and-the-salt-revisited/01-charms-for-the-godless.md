## 1. Charms for the Godless

The morning fog came off the harbor the way it always did in Salt Revisited — slow and gray and smelling of old grief, as if the drowned city beneath their feet were exhaling through the pilings. Sable Dravenmoor had lived above that drowned city for ten years, and he had learned to read its moods the way other men read weather. This morning it was restless.

He noticed it first in his knots.

He worked at a folding table set against the wall of a chandlery on Tidewrack Lane, where the awning kept off most of the drizzle and all of the direct questions. His wares were modest by design: luck-knots braided from tarred cord and sea-glass, small charms to keep nets full and children feverless, love-tokens that worked only as well as any honest trinket ought to. Nothing that would draw the eye of the Wardens. Nothing that *worked*, if anyone had thought to test it — which no one did, because everyone knew magic was dead, burned out of Kestrel a generation ago along with the kingdom itself. What Sable sold was comfort with a knot in it. That was the whole trick of survival: be small, be dull, be a man who sold string.

Except this morning the string was humming.

He felt it under his fingers like a plucked wire — a faint, wrong vibration running up through the tarred cord, through his knuckles, into the bones of his wrist. He had tied four hundred of these knots in ten years and not one of them had ever done anything but sit there and look hopeful. Now the half-finished luck-knot in his hands seemed to lean toward the harbor, the way a dog leans toward a door before the knock comes.

"Master charm-seller?"

He looked up. A girl stood at the edge of his table, maybe sixteen, with her hair bound back in a fisherman's kerch and salt-cracked hands folded politely in front of her apron. Her father's coat hung off her shoulders two sizes too big. Behind her, the fog was thinning over the water, and the cranes of the fish-quay stood up out of it like the ribs of something enormous sleeping just offshore.

"You're the one what does the knots," she said. "For luck. Marta Voss says yours hold better than the ones from the temple stalls."

"The temples charge more," Sable said. "That's all they do better."

She smiled the way dock people smile — quickly, sparingly, saving the rest for when there was something worth spending it on. "It's for my da. He's going out past the Mole tomorrow, first time since the storm season. The *Kindly Ann*. And he says—" She stopped, and her politeness cracked just enough to show the fear underneath, plain as a coin dropped on stone. "He says the water's been strange lately. Singing, near enough. The old men say it's nothing. But he asked me to get him a charm, and I'd rather pay you than pray."

*So would I,* Sable thought, and did not say it.

He looked down at the knot in his hands. It was still humming. No — not humming. *Listening.* That was the word that came, unbidden and unwelcome, into the front of his mind. The cord lay slack across his palm and yet he could feel it straining toward the girl like a compass needle, and beneath the harbor, beneath the drowned streets and the sunken bell towers of Old Kestrel, something vast and patient had turned its attention upward, the way a sleeper turns toward a voice saying its name.

Ten years. Ten years of being nobody, of selling string to godless fishermen in a city built on the corpse of the country that had outlawed what he was. Ten years since the massacre at Dravenmoor Keep, since the oaths were broken and the swordspeakers died and Sable Dravenmoor had buried his name so deep even he sometimes couldn't find it. He had been careful. He had been *so careful.* He had never once, in ten years, let a charm do more than look pretty.

And now the world underneath the world was answering prayers, and his knots could feel it coming up through the mud like warmth through floorboards.

"How much?" the girl said.

Sable made himself breathe. Made himself finish the knot — three passes, the old pattern, the one his mother's mother had used, the one that meant *come home whole* in a language that officially had never existed. His fingers knew it without him. That was the trouble with oaths; they lived in the hands long after you'd cut them out of your heart.

"For your father," he said, "two coppers."

She paid without haggling, which told him how frightened she was. Dock folk always haggled. He wrapped the knot in a scrap of oilcloth and held it out, and when her fingers touched his — brief, cold, calloused — the humming in the cord became a chord, low and resonant, and Sable felt the prayer.

Not heard it. *Felt* it. It rose out of the girl herself, out of the tight place behind her sternum where she kept the thing she couldn't say aloud: *let him come back, let him come back, let him come back.* Three words worn smooth as sea glass from repetition. And below the harbor — below the silt and the drowned doors and the bones of the capital that Kestrel's new masters pretended had never been a capital at all — something answered.

The fog over the water shivered.

It was a small thing. Anyone watching would have called it wind. But Sable saw the mist pull inward, all along the quay, drawn toward the girl like breath drawn before a word, and the cord in her hand glowed — not with light, exactly, but with *attention*, the way iron glows before it ever shows color. The girl blinked down at her purchase, puzzled, pleased. "Warm," she said. "Like it's been sat by a fire."

"Don't lose it," Sable managed.

"I won't." She tucked it inside her father's coat, against her own heart, and went up the lane toward the fish market, and the fog closed behind her, and Sable sat very still at his little table with his little charms and understood, with the calm clarity of a man watching the tide come in over his boots, that his life was over.

Not dramatically. Not yet. But the way a rope is over once the strand begins to part — quietly, fiber by fiber, somewhere below the waterline where no one can see.

Because the gods were dead. Everyone knew it. The usurper's histories said so, printed in neat black type: *the sorcerous kingdom of Kestrel was justly destroyed, its gods proved false, its magics extinguished.* The Wardens enforced the knowing. The temples of the Nine kept candles lit for gods they'd long since filed down into safe, toothless virtues — Prudence, Diligence, Order. Nobody prayed anymore, not really. Not the way the girl had prayed, raw and specific and desperate, with her whole chest.

And yet something down in the dark had heard her. Something was waking in the drowned country, and it was answering, and it had reached up through *his* knot to do it — through the old patterns, the true patterns, the ones written into his hands before he was old enough to refuse them. As if his charms had spent ten years being windows painted shut, and now someone was breaking the paint from the other side.

Sable began, mechanically, to pack his table. Cord, sea-glass, the little tin of wax, the folding knife with the worn bone handle. His movements were steady. He was proud of that, later — that his hands stayed steady, even while everything under the harbor was not.

"Marta Voss's girl says you do honest work," said a voice, and he looked up into the brown, weathered face of Ives Rell, smuggler, sometime customer, and the closest thing to a friend that a dead man's shadow could afford. Ives leaned on the chandlery wall with the ease of a man who owned nothing worth confiscating and knew every Warden patrol route in the city by heart. "Got cargo needs moving Thursday, if you're wanting coin. Nothing spicy. Mostly."

"Not today," Sable said.

Ives's eyes narrowed — not much, just the fraction a sailor gives a sky that's gone the wrong color. "You look like a man who's seen weather coming."

"I look like a man selling string."

"Aye," Ives said mildly. "That too." He pushed off the wall, then paused. "Fog's behaving strange this morning. Whole quay's talking about it. Water sang last night, out past the Mole. Old men blaming eels." He grinned, but the grin didn't reach anywhere important. "Thursday, then. If there is a Thursday."

He ambled off into the thinning fog, and Sable stood alone on Tidewrack Lane with his packed table and his hidden oath and the taste of copper at the back of his throat.

Above the chandlery roof, gulls wheeled and screamed, and beneath them — beneath everything — the drowned city lay silent as it had lain for thirty years. But Sable could feel it now, the way you can feel a tooth you've been ignoring: the attention pressing up through the mud, patient, immense, *grateful*. Somewhere below, an old god had received its first honest prayer in a generation, and it had answered through a luck-knot bought for two coppers, and it was not going to stop.

He thought about running. He had a go-bag sewn into the lining of his coat, had had it for ten years; he knew three ways out of the city that didn't pass a gate. He could be on the salt roads by dark, another nameless man in a country of nameless men, and whatever was waking could wake without him.

But the girl's prayer was still in his hands. *Let him come back.* He could still feel the shape of it, worn smooth, and he could feel — horribly, unmistakably — that the answer had passed *through* him, that some part of him had been the door it came through, and that doors, once opened, remember.

A knot doesn't choose what it holds. That was the first lesson of the swordspeakers, the lesson that had gotten them all killed: the binding goes both ways.

Sable looked out at the harbor, at the fog lifting off water that covered a kingdom, and felt the first thread of his careful, cowardly, precious decade come quietly apart.

Down in the deep, something said his name.

Not *Sable*. Not the name he wore now, the flat trade-name he'd taken like a borrowed coat. The old one. The true one. Spoken in a voice like tide withdrawing over stones, gentle as an apology, patient as debt.

*Dravenmoor.*

He picked up his table and went home the long way, avoiding the quays, and all the while the salt wind followed him through the lanes of Salt Revisited like a dog that has finally found its master — and will not, ever again, be sent away.
