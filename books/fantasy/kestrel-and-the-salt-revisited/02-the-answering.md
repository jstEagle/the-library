## 2. The Answering

Ives Rell had stolen ships, cargo, identities, and once — briefly, disastrously — a bishop's mistress, but she had never stolen a tide. So when the harbor began to drain at half past the bell of morning, she stood on the deck of the *Kindly Liar* with her hands full of rope and her mouth full of curses, watching the sea walk backward out of Salt Revisited like a debtor slipping away from a table.

The water went out fast. Not the slow breathing pull of a normal ebb but a rush, a great silver sheet sliding off the mudflats and between the pilings, uncovering things that had no business being uncovered: drowned foundations from the old kingdom, black with weed; a chapel bell fused to its fallen tower; the ribs of something long and deliberate that might have been a ship or might have been a jaw. The mud steamed in the cold air. Gulls wheeled and screamed and would not land.

"Captain," said Fen, her bosun, a woman built like a capstan and about as talkative. "That's wrong."

"Thank you, Fen. I'd mistaken it for Tuesday."

Fen didn't smile. Fen never smiled before noon, and the sun wasn't properly up yet, just a bruise of light behind the cloud-bank to the east. Ives looked out over the exposed harbor floor and felt the particular itch at the base of her skull that had kept her alive through nineteen years of smuggling: the feeling of standing somewhere she was not supposed to be.

Because the water hadn't just gone out. It had gone *out*, as if called. As if something under the harbor had said *come here* in a voice made of pressure and old salt, and the sea, which Ives had trusted her whole life to be indifferent, had obeyed.

On the mudflats below, people were already climbing down. Of course they were. This was Salt Revisited, where half the city made its living picking the pockets of the drowned kingdom, and a drained harbor was a festival, a bounty, a miracle with coins in it. She watched them scatter across the wet gray expanse with lanterns and hooks and sacks, prying at the ruins, and she thought: *run, you idiots.*

Then the tide came back.

It did not come back the way tides come back. There was no gathering swell on the horizon, no warning wall. The sea simply returned all at once, everywhere, like a lid closing, like a hand slapping down on a table — and it came back *higher*, surging up the harbor stairs in a single green-gray breath that took three dockworkers off their feet and carried them, thrashing, into the deep channel between the pilings.

Ives was moving before she decided to move. Nineteen years of instinct put her at the rail with a coil of line in one hand and a boathook in the other, and she threw the line to the first swimmer — a boy, maybe twelve, slick with harbor filth — and hauled him up the hull hand over hand while Fen leaned out and snagged the second by the collar of his coat. The third, an old woman in a fishwife's shawl, had gone under twice already when Ives tied the line around her own waist and went down the side into water cold enough to stop her heart if she let it.

She caught the woman by the hair. That was all. No heroics, no skill — just a fistful of wet gray hair and the line, and Fen hauling them both up the hull like landed fish, and then Ives was on her deck again, coughing brine, with three strangers dripping on her planks and the harbor lying flat and calm as a millpond, as if nothing had happened at all.

Except that everything had happened, and everyone on the waterfront had seen it. Seen the impossible tide. Seen the *Liar*'s captain go into the water after a stranger, which was itself so far outside the natural order that several onlookers crossed themselves with the wrong hand, using gestures from the old faith, the outlawed one.

"Captain," Fen said quietly, behind her. "Your hands."

Ives looked down. Her palms were glowing. Faintly, coldly, a blue-white shimmer like moonlight on ice, fading even as she watched — and beneath it, for one heartbeat, she felt something answer. Something vast and patient underneath the harbor, turning its attention toward her the way a whale turns toward a falling coin. A voice that was not a voice, in a place that was not a place:

***Heard.***

Then it was gone, and her hands were just hands, shaking, smelling of rope and harbor water.

"I'm going below," Ives said. "Nobody speak to me."

* * *

Across the city, the prayers were being answered.

In the shrine-row behind the tanneries, where the godless of Salt Revisited left offerings out of habit rather than faith — a cup of wine for a dead mother, a carved bird for a lost son — the offerings were vanishing. Not stolen. *Accepted.* The wine cups emptied themselves upward. The carved birds turned their heads to follow passersby.

At the lamplighter's guildhall, a journeyman knelt in the stairwell where his daughter had died of lung-rot two winters past and whispered the forbidden words of the dusk-prayer, the ones that got your tongue marked if an inquisitor heard them, and every lamp in the district lit itself at once, at midmorning, burning white and cold, and stayed lit until he stopped weeping.

In the tenements off Rope Walk, a fevered child woke whole and asking for bread while the fever-shed around her emptied of sickness like a jug poured clean, and the mothers who had prayed over her fell to their knees in the muck of the street, laughing and sobbing in equal measure, and the neighbors who had not prayed backed away from them slowly, because answered prayers were worse than unanswered ones. Answered prayers meant someone was listening. And everyone in Kestrel knew what happened when the old gods listened: the usurper's inquisitors came, and iron came with them.

And in the market square by the customs house, in a narrow stall hung with knots and charms and cheap luck-sigils, a fire burned that gave no heat.

Ives saw it herself, an hour later, because of course she went — because the boy she'd pulled from the harbor, whose name was Tam and who would not stop thanking her in a whisper like a prayer, had grabbed her sleeve and pointed and said, "That's him. That's the knot-seller. He's the one the water was talking about."

The stall was easy to find because of the crowd, and the crowd was easy to find because they were standing very still, the way crowds stand around a snake. In the middle of them, the charm-stall burned with cold fire — pale blue-white flames licking along the awning poles, across the display of knotted cords, up the sleeves of the man himself, and nothing charred, nothing blackened, nothing warm. The flames cast no shadow. They bent toward the crowd like curious animals.

The knot-seller stood in the heart of it with his arms slightly away from his body, palms out, wearing the expression of a man reading his own death warrant and finding a clerical error in it. He was younger than Ives expected. Ordinary-looking, dark-haired, with the careful blankness of someone who had spent a decade practicing how to be overlooked. His wares — luck-knots, salt-charms, little woven promises — floated gently in the cold fire, turning like things suspended in water.

"It's Dravenmoor," someone breathed. "The last swordspeaker."

"No," said the knot-seller, in a voice flat with exhaustion. "There are no swordspeakers. There's no such thing. I sell knots."

"You sold me a knot," said a girl at the front of the crowd, a dockworker's daughter with a chipped tooth and fierce eyes, holding up a cord that now glowed softly in her fist like a captive star. "You told me it was for safe travel. My da's boat came through the tide without a scratch. Everyone else's didn't."

"That was luck," Sable said.

"That was *you*."

The cold fire flared. Every head in the crowd turned — including, Ives noted grimly, the heads of two men in gray coats near the customs house steps, men with the bored posture of officials memorizing faces. Iron would come. Iron always came. The usurper's writ ran in Salt Revisited same as anywhere, and the penalty for the old arts was the tongue-mark, the pyre, or the long questioning, depending on the mood of whoever held the irons.

Sable saw the gray coats too. Ives watched him see them — watched the calculation move behind his eyes like a fish under ice. Ten years of hiding, she thought. Ten years of selling charms that worked just well enough to be dismissed as luck. And now the sky itself had torn his name off him and nailed it up in the marketplace.

He could run. He was already turning to run.

Tam got there first. The boy Ives had hauled from the harbor pushed through the crowd, soaked to the waist, and planted himself in front of the stall like a fence post, and said, loudly, in the ringing silence: "He saved us. The water went out and came back and it was going to take us, and something *answered*, and it was him. It was him the whole time."

Every eye swung to Ives. She cursed inwardly with the fluency of a woman who had sworn oaths in four languages. She should have walked away. Nineteen years of survival said walk away, say nothing, be nobody. But the boy was dripping on the cobbles, and the girl with the glowing knot was looking at the knot-seller like he was the last door out of a burning house, and the cold fire was bending toward Ives now, toward her salt-wet sleeves, recognizing her, *acknowledging* her, and that vast patient attention under the harbor turned its face toward her again like a sun coming up.

***Heard. Also you.***

"Oh, bugger," Ives Rell said, aloud, in front of forty witnesses.

The gray coats started down the steps.

What happened next happened fast, and Ives would spend the rest of the evening insisting she had planned none of it. The knot-seller moved — quick, economical, a swordspeaker's economy even stripped of swords — sweeping his floating charms into a sack with one hand while the other closed around Tam's shoulder. The girl with the knot grabbed his sleeve. The gray coats broke into a run. And Ives, whose ship was berthed thirty yards away and whose entire career was built on moving contraband quickly through hostile streets, heard herself shout, "Alley! Now! All of you!" and found herself leading a fugitive charm-seller and two children through the back ways of the customs quarter like the world's most reluctant shepherd.

They came out on the *Liar*'s deck breathless, with the gray coats' shouts fading behind them and the hatch covers dropped. Sable stood in the middle of her deck, dripping cold fire that was finally guttering out, and looked at her with an expression she recognized because she wore it daily: the look of a person whose life has just been rearranged without consultation.

"I can pay," he said.

"With what? Luck?" She wrung seawater from her braid. "You've got about as much of that as I do right now."

"I can pay in coin. And I need passage. Out of the city, or—" he hesitated, and the hesitation was interesting, "—or wherever the road goes. They'll have my description by nightfall. By tomorrow they'll have my name, and the name is the dangerous part."

"The name," Ives said, "being Dravenmoor."

Something flickered across his face — grief, or fear, or the memory of both. "Being Dravenmoor," he agreed quietly.

Tam sat down abruptly on a coil of rope, shivering, and the girl — her name was Wren, no, wait, the girl said her name was Ash, though she glanced sideways when she said it, the way people do when names are recent purchases — the girl sat beside him and held up her glowing knot like a votive lamp.

"They're answering everybody," Ash said. "All over the city. Prayers. Did you feel it? Under the water. Like something waking up."

"I felt it," Ives said sourly. "It said *heard*. To *me*. I don't pray. I've never prayed in my life. What business does anything have hearing *me*?"

"Maybe," Sable said slowly, looking at his own hands, at the last blue ghost of the cold fire fading from his knuckles, "it wasn't answering what you asked for. Maybe it answers what you'd give." He looked up at her, and for the first time since the marketplace there was something in his face besides dread — something tired and stubborn and horribly like responsibility. "You went into the water for a stranger. I've spent ten years making sure I wouldn't be seen doing exactly that."

"Aye, well." Ives turned away, busying herself with rope that didn't need her. "Someone had to. You were busy being a rumor."

Behind her, she heard him laugh — a short, cracked sound, more surprise than humor. Then, quieter, almost to himself: "Ten years. And it ends in one morning, because the gods forgot how to sleep."

Above the harbor, the gulls had finally landed. Below it, in the deep channel where the tide had swallowed its own miracle, something vast turned over in the dark, satisfied, and began — patiently, terribly — to listen harder.

And on the customs house steps, unnoticed among the dispersing crowd, a woman in traveling grays folded a paper bearing a charcoal sketch of a knot-seller's face, tucked it into her coat, and set off through the streets of Salt Revisited at an unhurried walk, as though she had all the time in the world.

As though she already knew exactly where he would run.
