## 3. Inquisitor Ashen

The ship that brought her to Salt Revisited was called the *Merciful*, which Wren Ashen thought was either a joke or a threat, depending on who had named it. She stood at the rail as the harbor opened before her, gray water under a gray sky, and watched gulls wheel over the drowned quarter where old Kestrel's spires still broke the surface at low tide like the fingers of something patient.

Ten years since she had seen this city. Ten years since she had seen anything of the country she'd been born in — except that Kestrel no longer existed. That was the first law of the Protectorate: there was no Kestrel. There were only provinces, and ports, and the salt roads between them, all of it administered by the usurper's clerks with their neat ledgers and their neater fires.

She wore iron now. Not the soft iron of a soldier's fittings but the worked, blessed, terrible iron of an inquisitor's regalia: collar and cuffs etched with the Nine Prohibitions, a breastplate polished to a mirror so that anyone speaking to her would see only themselves, warped. Her hair was cropped short beneath the hood. Her name was not Wren anymore. Names were how magic found you; names were doors. She had burned hers in a brazier at the Citadel and been given *Ashen* in its place, and she had told herself the ash was clean.

The dockworkers parted for her without being told to. They always did. Fear traveled ahead of iron like a smell.

"Inquisitor." The harbormaster was a fat man sweating through his finery despite the cold, bowing too many times. "We hadn't word of your coming."

"No," she said. "You wouldn't have." She let him sweat a moment longer. "There was a tide two nights past. It came up the harbor against the moon. It took the fishmarket and left everything else standing. Explain that to me."

He explained nothing well. He spoke of undertows, of seismic tremors far out in the deep water, of a wharf that had rotted through. She listened with her hands folded and let him finish, because letting people finish their lies was a courtesy she still permitted herself, one of the last ones.

"And the cold fire?" she said. "Witnesses speak of a stall on Tanner's Row that burned with it. Blue flame, frost on the cobbles in high summer. Three people pulled from a collapsing building by a man whose hands were full of light."

"The witnesses are confused, Inquisitor. Frightened people see—"

"Yes," she said. "They do." She turned from him and looked down the long curve of the waterfront, at the stalls and the smoke and the small busy lives stacked on top of a drowned kingdom like barnacles on a hull. Somewhere down there was a man who made luck-knots and sold them for coppers, and whose charms had stopped being charms three nights ago and started being answers.

She knew his hands. She had tied knots with those hands, once, in another life, in a country that officially did not exist.

* * *

The interrogation room they gave her in the harbormaster's hall was really just a storeroom with a table dragged in, smelling of tar and old rope. They brought the man to her in chains, though she had asked for no chains, and she looked at him and felt the familiar tired anger at the machinery she served: it chained everyone, even the innocent, especially the innocent, because innocence was harder to frighten and fear was the whole of its grammar.

His name was Aldous Ferrin. Dockworker. Forty years old. A daughter, eleven, who had bought a luck-knot from a stall on Tanner's Row the morning before the tide.

"Tell me about the knot," Wren said.

Aldous Ferrin told her about the knot. He told it badly and at length, the way frightened men do, circling the thing itself as if it were a hole in the floor. But the shape of it came through: a cord braided in seven strands, sold by a quiet man with gray in his beard who had looked at her — at the daughter — and said, *This will hold.* And when the tide came up the harbor like a hand closing, when the water took the fishmarket and the girl was swept off the quay into the black churn of it, the knot in her fist had gone warm. Had held. Had hauled her back against the current as if the sea itself had changed its mind.

"She says it whispered," Ferrin whispered himself, as if the room might overhear. "In the water. She says it said her name."

Wren wrote nothing down. She wanted very much to write nothing down forever.

"Describe the man who sold it."

"Ordinary. Kind eyes, maybe. Tired. He charged her two coppers and wouldn't take more." Ferrin's voice cracked. "Inquisitor, is my daughter in trouble? Is she marked? The priest at the temple of the Nine — he looked at her and crossed himself twice—"

"Your daughter is a child who nearly drowned," Wren said, "and was saved. That is all she is. Go home."

He stared at her. Inquisitors did not say *go home*. Inquisitors said *remain available*. But she was already looking past him at the wall, at the map of the province pinned there with its neat provincial borders drawn over the bones of Kestrel, and Ferrin understood, in the way of small men before large mercies, that he had been given something rare, and he took it and ran.

When the door closed she sat alone for a long time with her hands flat on the table.

*This will hold.*

She could hear the voice saying it. Not the memory of it — memory would have been bearable. She could hear it *now*, faintly, the way you can sometimes hear a bell after it has stopped ringing, if the bell loved you enough.

* * *

She found the stall at dusk, when Tanner's Row was emptying and the lamps were being lit one by one down its crooked length. The stall was shuttered. Of course it was. A man whose charms had started answering prayers from below the harbor would be a fool to keep selling, and Sable Dravenmoor had never been a fool. That had been the trouble with him. Everyone else in their oath-company had been fools in some proportion or other — brave fools, loyal fools, fools who died with the oaths still on their lips — and Sable alone had understood that survival required a kind of cowardice, and had carried that understanding like a wound ever since.

She stood before the shutters a while. Then she walked around to the narrow door at the back, the one that opened onto the alley, and knocked: twice, pause, once. The old signal. *It's me. It's safe. Come out.*

She heard nothing. Then, after a silence long enough to hold an entire decade, the bolt drew back.

He looked older. That was the first stupid observation, and she hated herself for it. Gray at the temples, lines around the eyes carved by ten years of squinting at customers over cheap cord. He wore a dockworker's coat. His hands — the hands that had once moved faster than any blade in the nine provinces, the hands that had spoken swords the way priests spoke scripture — hung at his sides like ordinary tools.

"Wren," he said.

"Ashen," she corrected, and heard how hollow it sounded, and pressed on anyway. "Inquisitor Ashen, of the Citadel. You'll address me by my office."

"I'll address you by your name," Sable said quietly, "because I'm the last person alive who has the right to it. Come inside before someone sees iron on Tanner's Row."

* * *

The room above the stall was small and clean and almost bare: a cot, a stove, a chest, cords of every color hanging from the beams in careful braids, waiting to be luck. He poured her tea without asking, in a clay cup chipped at the rim, and she sat across from him with her breastplate catching the lamplight and throwing his own face back at him, warped, and thought: *this is what I am to him now. A mirror that lies.*

"You know why I'm here," she said.

"The tide. The fire. The girl." He wrapped his hands around his own cup. "I know why you're here better than you do, maybe. Something under the harbor has woken up, Wren. The gods. The real ones, the drowned ones, the ones this country drowned along with its name. They're answering prayers again. All of them. Everywhere. And my charms—" his mouth twisted, "—my charms aren't mine anymore. They're doors. Every knot I tie opens onto something listening."

"That is precisely what the Citadel believes," she said, "and precisely why I was sent. There are protocols, Sable. There are procedures written in blood for exactly this. A swordspeaker found alive, in the dead country, with his craft answering to the old gods — do you understand what they'll do? Not kill you. Killing is quick and the Citadel wastes nothing. They'll take you apart slowly to learn how the doors work, and then they'll take the city apart to learn why, and then—"

"Then why are you telling me this instead of doing it?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside a drunk was singing a song about a girl from the free cities, badly.

"Because I am giving you until dawn," Wren said, and her voice did not shake, because she had spent ten years learning to make sure it didn't. "Because my report will state that the seller of the luck-knot could not be located, that the trail went cold at the harbor, that I require reinforcements before proceeding. Reinforcements take time to arrive. Boats are slow. Bureaucracy is slower." She set down the cup. "By the time they come, you should be on a boat of your own, or gone inland, or anywhere that isn't here. Because they won't send me next time, Sable. They'll send someone who never tied knots with you. Someone who never—"

She stopped. He was watching her with those tired kind eyes, and she found she could not finish the sentence, because the end of it was *someone who doesn't love you*, and love was not a word that existed in the Nine Prohibitions, and she had burned her name to keep from having to say things like it.

"There's a smuggler," Sable said at last. "Ives Rell. Runs the causeways. If I go, I don't go alone — there's a boy, Tam, god-touched, the gods are already talking to him. And a priest. Odo. A deserter whose god answers him whether he wants it or not."

"You've gathered them already." She laughed, and it came out broken. "Of course you have. Ten years hiding and the moment the sky cracks open you start collecting strays."

"It's what we swore," he said simply. "Once. To stand between the fire and whoever's nearest."

"The oaths died with Kestrel."

"Then it's habit," Sable said. "But it's a strong one."

She stood. She straightened her collar, and the etched prohibitions caught the light, and she made herself look at him one last time across the little room that smelled of tea and rope and a life half-lived in hiding.

"If you run," she said, "run toward something. Not away. The Citadel hunts the running ones hardest." She paused at the door, her hand on the frame, not turning around. "And Sable. When you find whatever it is the gods want you to find — the Doorway, the gate, whatever the old stories call it —"

"How do you know about the Doorway?"

She was silent a moment too long, and he read it, because he had always been able to read her, curse him.

"Because I've read the same stories," she said. "And because part of me — the part that isn't iron yet — hopes you find it. And the rest of me has orders to make sure you don't." She stepped out into the dark of the alley. "Dawn, Sable. Don't waste the night."

The bolt slid home behind her. She stood in the cold with her breath steaming and let herself feel it — all of it, the grief she had dressed as duty for ten years, the sisterhood drowned with the kingdom, the name she had burned coming back to her like smoke.

Then Inquisitor Ashen walked back toward the harbor lights, and began composing the report that would lie to the Citadel, and wondered, with the first honest fear she had felt in a decade, what she would do when the orders came that she could no longer refuse.

Behind her, in the dark above Tanner's Row, a lamp went out — and somewhere beneath the harbor, something ancient turned over in its sleep, and began, gently, to listen to her too.
