## 4. The Deserter's Psalm

They walked out of Salt Revisited before the ash stopped falling, and Sable counted the bells behind them like a man counting his own heartbeats.

Three days north along the drowned causeway, where the old kingdom's roads ran knee-deep in brine and the salt had eaten the milestones down to blind white teeth. Ives Rell set a pace that was half smuggler's efficiency and half spite, as if she could outwalk the memory of cold fire climbing her rigging. Tam said nothing at all, which frightened Sable more than talking would have. Children who went quiet after seeing magic were either broken or changing, and he did not know which prayer to make.

He made neither. His charms had stopped being his.

On the third evening the causeway climbed out of the water onto a shelf of black rock, and there — absurd, impossible, stubborn as a weed through flagstone — stood a chapel. It was small enough that a giant could have worn it as a hat, built of the same dark stone as the causeway itself, its single bell tower leaning like a drunk telling a secret. Light showed in one window. Smoke from one chimney. A garden of gray-green herbs growing in soil someone had carried up from the mainland, sack by sack, because nothing grew in Kestrel anymore except guilt and saltgrass.

"I know this place," Ives said, and spat with professional disapproval. "The mad priest. Odo. He buried three of my crew once — drowned runners the usurper's patrols cut down. Wouldn't take payment. Said the sea owed *him*."

"Does it?" Tam asked. The first words in hours.

"Everyone owes everyone something on this coast, sprat." She looked at Sable sidelong. "Question is what we're carrying that the sea might want back."

Sable looked at his own hands. Under the skin, faint as veins in marble, the old oath-marks still glimmered when he let them — the script of a kingdom that no longer existed, written by gods who had spent ten years being politely dead. Since the harbor, since the tide, they hadn't stopped. He kept them wrapped in linen now, like wounds, or like evidence.

"We need walls for one night," he said. "And I need to ask a priest about doors."

* * *

Brother Odo turned out to be enormous — a bear of a man gone soft at the edges, bald as an egg, with forearms tattooed in the faded blue script of the old liturgy, which was illegal in six ways and which he wore openly, rolled to the elbow, like a dare. He took one look at the three of them standing in his doorway — the smuggler with salt-crusted knives, the child with hollow eyes, the stranger with bandaged hands — and stepped aside without a word, the way men do when they've been expecting company for years.

"You'll eat," he said. "You'll sleep. In the morning you can lie to me about who you are, if it makes you feel holy."

The chapel smelled of tallow and dried fish and old incense, the sweet ghost of a faith the country had outlawed so thoroughly that even the word *temple* had been struck from the maps. Odo fed them barley soup with seaweed in it, and Tam ate three bowls with the desperate mechanical hunger of a child who had learned not to trust the next meal, and Ives interrogated the priest about tides and patrol routes under the cover of asking about weather, and Sable sat with his bandaged hands in his lap and felt the chapel listening to him.

That was the thing he couldn't explain yet. Walls didn't listen. Stones didn't listen. But this chapel did — or something beneath it did, something patient and vast and *below*, the same below that had answered the dockworker's daughter's prayer through him, the same below that had sent the tide. The whole coast of dead Kestrel was a held breath, and Sable could feel it wanting to exhale.

"You feel it too," Odo said quietly, later, when Ives had taken Tam up to the loft and the lamp had been trimmed low. "Don't insult us both by denying it."

Sable looked at the priest's tattooed forearms. "How long?"

"Since the night the harbor answered." Odo turned his hands over, palms up, and Sable saw that the blue liturgy-script was moving — slowly, gently, like kelp in current, the letters rearranging themselves into prayers Odo had never learned. "Forty years I served a god who never once spoke. That was the bargain. That was the *comfort* of it. You pour out your heart into a silence, and the silence holds it, and nothing comes back to demand anything of you." His voice cracked, just slightly, on a laugh. "Now it answers. Every psalm I sing, something sings back. Every blessing I give, it *takes me up on*. Last week a fisherman prayed through me for his nets mended, and my hands moved without me and mended them. I haven't slept properly since. I am a door that keeps opening when I knock, and I never asked to be a door."

Sable unwound the linen from his hands.

For a long moment the two of them sat in lamplight, a hidden swordspeaker and a deserter priest, showing each other their marks like lepers comparing sores. Odo studied the oath-script crawling over Sable's knuckles and wrists and did not flinch, which Sable realized he had been bracing for — flinching, or worse, kneeling.

"Swordspeaker," Odo said at last. Not a question.

"The last one." Sable flexed his fingers. The marks glowed faintly, answering the chapel's attention. "There were nine hundred of us when the oaths were sworn. There was one of me when the fires ended it. I've spent ten years selling luck-knots and pretending the script on my hands is a birthmark."

"And now the gods are waking up underneath the harbor, and your pretending has come due." Odo nodded slowly, unsurprised, unsurprising. "Yes. That's how it always goes. Grace doesn't ask permission. That's what makes it grace, and that's what makes it terrible."

"I need to know," Sable said, and heard his own voice go rough, "about a door."

Odo went very still.

Then he rose, took the lamp, and led Sable down a stair cut into the chapel's living rock — down past a crypt where three drowned runners slept under unmarked stones, down to a wall where forty years of chalked prayers covered the stone floor to ceiling, thousands of them, layered like guano, like sediment, like the patience of grief. Odo set the lamp on a niche and touched the oldest layer of chalk, faded almost to nothing.

"When the usurper burned the temples and struck Kestrel from the maps," he said, "the priests who survived went underground. Literally. And they wrote down everything the fires would have eaten. Most of it was lost anyway — copied onto paper, paper rots. But some of it got memorized, and memorized things get passed mouth to mouth, and mouths are harder to burn than books." He tapped the wall. "One of the things that came down to me, priest to priest, forty hands deep, is a legend. The Doorway."

"Tell me."

"The old capital — Sunken Vael, they call it now, though its name was Vael-Under-Kestrel, city of a hundred gates — went into the sea when the kingdom fell. Not all at once. Slowly, over a century, street by street, until only the towers' tops broke the water like drowning men's fingers. But the priests taught that the gods, before they went silent, built one last gate in the deepest part of the drowned city. Not a gate of stone. A gate of *oath*. And it stands open always — but nothing can pass through it, in or out, because passing requires a toll that no one has ever been able to pay." Odo turned and looked at Sable, and his eyes in the lamplight were terribly kind. "The gate opens only for a named soul willing to surrender name and oath both. To become, itself, the passage. To stop being a man and become a way through."

The chapel above them seemed to lean closer. Sable became aware that the chalked prayers around them had begun, very faintly, to glow — every one of them, thousands of prayers, answering the word *Doorway* like iron filings answering a magnet.

"That's not hope," Sable said. "That's a eulogy with better architecture."

"It's both. Everything worth having is both." Odo picked up the lamp. "I'm not telling you because I think you should pay it. I'm telling you because you asked, and because a man deserves to know the shape of the trap he's already caught in. You've been running from your oath for ten years, swordspeaker. Now you know where it ends."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat in the loft near Tam instead, watching the boy breathe, and somewhere past midnight the boy's eyes opened in the dark — fully awake, instantly, the way soldiers wake — and fixed on Sable.

"They talk in my head," Tam said. No fear in it. Just report, flat as a ledger. "Since the harbor. They say the water remembers. They say—" the boy frowned, working at it, "—they say the door knows your name already. They said it before the priest told you. I thought you knew."

Sable's chest went tight and cold. "What else do they say?"

Tam considered. "They say you keep saying *no* to things you already decided."

From the loft's little window, Sable could see the coast road below, moonlit and empty — and then, far off, not empty: a line of lanterns swinging in formation, patient as a rosary being counted. Patrol route. Or search pattern. He thought of Wren — of Ashen, rather, the name she wore now like armor over the wound of her real one — and wondered if she had reached Salt Revisited yet, and whether she was reading his trail in the ashes of his stall, and whether her orders allowed her the mercy of being slow.

Below, faintly, Brother Odo had begun to sing the dawn psalm hours early — off-key, defiant, a big man's voice filling a small chapel. And beneath the singing, unmistakably, something vast and patient sang back, harmonizing, and the chalked prayers on the crypt wall glowed through the floorboards like embers under ash.

Tam sat up beside him. "So," the boy said. "When do we leave for the sunken city?"

Sable looked at the lanterns on the road, and at the glowing floor, and at the child who heard gods the way other children heard rain.

"Soon," he said, and hated how much it sounded like a vow.
