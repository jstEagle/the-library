## 5. Salt Roads

The causeways out of Salt Revisited were built for pilgrims, back when Kestrel had gods it admitted to and roads it maintained. Now they were broken spines of granite running through drowned farmland, and Ives Rell hated every furlong of them.

Not because of the water. Water she understood. Water kept its promises — it rose, it fell, it took what you gave it and gave back what it took, eventually, in pieces if necessary. No, what she hated was the *openness* of the causeway: a mile in any direction with nothing to hide behind but reeds and your own good sense, and somewhere behind them, always, the sound of conscription drums.

"Left side," Tam said, without looking up from the puddle he was studying.

Ives looked left. Nothing. She looked right. A sweep boat, maybe four hundred yards out on the flooded barley fields, six soldiers poling it along, one of them holding a lantern against the grey morning like it might confess something.

"How," she said, "do you keep doing that."

Tam shrugged. He was fourteen, all elbows and secrets, and he'd been doing this for two days now — knowing things. Which way the patrols would come. Where the causeway bridge had rotted through. That the third farmhouse past the salt flats had a dog, and the dog was friendly, and the dog's owner was not.

"You walk lighter than the rest of us," Sable offered from the rear. He said it mildly, the way he said everything, as though the world were a slightly disappointing meal he'd agreed to finish anyway. "Some people are just quiet."

"I'm not quiet," Tam said. "I'm *listening.*"

Ives stopped walking. "Listening to what?"

Tam's mouth opened. Closed. He looked at Sable, and something passed between them that Ives didn't have a name for and didn't like having to watch. Then the boy said, "The water," and walked on, and Sable followed him, and Ives stood alone on a dead pilgrim road with a sweep boat full of soldiers drifting closer and thought, with the particular clarity of a captain whose crew has stopped being cargo: *this is going to get us all killed.*

She caught up at the ruined tollhouse, where the causeway narrowed between two leaning arches. The soldiers were behind them now, or beside them, hidden by the arches' shadow. Sable pressed his palm flat against the old toll-stone as they passed — an old habit, Ives guessed, some swordspeaker courtesy to a gate that no longer asked for tolls — and for half a heartbeat the stone *glowed*, faint as foxfire, and the water beneath the arches went still as glass.

Then it was just a wet rock again, and Sable was just a tired man with a sword-shaped secret, and Ives decided, firmly, not to have seen anything.

* * *

They made camp that night in the ribs of a granary, half-drowned, its upper floor tilted like a ship run aground. Ives got a fire going small and smokeless, the way her father had taught her — green reeds, dry dung, patience — and Odo heated water for the barley porridge that was rapidly becoming the only food any of them could agree on.

"The salt roads were processional ways," Odo said, stirring. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with the permanent wince of someone whose god had recently started talking to him mid-sermon. "Pilgrims walked them barefoot, you know. From the coast all the way to Dravenmoor Keep. Barefoot, in winter, singing."

"Singing," Ives said. "In winter. Barefoot."

"Devotion is not comfortable."

"No," Sable said quietly, "it isn't."

The fire popped. Tam lay on his back watching the hole where the roof used to be, counting stars or listening to whatever the water was telling him now. Ives found herself studying Sable across the flames — the way he sat with his back to the wall and his knees loose, the way his eyes never settled anywhere longer than three heartbeats. Ten years she'd known him, off and on, buying charms, selling passage, sharing storms. Ten years of a man who was a locked room, and in the last week the door had started coming off its hinges.

"The keep," she said. "What exactly are we walking into?"

"Ruins," Sable said.

"Ruins of *what?*"

He turned his hands over, palms up, as if the answer might be written there. "A massacre."

Nobody spoke. Even Tam stopped counting stars.

"It was the end of the oaths," Sable went on, and his voice had the flat evenness of a man reading a ledger he wished were someone else's. "Kestrel outlawed magic, and then it discovered that outlawing a thing doesn't kill it, it just drives it indoors. So they gathered everyone who still held an oath — swordspeakers, stormcallers, hearthbinders, all of them — into Dravenmoor Keep, and told them it was for their protection." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "It was easier to burn one building than a thousand."

"And you weren't inside," Ives said.

"I was outside." He fed a stick to the fire. "I've spent ten years not talking about why."

Odo cleared his throat. "There's a legend about the keep," he offered, gently, the way a priest offers anything — like a hand extended over a gap. "That the walls remember. That what happened there never finished happening. Pilgrims used to say you could hear the oaths being spoken again if you stood in the great hall at dusk—"

"I know the legend," Sable said.

"Right," Odo said, and busied himself with the porridge.

Tam sat up suddenly. "It's true, though."

"Tam," Ives warned.

"It's true." The boy's voice had gone strange, thin, pitched to a frequency Ives didn't care for. "The walls aren't remembering. They're *waiting*. There's—" He pressed both hands flat against the tilted floorboards, as if steadying himself against a swell only he could feel. "There's something under the salt. Under everything. It's been asleep for ten years and it's waking up and it knows our names. It knows *his* name." He pointed at Sable without looking at him. "It says it first. Every time. It says *Sable Dravenmoor* like it's ringing a bell."

The fire guttered. Not the wind — Ives checked, automatically, twenty years of deck-sense — the wind hadn't moved. The flame simply bent away from the boy, the way iron filings bend from a lodestone, and then recovered, and burned on.

Sable reached over and put his hand on Tam's shoulder. Whatever passed through that contact, it settled the boy like ballast settles a hull. "When it gets loud," Sable said, "you tell me. Not the others. Me. Can you do that?"

"Why not the others?"

"Because the others will worry," Sable said, "and worrying is my job."

"That's not a job," Tam muttered. But he lay back down, and within minutes his breathing went long and even, the way it only did when he felt safe, which Ives noted, with some irritation, was not a feeling the boy had ever displayed in her presence before tonight.

* * *

Second day out, they nearly died twice.

The first time was the conscription sweep at Marrow Ford — a chain of soldiers strung across the causeway with pikes and a sergeant with a ledger, taking names and taking boys. They hid in a drainage culvert while boots crossed overhead, close enough that Ives could smell the tallow on their lanterns, and Tam put his mouth against the wet stone and *hummed*, low, and the water in the culvert rose around them like a curtain, muffling sound, hiding heat, and when the soldiers passed the culvert was empty of anything worth reporting.

Ives didn't ask. She was learning not to ask.

The second time was the bridge at Weeping Arch, which had rotted since the last maps were drawn, and would have dropped Ives into thirty feet of black floodwater if Sable hadn't caught her wrist — fast, impossibly fast, faster than a man should move — and held her over the gap while Odo and Tam hauled her back.

"You're welcome," Sable said.

"Don't make it strange," Ives said, heart hammering, dignity in ruins.

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"You're making it strange right now."

"A little," he admitted.

And that was the moment, absurdly — hanging off a dead bridge over drowned barley with her wrist in the grip of the last swordspeaker in Kestrel — that Ives Rell understood what was happening to her crew. To her *family*, said the word in her head, uninvited, and refused to leave. Somewhere between Salt Revisited and here, between the luck-knots and the answered prayers and the burning stall, these three impossible people had become hers, and she had become theirs, and there was no chart for that, no tide-table, no way to plot a course back to the safe shallow water where everyone was alone.

She hated it. She'd have cut out her tongue before admitting otherwise.

* * *

Third night, the rain came, and they sheltered in the shell of a chapel dedicated to a god whose name the usurper had struck from every register. Odo knew it anyway. Of course he did.

"Vess," he said, tracing the worn hollow where the altar-stone's face had been. "God of thresholds. Doorways. Crossings." He laughed, short and unhappy. "My god keeps mentioning him lately. Keeps saying we should pay our respects."

"Your god talks too much," Ives said, wringing out her cloak.

"My god," Odo said, "has recently developed opinions. There's a difference."

They ate. The rain drummed. And then Tam, who had been silent by the fire for an hour, said: "It's getting louder."

Sable set down his bowl. "How loud?"

"Loud enough that I can't pretend it's the water anymore." The boy hugged his knees. "It's not just words now. It's — directions. It wants us to go a certain way tomorrow. Off the causeway, north, through the sunken orchards. It says the sweeps won't look there because the trees are cursed." He glanced up, defiant, daring anyone to laugh. "It says *please*. Did you know gods say please? Because I didn't. I thought they'd be more — I don't know. Thunderous."

"What happens if we don't go the way it wants?" Ives asked.

Tam considered this with the gravity of a boy negotiating with the sea itself. "I think it finds another way to ask," he said. "And I don't think I'd like the other way."

Silence, except the rain. Then Sable nodded slowly, and Ives watched him weigh it — not whether to believe the boy, she realized, but how much of himself it would cost to act on it.

"We go through the orchards," Sable said.

"You're taking route advice from a haunted child," Ives said.

"I'm taking route advice from the only member of this company the country itself is talking to," Sable said. "You have a better navigator?"

Ives opened her mouth. Closed it. "I hate this expedition," she announced, to no one, to everyone, and pulled her cloak around her shoulders, and took the first watch, because that was what captains did — they stood awake so the people who belonged to them could sleep.

* * *

Near dawn, the rain stopped, and the sky over the drowned orchards went the color of a healing bruise, and Ives saw them.

Trees standing in black water to their shoulders, hundreds of them, apple and quince, dead and yet somehow still bearing fruit — pale, bloated, luminous fruit that glowed faintly against the greying light, each one like a lantern hung in a drowned garden. The whole orchard breathed mist. And beneath it, beneath the water, beneath everything, Ives felt — actually *felt*, for the first time, in her own chest, no boy required — a vast attention turning toward them like a lighthouse beam.

Tam took her hand. She let him.

"It says you're allowed to be afraid," the boy reported. "It says fear is just belonging, arriving early."

Ahead of them, Sable Dravenmoor drew his sword an inch from its sheath — checking, perhaps, whether it still answered him, or whether it had begun answering something older — and Ives saw his face in the pale fruit-light, and it was the face of a man hearing his own name called from very far below.

"Keep moving," Sable said. "The keep remembers we're coming."
