## 7. Iron and Old Names

The keep found her before she found it.

Wren Ashen had ridden through the drowned country for three days, and the whole way the salt had been speaking — not to her, never to her, she was iron and iron did not listen — but around her, in the gulls and the wind off the causeways, a language she almost remembered. She had been someone else once who understood it. That girl had died at fourteen, on the night of the oaths, and what rose from her ashes wore the usurper's gray and answered only to *Inquisitor*.

She rode anyway. Toward Dravenmoor Keep, where the reports said a swordspeaker had been seen. Where the reports said miracles were pooling like rainwater in the ruins.

Where Sable was.

She knew it the way you know a scar is yours.

* * *

They came at dusk, when the light went the color of old pewter and the flooded courtyard held the sky upside down. Wren dismounted at the broken gate with six soldiers behind her and her writ of seizure in her breast pocket, and she stood a long moment listening to the keep breathe.

It was not silent. Ruins should be silent. This one murmured. The walls had their voices back, whatever the gods were doing now that they had woken, and if she pressed her palm flat against the gatestone she could feel something underneath the stone like a pulse under skin.

"Stay close," she told her soldiers. "The man we want is dangerous, but he is one man. The priest may be worse."

Brother Odo. The deserter whose god had started answering him against his will — she'd read the interrogations from the temple at Vail, four priests dead of joy before anyone thought to ask what they'd been praying for. Wren had read those pages twice. There was a part of her, the part buried deepest under the iron, that wanted to meet such a man the way a drowning woman wants to meet the sea.

She hated that part. She fed it nothing. She marched it forward instead.

They found them in the great hall, or what remained of it — the roof open to the darkening sky, the floor still bearing the ghost-lines of where the oath-tables had stood a hundred years ago. Four figures by a small fire: a broad smuggler woman with a captain's knife already drawn; a boy, thin as a gull's leg, standing half in front of an older man; the older man himself, gray at the temples, hands empty and open, exactly as she remembered them.

Sable Dravenmoor looked up at her across the ruined hall, and his face did the thing she had dreaded for ten years. It softened.

"Wren," he said. Her old name, out loud, in front of her soldiers, in front of the gods. It went through the iron like water through a hull.

"Inquisitor Ashen," she said, and heard how much colder her voice had to work to be. "By authority of the Lord Protector's writ, I place you under arrest for the practice of forbidden arts."

"You could have just knocked," said the smuggler.

"Ives Rell," Wren said without looking at her. "Two counts of smuggling, seven of harboring. We'll add them later." She stepped forward over the fallen masonry, boots ringing on stone that seemed to ring back. "Sable. Ten years. You couldn't have stayed in Salt Revisited selling luck-knots?"

"The luck-knots started answering prayers," he said. "You'd know that if you'd read your own reports instead of signing them."

One of the soldiers laughed and then strangled it. Wren let the silence do its work. This was the craft of her office: silence first, then names, then ruin. But her heart was hammering like a girl's, and the hall was filling with something she had no protocol for — a warmth, a pressure, like the moment before a storm breaks, except it came from below, from the stones, from everywhere.

The boy was hearing it too. She saw him tilt his head, birdlike, toward the walls. "They're saying your name," he told Sable. "Both of them. The old one and hers."

"Tam," Sable said quietly. "Hush."

"They're *saying* it—"

"I said hush."

And then Brother Odo stood up.

He was not what the interrogations had prepared her for. He was a big, soft, tired man with ink-stained fingers and the look of someone who had been apologizing for decades. He put himself between Wren and the others with no weapon at all, and he smiled at her — smiled, the madman — with terrible kindness.

"Inquisitor," he said. "I've been praying for you since Vail."

"You'll pray for the Lord Protector's mercy instead," she said. "Kneel. All of you. You come with me or you don't come at all."

"No," Odo said gently. "I don't think so. You see, my god has been answering me whether I will it or no, and lately I've stopped willing otherwise. And right now, Inquisitor Ashen, I am asking Him for one thing only." His smile didn't waver. "Time. Just a little time. For them to reach the door."

"There is no door," Wren said. "There is no Kestrel. There is no—"

The god answered.

She felt it before she saw it — every hair on her arms rising, the taste of lightning at the back of her teeth — and then the fire in the hearth stood up. Not the flames. The *fire*, all of it, rising off the coals like a golden column, and inside it Brother Odo spread his arms and became something more than a fat deserter priest. Light poured through him as though he were glass. When he spoke, his voice came doubled, layered, a man's voice and beneath it a voice like bells rung underwater.

"*These are mine,*" said the god of Brother Odo. "*This ground is mine. These children are mine. Turn back, iron daughter.*"

Her soldiers broke. Two ran outright. One dropped his sword and knelt, weeping, which was worse. The sergeant — good man, steady Halvard — raised his crossbow with shaking hands and fired, and the bolt stopped in the air a hand's width from Odo's chest and hung there, trembling, like a bee considering a flower.

"Odo!" Sable lunged forward. "Stop it, stop, you'll burn yourself out—"

"Somebody has to hold the line," Odo said, in his own voice, small and human and glad. "That's the whole of the priesthood, lad. Somebody holds the line while everyone else gets away. I only ever wanted to be useful at it once." He turned his glowing head to Wren, and his eyes were full of pity. "You too, child. Whatever name you're wearing. He's been praying for you longest of all. Did you think the salt forgot? The salt forgets nothing."

"Arrest him," Wren said to Halvard. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Arrest—"

Halvard fled.

* * *

Afterward, Wren could never make the pieces sit in order. There was light, and there was shouting, and there was the smuggler hauling the boy backward through a gap in the east wall, and Sable turning at the gap with his face lit gold and ruined, shouting something at her — her old name again, always her old name, *Wren, Wren* — and Odo's voice going up and up into registers no throat should hold until the windows that had no glass sang in their empty frames.

Then it ended. Gods spend themselves quickly in this world; that was the oldest lesson and the truest. The light went out like a breath, and Brother Odo folded down onto the oath-table stones, and he was only a soft dead man with ink on his fingers, smiling.

The silence afterward was the worst thing Wren had ever stood inside.

She walked to him. Her soldiers were gone — fled, kneeling, scattered — and so it was alone that she knelt beside the deserter priest and closed his eyes with two fingers, the way you do for family, because some animal part of her insisted that was what he was.

"He bought you a night," she said to the empty air, to Sable, to whoever listened. "Not a head start. A night. That's all I can give."

But even as she said it she knew it was a lie, and she was so tired of lying.

* * *

She caught them at dawn on the western causeway, alone, having sent her soldiers back with a forged report and burned her own writ in the keep's last standing hearth. Sable turned when he heard her boots on the wet stone, and Ives Rell put herself between them with her knife out, and the boy Tam looked at Wren with eyes that saw far too much.

"You followed us," Ives said.

"I relieved myself of duty," Wren said. "There's a difference. It took me ten years to learn it." She looked past the knife, past the smuggler, to Sable. "They'll send others. What Odo did at the keep — the whole province felt it. By week's end there will be a company of the Gray Guard on these roads, and after them the inquisitors proper, and they are not me, Sable. They will not remember your face."

"What are you doing?" he asked. Quietly. As if the answer might break something.

"Getting ahead of them," she said. "Clearing the road. Buying you the night Odo paid for, and the next one, and as many as I can steal." She pulled the gray sash of her rank over her head and let it fall into the black water of the causeway ditch, where it sank like a wounded bird. "There. No more Inquisitor Ashen. The Lord Protector can bill me for it."

"Wren—" Sable started.

"Don't." Her voice came out sharp enough to cut herself on. "Don't say it kindly. If you say it kindly I'll come apart on this causeway and there isn't time." She breathed. The salt wind moved through her hair, and for one unguarded moment it felt like a hand, like recognition, like the country itself remembering a girl who used to run these flats barefoot. "Go find your door. Go through it. And take—" Her voice failed and restarted. "Take me with you. Or leave me here to slow them down. Either way, stop looking at me like I'm worth saving, because I'm not sure I am, and I can't afford the doubt."

Tam spoke up, small and clear. "The gods say you're worth saving. They say it a lot. It's kind of annoying actually."

Wren laughed — one broken note, surprised out of her — and the sound of it frightened her more than Odo's god had.

Sable crossed the wet stones and took her hands, the way he had when they were fourteen and swearing oaths neither of them fully understood, oaths to a kingdom that would be dead within the year. His palms were warm. His eyes were the same.

"Then walk with us, sister," he said. "We've lost enough family to the iron. Come be family to something else."

Behind them, far to the east, horns began to sound along the salt roads — the Gray Guard, early, faster than any report had promised — and the tide was coming in across the causeway flats, and somewhere beneath the drowned capital a door waited that opened only for a name freely given.

Wren looked at the horns' dust, then at the people around her, and understood with perfect clarity which direction her remaining life would run.

"We need to move," she said. "Now. Before the water cuts the flats."

And the salt wind, grateful, pushed at their backs.
