## 8. The Free Cities Burn Late

Tam had learned to count in a workhouse where counting was survival — how many bowls, how many blows, how many days until the next beating. Now she counted people, and the numbers kept breaking against her like waves.

Four hundred and eleven. That was what she made it, standing on the spine of the drowned causeway as the light went copper and the salt roads below filled up with everything Kestrel had left. Old men pulling carts with wagon wheels gone soft from brine. Women carrying children who were too big to carry and too frightened to walk. A whole family of netmenders from Vell's Landing who had walked three days with their boat on their backs because they could not bear to leave it behind. Four hundred and eleven souls, give or take the ones hidden under tarps, the ones too proud to be counted, the ones who would not exist tomorrow.

Behind them, far off but not far enough, the sky over the coast road was the wrong color. Not sunset. Sunset came and went. This stayed, a bruised orange smear that pulsed when the wind shifted, and every time it pulsed the column flinched like a single animal.

"The free cities burn late," Ives said beside her. He said it the way he said most things, flat as a ledger line, but his knuckles were white on the rail of his own shoulder where his hand rested, as if holding himself together by main strength. "That's what my grandmother used to say. The usurper's armies always arrive after dark. Gives the fires time to look pretty."

"They're not burning yet," Tam said.

"No," Ives agreed. "They're walking."

* * *

It had started three days ago with nine people. Nine was a number Tam could hold. Nine was Ives and Sable and herself and old Marta the fishwife and two brothers off a wrecked ketch and a woman named Pell whose hands shook so badly she couldn't tie her own boots, and Tam had tied them for her every morning without being asked, because that was the kind of thing you did now, apparently. That was the kind of person the last week had made her.

Then the nine became thirty at the ferry ruin, because word moved faster than feet on the salt roads, and the word was: *the swordspeaker's people know the way through the drowned country.* Then thirty became a hundred and some at the shrine of the Tide-Mother, where Brother Odo's psalms still hung in the air like frost that wouldn't melt, and refugees touched the walls where he'd prayed and wept and walked on. And now it was four hundred and eleven, and Tam stood at the front of it with a stick for a staff and a voice gone raw from shouting, and somewhere behind her Sable Dravenmoor walked with his hood up and his charms quiet, looking like a man carrying a house on his back.

She found him at dusk, at the edge of the camp they'd made in the lee of a collapsed seawall. He wasn't sleeping. He was sitting with his palms open on his knees, and the little knots of cord and bone he wore — the luck-knots, the door-charms, all the small magics he'd spent ten years pretending were trinkets — were glowing faintly, each one pulsing out of rhythm with the others, like hearts arguing.

"Stop that," Tam said.

He looked up. In the firelight his face was all angles and exhaustion. "I'm not doing anything."

"That's what scares me." She sat down beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, because he'd stopped flinching from that somewhere around the keep and she'd decided not to remind him he used to. "They're answering again. The gods."

"A prayer went up an hour ago. From the column. Somebody praying for their mother's fever to break." His jaw worked. "It broke. I felt it go through me like cold water. I didn't choose it, Tam. I haven't chosen any of it since the harbor."

"Good," Tam said.

He turned to stare at her. "Good?"

"You spent ten years choosing nothing. Choosing small. Choosing invisible." She picked up one of his charms, turned it over — a knot of gray cord, worn soft as cloth. "The gods aren't asking your permission anymore. Neither is anybody else. There are four hundred people back there who think you're going to get them out of Kestrel, and you know what? Most of them are right to think it. Not because you're magic. Because you're the only one who knows the way, and you keep walking."

"And if the way ends?" Sable said quietly. "At the Doorway or before it. What do I tell them?"

Tam thought about the workhouse, about the morning the matron told them there would be no more food until the ships came, and the ships did not come, and how Sister Brannick — mean old Sister Brannick, who beat them and fed them and loved them in her iron way — had stood in the doorway and said, *then we tell them the truth, and we share out what's left, and we do it again tomorrow.* Tam had been seven. She had never forgotten it.

"You tell them the truth," she said. "And you share out what's left. And you do it again tomorrow."

Sable looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face loosened, just slightly, like a knot giving a half-turn. "When did you get wise?"

"I've always been wise. Everyone was too busy being tall to notice."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of him, which had been the entire point.

* * *

The vanguard reached the coast in the night, and Tam knew it before any scout brought word, because the gods told her.

She woke with salt water in her mouth though they were miles from open sea, and the wind was speaking — not in words, exactly, but in pressures, in the way a room speaks when someone has just died in it. *Burning,* the wind said, in the language of ash on the tongue. *Burning, burning, coming.* She sat up so fast she cracked her head on Ives's elbow, because he was already awake, already standing, already listening to the same silence.

"Vell's Landing?" she asked.

"Gone by midnight," Ives said. "Scout came in. They're moving along the coast road. Three days, maybe less, if they don't stop to burn things properly." He said it without drama, but Tam saw him swallow once, hard. Vell's Landing was where the netmender family had come from. Their boat was leaning against the seawall twenty feet away.

By dawn the whole camp knew, and the knowing nearly broke them. Tam watched it happen the way you watch a keel take water: first the panic, spreading person to person like bilge, then the screaming argument about whether to run for the hills or push for the Doorway, then a woman fainting, then a fistfight between two men who'd been friends yesterday. Four hundred people discovering at the same moment that hope and terror weigh exactly the same in the hand.

It was Marta the fishwife who stopped it, in the end. Marta, seventy years old, built like a mooring post, who climbed onto the seawall in her nightclothes and bellowed in a voice that had shouted down gales:

"*If you've got breath for fighting, you've got breath for walking!*"

And the strange thing — the thing Tam would remember all her life — was that it worked. Not completely, not cleanly. But the column formed. The carts creaked. The children were gathered. And they walked.

* * *

They walked for two days, and Tam learned what it meant to lead without meaning to.

It wasn't decisions, mostly. It was smaller than that. It was remembering who hadn't eaten. It was noticing that Pell's shaking hands had gotten worse and quietly retying her boots each morning and never once mentioning it. It was standing at the fork in the flooded causeway where the deep channel cut across and pointing left, loudly, confidently, having checked with Sable twice in a whisper first, because four hundred people will forgive a leader almost anything except hesitation. It was carrying a child named Sorrel on her shoulders for six miles because Sorrel's mother had blistered feet and Sorrel had decided Tam was hers now, the way children decide things, absolutely and without appeal.

It was also the counting. Every evening, in the last light, Tam counted. Four hundred and eleven became four hundred and nine when old Joss lay down in the salt grass and would not rise, and his sons buried him in an hour because there was no time to grieve properly, and the column walked on. Four hundred and nine became four hundred and fourteen when a straggler family caught up from a side road, hollow-eyed, carrying nothing. The number went up and down like a tide, and Tam wrote it every night in the margin of Ives's charts, because someone had to, because the dead deserved at least arithmetic.

On the second night, Sorrel climbed into her lap by the fire and asked her the question.

"Is the swordspeaker going to save us?"

Tam looked across the flames. Sable was at the edge of the firelight, as always, talking low with Ives, and even from here she could see the charms at his wrists glowing like embers that refused to die. Behind them, the coast burned. Ahead of them, the drowned country waited, and beneath it, somewhere in the sunken capital where no living map went, the Doorway — if Odo's psalm had told true, if the legend held, if the gods kept their bargains, which the gods of Kestrel had never once been famous for.

"He's going to try," Tam said.

Sorrel considered this with the ruthless honesty of the very young. "Trying isn't saving."

"No," Tam agreed. She pulled the girl's blanket higher. "But it's what comes before it."

* * *

On the third morning they reached the edge of the drowned capital, and the column stopped as one body, the way a herd stops at a cliff's edge.

The city lay ahead of them under shallow green water — spires like drowned fingers, rooftops just breaking the surface, streets visible in the clear salt like veins under skin. The old capital. The heart of dead Kestrel. Somewhere down there, beneath the palace district, beneath the silt and the years, the last gate out of a country that officially no longer existed.

"We can't all fit," Ives said. He'd done the same math Tam had; she could see it in his face. "Whatever the Doorway is, however big it is — it can't be four hundred people big. It might not be nine people big."

"Then we carry as many as it holds," Tam said. "And we keep carrying until it closes."

Ives looked at her — this orphan girl he'd fished out of a smuggling run half a lifetime ago, it seemed like now — and whatever he saw made him nod slowly, the way sailors nod at weather they can't argue with.

It was Sable who spoke last. He had come up beside them without sound, hood back, and the wind off the drowned city was moving through his hair and his charms and, Tam thought, through *him*, as if he were already half made of it. His eyes were fixed on the sunken spires, and his voice, when it came, was very quiet.

"There's a price," he said. "There's always been a price. I think I've known what it is since the keep." He didn't say more. He didn't have to, somehow; the wind took the rest of it, and the wind sounded almost like consent.

Below them, in the green water, every bell in the drowned capital began to ring.
