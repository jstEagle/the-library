## 1. The Keeper's Routine

The salt ran out on the eastern threshold first, as it always did.

Wendell Brightwater noticed it the way other men noticed rain coming — not with his eyes but with something older, something that lived in the marrow his grandmother had told him was *keeper's bone*. He had been eating breakfast when it happened: bread fried in goose fat, tea gone lukewarm in its chipped clay cup, and then a small cold feeling behind his sternum, like a coin dropped down a well. He set down his cup. He listened. The Gatehouse creaked its usual morning creaks — the settling of old beams, the tick of the stove cooling, the wind worrying the loose shingle above the kitchen window — and beneath all of it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the wards hummed their low gray note.

But the eastern threshold had gone quiet.

He dressed without hurry. That was the discipline of it, the thing his grandmother had beaten into him across twenty years of winters: *a keeper who runs is a keeper who's already lost.* Wool trousers, two shirts because the valley mornings bit even in high summer, boots oiled the night before because a keeper's feet were the only tools he could not replace. Last of all, the key ring — seven keys, six of them brass and honest, one of them black iron and older than the Gatehouse itself, older than the valley, older, his grandmother had once said in a voice he'd never heard her use again, than the idea of doors.

He filled the salt bucket from the barrel in the pantry. Coarse salt, gray-white, cut from the flats below the western hills where nothing grew and nothing ever would. His grandmother had called it *honest salt* — salt that remembered being sea, though no sea had come within three hundred miles of Kestrel's Rest in anyone's living memory. Wendell had never asked how she knew it remembered anything. Some questions you didn't ask in a house built beside a wall that hummed.

The morning outside was the kind the valley did best: pale gold light spilling down the terraces, mist still pooled in the low fields like milk someone had forgotten to put away, the smell of woodsmoke and wet grass and, underneath, always underneath, the mineral breath of the Salt Gate itself. Wendell stood on the doorstep a moment and let it all settle over him the way a man lets a familiar coat settle onto his shoulders.

*This is mine,* he thought, the way he thought it every morning. *All of this is mine to keep.*

He tried not to hear the small voice underneath, the one that sometimes asked: *and what, exactly, are you keeping it from?*

* *

The eastern threshold was a half-mile walk along the base of the Gate, following the wall where it rose out of the hillside like the spine of something buried alive. That was the image Wendell had carried since childhood, though he'd never spoken it aloud, not even to himself on the worst nights: the Gate as a buried creature, vast and sleeping, its stones the vertebrae of a back too long to see either end of. Nine feet tall where it crossed open ground, taller where it climbed into the crags, fitted stones so old their surfaces had gone smooth and glassy, veined with lines of white crystal that caught the light and held it wrong — held it a half-second too long, the way a mirror holds your face after you've turned away.

His grandmother had called those veins *the stitching.*

Wendell walked the wall's shadow and counted, under his breath, the ward-stones set into its base at every third pace. Gray river stones, each one the size of a skull, each one humming. He counted them the way a shepherd counts sheep: not because counting changed anything, but because the counting was the tending, and the tending was the whole of him.

At the eastern threshold — a gap in the wall no wider than a garden gate, framed in white stone that the veins ran through like thread through cloth — he knelt and saw the truth of the cold feeling in his chest. The salt line was broken. Not scattered, not washed away by any rain; there had been no rain. Simply *interrupted*, a clean gap in the poured line, as if someone had lifted a section of it out with a spoon.

Wendell looked at the gap for a long time.

Beyond the threshold, the ground continued — grass, then scree, then the grey shoulder of the mountain called the Blind Man, and above it a sky so blue and empty it made his teeth ache. There was supposed to be nothing beyond the threshold. That was the whole architecture of his life: the valley inside, the world outside, and the Gate between, and the Gate did not open. It had never opened. It had not opened in four hundred years, since the last keeper whose name was recorded anywhere — his great-great-great-grandmother Alva Brightwater, whose journal sat wrapped in oilcloth in the bottom of the cedar chest, which Wendell read one page of every night before sleep, the way other men said prayers.

Alva's journal said: *The Gate does not keep things out. Remember this, whoever reads. A wall keeps things out. This is not a wall.*

Wendell had read that sentence perhaps a thousand times, and every time he arrived at it the same way a man arrives at a locked door in his own house — knowing it's there, walking around it, never once trying the handle. Because if the Gate wasn't a wall, then what had his family been doing all these centuries? What had *he* been doing, thirty-one years of salting and counting and listening, if the thing he tended wasn't what he'd been told it was?

He poured the salt. His hands knew the work; they moved without instruction, tipping the bucket, laying the line thick and unbroken across the threshold, whispering the words his grandmother had taught him before he could write his own name. The words weren't in any language he knew. They weren't in any language at all, he suspected; they were older than language, the sound salt makes when it remembers being sea. As he spoke them, the ward-stones along the wall brightened by a degree — not visibly, exactly, but the way a room brightens when a cloud moves off the sun — and the humming deepened, settled, became content.

The cold feeling behind his sternum dissolved.

"There," Wendell said aloud, to no one, because there was never anyone. "There, then."

His voice fell flat in the wide morning. He stood, brushed the salt from his knees, and allowed himself, as a small daily mercy, one look through the threshold at the country that wasn't there.

Just grass. Just scree. Just the Blind Man holding up his empty sky.

He turned away quickly, the way you turn away from a window at night.

* *

Tam Weller came up the goat track at noon, which meant it was Thursday, which meant the world still turned.

Tam was the last postman in Kestrel's Rest, and possibly the last postman in the valley's entire history, because the valley had forty-one souls left in it and shrinking, and most of what Tam carried was seed catalogues, a letter from a cousin in Lowmarsh twice a year, and the weekly bundle of newspapers that reported on a world Wendell had never seen. He was sixty, bowlegged, bald as an egg, and he talked the way water talks going over stones — constantly, musically, about nothing in particular.

"Keeper!" he called from fifty yards off, raising his stick. "You'll want to sit down for this. I've got news."

"You say that every week, Tam."

"And every week it's true." Tam dropped onto the bench by the Gatehouse door with the groan of a man who had earned every one of his joints. Wendell brought him cider, because that was also part of the routine, though no journal had ever commanded it. "News first or gossip first?"

"News, I suppose."

"Disappointing. News is thin this week. King's men quarreling in the capital, harvests good in the lowlands, some scholar says the stars are farther away than anyone thought." Tam waved a hand. "Stars! As if anyone needed telling. Now — gossip." He leaned in, lowering his voice to a stage whisper audible across the whole yard. "Marta Hollis has taken a lodger."

"Has she." Marta Hollis kept the inn at the crossroads, forty-one souls being too few to support an inn and yet somehow supporting one anyway, the way the valley supported everything — stubbornly, inexplicably, against all arithmetic.

"A young woman. Came down from the north passes three days ago, alone, which Marta says is strange enough, but here's the thing—" Tam's eyes went bright. "She paid in old coin. Old-old coin. Silver with a stag on it, and Marta's grandfather was a mint-man before the mines closed, and he'd have known that stamp, and she doesn't. Nobody does."

Wendell felt the cold feeling flicker behind his sternum, faint as a match struck in another room. He kept his face still. "People come through the passes sometimes."

"People come through the passes," Tam agreed comfortably. "But they come with roads behind them. This one came with nothing. No pack-horse, no guide, no mud on her boots past the knees — and the passes are knee-deep in meltwater this month, everyone knows it. Marta says it's like she stepped out of the air." He drank his cider. "Pretty, too, Marta says. Sharp-eyed. Asked more questions than she answered, which around here puts her ahead of the average."

"What sort of questions?"

"Oh — the valley, mostly. How many folk. Whether the old families were still here." Tam scratched his jaw. "Whether the Gate was still *kept*, if you can believe it. Who asks that? Who even thinks of it?"

Wendell thought: *I do. Every hour of every day. And my mother did, and her mother, back and back until the names run out.*

"Passing curiosity," he said. "Valley's famous for its cheese, I suppose."

"Famous for something," Tam said, and laughed, and the laugh was easy, and Wendell envied it with his whole chest. That was the thing about Tam, about all of them, the forty-one: they lived their lives pressed up against the Gate the way sleepers press against a warm wall, and they never once wondered what was on the other side of it. They had grown up in its shadow and simply stopped seeing it, the way you stop seeing your own nose. Only Wendell saw it. Only Wendell had been taught to see it, and taught to be afraid of it, and taught — this was the deepest lesson, laid down under all the others like bedrock under soil — taught that the fear itself was the inheritance, and that a Brightwater who stopped fearing the Gate would stop being a Brightwater at all.

He was lonely the way a lighthouse is lonely. He had made his peace with it, mostly. Mostly.

"Your mother's grave is looking tidy," Tam said, gentler now. "I passed the churchyard on the way up. Someone's been cutting the grass round it."

"I cut it Tuesdays."

"Tuesdays," Tam repeated, nodding slowly, as if this confirmed some theory of the universe he'd been developing for decades. "Well. Same time next week, Keeper?"

"Same time next week."

Tam raised his stick in salute and went clattering back down the goat track, talking to himself the whole way, and Wendell stood in the doorway of the Gatehouse and watched him shrink to a dot and vanish among the terraces, and felt the silence close over the yard like water closing over a thrown stone.

Then he went inside, took down Alva's journal from the cedar chest, unwrapped the oilcloth, and read the day's page. It was a short entry, dated in a spring four hundred years dead:

*Salted all nine thresholds. The eastern one troubles me. Something tests it in the nights — not pressing, only testing, the way a prisoner taps a wall. I have written to the other keepers and received no answer, which is itself an answer. If you are reading this, child: the Gate holds because we hold. Do not go looking for what we hold it shut upon. Curiosity is how walls become doors.*

Wendell read it twice. Then he wrapped the journal back in its oilcloth, put it in the chest, and locked the chest with the smallest of the six brass keys.

He did not think about the broken salt line. He did not think about the young woman paying in coins no one could name, asking whether the Gate was still kept. He did not think about the phrase *something tests it in the nights,* or about the fact that his own eastern threshold had failed for the first time in his thirty-one years of keeping it.

He banked the stove. He trimmed the lamp. He ate his supper standing at the window, looking out at the wall going violet and then black as the sun went down behind it, and he told himself, in the steady voice his grandmother had used, that all was well, that the valley was safe, that the routine was the keeping and the keeping was enough.

That night, for the first time in years, he dreamed of the other side of the Gate.

It was not a nightmare. That was the terrible part. In the dream he stood in a city of white towers under a sky the color of pewter, and the towers sang, and somewhere far off a bell rang in a rhythm he almost recognized, and a voice that sounded like his own said: *you forgot us, and we could not forget you, and now the ledger must be balanced.*

He woke before dawn with salt sweat drying on his skin and the wards humming louder than he had ever heard them — not alarmed, not yet, but awake, attentive, like a dog that has lifted its head in the dark.

And out past the eastern threshold, out in the country that wasn't there, a light was moving. Small. Steady. Coming toward Kestrel's Rest.
