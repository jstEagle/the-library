## 2. The Woman Who Crossed Twice

The valley of Kestrel's Rest had eleven ways in and out, if you counted the goat tracks, and Sera Vane had memorized all of them before she was old enough to be hanged for knowing.

She came down the eastern ravine tonight, the one the maps called Hollowman's Cut because a smuggler had died there once and the valley liked its warnings where it could see them. The moon was a thin nail-paring overhead, and beneath it the valley lay pale as a held breath: terraced fields gone silver with frost, the black stitch of the river, and at the far western edge, small from here, the grey shoulder of the Salt Gate rising out of the hills like a headstone someone had forgotten to finish carving.

Sera did not look at it. That was a discipline, like counting her knives or checking her bootlaces twice. You didn't look at the thing you'd sworn never to go near again. You especially didn't look at it when you were about to spend three nights within a mile of it, doing the one job in the world that required it.

*In and out,* she told herself, settling her pack higher on her shoulders. *Three days. Find it, take it, leave. Nobody has to know you were ever here.*

But the valley already knew. She felt it the way she'd felt it seven years ago, crossing the other direction — a pressure against the skin, faint and total, like walking through standing water that reached exactly to the collarbone. Kestrel's Rest remembered everyone who entered it. It simply had no way of saying so.

She moved along the cut's floor, boots finding the old smuggler's path by feel — left of the fallen oak, right at the cairn, down through the bracken that grew waist-high and rusted gold even in high summer. The cold came up off the stones and into her bones, and she let it. Cold was honest. Cold asked nothing of you except that you keep moving.

At the mouth of the ravine she stopped, crouched in the shadow of a leaning fencepost, and looked out across the first of the valley's fields.

A light was moving there. A lantern, low to the ground, swinging in slow patient arcs — the walk of a man checking something line by line, the way a scribe checks a ledger. And behind the lantern, faintly, a smell rode the night air that made Sera's stomach drop through the bottom of her: salt. Old salt, blessed salt, the salt of thresholds.

Someone was salting a field boundary at midnight.

*No,* she thought. *Not a field boundary.*

She knew that rhythm. She'd watched it once, years ago, from the wrong side of a wall of grey stone, watching a boy walk the Gate's foundations with a lantern and a sack of coarse salt, laying down protection against a country that no longer existed. She'd been seventeen and bleeding and desperate and she'd thought: *there is a person whose whole life fits inside one evening, over and over, forever.* She'd pitied him. Then she'd used him, in the smallest possible way — she'd waited until he passed out of sight around the Gate's northern flank, and she'd crossed.

He'd never seen her. He didn't know she existed. But she knew his walk now, the particular unhurried patience of it, and the man in the field below walked the same way, and Sera Vane stood very still in the dark and understood that Kestrel's Rest had not forgotten her either.

*It doesn't matter,* she told herself. *You'll never cross paths. The valley is wide and your business is narrow.*

Below, the lantern swung, and its light caught the salter's face for half a second — young still, younger than her, with the kind of open, unguarded expression that in Sera's trade got people killed — and turned away toward the river, and Sera exhaled, and began to move.

* * *

Wendell Brightwater noticed the stranger on the third night, and hated himself for how long it took.

He'd been uneasy since the first evening, though he'd have struggled to say why. The wards had sung true at dusk — the low, grain-sweet hum that meant the Salt Gate held and the valley slept safe inside its circle. He'd walked his rounds, all nine miles of them, salting the secondary lines the way his grandmother had taught him, and everything had been exactly, perfectly normal.

That was what had been wrong. He just hadn't known it yet.

On the third night, coming back along the river path under a sky scoured clean of cloud, he saw the lantern-light in the miller's fallow field where no lantern should be, and his first thought — the thought trained into him before he could write his own name — was *breach.* His hand went to the salt-horn at his belt and his heart went to the Gate behind him, and he cut across the frozen furrows with his lantern held high and his voice pitched to carry:

"This is ward-line ground. State your business."

The figure by the ruined mill wall didn't startle. That was the first strange thing. Everyone in Kestrel's Rest startled at Wendell's voice, not because he was frightening but because nobody ever spoke on the boundary paths after dark; his voice was a sound out of season, like snow in July. But this person simply turned, unhurried, and lifted their own lantern, and the light crossed the space between them and showed him —

— a woman, thirtyish, weathered brown as old rope, with a knife-callused hand resting easy on her belt and eyes the grey-green of the sea in bad weather. A stranger's face. A stranger's clothes, oiled canvas and soft leather, built for silence. And yet something about the set of her shoulders, the angle of her chin as she looked at him —

"You're the keeper," she said. Not a question. Her voice was low and roughened, an accent he couldn't place — somewhere east, maybe, or nowhere at all. "Brightwater. I heard the valley still kept one."

"Who are you?" Wendell kept his distance and kept his lantern up. Old habit: you never let a stranger's shadow touch the ward-line until you knew their name. "There's no road through here. If you've come over the fells you've wandered well off it."

"I don't wander." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile; more like the memory of one, worn smooth from disuse. "I'm looking for something I lost. In this valley."

"People who lose things ask at houses. In daylight." He was aware of how young he sounded, how small, and pressed on anyway. "They don't creep along the ward-lines at midnight with their face wrapped."

"And yet here we both are." Her gaze slid past him, westward, toward the hills where the Salt Gate stood invisible in the dark, and Wendell saw — clearly, unmistakably — the moment her eyes found it. Not the stone itself; the dark hid the stone. What she found was the *absence* of stars above it, the little blind patch of sky that hung over the Gate always, that every child in the valley learned to navigate by without ever being taught what it meant. Strangers never noticed it. This woman found it in two seconds flat, the way a sailor finds north.

And then she looked back at Wendell, and her expression did something complicated and quick, like weather changing over water, and she said, quietly:

"How long have you kept the Gate?"

"That's not your business."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She shouldered her pack. "I'll be out of your valley in three days, keeper. I won't touch your lines. You can follow me around playing lighthouse if it makes you feel useful."

"I know you," Wendell said.

It came out of him before he decided to say it, and the moment it was said he knew it was true, and the knowing frightened him more than any breach ever had. The woman went still. Completely still, the way animals go still, the way his grandmother's journal said the old ward-stones went still when they were listening.

"Seven years ago," Wendell said, and his voice had gone strange in his own ears. "Autumn. There was a girl crossed the Gate — from the far side, into the valley. I only saw her from behind. She was hurt. She had blood on her sleeve, and she stopped at the threshold and looked back the way she'd come, for a long time, and then she walked down into the valley and nobody ever — " He stopped. Started again. "I reported it. My grandmother said the Gate lets through what it lets through, and the valley would do with her what the valley does. And then no one ever mentioned her again, and I told myself I'd imagined the blood, imagined the whole thing, because the alternative was — "

"The alternative was that you live next door to a door," the woman said, "and you've never once opened it."

The words landed in Wendell's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He heard it falling for a long time.

"That girl," he said. "That was you."

The woman looked at him for a long moment. The frost ticked and settled in the furrows. Somewhere off toward the village a dog barked twice and gave up.

"Yes," she said finally. "It was me. And you should thank whatever gods the valley pretends it doesn't have that I'm not telling you what I saw on the other side, Brightwater, because you sleep easier than anyone I've ever met, and I'd hate to be the reason you stopped."

Then she turned and walked away into the dark, unhurried, her lantern swinging in that patient ledger-keeper's arc, and Wendell stood alone in the frozen field with his heart going like a trapped bird, and did not follow her, and did not go home, and did not, for a long time, move at all.

* * *

Her name, he learned by morning, was Vane. Sera Vane. She'd taken a room at the Drowned Kestrel, paying in coin that was real but old — coins minted, the innkeeper's wife whispered later, in a style nobody had seen in a hundred years, though the wife laughed when she said it, the way people in the valley laughed at anything that brushed against the edges of things. Sera Vane had told the room she was a tinker. She had a tinker's pack. She did not, in two days of observed comings and goings, tinker at anything.

Wendell watched her the way he watched the weather, which was to say constantly and with a professional's unease. He told himself it was duty. The ward-lines ran within a quarter-mile of the village well; a stranger poking about the boundaries was his business whether she liked it or not. He told himself it was curiosity, which was rarer and therefore easier to excuse. He did not tell himself the truth, which was that seven years ago a girl had walked out of the impossible and into his valley, and he had spent seven years pretending he hadn't seen her look back over her shoulder at a country that did not exist, and now she was here, eating bread at the Drowned Kestrel, and pretending had become impossible.

He cornered her on the second evening, on the bridge below the mill, where the river ran loud enough to cover a conversation and shallow enough that neither of them could be dragged out of it easily. She wasn't surprised to see him. He was beginning to suspect she was never surprised by anything, and that this was the saddest thing about her.

"Tinker," he said.

"Keeper."

"What are you hunting?"

"A thing I lost." She leaned on the bridge rail, watching the water. Up close, in honest lamplight, he could see the map of her history written on her: a scar through one eyebrow, another at the jaw, hands that had been broken at least once and set by someone in a hurry. "It's small. Metal. About the length of your finger. If you find it lying about, I'll pay for it."

"What is it?"

"It's mine."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you get." She glanced at him sideways, and there it was again, that flicker of something almost warm, quickly banked. "You're persistent. They didn't mention that in the stories."

"What stories?"

"The ones about the Brightwaters." She said it lightly, but her knuckles had whitened on the rail. "Gatekeepers. Salt-bloods. The family that stands in the doorway so the rest of the valley doesn't have to look at the door." She pushed off from the rail. "Get some sleep, Wendell Brightwater. Three nights is a short time to be a person, and you strike me as someone who's going to want to remember this week."

"Why?" he called after her. "Why would I want to remember it?"

She paused at the end of the bridge. The river roared underneath. When she answered, she didn't turn around, and the wind took most of it, but he caught the shape of the words:

"Because something is coming. And when it comes, you'll need to know I tried to warn you gently."

* * *

Sera lay awake that night in the Drowned Kestrel's attic room with her pack open on the bed and her fingers resting on the object she'd come three hundred miles to find — or rather, on the empty space where it should have been, because the room she'd been told about, the dead courier's cache under the hearthstone in the miller's ruin, had been empty. Cleaned out. Recently. By someone who knew exactly what they were taking.

Which meant Ilsette's people were already in the valley. Which meant the queen's reach had crossed mountains that weren't supposed to exist anymore. Which meant the timeline Sera had been working with — careful, cautious, *three days, in and out* — was worthless, and the thing she'd lost was already moving toward the Gate, or already past it, or already in the hands of someone who would use it to pry that wound wide open and pour the erased kingdom through it like floodwater through a breached dam.

She stared at the ceiling beams and did the arithmetic of catastrophe, and somewhere in the middle of it she realized she was thinking about the boy on the boundary path. The keeper. Wendell. The way he'd said *I know you* like a man discovering a hole in the floor of his own house. The way his whole face had been nothing but questions, honest ones, the kind that got people killed in her line of work — and the kind, some treacherous part of her noted, that she hadn't heard in seven years, because everyone she dealt with now already knew all the answers, and most of the answers were lies.

*Warn him,* she thought. *I warned him. Gently. That's more than I gave anyone else.*

Outside, far to the west, over the hills where the Salt Gate stood its endless watch, the sky was clear and cold and full of stars.

Except, Sera noticed — sitting up slowly, her breath gone shallow, her trader's instincts ringing like a struck bell — except that there were fewer of them than there had been last night. Fewer than there should be. As if, over the blind patch of sky above the Gate, the darkness had begun, very slightly, to spread.

She dressed, and checked her knives, and counted her bootlaces twice, and did not sleep again.
