## 3. Stars Going Out

The first star went out while Wendell was salting the western threshold, and he did not notice.

He noticed the cold instead — a thinness in the air that hadn't been there at dusk, as though someone had opened a door at the top of the world and let a draft down through it. He straightened up from his work, brush in hand, coarse salt scattered in the old patterns his grandmother had taught him: three lines across the threshold stone, a circle broken at the north, a knot-work of crossed strokes where the warding runes ran beneath the Gate's foundations like veins beneath skin. The salt caught the last grey light and glittered, ordinary and obedient, doing what salt had always done.

He looked up to judge how much evening he had left, and that was when he saw the hole in the sky.

Not a cloud. Not a haze. A place where a star had been — the bright one low over the eastern ridge, the one the valley folk called the Shepherd because it rose ahead of the flock of winter constellations — and now there was nothing there at all. No dimming, no fading. Just absence, clean-edged, as if the sky were a woven cloth and someone had picked out a single thread.

Wendell stood very still for a long moment, brush forgotten, salt drying on his fingers.

Then he told himself it was clouds. It was always clouds. In thirty-one years of tending the Salt Gate, the sky above Kestrel's Rest had done nothing worse than rain, snow, and once — memorably — a hailstorm that had cracked two roof tiles on the Gatehouse and given him a week of grumbling repairs. The sky was not a thing that happened to you. The sky was wallpaper. You lived your whole life under it and never once thought about what held it up.

He finished the western threshold. His hands knew the work so well they could have done it sleeping, and perhaps that was the trouble, because his mind was free to keep drifting upward, checking, rechecking, finding the dark patch where the Shepherd should have been and confirming, again, with a small cold insistence, that it was still there. Still empty. Clouds moved. This did not.

* * *

Inside the Gatehouse, he lit the lamp and pulled his grandmother's journal from its shelf.

It was not a grand book. It was a ledger, really, half its pages given over to accounts — sacks of salt purchased from the quarry town, nails, lamp oil, the price of boot leather — because the Brightwaters had never been able to afford sentiment. But interleaved through the accounting, in her cramped left-slanting hand, were the instructions that mattered. *Renew the north seal before first frost or the frost will renew it for you.* *Salt is cheap; regret is not.* And on the page Wendell turned to now, dog-eared soft as cloth from years of handling, an entry he had read a hundred times without ever once believing:

*If ever the stars begin to go out — and I have seen it once, in my mother's time, though she swore me to silence and I have kept it poorly — do not wait for them to finish. When the sky forgets us, the Gate forgets its purpose. A gate with no purpose is only a hole.*

His grandmother had written that in the same ink she used for noting the price of nails.

Wendell sat with the journal open on his knees and listened to the house settle around him, and outside, faint but unmistakable, the wards began to sing.

They sang all the time, if you knew how to listen — a hum below hearing more than above it, felt in the teeth and the breastbone rather than heard by the ear, a steady note like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. He had slept inside that note his whole life. It was the sound of safety itself, the sound of the valley being a valley and the world beyond the stones staying where it belonged. He could not have described it to anyone who hadn't grown up inside it, any more than he could have described the taste of water.

But tonight the note changed.

It climbed. That was the only word for it — the deep, patient hum slid upward through registers he had no names for, thinner and thinner, until it passed out of his bones and into his actual ears, a whine like a kettle deciding whether to scream. The lamp flame stood perfectly still. The salt in the bowl on the mantel — his emergency reserve, blessed and ground fine — shivered, each grain trembling in place like a swarm of insects pretending to be sand.

And then, through the window over the sink, Wendell watched the second star go out.

This one he saw properly. It didn't fade. It went the way a candle goes when pinched between wet fingers — a last flare, a smudge of afterimage burned into his vision, and then black. The constellation of the Plough lost its handle. The night sky over Kestrel's Rest now had a gap in it, and gaps, Wendell understood with a certainty that arrived whole and uninvited, were things that wanted filling.

The wards' note broke. Not faded — *broke*, the way a string breaks, and for three full seconds the Salt Gate made no sound at all, and Wendell discovered that the silence was the worst thing yet, because he had never in his life heard the wards stop singing, and a body can bear almost anything except the absence of a sound it mistook for silence's opposite.

Then the note came back. Lower now. Wrong. Like a man humming a hymn he'd only ever heard sung by someone else.

* * *

He did the checks. Of course he did the checks; he was a Brightwater, and Brightwaters answered fear with procedure, because procedure was the wall they had built instead of understanding. He walked the full circuit of the Gate in the dark with the lamp hooded, pressing his palm to each wardstone in turn, reading their warmth the way other men read weather.

The stones were cold. All of them. Not dead — he would have known dead, his grandmother had made sure of that, had pressed his childish hand to a cracked stone one winter and said *remember this, this is what dying feels like* — but cold the way a body goes cold that is still breathing. Holding on. Spending something fast to do it.

At the keystone, the great central stone where the Gate's oldest rune was cut so deep that moss had never dared grow in it, he stopped. The rune was weeping. Not water — the stone was dry everywhere else — but a slow bead of something clear and slightly luminous welling up from the carved channels and running down the granite face like sweat down a laborer's spine. He touched it. It was cold, and it tasted of salt when he instinctively, stupidly, put his fingertip to his tongue.

Salt. Everything about the Gate came back to salt. Salt preserved; salt sealed; salt remembered. His family had tended salt and stone for longer than the valley had kept records, and in all that inheritance, in all the journals and the oral instruction and the muscle memory of nine generations, there was exactly one entry about stars going out, and it ended with the words *a gate with no purpose is only a hole.*

Wendell stood under the wounded sky with his hand flat against the weeping stone and let himself, for the first time in his careful life, ask the question underneath all the other questions.

*A gate keeps something out,* he thought. *Or keeps something in. Which are we?*

He had never asked before. That was the honest shape of it, and it shamed him even as the thought formed: thirty-one years of duty and he had never once asked what the duty was *for*. His father hadn't asked either. Nor his grandfather. The Brightwaters kept the Gate the way rivers run downhill — because it was the shape they had been given, and questioning the shape was not part of the flowing. The valley beyond the hills grew barley and raised sheep and sent its children to school in the spring, and none of them, not one soul in Kestrel's Rest, had ever asked Wendell what was on the other side of the stones, because asking would have meant admitting the stones mattered, and admitting the stones mattered would have meant admitting the world had edges, and nobody wants to live at the edge of anything.

So they had all agreed, silently, generation upon generation, to pretend the Gate was scenery. And Wendell had built his whole quiet contentment on top of that pretense like a house on a frozen lake, and tonight, for the first time, he heard the ice creak.

* * *

Toward midnight he gave up on sleep and sat in the kitchen with the journal, the lamp, and the last of the plum brandy, and he read his grandmother's account of her mother's night properly, slowly, the way you read a letter that turns out to be addressed to you.

*My mother woke us all. She said the stars were going out like lamps in a house where someone was walking room to room. She took the salt to the thresholds herself, though her hands were bad with the rheumatism, and she sang the sealing verse until dawn. By morning the sky was whole again and my father said she'd dreamed it. But she made me swear, and I have kept the swearing, mostly, and she never spoke of it again except once, the winter before she died, when she looked at me across the table and said: "Astrid, girl, remember that the Gate does not belong to us. We belong to it."*

Wendell read that last line four times.

*We belong to it.* He had always read it backwards, the way you read everything comfortable backwards — the Gate belongs to us, our charge, our inheritance, our family's proud burden. But that wasn't what she wrote. What she wrote was the other thing entirely. The thing a woman says when she has understood, late and unwillingly, that she was never the owner of her own life.

Outside, the third star went out, and this time he felt it happen — felt it the way you feel a tooth loosen, a small wrong give in something structural. The wards' new low note stuttered. Down in the valley, a dog began to bark, then another, then a chorus of them, all along the river road, every dog in Kestrel's Rest awake and announcing the same invisible arrival.

Wendell set down the brandy untasted.

He counted. He couldn't help counting; counting was the other wall Brightwaters built. Three stars gone since dusk. His grandmother's account said her mother's sky had lost seven before dawn, and then stopped, and stayed stopped for forty years, and everyone agreed to call it weather.

He looked at the journal, and at the weeping keystone visible through the window, silver-lit now by a moon that seemed, he realized with a lurch, to be shining slightly too hard — compensating, the way a lone singer raises her voice when the choir falls away.

Seven, his great-great-grandmother's sky had lost.

Wendell counted again, and got three, and understood that he was going to spend the rest of the night watching the sky, and that some part of him already knew how the arithmetic would end.

* * *

The fourth star went out at the hour when the night is oldest and most honest, and the fifth followed it so quickly that the afterimages overlapped, two wounds in the firmament close enough together to look deliberate — a pattern, or the beginning of one. The wards were singing their borrowed hymn now, low and continuous, and Wendell had begun to hate the sound more than he would have hated silence, because the song was the Gate trying to remember what it was for, and failing, and trying again.

He was standing at the threshold with the salt bag open over his arm when he saw the light on the eastern road.

Not starlight. Lamplight, swinging with a walker's gait, coming up the valley road at three in the morning, which no one in Kestrel's Rest had done in living memory. The dogs had gone quiet all at once, which was somehow worse than the barking. Wendell stood in the shadow of the Gate arch, salt in his fist, and watched the light come on toward him through a landscape that was losing its stars one pinch at a time.

And he thought — with the strange clarity that arrives only when everything familiar has begun, gently, quietly, to come apart — that whatever was walking up that road had chosen the worst night in the history of the valley to arrive, or the best, and that either way, nothing about tomorrow was going to be routine.

The light drew closer. Behind it, overhead, the sixth star flared, guttered —

— and went out.
