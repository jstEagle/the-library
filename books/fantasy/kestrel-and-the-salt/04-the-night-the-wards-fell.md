## 4. The Night the Wards Fell

### Wendell

The wards began to sing at dusk, and Wendell Brightwater knew every note they had ever sung.

There was the low hum of the threshold stones, a sound like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, steady as his own pulse. There was the thin silver thread of the salt lines, almost too high to hear, the note he'd once described to the postman as *the sound of a held breath*. And beneath them both, felt more than heard, the deep patient drone of the Gate itself — the great arch of grey stone that had stood at the valley's throat since before his grandmother's grandmother, older than any name anyone still used.

He knew these sounds the way other men knew their mothers' voices. He had fallen asleep to them for thirty-one years. He had woken in the black hours before dawn more than once because one of them had gone flat by a hair, and he had risen, and re-salted a line, and mended what needed mending, and lain back down content.

Tonight, at the moment the sun went down behind Kestrel's Rest and the first star should have kindled above the eastern ridge — should have, except there were no stars left to kindle, only a sky like the inside of a closed fist — the wards sang a note he had never heard.

It came up through the soles of his boots first. Then through the floorboards of the Gatehouse. Then through his teeth.

Wendell set down the ladle he'd been stirring supper with and stood very still in his kitchen, listening the way a deer listens, with his whole body, with the animal part of him that predated duty and language and names.

The new note was wrong in a way he couldn't describe. It wasn't discordant. That was the horror of it. It was *beautiful* — a low, sweet tone like a bell rung underwater, like a choir heard through a wall — and it did not belong to anything he had built or tended or inherited. It was not the sound of the wards holding.

It was the sound of them being answered.

"No," he said aloud, to the kitchen, to the soup going cold on the stove, to nobody. "No, no, no."

His grandmother's journal lay on the shelf above the hearth where it had always lain, its leather spine cracked soft as old bread. He had read it so many times the words lived in him like scripture: *When the sky forgets us, the Gate forgets its purpose.* He had read that line a hundred times and never once believed it would apply to him. Warnings in old books were for other keepers, other centuries. His job was salt and routine and the postman's gossip. Danger was a story told to children about the stones.

He took the journal down anyway, hands not quite steady, and carried it out into the yard.

The cold met him at the door.

Not valley cold — Kestrel's Rest autumns were mild, woolen-sweater weather, woodsmoke and frost on the pump handle by morning. This was another order of cold entirely. It poured down from the Gate like water over a weir, visible almost, a shimmer in the air, and where it touched the grass the blades stiffened white and brittle. The apple tree by the fence — the one his grandfather had planted, the one that had borne fruit every year of Wendell's life — stood suddenly bare, its leaves falling all at once, all together, like birds startled off a wire.

Beyond it, the Salt Gate filled the notch in the hills, and it was glowing.

Not the warm amber glow of the ward-lamps he lit each evening. A pale light, the color of moonlight through ice, the color of the sky that had eaten the stars. The arch's carvings — worn smooth shapes he'd traced with his fingers since childhood without ever deciphering them — were moving. Slowly. Like things turning over in sleep.

Wendell walked toward it. He would think about that later, the walking-toward-it, and never fully forgive himself or fully understand it. Every instinct screamed to run the other way, down the valley, to hammer on doors, to shout the village awake. But he was the keeper. The Gate was singing a note it had never sung, and the keeper went to the Gate. That was the whole of him. Take away that one sentence and there was nothing left standing.

*That,* he would think later, in the dark country on the other side, *was the problem.*

The threshold stones were weeping salt. Not the dry white grains he scattered each week from the barrels in the shed — this was brine, welling up out of the seams between the stones as if the ground beneath had become an ocean floor, running in rivulets that steamed where they crossed the frost-killed grass. He knelt at the edge of it and dipped two fingers, out of habit, out of forty years of habit, and brought them to his lips.

Salt. But underneath the salt, something else. Something old and vast and grieving, a taste like the bottom of a well that had drowned someone.

"Grandmother," he whispered, "what do I do?"

The journal had no answer for this. He knew that without opening it. Her warnings had ended at the edge of the failure; everything past it was blank pages, and blank pages were his now.

The new note swelled.

And the seals broke.

He felt them go one by one, up the arch, like knuckles cracking in a giant's hand — seven seals, though he'd never known there were seven, had never known they were *seals* at all, had thought the carved bands across the arch were decoration. Each one shattered with a sound he felt in his sternum rather than heard, and each shatter released a wind that smelled of a place he had never been: cedar smoke and hot stone and rain on ruins, and under it all, faintly, unbearably, the smell of bread baking somewhere far away, the smell of ordinary life, of a kingdom's ten thousand kitchens.

The pale light went out.

For one heartbeat the Gate was just the Gate — grey stone, old carvings, the dark notch of the pass beyond it, the same view he'd seen every day of his life.

Then the darkness inside the arch became a door, and the door opened, and the cold came through like floodwater.

It didn't blow. It *poured*. It hit him in the chest and drove the breath out of him and lifted him off his feet — lifted him, a grown man who had wrestled ward-barrels and postman's stubborn mules, as easily as a leaf off a drain grate — and the last thing Wendell Brightwater saw of the valley of Kestrel's Rest was upside down: the Gatehouse windows gold with lamplight, his supper still warm on the stove, the apple tree bare and glittering, the roofs of the village below huddled against the coming night, smoke rising straight and peaceful from forty chimneys.

Forty chimneys. He'd counted them a thousand times. He knew which chimney belonged to the Tanners, which to old Marta whose dog he fed when she was abed, which to the schoolhouse where the teacher kept a jar of barley sugar on her desk for children who cried on their first day.

They were all still there. They were all still burning.

Then the world behind him simply stopped existing.

There was no explosion. No scream. That was the unbearable part, the part his mind would circle for days afterward like a tongue probing a broken tooth. One instant the valley was there, whole and warm and lit; the next it was not — not destroyed, not darkened, but *unmade*, replaced by a darkness so complete it made the night around it look like noon. No hills. No lights. No forty chimneys. Just absence, smooth as spilled ink, where everything he loved had been a half-second before.

He screamed then. He was proud, later, insofar as he could be proud of anything, that he waited until after. He screamed his grandmother's name, and the village's name, and then no words at all, just sound, while the cold carried him backward through the arch like a river carrying a stick, and the stars-that-were-not-stars wheeled overhead, and the Door — some vast and distant thing he could feel rather than see, waiting at the heart of everything like a heart itself — began, very softly, to laugh.

Or perhaps to sing. In that country, he would learn, the two were not always different.

### Sera

Sera Vane was three miles south of the Gate, halfway up the scree slope below Widow's Tooth, when the sky tore.

She'd felt the wards failing for hours the way you feel a storm front — pressure behind the eyes, hair rising on her arms, the old scar across her palm aching with a heat she hadn't felt in nine years. She had planned her approach for weeks. She knew the shepherd paths, the blind spots in the valley's sightlines, the exact hour the keeper doused his outer lamps. She had crossed this border twice before, once out and once — the time that had ruined her — back in, and she had told herself the third crossing would be on her terms, quick and quiet, in and out before anyone knew she'd come.

Then the stars had started dying, and every plan had turned to ash in her hands, and she had spent two days running down the map toward a Gate she already knew was lost.

So when the light went up over the notch in the hills — pale, total, wrong, throwing the whole southern sky into sharp relief like a second dawn that had come to the wrong place at the wrong hour — Sera did not waste breath cursing. She dropped her pack, took the slope at a dead run, and ran toward the thing every sane creature in the valley was running away from.

Because she knew what it was. She had seen it once before, from the other side, the night Ostrava was pulled out of the world: a seam opening. And she knew — had always known, had carried the knowledge like a swallowed stone for nine years — that when the seam opened, everything on either side of it would start pouring through.

She crested the shoulder of the hill below the Gatehouse and stopped so hard she nearly went down on the scree.

The valley was gone.

Not hidden. Not fogged. Gone. Where Kestrel's Rest had lain — forty chimneys, the millpond, the crooked lane, the church with its leaning spire, six hundred souls she'd spent a decade pretending she hadn't memorized — there was nothing. A perfect, absolute nothing, edged in starlight, smooth as the surface of deep water. She could see the hills beyond where the valley should have been, hills that had no business being visible, framed inside the absence like a picture hung on a wall of black glass.

Her legs decided before she did. She was moving again, down through the frozen fields, past apple trees sheathed in ice, past a farmhouse with its door standing open and its supper table set — but empty, the chairs pushed back, the whole valley emptied of people as cleanly as if a hand had swept them off the board — and all the while the cold thickened around her until her breath fell as frost that didn't melt.

At the Gate itself, the air was full of salt spray, though there was no sea within four hundred miles. The great arch stood open. Beyond it, instead of the pass, instead of the road she remembered from her first crossing, there stretched a country under a bruised and starless sky — terraced hills, distant towers, a sun nailed low on the horizon like a coin someone had tried to spend and failed.

And on the threshold, crumpled like a dropped coat, was the keeper.

She knew him. Of course she knew him. She'd watched him for years from the far side of the stones, back when watching was all she'd had — the boy who tended the wards, who salted the thresholds, who waved at the postman with a whole heart and no idea what he was guarding or why. Wendell Brightwater. He'd been sixteen the last time she'd seen him up close, all elbows and earnestness, asking her once, shyly, if the stories about the other side were true, and she had lied to him with a smile because lying to gatekeepers was the first law of smuggling.

Now he was thirty-odd and hollow-eyed and unconscious in the salt, and the mark on her palm burned like a brand, and somewhere beyond the arch a kingdom that should have stayed dead was waking up hungry.

"Sera," she said to herself, crouching over him, checking his pulse with fingers gone numb, "you are the biggest fool in two worlds."

He breathed. Thank whatever was left to thank. She got an arm under his shoulders, hauled him back from the threshold — every foot further from the arch felt like walking upstream through a river that wanted him — and got him propped against the Gatehouse wall, out of the worst of the pour.

His eyes opened. Grey, dazed, fixed on her face with no recognition at all, and then — slowly, horribly — with all of it.

"You," he whispered. His voice was wrecked, scraped raw. "You're the girl. The girl who crossed." His gaze slid past her, to the place where his valley had been, and she watched the memory land, watched it break something in him that she suspected had been the load-bearing part. "Where—" He stopped. Started again. "I saw it. I saw it stop."

"I know." She said it flatly, because kindness would crack her open and she could not afford cracks, not here, not now, with the seam wide open and the exiles pouring through and a queen she shared blood with somewhere on the other side of that arch gathering herself. "Listen to me. Can you stand?"

"The valley—"

"Can you *stand*, keeper?"

He stood. It cost him; she saw it cost him, saw him leave most of himself behind in the act, the way a man leaves himself behind in a fire. But he stood, and he looked at her with an expression she recognized because she'd worn it once, nine years ago, on the far side of these same stones: the expression of a person whose entire world has just revealed itself to have been a room, and who has just seen the door.

"What am I?" he asked. Not *what happened*. Not *where is my home*. She would remember that. Whatever else the man was — naive, stubborn, maddening — he had gone straight past the small questions to the one that mattered.

Sera looked at the open Gate. At the pale, unfinished sun crawling up over a country that refused to end. At the first figures already moving down the far slopes — tall, slow, unaging, wearing the fashions of a nation erased from every map, faces she knew, faces she owed.

"You're the reason it happened," she said. "And you might be the only reason it can unhappen. Which means you're coming with me."

She held out her hand. The scar across her palm glowed faintly in the Gate's pale light, and she saw him see it.

Behind them, in the country that had come back from the dead, something began to ring — a bell, or many bells, or one enormous bell heard through the bones of the earth — and the cold poured faster, and the Door at the heart of everything waited.

Wendell Brightwater looked at her hand for a long moment. Then, because he was the keeper and the keeper goes to the Gate, he took it.
