## 5. Ostrava

Wendell woke on a road that had no business existing.

He knew this because he had spent thirty-one years memorizing every road within a day's walk of the Salt Gate — the shepherd's track that switchbacked up Kestrel's spine, the mill road with its washboard ruts, the footpath to the shrine at Coldwell Spring where his grandmother had taught him the warding verses. He knew roads the way other men knew their own hands. And this was not one of them.

It was paved in something like slate and something like shell, veined with faint silver that caught light from no source he could name. It ran straight as a ruled line toward a horizon that refused to resolve — hills there, yes, but hills drawn by someone who had only ever heard hills described. The grass along its edges grew in perfect spirals, each blade identical to its neighbor, as if the land had been stamped rather than grown.

Above him, where the stars should have been, there was nothing. Not cloud. Not night. A blankness, pale and total, like the sky behind a painted backdrop.

"Right," Wendell said aloud, because saying things aloud was how he kept from screaming. "Right."

The last thing he remembered was the Gate. The seals singing that wrong note, the salt on the threshold lifting into the air like a flock of startled birds, the cold pouring through the stones — cold with *weight* to it, cold like a hand closing around his chest — and then the sensation of being pulled through his own doorway by the collar of his coat, backward, into the place he had spent his whole life guarding against. He had seen the valley as he went. Seen it the way you see a room through a closing door: the orchards, the rooftops of Little Fenn, the smoke of morning fires.

And then the valley had simply stopped. Not faded. Stopped, the way a clock stops, all its motion gathered up and put away somewhere he couldn't follow.

Now he stood on an impossible road in a country that did not exist, wearing his nightshirt and one boot, and the only thing he had managed to hold onto was the iron key that hung at his throat — the gatekeeper's key, warm against his sternum, warmer than it had ever been.

*Walk or sit,* he told himself. *Those are your options. Sitting is how you die.*

He walked.

* * *

The country announced itself slowly, the way weather does.

First the road acquired companions: waystones taller than a man, carved with faces worn smooth by centuries of rain that may never have fallen here. Then fences of black iron whose bars curved like handwriting. Then, cresting a rise, he saw the first city — or what he took for a city until his eyes adjusted and understood that no one could have built it, because it was still building itself.

Towers rose out of the ground like teeth. Bridges arched between them, some complete, some half-finished, their ends trailing stone filaments that reached for one another across the gaps with the slow patience of vines. Spires leaned at angles that would have toppled any structure in the valley, and did not topple, and seemed to Wendell's trained eye — the eye of a man who had maintained one wall for three decades — to be *arguing* with gravity and winning on a technicality.

And everywhere, people.

They came out of doorways and down spiral stairs and up from cellars, and they stopped when they saw him, and they stared, and Wendell understood why. They were dressed in fashions forty years dead — high collars, long coats, dresses the color of wine and rust — and they were all, every one of them, unmarked by time. A woman with white hair and the unlined face of a girl of twenty. An old man's stoop on a young man's body. They moved carefully, the way sleepwalkers move, and their eyes had the shine of people looking at something they had been promised and long since stopped expecting.

"You're *bleeding*," said the first one who spoke, a broad-shouldered fellow in a butcher's apron, pointing at Wendell's bare foot. "You're bleeding and it's *red*."

"It usually is," Wendell said.

The butcher laughed — a cracked, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering its purpose — and then pressed both hands over his mouth as though the laugh had escaped without permission. Around him, the others were murmuring. Wendell caught fragments. *From outside. Through the Gate. It opened.* One woman simply sat down in the road and wept into her apron, quietly, the way people cry when they have practiced not being heard.

"I'm looking for—" Wendell began, and realized he didn't know what he was looking for. Home was gone. The Gate was behind him, wherever behind was. "I'm looking for the way back."

No one answered him directly. But the butcher took his arm — gently, reverently, the way you'd handle something rescued from a fire — and said, "Come inside before the sun thinks about it. You don't want to be standing in the open when it tries."

"Tries?"

"You'll see," the butcher said, and his face did something complicated. "Everyone sees."

* * *

They gave him tea in a house that was larger inside than out, in a room whose windows looked out onto three different streets, none of which matched the street the front door faced. The tea tasted of salt and honey and something medicinal he couldn't name, and it steadied him more than he wanted to admit. His host — the butcher, whose name was Corvin — sat across from him with his huge hands wrapped around his own cup, watching Wendell drink with an expression of naked hunger that had nothing to do with food.

"Say something else," Corvin said. "Anything. I haven't heard an outside voice in — I don't actually know. We don't count years well here. Years are a thing that happens to places that end."

"This is Ostrava," Wendell said. Not a question. He'd known the name since childhood, the way you know the name of the thing under your bed: from stories meant to frighten children into obedience. *Stay away from the Gate. That's where Ostrava went.* His grandmother had never said more than that, and her silence had always been louder than her warnings.

"Was," Corvin corrected softly. "Is again, apparently. You'd know better than us — we can't see past the hills anymore. Used to be able to, before." He waved a hand at the blank sky. "Before the sky forgot us."

"The sky didn't forget you," Wendell said, and then stopped, because he hadn't known he was going to say it. But it was true; he felt its truth settle in his chest like a stone dropped in a well. "The sky went out because — my grandmother's journal. She wrote about it. *When the sky forgets us, the Gate forgets its purpose.* I thought she meant our sky. Our stars."

Corvin set down his cup very carefully.

"Our stars," Wendell said, "and yours. They're the same stars, aren't they. They always were. And when the wards started failing — when the Gate started forgetting what it was for — the sky couldn't hold both countries anymore. So it let go of the one that was already—" He searched for the word and hated every candidate. "Already erased."

The room was silent. In the walls, faintly, Wendell heard the house shifting — timbers settling, or the house equivalent of breathing.

"That's not how Tam told it," said a voice from the doorway. A woman stood there, gray-haired and twenty-faced, holding a basket of bread that steamed in the cool air. "Tam was a warden's clerk, before. He said the stars went out one at a time, over years, and each one was a town. He said the sky was being *spent*."

"Spent on what?" Wendell asked.

"On not ending," the woman said simply, and put the bread down, and left the room, and would not come back no matter how Corvin called.

* * *

He saw the sun try that afternoon.

Corvin walked him out to a hillside above the half-built city, along with perhaps two hundred others, because apparently this was a thing people did — gathered on the western slopes at the hour when the sun should have set, to watch it fail. They arranged themselves on the grass with the grim familiarity of a congregation, and someone passed around flasks, and an old soldier type near the front had brought a stool.

The light came up out of the west like a rumor. It never broke the horizon. It raised a glow — gold, then bruised orange, then a thin, exhausted rose — that lit the undersides of the clouds and turned the impossible towers to silhouette, and then, having gotten that far, it stopped. It hung there. The whole sky held its breath around a sun that strained at the edge of the world and could not lift itself over.

"It's been trying to rise since the erasure," Corvin said quietly. "Forty years of dawn that never finishes. Some days it gets further than others. On good days you can almost see the disc." He glanced at Wendell. "On bad days it goes backwards."

"Why doesn't it just—"

"Because a sunrise is a promise that time is moving," said the old soldier on the stool, without turning around. "And time isn't moving here, boy. Time is what they took from us. A kingdom that ends has mornings. A kingdom that's been *refused* its ending—" He spat, with great precision, off the side of the hill. "Gets this."

Wendell stood in the unfinished light among two hundred people who could not die, and felt the shape of the truth arrive in him whole, the way grief arrives, the way storms arrive — not gradually but all at once, already overhead.

The stars hadn't gone out as an attack. They had gone out as an *accounting*. Somewhere beyond the Salt Gate, in the valley he had guarded so faithfully, in the quiet green country where nothing dangerous was ever allowed to happen, a debt had been coming due for forty years. Ostrava had been erased — unwritten, unremembered, denied even the mercy of destruction — and the erasure had to be paid for out of something, and the something was the sky itself. Every star snuffed was a candle burned to keep a lie lit. And now the candles were gone, and the lie was bankrupt, and the wound called the Salt Gate had torn open, and everything that had been hidden was pouring through.

Including him.

"They sent you," the old soldier said, turning at last. His eyes were pale as skimmed milk. "The valley finally sent somebody through. Took them long enough."

"The valley didn't send me. The Gate pulled me through. I'm the keeper — I mean, I *was* the keeper, I kept the wards, I never wanted—" Wendell heard his own voice climb and made himself stop. Breathe. His grandmother's trick: breathe, count the stones, name what is real. "I tended the Gate my whole life. I thought I was keeping something *out*."

"You were," Corvin said. There was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. "That was the job, wasn't it? Keeping us out. Keeping us forgotten. Somebody's grandfather built that wall, and somebody's grandmother salted the thresholds, and none of them ever asked what it cost the people on this side." He looked out at the strangled dawn with an expression too tired for anger. "We're not monsters, keeper. We're just the price. And prices don't stop being owed because the buyer looks away."

* * *

He walked the city for three days. Or what he took for days; without a true sun, time here was a matter of consensus, bells rung at intervals someone had decided upon decades ago and no one had the heart to change. The bells rang six times a day, and between the fourth and fifth bell the light dimmed to a false dusk, and people slept, and the city dreamed audibly — Wendell could hear it, a low murmur in the stones, streets rearranging themselves in the dark with a sound like a deck being shuffled.

He learned the rules quickly, because rules were the one language he was fluent in.

The architecture obeyed no plan but had habits. Doorways migrated; you learned to knock on walls and wait for an answer. Water ran uphill in the eastern quarter and nobody remarked on it. The exiles did not age, did not hunger in any way that killed them, and could not leave — he watched a man walk west for a full day and arrive back where he'd started without ever turning, the road folding beneath him like a tongue returning a stone to the mouth. The country held its people the way a fist holds sand.

And underneath everything, everywhere, the salt. It crusted the waystones and rimmed the fountains and glittered in the mortar of the oldest buildings, and when Wendell touched it, the key at his throat warmed, and something in his blood answered — a low, wordless recognition, like hearing your own name spoken in a crowd.

On the third evening he found the mural.

It covered the entire face of a granary wall, sixty feet of painted history, and it stopped him the way a blow stops you. Ostrava as it had been: a river city, bright-roofed, its harbor thick with masts, its sky *full of stars* — the painter had given it more stars than any real sky, hundreds of them, dense as a spilled sack of silver grain. Below the city, processions of figures. Kings with kind faces. Farmers. Dancers. And at the bottom, painted smaller than all the rest, a line of figures walking away into darkness, and above them, in letters that someone had later tried to scrub off and failed:

**THEY TOOK THE SKY SO WE WOULD NOT BE REMEMBERED.**

Someone had added fresh paint beneath, recent, careful: **BUT WE REMEMBER.**

Wendell stood before it until the false dusk came down, and his legs gave out, and he sat down in the dust at the mural's foot like a man at a graveside.

Because he recognized the handiwork. Not the painting — the *scrubbing*. The attempt to erase the erasure. He knew those strokes. He had made ten thousand of them himself, over thirty years, with a salt-brush and a bucket, smoothing the wards, renewing the seals, keeping the stones of the Salt Gate clean and blind and silent. He had thought it was maintenance. Tending. Duty, handed down like a hearth-fire from grandmother to grandson, pure and simple and good.

It had been censorship. He had been the censor. Every verse he'd chanted, every threshold he'd salted, every morning he'd walked the wall with his little brush while the valley slept safe behind him — it had all been one long act of forgetting, performed by the only man willing to do it, on behalf of everyone who preferred not to know.

And the worst of it — the part that sat in his chest like swallowed ice — was that it had *worked*. He had been happy. All those years of small contentment, the postman's gossip, the kettle on the Gatehouse stove, the pride of a job done well: all of it built on a country's grave, and he had never once, not once, wondered what the wall was for.

"First time sitting at the wall?" said a voice behind him.

A girl — no, a woman, though she had a girl's face and a grandmother's posture — stood at his shoulder with a lantern she didn't need. She nodded at the mural.

"Everyone sits here eventually," she said. "Usually right after they understand. Before, they just look. After, they sit." She lowered herself beside him, joints cracking, face unlined. "How long were you the keeper?"

"Thirty-one years."

"Mm. My father was a customs man at the east wharf for fifty. Same face you've got. The face of a man doing arithmetic he can't finish." She handed him the lantern, oddly formal, like passing a torch. "You'll want to know one thing before anything else, so I'll say it plain. There's a queen come back with us. Ilsette Vane. She was our last general, and she has decided that since we were denied an ending once, we shall never have one at all — and she means to pay for eternity with whatever sky is left, including yours, including whatever's left of your valley. Half the city loves her for it. Half is afraid of her. Both halves are right."

Wendell's hands tightened around the lantern. Somewhere across the darkening city, a bell rang the fifth hour, and the towers shifted, resettling, like a sleeper turning over.

"And the other thing," the woman said, rising, brushing off her dress. "The thing you really came to ask. Yes — there's a way to end it properly. A Door, in the old capital, at the heart. The honest ones have been looking for it for forty years." She paused at the edge of the lantern light, and her young-old face was unreadable. "But I'd hurry, keeper. Because the queen's hunters found your trail this morning, and Ilsette Vane has been waiting a very long time to meet the man whose blood holds the stitch."
