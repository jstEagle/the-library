## 6. Sera's Debt

Sera Vane had crossed the Salt Gate twice in her life, and both times she had lied about it.

The first lie was small, the kind you tell a customs man: *nothing to declare, nothing to fear*. The second lie she had told herself for eleven years, and it had grown fat and comfortable in the dark of her — *you went back for them, you tried, there was nothing more to do*.

Now she stood on the far side of a shattered threshold with salt dust burning in her throat and the second lie dying in her chest, and the truth stood up inside her like a blade being drawn.

She had followed Wendell Brightwater through on purpose.

* * *

The crossing itself had not been gentle. Nothing about the Gate was gentle when it broke. She had felt the wards fail from three valleys away — a sound like a bell struck underwater, a note that lived in the teeth rather than the ears — and she had run the last two miles of the pass with her lungs full of frost and her heart doing something that wasn't beating so much as hammering at a door.

She'd reached the Gatehouse as the stars were going out over Kestrel's Rest, one by one, like lamps being carried away down a long corridor. She had watched Wendell stand in his doorway with a lantern held up like a man defending a hill with a stick. She had watched the seals split. She had watched the cold come through — not wind-cold, not winter-cold, but the cold of a place that had been holding its breath for a century and finally exhaled.

And she had watched him get taken.

That was the word for it, though it wasn't the whole truth. The Gate hadn't dragged him so much as *claimed* him; he had gone through the shattering stones the way water goes down a drain, spiraling, arms out, mouth open around a shout that never finished. And Sera, crouched behind the boundary wall with her hood up and her knife loose in its sheath, had made a choice in the space of half a heartbeat.

She could have stayed. The valley would have needed people, after. Someone to organize the survivors, count the dead, bury what couldn't be saved. There was honor in that work. There was safety in it too, and Sera had spent eleven years learning that safety was just debt with the interest deferred.

Instead she had stepped into the light.

It had taken her the way it took him — no, not the way. It had taken her *rougher*, because the Gate knew her blood and remembered what she owed. For a moment she had been nowhere, everywhere, every age at once; she had tasted copper and candlewax and someone else's grief; she had heard a woman's voice saying her name in a tone she hadn't heard since she was nineteen years old.

Then stone under her hands. Night air. A sky with no stars in it at all — not clouded, not moonless, but *empty*, the way a hearth is empty after the fire has been raked out. And somewhere below her, down a slope of black grass, a lantern still burning in a dead man's grip.

Not a dead man. Not yet. But the lantern told her where he was before her eyes did, and she went down the slope fast and quiet, because old habits are older than decisions.

* * *

Wendell Brightwater woke the way a door slams.

One moment nothing; the next he was on his feet with the lantern swinging and his voice cracking out across the dark grass like a whip — "Who's there! I can hear you! I'm armed!" He was not armed. He was holding a salt spoon. Sera watched him realize this, watched the shame move across his face like weather, and something in her chest twisted that she refused to name.

"It's me," she said, stepping into the lantern light with her hands visible, palms out. "Vane. Sera Vane. From the valley."

He stared at her. In the lantern light his face was all angles and disbelief, a young man's face wearing an old man's expression — the look of someone who has buried everything familiar in a single night and is waiting to be told it was a mistake.

"You," he said. "You're the smuggler. You're the girl who—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "You came back."

"I did."

"Nobody comes back." His voice shook on the word. "My grandmother said nobody comes back. Nobody crosses and nobody comes back and nobody— where *are* we? Where is the valley?" He turned a slow circle, lantern raised, and the light found only grass, black grass, rolling away into a darkness that had no edges. No valley walls. No mountains. No sky worth the name. "There was a valley," he said, quieter now, and the fury in his voice was starting to crack along its seams into something worse. "There was a whole *valley*, and I was *in* it, and I was keeping the Gate, and now—"

"Wendell."

"Don't say my name." He rounded on her, and the lantern light caught tears he didn't know he was shedding, running clean lines down a face streaked with salt dust. "Don't you dare stand there and say my name like we're neighbors. You *knew*. Didn't you? You knew this would happen. You were there, you were watching, you were hiding behind my wall like a thief while my whole life—"

"Yes," Sera said.

The word landed between them like a dropped stone. He blinked. Whatever he had expected — denial, excuse, a fight — it wasn't honesty, and honesty left him standing in the wreckage of his own momentum.

"I knew the wards were failing," she said. "I've known for months. I came to warn you, if it helps. It probably doesn't help."

"Warn me." He laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as the salt he'd tended all his life. "Warn me how? 'Excuse me, keeper, your entire world is a lid on a pot and the pot's about to boil'?"

"If you'd rather I'd let you be surprised—"

"I'd rather you'd told me *before*!" The lantern swung wild. "I'd rather one person in that whole valley had looked at me, one time, in thirty years, and said, *Wendell, the thing you guard isn't a wall, it's a wound, and you're the bandage.* I'd rather anyone had thought I deserved the truth instead of the *duty*!" His voice broke on the last word and he hated it, she could see him hating it, hating the crack, hating the tears, hating her for witnessing both. "I gave that valley everything. My grandmother gave it everything. Her mother before her. And it was all — what? A cage? A lie? Some kind of *maintenance*?"

Sera said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't make it worse, and she had learned long ago that silence was a tool like any other, and crueler than most.

He caught his breath eventually. Men always did; grief ran on a clock even when it didn't want to. When he spoke again his voice was low and level and much more dangerous than the shouting had been.

"Why did you follow me?"

Ah. There it was.

"Because you'll die out here in a day without help," she said.

"That's not an answer. That's a reason. Why did you *follow* me?"

Because the exiles of Ostrava had fed her when she was nineteen and starving. Because they had hidden her in a cellar in a city whose streets rearranged themselves, and taught her which doors were lies, and given her passage out when the queen's hunters closed in — and she had promised, with her hand pressed flat against a dying woman's door, that she would come back. That she would find a way to end what was being done to them. That she would not forget.

Because she had forgotten anyway, or made forgetting into a profession, and eleven years of successful smuggling had taught her exactly how heavy a promise gets when you carry it long enough.

Because the Gate had known all of that, and had taken her rough, and had whispered her name in a dead woman's voice, and she was tired — gods, she was so tired — of paying interest on a debt instead of the principal.

"Debt," she said aloud, because Wendell Brightwater deserved better than another lie, even if he'd hate it. Especially if he'd hate it. "I owe the people here. I crossed once before, years ago. They helped me. I said I'd come back and fix things. I didn't. So when the Gate opened, I walked through it. On purpose. With my eyes open."

He stared at her for a long moment. The lantern flame steadied between them, and beyond it the starless dark seemed to lean in and listen.

"You knew," he said again, but softer now, turning the words over like stones to see what lived underneath. "All this time. You knew what the Gate was."

"I knew some of it."

"And you let me keep it anyway. You let me salt thresholds and check wards and live my whole small life on top of a wound, and you never said a word."

"Yes," Sera said, and found that the armor of guilt fit her as well as it ever had, buckled tight across the chest, weighing exactly what it had always weighed. "I did. And I'll answer for it. But not tonight, and not here, because in about twenty minutes the lights you can see on that ridge—" she pointed east, to a faint smudge of gold against the empty sky, "—are going to start moving, and when they move, they hunt, and right now every ward in this country can feel your blood the way a dog feels thunder."

His hand went instinctively to his wrist — to the skin where, since the crossing, thin lines of silver had begun to surface, faint as old scars, tracing the paths his grandmother's journal had drawn in its final pages. The stitch-marks. He looked at them the way a man looks at a diagnosis.

"What am I?" he asked quietly.

"Trouble," Sera said. "For the queen, for the hunters, and mostly for yourself. Which is why you're going to pick up that lantern, and you're going to walk east with me, and you're going to stay angry at me the entire way, because anger will keep you upright better than grief will. Grief you can do later. In a cellar. Preferably a deep one."

He didn't move. The starless dark pressed close, and somewhere far off across the black grass, something howled — not a wolf, not anything with a throat, a sound like a hinge the size of the horizon.

"They're real," he whispered. "The exiles. Ostrava. All of it. It's all *real*."

"Oh, it's real," Sera said grimly, shouldering her pack. "Real enough to have fed me once. Real enough to have a queen who thinks eternity is a favor she's doing everyone. Real enough to kill you, keeper, unless you walk."

Another howl, closer. The golden smudge on the ridge had begun, very slowly, to move.

Wendell Brightwater looked at the darkness where his valley used to be. Then at the silver lines rising on his own wrist, catching the lantern light like veins of ore. Then at her.

"I'm not forgiving you," he said.

"I'd worry if you did."

"But I'm coming with you. Because you owe them, and apparently I—" he flexed his hand, watching the silver shift, and his voice dropped to something raw and wondering and furious all at once, "—apparently I *am* the debt. Or the payment. Or whatever I am. And I want answers, Sera Vane. Every single one. Before anyone asks me for anything else."

"Fair terms," she said, and meant it, and hated how much relief felt like weakness. "Walk fast, keeper. Talk never killed anyone. Yet."

They went east together, toward the moving gold, one carrying a lantern against a sunless dark and the other carrying eleven years of unpaid promises — and neither of them saw, high on the southern ridge, a rider in pale armor pause, turn toward the scent of keeper's blood on the night wind, and raise a horn to lips that had not breathed in a hundred years.

The horn's note rolled across the black grass like the first syllable of a verdict.

* * *

Behind them, the dark listened.
