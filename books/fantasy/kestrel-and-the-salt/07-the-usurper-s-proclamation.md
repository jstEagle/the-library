## 7. The Usurper's Proclamation

The square had been called the Square of Weights and Measures once, back when Ostrava was a country that traded in honest things — grain, salt, wool, the hours of a working day. Now the scales were gone from their niches, melted down or repurposed, and in their place stood the Pale Court's ward-engines: brass and bone contraptions that hummed like hives and drank something invisible out of the air.

Wendell felt it before he understood it. He had been in Ostrava three days, hidden in the upper rooms of a dyer's house with Sera, watching the streets through slats of shuttered glass, and all three days his blood had been singing a low, wrong note. It took him until the second morning to name the feeling. It was the feeling of a threshold being crossed. Of wards being pulled taut. Except here, nothing was being kept *out*. Something was being kept *in*.

"They're drinking the valley," Sera said, when he finally said it aloud.

She sat on the floor with her back against the wall, sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen. Her hands were steady. Her eyes were not.

"The valley's magic," Wendell said. "The little there is."

"There's more than you think. Your grandmother knew it. The Gatekeepers built Kestrel's Rest on top of a wellspring — that's why the Gate could be fed for six hundred years without anyone in the valley ever noticing anything was missing." She tested the edge on her thumb, didn't draw blood, seemed disappointed. "The engines are siphoning it across the wound. Every hearth-fire that burns a shade too fast, every child who tires too young, every crop that comes up thin. That's not blight, Brightwater. That's *taxation*."

"And no one stops them."

"Who?" Sera gestured at the window, at the pale sun that never climbed past mid-morning, at the towers that leaned at angles architecture should not permit. "Half these people can't remember wanting anything else. The other half remember perfectly well, and they've learned what remembering costs."

---

Ilsette Vane dressed for the proclamation the way she had once dressed for battle: plainly, deliberately, in grey wool and unpolished steel, so that no one could say the queen had put on splendor while her people went hungry. Splendor was for the Court. For the people she wore the uniform of the last campaign, the one they had lost, the one that had ended not in defeat but in *erasure* — which was worse than defeat, because defeat at least left graves.

Her chamberlain — a nervous man named Ostrevan who had been old when the kingdom vanished and was old still, unaging, his wrinkles fixed on his face like a sentence already served — helped her pin the brooch at her collar. It was a small thing, tin, shaped like a kestrel in flight. Every soldier of the Third March had carried one. Hers was the only one left in the world, as far as she knew, and she wore it over her heart the way other monarchs wore crowns.

"You'll speak from the Balcony of Accounts," Ostrevan said. "The engines are positioned in the square. When you finish, the absorption will be visible. The people will see the valley's strength flowing in. It will be—" he searched for the word, "—*reassuring*."

"It will be theft," Ilsette said, "made visible. Which is better than theft made invisible. At least then they know what their eternity costs."

Ostrevan flinched. He had never learned how to take her honesty. In the old days generals were not permitted honesty; they were permitted certainty. Ilsette had been certain for thirty years and it had gotten her kingdom erased, and somewhere in the long dark between erasure and return she had decided that if certainty was a lie, she would rather lie beautifully than tell the truth badly.

She looked at herself in the glass. A broad woman, weathered, silver-streaked, with her niece's same sharp jaw and none of her niece's softness. *Sera*, she thought, and the thought arrived with its usual cargo of grief and fury in equal weight. The girl had come to her years ago asking for passage out, and Ilsette had given it, because family was family even when family broke your heart — and the girl had taken the passage and never sent so much as a letter, and now the kingdom had returned and Sera Vane was somewhere in it, hiding, and Ilsette's hunters reported a second figure with her. A young man. Valley-born. With keeper's blood in him like a lantern in his veins.

*You could have come home,* she thought at her absent niece. *I would have forgiven you everything.*

She pinned the kestrel straighter and went out to lie to her people magnificently.

---

They heard the proclamation from the dyer's house, which stood close enough to the square that the queen's voice came up through the shutters with unnatural clarity — carried, Wendell realized, by the ward-engines themselves, the same machines that drank the valley's magic now delivering its usurper's words to every ear in the city.

"People of Ostrava," the voice said, and it was a good voice, low and worn and certain, the voice of someone who had slept in ditches beside soldiers and meant it. "For six hundred years we were stolen. Not conquered — stolen. Our names were taken from the maps. Our dead were taken from their graves' remembrance. Our children's children were never born, because the world decided we had never lived."

In the dyer's room, Wendell found he was holding his breath.

"I will not lie to you," the queen continued. "Our return has cost. The sun rises slowly because the sky must learn our shape again. The roads wander because the land remembers being erased and does not trust itself. And so I have done a hard thing. I have reached across the wound that was cut through us, and I have taken back what was always ours — the strength that fed the Gate that imprisoned us. The valley called Kestrel's Rest grew fat on our stolen years. Now it pays its debt, and we grow strong, and no power in this world or under it will erase us twice."

A roar went up from the square. Wendell pressed his eye to the shutter-slat and saw thousands of faces turned up toward the Balcony of Accounts, and the terrible thing — the thing he would carry with him for the rest of whatever life he had left — was that the faces were not afraid. They were *grateful*. An old woman near the fountain was weeping. A boy on his father's shoulders pumped his fist at the sky that never fully brightened. They had been erased, and here was a woman promising, in a soldier's plain voice, that it would never happen again, and what was tyranny compared to that? What was a little stolen fire from a valley they'd never seen?

"She believes it," Wendell said quietly. "That's the worst part. She isn't lying."

"Lying's easy to fight," Sera said. She hadn't gone to the window. She sat facing the wall, and her voice had a flatness he'd learned to fear. "It's conviction that kills you."

Then the queen said the name, and everything changed.

---

"There is one among you," Ilsette said, and the crowd's roar gentled into silence the way a sea flattens before wind it trusts, "who says my reach is theft. Who has drawn maps — beautiful maps, I will grant it — showing the Door at the heart of our capital. Showing how it might be opened. Showing how our kingdom might be *ended*."

On the Balcony of Accounts, guards brought forward a thin man in an ink-stained apron. His hands were bound with grey cord. He did not struggle. He looked out at the crowd with the mild, nearsighted expression of a man checking his own work for errors.

"Corvus Aldemar," the queen named him. "Cartographer to the old court. A man I once trusted with the shape of the world." Something moved behind her certainty then, some small tremor, quickly buried. "He says a country has no right to live forever. He says we should open the Door and let ourselves end, like a candle, like a song. I ask you: after six hundred years of being told you do not exist, do you accept a man who tells you to stop?"

"*No!*" The square answered as one throat.

"Then witness what happens to those who would unmake us."

What happened next, Wendell watched through two inches of shuttered gap, unable to look away, his hand white-knuckled on the sill.

They did not hang Corvus Aldemar. Hanging was for traitors, and the queen's law had apparently decided that mapmakers were something else. They brought him to the largest of the ward-engines — a towering thing of brass rings and humming cores, taller than the fountain, its surface crawling with engraved script that hurt to read directly — and they bound him to it, standing, arms spread, and Wendell understood with a lurch what the machine was for before anyone said a word.

It drank him.

Not his blood. Not his breath. Something finer. The engine's hum deepened, sweetened, and Corvus Aldemar began to fade the way a drawing fades — edges first, then features, then the ink-stained hands, until the grey cord fell through the space where his wrists had been and lay coiled on the cobbles. The crowd made a sound Wendell had never heard a crowd make: not horror, not triumph. *Relief.* Six hundred years of hunger, fed one dissident at a time.

And in the last instant before he was wholly gone, the cartographer turned his fading face — not to the queen, not to the crowd, but outward, past the square, past the city, with the precision of a man who had spent his life plotting true north — directly toward the window where Wendell stood.

His lips moved. No sound came. But Wendell's blood, the keeper's blood, the stitch-blood, read the shape of the words as clearly as if they had been written on his own skin:

*The Door remembers. Find the half.*

Then he was gone, and the engine's rings spun down to a satisfied idle, and the queen raised her hand and said, "Let it be known: Ostrava is eternal," and the crowd roared again, and somewhere in the dyer's house Sera Vane finally made a sound — a single syllable, swallowed instantly, that might have been a name.

Wendell turned. She was on her feet. All the steadiness she'd worn like armor for three days had cracked clean through, and beneath it was something raw and young and terrified, and it took him a moment to understand why, because the executed man had been a stranger to both of them.

But the queen had not been.

"Sera," Wendell said carefully. "Who is she? To you?"

Sera looked at him, and for the first time since he'd met her — since the night she'd slipped through his Gate like a rumor given boots — she looked *caught*.

"Ilsette Vane," she said, "raised me. Taught me to read stars and lies. Fed me from her own plate when my mother died." Her jaw worked. "She's my aunt. And six years ago, when I asked her to get me out of this place, she asked me one question in return, and I answered it, and I have been running from that answer ever since."

"What question?"

Sera picked up her knife, put it down again, and looked at the shutters, where the light of a sun that never finished rising lay in thin grey bars across the floor.

"'How do the valley's wards fail?'"

Outside, the ward-engines hummed their deep, fed, contented hum, and far away — though Wendell felt it in his teeth, in his stitched and singing blood — the first of Kestrel's Rest's remaining wards quietly went dark.

* * *

That night, in the dyer's attic, Sera told him everything she could bear to tell, and several things she couldn't, and when she finished, the silence between them had a new weight to it, the weight of a door neither of them dared touch.

But it was Wendell who broke it, because his blood had begun to sing again — a different note now, urgent, directional, pulling eastward through the city's wandering streets like a compass needle finding its north.

"Sera," he said. "The cartographer's last words. *Find the half.* Half of what?"

Sera went very still. Then she reached into her coat and drew out a folded scrap of heavy paper, creased and refolded so many times the fibers had gone soft as cloth, and opened it on the floor between them.

It was a map. Or the beginning of one — the western quarter of the city of Ostrava, drawn in a fine sure hand, and at its center, where the capital's heart should have been, a circle marked with a symbol Wendell knew better than his own name: the gatekeeper's knot, the sign that had hung above the Salt Gate for six hundred years.

"Corvus Aldemar wasn't the only one who drew maps of the Door," Sera said. "Six years ago, before I ran, someone pressed this into my hands and told me to carry it out of the country and never come back." Her voice dropped. "I never knew who tore it in half. Or where the other half went."

The map lay between them in the lamplight, and the gatekeeper's knot gleamed like a small closed eye, and somewhere across the city a bell that had not rung in six hundred years began, very slowly, to toll.
