## 9. Road Through the Ash Orchards

The caravan moved at the pace of people who had nowhere to be.

Sera noticed it within the first hour of falling in among them — that particular unhurried rhythm, oxen plodding and children dawdling and old men stopping to argue about fence posts as though time were a well they could draw from forever. She'd forgotten it. Or she'd made herself forget it, which amounted to the same thing in the end.

There were perhaps sixty of them, the exiles of the ash orchards, traveling the long road east toward the drowned capital with their wagons and their goats and their impossible patience. They could not die. They could not leave. And so they walked, generation after generation of the same faces, through a country where the sun never climbed higher than a coin held at arm's length.

"They're staring at you," Wendell murmured at her shoulder.

"They're staring at *you*," Sera said. "You're the first new face most of them have seen since before the erasure."

"That's not true." He nodded ahead, to where a woman walked beside the lead wagon. "That one keeps looking at you like she's deciding something."

Sera didn't answer. She knew exactly what Yeva was deciding, and she knew how the decision would go, because Yeva had been deciding it for eleven years and had never once changed her mind.

The road ran between the orchards that gave the country its only color. Ash trees, the exiles called them — though they were not truly ash, and not truly trees either, but something Ostrava had grown in the last desperate years before the erasure, when its mages had tried to feed the land with magic instead of sun. The trunks were grey-white and smooth as bone. The leaves were silver on one side and dark on the other, and when the wind moved through them the whole orchard shimmered like water, like fish turning, like ten thousand small mirrors refusing to hold still. The fruit was pale and heavy and no one ate it. It fell and lay in the furrows and did not rot.

Wendell reached out as they passed under the first branches, and Sera caught his wrist.

"Don't touch them."

He looked at her hand on his arm, then at her face. "Why?"

"Because everything here remembers being touched." She let him go. "And some things remember longer than others."

He pulled his hand back slowly, and she watched him look at the orchard differently after that — not with fear, exactly. Wendell didn't seem built for fear the way other men were. He looked at it with the careful attention of a man checking a ward-stone for cracks, cataloguing what was wrong so he could mend it later. That was going to get him killed, she thought. Or it was going to save everyone. With Wendell Brightwater she genuinely could not tell which.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath a hill crowned with ruined windmills, their sails long since fallen, their towers leaning together like conspirators. The exiles pitched tents and lit fires from wood that burned without warmth — another of Ostrava's compromises — and Sera sat apart, as she always sat apart, sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen.

She heard him coming. Of course she did; Wendell walked like a man who had spent his life listening for footsteps and assumed everyone else deserved the same courtesy.

"You knew them," he said. "Before. You knew these people."

It wasn't a question, but she treated it like one anyway. "I told you I crossed the Gate once."

"You told me you crossed it. You didn't tell me you'd lived here." He lowered himself onto the rock beside her, uninvited, patient. "Eleven years, Sera. That woman — Yeva — she watched you all day like someone watches weather they've been promised. And the old man by the second wagon crossed himself when you went past, but not the way you cross yourself against evil. The way you do against grief."

Sera turned the knife over in her hands. The whetstone lay idle in her lap.

"There was a family," she said finally. "The Hallowells. Tam and Marta and their boy, Piet. Tam was a ward-smith — a real one, not a salt-and-superstition keeper like my family pretended to be. He'd worked on the Gate itself, back when the kingdom still had gate-smiths. When I came through the first time, half-dead, seventeen years old, with nothing but a stolen compass and my aunt's name in my mouth, they took me in. Marta fed me. Tam taught me the deep wards, the ones your grandmother's journal doesn't have. Piet followed me around for two summers calling me his sister."

The fire across the camp popped. Somewhere a child laughed, and the sound was wrong here, too bright, too alive for a country that had been refused its ending.

"Ilsette found out what Tam knew," Sera went on. "What he could build. She wanted him to make her something — a key, an engine, I never learned exactly what. He refused. So she gave him a choice: his family dies slowly, or he works for her and they live. And Tam — stubborn, decent Tam — he chose a third thing. He sent them away. Hid them with the caravan, out here in the orchards where the Court's riders don't like to go. And he came to me, because I was the only person he knew who could move unseen between worlds, and he asked me to take his wife and son through the Gate. Out of Ostrava entirely. To anywhere. He'd stay behind and keep Ilsette busy."

"And you said yes," Wendell said quietly.

"I said I'd try." The knife stopped moving. "I got Marta as far as the foothills below the Gate. Two days' walk. And then I stopped."

"Why?"

"Because I understood, standing there, that if I took them through, I couldn't come back. The Gate only opens for blood, Wendell. Keeper's blood or smuggler's cunning, and I had exactly enough cunning for one crossing each way. If I brought them out, I'd be stranding myself in your valley — a stranger with no papers, no story, in the one place in the world built to keep people like me out. And if I stayed—" She made herself say it plainly. "If I stayed, I owed Ilsette a debt for my passage, and debts here are paid in kind. She wanted a smuggler. Someone who could go where her soldiers couldn't."

"So you left them in the hills."

"I left them in the hills," Sera said, "and I walked back to the Court, and I told myself it was strategy. Told myself I'd find another way for them, later, once I'd earned my freedom. Eleven years, Wendell. Marta died in the third year — not dead the way we mean it, just *stopped*, the way things stop here when they give up. Piet is twenty-six now and has been twenty-six for four years. He's in this caravan. Third wagon. He won't look at me, and I haven't given him a reason to start."

The silence between them stretched long enough that she could hear the orchards breathing — that faint silver rustle that never quite stopped.

"You could have told me," Wendell said at last.

"When? On the road, before I'd decided whether to trust you? After?" She shook her head. "You look at me like I'm something worth following into hell, Brightwater. I wasn't going to hand you the reason not to."

"That's not why I'm angry."

"No," she agreed. "You're angry because I lied. And underneath that, you're angry because you know I'd do it again." She met his eyes. "Wouldn't I?"

He didn't answer, which was its own answer, and she respected him for it. Most people lied beautifully at moments like this. Wendell just sat with the truth of her, frowning at the fire like it was a ward that had failed, trying to work out how to mend a person instead of a wall.

"Come on," she said, standing. "If you're going to travel with these people, there's someone you should meet. And someone who deserves to hear it from me before he hears it from anyone else."

* * *

Piet Hallowell was mending a wagon axle by lantern-light, his sleeves rolled, his hands black to the elbow with grease that never washed fully clean. Up close the wrongness of him was worse — he had the face of a young man and the eyes of someone who had buried his mother and then kept living inside the burial. He saw them coming and did not stand. Did not put down his tools. Just looked at Sera with an expression she had rehearsed receiving for eleven years and found, in practice, unbearable.

"Sera Vane," he said. "The caravan says you've come back."

"I've come back."

"Took you long enough to finish the job."

"Piet—"

"My mother waited in those hills for six days." His voice stayed level, which was somehow worse than shouting. "Six days, in the cold, with a boy who wouldn't stop asking when the lady was coming back. Then the queen's riders swept the pass, and we ran, and we've been running in circles ever since, because there is nowhere in this country to run *to*. You knew that. You were the one person alive who knew what was outside, and you knew we'd never see it, and you walked away anyway."

"Yes."

Not an excuse. Not an explanation. Just the word, set down between them like a stone. Sera had learned that much from eleven years of carrying it: explanations were a kind of theft. They took the weight off your shoulders and put it on the person you'd wronged, who then had to decide whether your reasons were good enough to forgive. The truth needed no verdict. It simply stood there.

Wendell shifted beside her, and she felt him about to speak — felt the apology gathering in him, the instinct to mend — and she put a hand on his arm. *No.* This was not his wound to dress.

"I'm not asking you to forgive me," she said. "I'm telling you I'm going east, to the Door, and I'm going to open it. And when it opens, everything ends — gently, if we do it right. Everyone rests. Including her, Piet. Your mother gets to actually die, instead of whatever this is." She gestured at the camp, the bone-pale trees, the whole suspended country. "I can't give you back the six days. I can't give you anything except the ending. But I'm giving it, and I'm giving it whether you spit on me or not."

Piet set down his wrench very carefully, the way a man does when the alternative is throwing it.

"The Door," he repeated. "The one in the capital. The one my father died rather than build for her."

"It opens the other way," Wendell said quietly. "From inside. I'm the one who opens it."

Piet looked at him then — really looked, taking in the salt-grey coat, the keeper's calluses, the absolute absence of guile — and something in his face cracked, just slightly, like ice over deep water.

"A gatekeeper," he said. "In Ostrava. My father used to say the Gate would undo itself someday, from the keeper's side. He said it like a prayer." He picked his wrench back up. "Third wagon has room. We leave at first light. Don't talk to me on the road, Vane. But if you get us to that Door—" His jaw worked. "Then maybe I'll let you talk to me after."

It was more than she deserved. Sera took it the way you take mercy: without argument, and with your head bowed.

* * *

She couldn't sleep. Around midnight she gave up pretending and walked the perimeter of the camp, past the sleeping wagons, out to where the orchard began. The silver leaves turned overhead in a wind she couldn't feel. Under the trees, the fallen fruit lay in rows like offerings no god had ever come to collect.

Yeva was there, of course. Standing among the trunks with a shawl over her head, watching the leaves turn.

"He'll come around," the old woman said, without preamble. "Piet. Grief keeps its own calendar."

"I wasn't looking for absolution."

"No. You never were. That was always the trouble with you, girl." Yeva turned. In the half-light her face was a map of every year it had refused to count. "Tam Hallowell was my nephew. Did you know that? No, of course you didn't. You were a frightened child eating our bread, and we didn't trouble you with our genealogies." She smiled, and it was a terrible gentle smile. "I've had eleven years to decide what I think of you. Do you want to hear it?"

"Not especially."

"Good. Then I'll tell you anyway." Yeva stepped closer. "I think you are a coward who does brave things, and a liar who tells the truth when it matters, and I think you came back not because you're good but because you finally ran out of places to hide. And I think—" her voice caught, just once, on the edge of something eleven years deep, "—that if you actually manage to open that Door, I will forgive you everything, and I will hate that I forgave you, and I will do it anyway, because that is what the living owe the almost-dead. We take the mercy even when it comes late. Especially then."

Behind her, at the edge of the orchard, Sera heard Wendell's footsteps stop — heard him choose, deliberately, not to approach. He stood in the dark with his hands in his pockets and let the two women have their reckoning, and that, more than anything he'd said all day, was the thing that undid her a little.

"Your keeper," Yeva said, following her gaze. "He has his grandmother's jaw. And her eyes. I knew Adelheid Brightwater, you know. Before the erasure. She came through once, young, full of questions, the way all your family comes through eventually. She stood right where you're standing and asked me what we wanted." Yeva's voice dropped. "Do you know what I told her? I told her: we want to be allowed to finish. That's all. Not saved. Not avenged. Finished."

"She never wrote that down."

"People rarely write down the things that shame them." The old woman pulled her shawl tighter. "Get some sleep, Sera Vane. Tomorrow the road goes through the Weeping Fields, and the queen's riders patrol the far side. You'll want your knife sharp and your conscience quiet, and I suspect you'll manage one of those."

Sera watched her go, a grey shape dissolving between the silver trees, and stood alone a while longer under the turning leaves.

When she finally walked back to the fire, Wendell was awake, sitting up with his bedroll around his shoulders, watching her with that steady, unguarded attention that she still hadn't learned to bear.

"You heard," she said.

"Some."

"And?"

He considered it honestly, which was the most infuriating thing about him. "I think you've been punishing yourself so long you've forgotten punishment isn't the same as repair." He scooted over, making room. "Repair looks like getting them to the Door. So. Get them to the Door."

She lay down beside him, not touching, close enough to feel the warmth — real warmth, valley warmth, the warmth of a world that still knew how to end its days properly. Above the orchard, through the turning silver leaves, she could see the sky, and she counted the stars out of habit, the way she'd done every night since they'd started coming back.

Fourteen. There had been fourteen for three nights now.

She was still counting when she realized the number was wrong.

Fifteen. A new star, low in the east, burning above the drowned towers of the capital — and burning *steadily*, not flickering, not fading. A star that hadn't been there an hour ago.

Sera sat up slowly, cold walking up her spine despite the firelight.

"Wendell," she said. "How many stars did you count tonight?"

He followed her gaze, and she watched him arrive at it — watched the hope and the horror cross his face in the same moment, because a returning star meant the Door could still work, and it also meant that somewhere in the Pale Court, someone else was counting too.

"Fifteen," he whispered.

And far off across the Weeping Fields, thin as a needle, came the sound of horns.
