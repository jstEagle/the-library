## 14. Ilsette's Offer

The trap was set with salt.

Miren should have known. She had grown up on her father's maps, had memorized the way Ostrava's roads bent like grammar rules, and still she walked into it because the message had been written in her father's hand — *Miri, come alone, I'm alive, the ash orchards, third row* — and grief is a door that opens from the inside every time.

She went at dusk, when the pale sun hung low and refused to set, as it always did, as it had done since the kingdom came back wrong. The ash orchards stretched white and skeletal to the horizon, trees that had burned in some old war and never finished dying, their branches blooming each morning with flowers of fine grey soot that fell by noon like snow that remembered being fire. Her boots crunched through drifts of it. Somewhere ahead, a lantern swung.

"Papa?"

The lantern stopped. A figure stood between two trees, tall, wrapped in a soldier's grey coat, and for one impossible heartbeat Miren let herself believe — and then the figure turned, and it was not her father. It was a woman with a face like a coin worn smooth: beautiful once, handled too much, all the sharpness rubbed into something serene. Queen Ilsette Vane stood alone among the dead orchard, holding a lantern in one hand and her daughter's— no. Holding a letter in the other.

"You wrote this," Miren said. Her voice came out flat. Behind the flatness, something was screaming.

"I wrote it well," Ilsette said. "I have had practice writing in other people's hands. It is a skill the erasure taught us." She did not approach. She did not need to; the Pale Court moved in from the treeline on either side, silent as frost forming, six of them in their bone-white uniforms, and Miren understood that she had been surrounded since before she entered the orchard. "You are the cartographer's daughter. You carry half a map to my Door."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Miren." The queen said her name gently, the way you'd speak to a child standing too near a cliff edge. "Your father drew the Door into his last survey. He hid the drawing inside a map of nothing — a blank quarter-sheet, because who steals a map of nothing? Half of it is sewn into your coat lining. The other half he gave to a drowned man in a bell tower. I know this because I have been patient, and patience is the only currency left in Ostrava." She extended the forged letter, and Miren took it without meaning to, because her hands wanted something of her father's even a lie shaped like him. "Give me the map, and walk out of these orchards, and I will forget your face."

"I'd rather you didn't," Miren said, and tore the letter in half, and ran.

She made it eleven rows of ash trees. She would count them later, in the cell, because counting was what her father had taught her — *when you're afraid, count the world, Miri, the world is always countable* — and eleven rows before a ward-engine's hum dropped out of the sky like a net made of cold. The last thing she saw was the queen walking toward her through the falling soot-blossoms, unhurried, lantern raised, wearing an expression that was almost apology.

* * *

They kept her in a room with no locks.

That was the worst of it, Miren decided on the first night. Not the cell itself — a comfortable room, actually, with a bed and a washbasin and a window of thick glass looking out on the capital's rearranging streets — but the absence of locks. The door was never barred. Guards never posted. Because there was nowhere to go. The city ate roads; it rearranged itself around the queen's will like water finding its level, and every corridor Miren fled down delivered her back to the same room, sometimes within minutes, sometimes after hours of running, until she learned to stop running and sit by the window and count.

On the third day, the queen came herself.

Ilsette dismissed the guards, poured two cups of tea from a pot that steamed though there was no fire in the room, and sat across from Miren with the ease of someone visiting a niece. "You haven't eaten the honey cakes," she observed.

"Poisoned?"

"With what? You are worth more to me whole and willing than harmed and stubborn." She pushed a plate closer anyway. "But no. Not poisoned. I don't poison children of people I regret killing."

Miren looked up sharply. "Regret."

"I signed the order myself. Your father refused three times to surrender the survey. The fourth time, I stopped asking." The queen's hands were steady around her cup. That steadiness was the most frightening thing about her — not cruelty, but certainty, worn smooth as river stone. "I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. I killed him. I would kill him again. And I will tell you why, if you'll listen, because everyone else in this city tells you I'm a monster, and monsters are easy to dismiss. Monsters don't make sense. I do."

"Make sense, then."

"My country died once already." Ilsette set down the cup. "Not destroyed — *erased*. Every soul in Ostrava pulled out of the world's memory like a thread pulled from a hem. Do you know what that is, girl? It isn't death. Death has witnesses. We had none. Our names were unwritten, our graves unmade, our children forgotten by grandparents who were still alive on the other side of a salt line. I watched my own mother look at me with a stranger's eyes before the forgetting took her too." She leaned forward. "And now we are back, and the world wants to put us back in the dark, and you — you and your smuggler and your gatekeeper boy — want to open a Door that ends us. Permanently. Call it mercy. Call it rest. From where I stand, it is a second erasure, tidier this time, with our consent."

"The exiles get a vote," Miren said. "That's the difference."

"A vote." Something flickered across the queen's face — not anger; hunger, briefly, like a woman smelling bread. "Yes. I've heard. Democracy. The valley's little religion." She rose, walked to the window, and stood with her back straight as a blade. "Here are my terms, and I will only make them once. Wendell Brightwater comes to me. His blood, freely given — not taken, *given*, sworn at the Door with intent, which is the only way the stitch binds permanently. Ostrava endures. Eternal. No more fraying edges, no more fading exiles, no more slow starvation of a kingdom the world refuses to remember." She turned. "And in exchange, I release every exile in my prisons. All of them. The ones I hold for dissent, the ones I hold for desertion, the ones I hold because they asked me questions I didn't like. Four hundred souls, give or take. Freed, fed, allowed to live out their unending lives in peace under my protection."

"And if he refuses?"

"Then the fraying continues, the exiles continue to fade, and your father's map burns along with everything else he ever touched." The queen gathered her gloves. "I am not asking you to answer. I am asking you to carry the offer. You are the only messenger they'll believe, because you hate me honestly. Liars make poor couriers."

At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "For what it is worth, Miren — and it is worth very little — your father was the finest mapmaker Ostrava ever produced. I read his surveys at night, sometimes. All those roads going somewhere." A pause. "The guards won't stop you leaving. The city will. It always does."

* * *

Sera found the message pinned to the Gatehouse door with a knife.

Not a note — a proclamation, printed on the Pale Court's heavy cream stock, addressed in formal terms to *the Keeper of the Stitch, Wendell Brightwater*, and beneath the official language, in ink that still smelled fresh, the personal addendum in a different hand: *Four hundred lives. Freely given or not at all. You have six days.*

"They took Miren three days ago," Sera said. She stood in the Gatehouse kitchen, which was not really a kitchen anymore but a war room, maps spread over the table where Wendell had once salted thresholds and counted spoons. "One of the underground got word out. They're holding her in the capital, in a room with no locks, which sounds like mercy and is actually the city itself being her cage."

Wendell read the proclamation twice. His hands were steady, which surprised him; he had expected them to shake. Perhaps he had spent all his shaking already, back in the drowned tower, back in the skirmish at the salt flats, back in all the small deaths of the person he used to be. "Freely given," he said. "What does that mean, exactly? Given how?"

"It means she needs you to *want* it." Sera pulled out a chair and sat, hard, the way she did when she was angry at furniture for existing. "Intent matters. It's the same principle as the wards — a lock opened with a key turns smoothly, a lock broken leaves scars. If she takes your blood by force, the stitch tears and the whole thing unravels, maybe including her precious eternal kingdom. So she needs you kneeling at that Door saying yes with your whole heart." She laughed, short and bitter. "It's elegant, really. She's built a trap where the only way to spring it is to mean it."

"And four hundred people go free if I do."

"Don't." Sera's voice cracked like a whip. "Don't you dare start doing that arithmetic. I've seen good people do that arithmetic. My aunt does that arithmetic every morning over breakfast, and it always comes out the same: the few for the many, the now for the forever, and somehow it's always other people in the few."

"She's offering to spare them, Sera. Actual people, in actual cells, fading at the edges—"

"And she put them there!" Sera was on her feet now, palms flat on the table, leaning across the maps of a country that wanted her dead. "Every one of those four hundred is in a cell because they dissented, or deserted, or asked a question. She manufactured the hostages, Wendell. That's the oldest trick there is — create the suffering, then sell you the cure. And the price—" She stopped. Looked at him. And what was in her face then made him look away, because it wasn't anger anymore; it was fear, raw and specific, fear with his name on it. "The price is you. Forever. Not dead — *kept*. Standing in that threshold until the stars burn out, holding shut a wound with your body, while she builds her eternity on top of you. I crossed that Gate once. I know what keeping costs. I watched what it cost you just being half a keeper in a valley that lied to you your whole life."

"So the alternative is letting them fade." Wendell set the proclamation down. "Four hundred people, Sera. Some of them children. Unaging children, locked in cells, coming apart at the seams because their queen needs leverage." He met her eyes. "Tell me the arithmetic doesn't work. Tell me, and I'll believe you."

Sera opened her mouth. Closed it. And Wendell watched — and would remember for the rest of his life — the exact moment the smuggler who always had an answer discovered she didn't have this one. Because the arithmetic did work. That was the horror of it. Ilsette had built her offer the way she built everything: soundly, load-bearing, every beam true. Four hundred lives against one keeper's freedom, and the one keeper was already half-ghost, already marked, already a stitch waiting to be pulled.

"It works," Sera admitted finally, and her voice was very quiet. "God help me, it works. That's what makes her dangerous. Not the armies, not the ward-engines. She makes offers that work." She sank back into the chair. "So we don't play her game. We change the board. We get Miren out, we free those prisoners ourselves, and we tell the queen exactly where she can put her bargain."

"That's a war."

"We're already in one. We just hadn't noticed whose flag we were fighting under."

Wendell walked to the window. Beyond the glass, the returned kingdom sprawled under its half-risen sun, beautiful and wrong, spires that grew like coral, streets that breathed. Somewhere in it, Miren was sitting in a room with no locks, counting. Somewhere in it, four hundred exiles were fading. And here was the truth he could not say aloud, the temptation coiled at the center of the clean terrible bargain like a worm in fruit: *some part of him wanted to say yes.* Not for the hostages. For the ending. For the certainty of it. To walk to the Door, kneel, give his blood with intent, and be *done* — no more choosing, no more road, no more weight of being the last Brightwater. Ilsette had seen it in him, he realized. That was the real elegance of her trap. She hadn't offered four hundred lives for his freedom. She'd offered him permission.

"Sera," he said slowly, "if I went to her — if I gave her what she wants — would you stop me?"

She was quiet long enough that he turned. She was looking at him with an expression he'd never seen on her before, stripped of armor, guilt, wit — all of it gone, just the woman underneath. "No," she said. "That's the truth you're asking for. If you chose it — really chose it, with your eyes open — I wouldn't stop you. I'd hate it. I'd probably never forgive the world for it. But I watched my aunt decide for an entire country what its future would be, and I swore I'd never do that to anyone. Not even to save them." She stood, and crossed to him, and took his face in her hands the way you hold something you're afraid is already breaking. "So I'm asking instead. One smuggler's opinion, freely given: don't. Find another way. There's always another way, even when there isn't."

Wendell covered her hands with his own. Outside, the false sun climbed a little higher and refused, as always, to finish rising.

"Six days," he said. "Then we'd better find the other way fast."

* * *

In her room with no locks, Miren sat by the window and counted.

Eleven rows of ash trees. Six days on the queen's clock. Four hundred prisoners whose names she was starting to learn from the guard who brought her food — a young deserter named Pell who talked too much and looked away when he mentioned the cells. Three halves of a map, if you counted the part her father had drawn from memory into her, the part no one could confiscate: *the Door faces east, Miri. Doors always face the thing they're afraid of.*

And one question, circling like a moth against the glass: what would her father have done?

She thought she knew. He had died rather than surrender the survey — but he had also spent his whole life drawing roads for people, all people, every road going somewhere. He would not have wanted four hundred souls spent to keep his map safe. But he would not have wanted a boy's blood spent either, freely flowing into a queen's eternity.

*Both things can be true,* he used to say, inking coastlines by lamplight. *Maps are honest, Miri. It's people who want them to pick a side.*

Below her window, the living city shifted its streets with a sound like a sigh, rearranging itself around the queen's will. Miren pressed her palm flat against the cool glass and made herself a promise in her father's language, the language of lines and distances:

*I will get out. I will reach them. And I will bring them the offer — all of it, honestly — because they deserve to choose, even when the choice is a knife.*

Somewhere far off, a ward-engine hummed, patient as winter.

Five days remained.
