## 16. The First Star Returns

Wendell had learned to sleep in Ostrava the way a man learns to sleep in a house with a stranger in it — lightly, and always listening.

The caravan of exiles had made camp that night in the lee of a hill called, according to no map at all, the Sleeping Shoulder. It was a long green slope stitched with wild thyme, and it smelled like his grandmother's garden, which was a cruelty he hadn't expected from this country. The tents were patchwork things, sewn from sailcloth and funeral banners and one enormous curtain of yellow silk that Sera claimed had once hung in the Pale Court itself. Around the fires, the unaging dead of Ostrava told stories they had told for two hundred years, polishing each one like river stones until nothing sharp remained.

Wendell sat apart from the fires, as he usually did, with his grandmother's journal open on his knees and his hands cupped around a mug of something the exiles called tea but which tasted like boiled pine and regret.

He wasn't reading. He was watching the sky.

The sky over Ostrava never fully committed to anything. Its sun rose each morning to the color of an old coin and stopped there, hanging low and apologetic above the eastern hills, so that the whole country lived in a perpetual hour before dusk. And its stars — its stars were wrong. There were fewer than there should have been, gaps in the constellations like teeth knocked out of a jaw, and Wendell had spent three nights now lying awake trying to work out why the absence bothered him more than the presence.

Tonight he understood.

He had been staring at the place where Kestrel's Rest's brightest star should have been — the Kestrel Star itself, the one his grandmother had named him under, the one that hung directly over the Salt Gate on midwinter nights — and he had been thinking about what the ghost of old Gatekeeper Halloway had said in the drowned bell tower, *the sky forgets us when we forget ourselves*, and then—

Then the star came back.

It did not fade in. It arrived. One moment there was a hole in heaven, and the next the Kestrel Star burned there, white and hard and furious, throwing a shadow off Wendell's own shoulders across the thyme.

He stood up so fast he spilled the tea down his coat and didn't notice.

"Star," he said, out loud, stupidly, because there was no other word for it and no one yet to say it to. "Sera. *Sera.*"

She was already moving. He would learn this about her eventually, that she slept the way cats do, in shallow layers, and surfaced at any change in the world's texture. She came out of the yellow-silk tent with a knife already in her hand, saw his face, and followed his pointing finger upward.

For a long moment she said nothing. The firelight moved on her face. Above them both the star burned, and Wendell felt something in his chest that he had not felt since the night the wards fell — something warm and dangerous and entirely unfamiliar.

Hope. That was the word. He'd almost forgotten its weight.

"It's mine," he said. "That's the Kestrel Star. It hangs over the Gate. I used to check it every night — it was part of the rounds, you log it in the ledger, *Kestrel Star: present, steady*." His voice cracked and he hated it and kept going anyway. "It went out with all the others. And now it's—"

"Now it's back." Sera lowered the knife slowly. She didn't look hopeful. She looked like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "When did it come back? Exactly."

"I don't know. Minutes ago. I was looking right at the gap and it just—" He turned his hand over, palm up, the way he did when he talked about the blood-thing, the way the salt remembered him and he remembered the salt. "I felt it. In here. Like a stitch pulling tight."

Sera closed her eyes.

"Sera?"

"You lit it," she said. "You understand that, don't you? You didn't find it lit. You lit it." She opened her eyes and looked at him with an expression he was beginning to dread, an expression that meant she had worked something out several steps ahead of him and wished she hadn't. "Your blood is the stitch. The kingdom frays where you walk — you've felt it, you've *said* you've felt it, the edges knitting behind you like frost melting in reverse. Well. Tonight you walked close enough to a piece of the sky that belongs to your Gate, and your blood pulled it back into place. One star. Out of ten thousand."

"One star," Wendell repeated. It sounded small when she said it that way. It didn't feel small. It felt like the first true thing in a country built out of lies.

"One star," Sera agreed, "which means the Door can still be opened. Which means everything we're walking toward isn't a fool's errand. Congratulations, Brightwater. You've just proven the world can be saved." She sheathed the knife. "And you've also just fired a signal flare over your own head that every hunter Ilsette has can see."

The warmth in his chest stuttered.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean the queen isn't blind, Wendell. Her whole claim is that Ostrava is eternal and unchanging. A star coming back after two hundred years is a crack in that claim big enough to drive a caravan through. She'll know it means the keeper's blood is active. She'll know it means us." Sera turned toward the camp, raising her voice without shouting, the way smugglers do. "Osk! Get everyone up. Break camp. Now."

Around the nearest fire, a broad man with a shipwright's forearms looked up from his story and, seeing Sera's face, began moving without a single question. That was the thing about the exiles, Wendell was learning. They had learned two hundred years ago that when Sera Vane said *move*, the correct response was to move first and ask questions in whatever time remained.

* * *

They broke camp in the dark, or in what passed for dark in a country whose night was only a dimmer version of its day. The exiles worked quietly, folding tents, banking fires, loading the two wagons they'd acquired outside the ash orchards. Miren appeared at Wendell's elbow with the half-map wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, her father's compass in her other hand, and said nothing, which from Miren was a kind of courtesy.

But Wendell couldn't stop looking up.

The Kestrel Star burned steady above them, and every time he glanced at it he felt that pull in his blood, gentle and insistent, like a thread tied to his sternum running up into heaven. It was the same feeling he'd had all his life at the Salt Gate, he realized — the hum in his teeth when he checked the wards, the way the salt seemed to lean toward him. He had thought it was duty. It had been geography all along. His body knew where its pieces were.

"It's beautiful," Miren said quietly, following his gaze. Then, with the flat pragmatism that was her inheritance from a hanged cartographer: "It's also a beacon. My father used to say a map tells everyone where you are, including the people hunting you. A star's just a map in the sky."

"Thank you," Wendell said. "Both of you. For the poetry and the warning."

"Don't thank me," Miren said. "Watch the treeline."

They took the road north along the shoulder of the hill, wagons creaking, forty-odd exiles walking in the loose formation of people who had fled things before. Sera ranged ahead with two scouts. Wendell walked near the middle, where the oldest exiles walked, because they had decided — without ever saying it to him — that the keeper belonged in the safest place, and their quiet protectiveness embarrassed him more than any argument could have.

An old woman named Bethel walked beside him for a while. She had been a schoolteacher in Ostrava before the erasure, and she still carried chalk in her pocket and wrote sums on fence posts when she thought no one was looking.

"You lit the star," she said. It wasn't a question.

"So I'm told."

"My husband's family were stargazers. Before. We kept the almanac for the western provinces." She smiled at the sky with an expression two hundred years old. "He used to say a relit star was a letter from someone who loved you. That light takes so long to travel, you see. By the time it reaches you, the person who sent it might be gone. But they loved you when they lit it."

Wendell's throat tightened. "I'm sorry about your husband."

"He's not gone. None of us are gone. That's rather the whole problem, dear." She patted his arm with a hand like paper. "But thank you for the letter anyway."

They came down off the Sleeping Shoulder an hour before dawn-that-wasn't, into a valley of terraced fields gone wild, and that was where the scouts came back at a run.

* * *

"Hunters," Sera said, dropping to one knee at the head of the column, and the word went down the line of exiles like a current through water. "Three riders on the ridge behind us. Pale Court livery. They haven't closed yet — they're pacing us."

"Pacing us?" Wendell asked.

"They want us to lead them somewhere." Sera's jaw worked. "Or they're waiting for more hunters. Three riders doesn't take a caravan. Three riders is a leash."

Miren had the compass out, though it pointed at nothing useful here; her father had rebuilt it to point at the Door, and the Door lay north by northwest through country none of them knew. "How far to the capital?"

"Four days if the roads behave," Sera said. "And the roads won't behave. The city eats roads, I told you. As we get closer, the land starts rearranging itself to funnel travelers where the queen wants them."

"Then we don't take the road," Wendell said.

Everyone looked at him. He was surprised to hear his own voice come out level. Somewhere in the last weeks the boy who had been afraid to walk past the third ward-stone had been left behind, and someone else was wearing his coat.

"The star proves my blood works on the sky," he said. "Maybe it works on the ground too. The salt remembers — Halloway said the whole kingdom is salted ground, every boundary stone, every threshold. If I can feel the edges fraying, maybe I can feel where the land is *honest*. Where it hasn't been bent by the queen's engines yet. We go cross-country, along the seams."

"That's mad," said Osk the shipwright.

"It's also the only direction the hunters won't predict," Sera said slowly. She was looking at Wendell with an expression he couldn't read — pride, maybe, or fear, or the particular grief of a woman watching someone she cares about become exactly the kind of person who gets himself killed for other people. "Can you actually do it? Feel the honest ground?"

Wendell knelt. He pressed his palm flat against the wild grass, the way he'd pressed it against the threshold stones of the Salt Gate ten thousand mornings, and let himself listen.

At first there was nothing but dirt and root and worm. Then, beneath that, the way you can hear the sea inside a shell, he heard the hum. The whole country was singing, very faintly, a note sustained for two hundred years past its natural end — and the note was not uniform. It wavered. It frayed. Here, toward the east, it rang smooth and false as polished glass, the queen's work, the ward-engines' work. But west and north, in broken threads, the song cracked and splintered and *ached*, and those cracks felt like home. They felt like the Gate.

"There," he said, pointing northwest, away from the road, toward a line of grey cliffs veined with white stone. "Salt veins. Old boundaries. The land's still arguing with itself there — the queen hasn't smoothed it over. We follow the seams."

Bethel laughed softly. "The keeper reads the country like a ledger."

"Ledgers are honest," Wendell said, standing, brushing off his knee. "That's more than I can say for anything else here."

They cut northwest. Behind them, on the ridge, the three riders held their distance for another hour, patient as vultures, and then — Wendell watched it happen — they turned and galloped away south, carrying their news to whoever gathered it.

"They know," Sera said grimly, falling into step beside him. "By tomorrow there'll be thirty of them. By the day after, the Pale Court's own guard. Whatever window we had, Brightwater, we just spent it buying a star."

Wendell looked up. The Kestrel Star burned on, steady, faithful, a letter from someone who loved him.

"Good," he said.

Sera stared at him. "*Good?*"

"It means it works." He found he was smiling, and the smile felt strange on his face, like a coat borrowed from a bigger man. "Sera — two hundred years, and nobody could end this. Not the dissidents, not the deserters, not your underground, not poor Miren's father with his maps. Nobody could even prove it *could* end. And last night, without meaning to, without even trying, I put one star back in the sky." He turned to look at her, and the pull in his chest ran all the way from his sternum to the burning point of light above, and he let himself say the thing he'd been carrying since the bell tower. "If I can light a star by accident, imagine what happens when I get to the Door on purpose."

Sera held his gaze for a long moment. Firelight and starlight fought over her face, and neither won.

"Just remember," she said finally, quietly, "that everything you light, she sees. Hope's a flare, Wendell. It draws fire. That's not a reason not to hope. It's just — know what it costs before you strike it."

"I know what it costs," Wendell said. "Halloway told me. The Door ends the kingdom, and the kingdom ends its keeper. I've done the arithmetic, Vane. I've been doing it since the tower."

"That's not the cost I mean."

But she wouldn't say more, and the column was moving, and the grey cliffs with their honest, aching seams rose ahead of them out of the half-light like the spine of some enormous sleeping animal.

* * *

They made nine miles before dusk-that-was-dusk, following the salt veins through a landscape that grew stranger the further they left the road. The fields gave way to orchards — not ash orchards this time but apple trees, ancient and crooked, heavy with fruit that glowed faintly from within like lanterns, because in Ostrava even the trees refused to finish ripening and fall. The exiles picked them silently as they passed, and ate, and Wendell understood that this was how the unaging dead fed themselves: on light that never quite became sugar, hunger that never quite became starvation.

It was full dark — real dark, the first true dark Wendell had seen in Ostrava — when the star changed.

He felt it before he saw it. The thread tied to his sternum went taut, then slack, then taut again, thrumming like a plucked string. He stopped in the orchard aisle and looked up, and the Kestrel Star was flickering.

Not steadily. Not naturally. It pulsed — bright, dim, bright, dim — in a rhythm too regular to be chance, and Wendell's blood answered it beat for beat, his heart stumbling into the same rhythm, and cold walked up his spine on small feet.

"It's not flickering," he whispered. "It's being *pulled*. Something's — Sera! Sera, something's holding onto it!"

She was beside him in three strides, following his eyes upward. Around them the exiles were stopping too, faces tilting back, and Bethel murmured, "Oh," in the voice of someone recognizing a hymn played wrong.

The star flared — blinding, wrong, a white wound in the black — and then it *moved*. It slid sideways across the heavens, dragging its light like a nail dragged across cloth, tearing a thin luminous seam behind it. And through that seam, faint and far and unmistakable, Wendell saw mountains. Grey-green mountains under a proper sun, and a valley folded between them like a cupped hand, and a wall of white stones at the valley's mouth.

Home. Kestrel's Rest, seen through a tear in the sky, from the wrong side.

The vision lasted four heartbeats. Then the seam snapped shut, the star snapped back to its place, and the night was ordinary again except for the sound of forty exiles breathing.

"She found it," Sera said. Her voice was very quiet. "Ilsette found the star's anchor. Your Gate, Wendell. She's found your Gate — or what's left of it — and she's using it to pull on the stitch. Testing the thread." She turned to him, and in the lantern-light of the glowing apples her face was pale and set. "Which means she knows exactly what you are now. Not a rumor. Not a runaway keeper. The stitch herself, walking around loose in her kingdom, heading for her Door."

"And the hunters?"

As if the question had summoned them, Osk's whistle sounded from the rear of the column — two short notes, one long. Contact.

Wendell ran.

Behind the last wagon, at the edge of the orchard where the salt-veined cliffs began, torchlight had bloomed among the trees. Not three riders this time. A dozen at least, dismounted, moving through the apple rows in a spread line, unhurried, the way you advance when you are certain the quarry has nowhere to go. Their armor was the white-and-silver of the Pale Court, and their leader carried a banner Wendell recognized from Miren's furious descriptions: a silver door on a field of black, the sigil of the queen who would not let her country die.

"Form up!" Sera's voice cracked across the orchard like a whip. "Wagons in the center! Keeper in the center — *Wendell, get behind me*, that is not a request!"

But Wendell had stopped running. Because he could feel them now — really feel them, the way he felt the wards, the way he felt the salt — and what he felt made his stomach drop.

The hunters weren't alive. Not exactly. Under the armor, under the skin, they hummed with the same sustained note as the kingdom itself, the note that should have ended two hundred years ago, stretched and stretched past breaking. They were exiles. Ostravan soldiers, dead and undying, bound into the queen's service, and their song was frayed in exactly the places his blood itched to mend.

They were the wound, walking. And some deep gatekeeper instinct in him, older than his name, reached toward them the way a hand reaches toward a splinter.

"Wendell!" Sera grabbed his arm. "Whatever you're thinking — and I can *see* you thinking it — don't. Not here. Not against twelve of them with forty civilians at your back. Come *on*."

The first arrows fell among the apple trees, trailing pale fire. An exile screamed. The wagons lurched forward, and the night filled with shouting and the sweet smoke of burning lantern-fruit, and Wendell let Sera drag him into the center of the column with the star burning overhead like an accusation and the torn seam of the sky still faintly visible to eyes that knew how to look.

Hope, he thought, ducking as a burning branch burst overhead. Hope is a flare that draws fire.

Above them, steady and faithful and doomed, the Kestrel Star burned on — and far to the south, riding through the dark at the head of her Pale Court, the queen turned her face toward its light and smiled.
