## 18. The City That Eats Roads

They saw the capital from the ridge of a dead highway, three days out of the ash orchards, and for a long moment none of them spoke.

Ostrava did not sit in its valley so much as *crouch* there — a sprawl of pale spires and copper roofs that caught the never-rising sun and held it hostage, glowing with a light that came from nowhere Wendell could name. The city had walls, but they seemed almost an afterthought, the way a ring is an afterthought around a finger. What struck him was the motion. Even at this distance he could see it: rooftops shifting like feathers on a living bird, streets rearranging themselves with the slow patience of roots underground.

"It's moving," Miren said flatly.

"Cities move," Sera said. "This one just doesn't pretend otherwise."

Wendell had heard the stories from the exiles in the caravan — told in low voices, the way you speak of a relative who went mad. Ostrava had always been half-alive; the old kings had built it on the spine of something buried, and stone remembers what it grows from. But since Ilsette's return, since she began feeding it the stolen magic of Kestrel's Rest, the city had woken all the way. It loved her. And it hunted for her, the way a body hunts fever.

"The roads will not hold still," said Old Vetch, the caravan's eldest, whose face was smooth as a boy's above his hundred-year eyes. "The city eats roads, girl. It eats them and digests them into other roads, roads that lead where the queen wants you to go. You want the Door at the heart? Then you must enter as water enters — everywhere at once, in many mouths, so the city cannot swallow any one of you whole."

So they split.

It was decided in the shadow of the last orchard, quickly, because dusk in Ostrava's country lasted eleven hours and none of them wanted to be outside the walls when true dark fell. Miren would go east with the deserters and her half of the map. Old Vetch and the exiles would come in from the south, openly, as returning citizens — the city still honored its own. And Wendell and Sera would take the drowned river-road under the western wall, the smuggler's way, the way Sera had gone once before and never come back.

"Stay where I can see you," Sera told him, and it was not tenderness in her voice but arithmetic. "In there, if I lose sight of you for more than a minute, I may never find you again. The city doesn't misplace people. It *keeps* them."

"I've been keeping things my whole life," Wendell said. "I can be kept."

She didn't laugh. That frightened him more than the walls did.

* * *

The river-road under the wall was a throat of black brick, dry now for a century, and it smelled of salt and old iron — the smell of the Gate, Wendell realized, the smell of home, and he could not decide whether that comforted him or insulted him. His blood hummed against it. Somewhere far beneath them, the stitch that was his family's line ran through the foundations of this place, and the city felt him walking the way a sleeper feels a hand on their chest.

Sera moved ahead with a shuttered lantern, counting arches under her breath. "Forty-one," she murmured. "There were forty-one when I came through. If we reach forty-two, the road has changed and we're inside its stomach."

"What happens then?"

"Then we stop being travelers," she said, "and start being food."

At the thirty-ninth arch the air changed. Wendell felt it first in his teeth — a pressure, a listening. The lantern flame leaned sideways though there was no wind. Ahead, the tunnel's end, which should have opened onto a customs yard, instead opened onto a stair going down.

"There is no stair," Sera said quietly. "I memorized this route for nine years. There is no stair."

The stair was beautiful. That was the worst part. Each step was cut from a single piece of veined marble, and the walls were set with niches holding small clay lamps, unlit, waiting to be lit, patient as everything else in this country that refused to end. From somewhere below came the sound of a market: voices, a hammer on a nail, a child laughing.

"Sera." Wendell touched her shoulder. "Listen."

"That's not a real market."

"How do you know?"

"Because I heard the same one seventeen years ago," she said, "and I followed it, and it took me three days to claw back out, and what I found at the bottom of it—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "What I found is why I owe these people. Come on. We don't go down. We go *through*."

"Through what? It's a wall of—"

The wall was no longer a wall. Where the tunnel's end had been solid masonry a moment before, there was now a doorway, tall and arched, opening onto a street — a real street, cobbled, lamplit, running between shuttered houses under a sky the color of pewter. A cat sat in the middle of it, washing one paw, regarding them with the supreme indifference of cats in every world.

"That wasn't there either," Wendell said.

"No," Sera said. "It wasn't."

The cat stood, stretched, and walked away down the street, glancing back once — and Wendell understood, with the cold certainty of his blood, that they were being invited. Or lured. In this city he suspected the two were spelled the same way.

"We go back," he said. "We find another way in."

"There is no back. Look."

He looked. Behind them, the tunnel had shortened. The forty-one arches were now perhaps twenty, crowded together like ribs after a feast, and beyond them the black mouth of the river-road had narrowed to a slit no wider than a hand. The city had closed its throat behind them, gently, without malice, the way a mouth closes.

"Stay where I can see you," Sera repeated, and took his hand, and stepped through the doorway onto the street.

* * *

Ostrava's streets were paved with memory. That was Miren's phrase later, and it was exactly right, though neither of them knew it yet. Every cobble bore a worn inscription — names, mostly, or fragments of names, ground nearly smooth by centuries of feet. Wendell walked on *MARTA*, on *THE BELLRINGER'S SON*, on half a word that might have been *KESTREL*, and each step sent a faint ache up through his boots, his blood recognizing kin in the stone.

The street behaved itself for six turns. Sera navigated by chalk marks and stubbornness, refusing to ask directions of the few figures they passed — pale, courteous people who bowed slightly and always seemed to be walking toward somewhere just behind you. At the sixth turn the street ended at a canal where there should have been a bridge, and offered instead a flight of steps up into a district of glassblowers, and Sera swore with a fluency that made a passing grandmother smile approvingly.

"It's steering us," Wendell said.

"Of course it's steering us. It's been steering us since the stair." She pressed her palm flat against a doorpost, as if taking its pulse. "Ilsette knows we're coming. She's known since the orchards, probably since the star came back. The city isn't trapping us, Wendell. It's *herding* us. Everything here leads to the Pale Court, eventually. Every road is a tributary."

"Then we stop following roads."

"And do what, walk through walls?"

"Maybe." He put his hand on the cobblestones, on the name worn into them, and let his blood speak. It was like pressing an ear to a door. Beneath the city's surface intention — the long slow current pulling everything toward the palace — he felt the older thing, the buried spine, the wound's scar tissue running under the streets in lines that did not match any map. Lines like stitches. Lines like the wards he'd tended his whole life, transposed, inverted, grown vast.

"Sera. The Gate runs under this city. My Gate. The same ward-work, the same blood in the mortar. If I can feel the seams, I can walk along them — places the city can't easily rearrange, because rearranging them would hurt it."

She stared at him. In the lamplight her face was stripped of all its armor for one unguarded second, and what was underneath was fear — not for herself.

"You're talking about using yourself as a compass needle in a city that wants to eat you."

"I'm talking about the only map that doesn't change." He managed something that was almost a grin. "You crossed the Gate once and never came back. Let me do the impossible navigation for once."

For a moment he thought she would refuse. Then she laughed — short, sharp, real — and squeezed his hand hard enough to grind bone. "Fine. But the rule stands. You stay where I can see you. One minute, Wendell. That's all the city needs. One minute."

* * *

The seams took them down.

Through a courtyard where laundry hung frozen mid-flutter, unmoving for decades. Through the interior of a church that had been built around a fragment of wardstone so old it predated the erasure — Wendell laid his palm on it and the whole building sighed. Through a cellar where a family of unaging exiles played cards by candlelight and pretended not to see them, though the youngest, a girl of perhaps sixty, watched them go with wide dark eyes.

And then, at the bottom of a stair that had definitely not existed a moment before, the city stopped pretending.

The street they emerged onto was broad and white and utterly silent, lined with statues of the old kings, and it stretched ahead of them toward a distant glow that could only be the palace. Behind them, the way they'd come was simply gone — not walled off, not blocked. *Absent.* As if the street had never extended anywhere else, as if they had been born here, standing together under the pewter sky.

"Sera," Wendell said slowly. "Where are the others?"

She turned a full circle. The silence had texture now, a held breath. "They entered from the east and south. They're fine. They have to be fine. The city splits people, it doesn't—" She stopped.

Down the white street, at the far end near the glow, a figure detached itself from the shadows between two kings. Tall, robed in the gray of the Pale Court, unhurried. Then a second figure, from the opposite side. A third, from a street that slid smoothly into existence to admit it, like a drawer opening.

"They're not attacking," Wendell whispered.

"No," Sera said. Her hand found his again, gripping hard. "They're *separating* us. Wendell — whatever happens, don't let go of—"

The street moved.

There was no other word for it. The cobbles rippled like the skin of a drum, and the white avenue bent — not cracked, not shifted, but *bent*, the way a blade of grass bends, and Wendell felt the world tilt and slide. He grabbed for Sera. Their fingers interlocked for one instant, two —

— and between them, politely, silently, a seam of new street rose up out of the earth, thin as a knife, taller than a man, and the city folded it between them like a page being turned.

His hand closed on empty air.

"*SERA!*"

His voice came back to him wrong — flattened, swallowed, the acoustics of a room that didn't exist a moment ago. He hammered his fists against the fresh-laid wall of stone. It was warm. That was the horror of it, the detail he knew he would carry forever: the stone was warm, like skin, like something alive that had just done a small necessary thing and felt no shame about it.

Through the stone — faintly, muffled, already receding — he heard her voice, and she was not screaming. She was giving orders. "*East! Go east, follow your blood, I'll find you — don't you dare let them—*" And then the city absorbed even that, folding the sound away like a letter into a drawer, and Wendell Brightwater stood alone in the capital of a kingdom that had been erased from the world.

The robed figures were closer now. Not running. They never needed to run; the street simply delivered them, the way a river delivers leaves. Wendell backed away until his shoulders met the cold pedestal of a king whose face had been chiseled off, and put his hand against it, and reached for his blood, for the seams, for anything—

The stone under his palm pulsed. Once. Slowly. Patiently.

Like a heartbeat. Like a trap that had already sprung, days ago, miles back, at the thirty-ninth arch, and had merely been carrying him gently ever since.

The nearest figure pushed back its hood. It had a kind, ordinary face, the face of a schoolteacher or a clerk, and it inclined its head with genuine courtesy.

"The Queen bids you welcome, Keeper," it said. "She asks that you come without struggle. She wishes you to know that the woman is unharmed. For now. The city is holding her somewhere soft."

Somewhere behind the white street, deep in the living stone, Wendell thought he felt — faint, furious, unmistakably alive — the flare of Sera's presence, like a coal refusing the dark. East, she'd said. Follow your blood.

The clerk waited, hands folded, infinitely patient. The city waited too. The whole weight of it, every street and statue and sleeping lamp, had turned its attention upon him the way a cat turns its eyes upon the one mouse foolish enough to move.

Wendell took his hand off the dead king's pedestal.

"All right," he said, and his voice did not shake, which surprised him. "Take me to her. But understand something first." He let his blood rise, let it touch the stones, and felt the whole street flinch — a shudder running away into the dark like a ripple across a pond. "Your queen thinks she's caught a keeper. Tell her keepers don't get kept."

The clerk's smile did not waver. "Oh, Keeper," it said gently, turning to lead him down the bending, breathing street, toward the glow of the Pale Court. "That is precisely what she intends to test."

Behind them, in the dark where the city had folded her away, Sera Vane woke alone on a soft bed of stone in a room with no doors — and began, methodically, to count the walls.
