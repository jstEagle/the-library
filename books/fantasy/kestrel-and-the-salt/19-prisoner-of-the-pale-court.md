## 19. Prisoner of the Pale Court

They did not bind his hands.

That was the first thing Wendell noticed, being marched through the living streets of Ostrava under the escort of six hunters in bone-pale livery: no ropes, no irons, no chain between his ankles. The city itself did the binding. The roads moved beneath him like slow water, and every time he thought of turning — of running, of shoving past a guard, of anything at all — the street ahead simply rearranged itself, a wall of pale stone rising where an alley had been, a doorway opening where he needed none. The capital had swallowed him whole, and it was in no hurry to digest.

He tried to keep track. It was what a keeper would do; it was all he knew how to be now that there was no gate left to keep. *Left turn at the fountain with no water. Right where the lamplighters hang their lanterns upside down.* But the fountain appeared three times on different streets, and the lanterns hung right side up when he looked back, and by the time they crossed the fourth bridge over a canal that flowed with something too thick and too silver to be water, Wendell understood that the city was not letting him remember. It was editing him as it went, the way Sera said Ilsette edited everything else.

The hunters walked in silence. They were exiles — he could see it now, the way you could see it in all of them after weeks in this country: the faint translucence at the edges of them, as though they'd been left out in rain for a hundred years and dried without damage. One of them, a woman with a scarred jaw and eyes the color of pond ice, kept glancing at his hands. At his blood, he realized. Word had traveled ahead of them through the city's veins faster than any horse could carry it. *The stitch has come to the queen.*

The palace, when they reached it, was not what he expected. He had imagined spires, banners, gates of pearl. Instead the Pale Court sat inside what had clearly once been a granary — a vast low building of honey-colored stone, its roof beams black with age, its windows tall and narrow as arrow slits. A kingdom's throne room built from a place where grain had been stored against hunger. There was a story in that, Wendell thought, and he suspected it was not a flattering one.

Inside, the air smelled of salt. His salt. The Salt Gate's salt, carried here somehow, or perhaps the same salt, called home across the distance the way iron filings answer a magnet. His arms prickled with it. Somewhere in this building, something of his grandmother's work still lived, and it knew him.

"Wait here," said the woman with the pond-ice eyes, and for the first time her voice was not hard. "She'll want to speak to you alone. She always does."

*Always.* As if there had been others. As if there had been many.

* * *

Ilsette Vane received him in a small room off the great hall, a room that might have belonged to a clerk or a schoolteacher. There was a desk of plain oak. There were maps pinned to the walls — hundreds of them, overlapping, some so old the ink had gone brown, some drawn so recently the lines still glistened. And there was a teapot, white ceramic, chipped at the spout, sitting on a tray beside two cups.

The queen herself stood at the window with her back to him, and she was smaller than he had imagined. Smaller, and grayer, and tired around the shoulders in a way that no portrait of tyranny ever admits. Her hair was bound back plainly. She wore no crown that he could see.

"You're the Brightwater boy," she said, without turning. "Sit down, please. The tea is real. I know how much that means here."

Wendell did not sit. "I'm not a boy."

"No." She turned then, and her face was kind, and that was worse than cruelty would have been. "No, you're not. Forgive me. I've been speaking to ghosts for so long I've forgotten how to address the living." She poured two cups anyway, unhurried, steam curling up into the gray light. "Your grandmother kept a journal. Did you know that? We had copies of it, before the erasure. Before your family's line was... repurposed." She said the word gently, the way one says *amputated*. "She wrote about you, near the end. About the child who would come after her children failed to come at all. 'He will love the Gate,' she wrote, 'because he will never have been given anything else to love.'"

The words landed in Wendell's chest like stones dropped down a well. He heard them fall a long way before they struck bottom.

"That's not true," he said.

"It is entirely true," Ilsette said, "and you know it, which is why it hurts. Sit. Drink. I am not going to hurt you, Wendell Brightwater. Hurting you would be the stupidest thing I could do, and I have survived four hundred years by never doing the stupid thing."

He sat. He drank. The tea was real — bitter, oversteeped, made from leaves that had no business existing in a dead country — and its warmth was almost unbearable in its ordinariness. When had anyone last made him tea? He couldn't remember. That was the thing about Kestrel's Rest that he was only now beginning to understand: it had not been cruel to him. It had simply never once, in twenty-six years, been warm.

"Do you know what you are?" Ilsette asked him.

"A keeper."

"A stitch." She set down her cup. "A knot tied in a wound by frightened people who wanted their problem to go away and never asked what the problem felt about it. Your ancestors sealed my country out of the world because we were inconvenient — because our magic leaked, because our wars spilled, because containing us was easier than helping us. And when sealing wasn't enough, they erased us. Not killed. Erased. Every map, every memory, every grave. My people did not die, Wendell. They were *unhappened*." Her voice stayed level, but the tea in her cup trembled, a fine ring of ripples. "And the only reason they cannot rest, cannot end, cannot even properly exist — is that your family's blood keeps the wound closed. Keeps us in. Keeps us *neither alive nor dead nor free*."

"I didn't ask for that."

"No one asks for anything in this story. That is rather my point." She rose and went to the maps, and her hand hovered over them without touching. "Come here. I want to show you something, and I want you to look at it honestly, because you strike me as an honest man, and honest men are the only ones worth arguing with."

He came. He could not have said why. Perhaps because the tea was warm. Perhaps because she had quoted his grandmother's journal as if she had read it a hundred times, and some starved part of him wanted to hear more.

The map she stopped before was not of Ostrava. It was of the valley — Kestrel's Rest, rendered in loving detail, every farmstead and footpath, the Salt Gate marked with a small careful symbol he recognized from his grandmother's margins. But overlaid across it, in red ink gone dark as dried blood, were lines. Hundreds of lines. Threads, running from the valley outward, converging on points throughout the returned kingdom.

"What are these?"

"Leashes," Ilsette said. "Every working ward in your valley draws its power from somewhere, Wendell. Magic does not come from nowhere; that is a bedtime story comfortable people tell themselves. Your valley's wards — the ones that kept the magic out, that made it such a safe, quiet, godly little place — draw from *us*. From the exiles. From four hundred years of lives that were never allowed to finish." She touched one thread, and somewhere far away, Wendell felt it — a tug behind his ribs, faint as a fisherman feeling a bite at the end of a very long line. "When the stars went out and the Gate fell, those leashes pulled taut. And now my people are being drained to keep your valley's remnant magic alive while it dies. Do you understand? The valley you mourn is drinking my people even now."

"The valley is gone," Wendell said. "I saw it stop existing—"

"It stopped existing *for you*. It is not gone. Nothing here is ever allowed to be gone — that is the entire disease." She laughed, briefly, and there was no humor in it at all. "I am trying to make Ostrava permanent. Real. Finished. A kingdom that ends properly, with borders and graves and a history other people can read. To do that I need power — power enough to hold a shape the world spent four centuries refusing to give us. And you—" she turned, and took his hands, and her grip was dry and strong and desperately gentle, "—you are the keystone. Your blood freely given, willingly, at the Door, would anchor the kingdom forever. No more draining. No more leashes. No more half-life. My people would *endure*, Wendell. Truly endure. All I have ever wanted is that they should simply be allowed to continue."

"And Miren?" he said quietly. "The cartographer's daughter you're holding. Does she get to continue?"

Something flickered across the queen's face — fast, quickly mastered, but he saw it. Guilt has a particular shape, even on a face trained for four hundred years, and Wendell had grown up learning to read faces across a threshold, alone, with nothing else to do.

"Miren will be released unharmed," Ilsette said. "You have my word."

"Your word."

"My word is the only currency I have left, and I have never once defaulted on it. Ask the underground, if you doubt me. Ask them why I execute traitors publicly and honorably instead of disappearing them into cells. Because I want my people to see that law still exists here. Because law is the last thing erasure left us, and I will not surrender it." She released his hands and stepped back, and for a moment she looked at him with an expression he could not name — longing, maybe, or grief wearing longing's clothes. "You think I am a tyrant. I know. The smuggler has told you stories, and the girl with the map hates me, and you are a good man who believes good men. So let me ask you the only question that matters, Wendell Brightwater, and I ask it honestly, because I genuinely do not know the answer, and it has kept me awake for longer than your valley has existed."

She gestured, and the room changed — the maps sliding aside, the walls becoming windows, the windows becoming something vaster. Wendell gasped. He was standing above Ostrava. Not a picture of it: *above* it, the whole country spread beneath him like a quilt shaken out, cities and orchards and ash-fields and the drowned bell tower he had nearly died in, all of it lit by a sun that hung forever at the edge of rising, casting shadows in every direction at once.

And the people. He could see the people. Millions of them — no, thousands, tens of thousands, translucent at their edges, moving through their unending days. Farmers harvesting fields that never ripened. Mothers rocking children who had been children for three centuries. An old man teaching the same lesson to the same classroom, generation after generation of students who could not age past the day they were taken. A woman standing at a shoreline, looking out at a sea that had no other side, waiting — Wendell understood suddenly, with a pain like a hook — for someone who had been erased before she could say goodbye.

"They cannot die," Ilsette said softly, beside him. "Do you understand what that means? Not immortality. Immortality is a gift. This is a sentence. They cannot die, and they cannot leave, and they cannot change, because change requires an ending — a day that finishes, a self that is laid down. My people have been mid-sentence for four hundred years, Wendell. And the only key to finishing the sentence is your blood. Freely given, the old texts say. Willingly. Which is why I have not taken it, though I could. Which is why you stand here drinking tea instead of bleeding into a bowl." Her voice frayed, just slightly, on the last words. "So I am asking you. Man to man. Keeper to queen. Who are you — what gives *you* the right — to walk to my Door and end them? To decide that four hundred years of suffering should conclude in annihilation rather than endurance? You are twenty-six years old. You have tended one gate in one valley your whole life. Who are you to end a nation?"

The question filled the room like floodwater.

And the terrible thing — the thing Wendell would carry for the rest of his life, however long that proved to be — was that he had no answer. He stood above the whole of Ostrava and looked at the woman at the shore and the old teacher and the unaging children, and every argument he had brought with him — Sera's fury, Miren's maps, the ghost's warnings, justice, mercy, the natural order of things — every one of them dissolved in the face of ten thousand people who simply wanted to be allowed to *continue*.

"I don't know," he said finally. His voice sounded very small. "I don't know who I am to end them."

"Thank you," Ilsette said, and she meant it, and that was the most frightening thing yet. "That is the first honest answer anyone has given me in a century." She waved a hand, and the vision folded away, and they were in the clerk's room again with the chipped teapot and the brown maps. "But I will tell you what I know, since you were honest with me. I know that endings chosen are different from endings imposed. I know that your valley taught you that duty inherited is a cage — you learned it from a smuggler, I'm told, and from a girl who watched her father die for drawing lines in the wrong places. Well. My duty was inherited too, Wendell. I was a general when the world closed around us. I did not choose this crown. I picked it up because no one else would, and I have carried it so long I can no longer feel my hands." She looked down at her own hands as she said it, as if checking whether they were still there.

"And yet," Wendell said slowly, "you executed a cartographer. In public. For drawing a map."

"For drawing a map *to the Door*," Ilsette corrected, "and giving it to people who would use it to open that Door over the bodies of everyone who begged them not to. Yes. I executed him. And I have regretted it every hour since, and I would do it again tomorrow, and both of those things are true, and that is what four hundred years of impossible choices makes of a person. You will learn this, if you live long enough. Mercy is easy when someone else pays for it."

The silence stretched between them, long and strange. Outside, somewhere in the granary-halls of the Pale Court, Wendell could hear singing — low, wordless, the sound of a people keeping themselves together by breath alone.

"Why show me this?" he asked. "Why not just take the blood?"

"Because freely given," Ilsette said, "means *convinced*. And because—" she paused, and the tiredness in her shoulders became, for one unguarded moment, the whole of her, "—because if I am wrong, Wendell, if I have spent four centuries becoming a monster to save people who wanted rest instead of rescue, then I need to hear it from someone who isn't afraid of me. Someone whose grandmother wrote about. Someone with salt in his veins and nothing in his pockets. So I am asking you again, and this time not as a challenge. I am asking you as the loneliest woman in a kingdom of lonely people."

Wendell looked at her. He thought of the woman at the shore. He thought of Miren in a cell somewhere beneath them, and of Sera, who had told Ilsette how to break the wards and had never forgiven herself, and of the vote the exiles deserved and had never been given. He thought of his grandmother's journal, and the child who would love the Gate because he'd been given nothing else.

And he found, to his own surprise, that he was not afraid anymore. Whatever else the queen had done to him today, she had done that.

"You're wrong," he said quietly.

Ilsette went very still.

"Not about the suffering. Not about the erasure. You're right about all of it, and I'm sorry — truly — for every ancestor of mine who made it so." His voice shook, but he let it. "But you don't get to choose for them. Not anymore than my family got to choose for yours. That's the whole disease, like you said — people deciding what other people must endure. Endurance or ending, crown or Door — it isn't ours to pick. It's theirs. All of them. Even the ones who'd choose wrong." He met her eyes. "Especially those."

For a long moment the queen of Ostrava said nothing at all. Then she smiled, and it was the saddest expression Wendell had ever seen on a human face, and she reached for the teapot and refilled his cup with steady hands.

"There it is," she murmured. "There's the Brightwater spine. Your grandmother had it too, you know. Right up until the end." She set the pot down. "I'm sorry, Wendell. Genuinely. Because I gave you my word about the girl, and I keep my word — but words are not the only thing I keep."

The door opened behind him. The hunter with the pond-ice eyes stood in it, and her face was carefully blank, and behind her the corridor was full of pale livery, and the singing outside had stopped.

"The Court convenes at moonrise," Ilsette said, lifting her cup. "You'll be comfortable until then, I hope. Think about what I showed you. Think about the woman at the shore." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "And when your friends come for you — and they will, the smuggler has never once abandoned a debt, it is her single virtue — do tell them the city already knows they're coming. It has known since the moment they entered. Ostrava eats roads, Wendell Brightwater. Tonight, it eats everything else."

* * *

They put him in a room with a bed, a window, and no door.

Not a bricked-up door — simply no door, the wall seamless as an eggshell, though he had clearly been brought in through it. The window looked out on a courtyard where an ash orchard grew in perfect rows, and beyond it the sun hung at its eternal almost-rising, and Wendell sat on the edge of the bed and pressed his palms flat against his knees and felt, for the first time in his life, what it was to be entirely inside someone else's power and unable to hate them for it.

That was the trap, he understood now. Not the walls. The understanding. She had shown him the truth of his enemy and it was *almost right*, and almost-right was a poison no antidote in any valley could draw.

Outside, the light began, very slowly, to fail toward a night that would bring the moonrise, and the Court, and whatever Ilsette Vane had decided honesty required of her.

And far off — so far he might have imagined it, so far it might have been hope itself misfiring in his chest — Wendell felt a tug at the edges of his blood. Faint. Familiar. Salt.

Someone was coming.
