## 20. The Stitch

Ilsette Vane had not slept in ninety years, and she had stopped counting the nights as a kindness to herself.

Sleep was for people with futures. Sleep was the luxury of a country that still existed. She sat instead in the Pale Court's high chamber, in a chair carved from the rib of a cathedral that had been standing when her grandmother was born, and she read reports by the light of lamps that burned without oil — ward-light, valley-light, stolen light. The lamps were the color of dawn that never quite arrived, and she had long ago stopped noticing how much she hated them.

The chamber had once been a war room. She had made it one again. Maps covered the walls now, but they were not the maps of Ostrava's glory days; those were gone, erased down to the ink and the memory of ink. These were maps of what remained: the returned kingdom, ragged at its edges like a coat worn through at the elbows, and beyond it the valley of Kestrel's Rest, small and green and infuriatingly whole.

She pressed her thumb against the valley on the map, hard enough to whiten the nail.

*Soon,* she thought. *Hold. Hold.*

A knock at the door — three soft strikes, the rhythm of her own household guard, the only rhythm left in Ostrava she trusted without thinking.

"Enter."

Corvess came in the way he always did, as if the room might be a trap set by someone cleverer than him. He was a big man gone gray at the temples though his face remained unlined — nothing in Ostrava aged properly anymore, which Ilsette counted among the many obscenities of their condition. He carried a slate, and on the slate was written the night's tally, and the tally was bad.

"Three more wards failed along the western fringe," he said. "The orchard roads are fraying. Two families report their house has... repeated itself. Same house, twice, side by side. The second copy is older than the first."

Ilsette closed her eyes. "And the people?"

"Unharmed. Confused. One of the children thinks she lives in both houses now." Corvess hesitated. "She may be right."

"Of course she may be right." Ilsette rose and went to the window. Below her, the capital spread out under its bruised perpetual dusk — towers leaning at angles architecture should not permit, streets that rearranged themselves overnight like a dog turning in its sleep, the great pale bulk of the Door at the city's heart, sealed, silent, humming a note so low that most people felt it in their teeth rather than heard it. Her city. Her beautiful, impossible, half-dissolved city, holding itself together the way a drowning man holds water.

"We are coming apart," she said quietly. "Not dying. Dying would be simpler. We are coming apart, stitch by stitch, and every day I find a new seam."

"The engines—"

"The engines are buying us time. Time is not a country." She turned back to him. "Where is the boy?"

"The gatekeeper? Held in the east wing, as you ordered. Comfortable. Fed. Frightened, but less than you'd expect." Corvess allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "He asked for salt this morning. For his food. Old habit, I think."

Something in Ilsette's chest turned over — an old, complicated feeling she had no time for. A keeper's child, asking for salt. Ritual lived deep in some bloodlines, deeper than sense.

"Prepare the audience chamber," she said. "I'll receive him tonight. And Corvess — no chains. No guards in the room. If we cannot make a frightened boy trust us with our faces alone, then we deserve to dissolve."

* * *

She had been a general once. That was the part no one in the valley would ever understand, because the valley had been built specifically never to understand it.

Ilsette Vane had been twenty-six years old when the Order of the Gatekeepers came to the palace of Ostrava with their polite letters and their terrible arithmetic. She remembered the meeting with a clarity that felt less like memory and more like a wound kept open on purpose. She had stood at the foot of the throne — young for a general, scarred from the border wars, decorated twice — while a soft-spoken man named Aldous Brightwater explained, with diagrams, why her country had to die.

Not be conquered. Not be punished. *Die.* Erased. Unmade from the world's memory like a debt forgiven by a creditor who also burns the ledger.

"There is a wound in the world," Aldous had said, tapping his diagrams, "and Ostrava is where it bleeds. Magic pools here. It warps here. Left alone, it will spread — it will eat outward until there is no stable land anywhere. We can close the wound. But a wound closes over flesh, General. Something must be inside the closing."

"And that something is us," Ilsette had said.

"That something is you," Aldous agreed, gently, the way men say terrible things when they have decided gentleness costs them nothing. "Your kingdom becomes the clot. Your people become the scar tissue. And the world outside goes on, safe, and in time it will not even remember to mourn you, because mourning requires memory, and memory is precisely what we must take."

She had looked at her king, old and ill and kind, and seen that he already knew. Had known for years, perhaps. Had been negotiating the terms of his country's death with the courtesy of a man settling an estate.

"And if we refuse?" she had asked.

Aldous had folded his diagrams away. "Then we do it anyway, General, and your last century is spent at war instead of at peace. I would prefer to spare you that. I am told I am a compassionate man."

They gave Ostrava a generation to put its affairs in order. One generation — fifty years, a blink, an insult dressed as mercy. Ilsette spent all fifty of those years preparing her people for erasure the way other generals prepare armies for siege. She built the exiles' registries so that no soul would go unrecorded. She negotiated the terms of the warding — the slow-time, the unaging, the preservation of the dead in their barrows — squeezing concessions from the Gatekeepers line by line, like a woman haggling over the price of her own funeral. She married late and lovelessly, to a good man who understood the marriage was a message: *the Vane line continues into the dark.*

And when the day came — when the stars went out over Ostrava one by one like candles pinched by a careful hand, when the borders dissolved into salt and silence — Ilsette Vane stood on the palace steps with her soldiers drawn up behind her and watched the sky forget her name.

She had expected terror. What she had not expected was the *patience* of it. Erasure was not fire or flood. It was a long gray afternoon that never ended, in which nothing hurt and nothing mattered and everyone she had ever sworn to protect stood beside her, unchanged, unaging, waiting for a dawn that the Gatekeepers' arithmetic said would never come.

Ninety years of that afternoon.

And then, without warning, without justice, without anyone asking her leave — the wards had failed, and Ostrava had *bled back into the world*, and Ilsette had woken from the gray afternoon into a green valley full of strangers who did not know she existed, standing on land that had been stolen from her people's grave.

She had wept that first night. She would admit that to no one. She had knelt in the mud of the threshold with her soldiers around her, feeling grass under her hands for the first time in ninety years, and she had wept like a girl, and then she had stood up and wiped her face and begun issuing orders, because that was the only prayer she knew.

*We died once,* she told them, gathered in the ruins of the first village they crossed. *We died politely, on schedule, for the safety of people who never learned our names. I am telling you now, as your general and your queen: we will not die twice. Whatever it costs. Whoever it costs. We will not do it twice.*

They had cheered her. That was the thing she held onto in the worst hours. They had *cheered.*

* * *

The audience chamber was lit low, the way she liked it — enough light to see a face honestly, not enough to flatter one. When they brought Wendell Brightwater in, she studied him the way she had once studied enemy officers across parley tables: posture, hands, eyes, the tells of a person deciding what they were willing to believe.

He was younger than she'd expected. Thin, sunburned, salt-bleached at the cuffs. He walked like a man who had measured thresholds his whole life — carefully, respectfully, as if floors might have opinions. His eyes went, before anything else, to the corners of the room. Checking the wards. Checking the exits. A keeper's eyes, even now.

"You asked for salt," she said, by way of opening.

He blinked. "I — yes. Habit. My grandmother always—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, and Ilsette saw him decide not to explain his grandmother to the woman holding him prisoner.

"Aldous Brightwater," she said. "Your ancestor. The architect of the erasure."

"I know what my family did." His voice was steady, but his hands were not; he clasped them behind his back like a soldier at attention, and she noted that too. Trained composure, self-taught. Interesting. Dangerous, possibly. "Do you?"

"I know everything," Ilsette said, "that the Gatekeepers wrote down. Which is nearly everything, because they were proud men and pride keeps records." She gestured to a chair. He didn't take it. Fine. Neither would she; let them both stand, two tired soldiers of opposing wars. "I know about the wound in the world. I know your family was charged with being the stitch that held it closed. And I know, Wendell Brightwater, that the stitching is failing — and that when it fails completely, my people do not merely fade. They come apart. Houses repeating themselves. Children living in two places at once. Roads that forget where they're going mid-stride. Have you seen it? On your way to my city?"

"I've seen it," he said quietly.

"Then you understand my position better than most." She stepped closer, and let him see her face without armor, because that was the weapon she had left that still worked: the truth, worn openly. "I am going to ask you for your blood. Freely given. And before you refuse me — and you will want to refuse me, and I would think slightly better of you for wanting to — I am going to show you what your refusal buys."

She led him to the eastern gallery, where the windows overlooked the lower terraces of the city, and she showed him the queues. The exile families waiting at the distribution houses for ward-rations, quiet and orderly, because her people had ninety years of practice at being patient in the dark. The barrow-fields where the honored dead lay preserved, neither alive nor decayed, waiting for a country to exist around them again. The school where teachers taught lessons that had been current a century ago, because the children could learn nothing new — new knowledge slid off the erased like rain off glass — and yet the teachers taught anyway, every day, because the alternative was to admit the children were learning nothing at all.

"This is what devotion looks like when it has nowhere left to go," Ilsette said. "My soldiers hold the borders. My engineers keep the engines burning. My people wait. Ninety years, Brightwater. They waited through the erasure without complaint because I asked them to, and now they wait through the unraveling without complaint because I ask them to. Every concession I wrung from your ancestors bought them this much dignity and no more. And now the stitches are pulling loose, and I have exactly one thread left in the world that can hold them." She turned to face him fully. "You."

Wendell was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was careful. "There's a Door. At the heart of the city. My blood opens it — and opening it ends Ostrava. Ends the suffering. Lets your people rest."

"Yes," Ilsette said. "That is the arithmetic the Gatekeepers would offer you. Rest. Peace. A gentle ending. They were very fond of gentle endings — for other people." She leaned toward him, and let ninety years come up through her voice like cold water through a floorboard. "Let me offer you different arithmetic, keeper. There are forty thousand souls in Ostrava. Forty thousand people who trusted their queen to save them, and whom their queen intends to save. If you open that Door, you kill every one of them — call it rest, call it mercy, call it whatever salves your conscience — and you do it to solve a problem your family created. Your blood made this wound. Your blood closed it over my country. And now your blood proposes to finish the job, and everyone will call it justice, because the dead are always easier to bury the second time."

"They voted," Wendell said suddenly, and Ilsette went still. "At the Door. The exiles. They voted whether to end. Some said yes. Some said no. Sera told me — the ones who refused, you'd be keeping them in this. In the unraveling. Forever."

Ah. So the smuggler had reached him. So the vote had happened. Ilsette filed the betrayal away — Sera Vane, her sister's daughter, her own blood, carrying tales to a Brightwater — and kept her face smooth, because a general's face was a fortress and hers had never once fallen.

"Some said no," she agreed. "Some always say no. In the border wars, some of my soldiers wanted to surrender every winter. I did not surrender them. Leadership means carrying the ones who cannot carry themselves." She held his gaze. "You are young, and you have been lied to beautifully — by ghosts, by smugglers, by a world that taught your family to call genocide 'warding.' But look at my people, Brightwater. Truly look. And then tell me whose mercy is the crueler."

He looked. She made him look for a long time.

When they took him back to the east wing, he went without chains, and he did not answer her, and she had commanded enough men to know what that silence meant: not agreement. *Delay.* He would go looking for another way. They always did, the idealists. It was almost touching.

* * *

Alone in the war room, Ilsette unrolled the oldest map she owned — not a map of land but of the wound itself, copied from the Gatekeepers' own archives in the last days before erasure, when a sympathetic archivist had traded it to her for a promise she had kept. The wound was drawn as a spiral, and Ostrava sat at its center like a pearl in a rotten oyster, and the stitches ran inward from the world's skin, and every stitch was labeled in Aldous Brightwater's neat, confident hand.

She had spent ninety years studying that handwriting. She knew his flourishes. She knew where he'd hesitated — there, at the fourth ring, where the ink pooled, where his pen had shaken just slightly. Even the butcher's hand trembled, once.

Her captains thought her ruthless. Let them. Ruthlessness was a coat you wore so that the softer things underneath stayed warm. What she was, beneath the crown and the gray afternoon and the lamp-light that never became morning, was simple:

She was the last officer of a country that had obeyed the rules, signed the terms, laid down on the table, and died on schedule — and the world had not even kept her flag.

She would not be reasonable. Reasonable was what Aldous Brightwater had been, with his diagrams and his compassion. She would be devoted, instead, all the way down, all the way through, and if devotion required her to burn the valley that had been built on her people's grave — well. The valley's founders had called that arithmetic mercy too.

A runner arrived breathless: the underground had struck again. Two ward-engines sabotaged in the ash orchards, the cartographer's daughter at the head of them — Miren, the map-maker's get, leading deserters and ghosts against her own salvation.

Ilsette listened, thanked the runner, and dismissed her. Then she stood alone with the spiral map, and her thumb found the center of the wound, and rested there.

"Miren," she said softly, to the empty room. "Your father drew beautiful maps. Let's see if you draw a better ending."

She rang for her guard. "Ready the Pale Court," she told Corvess. "We march on the orchards at dusk. Bring the engines' replacement parts — and bring fire."

Outside the window, the false dawn that never broke hung over her crumbling, beloved, unforgivable country, and Ilsette Vane began, at last, to prepare for war.
