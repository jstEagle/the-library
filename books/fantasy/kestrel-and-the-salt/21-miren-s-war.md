## 21. Miren's War

The ward-engine at Vaskel Bridge breathed.

Miren had learned its rhythm over three nights of watching from the reed beds: a slow inhale of pale light, a slow exhale of heat that made the river steam even in the cold. It stood on iron legs in the middle of the crossing like a heron made of bones and glass, and every breath it took fed the Pale Court's net — the lattice of stolen magic stretched across Ostrava's throat, the thing that let Ilsette hear whispers three provinces away and send her hunters down roads that rearranged themselves to serve her.

Her father would have drawn it. That was the thought that came, as it always came, uninvited: *her father would have drawn this engine, mapped its veins, labeled each part in his small precise hand.* He had been executed for less than what she was about to do. For ink. For lines on paper. And she was going to do this with fire.

"Third watch," whispered Doss behind her, in the reeds. "Change of guard."

Miren counted. Two soldiers crossing the bridge, their pale cloaks gone gray with river damp, pikes shouldered, bored. Bored was good. Bored men looked at the water instead of the reeds. She waited until they reached the far end, then touched Doss's shoulder and moved.

They crawled through mud that smelled of rot and old salt — the whole country smelled of old salt now, since the Gate had fallen, since the wound had opened and everything bled through. The underground called it the Tide. Nobody said *the kingdom is leaking* where walls might listen. The city ate roads; the Court listened through them.

At the bridge's foot crouched the engine's keeper: a thin man in Court gray who fed it measured spoonfuls of something luminous from a lead-lined box. Miren had watched him for three nights too. He never sang. He never spoke to the machine. He only worked, hunched, careful, like a man feeding an animal he feared.

That was the line, she thought. That was the line she'd sworn not to cross, back when the underground was a handful of deserters and smugglers meeting in a drowned cellar, back when she'd said it aloud with her father's map spread between them: *We don't kill keepers. They're conscripts. They're us.*

She had believed it the way you believe things before you've held a knife.

"Signal," she breathed.

Across the river, a lantern blinked twice. Old Sef, seventy years erased and still going, up on the mill hill with the decoy fire. Somewhere below, in the culverts, Tam and the twins were already swimming with the charge — a clay pot of Wendell Brightwater's blood-salt mixed with resin, the only thing they'd found that burned through ward-glass. A boy who wasn't even here anymore had armed them all. Miren tried not to think about that either. There was a long list of things she tried not to think about, and it grew every day, and she suspected that was what war was: a list getting longer while you got better at not reading it.

The engine inhaled.

Now.

The explosion came up through the soles of her feet before she heard it — a white concussion, a shriek of glass, and the heron of bones and glass folded inward on itself like a knee breaking. Light spilled everywhere, wrong-colored light, and the net above them — invisible, but Miren could feel it the way you feel a hair pulled taut against your neck — the net *tore*. She heard it tear. Everyone did. A sound like a harp string snapping across the whole sky, and for one glorious second the night was just night, dark and dumb and free.

Then the screaming started.

Not the soldiers'. Not yet. The engine's. It had a voice, she realized, it had always had a voice, pitched too high for most ears, and now it died loudly, wailing down through octaves like a kettle falling off a cliff. The keeper was running toward it, arms out, shouting words she couldn't parse — *no, no, don't, it holds the—*

"Holds the what?" Doss gasped beside her. "What does it hold?"

Miren didn't know. That was the problem with sabotage. You broke the machine, and the machine's purpose stayed a secret, and the secret came out of the wreckage wearing your face.

"It holds the bridge," Sef shouted from the mill hill, too late, his old voice cracking across the water. "Girl, the engines hold the *bridges*—"

Vaskel Bridge groaned.

It was a beautiful bridge, or had been — Ostravan architecture never did anything by halves, arches stacked on arches like a hymn written in stone. Now the hymn changed key. Miren watched, frozen in the reeds, as the central span sagged, stone dust blooming white in the engine-light, and the two bored soldiers at the far end turned and ran back toward the collapse instead of away from it, because there were people on the bridge, exiles, a family with a cart, refugees from the eastern orchards who'd chosen the night crossing because the Court taxed the day ones—

The span went.

The sound was enormous and then it was nothing, and the river closed over the wreckage with a slap, and the steam rose, and the family with the cart was under it.

Miren was moving before she decided to move. Mud, water, cold like a hand closing around her chest — she dove into the black churn where the cart had gone down and found a wheel, found a woman's hair, found a child. She dragged the child up. Someone else dragged her up. Doss. Sef's people. Strangers from the underground she'd led here, hauling bodies out of the river by twos and threes, laying them on the bank in a row that got longer and longer.

Seven alive. Nine dead. One soldier, half-drowned, coughing river water and weeping, begging — not for mercy, for *forgiveness*, which was worse, because he kept saying he'd seen the family on the bridge and hadn't warned them, orders, he had orders—

"We don't kill keepers," Miren said. Her voice came out strange. Flat. "Get him dry. Get him gone."

"You just killed nine people," Doss said quietly. Not cruelly. That was the worst part. Doss was never cruel. "Ilsette's hunters kill nine people and it's tyranny. We do it and it's war. Who told you it was different?"

Nobody answered him. Miren knelt in the mud beside the row of the dead and did the arithmetic she knew she'd be doing for the rest of her life: one engine destroyed, one thread of the queen's net cut, nine civilians drowned, one bridge gone that the exiles needed, one child saved who would grow up hearing how she'd been pulled from the water by the woman who broke the bridge.

Her hands wouldn't stop shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs and made herself look at each face in the row. Her father had taught her that — *a mapmaker honors the territory by looking.* These were territory now. She was making war on her own country, and her country was these faces, and she had sworn she wouldn't make any more of them.

*We don't kill keepers,* she had said. And tonight she hadn't killed the keeper. She'd killed everyone around him.

* * *

They buried what could be buried at dawn — dawn being a relative mercy in Ostrava, where the sun cleared the hills by a finger's width and hung there, sullen, forever climbing toward a noon it never reached. The exiles dug in the soft ground above the flood line, and Miren worked beside them because she refused to stand apart, refused the general's distance, though her shoulders screamed and her hands were raw from the river.

Sef came and stood next to her, leaning on his spade.

"In the first years after the erasure," he said, "we tried to end ourselves. Did Sera ever tell you that? Whole families walking into the ash orchards and just... stopping. You can't die here, but you can stop. Like lamps." He turned the spade over. "Ilsette found us. Carried some of them home on her own back. That was before the crown, before the Court. She wept over them, girl. I saw it. And I have spent a hundred years trying to decide whether she was right to save us and wrong about everything after, or wrong to save us and right about everything after, and I still don't know."

"I know," Miren said. "I know exactly what I am tonight."

"And what's that?"

"A person who breaks bridges." She looked at the river, at the broken arches standing in the current like ribs. "My father drew maps so people could find each other. That was the whole point of maps, he said. Not power. Finding each other. And I've spent a month cutting the country into pieces so the queen can't find us, and I can't tell anymore if I'm fighting her or becoming her with worse funding."

Sef laughed — a dry, surprised bark, quickly strangled. "Worse funding. Yes." He sobered. "You want absolution, girl, go find a priest. What I can give you is this: the difference between you and Ilsette isn't the body count. It's that you're counting. She stopped counting a century ago. The moment you stop counting, come find me, and I'll tell you honestly which one you've become."

He shuffled off to help with the graves, and Miren stood alone with the counting, which was heavier than any spade.

Tam found her at midmorning. He was sixteen, river-pale still, wrapped in someone's spare cloak, and he wouldn't meet her eyes.

"The twins won't come back," he said. "After last night. They say—" His jaw worked. "They say you knew the bridge might go. They say you should have evacuated the crossing first."

"Did I know?"

"You *could* have known." Now he looked at her, and his eyes were old, older than hers, and that was obscene, a boy of sixteen with eyes like that. "You're the cartographer's daughter. Reading terrain is what you *do*. You looked at that bridge and you saw an engine on it and you didn't ask what the engine was holding up. Because asking would have cost you the strike. So you didn't ask."

The truth of it went through her like the cold had, clean and total. She had known. In the place where knowing lived before you admitted it, she had known, and she had chosen not to look, because looking was expensive and the strike was cheap, and that trade — that exact trade — was the one Ilsette made every day of her long life.

"Tell them," Miren said, "that they're right."

Tam blinked. "That's it? No argument?"

"They're right. Tell them I'll bring the evacuation plans to every target from now on, before approval. If they want to vet them themselves, better. And tell them—" She stopped. Started again. "Tell them I'm sorry. Not the word. The thing the word is supposed to mean. I'll show them the thing."

Tam studied her a long moment, and something in his face loosened, not forgiveness — she hadn't earned that — but maybe the suspension of verdict. "The twins will want the evacuation plans anyway," he said. "They just wanted you to say it out loud." He hesitated. "There's also a message. From the Court side. A hunter brought it to the cellar drop, which means they know where the drop is, which means—"

"Which means it's a trap or a taunt."

"It's addressed to you. By name." He handed over a slip of paper, heavy stock, sealed with pale wax. "The hunter was blindfolded and released. That's not how hunters work."

Miren broke the seal. The handwriting inside was elegant, unhurried, the hand of a woman who had never once needed to write quickly:

*Miren Cartwright. Your father drew my borders for eleven years. He was the best I ever employed, and I regret the necessity of his death more than you will ever be permitted to believe. So hear me once, as one professional to another: the engines you are breaking are not mine. They are the stitches' opposite — the sutures the Gate left in my country when it tore loose, and every one you destroy widens the wound. Ask the gatekeeper's ghost what the Door requires. Then ask yourself what your father would have drawn, if he had seen what you saw last night.*

*Come to the Pale Court. Or don't. But stop breaking things you haven't read.*

*I., Q.*

Miren read it three times. Around her, the exiles filled the graves, and the river ran through the ruin of the bridge, and somewhere north, she knew, Wendell Brightwater and Sera Vane were moving toward the capital with a key, and every hour she spent doubting was an hour the queen gained.

But the words sat in her chest like swallowed stones. *Stitches' opposite. Widens the wound.* Last night the net had torn, and the tearing had felt like freedom, and freedom had drowned nine people, and what if the tearing had done something else besides? What if the country itself was bleeding faster now, through cuts she'd made?

She thought of her father's last map — the half-map to the Door, hidden in the lining of her coat, drawn in a hand that had grown shakier over eleven years of serving a queen he must have hated and feared and, God help him, understood. Had he known what the engines were? Had he drawn their schematics believing he was mapping a cage when he was really mapping bandages?

*A mapmaker honors the territory by looking.*

She had stopped looking. That was the whole of it. Somewhere between the execution and the reed beds, she had stopped looking and started striking, and striking was easy, and easy was how tyrants were made.

"Doss," she called. He looked up from a grave. "Get the cellars ready to move. All of them. And send word to the ghost — the old gatekeeper. I need to see him."

Doss frowned. "About the next target?"

"About whether there *is* a next target." She folded the queen's letter into her coat, next to her father's map, tyrant and father riding together over her heart. "And Doss — if I'm wrong about this, if the letter's just poison dressed as doubt, then I'll lead the next strike myself, first through the door. But if I'm right—" She looked at the nine mounds of fresh earth, at the child sleeping off her rescue under a stranger's cloak. "If I'm right, then last night I didn't fight a war. I helped dig a grave for a whole country, and I put nine innocent people in it ahead of schedule."

She turned north, toward the drowned bell tower, toward the ghost, toward whatever terrible answer was waiting.

Behind her, the river kept running through the broken bridge, patient as arithmetic, carrying the cold down toward the sea and the valley beyond — carrying, though no one in Ostrava could have known it, the first faint taste of salt toward the place where a keeper's blood was already fraying at the edges of the world.

And far off, at the limit of sight, one star flickered — the first star, the returned star — burning over the hills like a lit fuse.
