## 23. Sera in the Undercity

The city had swallowed the road an hour ago, and Sera Vane was still falling.

Not falling the way a body falls — there was no ground rushing up to meet her, no wind in her teeth. She fell the way a name falls out of use: gradually, then all at once, the streets of Ostrava rearranging themselves beneath her boots like a hand of cards being reshuffled by something that had never learned mercy. The cobbles rippled. A stairway she'd climbed three breaths ago now descended into standing water. The alley mouth she'd aimed for had become a wall, and the wall had become a door, and the door had become a face — pale limestone, hollow-eyed, watching her with the patient hunger of architecture.

"Go on, then," Sera told it, and spat blood onto its threshold.

The city flinched. Small victory. She took it.

She had been running since the convulsion began — since the whole capital shuddered like a dog shaking off rain and the street she stood on folded itself around Miren's people and spat them out in three directions. Wendell had been on the far side of the square when it happened. She'd seen him for half a second, salt-light crawling up his arms like frost climbing a window, and then the buildings closed between them like a book snapping shut, and she had been alone in the dark with a city that wanted her lost.

It wasn't personal. That was the worst part. Ostrava didn't hate her. It simply served its queen, and its queen wanted the keeper, and everything else — smugglers, nieces, ghosts, the whole ragged parliament of the unwanted — was debris to be swept aside.

Sera swept aside anyway. It was the one thing she'd always been good at.

* * *

The undercity began where the city's pretending stopped.

Above, Ostrava wore its Pale Court face: white avenues, banners of unbleached silk, fountains that ran with light instead of water. Below — down stairs that hadn't existed until you needed them, through archways the masons had never built — the bones showed. Here the walls were old grief and older stone, and here the city's rearranging slowed, because even a living thing grows tired of moving what it has already buried.

Sera moved through the buried parts like a woman reading a letter she'd written long ago and hoped never to reread.

There was the cistern where Dossen had taught her to hold her breath for four minutes, back when she was nineteen and certain that owing nothing to anyone was the same as being free. There was the shrine niche where the exiles had left offerings to saints who no longer had names — just shapes carved in salt, worn smooth by centuries of thumbs. Her own thumb found the groove without asking permission. She snatched it back as if the stone had bitten her.

"You came back," said a voice from the dark, and Sera's knife was in her hand before the echo finished.

Dossen stepped into the grey light, and he looked exactly as he had twenty years ago, because everyone in Ostrava looked exactly as they had, forever, that being the entire obscene point of the place. Same crooked nose. Same rope belt. Same eyes that had once watched her walk out of this country and had not been surprised.

"I came back," she said.

"For him." Not a question. "The keeper."

"For all of it. For everything I walked away from." She sheathed the knife slowly, deliberately, so he could see her do it. "I'm not asking forgiveness, Doss. I'm asking passage."

He studied her the way he used to study cargo — turning her over, checking for cracks, weighing what she'd fetch. Then he laughed, short and ugly, and turned back into the dark.

"Come on, then. You know the toll."

"The toll," Sera repeated. "I paid the toll when I was nineteen. I paid it when I told Ilsette how the wards were keyed, and I've been paying interest ever since."

"I know." He didn't turn around. "That's why it's going to hurt."

* * *

It hurt.

The way through the deep city ran along the Spine — the old processional road that the exiles had dug out of the rock in the years after the erasure, when they still believed someone would come back for them. It was guarded now. Of course it was guarded. Ilsette's wardens had found the undercity generations ago and garrisoned it with men who could not die and therefore had nothing left to fear except their queen's disappointment, which was worse.

Dossen got her past the first checkpoint by the simple expedient of walking up to his former comrades and starting an argument about grain rationing that had apparently been running, off and on, for sixty years. Sera slipped behind them while they shouted numbers at each other with the passion of scholars defending a thesis. She was almost clear when the youngest warden — a boy who'd been a boy when the stars went out and would be a boy when they burned again or didn't — caught her sleeve.

"You're the Vane girl," he said. Wonderingly. "You're the one who crossed twice."

"Third time now," Sera said. "I'm collecting crossings like other women collect jewelry."

"They say you're why we can't leave."

The words landed exactly where he'd aimed them, which meant somewhere under her ribs, in the soft tissue where guilt lived and grew fat. She let him hold her sleeve a moment longer than she needed to. Let him see her take it.

"Yes," she said. "I am. And I'm going to be why it ends, if I get where I'm going. You can tell your friends that. Tell them Sera Vane is done paying in other people's coin."

Then she broke his grip and ran, because sentiment was a luxury and the city was still moving.

Behind her, she heard the young warden shout — not alarm. Something else. Something that sounded, absurdly, like hope being passed hand to hand down a line of dead men who couldn't die.

* * *

The third debt nearly killed her.

It was waiting at the Weeping Stair, where groundwater bled through the rock in slow silver threads and the air tasted of iron and old prayers. Sera had owed a favor there for two decades — owed it to a thing called the Tollmistress, who had once ferried her across a flooded quarter for the promise of a future service, unspecified, uncapped. Smuggler's arithmetic. She'd signed it young and stupid and had spent every year since praying the Tollmistress would die first.

Living things died. The Tollmistress was not entirely a living thing.

"You smell of the valley," the Tollmistress said, unfolding from a doorway that had definitely not contained a doorway a moment before. She was tall the way reeds are tall, and wrong the way reeds are not, and her voice came out of her like water out of a cracked jug. "Salt and sunlight and a man's blood on your hands. You want the Door."

"I want the man who's going to open it."

"One and the same, near enough." The Tollmistress drifted closer, and Sera made herself stand still, made herself breathe, made herself remember that fear was just information wearing a costume. "The city is closing its throat, little smuggler. Every road to the Door is becoming a road away from it. By midnight there will be no path at all — unless someone who knows the city's old body walks you through its new one."

"And the price?"

"What you promised me. A service. Unspecified." The Tollmistress smiled, and her smile was a crack in a jug. "Here is my specification. When the kingdom ends — and it will end, girl, I have felt it coming in my stones for a hundred years — some part of me wants to end with it. But I am bound to the city's bones, and the bones will persist, empty, singing. Someone must carry my name out. Speak it in the world beyond. Give me a grave in a country that has graves."

Sera thought of Miren, who kept records. Who had made it her whole furious business that nothing vanish twice.

"Done," she said. "Miren Delacourt will carve your name where the valley can read it. I'll stand there while she does."

"Miren." The Tollmistress tasted the name. "The cartographer's whelp. Good. Haters make the best monument-builders." She extended a hand like a bundle of grey sticks. "Take my arm. And hold your breath, child. We are going to go the way the water goes."

* * *

They went the way the water goes.

It was not travel so much as surrender — the Tollmistress folding herself into a fissure barely wider than a coffin and pulling Sera after her, down through strata of the city's memory: a cellar where someone had once baked bread for ten thousand; a wall of scratched names, thousands deep, the exiles' census of everyone erased; a chamber full of ward-engines humming the queen's eternal note, which Sera passed with her heart hammering and her hands itching for a hammer. Once, impossibly, they surfaced inside a fountain in the Pale Court itself, and Sera saw, across a courtyard of white marble, Queen Ilsette Vane standing at a window with her back turned, motionless as a portrait of herself.

Her aunt. Her blood. The woman she had armed.

Ilsette did not turn. Perhaps the city hadn't told her yet. Perhaps she knew and let her niece look, the way you leave a trap visible so the animal understands it was never clever.

Then the water took Sera under again, and the moment went with her, and she hated herself for being grateful.

* * *

The last stretch she did alone, because the Tollmistress's body ended where the Door's hill began, and the old thing pressed something into her palm as they parted — a river-smoothed pebble, warm as a held coin.

"For luck," the Tollmistress said. "Or for weight. Whichever you need more."

The hill above the undercity was screaming.

Not metaphorically. The living city had raised itself around the Door's approach like scar tissue around a splinter, walls leaning in, streets coiling, and the sound it made was the sound stone makes when it is asked to betray its nature — a low grinding shriek that Sera felt in her molars. Wardens swarmed the approaches. Above them, on the ridge, she could see the flash of the exiles' underground holding a line — Miren's people, deserters and ghosts, dying badly and getting back up because death in Ostrava was a revolving door and courage was the only thing you couldn't come back from.

And past all of it, at the top, a doorway of older darkness than the city's, with a figure standing before it, arms bare, salt-light climbing him like ivy.

Wendell.

Something in Sera's chest that had been clenched since the square finally, partially, unclenched. Not relief — relief was for afterward. More like a compass needle finding north after spinning: the simple animal fact of direction restored.

She fought the last hundred yards the ugly way. No finesse left, no debts to spend, no favors to call — just a knife, a smuggler's instinct for the gap in any guard, and twenty years of guilt converted at last into fuel. A warden's pike opened her forearm to the bone and she used the arm anyway. Another caught her ankle and she put her knife through his gauntlet into the meat of his hand and felt him let go and heard herself say *sorry*, actually say it, because these were Dossen's people, were her people, were the people she'd sold once and was now spending her blood to un-sell, and none of it was clean, none of it had ever been clean, and she was done pretending otherwise.

She crested the ridge. Cut down the last warden between them — he was young, he was crying, she'd think about him later or she wouldn't survive thinking about him now —

— and there was the Door.

It was smaller than she'd expected. Older. A plain arch of black stone in a frame of white, and cold pouring off it like breath off a winter lake, and Wendell Brightwater standing in front of it with a key in one hand and his whole ruined, decent face turned toward her.

"Sera." His voice cracked on it. "How did you — the city —"

"The city owes me. Everyone owes me. Turns out that's finally worth something." She limped the last few steps, dripping, bleeding, grinning like a skull. "Don't look at me like that. I'm not here to talk you out of it."

His jaw worked. "Miren said you might try to stop me. After the vote —"

"After the vote." She waved it off, though her stomach dropped hearing it confirmed — the exiles were really doing it, really gathering, really choosing. "Listen to me, gatekeeper. I spent nineteen years telling myself I crossed that Gate alone and owed nobody anything. It was a lie. It was the biggest lie of my life and it cost a kingdom." She stepped close enough that he could see the blood sheeting down her arm, close enough that he reached out — instinctively, helplessly — and pressed his palm over the wound, and his salt-light sank into her skin and the pain dulled to a hum, and she let him, because pride was another lie and she was fresh out.

"So no," she said. "I'm not here to stop you. I'm here because whatever you do at that Door, you don't do it alone. Not while I'm breathing. That's not love poetry, Wendell. That's arithmetic. Everything I owe your family, your bloodline, that valley, this one — it comes due tonight, and I pay my debts standing next to the people I owe them to."

Wendell looked at her for a long moment. Behind him the Door breathed cold. Around them the battle roared and the city screamed and somewhere below, drums were gathering — the Pale Court, marching.

"You're bleeding through my fingers," he said at last.

"I know. Fix it later." She turned to face the slope, knife out, shoulder set against his. "Here they come."

Down through the burning streets, banners of unbleached silk rising like foam on a wave, the Pale Court was coming — and behind them, scattered and stubborn and carrying lanterns, the exiles were coming too, coming to vote, coming to choose, coming to decide whether a kingdom should live forever or be allowed, at last, to rest.

Sera rolled her wounded shoulder, spat blood, and grinned at the dark.

"Well, Auntie," she murmured. "Let's find out what we're all made of."
