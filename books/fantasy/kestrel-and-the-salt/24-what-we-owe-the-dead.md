## 24. What We Owe the Dead

The Door did not look like a door.

That was Wendell's first thought when they finally stood before it, in the ruined heart of Ostrava where the capital's living streets had gone still and gray as old scar tissue. It was a seam in the air, a vertical line of pale light no wider than a hand, standing alone in a plaza of cracked white stone. No frame, no hinges, no wood or iron. Just light, thin as a knife's edge, humming with a note he knew in his bones — the same note the Salt Gate had sung on the night the stars went out.

Around that seam, the plaza was filling with people.

They came in ones and twos at first, then in streams — exiles who could not die and could not leave, pouring out of the ash orchards and the drowned districts and the undercity tunnels Miren's underground had opened for them. Some walked. Some were carried. One ancient man came borne on a bier of woven reeds, attended by six descendants who looked exactly as old as he did, because in Ostrava everyone had stopped at whatever age the erasure had caught them, and time here was a coat you wore rather than a river that carried you.

Wendell stood near the seam with the key heavy against his chest, wrapped in cloth against his skin, and watched a nation assemble.

"They're afraid," Sera said beside him. Her voice was low, roughened from the fighting in the streets. There was a cut above her eyebrow she refused to let anyone treat. "Look at them. They've been told for three hundred years what to want. Now nobody's telling them anything."

"Good," said Miren, on his other side. The cartographer's daughter had ink still ground into the creases of her knuckles from drawing maps by candlelight, maps of a country that rearranged itself nightly. She had a way of saying *good* to things that frightened everyone else. "Fear means they understand the question."

* * *

It had been Miren's idea, though Wendell would spend the rest of his life believing it had been the Door's idea speaking through her.

They had gathered the night before in the flooded bell tower's dry upper room — Wendell, Sera, Miren, and the ragged parliament of the underground: smugglers, ghosts, deserters, a one-armed woman named Kess who had once been Ilsette's quartermaster and now kept the resistance's ledgers. Wendell had laid the key on the table between them, and the argument had begun before the candle finished guttering.

"You can't put it to a vote," Kess had said flatly. "A vote is a luxury of the living. These people haven't been alive in three centuries. You'd be asking them to choose their own funeral."

"I'd be asking them to choose," Miren had answered, "which is more than anyone has ever offered them. Not even Ilsette offers them that. She offers them forever whether they want it or not. Forever *is* the cage, Kess. A door that only locks from outside is just a prettier cell."

"And if they say no?" the old quartermaster had demanded. "If they vote to keep the kingdom? Then what does our gatekeeper do with his key?"

Everyone had looked at Wendell then. He had felt the weight of it settle on him like snow — not the weight of the key, which was iron and small, but the weight of being the instrument of other people's salvation without ever having been asked what they wanted saved *from*.

He had thought of his grandmother's journal. He had thought of the Salt Gate, and how he had tended it his whole life believing he was protecting the valley, never once asking whether the valley wanted to be protected from something it couldn't see. Duty, he understood now, could be its own kind of tyranny when it never paused to listen.

"We honor it," he had said, and heard his own voice come out steadier than he felt. "If they say no, I put the key away. If they say yes — if even most of them say yes — then we have to talk about what happens to the rest." He had looked around the table. "But I won't end a country over the heads of the people in it. That's what the Gatekeepers did. That's what Ilsette does. I didn't walk across a dead kingdom to become another person who decides for them."

Sera had said nothing through all of it. She had sat with her arms crossed, watching him with an expression he was learning to read — the look she wore when something he said had landed somewhere old and bruised. Later, on the tower stairs, she had caught his sleeve.

"You know what you just did," she'd said quietly.

"Voted wrong, probably. Half of them will hate it."

"No." She'd shaken her head. "You gave up the ending. Writers would kill for the ending you just gave up — the clean one, where the hero turns the key and everything hurts but it's *right*. You traded it for a room full of arguing ghosts." Her mouth had twisted, not quite a smile. "My aunt would call that weakness. I'm starting to think it's the only strength there is."

* * *

Now the plaza filled, and the question of how to hold a vote became real and awkward and human.

There was no precedent. Ostrava had been a kingdom for a thousand years and erased for three hundred; in all that time, no one had ever asked its people for their consent to anything. Kess produced the old census rolls from the resistance archives — names, districts, the accounting of the unaging dead. Miren drew a great circle on the white stone of the plaza in chalk mixed with ash, dividing it into sections by district, so that no one would be trampled and every voice would stand within sight of the seam.

Sera organized the counting. She was good at this, Wendell realized — good at logistics, at moving frightened people, at turning chaos into lines. It was the smuggler in her, the woman who had once moved contraband through a hole in the world. She posted runners. She set up lanterns, though the sun of Ostrava hung low and coppery and would not set, would never set, and the lanterns burned more as ritual than necessity.

By what passed for midday, there were thousands in the plaza. Perhaps ten thousand. Perhaps more, still coming down the avenues, some of them walking out of buildings that had been tombs for centuries, blinking in the copper light like things newly hatched.

Wendell climbed onto the low steps of a shattered fountain so he could be seen, and discovered that ten thousand faces was a thing the body responded to before the mind could intervene. His hands shook. He gripped the key through his shirt.

"I'm Wendell Brightwater," he said, and his voice cracked across the silence like ice on a pond. "I was the keeper of the Salt Gate. I know most of you have no reason to trust me. My family helped build the thing that erased you. I'm not here to apologize my way into your good graces — there isn't time, and apologies don't weigh much against three hundred years. I'm here because I found the Door. And I found the key. And I need you to know what both of them do before anyone asks you to decide anything."

He told them. All of it — the wound, the stitch, his bloodline, the truth the ghost of the old gatekeeper had given him in the dark beneath the capital. That Ostrava persisted because it had never been allowed to finish. That the kingdom devoured the valley's magic to sustain itself, and would devour the valley entire, and after the valley there would be nothing left to eat and Ostrava would starve slowly, eternally, a country preserved like a fly in amber. That closing the Door would let the kingdom end — gently, he believed, the way a held breath ends — and let its people go wherever the unmade go.

"And I won't lie to you about what ending means," he said. "I don't know if there's anything after. I don't know if it's rest or nothing. Nobody knows. What I know is that this—" he gestured at the copper sky, the frozen sun, the streets that moved like muscle when the queen willed it— "isn't living either. But that's my judgment, and I've learned my judgment isn't the one that matters here. So we're going to do something that has never been done in Ostrava."

He let the silence stretch until it hurt.

"We're going to vote."

* * *

The sound that went up from the plaza was not cheering. Wendell had expected cheering, or rage, or both. What he got was a low murmur that rolled outward through the crowd like wind through wheat — thousands of voices repeating the word to neighbors, to children, to the dead-beside-them who had forgotten the concept. *Vote.* Somewhere in the middle distance a woman began to laugh, high and disbelieving, and could not stop, and no one told her to.

Kess read the question aloud from a scroll, in the formal cadences of the old courts, because formality was a kind of respect: *"Shall the kingdom of Ostrava consent to its own ending, and release its people from the erasure?"*

The method was simple, because simple methods are hard to corrupt. Two great vessels of clay, salvaged from the ruins, set on either side of the Door's seam. Each voter would come forward and place a token — a white stone for yes, a black stone for no — stones gathered from the plaza itself, from the bones of the city. The vessels would be sealed and broken open only when the last name on Kess's rolls was called or the last voter stood down.

It took the rest of the day.

Wendell had imagined, in the naive hours before dawn, that the yes votes would come easily — that a people suffering under eternity would leap at the chance to stop. He had not accounted for what eternity does to a soul. He had not accounted for the farmer who came to the vessel weeping and dropped a black stone, whispering, "My orchard is still here. My trees are still here. If the kingdom ends, where do my trees go?" He had not accounted for the young man — young for three hundred years — who pressed a black stone into the jar and said, "I was engaged when the erasure came. My betrothed is dust somewhere in these streets. This kingdom is the last place she existed. If it ends, she ends twice, and I'll have voted for it."

He had not accounted for the mother of four who stood before the white-stone vessel for a long time, tokens in both fists, and finally put one white stone in and one black stone in, and when the tellers moved to stop her she said, "Two of my children want to stay. Two want to go. I am both of them. Count me however you like and may God forgive the arithmetic."

Sera worked the crowd all day, and Wendell watched her do it, and understood that this too was penance. Every exile who came to her confused and terrified, she took by the shoulders and explained the choice plainly, without persuasion, offering neither side. Once, when a bent old woman spat at her feet and called her *the queen's blood*, Sera only nodded and said, "Yes. And today your voice counts more than mine. Go vote." Wendell saw the old woman pause at that — saw something shift behind her eyes — before she shuffled toward the vessels.

Miren counted. That was her station, with two of the underground's scribes, tallying district by district on fresh paper, and her face throughout was the face Wendell had first seen on the road through the ash orchards: fierce, unsentimental, and lit from underneath by something she would have denied was hope.

The line moved for hours. The copper sun did not move at all.

* * *

Near dusk-that-was-not-dusk, the flow of voters thinned, and Wendell walked the edge of the plaza to stretch legs gone numb, and that was where he found the dissenters.

They had gathered apart, several hundred strong, in the shadow of a collapsed colonnade — those who had voted no, or who had not voted at all, standing together the way people stand together when they suspect they are about to lose. An old soldier addressed them from a broken statue's plinth, a broad man with a voice like gravel poured slow. Wendell recognized the uniform under the weathering: the Pale Court's colors, but faded, abandoned. A deserter, then. One of Ilsette's own.

"You hear them in there?" the soldier was saying, jerking his chin toward the plaza. "'Let go, let rest, let end.' Easy words from a valley boy and a smuggler and a chalk-slinging girl who's never buried anyone she couldn't visit. I fought for this kingdom before the dark came. I watched the Gatekeepers' spell take the sky. And I will not stand in line and politely vote myself into oblivion because a foreigner found a shiny key."

A rumble of assent. Wendell should have walked on. Instead he stepped into the colonnade's shadow, and hundreds of faces turned to him, and he felt the old familiar terror — the terror of the world beyond the stones, except now the stones were people.

"You think I want this," he said. His voice carried badly in the colonnade, so he climbed onto a fallen block, clumsy, undignified. "You think I crossed a dead country and fought the queen's hunters and nearly drowned in a bell tower because I *want* your kingdom to die. I don't. I've seen it. It's the most beautiful terrible thing I've ever seen, and half of me wants to lock the Door and throw the key in the sea and let you all have your frozen forever." He swallowed. "But forever is eating my home. And when my home is gone, forever starts eating you — slowly, from the inside, a kingdom with nothing left to be a kingdom *for*. Ilsette calls that salvation. I call it a very long way of refusing to grieve."

"So we should grieve?" someone shouted. "On command?"

"No," Wendell said. "You should *choose*. That's all today is. Not me choosing for you, not the queen choosing for you, not three-hundred-year-old spells choosing for you. If the vote goes your way — if the no wins — I will put this key away and stand between Ilsette and anyone who tries to force the Door, including her, including me. That's what honoring a vote means. Both sides of it." He looked out at their angry, frightened, immortal faces. "I spent my whole life keeping a gate I never chose, for people who never asked me. I know what it does to a person. I wouldn't wish it on a country."

Silence in the colonnade. Then the old deserter soldier looked at him for a long moment and said, gruffly, almost gently, "You're either the honest fool they say you are or the best liar the valley ever made." He turned back to his people. "Either way — get in line. Let's count properly. If we win, we win clean."

* * *

They broke the vessels at what would have been midnight, under the unmoving copper sun, before the whole assembled multitude.

Kess broke the seals herself, with the ceremony of a woman who had audited armies. Miren and her scribes brought the tallies forward. Sera stood at Wendell's shoulder, close enough that he could feel her breathing, and he noticed distantly that her hand had found the back of his belt and gripped it, the way you grip something when the ground might give.

"The rolls are complete," Kess announced, in a voice trained to carry across parade grounds. "Every district heard. Every name called. Spoiled ballots noted and excluded." She looked down at Miren's papers, and Wendell saw the old quartermaster's jaw tighten, and thought: *no.*

"The question was the ending of Ostrava," Kess said. "The count stands: four thousand one hundred and six against."

The colonnade dissenters roared.

"Six thousand nine hundred and forty-one for."

And the rest of the plaza — the greater, quieter multitude — made a sound Wendell had never heard a crowd make. Not a cheer. Something lower than cheering. A long, collective exhale, ten thousand lungs releasing a breath held for three hundred years, and underneath it, rising, a few voices singing — an old song, a harvest song maybe, or a lullaby, he couldn't tell — taken up line by line through the districts Miren had chalked, spreading like dawn across the frozen country.

Sixty-three percent. Wendell turned the number over in his mind and found it did not feel like victory. It felt like a wound agreeing to close. Nearly a third of a nation had looked at peace and said *no, not yet, not like this* — and their no was written in Miren's neat hand on the tally sheets, and it would be honored, and it would be remembered, and that was the whole difference between this and everything Ostrava had ever known.

"What happens to them?" he asked Miren quietly, nodding toward the colonnade, where the dissenters stood stunned amid the singing. "The sixty-three percent ends. The thirty-seven percent — if the Door closes, they close with it. That's not a vote. That's arithmetic wearing a vote's clothes."

Miren was quiet a long moment. Ink-stained fingers tight on her tally book.

"I know," she said finally. "I've been doing the arithmetic since the second hour of voting. There's no clean version, Wendell. There's only versions where the choosing is real and versions where it isn't. We got the real one." She met his eyes. "The thirty-seven percent deserve to be named. All of them. In the record. Refusal is a kind of testimony too."

Behind them, Sera had gone very still.

"Sera?" Wendell said.

She wasn't looking at the crowd. She was looking south, past the plaza's edge, toward the avenue that climbed toward the Pale Court — and Wendell followed her gaze and saw what she saw: lights. Not the copper glow of the frozen sun. Torchlight, wardlight, the cold white glare of the Court's engines, moving down the avenue in formation. A column. A long one.

"She knows," Sera said. Her voice had gone flat and fast, the smuggler's voice, the voice that meant run. "The vote — it needed the whole city gathered in one place, and the city is *hers*, it breathes for her, it must have told her the moment the first stone hit the jar." She grabbed Wendell's arm. "She's coming. She's coming with everything she has left, and she will not let a vote undo three hundred years of her devotion. Wendell — the key. Where's the key?"

Wendell's hand went to his chest. Through the cloth, against his skin, the iron was warm — warmer than it had ever been, warm as a living thing, as if the Door already knew what was coming and had begun, gently, hopefully, to open.

Across the plaza, the singing faltered, note by note, as the exiles too turned to see the lights coming down the avenue like a tide of small cold stars.
