## 25. Ilsette's Last March

The news came to the Pale Court the way all bad news came now: carried by a boy who could not die, breathing hard though his lungs had long since stopped needing breath.

"Your Majesty." He knelt on the white marble, and his voice shook in a way that had nothing to do with fear of her. "The exiles. At the Door. They're voting."

Ilsette Vane stood at the window of her audience chamber, where she had stood every morning for a hundred years, watching a sun that never finished rising smear its pale gold across the rooftops of Ostrava. She did not turn around.

"A vote," she said.

"On whether to end, Majesty. Whether to let the Door close. They say—" The boy swallowed. "They say it's close."

* * *

She dismissed him gently. That was the thing no one understood about her, the thing the smuggler and the keeper and the cartographer's angry daughter would never see: Ilsette Vane was gentle. She had been gentle through the erasure, gentle through the century of exile, gentle through every terrible act she had committed in the name of her people. Cruelty required appetite. She had none. What she had was arithmetic, and the arithmetic never changed.

One hundred thousand souls, unaging, unending, unable to leave. A kingdom that had been unwritten from the world so thoroughly that even its own dead had nowhere to go — they simply lingered, like guests after a feast that would not conclude. She had watched them fade by fractions. Not dying. *Thinning.* A woman in the Ash Orchards who had once sung so beautifully that birds landed on her shoulders now sang in a voice like wind through a keyhole, and every year the wind got thinner.

And now a handful of frightened exiles, gathered at a door with a boy-keeper's blood on their hands, proposed to end it. To finish what the world had started. To give Ostrava the mercy of annihilation and call it justice.

Ilsette turned from the window. Her reflection moved in the tall glass — a woman in bone-white armor gone soft at the joints with age that refused to complete itself, hair the color of ash, eyes the gray of a sky deciding whether to snow. She had been beautiful once. She had been a general once. Both facts seemed to belong to someone else, someone she had buried with the old kingdom and dug up again when the stars went out.

"Vessal," she called.

Her chamberlain appeared from the shadowed doorway, as he always did, as he had done for a hundred years. He had been her quartermaster at the fall. He had held the eastern wall with three hundred men against an army that came out of a fog that ate sound. He had been erased mid-order, his last word half-spoken, and sometimes — only when he thought she wasn't watching — his mouth still shaped the ghost of that unfinished syllable.

"Majesty."

"Ready the Court. Full muster. We march for the Door."

Vessal did not move. In a hundred years he had never hesitated, and the hesitation now was louder than any refusal.

"They are our people," he said carefully. "The ones at the Door. They are voting."

"They are afraid," Ilsette said. "Fear makes people sign away things they don't understand. I have seen soldiers surrender fortresses they could have held for a year because one night of cold convinced them the war was lost." She crossed the room, her boots silent on marble worn smooth by a century of pacing. "This is not a war we can afford to lose, Vessal. There is no second kingdom after this one. There is nothing after this one but whatever the keeper's blood decides."

"And if the people choose it?" His voice was very quiet. "If they choose peace?"

Ilsette stopped at the great map table that dominated the eastern wall — the map of Ostrava, drawn by the cartographer whose execution she had ordered, corrected by hand in places where the living city had rearranged itself overnight. She looked down at her country, small and stubborn and impossible, pinned like a moth to the valley it was slowly devouring.

"Then I will carry the weight of overruling them," she said, "and they will hate me, and they will be alive to do it. That is the whole of my reign, Vessal. It has always been the whole of it. I keep them alive long enough to hate me properly."

* * *

The Pale Court mustered in the Square of Bells, which had no bells anymore; the bells had been melted down in the last winter before the erasure, cast into arrowheads, and the square had kept the name the way amputees keep the ache.

They came in their thousands. That was the thing about a people who could not die: you could muster all of them. Every soldier who had ever served, every farmer who had taken up a spear in the last desperate spring, every child too young then and ancient now. They assembled in silence, because silence was the discipline of the erased — they had learned that noise drew the attention of whatever had unwritten them, and a hundred years of habit does not loosen.

Ilsette rode at their head on a horse that had died in the first year of exile and never noticed. It was gray, or had been. Its hooves made no sound. Behind her rode the officers of the old army, and behind them the Pale Court proper — her council, her generals, the men and women she had raised from the ranks of the hopeless and given purpose, which is the oldest and most dangerous gift a ruler can give.

She looked out over them and felt the familiar grief that she had learned to mistake for resolve.

They were thinning. All of them. She could see it now that she knew to look — the way light thins at the edge of evening, imperceptible moment to moment, undeniable across hours. The soldier in the third rank had been a boy of nineteen when the world forgot them; he was still a boy of nineteen, but the boy was fainter, as if someone were rubbing at the charcoal of him. Another hundred years and there would be nothing left to rub. No vote, no Door, no choice. Just a valley full of empty air and a wound that had finally healed over the corpse of a country.

That was the future the keeper offered them. Rest, they called it. Peace. *Ending,* said in the soft voices people used for funerals, as if dressing slaughter in linen made it something other than slaughter.

Ilsette had heard that voice before. She had heard it from the ambassadors of Kestrel's Rest, a hundred years ago, when the valley's mages had come to the border under a flag of parley and explained, with genuine sorrow, with real tears in some of their eyes, that Ostrava's magic was a sickness, that the kingdom's existence endangered the world, that the kindest thing — the *only* thing — was erasure. Gentle. Reasonable. Utterly certain of its own mercy.

She had believed, for one black hour, that they might be right. Then she had watched her country folded up like a letter, and she had stopped believing anything except this: no one gets to decide that a people are better off ended. Not mages. Not queens. Not votes.

Not even the people themselves, when the question is asked by a boy whose blood was bred for exactly this, in a kingdom built to cage his line, by exiles too tired to remember what they were fighting for.

"We march," she told them, and her voice carried without effort, because a hundred years of command had soaked into the stones of her throat. "Not against our own. Never against our own. We march to stand between our people and a mistake made kindly. Some of them will curse us. Let them. I would rather be cursed by the living than mourned by no one."

A sound went up from the ranks — not a cheer. Something older and stranger, a low sustained note, like the wards of a gate singing. Ten thousand voices holding one tone, and the tone was grief, and the grief was iron.

They marched.

* * *

Ostrava did not want them to go.

The capital had grown strange in the years since the return, streets sliding like snakes in the night, districts migrating toward the queen's will and away from everything else. But the city loved its people the way a body loves its blood, and ten thousand of its people walking out of it toward the Door felt, to the city, like bleeding.

The roads fought them. Bridges lengthened. Crossroads multiplied. In the district called the Throat, the street simply closed ahead of the vanguard, cobbles rising like hackles, walls leaning together overhead until the way forward was a seam of darkness.

Ilsette dismounted and laid her palm flat against the nearest wall.

"I know," she said softly. "I know. But they have to go, and I have to go with them, and you — " She pressed her forehead to the stone. The stone was warm. Everything in Ostrava was warm now, warm the way a fever is warm. "You will have to learn to let go. That is what we are marching to teach them, and I need you brave enough to let me fail."

The wall shuddered. Slowly, grudgingly, the street opened, and the Pale Court poured through the gap like water finding its level.

They walked through the night, such as the night was here — a permanent dusk beneath a sky with no stars, lit by windows that glowed the color of old honey. The exiles they passed along the road came out of their houses to watch. Some wept. Some spat. One old woman, thin as paper, pressed a loaf of bread into the hands of a soldier and said, "Bring them back, Ilsette. Bring them back alive, or don't bring them back at all," and Ilsette took the bread and bowed her head as if receiving a sword.

By dawn-that-was-not-dawn they had left the city behind and entered the Ash Orchards, where the trees grew silver and bare and bloomed flowers like small cold fires. Here the land remembered the Gate most keenly. Here Wendell Brightwater's blood had burned in the soil, and the hunters' reports spoke of a keeper who could feel the kingdom's edges fraying under his feet.

Fraying. That was the word. As if her country were cloth, as if a hundred thousand lives were threads, as if pulling one stitch — one boy, one bloodline, one willing sacrifice — would unravel the whole garment into nothing.

And the worst of it, the thing that kept her awake in the dusk-hours when even the unaging needed to close their eyes: the worst of it was that she understood him. She had read the reports. The keeper was not a conqueror. He was a dutiful man doing what his duty demanded, walking willingly into his own unmaking because he believed it was owed. She had commanded a thousand soldiers like him. She had loved two of them. Duty like that deserved honor, and honor was exactly why she could not let him succeed.

* * *

They sighted the Door on the second day.

It stood in a valley fold where the land of Ostrava thinned to gauze — the original Gate, the wound itself, rebuilt around itself by centuries of keeping until it was less a structure than a scar given architecture. Salt crusted its threshold in drifts. And gathered before it, in their hundreds, were the exiles: farmers and deserters and ghosts, the underground, the smugglers, the cartographer's daughter standing at the front with her jaw set like her father's compass needle.

And above them, faint as breath on glass, the figure in the threshold. The keeper. Half in, half out, his blood unraveling stitch by stitch, holding the Door open with his own dissolution while his people voted on whether to walk through it into rest.

Ilsette reined in her dead horse and looked for a long time.

"He's already doing it," Vessal said beside her. His voice was hollow. "Majesty. Look at him. He's already — "

"I see him."

"If we charge now, we kill our own people to save them. If we wait, the vote finishes and — " He stopped. His mouth shaped the old unfinished syllable, the order he'd never completed, and for a moment Ilsette saw him as he had been: a quartermaster counting sacks of grain, certain that numbers were the same thing as safety.

"No," she said. "We do not charge. Sound the parley. White banners." She swung down from the saddle, and her boots found the salt-crusted earth, and she began — alone, unhurried, in full view of both armies — to walk toward the Door.

"Majesty!" Vessal's voice cracked like ice. "They'll take you! They'll — "

"They won't." She did not turn around. "I am going to offer them everything I have. And if they refuse me, then at least they will refuse me to my face, and no one in this valley will ever be able to say that Ostrava ended without its queen standing in front of it, asking."

Behind her, the Pale Court raised the white banners, and ten thousand of the erased fell silent, and the dusk sky leaned close to listen.

Ahead, at the threshold, the half-real keeper turned his fading face toward her, and beside him the smuggler Sera Vane — her sister's daughter, her own blood, the traitor and the debt and the girl she had loved enough to damn — lifted a blade not in threat but in warning.

Ilsette Vane walked on through the salt, certain of her mercy, certain of her arithmetic, certain that love meant refusing to let go —

— and did not see, high above the Door, where the sky had been starless for a hundred years, the first returned star begin, very quietly, to burn.
