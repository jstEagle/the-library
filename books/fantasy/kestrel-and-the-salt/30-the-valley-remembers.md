## 30. The Valley Remembers

The first thing Miren learned about the valley of Kestrel's Rest was that it had a talent for forgetting.

She saw it in the weeks after the stars came back, in the way the farmers returned to their fields with the careful cheerfulness of people stepping over a grave. The valley had spent generations believing itself blessed — tucked safe behind its Salt Gate, spared every war, every famine, every grief that had ever visited the wider world. And now they knew what the blessing had cost. They knew there had been a kingdom on the other side of those stones, whole and breathing and full of people with names and songs and grievances, and that their own ancestors had helped fold it out of existence so that no one would ever have to look at it.

Some of them could not bear knowing. Miren watched them not know it, day after day, deliberately, the way you might close your eyes against a wound.

Her father had been a cartographer. He had drawn coastlines for a queen he despised and borders for a country he loved, and Ilsette Vane's Pale Court had strung him up in the market square of Ostrava for the crime of making maps that told the truth — maps that showed how far the kingdom's edges were fraying, how thin the queen's eternity really ran. Miren had carried half of his last map through ash orchards and drowned towers to the Door at the heart of everything. She had held the line at the Battle of the Threshold while Wendell Brightwater turned the key.

And now she lived in a valley where the tavern-keeper asked her, kindly, twice a week, whether she'd ever thought of going home.

"This is home," she said, the second time, setting down her cup harder than she meant to.

"Well, sure," the tavern-keeper said, wiping the counter with the serenity of a man who had never seen anyone die. "Only you've got that look. Like you're always reading something just past my shoulder."

He wasn't wrong. She was always reading something just past his shoulder. It was her father's map, mostly, unrolled in her mind: Ostrava in all its impossible geometry, the capital that ate roads, the drowned bell tower, the orchards where the trees grew white blossoms out of grey ash because even the trees there hadn't been allowed to finish dying. Every night she redrew it from memory, line by line, in a ledger she kept under her bed, because paper burned and memory could be argued with but ink — good ink, pressed hard, witnessed — ink stayed.

"You're doing it now," the tavern-keeper said, amused.

"Sorry," Miren said, and did not stop.

* * *

The tribunal came first, before the archive, before any of it. Sera Vane stood before the valley's makeshift court in the meetinghouse — the same room where, a generation ago, the elders of Kestrel's Rest had voted to keep the Gate sealed and ask no questions about what lay beyond it. Miren sat in the back row and listened to the whole ugly history come out into the light: the Gatekeepers' bloodline bred like livestock to hold the wound closed; the valley's founding families complicit; the erasure not an accident of old magic but a decision, minuted and signed, by men whose portraits still hung in the meetinghouse with gilt frames around their smug faces.

Sera pleaded guilty. All of it. The smuggling, the crossing, the debt to the exiles, and worst of all the thing she'd confessed on the road — that years ago she had sold Ilsette the knowledge of how the wards could be broken, for passage out, before she understood what the queen wanted done with it.

"I didn't know," Sera said, standing straight in the lamplight, her voice flat as a blade laid on a table. "But I didn't ask, either. Asking would have made it harder to take the money. So I want it recorded that I'm guilty of the asking I refused to do."

Miren had hated Sera Vane once, briefly and fiercely, on the road through the ash orchards. Then she had fought beside her at the Door, and hatred had become something more complicated, the way it does when someone bleeds for you. Now, watching her stand in front of the tribunal and refuse every softening excuse offered to her, Miren felt something else again — recognition, almost. This was what her father had died for. Not maps. This. A person standing in a lit room saying *this is what happened, write it down.*

The tribunal exiled her. From the valley only — she was free to go anywhere else in the world, which was the tribunal's idea of mercy, and everyone in the room knew it was also its cruelty, because the one place Sera Vane wanted to be was the one place they were taking from her.

Miren found her afterward, sitting on the meetinghouse steps in the cold, looking up at a sky that had all its stars back and seemed embarrassed about it.

"You could appeal," Miren said.

"To who? They're right." Sera rubbed her hands together. Her breath fogged. "That's the thing nobody tells you about justice. When it finally shows up, it doesn't care whose side you're on."

"They took the one thing—"

"They took the thing I used to buy my way out of consequences." Sera looked at her then, and her eyes were dry and very tired. "Your father got hanged for telling the truth, girl. I get exiled to anywhere-but-here. We're not owed the same arithmetic."

Miren sat down next to her on the cold stone steps. Neither of them spoke for a while. Down in the valley the lamps were coming on, one by one, in windows that had gone dark for a season and come back, and Miren thought: *they think the story is over.* That was the trouble with valleys. Everything ended neatly here. The Door closed, the stars returned, the dead went out like lanterns, and the living woke up in their fields and called it a miracle and started planting.

Nobody was writing it down. Nobody was keeping the names.

"Sera," she said. "Tell me everything. About Ostrava. Everyone you met, every name, every street. Before you go."

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to write it down. All of it. And I'd rather have it from someone who was there than from ghosts."

Sera studied her for a long moment. Then she laughed — short, surprised, not entirely kind. "You're going to make this valley look at what it did."

"I'm going to make it impossible for it to look away."

* * *

They gave her the Gatehouse.

It had stood empty since the wards fell — Wendell's house, or his grandmother's before him, all salt-scoured floors and shelves of journals in a spidery hand. The valley council had argued about what to do with it. Burn it, some said; it was the house of the lie. Make it a shrine, others said; the keeper had saved them all. In the end they gave it to a foreign girl with half a map tattooed on the inside of her eyelids, because she was the only one who volunteered, and councils are cowardly creatures that mistake a volunteer for a consensus.

Miren moved in with two trunks: her father's surviving papers, and blank ledgers bought with the last of the money Sera had left her. On the first night she built a fire in Wendell's hearth, hung his grandmother's journals back on their shelf with respect, and sat down at the kitchen table with a fresh page and a pen.

At the top she wrote: *A Record of Ostrava, Being an Account of a Kingdom Erased, Kept Against Forgetting.*

Then she stared at the page for a long time.

Where do you start a country? With its borders — but the borders had moved like living things, and half of them had been inside buildings. With its rulers — but its last ruler had dissolved into starlight holding up a doorframe so that her enemy wouldn't be crushed, and Miren had watched it happen and still did not have words big enough for it. With its people, then. Its people.

She wrote the ones she knew first. *Tomasin Grell, ferryman of the Ash Orchards, who sang to his boat because boats remember songs longer than people do.* *Old Wenna, who could not die and had stopped wanting to, and who chose to end at the Door with her hands folded like a woman going to sleep.* *The judge of the drowned bell tower, name unknown, who hid the key where only the honest drowning could find it.* She wrote Ilsette Vane, and stopped, and wrote three drafts of that entry before dawn — tyrant, martyr, general, aunt, believer — and finally settled on the truth, which was all of them at once, and let the entry stand as a tangle, because some lives are tangles and flattening them is another kind of killing.

By the time the fire burned low she had forty-one names. There had been tens of thousands.

She turned to a new page and began again.

* * *

Word got around, the way word does in small places. At first they came to argue. A delegation of elders arrived at the Gatehouse on a grey morning, four of them, led by Master Corren Hale, whose grandfather's signature was on the erasure decree and whose family had grown rich on the safety it purchased.

"We understand," Hale said, in the tone of a man opening negotiations, "that you are compiling some manner of history."

"A record," Miren said. "History is what happens when other people edit it."

"The valley has suffered enough upheaval. These accusations against our founders — unsubstantiated, mostly. Ancient grievances. If you publish such a document, you'll set neighbor against neighbor. There are families in this valley who will not thank you for digging up what was buried for very good reasons."

"It wasn't buried," Miren said. "It was erased. There's a difference, and the difference is a kingdom."

Hale's jaw tightened. "You weren't even born here, Miss Cartographer. You owe this valley nothing."

"No," Miren agreed pleasantly. "I owe it less than nothing. Which means I'm the only person here who can afford to tell the truth." She stood, opened the door, and let the cold in. "You can read the record when it's done. Or you can be in it. Those are the options, Master Hale, and I'd think carefully, because I write down refusals too."

He left. But he left slower than he'd come, and he glanced back at the shelves of ledgers on his way out, and Miren noted the glance, and later that week she wrote: *Corren Hale came to demand silence and left having measured the shelves. People always measure the shelves.*

After the arguers came the grievers. An old woman named Betha whose grandmother, it turned out, had been born on the Ostravan side of the mountains before the Gate went up, and who had spent eighty years thinking her strange lullabies were nonsense she'd dreamed. She sang them for Miren, weeping, and Miren transcribed the melody in a notation of her own devising, dots and dashes above the words, because the valley had no music-writing and neither, anymore, did anywhere.

A man brought a coin, blackened, stamped with a sun that never fully rose. A child brought a story her grandfather used to tell about a city where the streets rearranged themselves overnight, and Miren sat very still listening to a seven-year-old describe the capital of Ostrava more accurately than most survivors could, and wrote at the bottom of the page: *Memory leaks. The erasure was never total. Find every leak.*

Piece by piece, leak by leak, the record grew. Three ledgers became nine. Nine became a wall. Miren built the shelves herself, badly, and Sera — who came down from the high country once a month, exile or no, because exile apparently had a generous interpretation of borders — laughed at them until she helped rebuild them properly.

"You know what you're building, don't you?" Sera said one evening, sanding a shelf edge by firelight. "This isn't an archive. It's a mirror with the valley's name on it."

"Good," Miren said. "Mirrors are the only argument that works on people who won't listen."

* * *

In the spring, the tribunal reconvened.

Not for Sera — that was done, though Miren noticed the sentence had quietly softened in practice, the constables developing a sudden blindness to a certain smuggler's monthly visits. This time the summons went out to the founding families, to the descendants of the signatories, to Master Corren Hale himself, and the meetinghouse filled with people who had come expecting a formality and found instead a girl with ledgers.

Miren read aloud for two hours.

She read the decree of erasure, recovered from her father's hidden papers, with its signatures and its careful euphemisms — *the permanent securing of regional stability* — and named each signer and each signer's heirs. She read the testimony of the exiles, taken down before the Door closed, the accounts of people who had woken one morning to find their country unwritten. She read the lullabies and the coin and the child's story of the moving streets. She read Ilsette Vane's proclamation in full, the promise of eternity, and then she read the account of the vote at the Door — the first free ballot in Ostrava's history — where the exiles themselves had chosen to end, and how the queen had marched to save them from exactly that.

When she finished, the room was silent in the particular way rooms are silent when something has been set down that cannot be picked back up.

An old farmer stood. His hands shook. "My grandfather signed that decree," he said. "I used to tell his stories like they were about a hero. What am I supposed to do now?"

"That," Miren said, "is the question. And the fact that you're asking it out loud, in this room, is the first honest thing this valley has done since the Gate went up."

They talked until the lamps burned out. Some of it was ugly. A Hale cousin stormed out calling Miren a foreign agitator; two families demanded their names be struck from the record and were refused; Betha stood up and sang the lullaby in front of everyone, and half the room wept, and the other half sat with their arms crossed and their faces working, which Miren privately considered the greater victory.

Near midnight, Master Corren Hale rose one last time. He looked older than he had in autumn, the way men do when a foundation shifts under them.

"What do you want?" he asked Miren, and it was not hostile. It was genuinely lost. "Truly. Apologies? Reparations? What ends this?"

"Nothing ends it," Miren said. "That's what none of you understand yet. You think truth is a debt you pay off. It isn't. It's a country you live in. You don't finish with it. You just keep tending it, the way—" she stopped, hearing herself, and smiled despite everything, "—the way a keeper tends a gate. Every year. Forever. That's all justice is. Tending."

Hale nodded slowly. Then he said, in a voice that would matter more than he knew: "Put me in the record. Whatever I did and whatever my family did. Put it all in."

"It's already in," Miren said. "But I'll note that you asked."

* * *

She worked late that night, as she did most nights now, the fire down to embers, the Gatehouse quiet around her except for the wind worrying at the eaves. The Door itself stood at the end of the hall — closed, sealed, warm to the touch in a way that stone should not be. She passed it on her way to bed and rested her palm against it, as she did every night, a vigil of one second.

Warm. Listening. Not opening.

She paused.

There was a sound from the far side of the Door. Faint — so faint she might have made it out of hope, out of the hundred nights she'd stood here wishing. Not a knock. More like... a scratching. Small, patient, rhythmic. The sound of something being written.

Miren pressed her ear to the warm wood, and her heart began to hammer, because she knew that rhythm. She had invented that rhythm herself, dots and dashes, the notation for melodies that had nowhere else to live.

Someone on the other side of the Door was writing to her.
