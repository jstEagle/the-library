## 31. Sera's Verdict

The tribunal met in the old Gatehouse, because there was nowhere else large enough and no one wanted to say so out loud. Sera stood in the room where Wendell had once counted salt by candlelight, where the wards had sung their last note, and she thought: *of course it's here.* Everything in her life came back to thresholds.

They had built the bench from the door of the old meeting hall in Kestrel's Rest — the one that had stopped existing the night the valley did, and come back with everything else, warped slightly, as if wood could remember being unmade. Three people sat behind it: Aldous Crane, who had been the postman and was now, by unanimous default, something like a mayor; Mother Ivenna, who kept the shrine at the foot of the pass; and a thin, gray-eyed woman named Petra Solh, whom Sera had never met before this winter but who had lost a brother to the years the sky went dark. They were not judges. Everyone knew they were not judges. There had been no law in Kestrel's Rest for a generation, only habit, and habit had died with the stars and come back changed.

Miren sat at the side of the room with a ledger open on her knees, recording everything in her careful cartographer's hand. She would not look at Sera. That was worse than if she had.

Wendell was not there. He had asked to be, and Miren had told him gently that his presence would be either a mercy or a weight, and no one could promise which. So he was outside, somewhere, in the snow, being kept away by people who loved him, and Sera found that she was grateful. She did not want to be acquitted by his face.

"State your name for the record," Aldous said. His voice cracked on *record*. He had delivered mail to a valley that didn't exist for eleven years and had come back with all his letters undelivered, and he held himself now like a man who had learned that institutions were made of paper and paper burned.

"Sera Vane."

"Of Ostrava?"

She considered it. "Born there. Raised here. Neither, lately."

Petra Solh leaned forward. "You understand why you're here?"

"I told Ilsette Vane how to break the Salt Gate wards." The words came out level, which surprised her. She had rehearsed them shaking, in the dark, in the weeks since the Door closed. "Twenty-two years ago. I gave her the sequence of the salt-tides and the name of the gatekeeper's line. In exchange, she let me cross out of Ostrava. I was nineteen years old, and I wanted to live more than I wanted anything else, and I have never once stopped paying for it." She paused. "I'm not here to explain it. I'm here to answer for it."

Mother Ivenna's hands were folded, her face unreadable. "Answering and explaining are not the same thing," she said. "We've spent the whole winter learning that. Explain anyway."

So Sera explained. She told them about Ostrava as she had known it — the sun that never fully rose, the orchards of ash, the exiles who could not die and could not leave, the slow suffocation of a country erased from every map while its queen built engines to keep it breathing. She told them about the night she decided to run, and how Ilsette had found her at the wall of the drowned quarter, and how the offer had been made not as a bargain but as a kindness: *you're young, child. You deserve a sky.* She told them she had known the wards would weaken. She had not known the Gate was a wound, or that a boy with salt-burned hands was the stitch holding a kingdom shut, or that the weakening would one day become a tearing. She had told herself, for twenty-two years, that she had only opened a window.

"And then you came back through it," Petra said. "When it tore."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Sera looked at her hands. The scars from the undercity were still healing; the scar from the Pale Court's ward-engine, across her ribs, would never fully close. "Because I owed them," she said. "The exiles. Because I'd walked out of a burning house once and left everyone inside, and when the house followed me into the world, I understood that running isn't escape. It's just debt with a longer leash." She lifted her head. "And because of him. Because he pulled me out of the floodwater in the drowned tower without knowing what I'd done, and trusted me after, and I couldn't—"

Her voice failed there. She let it fail. She had decided, coming into this room, that she would not perform grief, and she would not suppress it either. Whatever came, came.

* * *

They questioned her for two hours.

Petra asked about the smugglers' routes, whether others had crossed before her, whether the valley's erasure had been gradual enough that someone might have escaped it. Sera answered honestly: yes, a handful, over decades, and no, the erasure was not gradual at the end. It was a tide going out all at once.

Mother Ivenna asked about the dead. Which dead, Sera asked, and the old woman said: *all of them*, and Sera understood she meant the ones the valley had lost during the dark years — the births that hadn't happened, the harvests that hadn't come, the eleven years folded up like a letter never sent. Were those Sera's doing? No, Sera said. But they were Ilsette's doing, and Ilsette had needed her key, and so in the way that debts propagate, some part of it ran back down the chain to a nineteen-year-old girl at a wall in the drowned quarter. Mother Ivenna nodded slowly, as if this matched something in her own theology, and wrote nothing down.

Aldous asked the question everyone had been waiting for.

"If you'd known," he said. "That night, at the wall. If someone had told you — the boy, the stitch, the whole of it. Would you have stayed in Ostrava?"

The room went very quiet. Outside, wind moved through the bare orchards, carrying the faint mineral smell of the old salt line, which even now, months later, no rain could quite wash out of the ground.

"No," Sera said.

Aldous blinked. Petra's mouth tightened. Miren's pen stopped moving.

"I'd like to tell you I would have," Sera continued, "because it's the answer that would make this easier for all of us. But I was nineteen and drowning and I would have taken the deal again, and then I would have spent another twenty-two years telling myself the same lie. What's different now isn't that I've become someone who'd refuse. It's that I became someone who came back to stand in front of you and say: this is what I did, and I won't call it anything smaller than betrayal, and whatever you decide, I'll carry it without asking you to make it light." She looked at each of them in turn. "You asked me to account for myself. This is the account. I don't get to edit it anymore."

Miren's pen started moving again. Her jaw was set. Later, Sera knew, there would be words between them — Miren had lost her father to Ilsette's proclamation, had watched the machinery of tyranny work up close, and she had every right to weigh Sera's guilt against her own losses and find the scale wanting. But Miren had also voted at the Door. She had stood among the exiles and raised her hand for ending, and she knew better than anyone in this valley what it cost to choose a hard thing freely. Sera held onto that. It was a thin rope, but she had crossed worse chasms on thinner.

* * *

The deliberation took less time than the questioning. The three of them withdrew to the Gatehouse kitchen — Sera heard the murmur of their voices through the wall, Aldous's steady, Petra's sharp, Mother Ivenna's low and unhurried — and Sera stood alone in the great room with the ledger of her own life spread open around her.

There was a window there, facing east, toward the place where the Door had stood. It was walled up now, sealed with stone and salt and Miren's careful inscriptions, but the wall was warm. Everyone remarked on it, visitors especially — pilgrims, almost, who came to press their palms against the stones and feel the faint heat, like a body sleeping under blankets. Sera pressed her hand there now and felt it, and felt also the thing beneath the heat: a listening. The Door no longer opened, Miren said. But it still listened.

"Wendell's out there," Miren said quietly, from the doorway. She had come in without Sera hearing, which was a skill the girl had learned in a war and would probably never lose. "He's been pacing a trench into the snow since dawn. He wants to see you after. He says—" Miren stopped, and her voice roughened. "He says it doesn't matter what they decide. He says he'll walk out with you either way."

"He can't do that."

"He knows he can't do that. He's going to try anyway."

Sera turned from the window. "You should hate me," she said. "Your father. The proclamation. All of it runs through me, somewhere upstream. You've had all winter to do the arithmetic, and you've done it, and you sit in that chair every day writing down my worst hour in your beautiful handwriting, and you should hate me."

Miren was silent a long moment. Snow ticked against the glass.

"My father drew maps," she said finally. "His whole life. And the first thing they teach you — the first thing, before ink, before instruments — is that a map is a promise. Every line you draw is a promise that the world will be findable. That someone standing where you stood can follow where you went." She looked up, gray eyes steady. "You broke a promise too. A different kind. And then you spent the rest of your life walking back along your own line to correct it. My father never got the chance to correct anything. They executed him before the ink dried." She picked up her ledger. "I don't hate you, Sera. I *checked*. Believe me, I checked. But I'm not going to pretend the scale balances, either. Some things don't balance. They just get carried."

She left. Sera stood with her palm against the warm wall until the tribunal called her back in.

* * *

Aldous read the verdict standing, which was not protocol, because there was no protocol, because they were inventing justice the way you invent a fire in wet wood — patiently, together, cupping the flame.

"The tribunal finds Sera Vane guilty of betrayal against the people of Kestrel's Rest and the people of Ostrava," he said. "Not by our law — we have none yet, and may we never need the kind that would make this easy. By the older law. The one where you look at what someone did and you don't look away."

No one spoke. Somewhere outside, Wendell's footsteps had stopped. Sera could feel him standing beyond the wall, the way she had learned to feel him across a battlefield, across a city that ate roads, across the width of a closing Door.

"The sentence is exile," Aldous continued. "From the valley of Kestrel's Rest. To begin in seven days, and to last—" he faltered, and Mother Ivenna finished it for him, her voice gentle and iron:

"To last until the exiled chooses otherwise and the valley consents. Exile as we mean it is not a wall. It's a distance. The distance honesty requires."

Sera nodded. She had expected this. She had, in the honest hours of the night, chosen it herself — had lain awake composing the argument for her own banishment, because she had seen what happened when queens decided their own guilt was negotiable, and she would rather cut off her own hand than stand in the valley Wendell might return to and be sheltered by a love that had outrun justice. If he came back — if the warm wall ever gave up what it held — he deserved to come back to a place that had proven it could hold its own people accountable. Starting with her.

"I accept," she said. "And I ask one thing. Not mercy. Logistics." A few faces in the small crowd frowned. "The archive. Miren's records of Ostrava — the maps, the testimony, the vote at the Door. Someone has to carry copies beyond the valley, to places that never knew Ostrava existed. Places that erased it without knowing they were holding the eraser. Let me be the one who carries them. Let my exile be useful. Let me spend it making sure no one gets to forget twice."

Petra Solh studied her for a long moment. Then, slowly, she inclined her head — not forgiveness, nothing so clean, but acknowledgment. The nod of one carrier of unbalanceable things to another.

"So be it," Aldous said. "Seven days."

* * *

She found Wendell at dusk, at the edge of the salt line, where the ground still remembered being a border.

He was sitting on the old boundary stone with snow in his hair, half-real the way he would always be half-real now — a little translucent at the edges in certain lights, a little too quiet, as though the Gate had taken a layer of him and left the rest to learn how to breathe on its own. When he saw her, he stood, and the motion had that new awkwardness he still hadn't trained out of himself, the body relearning the world chapter by chapter.

"They sentenced you," he said.

"They did."

"Exile." His jaw worked. "Sera, I can talk to them. I can—"

"You can't, and you shouldn't, and if you try I will never forgive you, and unlike some people in this story I intend to keep my forgiveness expensive." She said it lightly, but her eyes stung, and she let him see it, because hiding things from Wendell Brightwater had turned out, in the end, to be the one crime she refused to commit twice. "Listen to me. I pled guilty because I was guilty. I accepted exile because it's true. And it's true in the way maps are true — it tells you where you actually are. I am someone who broke a kingdom to save her own skin, and then helped end it, and both of those are real, and the valley needs to watch me carry the second one out the gate before it can believe the first one is over."

"And me?" His voice cracked on it, young and old at once. "What am I supposed to do? Wait at a warm wall for the rest of my life?"

"You're supposed to live," Sera said. "You spent your whole first life keeping a door. Your second one is supposed to be about learning what's on the other side of doors. That's the whole assignment. I'm not going to be the reason you fail it." She stepped closer, and put her hand flat against his chest, over the heartbeat that had come back to him imperfectly, stubbornly, like everything else. "But I'll tell you a secret, keeper. Doors open both ways. I've seen it. I'm betting my whole exile on it."

Above them, the sky was doing the thing it had done every clear night since the equinox: burning with stars, all of them lit, all of them accounted for — except one, high in the east, that flickered. Just barely. Just often enough that Miren had marked it on her charts with a question mark, and checked it nightly, and said nothing.

Sera watched it flicker, and smiled, and did not tell Wendell why.

Seven days, she thought. Seven days, and then the road, and the maps, and the long work of memory. And somewhere past all of it — a porch light, a warm wall, a hand's width of open door.

She turned back to him, and took his hand, and began, at last, to teach him the names of the stars.
