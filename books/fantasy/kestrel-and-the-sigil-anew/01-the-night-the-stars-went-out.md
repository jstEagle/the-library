## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The last day of harvest was also the first day of lying, and Zephyrine Ravensworth had gotten good at both.

They stood in the dooryard of their cottage at the valley's eastern edge, shaking apples down from the crooked tree their grandmother had planted, and all around them the ordinary machinery of an ordinary evening ticked along. Smoke rose straight from chimneys. Someone's goat complained about something. Down the lane, Maren Ostler's boys were arguing over who would carry the lantern to the festival grounds, their voices carrying in the cool air the way children's voices do when they believe the whole world is listening.

The apples came down thump, thump, thump into the basket. Zephyrine caught one that bounced and polished it on their sleeve out of habit, though they weren't hungry. They were listening — they were always listening — to the conversation happening overhead, in the hedge, in the eaves of the barn.

*Cold coming,* said the wren who lived in the woodpile. *Cold like teeth.*

*The kestrels are moving early,* answered a crow from the ridge, in the bored tone crows use for gossip. *Whole family of them, going south before the festival's even done. Rude, if you ask me.*

*No one asked you,* said the wren.

Zephyrine smiled despite themselves and dropped another apple into the basket. This was the secret architecture of their life: the valley above, with its lanes and fences and Founding Festival, and beneath it this other country, this chattering nation of small voices that no one else could hear. They had been born between the two countries, and for twenty-six years they had kept the border sealed.

Because in Harrow's Vale, there was no magic. That was the whole point of the valley. The founders had crossed the mountains three hundred years ago fleeing the Mage Wars — fleeing sigils and wardens and men who spoke with things that should not be spoken with — and they had built a place where such things simply did not happen. No magicians. No beasts that talked. The wards on the passes saw to it, humming night and day behind the sky like a held breath, and in exchange the people of the valley agreed to forget that anything else existed.

Most of them managed it beautifully.

Zephyrine had been four years old, standing in their mother's garden, when a sparrow landed on the fence post beside them and said, quite clearly, *You're the loud one, aren't you.* Their mother had been three feet away, deadheading marigolds, and had heard nothing but birdsong. Zephyrine had learned, in that single afternoon, the two great lessons of their life: that the world was full of voices, and that telling anyone about them was the fastest way to lose everything.

So they lied. Small lies, mostly. Lies of omission stacked like cordwood. When the miller asked why his horses calmed so quickly around Zephyrine, they shrugged. When old Tam Wicker swore his dog hated everyone but them, they laughed and said dogs liked anyone who smelled of bread. When the animals told them things — where a lost child had wandered, which field's soil was sick, that the Ostlers' well was going bad a full season before it did — Zephyrine passed the information along in ways that couldn't be traced. A dream, they'd say. A guess. A feeling in my knee.

The valley called them lucky. The valley called them odd. The valley did not call them what they were, because Zephyrine made sure the word was never available.

A beast-speaker. In the world beyond the passes, they'd heard — in the stories the elders pretended not to remember — that beast-speakers were burned. Or worse: harvested, their blood used to key the wards of the magician-lords, because nothing holds a boundary like the voice of the thing being kept out.

Zephyrine shook the last apple down and stood a moment with their hand against the tree's bark, feeling the slow green pulse of it, the tree's contentment at the end of another year. Around them the evening gathered itself for the festival. Tomorrow night the whole valley would crowd the commons with paper lanterns and cider and the long recitation of the Founding — the crossing of the mountains, the sealing of the passes, the promise that here, at least, the war would never find them.

Here, at least, thought Zephyrine. As if anywhere were only here.

*Festival tomorrow,* said the wren, dropping to the woodpile's top to eye the apple basket. *There will be crumbs.*

*There are always crumbs.*

*And yet I plan for them. That is wisdom.*

Zephyrine huffed a laugh and carried the basket inside. It was the last ordinary hour they would ever have, though of course they didn't know it. Ordinary hours almost never announce themselves. They just end.

* * *

By full dark the cottage smelled of baking apples and the tallow candles Zephyrine had been saving for the festival. They ate alone at the table by the window, as they always did, and through the glass the sky was doing something it had never done before.

At first they thought it was cloud. High, thin cloud can eat stars without seeming to; you look up and the sky is simply blank, the way a page is blank. But there was no wind tonight, and the moon — three days past full, fat and orange over the western ridge — hung in a sky that was otherwise empty. Not dimmed. Empty. Zephyrine counted, out of some nervous instinct, and got to forty before they stopped counting. On a clear autumn night there should have been thousands.

They went to the door and stepped out into the yard.

The silence was the worst part. Not the absence of stars — that was wrong enough — but the way every voice in the hedges and trees had gone quiet too. The wren wasn't complaining. The crickets had stopped. Somewhere up the lane a dog began to howl, then cut off mid-note as if embarrassed.

*Hello?* Zephyrine said, in the speech that wasn't speech, the one that lived somewhere under the breastbone. *What is it? What's happened?*

Nothing. The hedges were just hedges. The dark was just dark.

Then, far away to the north, toward the pass, there came a sound.

It was not loud. That was the terrible thing about it — it was not loud at all. It sounded like a wineglass breaking in another room, if the room were the size of the world. A high crystalline note, then a second, then a cascade of them, thousands upon thousands, ringing and shattering and ringing again, and Zephyrine felt it in their back teeth and behind their eyes, and they knew — with the absolute certainty of someone hearing their own name called — that it was the wards.

Three hundred years of humming, ending like dropped glass.

The note faded. The silence after it was deeper than any silence Zephyrine had ever stood inside. And then, faintly, from beyond the mountains, from the country the valley had spent three centuries pretending did not exist, came a feeling rather than a sound — a pressure, a warmth, an attention — like a door opening onto a lit room in a house everyone believed was empty.

Zephyrine's hands were shaking. They looked at them as if they belonged to someone else.

Down the lane, doors began to open.

* * *

They gathered in the Ostlers' yard because the Ostlers' yard was where people always gathered, the way water gathers in a low place. Within the hour there were thirty of them, forty, holding lamps and shawls and, in Tam Wicker's case, a pitchfork he seemed to have grabbed purely for comfort. The sky pressed down on everyone equally, starless and close, and nobody wanted to be the first to say what everyone was thinking.

Maren Ostler said it instead. "The stars are gone."

"They're clouds," said her husband, without conviction.

"It hasn't clouded in living memory on festival eve," said old Tam. "My father said the founders chose the date because the sky was always clear. Always." He spat, ceremonially, into the dirt. "And that sound. You all heard the sound."

"Ice," said the miller. "The river shifting."

"The river's a mile off and it isn't ice yet," said Maren.

Zephyrine stood at the edge of the lamplight, saying nothing, holding a basket of apples they had brought for no reason they could name. Around them the animals' silence had a texture now, and the texture was fear. Even the crow on the ridge had stopped its commentary. Once, briefly, Zephyrine reached out along the old channels — *what did you hear, what do you know* — and got back only a single impression from somewhere far off, thin as smoke: *the hum has stopped, the hum has stopped, the hum has—*

Then that too went quiet, as if whatever had spoken had thought better of it.

"The elders will know," someone said. "The elders will send to the pass."

"The elders are as frightened as we are," said Tam Wicker grimly. "Frightened people send for more frightened people. Mark me, by morning there'll be a council and by tomorrow night there'll be a vote, and the vote will be to seal the passes and double the watch, and none of it will matter, because—" He stopped himself, jaw working.

"Because what?"

Tam looked up at the blank sky, and his face in the lamplight was very old. "Because a ward doesn't break like that," he said. "A ward breaks when something hits it. That sound was something *eating*."

Nobody answered him. Nobody wanted to. The lamps guttered, and above them the starless dark sat on the valley like a lid.

Zephyrine slipped away before the crowd broke up, walking home fast with the apples untouched, and it was only halfway up the lane that they realized they were whispering *no, no, no* under their breath — not to the neighbors, not to the sky, but to the small frightened voices in the hedges, the ones they could no longer hear. *I'm still here. Whatever's coming, I'm still here.*

* * *

The cottage was cold. Zephyrine lit no candle. They stood in the middle of the dark kitchen, listening to the enormous new silence, trying to decide whether to pack.

That was the thought that finally scared them properly — not the stars, not the wards, but their own hands, already moving through the house in the dark, gathering things without instruction. The good knife. The wool socks. Their mother's ring, which they kept in a cup on the shelf and never wore. Some part of them, some animal part below the breastbone where the gift lived, had understood before the rest of them had, and that part was packing.

"Don't be foolish," they said aloud, and their voice sounded strange in the empty room. "The elders will sort it. There'll be an explanation. Clouds. Ice. Something."

But even as they said it, they were putting the ring in their pocket.

They had just sat down — just sat, the chair still creaking — when the window exploded.

Not shattered; *burst*, inward, in a spray of glass and feathers and a shriek that went through Zephyrine's skull like a nail. They flung up their arms and staggered back, and something struck the floorboards, tumbled, righted itself, and stood swaying in the middle of the kitchen with one wing bent at an angle wings do not bend.

A kestrel. Small, russet and slate, chest heaving, eyes like two drops of molten copper in the dark. Blood on the floorboards. Blood matting the feathers of the left wing, and fused into the wing itself — Zephyrine's stomach turned over — something that was not feather and not flesh. A sliver of stone, charred black at the edges, half-melted into the wound as though it had fallen burning out of the sky and been caught.

The kestrel looked at Zephyrine.

And Zephyrine, twenty-six years careful, twenty-six years silent, heard the words come pouring out of the bird in a torrent of pain and terror, in the speech under the breastbone, in the voice that no one else in the valley could hear:

*They're coming. Do you hear me? They ate the lights and they ate the hum and they're coming, they're coming over the mountain, and the stone — the stone told me to find you, the stone is screaming, it says the speaker, find the speaker, find the—*

The kestrel swayed. Its legs buckled. It caught itself, glaring up at Zephyrine with those copper eyes, furious at its own weakness.

*Find the speaker,* it said again, softer now, bleeding. *It says you're the speaker. Are you the speaker? Please. Please be the speaker.*

Outside, far to the north, something howled — not a wolf, not a dog, not anything with a name Zephyrine knew — and every remaining voice in the valley, every wren and cricket and crow, went silent at once.

Zephyrine knelt slowly, heart hammering, and put out their hands.

"Yes," they whispered, in the speech that wasn't speech, for the first time in their life meaning it as an answer rather than a hiding. "Yes. I'm the speaker. Tell me what's coming."

The kestrel collapsed into their palms, and the charred stone in her wing pulsed once, twice, like a small dying heart — and in the pulse Zephyrine felt, unmistakably, a hunger. Vast, patient, and already inside the borders.

Something had eaten the stars. And it had sent ahead an invitation, addressed to them.
