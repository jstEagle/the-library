## 3. Leaving the Valley

The last fires of the Founding Festival burned low in the square, and Zephyrine watched them from the window of a house that no longer felt like theirs.

Below, the neighbors were singing the old songs — the ones about the first families who had crossed the mountains, who had built the valley walls and raised the wards and promised each other, generation after generation, *no magic here, no hunger for it, only hands and harvest and the long quiet.* Their voices wavered. Nobody quite believed the words tonight. The stars were still gone. The sky above the valley was a smooth black lid, and everyone sang louder to drown out the fact of it.

Zephyrine packed by feel, in the dark, because a lit candle would show through the window and because they didn't trust their hands not to shake if they watched what they were doing.

A knife. Not for fighting — they'd never fought anything in their life — but for cutting, prying, the small brutal chores of staying alive. Dried apples wrapped in cloth. The good wool socks their mother had knitted, back when her hands still worked steadily, before the palsy. A flint. A waterskin. Sorrel's box, lined with fleece, though the kestrel refused to stay in it and sat instead on the bedpost with her injured wing held at an angle that made Zephyrine's chest hurt every time they looked at it.

*They will seal the passes,* Sorrel had said, in that voice like wind through dry grass that only Zephyrine could hear. *And then they will find me. And then they will find you.*

The elders had voted at sundown. Zephyrine had stood in the crowd outside the meetinghouse and listened to the argument bleed through the shutters: seal the passes, post watchers on the ward-line, say nothing to anyone about how the wards fell, and above all — *above all* — if any creature comes out of that broken country beyond, any bird or beast or anything that might carry taint, it is to be killed and burned and its ashes buried deep.

Someone had said, in a voice Zephyrine recognized as old Marta's, the one who sold honey at market: "And if it speaks? They say the tainted ones speak."

And someone else had laughed, a thin frightened laugh, and said, "Then it dies with more to answer for."

Zephyrine had walked home through the festival crowds with their jaw clenched so hard their teeth ached.

Now, in the dark, they finished packing and did the last thing, the hardest thing. They took down the little clay whistle from the mantel — shaped like a wren, glazed blue, made by their mother's brother who had died before Zephyrine was born — and they set it on the table where someone would find it. A message without words. In their family, leaving the whistle behind meant *I am gone and I chose this.*

Their mother was asleep in the next room. Zephyrine stood in her doorway a long moment, watching the rise and fall of her breathing, memorizing the shape of her in the dark — the gray braid coming loose over her shoulder, the way one hand lay open on the blanket as if she'd been holding something and let go. She had sung to the goats when she thought no one heard. She had never once asked Zephyrine about the way animals went quiet and attentive around them, the way birds landed near them and cocked their heads as if listening. She had known. Of course she had known. Mothers know.

She would wake tomorrow to an empty room and a clay wren on the table, and she would understand, and she would hate them for it, and she would keep them alive in her prayers anyway. That was the shape of love in the valley. Zephyrine carried it out the door like a stone under the ribs.

* * *

The ward-line ran along the northern ridge, a day's walk from the village, and Zephyrine meant to reach it before dawn while the festival's last fires still pulled every eye southward.

Sorrel rode inside their coat, against their chest, a warm weight with a heartbeat like a quick small drum. She had argued — oh, how she had argued, in her rustling, furious way — that Zephyrine should wait, should heal her wing first, should go in daylight like a sensible creature.

"You can't fly," Zephyrine murmured, breath fogging in the cold. "I can't fight. Between us we have half a bird and half a person. We'll make do."

*I do not like being carried,* Sorrel said, but she pressed closer anyway, which was her way of saying things she couldn't say aloud.

The path up the ridge was one Zephyrine had walked since childhood — gathering herbs, running from chores, lying on their back in summer grass watching real stars wheel overhead. They knew every stone of it. That was the cruelty of leaving: the world got most beautiful precisely at the moment you were walking away from it. Every familiar bend in the trail glowed like something about to be lost. The smell of woodsmoke from the valley. The particular hush of the pine forest at night, full of small sounds — a mouse moving under leaves, an owl asking its question, the creak of branches — all of it audible to Zephyrine in a way it would never be audible to anyone else, all of it a language they were fluent in and about to be exiled from.

Halfway up, a fox stepped onto the path ahead of them.

It sat down. It looked at Zephyrine with bright amber eyes, and Zephyrine felt the old pull, the opening behind the breastbone, the place where beast-speech lived — and stopped themselves. If they spoke to it, even silently, even a word, the fox would follow. The fox would follow them to the ward-line, past the ward-line, into a country where anything that traveled beside a speaker died for the company.

"No," Zephyrine whispered, and their voice cracked on it. "Go home. Go home and be wild and live a long time and forget me."

The fox tilted its head. Then it turned and flowed back into the dark, and Zephyrine stood in the path with tears freezing on their lashes and told themselves it was the cold.

* * *

They reached the ward-line an hour before dawn.

Everyone in the valley knew where it was, though few came here. It wasn't a wall. It was a line of standing stones, waist-high, moss-covered, unremarkable — except that nothing ever crossed it. Birds turned back at it. Deer drank from streams on the valley side and never wandered past. For two hundred years the stones had hummed with a power nobody named, and children were taught to skip stones in the stream below and never, ever to climb the ridge.

Now the stones were silent. Dead as river pebbles. Moss was already beginning to die on their north faces, turning brown and brittle, as if whatever had fed them had been sucked out through a straw.

Zephyrine put a hand on the nearest stone. Cold. No hum, no shiver, nothing. Just rock.

*It was eaten,* Sorrel said again, quietly, from within the coat. *I told you. Something drank it dry.*

"Something," Zephyrine said. "Or someone."

Beyond the stones, the land fell away into darkness, and there — Zephyrine hadn't noticed it until now, and the noticing made their skin crawl — there were no sounds. No owl. No mouse. No fox. The forest below the ridge was as silent as a painting of a forest. As if sound itself had been harvested along with everything else.

They stood at the line a long time. Behind them: the valley, the fires, their mother's sleeping breath, two hundred years of safety bought at a price they now suspected they knew. Ahead of them: silence, exile, a hanging offense they carried in their own blood.

"You could turn back," Zephyrine said to Sorrel. "I could carry you to the southern passes before the watchers seal them. Some warm loft. Someone kind."

*And in spring,* Sorrel said, *when my wing heals and I fly north, I will be killed at the border like a rat. And you will spend your life hearing the beasts ask after me and having no answer. No. We are past choosing easy things.*

Zephyrine laughed — a wet, broken sound — and wiped their face with their sleeve.

"When did you get wise?"

*I have always been wise. You were too busy being soft.*

Step. One step over the line. Nothing happened — no alarm, no lightning, just the crunch of frost under their boot on the far side of a dead stone. Zephyrine looked back once. The valley lay below in its bowl of darkness, the last festival fires guttering orange, small and stubborn, like embers of everything they'd ever loved.

"Goodbye," they said, not loudly. The word didn't echo. Even the valley seemed to hold its breath.

Then they turned downhill, into the silent country, and walked.

* * *

Dawn came gray and wrong.

Not the clean silver-gray of mountain mornings at home — a bruised, ash-colored light, as if the sun had to push through something filthy to reach the ground. Zephyrine walked through it and the wrongness accumulated detail by detail.

The trees were healthy enough. Oak and birch and alder, ordinary trees. But no birdsong. None. At home the dawn was deafening with it; here the branches hung empty and still, and when Zephyrine scanned them they saw why — there were nests, plenty of nests, old ones, but the birds sat far out on the thinnest tips of branches, as far from the ground, from the paths, from *people*, as a bird could get and still call itself living in that tree.

A rabbit broke cover ahead of them and bolted — not the lazy zigzag of a rabbit that feared foxes, but flat-out, blind panic, as if the devil itself were behind it. There was nothing behind it. There was only Zephyrine, walking slowly, hurting nowhere, wanting nothing from it. And still it ran as if the ground burned.

"They're afraid of us," Zephyrine said aloud, and their voice sounded obscene in all that silence. "All of us. Everything human."

*Not you,* Sorrel said. *But they cannot know that from a distance. Fear does not read hearts. It reads shapes.*

By midmorning Zephyrine found the road — or what had been a road. Two ruts gone to weeds, a milestone lying face-down in the grass as if it had been pushed over deliberately, or had fallen and no one cared to raise it. They followed it because there was nothing else to follow, and toward noon they passed the first farm.

It was abandoned. Not ruined — abandoned. Door standing open. A plow rusting in a field half-grown to weeds. Laundry still on a line, gray rags bleached by weather, stiff as bark. Whoever had lived here had left in a hurry or been made to leave, and nothing had come back — no swallows in the eaves, no mice in the walls. Even the rats had better sense than to live near people here.

Zephyrine stood in the dooryard a long time, and Sorrel crept out of their coat and perched on their shoulder, and together they looked at the empty windows.

"This is what the wards were hiding us from," Zephyrine said. "Not monsters. This. Whatever emptied the world."

*Whatever is drinking it,* Sorrel corrected. *Empty is what it leaves behind.*

They walked on. In the afternoon the silence got so complete that Zephyrine began talking to hear a voice — their own voice, telling Sorrel about the valley, about the festival songs, about the taste of honey bread, about their mother's laugh. Sorrel listened. Kestrels are patient listeners. Once, when Zephyrine's voice failed, she said:

*Tell me the one about the whistle.*

So Zephyrine told her about the clay wren, and the family code, and how their uncle had made seven of them before he died, one for each child, and how the whistle meant *gone and chosen.* And Sorrel was quiet a moment, and then she said, in her dry-grass voice:

*You left yours. So you are gone and chosen. Both halves are true. Hold both.*

"I'm trying."

*Try harder. Grief is a heavy thing to carry and you insist on carrying it in both arms. Put one arm around hope.*

Zephyrine snorted despite themselves. "Did you learn that from a crow?"

*Crows are fools,* Sorrel said with dignity, *but occasionally a fool says something true by accident.*

Toward evening they crested a low hill and saw, far off across a plain of tall grass going gold and gray, the lights of a town. Real lights. Lamplight, firelight, the orange glow of a place where people still lived — the first sign of human life since the ward-line. Smoke rose from chimneys. Somewhere distant, faintly, a dog barked, and the sound hit Zephyrine like a physical blow, because it was the first animal voice they had heard in a day and a half, and it was muffled, flattened, wrong — a bark with the music taken out of it.

"There," Zephyrine said. "People. Maybe food. Maybe news about what happened to the wards."

*Maybe wardens,* Sorrel said.

Zephyrine stopped.

*The ones who hunt speakers,* she went on, and her talons tightened almost imperceptibly on Zephyrine's shoulder. *I smelled them on the wind this morning, far south. Hounds. Many hounds. And under the hound-smell, something else — iron and old blood. They ride the roads between towns. If there are people, there are wardens.*

Zephyrine looked at the town lights a long moment. Warmth. Bread. A roof. And somewhere in those streets, men with hounds who could smell a speaker's blood across a field, and a rope ready, and a law that said what Zephyrine was deserved death — not for anything done, only for what they were.

"If I walk in there," Zephyrine said slowly, "I walk in as prey."

*Then don't walk in as yourself,* Sorrel said. *Walk in as a mute pilgrim. Pilgrims are everywhere these days, fleeing one ruin or another. Mutes are pitied, not questioned. And I—* she ruffled her feathers, wincing as the hurt wing shifted, *—I will be a falconer's bird, riding silent on your shoulder. Trained birds wear hoods. Get me a hood, and I will play the part. I have played dead for cats. I can play tame for wardens.*

"You've thought about this."

*I have had a day and a half of watching you grieve. Someone in this partnership must think.*

Zephyrine laughed again — the second time in two days, which was twice more than the world seemed to allow lately — and turned from the hilltop, angling away from the road, toward a fold in the land where they could approach the town unseen. The last light died. The bruised gray dark came down.

And it was in that dark, halfway down the hill, that Zephyrine heard it — faint, at the very edge of hearing, at the very edge of the gift's reach: a voice. An animal voice. The first in the whole silent country.

A horse. Far off, somewhere ahead near the town, screaming the same word over and over into the empty fields, in the cracked, fraying voice of something that has screamed for years and been answered by nothing:

*Alone. Alone. Alone.*
