## 4. The Warden's Hounds

The hounds smelled the taint before dawn, and Cassian Vey let them.

He stood at the edge of the ward-line's corpse and watched them work — six gray shapes moving through the frost with their noses down, their movements precise as clockwork. They were good hounds. The best the Order bred. Memory-bound, every one of them: each had been fed a single recollection at weaning, one memory, over and over, until nothing else lived in their skulls. His lead bitch, Vesper, carried the memory of a burning house. The others carried drowning, betrayal, a mother's voice saying *run*. It made them tireless. It made them obedient. A dog that knows only one thing will follow it anywhere.

Cassian knew what the Order said about that. *A warden must be like his hounds*, the instructors at the Citadel had told him, twenty years ago, when his jaw was still soft. *One purpose. One memory of why. Everything else is noise.*

He was forty-one years old now, and he had begun to suspect the noise was where he kept himself.

"Vesper." He said it quietly, and she broke off her cast and came to him, pressing her broad head against his thigh. Through the leather of his glove he could feel the tremor in her — not fear. Hunger. Somewhere on the wind was the thing they'd been sent to find, and it smelled to her like the only true thing in the world.

*Speaker-taint.* That was what the dispatch had called it. Cassian had read the word four times by lantern light before he'd let himself understand it. There hadn't been a confirmed speaker inside the Order's jurisdiction in eleven years. The last one had been an old woman in the fen country who'd talked crows into stealing for her, and they'd taken her to Ossuar in a wagon with no windows, and after that there had been nothing to file because there had been nothing left to file about.

Now this. On the very night the stars went out and the border wards fell — the same night, and Cassian did not believe in coincidence, because coincidence was noise.

He looked up at the sky while the hounds cast in widening arcs through the dead bracken. No stars. There had been no stars for three nights, and the men grumbled about omens, but Cassian had seen the Order's own surveyors come back from the ward-stones with instruments that read nothing, absolutely nothing, where the wards' anchor-lines should have run. The wards hadn't failed. Wards didn't fail. They were stone and covenant and they had held for four hundred years against everything weather and war could throw at them.

They had been *taken*.

And now something that spoke with beasts was walking away from the fallen valley, north by northwest, leaving a trail that made his hounds shake like dogs dreaming.

"Master Vey." Corporal Dunmore came up the slope at a trot, breath steaming, too young for this posting and proud of it. "Sergeant says the trail's fresh. Hours old, maybe less. Says we can have it closed by nightfall if we push."

"Tell the sergeant we push," Cassian said.

Dunmore hesitated. He was one of those young ones who thought doubt was a disease you caught from old men. "Sir — the dispatch said alive, if possible. But it also said the reward stands either way. For the blood."

"I read the dispatch, Corporal."

"Yes, sir." Dunmore went down the slope, and Cassian stood alone a moment longer with Vesper's warm weight against his leg, looking north.

Alive if possible. Either way for the blood. He'd written sentences like that himself, in his younger years, when the Order's grammar had fit his mouth without gaps. *Render the subject. Preserve the specimen.* As if a person were a moth in a case. He'd never thought about the grammar before. That was the trouble with grammar — you didn't hear it until someone else spoke it, and then you couldn't stop hearing it.

He clicked his tongue and the pack fell in around him, and they went north into the gray morning.

* * *

They found the first sign at a shrine.

It was one of the old roadside shrines the Order tolerated because tearing them all down would have cost more than leaving them — a squat stone niche with a worn figure inside that might have been a shepherd or might have been a saint, depending on your village. Travelers still left bread there. Superstition, the Order called it. Harmless.

Someone had left bread there last night. And the birds had not touched it.

That was the detail that stopped Cassian. He crouched beside the niche and looked at the loaf sitting whole in the frost, unpecked, and then at the sparrows working the hedgerow ten feet away, bold as brass, ignoring free food. Birds didn't ignore free food. Unless something had told them to leave it. Unless something had *asked*.

Beside the shrine, pressed into the mud, were footprints. One set, human, narrow, walking with the careful economy of someone used to being unheard. And around them, dozens of small prints — fox, crow, fieldmouse — converging and then fanning out again, like an audience dispersing after a performance.

Vesper put her nose to the mud and shivered all along her spine.

Cassian had seen speakers' trails before, in the archives, in the old reports from the Purification decades. The reports always described the same thing: animals gathering to the speaker like iron filings to a lodestone. Not driven. *Gathered.* Voluntary. The reports used the word *infestation*, but reading between the lines of dead men's dry prose, Cassian had always privately translated it to something else. Something like *company*.

He straightened up. Behind him, Dunmore was saying something to the sergeant about the prints, excited, and the sergeant was telling him to quit gawking at bird tracks.

"Corporal." Cassian kept his voice level. "What do you see here?"

Dunmore blinked. "Sign, sir. Fresh sign. Speaker passed within—"

"What *kind* of sign?"

Dunmore looked again. Cassian watched him work through it — the bread, the birds, the little crowd of prints. Watched him arrive at the edge of the understanding and step back from it, the way you step back from a high place.

"Animals *sought it out*, sir," Dunmore said slowly. "Like it called them."

"Or like it asked," Cassian said, "and they answered."

Dunmore's face did something complicated and settled on disgust, which was the safe answer, the Order answer. "Filthy business, sir."

"Mount up," Cassian said. "We're losing light."

But he took one more look at the bread in the niche before he turned away. Whoever had left it had been hungry themselves — the loaf was small, cheap barley bread, the kind pilgrims bought. And they'd given it to a stone saint instead of eating it, on a road where they were hunted, with a wounded bird on their shoulder, if the kestrel feathers in the mud meant anything.

Kestrel feathers. He'd noted them without noting them, and now they surfaced. A kestrel, flying in this cold, this far from the valley, with pinion feathers dropping — a hurt bird, carried or following. Speakers didn't use birds as tools; the old reports were clear on that too. The birds *chose* them, and stayed.

*Company,* Cassian thought again, and put the word away where he kept the other words he wasn't supposed to think.

* * *

They ran the trail hard through the afternoon, and the land got quieter as they went, which Cassian noticed and the younger men didn't.

It was quiet everywhere since the stars went out — the whole country had gone strange and muted, as if the world were holding its breath. But this was a different quiet. This was a quiet with edges. Twice they flushed game that should have flushed — a hare that sat frozen until they were nearly on it, a heron that stood in a ditch and let them pass at ten feet rather than take wing. Animals avoiding motion, avoiding sound, hiding from the column the way folk hid from a press-gang.

"They're scared of us," Dunmore said, pleased, as though fear were tribute.

"They're scared of *something*," Cassian said, and didn't say the rest: that in twenty years of riding these roads he'd never known wild things to hide from a warden's column. Wardens were law, and law was weather to animals — noticed, endured, ignored. This was different. This was animals acting like the column was *hunting them*, and there was only one reason a beast would think that.

Because it had heard, from somewhere, from some small feathered messenger, that the Order's hounds were coming, and what the Order did to things that spoke.

His hounds didn't scare the wildlife. His hounds were mute — the Order cut the voice from memory-bound litters at weaning, another efficiency, another piece of grammar Cassian had never examined until now. Mute hounds, bound hounds, hounds that knew one memory and nothing else. He'd always called it discipline. Riding through a countryside gone silent as a held breath, he found himself calling it something else, and the something else had teeth.

They lost the trail at dusk at a ford, and found it again an hour later, and lost it again, and Cassian began to understand that they weren't losing it. It was being *moved*. The trail bent where no walker would bend it, doubled where doubling served no purpose except delay. Something on the road ahead was thinking about them. Was watching the column and laying false scent like a card sharp palming coins.

Foxes could be asked to drag scent. So could stoats, so could crows carrying a rag. He'd read about it in the Purification reports and dismissed it as superstition dressed up for frightened magistrates. Now he rode behind six memory-bound hounds and watched the trail lie to him, and did not feel superstitious. He felt *outmatched*, and beneath that, absurdly, almost unwillingly —

Impressed.

* * *

They made camp in a hollow off the road, and Cassian walked the picket line in the dark, as he always did, checking the hounds.

This was the part of the day he'd never admitted, even to himself, that he loved. The hounds were different at night. Off the scent, the single memory went quiet in them, and what was underneath came up — dog-things, ordinary dog-things. Vesper liked to lean her whole weight against his knee. The youngest male, Brant, still had a habit of chasing his tail in circles when the camp went quiet, a puppy-foolishness the binding hadn't reached. He moved down the line scratching ears, and the hounds leaned and sighed, and for a few minutes they were just dogs, and he was just a man with dogs, and the Order was a building very far away.

At Vesper he stopped longer than the rest. She was restless tonight, whining low in her throat, her eyes fixed north into the dark.

"He'll keep till morning," Cassian told her. "Whoever it is."

She looked up at him, and he had the sudden mad certainty that she understood him — not the tone, not the command-shapes, but the *words* — and that what she understood was pity. Pity for the speaker ahead. Or pity for him. He couldn't have said which was worse.

"You don't know anything," he told her gently. "You know a burning house. That's all they ever gave you."

Vesper licked his glove, once, and went back to staring north.

Cassian stood a long time in the cold. Around him the camp slept, and beyond it the country lay silent under a starless sky, and somewhere out in that silence was a person with a hurt bird on their shoulder, walking north with every wild thing in the dark watching their back.

The dispatch sat in his mind the way a stone sits in a boot. *Speaker-taint. Alive if possible. Reward stands either way, for the blood.*

Blood. He'd harvested blood himself, early days, from subjects already wagoned to Ossuar — the Order used it to key the alarms, to tune the hounds, to bind the bindings. He'd never asked what else it was used for. He'd never asked what the new sigil ate, though everyone in the service knew the Regent's engines drank something, and the something had started flowing the same year the harvest-wagons multiplied.

He had spent twenty years not asking. That was the discipline. That was the whole architecture of him: duty laid over doubt laid over duty, mortared tight, load-bearing.

And yet.

And yet the bread at the shrine, untouched by birds that had been *asked*. The hare that hid. The trail that lied like a friend covering for a fugitive. The whole living country conspiring, quietly, voluntarily, to shelter one fleeing stranger — and the Order's answer to that was hounds with one memory apiece and a grammar of *specimens* and *blood*.

Cassian looked down at Vesper, leaning on his knee, knowing nothing but fire.

"One more day," he heard himself say. "We run it down tomorrow, and I do my duty, and that's the end of it."

Vesper's tail moved once, slow, like a metronome counting out a lie.

He went to his bedroll and did not sleep. In the black hours before dawn he heard, very far off, thin across the miles of silent country, a sound he had no right to hear and no category for: a kestrel crying. Not the hunting cry. Something longer, lower, repeated — almost like a name being called, or an answer to one.

The hounds heard it too. All six heads came up in the dark, all six throats working silently, and not one of them gave tongue toward it.

They were mute. They had always been mute. But lying awake in the starless dark, Cassian Vey would have sworn on his warrant and his mother's grave that what he heard in their silence was longing — and that for the first time in twenty years of service, the man who trained the Order's best hounds did not know whose side his dogs were on.

By first light he had his answer, and it was worse than he feared: the trail led north, clean and straight at last, no more lies, no more fox-dragged doubles. Straight toward the city of Ossuar, where the Regent's new sigil hung half-built above a hill of bought memories.

Straight toward the heart of everything the Order served.

Cassian saddled his horse in the gray dawn and told himself the flicker in his chest was only fatigue. He gave the order to ride.
