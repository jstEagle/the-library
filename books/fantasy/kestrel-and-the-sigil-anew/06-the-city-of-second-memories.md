## 6. The City of Second Memories

The horse — Bramble, who had once been voiceless and now would not stop talking — stopped talking the moment the road crested the last hill before Ossuar.

Zephyrine felt it too. A hush, like the one that falls over a room when someone important enters, except this hush went on for miles and had been going on for years.

"There it is," Bracken said. He'd appeared two days ago out of a drainage ditch with a stolen apple and an offer of partnership, and Zephyrine still hadn't decided whether trusting him was wisdom or the first symptom of madness. "The City of Second Memories. Pretty, isn't it?"

It was pretty. That was the horror of it.

Ossuar sat on its hill like a pearl in an open hand, white walls catching the afternoon sun, banners streaming rose-and-gold from every tower. The streets below it climbed in gentle terraces, and the whole city seemed to glow from within, as if the stone itself were lit by candles. From this distance it looked like the kind of place songs were written about.

From this distance you couldn't see what it stood on.

"Look at the hill," Zephyrine said quietly.

Bracken squinted. "What about it?"

"It's not a hill."

Bramble shifted his weight, uneasy. *The ground hums,* he said. *Like a struck bell that never finishes ringing.* Since Zephyrine had freed his voice outside Marrowfen, the cart-horse had become their early-warning system, their map, and their most relentless commentator on the quality of roadside grass. Now all three functions converged on dread.

Zephyrine slid down from Bramble's back and pressed a palm to the earth.

And felt it.

Memories. Thousands upon thousands of them, layered like sediment, like strata of dead things pressed into stone. Not the ordinary residue a place gathers — not the faint impress of footsteps or old fires — but whole memories, vivid and screaming-fresh, harvested out of living heads and poured into the hill until the hill itself had become a reservoir. Grief at the bottom, oldest and deepest. Above it, fear. Above that, shame, regret, the small daily agonies people pay to be rid of. And threaded through everything, thin as veins of ore, something else: power. Raw and humming, drawn up through the city's foundations the way roots draw water.

"They're not buying and selling trinkets down there," Zephyrine said, standing. Their voice came out flat. "They're trading pasts. The hill is made of them."

"Everyone knows that." Bracken shrugged, but his eyes flicked away, toward the city, and stayed there a moment too long. "It's the whole point. You sell what hurts. You buy what doesn't. The Memory-Houses keep fair ledgers — mostly. It's honest commerce." He said it the way people recite things they've been taught to say. "My sister sold her memory of the fire. Our house burned when we were small. She couldn't sleep after. So she sold it, and now she sleeps fine, and she doesn't remember why she flinches at candlelight."

"And you?"

"I kept mine." His jaw worked. "Somebody has to remember she had a brother who pulled her out."

*The city eats,* Bramble said simply. *Cities always eat. But this one chews.*

They came down to the gates as the light went long and gold. Zephyrine had prepared for this since Marrowfen: they could not speak here, not aloud, not in any way a warden might overhear. A mute pilgrim, traveling to the shrines of the Undying Accord — that was plausible; pilgrims came to Ossuar from everywhere, drawn by the promise that grief could be excised like a tumor. Zephyrine wore a pilgrim's gray hood, ash-marked on the brow, and carried a slate on a cord around their neck. Sorrel rode inside their coat, hidden, her talons gripping a padded sling Bracken had sewn from an old glove. A kestrel in the capital was unremarkable. A kestrel that spoke was death.

At the gate, a bored clerk in Order livery looked them over.

"Name?"

Zephyrine tapped the slate: *Pilgrim. Vow of silence. Here for the Houses.*

"Pilgrims don't usually travel with a horse and a cutpurse," the clerk said, nodding at Bracken.

"He's my guide. I can't ask directions."

That got a snort. The clerk stamped a lead token and waved them through. *Too easy,* Sorrel murmured against Zephyrine's chest, her voice a warm thread only they could hear. *Wardens don't guard gates. They guard blood.*

She'd said something similar about the border, and she'd been right then too.

*

Inside, Ossuar was worse than pretty. It was kind.

That was the thing Zephyrine hadn't been ready for. They had imagined a grim city, a tyrant's city, all black towers and marching boots. Instead the streets were clean and the people were smiling. Flower-sellers called soft greetings. Children ran laughing through fountains. In front of the first Memory-House they passed — a graceful building with silver doors and the sigil of the Regent's new order above the lintel, a circle broken and re-forged into a blade-shape — a crier read from a placard in a warm, honeyed voice:

*"Grieve no more! The House of Gentle Hours offers full extraction of painful memory — safe, painless, blessed by the Regent herself. Why carry what you can set down? First extraction free for new citizens!"*

A queue stretched from the silver doors. In it, a woman held a child's worn shoe and wept without sound, waiting her turn to forget. Behind her, a man argued cheerfully with his neighbor about which House gave better rates for childhood. A boy of maybe ten stood in line alone, counting coins, and Zephyrine caught the shape of what he was saving for: not an extraction. A purchase. He wanted to buy a memory of a father. Any father. The Houses sold those too, secondhand, pre-loved, guaranteed pleasant.

Zephyrine's stomach turned over.

*This is what the wards were fed with,* they thought. *Not just the valley's borders. All of it.* The border wards around the valley had drunk stolen memory for generations — Zephyrine had learned that much from Sorrel, from the charred fragment fused into her wing. But here it wasn't hidden. Here it was commerce. Festival. Charity. The Regent hadn't stolen the magic of memory; she'd built a marketplace where people lined up to sell it themselves, gratefully, and called the emptiness left behind peace.

"First time seeing it?" Bracken asked, watching their face.

"How do you live here?"

"You get good at not looking." He said it lightly, but his hand drifted to his pocket, where Zephyrine knew he kept a scrap of charred sigil-stone of his own — the fragment that had started all this, the one Sorrel had carried, now wrapped in oilcloth. "Or you get out. Or—" He nodded across the square, toward a narrow alley between two Houses. "Or you do what I do."

*What does he do?* Sorrel asked.

*Steals,* Zephyrine told her. *Apparently well.*

*

The inn Bracken chose was called the Drowned Bell, which Zephyrine found less charming than he clearly did. It sat low in a terrace street, its cellar dug deep into the memory-hill itself, and Zephyrine could feel the strata pressing up through the floorboards all night — whispers, half-songs, a child counting to ten over and over, someone repeating *I didn't mean to, I didn't mean to* like a prayer worn smooth. Sleep came in shallow dips between the voices.

In the morning, Bracken laid out the city on the inn table in charcoal and stolen paper.

"The Regent's palace sits at the crown of the hill. Underneath it — and I mean directly underneath, you could spit from the throne room into it if the floors weren't forty feet of granite — is the Reliquary. That's where the old sigil-stone lives. What's left of it." He tapped the map. "Now, everyone thinks the Regent keeps it locked up because it's dangerous. Dangerous, yes. But that's not why she keeps it."

Zephyrine wrote on the slate: *Why then?*

"Because it's hungry." Bracken leaned in, dropping his voice even though the room held nothing but them, a horse stabled below, and a kestrel pretending to be a decorative cushion. "I've run messages through the palace service. Seen things. The Reliquary isn't a vault, it's a furnace. Every memory extracted in every House in this city gets tithed upward — a tenth part, they call it, the Regent's Tithe — piped through conduits under these streets straight down to the sigil. Feeding it. Growing it back. The old war broke the sigil into fragments, right? Everyone knows that story. What nobody asks is how the Regent got hold of the heart of it. And what nobody dares ask is what she's building."

*She's rebuilding it,* Zephyrine wrote, and their hand shook so badly the letters came out crooked. *Fragment by fragment. Memory by memory. A whole sigil, fed on a whole city's pasts.*

"That's the rumor among people who think too much," Bracken said. "Which is why people who think too much tend to disappear. Quietly. No trials — trials leave witnesses. Just neighbors who wake up one day unable to remember why they liked the disappeared person, because the Order harvests those too. Tidy."

Sorrel stirred under Zephyrine's coat and pushed her head out into the lamplight. To anyone watching, a pet falcon grooming itself. Her eyes, though, fixed on Bracken with a flat golden attention that made him shift in his chair.

*Ask him,* she said, *about the tithe-day.*

"What's the tithe-day?" Zephyrine wrote.

Bracken glanced at the shuttered window. "Tomorrow," he said. "Once a month. The conduits run hot. You can hear the hill singing if you stand in the right drain. And the Regent comes down — actual flesh-and-blood Malvane, in the flesh, which almost nobody ever sees — to the Reliquary, to press her hands to the sigil-heart and take the month's feeding. Whole ceremony. Heavily guarded." He grinned, and it was a thief's grin, sharp and joyless. "Heavily guarded means heavily scheduled. Everything worth stealing happens on a schedule."

Zephyrine looked at the map. At the palace crowning the hill. At the silver doors of the Houses, and the woman with the shoe, and the boy buying a father.

They thought of the valley behind them, sealed now, its elders voting for walls while the world outside burned its own past for fuel. They thought of the fragment fused into Sorrel's wing, and how she had flown through fire to bring it, and what she had told them on the road: *The wards didn't fail. They were eaten. Something is gathering all the pieces, and when it has them all, it will not be a wall. It will be a mouth.*

*We have to see it,* Zephyrine wrote. *The Reliquary. Before anything else. If we're going to fight this, we need to know what we're fighting.*

Bracken stared at them. "You want to case the Regent's own reliquary. On tithe-day. When the whole Order will be standing around it like bodyguards at a coronation."

Zephyrine tapped the slate again. *You said everything worth stealing happens on a schedule.*

For a long moment Bracken said nothing. Then he laughed — a short, disbelieving bark — and dragged a hand down his face.

"A mute pilgrim and a talking bird," he muttered. "I really know how to pick them." He pulled the map closer and began, already, to sketch the palace's service corridors from memory. "All right. But if we're doing this, we do it properly. Which means tomorrow, before the tithe, we walk the procession route like tourists. And it means—" he pointed his charcoal at Zephyrine's chest, at the hidden kestrel, "—your bird needs to learn to play decoy."

*I heard that,* Sorrel said, with dignity. *I have excellent instincts for theater.*

*

They walked the route that afternoon, and it was there, standing in the shadow of the palace's outer wall while the Honor Guard drilled in the courtyard beyond, that Zephyrine felt it for the first time: the pull.

It came up through their boot-soles, through the memory-hill itself, faint but unmistakable — a current drawing at them the way a river draws at a wading traveler. Not their memories. Something older, hungrier, coiled beneath the city like a heart beginning to beat faster. And threaded through the pull, so faint they might have imagined it, a sound. A whispering. Hundreds of voices layered into one long note, and beneath the note, almost lost, a rhythm:

*Feed. Feed. Feed.*

Sorrel's talons tightened on their shoulder. *You feel it too,* she said. Not a question.

*Yes.*

*Then hear me, Zephyrine.* Her voice was very quiet, and very steady, the way it had been the night the stars went out. *Whatever is down there — it felt you the moment you stepped onto this hill. Things that eat memory know the taste of a mind that holds onto its own. We go in, we look, and we leave. Nothing more. Promise me.*

Zephyrine looked up at the palace, white and beautiful against the darkening sky, glowing softly from within like a lamp. Like a lure.

Below their feet, the hill sang its one long note, patient as hunger.

They raised the slate, and wrote:

*Promise. But Bracken's right about one thing. Everything worth stealing happens on a schedule — and tomorrow, the schedule brings us face to face with the thing that ate the stars.*

Far above, on the palace's highest balcony, a figure in rose-and-gold stood watching the city, and though the distance was impossible, though no human eye could have seen two gray-cloaked pilgrims in a shadowed street — the figure turned its head, precisely, toward them.

And smiled.
