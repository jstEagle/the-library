## 7. A Thief Named Bracken

Bracken had three rules, and he'd carved them into the underside of his own skull where no warden could read them: never steal from anyone poorer than you, never sleep in the same den twice in a week, and never — *never* — get curious about anything that wasn't yours to steal.

He was breaking rule three when he saw the mute pilgrim talking to rats.

Not talking-to-rats the way beggars talked to rats, which was to say muttering at them while they ate your crusts and judged you for it. Talking *with* them. The pilgrim sat on an overturned crate in the drowned lane behind the Tallowmarket, hood up, hands loose on their knees, and a ring of brown rats sat in a half-circle in front of them like a congregation waiting on a sermon. Their whiskers twitched. The pilgrim's head tilted. The rats went still.

Then all nine of them turned their heads at once toward the drain mouth at the end of the lane, and scattered, and the pilgrim stood up fast enough to knock the crate over.

Bracken stepped out of the shadow of the tanner's awning with his hands visible and his knife sheathed, because that was how you survived in Ossuar: look harmless until the moment you decided not to be.

"Don't run," he said. "The wardens run people down on the straightaways. Corners are mine."

The pilgrim went very still. Under the hood's edge Bracken caught a face too young for the gray road-dust on it, and eyes that flicked — quick, involuntary — to the drain, to the rooftops, to him. Weighing exits. He knew the arithmetic. He'd done it himself a thousand times.

"I'm not going to shout," Bracken said. "Shouting costs me more than it costs you. If I report a speaker-taint, I get a warden's coin and a warden's attention, and warden's attention is the one thing in this city that doesn't wash off." He crouched, slow, and picked up the fallen crate, righted it. An offering. "So here's my question instead. What did they tell you just now? Before they ran?"

The pilgrim said nothing. Of course. The mute-pilgrim act — gray robe, ash-mark on the brow, the little wooden silence-token on a cord. It was good work. Bracken had seen maybe four disguises in his life that good, and three of them had been his.

But the rats had answered something. And nobody faked that.

"They smelled the hounds," Bracken said, answering his own question, watching the pilgrim's face. "Didn't they. Down from the Gateward station, two handlers, memory-bound pair, working the Tallowmarket drains because somebody tipped them there was taint in the quarter." He let that sit. "That was you. You're the taint."

The pilgrim's hand drifted toward the knife at their belt — not threatening, just honest. Bracken liked honest. Honest was rare as silver in this city.

"Put it away or don't," he said. "I'm not here to take anything off you except your time, and I'll pay for that."

* * *

His name wasn't really Bracken. His name was a thing his mother had shouted down a stairwell exactly twice before the fever took her, and he'd spent it long ago, traded away with everything else soft. Bracken was what the undercity called him because he grew in cracks and was hard to burn out, and he'd decided that was close enough to a soul.

He led the pilgrim through the back ways because the front ways belonged to the Order now. Ossuar had changed in the two years since the Regent's coronation — not the way cities usually changed, brick by brick and coin by coin, but the way a body changes when something starts feeding on it. The streets were cleaner. That was the first wrong thing. Beggars gone from the cathedral steps, drunks gone from the fountains, and in their place these pale, contented people who smiled at nothing and couldn't tell you why they were happy. The Second Memory houses glowed on every corner with their honey-colored lamps: *Sell us your grief. Sell us your shame. Walk out light.*

Everybody walked out light. Nobody walked out whole.

Bracken took the pilgrim down through the Weep — the flooded cellar district where the city's oldest bones lay under standing water — along a ledge he'd mapped by falling off it twice, and into the den he'd be abandoning tomorrow anyway, per rule two. It was a dry corner behind a collapsed wine merchant's vault, hung with stolen lantern-glass that threw green light, and it held everything Bracken owned: a bedroll, a coil of rope, six lockpicks of his own manufacture, and a wall of paper.

The wall was the thing people always underestimated. Dozens of scraps, receipts, playbills, torn ledger pages, each one marked in his cramped cipher. Two years of listening. Two years of noticing that the Regent's new order ran on a fuel nobody wanted to name out loud, and that the fuel had a shape, and that the shape matched the broken star on the old war-memorials — the sigil, shattered, its fragments scattered like teeth.

"You're after them," Bracken said, closing the curtain behind them both. "The fragments. Don't bother lying, your face does it for you. The rats, the road dust, the way you flinched when I said *hounds*. There's a speaker moving through my city asking questions in gutters, and yesterday a fishmonger told me a kestrel landed on her stall and stared at her like it was taking dictation." He spread his arms at the paper wall. "I've been following the same story from the other end. So. Trade."

The pilgrim lowered the hood.

Underneath: someone younger than Bracken expected, road-worn, with a tiredness around the eyes that wasn't from walking. A kestrel sat on their shoulder — he hadn't even heard it come in, which was its own kind of impossible — and it fixed him with one yellow eye like it was reading his ledger over his shoulder.

"Zephyrine," they said. First words since the lane. Voice rough from disuse, or from deciding not to use it. "And I'm not trading information I haven't verified. Who are you, and why does a thief have a wall full of the Regent's secrets?"

"Because the Regent's secrets are the only valuables left that nobody's guarding properly." Bracken dropped onto his bedroll, deliberately casual, deliberately lower than them — small moves, unthreatening moves, the grammar of not getting knifed. "You want the short version? The wards around your valley didn't fail. They were eaten. Somebody's been harvesting memories for years — whole districts' worth — and grinding them down to feed something. The old sigil. The one that ended the last war by binding every magician's power into a covenant. Your elders sealed the passes rather than look at it. The Regent built a religion on top of it and calls the hunger *comfort*."

Something moved behind Zephyrine's eyes — recognition, and pain, and the specific fury of someone confirming a thing they'd already feared. The kestrel shifted on their shoulder and made a sound low in her throat.

"And you know where a piece is," Zephyrine said.

"I know where the second-biggest piece is." Bracken held up one finger. "One condition on the telling. Not payment — I'm not selling this, sell it cheap and it dies cheap. My condition is that when you do whatever mad thing you're planning to do with it, you do it loud. Publicly. I've watched this city get fat and hollow for two years and everyone keeps saying *someone should* and then buying themselves a nicer childhood so they can forget they said it. I want someone who'll say it where it can't be unsaid."

"That's not a condition," Zephyrine said slowly. "That's a prayer."

"I'm a thief. We pray opportunistically."

* * *

He drew it for them on the back of a playbill: the Spiremount, the Regent's palace, and beneath it — because everything in Ossuar was stacked on top of something older and worse — the reliquary.

"Not the public reliquary," Bracken said, tapping the ink. "That's theater. Glass cases, holy music, tourists weeping on schedule. The real one's below, behind the memory-engines, and it's guarded worse than the treasury because it matters more. Three locks. The first is a puzzle-lock keyed to a hymn — you have to hum it, exact pitch, and the pitch changes weekly; I have a man inside who copies the choir master's notes. The second is a blood-ward." He glanced up. "Keyed against speaker-taint specifically. Which means you can't touch it, and I can, because I'm uselessly ordinary. The third—" he hesitated, and hated hesitating, and did it anyway because lying to this one felt like lying to the rats, "—the third is a memory-toll. The door takes a memory as the fee. Any memory. But it has to be one you'd fight to keep."

Silence. The green lantern-light moved on the water beyond the curtain.

"How do you know all this?" Zephyrine asked.

"Because I stole it," Bracken said simply. "Piece by piece, over two years, off clerks and choirboys and one very drunk engine-tender. Rule of the undercity: knowledge is the only theft where the victim doesn't miss it." He rolled the playbill and held it out. "Here's what I know about you, Zephyrine-of-nowhere: you crossed a dead ward-line alone with a bird on your shoulder, you walked into the city that eats pasts, and you spent your first night in it talking to rats instead of running. Either you're the bravest person I've met or the stupidest, and in my experience those are the same person wearing different boots."

The kestrel made a sound — a short, sharp note — and Zephyrine's mouth twitched like they'd been scolded.

"She says you talk too much," they translated.

"She sounds wise. What's her name?"

"Sorrel."

"Sorrel." He nodded at her, formal as meeting a magistrate. She stared back, unimpressed, and he understood suddenly that he was negotiating with two of them, and that the smaller one had the casting vote.

"So here's the offer, all of it, no fine print," Bracken said. "I get you into the reliquary. My inside man gets us the hymn. I handle the blood-ward and the doors and the boring parts. You handle whatever it is speakers handle. In exchange, I want the truth out — the harvest, the eaten wards, all of it, shouted from somewhere tall. And I want—" he stopped. Restarted. This was the part he'd been circling all night, the part that broke rule three so thoroughly the rule probably needed a funeral. "I want in. After. Whatever comes after. I'm done stealing purses off a dying city. If the world's going to change, I'd rather change it than pick its pockets while it burns."

Zephyrine looked at him for a long moment. The kestrel looked too, head cocked, one eye then the other, the way birds weigh a thing twice.

"You understand they'll kill us," Zephyrine said. "If we're caught. You especially — you're not even useful to them alive."

"Oh, I'm extremely useful alive," Bracken said. "I'm just not useful to *them*." He grinned, and it was his real grin, the crooked one, the one that had gotten him fed by strangers and arrested twice. "Well? Do we have a partnership, speaker? Shake on it or throw me out, but decide fast, because my inside man says the choir changes the hymn in four days, and after that we're locked out till the next moon."

Zephyrine took his hand. Their grip was road-calloused and steady, and Sorrel leaned forward along their arm to fix Bracken with that yellow eye at a distance of six inches, close enough that he could see the charred sliver of stone fused into her wing feathers — sigil-stone, unmistakable now that he knew what he was looking at, humming faintly against his palm-sense like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

"Partnership," Zephyrine said.

"Partnership," Bracken agreed. Then, because honesty was a debt he apparently paid now: "One more thing you should know before we plan anything. The night I found you — the tip that brought the hounds to the Tallowmarket? It came from inside the palace. Someone in the Regent's own house knows a speaker's in my city, and they're not trying to catch you." He tapped the rolled playbill against his knee. "They're trying to herd you. Straight to the reliquary. Question is whether that's a trap for you — or whether somebody in there wants the Regent robbed, and picked us to do it."

Sorrel screamed once, high and thin, a sound like tearing silk.

"Yeah," Bracken said. "My feeling exactly."
