## 8. The Reliquary Job

**Zephyrine**

The reliquary did not look like a prison. That was the first thing Zephyrine noticed, crouched in the shadow of a buttress across the processional square, and it was the thing that frightened them most.

It looked like a library. A cathedral of a library, all pale stone and tall arched windows, and through those windows spilled light that was not candlelight and not sunlight. It was *colored*. Warm gold in the east wing, cool blue along the north colonnade, a deep bruised violet pooling in the tower rooms. Memory-light, Bracken had called it. The harvested pasts of ten thousand citizens, bottled and shelved and burned for illumination because the Regent's Ossuar had never learned to be satisfied with anything that hadn't been taken from someone.

"You're staring," Bracken murmured beside them. He had a way of speaking without moving his lips, a pickpocket's trick, words that seemed to come from the air itself. "Staring gets people noticed."

"I'm counting windows," Zephyrine said.

"Counting windows is worse."

Sorrel shifted on Zephyrine's shoulder, talons flexing once against the padded leather they'd sewn into their coat — a falconer's brace, disguised as a pilgrim's shoulder-roll. Through the bond, faint as a struck bell heard through a wall, came her mood: *ready, ready, ready.* Kestrels did not understand waiting. They understood the space between stillness and strike, and they lived there.

*Soon,* Zephyrine told her.

*You said soon an hour ago.*

"I said soon an hour ago too," Zephyrine muttered aloud, and Bracken gave them a look.

"Do that again and I'll sell you to the rat-catchers," he said. "Now. The pilgrim door takes offerings until midnight bell. After that, the night-wardens walk the memory corridors on a quarter-bell rotation, and the reliquary proper sits behind a blood-lock keyed to Order blood — which I can't open, but you can, because your blood tastes like heresy to anything the Order built." He grinned, quick and crooked. "First time your curse has been useful. Savor it."

"It's not a curse."

"No," Bracken agreed, softer than Zephyrine expected. "It isn't." Then the softness was gone, folded away like a stolen watch up a sleeve. "West stair to the second gallery. The fragment's in the Vault of Foundings — third alcove, behind the glass case with the war banners. I've mapped it from the outside. Inside, you're on your own, because inside is where my talents stop working and my legs start running."

"And if something goes wrong?"

"Something always goes wrong." He tapped his own chest, twice, over the heart. "Wrong means run. Meet me at the tannery drain. Don't be clever. Clever people die in nice buildings."

He melted into the dark between buttresses, gone the way water goes into sand, and Zephyrine was alone with a kestrel and a heartbeat that would not slow down.

They crossed the square as midnight began to gather itself. The pilgrim door stood open — the Order loved its theater, loved the sight of the faithful shuffling in to gaze at relics of the old war, the shattered sigil displayed like a trophy of a battle won rather than a covenant broken. Zephyrine joined the last trickle of worshippers, head bowed, hands wrapped, mute as they'd been for three days. A mute pilgrim drew no questions. A mute pilgrim was a hole in the world shaped like a person, and holes were easy to step around.

Inside, the memory-light washed over them and Zephyrine flinched.

It wasn't just colored. It was *loud*. Each corridor burned with someone's stolen past, and though ordinary citizens felt nothing but pretty glow, Zephyrine's gift reached out the way a hand reaches for a railing in the dark — and touched the memories burning in the lamps. A woman's wedding day, gold and warm, flickering in a sconce by the door. A boy's first snowfall, blue-white, guttering low because no one had fed it lately. Somewhere overhead, a violet lamp that made Zephyrine's teeth ache: grief, harvested and refined, someone's worst day spent lighting a hallway so strangers wouldn't have to carry candles.

*I'm sorry,* they thought, uselessly, at all of them.

The last worshipper filed out. The great doors boomed shut. Somewhere above, the midnight bell began — one stroke, two —

Zephyrine moved.

Up the west stair, boots silent on stone worn smooth by centuries of feet. Sorrel launched from their shoulder at the second landing and vanished upward into the dark, a shape against shapes, toward the high clerestory windows where she would wait for her cue. The second gallery stretched away in blue light — memories of rivers, Zephyrine guessed, or rain, or some other gentle water — and at its end stood the door to the reliquary proper.

Iron. Old iron, older than the Order, older maybe than the valley. And set into its center, a basin the size of a coin, rimmed with script Zephyrine couldn't read but could *feel*, the way you feel a storm coming in a healed bone.

The blood-lock.

Zephyrine bared the knife Bracken had given them — good steel, wickedly plain — and opened the pad of their thumb. One bead, then another. They pressed it to the basin.

The lock *tasted* them.

Zephyrine felt it happen, felt something cold and patient move through the single drop of blood like a finger through spilled ink, reading. Reading the gift. Reading the beast-speech coiled in every cell like a second language the body had never stopped speaking. For one endless moment the lock considered them the way a warden considers a suspect.

Then, softly, with something that might have been distaste, it unsealed.

The door swung inward on darkness shot through with red — war-memories, Zephyrine realized, the vault beyond lit entirely by harvested violence — and the smell rolled out over them: dust and old metal and, beneath it, faintly, the smell of Sorrel's wing when they'd first pulled the charred stone from her feathers. Sigil-stone. The whole vault breathed it.

*Third alcove,* Bracken's voice said in memory. *Behind the glass case with the war banners.*

Zephyrine went in.

* * *

**Bracken**

Bracken counted bells in the tannery drain and hated every one of them.

This was the part of any job he despised: the sitting. The waiting while somebody else did the dangerous work in a building full of things that could kill them, and all he could do was count. Eleven strokes. Twelve. Midnight, and the pilgrim door locked, and somewhere above him a mute pilgrim who talked to birds was walking into the Order's own heart wearing borrowed courage.

"They'll be fine," he told himself. "Mute ones always are. It's the talkative ones who get caught."

A rat emerged from the drain grate, regarded him with small black judgment, and sat down to wait too. Bracken had seen what happened between rats and Zephyrine — had seen them *converse*, in that silent way, whiskers and stillness and something passing between them that wasn't quite either of them. He'd reported stranger things to worse people for less coin. Instead he'd offered partnership, and he still wasn't entirely sure why. Something about the way Zephyrine had looked at the rat. Not like a man looks at a rat. Like a man looks at a friend he hasn't seen in years and can't explain to the room why.

Thirteen strokes. Fourteen.

At fifteen, the plan said, the kestrel flew.

And indeed — right on schedule, which already worried him, because nothing in Ossuar ever went to schedule — a shadow dropped from the rooftops above the reliquary and went screaming across the processional square, low and fast and furious, straight through the open window of the warden barracks where the night-watch took their supper.

Even from the drain, Bracken heard the uproar. Shouting. A bench going over. Someone loosing a crossbow bolt that chipped stone. The kestrel — Sorrel, the kestrel had a name, which was another thing wrong with this whole arrangement, thieves didn't name their accomplices — banked hard and led half the garrison on a merry chase across the rooftops, darting between chimneys, vanishing and reappearing, a feathered insult no soldier in the city could ignore.

The square emptied of wardens. The reliquary stood unwatched.

"Now we find out if the mute one can count," Bracken muttered, and settled in to count some more.

Thirty strokes of the quarter-bell. Forty. His palms were sweating, which annoyed him; he wiped them on his knees and kept counting. Fifty. At sixty the night-wardens resumed their corridor rotation, per the map he'd bought off a disgraced acolyte for more money than the acolyte had probably deserved, and after that Zephyrine would be walking blind through a building that woke up every ninety seconds.

Seventy strokes.

Eighty.

At eighty-three, every lamp in the reliquary turned white.

Not gold, not blue, not the bruised violet of grief. White — flat, sourceless, terrible — blazing out of every window at once, and a sound followed it that Bracken felt in his back teeth before he heard it with his ears: a tone, high and rising, like a wineglass singing itself toward shattering.

He knew what it was. Everyone in Ossuar knew what it was, the way everyone knows the sound of the plague-bell. The speaker-alarm. Keyed to blood, tuned to heresy, built to scream the moment a beast-speaker's gift touched anything the Order owned.

The garrison poured back out of the barracks like a kicked anthill.

"Oh, you absolute *idiot*," Bracken said — to Zephyrine, to himself, to the universe generally — and ran.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

The fragment had been almost easy. That should have warned them.

Third alcove, behind the glass case with the war banners — exactly as Bracken had mapped it. The case held a shard of sigil-stone the size of a knucklebone, black shot through with veins of silver, and when Zephyrine lifted it free the whole vault seemed to lean toward them, every red-lit memory bending like grass toward wind. The stone was warm. It hummed against their palm with a frequency that lived below hearing, and Zephyrine understood, suddenly and completely, why the Regent hoarded these. Holding it felt like holding a promise. Holding it felt like being *more* than they were.

That was how it got you, they thought. Not with teeth. With promises.

They wrapped it in cloth, tucked it inside their coat, and turned for the door — and that was when the drop of blood betrayed them.

They never saw the mechanism. Perhaps there wasn't one; perhaps the vault itself had tasted them at the threshold and simply been slow to swallow. But as Zephyrine crossed back over the threshold, the blood-lock flared, and the flare climbed the walls, and every red memory-lamp in the reliquary bleached to white, and the alarm began to sing.

*Speaker,* the building said, in a voice made of a thousand stolen voices layered over each other. *Speaker. Speaker.*

Zephyrine ran.

The second gallery strobed white-blue-white as they sprinted its length, shadows leaping around them like panicked animals. Behind, boots — many boots, the night-wardens abandoning their rotation, drawn by the alarm. Ahead, the west stair, and past it the pilgrim door, locked now, midnight long past.

Locked. Of course it was locked. Bracken's voice in their head, dry as dust: *something always goes wrong.*

Zephyrine skidded to a halt in the entrance hall, alarms shrieking, doors sealed, wardens pouring down the west stair with drawn blades and nets weighted with memory-iron — and above them, high in the clerestory, a window burst inward and a kestrel came down like a thrown dagger.

*Straight ahead,* Sorrel screamed, banking past their face. *The rose window. It's rotten, I've been pecking it for an hour, MOVE—*

Zephyrine moved. Across the hall, wardens shouting, a net whistling past their ear and shattering a lamp — someone's harvested childhood bursting across the marble in a spray of golden light, a birthday, a dog, a summer that belonged to no one anymore — and up the scaffolding the glaziers had left against the east wall, three rungs at a time, Sorrel screaming course corrections overhead.

The rose window was old glass in old lead, and Sorrel had been right: the lead was rotten as bread. Zephyrine hit it shoulder-first and the whole panel gave way like a sugar shell, and then they were falling through cold night air, turning, grabbing — the gutter, tiles, a slide, a drop, a roll that knocked the breath out of them and left them sprawled on the slates of the reliquary roof with the fragment digging into their ribs and the alarm still singing below.

*Roofline east,* Sorrel said, wheeling above them. *They'll cut us off at the bell tower. Go, go, go.*

Zephyrine went. Tiles slick underfoot, the city spread out below in its grid of memory-lit streets, wardens boiling out of doors beneath them like ants from a cracked hill. East along the ridgeline, a jump to the chandler's roof, another to the dye-house, Sorrel calling turns before Zephyrine could see them needed. Behind, crossbow bolts sparking off slate. Ahead, the edge of the merchant quarter and — please — the tannery drain.

They dropped into the alley behind the tannery and a hand shot out of the dark and hauled them through a gap in the wall no wider than a coffin, and then they were in the drain, in the dark, in the wet, with Bracken's face inches from theirs and Bracken's voice a furious whisper:

"The *rose window?*"

"Sorrel's idea."

"I mapped you a *door*—"

"The door was locked."

"You were supposed to be out before the doors—" He stopped. Dragged a hand down his face. In the dark his eyes were very bright. "Do you have it?"

Zephyrine pulled the wrapped fragment from their coat. Even through the cloth, even here, it hummed. Bracken stared at it the way starving men stare through bakery windows.

"That's two," he said quietly. "Two pieces of the thing that ended the world."

"Didn't end it," Zephyrine said. "Paused it."

Behind them, far back through the drain tunnels, the alarm was still singing — and now, threading under it, a new sound. Boots. Many boots. And dogs.

Zephyrine froze. Not the memory-hounds of the corridors — those were shelved things, bottled things. These were flesh-and-blood hounds, and they were coming down the tunnel with the sure, eager pace of animals that had been given a scent.

Bracken went gray. "Warden-hounds track by blood. Yours is all over that window frame—"

But Zephyrine was already listening past the words, gift reaching down the tunnel the way it reached for everything alive, and what came back made their stomach drop.

The hounds weren't hunting yet. They were *asking*. Throwing their voices ahead of them into the dark, the way dogs do, the way all beasts do when they've been handled well — *anyone there? anyone there?* — and underneath the question, wound through it like wire, the flat compulsion of the Order's binding: *find the speaker, find the speaker, find the speaker.*

They knew Zephyrine was there. They were asking permission to say so.

"Sorrel," Zephyrine whispered. "How fast can you fly in a tunnel?"

*In a tunnel?* A pause, affronted. *Badly. Why?*

"Because I need you to go tell six dogs," Zephyrine said, "that the speaker they're smelling is the one who freed the cart-horse on the south road. And that we'd take it kindly if they lost the scent."

Sorrel stared at them with one fierce gold eye. Somewhere in the small fierce machinery of her kestrel heart, Zephyrine felt her decide this was, at last, a worthy errand.

*If they eat me,* she said, *I am haunting you forever.*

She dropped off Zephyrine's shoulder and went flickering down the black throat of the drain, toward the voices and the boots and the wire-tight compulsion, and Zephyrine pressed their back to the wet stone and listened — listened harder than they had ever listened in their life — for the sound of dogs choosing a side.
