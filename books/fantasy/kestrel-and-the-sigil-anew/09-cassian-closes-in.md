## 9. Cassian Closes In

The hounds stopped at the mouth of Tanner's Run, and Cassian Vey knew before either of them turned their heads that the trail had gone strange.

He'd named them for virtues he no longer believed in. Verity was the gray one, broad-chested, patient as a held breath. Loyalty was smaller, quicker, all ribs and nerve. They were not dogs, whatever the Order's ledgers said. Dogs were made of flesh and appetite. These had been made the way everything in Ossuar was made now — from something taken from someone else. Memory-bound, the writs called it. Cassian had signed for their binding himself, eleven years ago, and had never once asked what memories they carried, or whose.

You didn't ask questions like that in the Order of the Sealed Sigil. Asking was how wardens ended up on the harvesting tables, smiling and empty, their useful pasts decanted into the Regent's reliquary to keep the new sigil fed.

"Go on," he said quietly.

Verity went first, nose down, moving into the undercity's throat with her ears flat. The stench of Tanner's Run rose around them — tallow and lye and old blood soaked into stone that had been stone since before the war, before the sigil, before anyone decided that magic needed an owner. Above them, through grates and gaps, the city went about its evening: lamplighters, pie-sellers, the clatter of the memory-exchange houses where citizens queued to sell their griefs by the ounce. Below, it was dark and dripping and honest, which was more than Cassian could say for anywhere else he'd served.

The trail was three days old and should have been cold. It wasn't. That was the first wrong thing. Speaker-taint didn't fade like ordinary scent; it *burned*, according to the manuals — a sweetness under the world's smell, like honey left too long near a flame. He'd been told that so many times he'd stopped noticing he'd never actually smelled it himself. The hounds smelled it. That was supposed to be enough.

Loyalty suddenly swung her head up, toward a drainage arch half-swallowed by rubble, and gave one short bark.

"There," Cassian said.

* * *

He'd been hunting this speaker for nine days, and in nine days he had built a portrait out of evidence, the way you build any portrait: from small things.

A campsite outside Thornhollow where the fire had been built small and dead-centered, by someone who respected flame. A stream crossing where the bootprints paused — paused, deliberately, long enough for the water to blur them — beside a place where a cart-horse's tracks showed it had stood free of its harness, unchained, for the better part of an hour. A village well with a rope freshly mended, though no villager would admit to mending it. And everywhere, always, the same detail: birdsong. Wherever the speaker had passed, the birds kept singing after dark. In the borderlands, where animals fled humans like plague-fleas, that was not natural. That was not even possible.

Except it was, because the speaker was real, and the kestrel that rode their shoulder was real, and the Order wanted both of them harvested down to the last useful hour.

Cassian crouched at the drainage arch and let his fingers rest on the wet stone. Nine days ago he'd have said he understood exactly what he was doing. Speakers were the reason the valley passes existed. Speakers were why the old war had lasted ninety years — beasts turned to weapons, whole forests given voices and grudges, fields that screamed when they were plowed. His grandfather had died in that war. Everyone's grandfather had died in that war. The Order hadn't seized power after it; the Order had been *begged* to take it, by people who never wanted to hear a talking animal again as long as they lived.

That was the story. He'd recited it at his induction with his hand on the sealed stone and meant every word.

Then, four nights ago, in a tavern in the Warrens, he'd watched a memory-broker pay a weaver six coppers for the memory of her daughter's face. Six coppers. The woman had walked out lighter, she said. Happier. She'd smiled the whole way to the door, and Cassian had sat there with his ale going warm, thinking: *that is what we feed the sigil with. That is what the wards eat.* And somewhere behind his sternum, quiet as a coin dropped down a well, something had begun to fall.

Verity whined. Low, uncertain. He'd never heard her make that sound.

"What is it?"

She looked back at him. Her eyes caught the little light there was, and for a moment — he would spend years deciding whether he'd imagined this — they were not a hound's eyes. They were someone's. Someone tired.

* * *

They found the speaker in the flooded chapel district, or rather the hounds did, and then refused to do anything about it.

It happened fast. One moment Cassian was moving along a collapsed colonnade above black water, hounds flanking him, blade loose in its sheath; the next, Loyalty froze mid-stride and Verity planted herself in front of him like a door swinging shut. Ahead, maybe thirty paces, a figure stood at the water's edge with a hood pushed back and a kestrel on their shoulder, silhouetted against the faint gray glow of memory-lanterns strung along the far wall. Cornered. The colonnade ended in a sheer drop to the floodwater; the only way out was past Cassian.

Perfect. Textbook. Every warden dreamed of a corner like this.

"Zephyrine Ravensworth." He used the name from the warrant, let it carry across the water. "Warden Second Class Cassian Vey, Order of the Sealed Sigil. You're under harvest writ. Kneel, put your hands flat on the stone, and your beast will be taken alive."

The figure didn't kneel. Didn't run, either. Just stood there, and the kestrel's head swiveled toward the hounds, and the bird made a sound — a low chittering cascade, nothing like a hawk's cry — and Cassian felt the air change the way air changes before summer rain.

What happened next, he would file no report about, ever.

Verity lay down.

Not cowered. *Lay down*, chin on paws, tail giving one slow sweep across the wet flagstones, the way a dog greets someone come home after a long time. Loyalty circled once and settled beside her sister, and both of them looked up at Cassian with an expression he had no training for and no language for, except that it was unmistakably a refusal, and unmistakably gentle.

"Hounds," Cassian said, and his voice came out wrong. "Take."

Nothing. The kestrel chirred again, softer, and Verity's tail swept the stones a second time.

"They've heard about you," the speaker said. Their voice was quiet, roughened by days of road dust, and younger than Cassian expected. "From the horse. From the rats in the Warrens. From every animal between here and the border." Zephyrine took one step forward, hands open and empty at their sides. "They heard that I unchained a cart-horse and asked nothing in return. They heard I listened." Another step. Close enough now that he could see their face: sharp-boned, exhausted, eyes the color of river ice. "Your hounds are bound with stolen memories, Warden. Do you know whose? Have you ever wondered? Because *they* wonder. Every day, they wonder. And they know I'm the first human who ever asked them anything besides *attack*."

"Stand back." His hand was on his sword and his hand was shaking and he could not make it stop. "I am ordering you—"

"You're not ordering me. You're ordering *them*. And look at them."

He looked. He couldn't help it. Verity's gray flank was trembling — not with eagerness, not with bloodlust, but with something that looked horribly like hope, the way a starving thing trembles when food is set down and it doesn't yet believe it's allowed.

"The Order says speakers started the war," Cassian said. The words sounded rote even to him, a catechism recited in an empty church. "Beasts given voices. Beasts turned to—"

"Some beasts were given voices, yes." Zephyrine's jaw tightened. "By magicians who wanted soldiers. And other magicians answered with the sigil, and the sigil ate a hundred years, and when it was over everyone agreed to blame the ones who couldn't defend themselves in court." The kestrel shifted on their shoulder, feathers sleek and battle-ready, and yet she made no move to strike. "I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to look at your own hounds. They've met me. What are they telling you?"

And the terrible thing — the thing that cracked something in Cassian Vey that would never fully set again — was that they *were* telling him. Plainly. As plainly as animals had ever spoken to anyone without words at all. *We will not hurt this one. This one is kind. Why have you never been kind?*

"I have a writ," he said, uselessly.

"You have a piece of paper that says my memories belong to the Regent." Zephyrine's voice didn't rise. If anything it gentled, which was worse. "Do you know what they harvest first, Warden? Not skills. Skills take training to extract. They take love, because love comes out clean. Mothers. First snows. The smell of bread. Your hounds are full of somebody's mother, and somebody's bread, and neither of them knows whose, and they heel to you anyway because the alternative is the table." A pause. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, counting seconds. "I'm sorry. I shouldn't have said that. But you were going to kill us, and I find I don't have time anymore for the polite version."

Cassian's sword arm lowered. He watched it happen as if from a distance, some other man's arm, some other man's life.

"If I let you walk," he said, "there will be another warden. There will be ten. The Regent has felt the fragments moving — the whole Order has felt it, the reliquary lights have been flickering for a week. You're not running from me anymore. You're running from *her*."

"I know."

"She'll use an elder from your valley as bait. It's what I'd do. It's what the manual says to do."

Something moved behind Zephyrine's eyes — fear, quick and real — and then resolve settling over it like a stone dropping into deep water. "Then I suppose I'll have to be faster than the manual."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The memory-lanterns guttered. Verity's tail thumped once against the stone, soft as a heartbeat.

"Walk north out of the chapel district," Cassian heard himself say. "There's a patrol rotation change at the second bell. The Grate Street entrance will be unmanned for a quarter hour. After that—" He exhaled. "After that I can't help you."

"Why?" Zephyrine asked. Simply. No triumph in it, no gloating, which somehow made it harder. "Why let us go?"

Because his hounds were lying at a stranger's feet like they'd finally come home. Because he'd watched a woman sell her daughter's face for six coppers and smile about it. Because eleven years ago he'd signed a binding contract without reading the annexes, and tonight, looking into a hound's borrowed eyes, he had finally wondered *whose* they were.

"Because I don't think the Order is what I swore to," he said. It was the truest sentence he'd spoken in a decade, and it terrified him more than the drop to the floodwater would have. "Now go. Before I remember what I'm supposed to be."

Zephyrine held his gaze a moment longer — weighing him, he realized; deciding what he was — then touched two fingers briefly to the kestrel's breast in some private signal, and slipped away along the colonnade's edge, sure-footed in the dark, gone the way water goes into sand.

The hounds watched them go. Then Verity rose, shook herself, and came back to heel at Cassian's left side as if nothing had happened at all.

As if he hadn't just become a traitor.

* * *

He filed the report at dawn, in the warden-house scriptorium, with his hand steady and his heart nowhere in his body.

*Pursued speaker-taint into lower city. Trail degraded in flooded district; subject likely exited via western culverts toward the river gate. Hounds lost scent at approximately the fourth bell. Recommend widening the search perimeter southward.*

Southward. Away from the fragments' trail, away from the reliquary's flickering lights, away from everything true. He wrote *lost scent* knowing the hounds had never once in eleven years lost a scent, and he initialed the page, and the clerk stamped it, and that was that. Somewhere in the Order's archives, a lie now existed with his name on it, official and permanent, and Cassian Vey discovered that treason felt exactly like paperwork.

He was clipping the hounds' leads back on when Captain Dreeve found him, a thickset man with the contented cruelty of the truly certain.

"Vey. Good work on the southern redirect. Though between us—" Dreeve leaned close, lowering his voice with the intimacy of men who enjoyed bad news, "—it won't matter. The Regent's already moving. She's pulled an elder out of that speaker's valley. Old woman. Founding Festival stock, very sentimental." He smiled. "Bait for a trap at Thornhollow. Should be closed within the week. You'll want to be there, Vey. Harvesting a live speaker — that's a career."

Thornhollow. An elder. Exactly what he'd warned them of, handed now to him as an invitation.

"Yes, Captain," Cassian said. "I'll want to be there."

Dreeve clapped his shoulder and moved on, and Cassian stood in the gray morning light with two memory-bound hounds sitting at his feet, looking up at him — waiting, patient, loyal to a fault — as if they already knew what he was going to do next.

So did he. He simply hadn't admitted it yet.

He gathered the leads. He turned south, toward Thornhollow, toward the trap — and for the first time in eleven years of service, Cassian Vey began planning how to arrive just late enough to ruin everything.
