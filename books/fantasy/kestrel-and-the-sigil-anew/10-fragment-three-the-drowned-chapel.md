## 10. Fragment Three: The Drowned Chapel

The fish found us at dawn.

They came up the river in a silver shoal, hundreds of them, turning as one body beneath the brown water like a single thought wearing scales. I was crouched on the bank washing my hands when the current changed around them and they began to circle — not feeding, not fleeing, but *waiting*, in that patient way animals wait when they have something to say.

*Down,* they said, in the wordless way of fish, which is mostly pressure and cold and direction. *Down and under. She is drowning. Come.*

"Who's she?" I asked, and then felt foolish, kneeling at a riverbank talking to minnows while Bracken slept off his watch behind me with his boots for a pillow.

But the fish only turned again, all together, all one motion, and pointed their noses downstream toward the drowned country where the old war had ended — or been ended, depending on which ruin you believed.

Sorrel woke before I finished saddling nothing, because we had no horse, only our feet and the cart-horse's promise to meet us at the far ford. She hopped along the bank beside me, feathers ruffled against the morning damp, and said, in her small fierce voice: *The fish are right. I can feel it too. Like a toothache in the world.*

"You feel everything like a toothache."

*I am small. Everything is proportionally larger.* She fluffed herself onto my shoulder, which was her way of ending an argument she had already won.

Bracken sat up, saw me staring at the river, and read my face the way he read locks — quickly, cynically, accurately. "Fish again."

"Fish again."

"And we're going where they say."

"We're going where they say."

He groaned and pulled his boots on. "I want it noted, for the record, that I have followed a kestrel, three rats, a horse with opinions, and now a school of bait into increasingly wet danger, and that in any sane city this would qualify me for the asylum rather than the gallows."

"Noted," I said. "The record is your own head, and your head is full of stolen candlesticks."

"Some of them were candelabra," he said with dignity, and shouldered his pack.

* * *

The drowned chapel lay two days downstream, in a valley the maps called Merrow's Hollow and the fish called simply *the deep place*. The war had come through here in its last year, Sorrel told me as we walked — she knew fragments of it, bird-memory passed down through generations of kestrels the way humans pass down songs. The armies had met on the flats above the river. The magicians of both sides had broken the valley to break each other, and the river had risen through the cracks they made, and by the time the truce was signed there was no dry ground left to sign it on.

"So they signed it underwater?" Bracken asked.

"They signed it in the chapel," Sorrel said. "And then the water took the chapel. Some things are signed so deeply that drowning doesn't unmake them."

I didn't ask how she knew. Bird-memory is older than books and less forgiving of questions.

We came over the last ridge at dusk on the second day, and the hollow opened below us like a held breath. The flood had never fully receded here. Half the valley stood in shallow gray water, dead trees rising from it like the masts of ships that had sailed down instead of across. And near the center, its bell tower leaning at a drunk angle, its roofline just breaking the surface, stood the chapel.

The water around it was clear. Too clear. I could see straight down through fathoms of it, past drowned pews, past pale drifting silt, to a floor of white stone where something glowed with a light that wasn't light — a slow pulse, blue-white, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

"There," I said, and my voice came out strange, flattened, as if the hollow had already taken part of it as payment.

Bracken looked at the water, then at me, then back at the water. "How deep?"

"The fish will tell us."

"The fish," he repeated. "Right. The fish." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Zeph, can I say something without you taking it as cowardice?"

"You've never once managed it. Try anyway."

"That thing down there is one of the pieces. Of the sigil. The thing the Regent is killing people's memories to rebuild." He kicked at a stone and watched it roll down into the shallows. "Every piece we've touched has taken something out of you. Your mother's face. Your name-day. You walk around with holes in you the size of holidays and parents, and you keep saying *one more, one more* like a gambler at midnight." He turned and looked at me straight on, and for once there was no irony anywhere in him. "What's left after this one?"

I didn't have an answer. I had a fragment-map in my head drawn by a dying kestrel and a dead warden's stolen papers, and a certainty that had long ago stopped being courage and become simply momentum. But I said, "Something worth having," because it was true even if I couldn't prove it yet, and because Bracken deserved better than silence.

He exhaled through his nose. "Fine. But I'm holding your hand on the way down, and if you drown I'm keeping the candelabra money."

* * *

The fish came for us at first light.

They rose around us as we waded in, the whole silver shoal of them, and pressed close — not touching, but near, the way a crowd presses near a fire. Their guidance came as cold currents against my skin: *left, down, hold, breathe.* The water swallowed sound and gave back pressure. Above us the sky shrank to a trembling coin. Below us the chapel grew from the murk, doors burst open, nave flooded with a century of quiet.

We went in through the roof gap, down between the drowned pews, and the glow brightened until it lit the whole interior from below, and I saw what the truce-makers had built.

The altar had been carved away entirely. In its place, set into the chapel floor like a cornerstone, was a ring of white stone wide as a wagon wheel — and in the ring's center, a socket shaped like nothing so much as a wound, empty. Around the ring ran letters in no alphabet I knew, worn nearly smooth, and threaded through the letters ran channels, and in the channels moved a faint blue light, circling, patient, waiting for the piece that had been pried out of it a hundred years ago and scattered.

The fragment itself lay in the crypt below the altar. The fish showed us the stair, half-collapsed, and Bracken went first because he was lighter and because he claimed — loudly, in bubbles — that thieves always go first, it was in the guild charter.

The crypt was small. Three stone shelves, two empty, and on the third, wrapped in rotted cloth that might once have been a banner, the third fragment of the sigil: a shard of white stone the length of my forearm, veined with that same blue pulse, humming against my teeth before I ever laid a hand on it.

It was warm. That was the worst part. Everything else down here was river-cold, and the fragment sat in its wrappings like a living thing pretending to be dead.

"Don't—" Bracken started, and I already had it in my hands.

* * *

The memory took me the way cold water takes a swimmer who has gone under in winter: completely, and without asking permission.

I was standing in the chapel, but the chapel was full — full of people, hundreds of them packed into the nave, soldiers in the livery of two different armies, magicians with their hands bound in ceremonial cord, civilians pressed against the walls. Candles everywhere. The air thick with smoke and fear and the particular smell of a room where everyone knows the fighting is supposed to be over and no one believes it yet.

I was an old man. I knew that immediately — the ache in my knees, the weight of a chain of office on shoulders that had carried it too long. I was the arbiter. The neutral party. The one they'd dragged out of retirement to witness the signing because both sides trusted me slightly more than they trusted each other, which is the most trust anyone gets in a war.

The fragment lay on the altar before me. Whole, then. Whole and blazing, and the two delegations stood on either side of it, and the ritual was simple: each side would give a memory to the stone. A memory of the war's worst hour. The sigil would hold those memories forever, and the holding would bind the peace — because a covenant written in shared grief cannot be casually broken. You do not forget what the stone remembers. You *cannot* forget. That was the design. That was the genius of the ones who made it, back when magic meant covenant and not conquest.

The eastern delegation signed first. Then the western. Then the magicians stepped forward to seal it —

— and the oldest of them, a woman with frost in her black hair and grief carved so deep into her face that it had become architecture, looked at the stone for a long moment. And I saw it happen, the way you see a cart wheel start to slip before anyone screams. She looked at the sigil, and she did not see a covenant. She saw power. Power enough to end wars before they started. Power enough to make peace *compulsory*. And somewhere behind her eyes, love curdled into certainty.

She spoke a word. Her colleagues spoke eleven more.

The stone screamed. I heard it scream — a sound I will hear until whatever takes my memories next takes that one. The binding tore. The delegates fell. The woman lifted the shattered heart of the sigil in both hands like a midwife lifting a newborn, and she was weeping, and she said — I heard her say it, in the old man's ears, in the old man's final moment:

*"Forgive me. Someone has to hold it. Someone has to hold it or it all happens again."*

Then the chapel shook, because the other magicians had chosen that moment to disagree, and the ceiling came down, and the river came through the walls, and the old man — the arbiter, the witness, the last honest person in the room — did not run. He gathered the largest piece of the shattered sigil off the altar, because someone had to, because if it scattered unguarded it would be found by whoever crawled out of the rubble with the strongest arms and the weakest conscience. He wrapped it in the fallen banner. He carried it down into the crypt. He laid it on the shelf.

The water reached his chest. He was old, and tired, and the door above had jammed, and he knew — the calm way you know things at the end — that he was not leaving.

So he stayed with it. He put his hands on the stone to keep it company, the way you'd stay with a frightened animal, and the stone drank him in. That was its nature, even broken. It held memories. It was holding his as the water closed over his head, holding every year of him, and his last thought — the thought the fragment kept for a hundred years in the dark, and gave to me now, whole, in the flooded crypt, in my own body, on my knees in another man's death —

His last thought was not of the war, or the stone, or the woman who broke the world to save it.

It was of a garden. A small garden behind a small house, and a woman whose name he couldn't quite reach anymore, dead these twenty years, bending over bean rows in evening light. His last thought was: *I never told anyone how much I loved that garden. Now no one will ever know.*

* * *

I came up gasping. Bracken had me by the collar; Sorrel was screaming somewhere above; the fragment was still in my hands, and I couldn't remember letting go of anything, because I hadn't — the fragment had simply reached *into* me and poured the old man out, and taken its toll on the way through.

That was the pattern. I understood it now, really understood it, in the way you understand a debt only when the collector arrives. The sigil didn't steal memories. It *traded* them. Memory for memory. It had given me the arbiter's last hour, complete, permanent, mine now forever — a garden I'd never seen, a woman I'd never met, a death I'd already died once. And it had taken payment in kind.

I waited for the hole to announce itself. My mother's face — gone since the first fragment. My name-day — gone since the second. What now?

"Zeph." Bracken's voice, very carefully level. "Zeph, look at me. What did it take?"

"I don't know yet. That's how it works. You find the hole by falling into it."

We waded out. The fish escorted us to the shallows and dispersed, their debt paid, their deep place quiet now that its hundred-year vigil was over. We made camp on the ridge above the hollow because neither of us could bear to sleep beside the water. Bracken built a fire with the exaggerated competence of a man who needs his hands busy. Sorrel sat on my knee and said nothing, which from Sorrel was a kind of vigil.

It came at moonrise. I reached for the memory of the road out of the valley — the festival fires, the ward-line crackling dead overhead, the last look back — and it was there, intact, fine. I reached for Bracken's face, the rats' names, the taste of the cart-horse's gratitude. All there.

I reached for Sorrel's first word to me.

There was a shape where it had been. I knew there had been a first word — every speaker remembers their first word, it's the hinge of the whole life, the moment the world stops being mute. I knew it had happened. I knew it had mattered more than anything. I could feel the importance of it like the impression a heavy book leaves on a shelf after the book is removed.

The word itself was gone. Taken. Traded for a stranger's garden and a stranger's drowning, and I sat there by the fire doing the arithmetic of the exchange and finding, for the first time, that I could not balance it.

"Zeph?" Bracken again. He'd seen my face.

"It took her first word," I said. "The first thing she ever said to me. I know it happened. I can't remember what it was."

The fire cracked. Somewhere downslope, the drowned chapel glowed faintly under the water, its empty socket waiting for the piece we'd carried away.

Sorrel pressed her head against my jaw, the way she did when words weren't the point. And I held her and grieved a memory I could no longer properly mourn, because mourning requires knowing what you've lost, and the sigil had seen to it that I didn't.

It was Bracken who broke the silence, quietly, without any of his usual armor: "There's a fourth fragment. The map says north. There's also a captured elder, a Regent who's noticed us, and a warden who let us live." He fed the fire a branch. "Whatever you decide about the cost — decide it soon. Because I think the choice is about to stop being ours."

Far to the north, past the black line of the hills, a light rose that was not dawn — thin, white, deliberate, a pillar of harvested memory burning against the stars. The Regent was arming the sigil. And she knew, now, exactly where we were.
