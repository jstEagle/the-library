## 11. What the Sigil Takes

I woke before dawn with the third fragment burning a hole through my bedroll.

Not literally. The stone was cool to the touch — it had been cool since I pulled it from the drowned chapel, cool as riverbed pebbles, cool as the hands of the dead. But beneath my palm, through canvas and wool and skin, I could feel it *listening*. That was the only word for it. The fragment lay there in the dark like an ear pressed against the floor of the world, and it was listening for me.

Beside the cold fire, Bracken slept sitting up, his back against a fallen birch, one hand curled around his knife even in sleep. Sorrel was a small warm weight under my jaw where she roosted in the crook of my collar, her talons kneading the fabric of my shirt in that half-dreaming way hawks have. She had not spoken since the chapel. Not because she couldn't. Because she was watching me the way you watch someone standing at the edge of a cliff.

I knew what the fragment wanted. I had known since the second one.

The first fragment — Sorrel's fragment, fused into her wing-bone by fire and desperation — had cost me nothing when I finally drew it free. Or so I'd thought. It was only later, reaching for the memory of the day I learned to whistle through cupped hands, that I found a smooth blank place where the memory should have been. A polished absence. My mind slid off it like water off oiled feathers.

The second fragment, taken from the Regent's reliquary with Bracken's lockpicks and Sorrel's diversion and far too much luck, had taken more. It took my name-day. All of them. Every celebration, every cake baked by my mother's flour-white hands, every year of turning older in a valley that pretended magic didn't exist. I remembered that name-days had happened. I remembered the *shape* of the tradition — candles, a song sung slightly off-key, a gift wrapped in cloth. But when I reached for any single one, any specific morning of being celebrated, there was nothing. Just the shape of a room with no furniture in it.

And now the third sat against my palm, patient as debt, waiting to be paid.

"Zeph."

Bracken was awake. Of course he was. He'd been sleeping like a cat since Thornhollow, since the warden's blade meant for me had opened him from hip to ribs instead. He healed wrong and slow and complained about both, but he noticed everything. It was his best quality and his most exhausting one.

"You're holding it again," he said.

"I'm just—"

"You're holding it again." He pushed himself upright with a hiss, favoring his left side, and came to sit across the dead fire from me. In the gray pre-light his face looked older than twenty-some years. "Say what it is. Out loud. Whatever it's going to take this time. Say it before you touch it, because once you touch it you'll forget you ever had anything to lose."

"I don't know yet," I said. And that was the truth, and the truth was the worst part. The fragments didn't take what they were given. They took what they *weighed*. They reached into you and found the heaviest thing and lifted it out, clean as a pickpocket, and you never felt the hand.

"That's worse," Bracken said flatly.

"It's the price. There are four fragments, maybe five if the map's lying, and then the heart itself in the vault. Malvane has half the sigil already and she's feeding it a city's worth of stolen memories. Every hour I spend being careful is an hour she spends getting stronger."

"And every hour you spend paying her price is an hour you spend becoming less." He leaned forward. Firelight would have helped him here; he had a face built for argument, all angles and insistence. Without it he just looked frightened, and he hated looking frightened, and he did it anyway. For me. "Zeph. Last night you called me by the wrong name."

My stomach dropped. "No, I didn't."

"You called me *Corvin*." He said it gently, which was somehow crueler. "There is no Corvin. I've never met a Corvin. You looked right at me and used a name for someone who doesn't exist, and then you laughed it off, and I let you laugh it off because I'm a coward about things that matter." He held up his hand before I could speak. "Whoever Corvin was — whoever he is to you — you're losing people you haven't lost yet. You're losing them *in advance*. Do you understand? The sigil isn't taking your past. It's taking your future too. It's taking everyone you were going to love."

Sorrel stirred against my throat. Her talons tightened, just slightly. A kestrel's weight is almost nothing; a kestrel's grip is everything.

"The elder," I said quietly. "Elder Maren. The Regent has her. Cassian's maps show the holding-house at Thornhollow. If Malvane finishes the sigil whole, there won't be a valley to go back to. There won't be elders or festivals or wards or lies about how we never had magic. There'll just be Ossuar, forever, eating its own children's memories and calling it comfort." I turned the fragment over in my fingers. Cool. Patient. Listening. "You want me to stop. I know you do. So give me the alternative. Give me one."

Bracken was quiet for a long time. Long enough that the sky went from gray to the color of old pewter, long enough that somewhere downslope a lark started up and then, remembering the muting-collars still strung along the hedgerows of this country, fell silent mid-phrase. Even here, a hundred miles from the capital, the Regent's quiet had teeth.

"There isn't one," he admitted finally. "That's what makes it a trap instead of a choice. But—" and his voice cracked, just once, on the word, "—there's a difference between walking into a trap with your eyes open and letting it eat you piece by piece on the way. At least tell me what you're losing. At least keep a ledger. Let me remember for you. That's all I'm asking. Tell me what it takes, and I'll carry it, so that when this is over — *if* this is over — somebody can give it back to you."

I looked at him for a long moment. This ridiculous, brave, dishonest little thief who had caught me talking to rats in an alley and offered partnership instead of a noose. Who had taken a blade for me and complained about the stitching. Who loved, I was beginning to understand, in the specific and terrible way of people who have never been able to afford keeping anything.

"All right," I said. "A ledger."

He produced the little grease-pencil he used for marking windows and shutters on jobs, and a scrap of oilcloth, and wrote at the top, in his quick criminal's hand: *What the Sigil Takes.* Then he waited.

I closed my hand around the third fragment.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the obscenity of it. The drowned chapel had nearly killed me; the reliquary had burned my palms; but the fragments themselves were gentle, gentle as drowning in warm water, and I felt the weight lift out of me and had one instant — one bright, screaming instant — of knowing exactly what it was carrying away.

My mother's face.

I saw it whole, one last time: the crease between her brows when she worried, which was always; the way her left eye crinkled higher than her right when she laughed, which was rarer than it should have been; the flour on her knuckles; the gray coming in at her temples like frost on a window. I saw her turn toward me across a kitchen thirty years gone, mouth opening to say my name—

—and then it was gone, and I was looking at a woman-shaped absence, a beloved silhouette with no face, and I could not have told you whether her hair was brown or black or whether she was tall, and the grief arrived a heartbeat behind the loss like thunder after lightning, enormous and useless and late.

"Zeph?" Bracken's voice, very far away. "Zeph, look at me. What did it take?"

"My mother," I said. My voice sounded fine. That was the worst part; my voice always sounded fine. "Her face. I have her voice. I have her hands. I have the way she stood in doorways. But her face is—" I made myself say it, because a ledger is a ledger. "Gone. Write it down. *Fragment three: my mother's face.*"

His pencil scratched. His hand was shaking. When I could see again — when the white noise faded and the world reassembled itself around the kestrel-weight at my collar — he was staring at the oilcloth like it might bite him.

"Stop," he said. "Zeph, stop. Three fragments. Three pieces of you, gone, and we're not even halfway. What happens at five? What happens at the vault? What's left of you to walk out of there?"

"Enough."

"*Enough?*" He laughed, and it was an awful sound, wet and short. "You don't know that. Nobody knows that. You're spending yourself like a drunk spends wages, and I'm supposed to stand here and hold the ledger and watch you vanish line by line, and then what, Zeph? Then I find some stranger wearing your face at the end of all this and hand them a list of everything they used to be? Is that the plan? Because it's a *terrible* plan."

"It's the only plan."

"No." He stood up, too fast, and winced, and kept standing anyway, looming over me with his stitched side and his shaking hands and his eyes bright with something he'd die before calling tears. "No, it's not. There's always another plan. Steal slower. Find another way. Use *my* memories — take mine, I've got plenty and half of them are garbage anyway—"

"They don't take from anyone but the bearer. You know that. Sorrel told us."

"Then find a way around it! You're the clever one! You talk to *birds*, you broke a mute-collar off a cart-horse with a *song*, you got us out of the reliquary when I had already written us off — you don't get to be clever everywhere except the one place it counts!" He stopped. Breathed. Sat back down heavily, the fight going out of him the way it always did, replaced by that terrible gentleness. "Please," he said, quietly. "Please stop. We'll run. North, south, over the sea, I know boats, I know bribes, I know a hundred ways to disappear. Let the world keep its sigil. Let Malvane keep her order. I'd rather have a broken world with you in it than a saved one without."

And there it was. Love against obsession, laid out on an oilcloth ledger between us, and the awful truth that we were both right.

Because I wanted to say yes. Sitting there with my mother's face freshly torn out of me and a hole in my chest shaped exactly like her, I wanted it so badly my teeth ached. Run north. Disappear. Keep what was left.

But I had heard the drowned ghosts in the chapel singing the truce-song of a war that ended the world once already. I had seen the hill Ossuar stands on, made of packed and powdered memory, and known that my own valley's peace was built on the same foundation. I had felt the sigil's heart dreaming under the city, hungry, patient, *listening* — and I knew, the way you know a storm is coming by the ache in old bones, that running would only mean it found us later, when there was even less of me left to stop it.

"I can't," I said. "Bracken. Look at me. I can't."

He looked. And whatever he saw in my face — the part of me that was still me, the part that hadn't been spent yet — must have convinced him, because he didn't argue. He just folded the oilcloth with exaggerated care, three times, corners aligned, and tucked it inside his shirt next to the scar.

"Ledger's open," he said. "But I'm adding a rule. After every fragment, you tell me one thing you *still* have. One thing that's yours, that it hasn't touched. Proof of life. Non-negotiable."

I thought about it. Reached inward, carefully, the way you probe a sore tooth.

"Sorrel's first word," I said. "She hasn't taken that. I checked."

Something moved through Bracken's face — relief, and under it a question he was too kind to ask aloud, though I answered it anyway.

"She was six weeks out of the egg, flying on her own maybe four days, and she landed on my shoulder in the middle of a rainstorm and said — beast-speech isn't words, not really, it's more like meaning arriving whole — but if you translated it, it would be *found you*. Not *you found me*. *Found you.* Like she'd been looking." I smiled, and it hurt, and I smiled anyway. "First thing she ever gave me. Still mine."

Bracken nodded slowly. Then he froze.

"Zeph," he said. "Where's the smoke coming from?"

I followed his gaze north. Past the black line of the hills, where last night a pillar of harvested memory had burned against the stars, the sky had changed. No pillar now. Instead, a thin gray smudge on the horizon, wide as a held breath — and beneath it, faint but unmistakable on the wind, a sound I had hoped never to hear outside my nightmares.

Bells. Many bells. The Order's hunting-peals, ringing not pursuit but *quarantine*: a cordon, closing.

And riding ahead of the bells, down the far slope where the lark had gone silent, a single rider in warden-gray — moving fast, moving alone, moving *toward* us, with something pale and desperate tied to his saddle.

Cassian Vey. Coming to defect — or to deliver us straight into the Regent's hands, and no way to know which until he was close enough to touch.
