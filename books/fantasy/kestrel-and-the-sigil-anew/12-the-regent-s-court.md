## 12. The Regent's Court

The Regent of Ossuar woke before the bells, as she always did, in a bed that remembered being someone else's.

That was the trouble with sleeping above a hill of harvested memories. The palace was built on the old vault, and the vault breathed. All night it exhaled other people's pasts through the stone — a farmer's first snowfall, a bride's terror at the altar, a child's fever-dream of drowning — and they seeped into her sleep like water into chalk. Most mornings she woke with someone else's grief already cooling on her face and had to lie still for a moment, sorting hers from theirs.

This morning she woke with a name that wasn't hers.

*Elenna,* said the memory, warm as bread. *Elenna, come see what I found.*

Malvane lay in the dark and let it pass through her like weather. She had learned not to flinch. Flinching was how the memories got their hooks in; the Order's harvesters called it "bleeding," when a citizen took a purchased past so deeply they forgot which griefs were their own. Half the nobility of Ossuar bled, these days. They wandered their marble halls wearing dead strangers' joys like borrowed coats, weeping at windows for children they'd never had.

She never bought. That was her one rule, and she kept it the way other women kept chastity or faith — absolutely, and with a certain pride. Everything Malvane carried, she had earned or endured. Her memories were all her own: the fire, the road, the years of gray rations and grayer lessons, the slow climb to a throne no one had wanted her to have.

And the sigil, half-assembled beneath her feet, drinking its nightly ration of other people's yesterdays.

She rose, dressed without help — she dismissed her attendants years ago; too many ears, too many eyes that might be bought — and went down through the waking palace to the Hall of Records, where the day's harvest was weighed.

The hall smelled of cold iron and lavender. The lavender was for the harvesters' benefit; the iron was for everyone else's. Along one wall stood the memory-engines, brass and glass and older than the Order itself, salvaged from the war's wreckage by the first Regent and improved upon ever since. They hummed this morning with particular satisfaction. Malvane could feel it in her teeth.

"Report," she said.

Master of Harvests Odile Voss was already there, of course. Odile was always there before her, a small gray woman with ink-stained fingers and the patient manner of someone who has spent thirty years weighing things that cannot be seen. She bowed exactly as deep as protocol required and no deeper.

"The night yield is strong," Odile said. "Four hundred and six units. The city quotas held. The border provinces are... slower."

"They're always slower."

"There's resistance in the western valleys." Odile consulted her ledger without appearing to. "Nothing organized. Old families hiding their elders rather than selling their griefs. We've had reports of a village — Thornhollow, in the Kestrel Vale — where an entire founding generation refuses harvest."

Kestrel Vale. The word landed somewhere under Malvane's ribs and sat there.

"And the fragments?" she asked, because that was the question underneath every question now.

Odile turned to a fresh page. "Two accounted for. The reliquary piece was taken nine days ago — you know this. But there's been movement since. The engines registered a resonance three nights ago, east-southeast, near the drowned chapel at Merrow's Reach. And again last night, closer. Much closer." She looked up. "Someone is gathering them, Majesty. Someone who knows what they are."

"Not someone." Malvane crossed to the great map that covered the eastern wall, the one with the sigil-roads marked in gold thread. "A speaker. The Order's wardens found speaker-taint along the border trail two weeks ago. Cassian Vey reported the trail going cold at the city gates." She tapped Ossuar. "It didn't go cold. It came inside."

"You believe the thief and the speaker are one party?"

"I believe a street rat doesn't breach my reliquary alone, and I believe a fragment doesn't sing three nights running unless something is carrying it toward something else." She traced the gold thread south, toward the mountains, toward a valley that appeared on no official map. "There is one place left in the world where speakers are born. One valley the old covenant hid behind wards. And those wards fell the night the stars went out."

Odile's pen had stopped moving. "You think the valley sent them."

"I think the valley *lost* them," Malvane said. "Which may be worse. A speaker sent with purpose can be bargained with. A speaker who chose exile over safety—" she smiled, thin and without warmth, "—that one believes in something. Believers are harder."

She knew believers. She had been one since the age of nine.

* * *

Her family had lived in Harrowgate, on the war's old front line, where the houses were built of siege-stone and the church bell had been recast three times because twice it had been melted for shot. Her father was a warden of the old Order, back when the Order still served the sigil instead of feeding it, and her mother kept bees, and Malvane — Sera, then, just Sera Harrow — spent her ninth summer learning which flowers the hives loved best.

Then came the last year of the war, and the magicians broke the truce at the drowned chapel, and everything after happened very fast and very slowly at once. Fast: the fire that took Harrowgate burned for two days. Slow: the years afterward, in the refugee columns, in the ration lines, watching grown men fight over a heel of bread while somewhere beyond the hills the magicians' duels lit the sky like obscene auroras.

Her mother had gone back into the burning house for the bees. Not for Sera — Sera was already out, standing barefoot in the road, screaming — but for the bees. Because the bees were alive and the house was lost and her mother had never in her life abandoned a living thing, and the roof beam came down, and Sera stopped screaming only because she had no voice left.

They told her later that the magicians hadn't meant it. Collateral. The truce-breaking was between armies; Harrowgate was simply in the way. As if a town could be *in the way* of its own destruction. As if her mother had been collateral.

The old sigil was supposed to prevent all of it. That was the story everyone told: the ancient covenant bound the magicians' power so no one hand could hold enough to burn a city. But the covenant was broken — the first magicians had shattered it for power, hoarding its fragments like dragons over gold — and the war was what happened when men with too much magic answered to nothing but each other.

So when Sera Harrow grew into Regent Malvane, she understood something the poets never had: the problem was never *which* hands held the power. It was that any hands held it at all, unbound, unaccountable, answerable to nothing but appetite.

Her solution was not gentle. She would be the first to say so. But gentleness had been tried; gentleness had watched Harrowgate burn. Her solution was to rebuild the sigil whole — under one authority, fed by one discipline, binding all magic to one law. No more free magicians. No more wars of appetite. Every gift in the world, chained to order.

And if the price was memory — if the citizens of Ossuar sold their painful pasts and the provinces were pressed to sell more — well. Pain was the raw material of chaos. A people without griefs had no grievances. She had watched the city soften, year by year, into something pliable and almost peaceful, and she had told herself every morning that peace built on traded pain was still peace.

Most mornings she believed it.

* * *

The trap assembled itself over three days, the way good traps do — quietly, in pieces, each part innocent alone.

First, the bait. Odile's harvesters had finally taken Thornhollow's founding generation — not by force, which would have cost lives and made martyrs, but by quota law: the village had fallen three seasons behind, and the law was the law. Among the elders brought up the mountain road in the gray wagons was a woman named Maren Ostwick, ninety-one years old, keeper of the vale's oldest orchard, whose harvest assessment noted — with a harvester's dry precision — "unusually rich interior landscape; recommends priority processing."

Priority processing meant the engines. Priority processing meant that within a week, Maren Ostwick's ninety-one years would be poured into the sigil like wine into a cask, and the woman walking out the other side would smile pleasantly and remember nothing worth keeping.

Second, the leak. Malvane wrote, in her own hand, a requisition ordering the elder transferred to the deep vault for processing — and then made sure the requisition passed through three pairs of clerical hands, any one of which might be bought, blackmailed, or merely careless. If the speaker had eyes in her city, and the reliquary job proved they did, the paper would find them.

Third, the net. She moved two warden companies into the undercity on pretext of a smuggling sweep, doubled the watch on the memory-mines, and gave Warden-Captain Renn a single instruction: *let them come*. No alarms until the bait was taken. Then close every exit at once.

Cassian Vey she did not consult. His report on the reliquary breach had been technically flawless and somehow hollow — the kind of report a man writes when he is answering a question he hasn't asked himself yet. She had read it four times. Something in the margins of his language bothered her, some absence where conviction should have been. She marked him for observation and said nothing. A warden who doubts is more useful watched than confronted. Confronted, he chooses. Watched, he wavers — and wavering men stay put.

On the third evening she stood at the window of the Map Room and watched the lamps of Ossuar come alight below her, ten thousand flames, each one a household trading away its worst day for a quieter tomorrow.

Odile joined her at the glass. "The requisition moved today. Through the chancery clerks, as planned."

"And?"

"One copy is unaccounted for. Fourth desk, second row. A boy named Pell — nineteen, mother in the low quarters, debts." Odile's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "He'll sell it within a day. He always sells what he finds."

"Then we have until the day after tomorrow." Malvane watched a lamp flicker and steady. "Do you know what I admire about speakers, Odile?"

"I couldn't guess, Majesty."

"Their loyalty runs downhill. To beasts, to strangers, to villages that sealed their own gates against them. A speaker will walk into a warden-net for one old woman with an orchard." She turned from the window. "It's their weakness and it's magnificent, and it means they are entirely predictable. Love makes them legible."

"And when we have them? When the fragments are recovered and the sigil stands whole?"

Malvane was quiet a moment. Below, the city glowed, soft and forgetful.

"When the sigil stands whole," she said, "there will be no more speakers. Not hunted — *unnecessary.* No gifts outside the law, because there will be no outside. Every creature bound, every voice accounted for, every wild thing quiet." She thought of her mother's bees, and did not let her face move. "Order, Odile. Real order, at last. Nothing left to burn."

Odile bowed and went to finish the day's ledgers.

Alone, Malvane descended one final flight — past the guarded doors, past the engines humming their contentment — to the vault antechamber, where the half-built sigil hung in its cradle of brass and light. Two fragments now, recovered and set, their edges drinking glow from the engines. Three more scattered in the world, singing faintly to each other across the miles like separated birds.

She laid her palm flat against the ward-glass and felt it hunger. It had been hungry for twenty years, and she had fed it, and it had never once thanked her, and she loved it anyway — the way you love anything you've given your whole life to and can no longer imagine living without.

"Soon," she told it. "I know. Soon."

Behind her, far below in the processing levels, an old woman from a valley of forbidden speakers sat patiently under a lavender-scented lamp, waiting to be emptied, unaware that she had already become the most important person in the world.

Maren Ostwick. Keeper of the orchard. Ninety-one years of unbroken remembering.

Malvane climbed back toward the light, and began, in her head, composing the message she would send to the wardens when the trap closed:

*The speaker comes for love. Be ready.*

And in a rented room above a dye-shop in the undercity, a kestrel slept on a chair-back with her head under her wing, and a thief counted stolen coin by candlelight, and Zephyrine Ravensworth dreamed — without knowing why — of an orchard full of white blossom, and a voice like wind through branches saying, *come quickly, little listener. Come before they take the trees.*
