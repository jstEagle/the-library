## 13. The Trap at Thornhollow

The elder's name was Maren Ostrow, and Zephyrine had known her their whole life — the woman who had once looked the other way when a ten-year-old was caught whispering to chickens, who had said nothing, said *nothing*, when silence was the valley's whole religion. That debt sat in Zephyrine's chest like a stone as Cassian spread the map across Bracken's table and pinned its corners with a knife, a spoon, and Sorrel's talons.

"Thornhollow," he said. "It's not on any road. An old waystation from before the Order, half-swallowed by briars. The Regent's people will have picked it because it can be approached from only three directions, all of them open ground." He tapped the knife against the parchment. "Which is why this is a trap."

"Obviously," Bracken said, chewing something that might have been jerky or might have been his own boot leather. "So we don't go."

"She'll be executed at dawn," Zephyrine said quietly. "The heralds read it out in every square. *Speaker-taint sympathizer.* They're going to hang her for what I am."

"For what she *protected*," Cassian corrected, and there was something raw under the correction, some old wound of his own. "The Order doesn't execute for association, Zephyrine. It executes to make an example. She'll die so that every other old man and woman in the valley learns to keep their doors shut."

Zephyrine looked down at the map until their eyes burned. "Then we go."

"It's a trap."

"Yes."

"You understand that." Cassian's voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man handling something explosive. "You walk into Thornhollow, you walk into forty warden-blades and a commander whose orders are to take you alive, because your blood is worth more than the elder's."

"I understand."

"And you still—"

"*I understand.*" The beast-speech came up through Zephyrine's human throat without permission, a low harmonic that made the candle flames lean and made Sorrel shift her weight on the shutter-ledge and made even Bracken go still with the ancient unease of prey animals hearing a predator's language in a person's mouth. Zephyrine pulled it back, swallowed it, said again, plainly: "I understand. She kept my secret when keeping it could have ruined her. I'm not leaving her in a cage."

Silence. Then Bracken sighed the sigh of a man who had already lost an argument he hadn't finished having. "Fine. Fine. But if we're doing this, we do it my way, which means nobody walks into anything. We go in through the briars, low, at the change of watch. And Cass—" He jabbed a finger at the defector. "You wear your old colors. You walk up the front path like you've caught us yourself."

Cassian went very still. "That's desertion dressed as duty. If they look too closely—"

"They won't. You're Vey. Your reputation does the walking." Bracken grinned, sharp and joyless. "Besides, it's the one plan where everyone lies, which means it's honest work by city standards."

* * *

They came to Thornhollow in the blue hour before dawn, when the mist lay in the hollow like milk poured into a bowl.

Zephyrine crouched in the briars with Sorrel pressed flat against their shoulder, feathers trembling. Through the thorn-screen they could see the waystation's broken roofline, the gallows-frame already erected in the yard — they hadn't waited for dawn, then, or they wanted the spectacle ready — and around it, exactly as Cassian had promised, warden patrols moving in slow, confident arcs across the open ground. Forty, maybe more. Lantern-light swinging. The smell of horses who did not want to be here; Zephyrine heard their unease as a low continuous complaint, *wrong place wrong place wrong thing in the dark*.

And beneath it, thinner, threaded through the mist like a hair through cloth: a human voice, old and cracked, humming.

Maren Ostrow, waiting for the rope, humming a Founding Festival song.

Zephyrine's throat closed. Beside their ear, Sorrel whispered: *Small one. The old one sings. The old ones always sing when they are frightened. Birds do it too.*

*I know,* Zephyrine breathed back.

*Will you make the loud noise now?*

*Not yet. When Bracken moves. Not yet.*

Below and to the left, Cassian stepped out of the mist onto the front path in full warden black, torch held high, and his voice rang across the hollow with twenty years of command behind it: "Command post! I have the speaker and two accomplices taken at the ford! Bring chains — and bring the Regent's courier, I want this documented!"

Lanterns swung. Boots converged. The yard began to fill with men and women whose faces were lit orange and hungry, and Cassian walked into the middle of them like a stone sinking into water, and Zephyrine watched him lie with his whole body, watched him point back toward the briars, watched the trap begin — as planned — to spring toward *them*.

It worked for ninety seconds.

What broke it was a horse. One of the picketed patrol horses threw its head and screamed — not the sound of fear but the sound of a word, almost a word, the shape of *false false false* carried in an animal throat — and a young warden near the picket line frowned and said, too loudly, "Why's it doing that?" And Commander Sella Rhune, who had been standing beside the gallows with the patience of a spider, turned her head very slowly toward the briars and said:

"Because it can smell them."

*Run,* Zephyrine told Sorrel, and stood up out of the briars, because the plan was dead and the only thing left was to draw the blades away from the old woman.

* * *

What happened next, Zephyrine would remember afterward in fragments, the way you remember a storm — not as events but as pressures.

They ran along the briar-line shouting, hood thrown back, making themselves the loudest thing in the hollow, and it worked, the patrols wheeled toward them like water finding a channel, and Zephyrine got within thirty paces of the gallows-frame before the first crossbow bolt split the air where their head had been and forced them down behind a fallen lintel-stone. From there they could see Maren, roped to the platform's post, eyes wide and wet and fixed on Zephyrine with an expression that was half terror and half — worse — *hope*.

"Hold fire!" Commander Rhune's voice cracked across the yard. "Alive! Take the speaker alive!" And then, softer, to the wardens nearest the platform: "The old woman is no longer necessary."

No. No no no—

Zephyrine came up over the lintel-stone with both hands raised and let the beast-speech out at full open throttle for the first time in their life — not a whisper to a kestrel, not a murmur to rats, but a *shout*, a harmonic blast that hit the hollow like a wave off a lake in flood. Every horse screamed at once. Every dog on the picket lines went mad. Crows erupted from the thorns in a black sheet, and into that chaos Zephyrine poured words in the oldest language there was, the language older than human throats: *Fly. Fly. Fly.*

The crows went for the lanterns. The horses broke their pickets. In the boiling dark of the yard, Zephyrine sprinted for the platform, tore the ropes off Maren's wrists with a stolen knife, and shoved the old woman toward the briars, toward Bracken, who materialized out of the mist precisely where he'd sworn he'd be, catching Maren's arm and hauling her down and away.

Which left Zephyrine standing alone on the gallows-platform as the wardens closed from three sides.

Which was when the blade came.

It didn't come from the front. It came from the side, from a warden Zephyrine hadn't seen, a big man moving fast and silent out of the smoke of a dropped lantern, sword already committed to the thrust, aimed at Zephyrine's back — and Zephyrine was turning, turning too slowly, the world gone syrup-slow, watching the point of it come on like a star falling—

—and Bracken came out of nowhere.

He didn't shout. He didn't do anything clever. He simply stepped between, the way you'd step between a child and a runaway cart, arms half-open, and the blade went into him below the collarbone and came back out red.

Bracken looked down at it with genuine offense, like a man discovering a pickpocket. "Oh," he said. "*Oh, that's not fair.*"

Then his knees went, and Zephyrine caught him, and they went down together onto the boards of the gallows-platform, in the exact spot where Maren Ostrow was supposed to have died.

"No no no — Bracken — stay with me —"

"'S fine," Bracken said, and laughed, wet and horrible. "Told you... nobody walks into anything. Nobody *listens*, that's the problem with this crew..."

The wardens were coming. Zephyrine could hear them coming, boots on planks, and their hands were full of thief and blood, and there was nowhere, nowhere, *nowhere* —

*Sorrel.*

The kestrel had been holding station overhead through all of it — Zephyrine felt her now, a bright furious spark above the smoke, waiting, *waiting*, held back by the last thread of the old rule, *stay small, stay hidden, stay safe*. And Zephyrine looked down at Bracken's face going gray, and at the wardens' blades coming up the steps, and let the last thread go.

"Sorrel," they said aloud, in front of forty humans, in a voice that was not entirely a human voice, "*call them.*"

*All of them?* Her thought hit like a struck bell. *The small one means — everything?*

*Everything. Everyone. NOW.*

Sorrel tore free of the sky like a thrown spear and rose, rose, rose until she was a speck against the graying dawn, and she opened her throat and sang — and it was not a kestrel's cry. It was the summons. The one the wild things had passed beak to wing since the world was young, the call that meant *war*, that had not been sung, Zephyrine understood in that moment, since the last time magicians had tried to own the world.

The hollow answered.

Out of the thorn-banks came the foxes, low and fast. Out of the ditches came the badgers, and the stoats, and the weasels, a river of small teeth pouring toward the warden lines. The freed horses wheeled and charged — not ridden, *choosing* — and the picket dogs turned on their handlers with hearts broken and muzzles closed, herding them, knocking them down, refusing — refusing — to let blades reach the platform. Crows fell out of the sky at lanterns and eyes and sword-hilts. Somewhere a stag bellowed in the mist like a war-horn, and the whole gray hollow filled with the sound of the world's oldest army arriving late to a war it had never agreed to stop fighting.

The wardens broke. Not all at once — but a line of men who trained all their lives to fight humans had no answer for the ground itself rising against them. They scattered, they ran, they dropped swords and fled into the mist, and Commander Rhune stood alone in the churning yard with her face white and set, looking up at the speck of the kestrel, and then at Zephyrine kneeling in blood on the gallows, and Zephyrine saw her mouth form a single word before she turned and went:

*Heresy.*

Or maybe *hope*. At that distance, in that light, it was impossible to say.

* * *

They got Bracken out. That was the whole of the victory, and it had to be enough.

Maren rode double with Cassian, wrapped in Cassian's own cloak, weeping silently and singing snatches of the festival song between the sobs. Bracken lay across the cart-horse's back — the horse from the road, the one whose voice Zephyrine had given back, the one who had come at Sorrel's summons from miles away at a gallop that ate the distance like it owed him money — and his blood soaked into the horse's gray hide and the horse did not complain once.

Zephyrine walked beside him with one hand pressed hard on the wound, and spoke to him the whole way, human words, useless words, *stay awake, stay angry, you're too mean to die* — and once, when his eyes rolled back, they leaned close and used the other language, the forbidden one, pouring it straight into his ear: *Hold on. You hear me? Hold. On.*

And Bracken, delirious, half-conscious, answered in a slurred mumble that wasn't beast-speech at all but was somehow, impossibly, in rhythm with it: "...heard that... first time y'talked t'the rats... knew you'd get me killed..."

"That was *months* ago and you *offered*—"

"...still holdin' you responsible..."

He stayed awake. Barely. Long enough for the farmhouse — a widow's farm two valleys over, a woman who owed Bracken three favors and asked zero questions — long enough for the stitching, the poultices, the fever that came at dusk like it had been hired to. Zephyrine sat by his bed through the worst of it, Sorrel a warm weight on their shoulder, watching the thief fight for breath and cursing himself between gasps with a creativity that was, honestly, reassuring.

Near midnight, his eyes opened, found Zephyrine, and focused with effort.

"Zeph." A whisper. "The birds. The birds came."

"They came."

"You called an army." His hand found theirs, gripped with what strength he had. "You called an *army* for me."

"Don't flatter yourself. I called it for the horses. You were collateral."

His laugh turned into a cough, and the cough cost him, and when he could speak again his voice was different. Smaller. "Zeph. There were wardens running. Scared out of their skins. And all I could think was — she knows now. The Regent knows what you are. What you can *do*. There's no hiding after tonight. No more sneaking. She's going to stop hunting you like a criminal and start hunting you like a *war*."

Outside the window, the night was full of sounds it hadn't been full of yesterday — wings settling, hooves stamping, the low breath of creatures that had decided, without being asked, to stand guard over a wounded thief and a tired speaker.

Zephyrine listened to them and felt the truth of Bracken's warning settle over the room like frost.

Because he was right. Something had ended at Thornhollow, and something else had begun. The gift had been a secret, then a crime, then a weapon — and weapons get met with wars. Somewhere south, in her gleaming city atop its hill of stolen memories, Malvane would be receiving the report. Would be learning that the beasts had chosen a side. Would be reaching, Zephyrine had no doubt, for the one answer the Regent had left: finish the sigil, feed it whole, and end this the way the first magicians should never have been allowed to begin it.

Sorrel shifted on Zephyrine's shoulder and spoke softly, for their ears alone.

*Small one. The wild things are asking a question. They are all asking it.*

*What question?*

*They ask:* she paused, and Zephyrine felt her choose the words with a care she rarely spent, *they ask whether tomorrow you will lead them south.*

Zephyrine looked at Bracken's gray face, at the bandages rising and falling, at the window full of waiting eyes.

"Not tomorrow," they said aloud, to the bird, to the room, to the listening dark. "But soon."

Far to the south, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, Zephyrine imagined they could feel the sigil growing — hungry, half-assembled, and closer now to waking.
