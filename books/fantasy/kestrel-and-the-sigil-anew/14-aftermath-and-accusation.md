## 14. Aftermath and Accusation

The fever came in colors.

That was the first thing Bracken understood about dying, or almost dying, or whatever this was — that pain had a palette, and his ran the whole ugly spectrum. Red for the wound in his side where the warden's blade had gone in. Yellow for the poultice Sorrel's... no, Zephyrine's hands had packed into it, some valley recipe that smelled like crushed pine and old honey. And a deep bruised violet underneath everything, spreading outward from the place where the sigil fragment sat wrapped in oilcloth beneath his head, because Zephyrine had insisted on keeping it close and he'd insisted on using it as a pillow out of pure spite.

He dreamed.

Not the ordinary fever-dreams of a thief — the falling dreams, the running dreams, the one where every lock in Ossuar opened at his touch and every door behind it was empty. These were older. Heavier. Dreams with weight to them, like stones dropped down a well, and he fell after them into a world that smelled of rain on hot stone.

* * *

He stood on a hill that did not exist anymore, in a time that had never been given a name, and the grass came up past his knees and there were people all around him who were not quite people. They were taller. Their eyes caught light like an animal's do, holding the lantern-glow of it long after the flame moved on. They stood in a great ring on the hilltop, hundreds of them, thousands, and in the center of the ring lay a stone the size of a fallen star, veined with light that breathed.

*This is what I made you,* said a voice that was not a voice, that came from everywhere, from the grass and the stone and Bracken's own borrowed chest. *A covenant, not a crown. The sigil is not power. It is promise.*

And Bracken — wearing someone else's body, someone vast and patient — understood it the way you understand your own heartbeat. The sigil had never been a weapon. It was a knot tied between all living things, a binding of mutual restraint: magic flowed through everything, but through the sigil it agreed to hold its shape, to keep from devouring the weak. The beasts could speak because the covenant let their voices cross over. The magicians of the first age drew their art from the same well as the sparrow's song and the wolf's patience, and none of them owned it. Everyone drank. No one hoarded.

*But,* said the voice, and the sky darkened at the edges like paper held near flame, *a promise is only as strong as the weakest hand that holds it.*

He watched it happen. That was the cruelty of the dream — he couldn't look away. The tall people with lantern-eyes began to notice that the well gave more to those who drew deeply. A woman whose name was a sound like wind through reeds learned to pull the covenant's flow into herself and hold it, and when she held it, she was glorious, and when she was glorious, others wanted to be glorious too. They called themselves magicians then, the first ones, and they stopped drinking from the well and started building walls around it.

The beasts went quiet first. Not mute — muted. The covenant fraying thread by thread wherever a magician's will pressed against it. Then the wars. Bracken watched cities burn in a single night, watched rivers run backward, watched the tall people turn their lantern-eyes on each other and go out one by one like a festival ending. And in the last hour of the last battle, the survivors — what few remained of both sides — took the great breathing stone and broke it, deliberately, into fragments, and scattered them across the world so that no one hand could ever gather the promise again.

*We broke it ourselves,* the voice said, and now it sounded tired, ancient, unbearably sad. *Better shattered than enslaved. Better silence than a master's mouthpiece. We hid the fragments and we paid the price: the age of hoarded magic began, and the beasts' voices faded from human hearing, and only the rarest children — the speakers — kept the old ear.*

Bracken tried to speak, to ask who the voice belonged to, and the dream turned its attention on him like a lighthouse swinging round.

*You are not the one I waited for,* it said, almost gently. *But you carry her blood-memory now, thief. You touched the fragment. You slept beside it. So listen, and carry this back to her:*

*The sigil does not want to be restored. It wants to be finished differently. There is a third door, and she has already seen it in her dreams, though she has not told you. Ask her why.*

* * *

He woke gasping, drenched, the violet pain flaring red again, and the first thing he saw was Sorrel perched on the bedframe watching him with one tilted eye, and the second thing he saw was Zephyrine asleep in a chair pulled up against the wall, upright even in sleep, the way soldiers sleep and guilty people sleep.

They were in a safehouse Bracken had used exactly once before, three years ago, a smuggler's loft above a tannery in the river district of a town whose name he'd already forgotten. Cassian Vey had carried him here. That fact alone should have been enough to make him distrust everyone in the room forever. The defector sat by the window with his back to the room, which was either tact or surveillance, and Bracken hadn't decided which yet.

"Water," he rasped.

Sorrel launched off the bedframe, and a moment later Zephyrine was awake and moving, bringing the cup, helping him lift his head, and their hands were steady and their face was careful and Bracken thought: *there it is. The careful face. The one you wear when you're hiding something.*

"How long?"

"Four days." Zephyrine's voice was rough with disuse. "The blade missed anything vital. You'll keep."

"I always keep." He drank. Set the cup down. Looked at them — really looked, the way he looked at a lock before picking it, reading the mechanism, finding the flaw. "You knew."

Zephyrine went very still. Even Sorrel stopped preening.

"The fragment," Bracken said. "When you touch them, they show you things. I know because I just spent four days having one poured into my skull through a pillow, so don't tell me I'm imagining it. And in the dream — the origin, the covenant, the breaking — there was a voice, and it said—" he stopped, made himself say it plainly, because plainness was a blade too, "—it said you've already seen the third door. In your dreams. And you haven't told me. So. Have you?"

The silence stretched. Outside, a cart rattled past on cobbles, and somewhere below the tannery vats turned over with a wet heavy sound.

"Yes," Zephyrine said finally.

Bracken laughed, and it hurt, and he laughed anyway. "Of course. Of course you have. How many times? How many fragments have you picked up and how many times has something spoken to you and how many times have you decided — alone, in that noble skull of yours — what the rest of us get to know?"

"Bracken—"

"No. Let me finish, I've been rehearsing this since the fever broke." He pushed himself up against the wall, and the wound screamed, and he ignored it because anger was a better medicine. "I bled for you. I took steel meant for you in a trap you walked into because a captured elder was bait, and fine, we rescue people, that's the job, I signed on. But I signed on thinking we were gathering pieces of a weapon before Malvane gets it. That's the deal. That's the shape of the thing. And now the dream tells me the weapon isn't a weapon, it's a *covenant*, and there's a third option nobody mentioned, and you've been sitting on it since — when? Thornhollow? Before?"

"Before," Zephyrine said quietly. "Since the drowned chapel."

"Since the drowned chapel." He nodded slowly. "So two fragments ago. Two fragments of my life you've known there was another way and didn't say."

"It wasn't certain. It still isn't. The voice in the dream — the fragment-memories — they don't give instructions, they give..." Zephyrine's hands moved, searching for words, and Bracken realized with a strange distant pity that this was the most articulate person he'd ever met and they were failing at speech. "They give weight. Direction. Like a current under a boat. I thought if I named it too early, I'd talk myself into believing it, or talk myself out of it. And either way I'd be choosing for everyone based on a feeling."

"So instead you chose for everyone based on nothing. Much better."

"Bracken." Zephyrine crossed the room and crouched by the bed, eye level, and their eyes were the worst part, because they weren't defensive. They were wrecked. "Every fragment costs me something. You've watched it happen. My mother's face. My name-day. Sorrel's first word. Do you understand what that does to a person? It makes you afraid to plan. It makes you afraid to *hope out loud*, because hope is a memory you haven't lost yet. I held the third door inside me like a coal because it was the only thing the fragments hadn't burned."

"That's not strategy," Bracken said. "That's just loneliness with better vocabulary."

Zephyrine flinched like he'd struck them.

Good, some mean small part of him thought, and then immediately felt sick about it, because the fever was still in him and it made everything too sharp. He dragged a hand down his face. "Look. I'm angry at the wrong people and I know it. I'm angry at the Order for harvesting memories out of children, I'm angry at Malvane for building a god out of stolen pasts, I'm angry at Cassian for four days of useful silence over there by the window — no offense."

"None taken," Cassian said, without turning around. "It's deserved."

"But mostly," Bracken went on, quieter now, the anger burning down to coals, "I'm angry at you, because you're the only person in this whole rotten story who's actually good, and good people keep deciding that goodness means carrying things alone. It doesn't. It never has. That's how the first magicians started — the dream showed me that too. They noticed the well gave more to those who drew deep, and they drew deep, and they told themselves it was *responsibility*. Somebody has to hold it. Somebody has to decide. And a hundred years later the beasts went quiet and the cities burned." He pointed at Zephyrine, finger unsteady. "You are one secret away from becoming the thing we're fighting. Do you hear me? One. Secret. Away."

The room was silent except for the tannery turning over below, and Sorrel's talons shifting on the bedframe, and Bracken's own ragged breath.

Then Sorrel did something she had never done in all the weeks Bracken had known her. She hopped down onto the blanket, walked up the length of his chest — each step a small deliberate pressure on the wound, which he bore with gritted teeth — and fixed him with both fierce golden eyes, and spoke.

"He is right," she said. Her voice in beast-speech was like wind through a keyhole, thin and sharp and absolutely clear. "I have carried the same coal. I heard the third door in the chapel water. I let Zephyrine keep it, because a kestrel knows what it is to be small and hunted, and secrets are small things you can hide under your wing." Her head dipped. "But wings are for flying, not hiding. No more coals. No more doors kept shut. If we die at the vault, we die knowing the whole shape of the thing."

Zephyrine closed their eyes. When they opened them, something had come loose — Bracken could see it, the way you see a knot give.

"All right," they said. "All right. No more coals." They sat down on the edge of the bed, and began, low and steady, to tell him everything: the current under the boat, the sense that the sigil didn't want restoring but *unbinding*, that the fragments whispered of scattering the power into every living thing rather than handing it to any hand at all — not victory, renunciation. Magic belonging to no one. To everyone.

"And the cost?" Bracken asked, when they finished. Because there was always a cost; the dream had taught him that much. "What does that door charge at the threshold?"

Zephyrine was quiet a moment. Sorrel had gone still on his chest.

"My gift," they said. "All of it. Permanently. I'd never hear a beast again. Never hear her." A nod toward the kestrel. "Silence, for everyone else's freedom."

Bracken looked at the speaker, at the kestrel, at the defector by the window who had finally turned around, his face unreadable and his eyes not.

"Well," he said at last, and swung his legs off the bed despite the screaming wound, because lying down suddenly felt like surrender. "Then we'd better win the argument with Malvane before she finishes her god. How long do we have?"

Cassian crossed to the table and spread the vault maps, and his voice, when it came, was flat and cold and certain.

"Three days. Maybe four. The Regent's harvest-carts left Ossuar yesterday — heading for the valley."
