## 15. Cassian's Choice

The summons came at dawn, and Cassian Vey had known it would come for eleven days.

He'd counted them the way a condemned man counts stairs. Eleven days since he'd stood in the undercity of Ossuar with his hounds' teeth an inch from a speaker's throat and watched those same hounds sit down in the filth like puppies and refuse. Eleven days since he'd filed a report saying the taint-trail had gone cold at the river, drowned out by tannery runoff, no further pursuit recommended.

Eleven days of waiting for the Order to notice that Warden Cassian Vey, decorated hunter of speaker-taint, seventeen years of service, had lied.

They noticed on the twelfth day.

* * *

The summons was delivered by a boy acolyte who wouldn't meet his eyes, which told Cassian everything before he opened the envelope. Wardens were summoned by horn or by word of mouth. Paper meant procedure. Procedure meant someone above him wanted a record of it.

The Orderhouse at Ossuar's crown was built like a fist closing around the sky — black basalt, narrow windows, the sigil's new emblem hammered into every door: not the old seven-pointed covenant-star but a single closed eye inside a triangle. The Regent's mark. The mark of watching.

Cassian climbed the stairs to the Hall of Inquiry with his boots ringing on stone, and he made himself notice things, because noticing was the last discipline he trusted. The corridor smelled of lye and hot metal. Two wardens he'd trained stood guard at the doors and looked through him as if he were already a ghost. And beneath the lye, faint but unmistakable, the sweetish char of memory-burn — the smell the harvesting engines left on everything they touched, like a candle snuffed too close to the wick.

Inquisitor Halven Roke waited for him alone at the center of the hall, standing beneath the great eye-and-triangle so that its shadow fell across his shoulders like a yoke.

"Warden Vey." Roke's voice was mild. That was the worst thing about Roke; he was always mild. "Seventeen years. Three hundred and six confirmed cleansings. You found more tainted blood than any three wardens of your generation."

"I did my work," Cassian said.

"You did." Roke turned a thin folio over in his hands. "And then, twelve days ago, you stopped doing it. A confirmed speaker-taint, witnessed by two hounds in active pursuit, and your report says—" he consulted the folio without appearing to read it, "—'trail lost to industrial contamination.' Tannery runoff." He looked up. His eyes were pale gray, the color of dishwater, and they held nothing cruel and nothing kind. "You've walked through tannery runoff, Warden. You know what it does to a scent-line. It doesn't erase it. It thickens it. Any handler worth his salt could have pushed through."

"The hounds couldn't," Cassian said. "Their noses burned. I wasn't going to ruin three years of training to chase a ghost."

"Your hounds." Roke set the folio down on the table between them, precisely, square to the edge. "Yes. Let's discuss your hounds."

* * *

They brought the hounds into the hall on chains, and Cassian understood then how badly he had miscalculated, because the Order didn't need his confession. They had something better. They had the beasts.

Bracken-hound and Sorrel-hound — no, their names were Ash and Marrow; he'd named them himself as pups, and the names felt suddenly obscene — sat when the handlers pressed them down, but their ears were flat, and they would not look at Cassian. He knew what that meant. Every handler knew what that meant. A hound that won't look at you is a hound that has been told something about you.

"Memory-bound," Roke said, circling them slowly. "You know the process better than I do, I imagine. We take the beast's own small life — its puppyhood, its first hunt, its love of its handler — and we burn it out, and in the space we put obedience. Clean, total, cheap. The Regent calls it efficiency." He stopped in front of Ash. "But binding leaves residue. Echoes. And echoes can be *read*, Warden, by anyone with the proper instruments. Do you know what we found in your hounds' echoes?"

Cassian said nothing.

"*Words*," Roke said softly. "Words in a voice no beast should know. Words spoken to them in the undercity, twelve days ago, by the very taint you failed to catch. Your hounds heard a speaker address them — kindly, if the echo-tone is to be believed — and they chose to obey that voice over yours." He crouched, peering into Ash's flat, unhappy eyes. "Which means either your hounds are compromised beyond reclamation, or their handler is. And hounds are replaceable."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far below, Cassian could hear the engines turning, the great slow heartbeat of the harvest, and he thought: *they're burning someone's mother right now. Someone's whole childhood. And it sounds like a mill.*

"Warden Vey," Roke said, rising. "By order of the Regent-Protector Malvane, acting through the Holy Order of the Sigil Anew, you are hereby remanded for cognitive cleansing. Full harvest. Your term of service, your family record, your name — all of it will be rendered to the sigil for the protection of the realm." He almost smiled. "You'll feel lighter. Everyone does."

"They'll make me a stranger to myself," Cassian said. "That's not cleansing. That's murder with the body left walking."

"It's mercy," Roke said. "It's the kindest thing this Order does. Now — will you walk down yourself, or shall we carry you?"

Cassian looked at his hounds. Ash had lifted her head at last, and she was looking at him now, and there was something in her face that he had spent three years teaching himself not to see because seeing it made the work impossible: she was afraid, and she was asking him what to do.

"Walk," Cassian said. "I'll walk."

* * *

They took his weapons, his badge, his boots — protocol, they said, for the harvested — and marched him down through the Orderhouse's spine toward the engine floors. The stairwell went down farther than any building should. The air grew warm and sweet with that candle-snuff smell, and the walls began to hum, and Cassian walked with his hands loose at his sides and his mind moving fast and cold, the way it moved on a hunt.

Because here was the thing Roke didn't know. Here was the thing no one in the Order knew, because Cassian had never told anyone, because telling would have ended him seventeen years ago:

He could hear them too.

Not speak — never speak; his gift was a deaf man's window onto a noisy street, one-way, faint, a curse rather than a craft. But since he was a boy, Cassian Vey had heard the animals. Their fear, mostly. Their fear had been the soundtrack of his entire career — every cleansing, every capture, every hound he'd bound, he'd heard exactly what the beasts thought of him, and he had learned to call that hearing *instinct* and that shame *duty*.

And twelve days ago, in the undercity, he had heard something he'd never heard before. The rats along the walls had been chattering about the speaker — not fear. *Praise.* The stray dogs had come out of the dark to look at her — at *him*, the tall one, the one who moved like a hunted thing and still stopped to press a hand to a hurt cat — and the dogs had said, in their simple, hungry way, *safe, safe, that one is safe.*

His hounds hadn't refused him because they were compromised. They'd refused him because for the first time in their bound lives, they'd been given a choice, and they'd chosen kindness.

Cassian reached the bottom of the stairs. The engine floor opened around him: a cathedral of brass and heat, a hundred harvesting chairs in rows, each one cradling a citizen with electrodes at the temples and a dreamy, grateful smile. The memories ran in silver threads from their skulls up into the great central drum, where the sigil drank.

And beside the door to the archive wing — he saw it, because he'd always seen everything, because noticing was the last discipline he trusted — hung a ring of keys, and beside the keys, a courier satchel marked with the vault-route seal, waiting for the morning run.

The guards flanking him were young. One of them kept glancing at the engines with the expression of a man at his first execution.

Cassian heard the chair-beasts — the Order kept rats in the walls everywhere, and the rats here were old and half-mad with everything they'd overheard — and the oldest rat, the one missing an ear, said clearly, in its dry rusted voice: *keys. keys. the tall sad one sees the keys.*

"I see them," Cassian murmured, under the engine-hum, and the rat's whiskers twitched with something like delight.

Then the nearest guard stepped forward with the headband for the first chair, and Cassian Vey, hunter of speakers, made his choice.

* * *

Afterward, he could never have said how long it took. Minutes. An hour. Time went strange in the engine-cathedral, measured in screams that weren't quite screams.

He broke the young guard's nose with his elbow and took his baton. He dropped the second guard with a strike he'd used a hundred times on fleeing speakers and hated himself for using here. He tore the key-ring off its hook, swept the courier satchel over his shoulder, and — because the moment demanded it, because some door in him had finally swung all the way open — he ripped the headbands off the nearest three chairs.

The citizens slumped, blinking, half-emptied, and the oldest rat screamed along the walls in a voice like tearing paper: *run run run the tall sad one runs,* and every beast in the building took up the cry, dogs in the kennels, pigeons on the roof, the hounds in their pens, until the Orderhouse rang with a sound the wardens had no protocol for.

Ash and Marrow burst from the kennel block as he crossed the yard — the rat had reached them, or the panic had, or perhaps they'd simply been waiting — and fell in at his heels, unchained, unbound in spirit if not yet in flesh, running with him not as tools but as pack.

Behind him, alarms. Ahead, the gray streets of Ossuar at dawn, and beyond the city, the hills, and somewhere in the hills—

Somewhere in the hills, a camp of beasts and thieves and one defector-to-be, and a speaker whose blood he'd hunted and whose kindness his hounds had chosen.

Cassian ran.

* * *

He found them four days later, or they found him — it was hard to say which, because the wild things had been tracking him since the city gates, and Zephyrine Ravensworth had known he was coming since the second night, when the crows started following his fire-smoke and reporting his progress in their flat, gossiping voices.

He came over the ridge at dusk with his hands empty and raised, his hounds at heel, the courier satchel heavy against his back, and found himself looking down at a camp that should not have existed: a thief whittling by the fire, a kestrel on a perch made of a broken spear, a cart-horse grazing loose, and a dozen other shapes in the treeline that his eyes kept sliding off — wild things, come to watch.

Zephyrine rose from the fire. Thinner than the reports suggested. Eyes old. On their shoulder, the kestrel went rigid, feathers tight, staring at him with a raptor's perfect hatred.

"That's close enough," Bracken said, and the knife appeared in his hand without seeming to move.

"Cassian Vey," Cassian said. "Warden, First Order of the Sigil Anew. Formerly." He knelt, slowly, and set the satchel on the ground between them, and opened it. "Maps. Vault-routes. Engine schematics. And this." He drew out a ledger, bound in gray leather, stamped with the eye-and-triangle. "The harvest registry. Every memory taken in the last three years, itemized. Names. Districts. What was taken and why." He looked up at Zephyrine. "Page forty-one. Cross-referenced against the valley census the Order stole eight years ago. You'll find your elders' names. Your neighbors'. The wards that protected your valley weren't fed by volunteers."

Silence. The fire cracked. The kestrel's talons bit wood.

"You hunted my kind for seventeen years," Zephyrine said quietly.

"Yes."

"And your hounds refused you because of me."

"Because of what you did in the undercity. You spoke to them. No one speaks to them." His voice roughened despite himself. "Do you know what it's like to hear fear your whole life? To be *made* of it? And then to hear, once, one time, a voice saying *safe*?" He shook his head. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to use me. I know the vault beneath Ossuar better than anyone alive outside the Order. I know which corridors flood, which locks answer to which lies, and I know the schedule of the Rite the Regent is preparing — because I stole it off Roke's own desk while his guards were bleeding." He paused. "She means to finish the sigil within the month. When she does, every mind in this country becomes hers to edit."

Zephyrine studied him for a long moment. Then they glanced at the kestrel, and something passed between them — a question asked and answered in silence, though Cassian caught the edge of it, the way you catch weather on the wind: *do we believe him?*

Sorrel's answer was a single sharp note.

The horse blew air through its lips. The wild things in the trees shifted. And Zephyrine held out their hand — not to help him up, but palm-up, open, the way you offer a hand to an animal that might bite.

"Welcome to the losing side, Warden," they said. "We were just planning how to rob the most fortified place in the world."

Cassian took the hand and rose. Around the fire, the thief grinned, the horse stamped, and high overhead, unseen, the crows turned back toward the city carrying word to every living thing that the hunter had crossed over.

And in the satchel at Cassian's feet, the ledger lay open to page forty-one, where the first stolen name was written in the Regent's own hand:

*Valley of Kestrels — wards fed, per contract — 214 souls, initial withdrawal complete.*

Two hundred and fourteen souls. And the Rite was less than a month away.
