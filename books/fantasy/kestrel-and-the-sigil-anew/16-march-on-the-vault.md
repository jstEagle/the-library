## 16. March on the Vault

**ZEPHYRINE**

They left Thornhollow before dawn, because Zephyrine had learned that everything worth doing in this broken country had to be done in the dark.

The company was eleven souls and one kestrel and it did not look like an army. It looked like what it was: a speaker with holes where their memories used to be, a thief still gray-faced from a wound that should have killed him, a defector warden in a stolen miller's coat, and a loose parade of beasts that no one had summoned and no one could turn away — two of Cassian's hounds, who had chosen their side the night they refused to bite; the cart-horse Bramble from the road of quiet beasts, whose voice Zephyrine had given back and who had apparently decided to follow them to the end of the world in payment; a one-eyed crow named Tatter; three feral cats who answered to nothing and no one but walked with them anyway.

Cassian counted heads at the treeline and said, quietly, "This is not how wars are won."

"No," Zephyrine said. "Wars are lost like this. We're doing something else."

He didn't argue. He'd stopped arguing somewhere on the second day, after the hounds slept against his back for the first time since their binding broke, and he'd wept into their fur like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was there.

The land got worse as they went east. That was the thing no one warned you about — you could see the harvest happening. Villages they passed stood whole and painted and wrong, their people smiling in doorways with the flat shine of the recently trimmed. In one town a woman was watering her garden and humming, and when Zephyrine asked her the name of the town she beamed and said, "I don't remember, isn't that wonderful? They took the sad part out."

"What was the sad part?" Zephyrine asked.

Her smile didn't move. "There isn't one," she said. "That's the point."

Bramble shuddered under Zephyrine's hand. *They cut them,* the horse said, low. *Like hair. Like it grows back.*

"It doesn't," Zephyrine said. "That's the other point."

Sorrel rode the wind above them all day, scouting, and came down at dusk with her wings held stiff and her voice scraped thin. *Roads full of wagons going toward the city. Not carrying grain. Carrying jars.*

Jars. Zephyrine had seen the memory-jars in Ossuar's lower markets, pale light sloshing inside glass like drowned moons. The Regent wasn't waiting for volunteers anymore. The countryside was being stripped like a field after harvest, every village a stubble-field of amputated pasts, and all of it flowing downhill to the vault beneath the capital, feeding the sigil whole.

"How long do we have?" Bracken asked. He'd come up beside them without sound, which was his way even wounded, especially wounded.

Zephyrine looked at the horizon, where Ossuar's hill glowed faintly gold against the coming night — lit from below, lit by everything it had eaten.

"Less than we need," they said. "We walk faster."

* * *

**BRACKEN**

Bracken had stolen a lot of things in his life. Purses, pies, a bishop's ring, once, off a sleeping man's finger while the man was dreaming loudly about soup. He had never stolen a war.

It felt about the same, actually. You watched the mark, you found the seam, you went in quiet and came out rich or dead. The only difference was the size of the room.

His shoulder ached where the warden's blade had gone through. The fever was gone but the weakness stayed, sitting on him like a wet coat, and he hated it, hated being the slow one, the one the others waited for. So he made himself useful the only way he knew: he listened.

In the taproom of a waystation inn two nights out from the city, wearing a hood and a borrowed limp, he listened to carter talk. Carters always talked. Carters were the sewers of information; everything in the country eventually drained through them.

What he heard turned his stomach cold.

"They're paying now," a carter said, slapping coins on the table. "Not much. But coin's coin. You drive your neighbors to the collection station, you get silver. You bring your own memories, you get double."

"Bring your own," another said, and laughed without any humor in it. "My wife sold her mother's funeral. Said she couldn't sleep for grieving. Now she sleeps fine and doesn't know why she ever cried." He drank. "She looks at me sometimes like she's trying to place me. Like I'm a coat she wore one winter."

"And the wagons go up the hill," the first said, "and the hill glows, and the Regent's criers say the age of chaos is ending. Say magic'll be safe soon. Contained. Ordered."

*Contained.* Bracken turned the word over. He'd grown up in Ossuar's undercity, where contained meant the debt-prisons and the tanner's row and every wall built to keep someone in or out. He knew what containment cost and who paid it.

He slipped out the back and found Zephyrine sitting with the hounds in the yard, teaching them — actually teaching them, hand-signs and spoken words both, like students — and for a moment he just watched. A month ago he'd caught this person talking to rats in an alley and thought: madwoman, or spy, or both. Now he thought: the only honest thing left in this country.

"You look like bad news," Zephyrine said without turning around.

"I'm a thief. I'm always bad news." He sat down on the mounting block. "Collection stations. All along the eastern roads. They're paying people to sell themselves now, and paying others to drag the reluctant. The vault's being fed faster than we planned. Whatever timeline Cassian's maps gave us, cut it in half."

Zephyrine was quiet a moment. Firelight moved on their face, and Bracken saw again the strange geography of them — the places where memories had been paid out like rope, the smooth blank spots behind the eyes. He'd begged them to stop after the third fragment. He'd begged ugly, with swearing. They hadn't stopped, and he understood why, and he hated that he understood.

"There's something else," he said. "The carter mentioned the mines. Under the city. Where they process what comes in. He said they've started calling it the Churn." He paused. "And he said the workers there don't come out. Not fired. Just — don't come out."

"The memory-mines." Zephyrine's jaw tightened. "That's our way in. Cassian's maps show a drainage gallery off the old aqueduct."

"Then we'd best get there before whatever's in the Churn gets fat enough to need a bigger pot." Bracken stood, and his shoulder screamed, and he ignored it the way he ignored most things. "Get some sleep, speaker. Tomorrow we steal a war."

* * *

**CASSIAN**

Cassian Vey had spent nineteen years believing that order was the highest good, because he had seen disorder. He had seen the borderlands burn in the last flare of the old war's leftovers; he had seen what unbound speakers could do when they broke. The Order had taken him in at fourteen, an orphan of exactly that kind of fire, and had given him hounds and rules and a purpose, and he had never once questioned the leash until the night his own hounds refused to bite.

Now he walked in a miller's coat with a forged pass in his pocket, carrying the Order's secrets in his head like a knife between his teeth, and he questioned everything.

At night he wrote. Not reports — those were done, those were ash — but a ledger. Every theft he'd helped conceal, every harvest order he'd countersigned, every village number that had crossed his desk labeled *relocation* when it meant *stripping*. Names where he could find them. Numbers where he couldn't. If he died on this road, the ledger would die with him, and that was unacceptable, so he wrote small and slept badly and kept the pages oiled inside his coat.

The hounds — Wren and Sorrel-son, though the second name made the kestrel puff up indignantly every time — had changed since their binding broke. They dreamed audibly now, whining and paddling, and once Cassian woke to find Wren watching him with an expression he could only call consideration. Being considered by a dog was a new experience. He found he minded it less than being obeyed by one.

On the fourth night, the crow brought warning.

It simply dropped out of the dark onto his bedroll, tapped twice, and said — in a voice like a hinge, because the crow had learned words the hard way, by listening to men — "Lights. East. Many."

Cassian was up before the echo faded. From the ridge he saw it: a column of lantern-light winding along the valley road toward Ossuar, half a mile of it, wagons and walkers and mounted wardens flanking the flanks. A harvest procession. And threaded through its middle, thin as a wire, a sound that reached even this distance on the night air.

Singing. Hundreds of voices singing the Regent's anthem, bright and hollow, the way people sing when the part of them that would refuse has been carefully removed.

"They're driving them," Zephyrine said at his shoulder. Their voice was very flat. "Whole villages. To the Churn."

"Yes."

"We could hit the rear guard. Free the tail end—"

"With forty blades against ours?" He shook his head. "You'd free thirty and doom the rest. The wardens would seal the column and accelerate the processing. I know the doctrine. I helped write it." Saying it aloud tasted like copper.

Zephyrine stared at the lights a long time. Cassian watched them decide not to do the merciful, suicidal thing, and watched what it cost, and thought: *this is what command is. No wonder I was never promoted.*

"Then we go under them," Zephyrine said finally. "Straight to the aqueduct. If the sigil finishes, none of those people get anything back anyway. If we win—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "If we win, everyone gets everything back. That's the whole point. That's the covenant."

*Covenant.* The word the thief used in his fever-dreams, shouting at the ceiling. Cassian had read the old texts; he'd been trained on the sanitized versions. But he'd also spent years in the vault archives, and he remembered a sealed folio he'd once been ordered to catalog and never discuss — a fragment of pre-war script, older than the Order, older than the sigil itself. He'd assumed it was heresy.

He was beginning to suspect it was a receipt.

"Tomorrow," he said. "We reach the aqueduct tomorrow. After that, speaker, I can't map it for you. Below the Churn, even my knowledge ends."

"Then we listen," Zephyrine said, and put two fingers to their lips, and whistled — a small, specific whistle — and far away in the dark, something wild whistled back.

**SORREL**

Small-wings carried her over the black country, and the country smelled wrong.

Wind-things told her. Wind carried the taste of fear from the villages, and under the fear a stranger taste, sweetish, empty, like water where fish had died. The tall-nests of the Great Hill drank and drank and were never full. She had felt hunger herself, lately, when the wing-muscle burned and the mice hid well, and she knew hunger as a thing that ends. This hunger did not end. This hunger grew a bigger mouth.

She flew the night roads and counted for the ground-walkers, because counting was hers now, her task, her gift-back. Two-legs in gray shells with metal claws. Wagons with jars that glowed like trapped dawns. Once, a cage-cart of foxes bound for the Churn — she could not say how she knew the word, except that the speaker's mind touched hers each dusk like a warm stone, and things leaked across, and stayed.

She could not open cages. Claws were for holding prey, not iron pins. But she could remember, and she could tell, and telling was more than most creatures had.

*Dawn-two-days,* she reported, dropping to the speaker's shoulder as the sky went gray. *Gray-shells march ahead. Road closed. Go under, go under, go under.*

"Under," Zephyrine murmured, half-asleep. "Yes. Thank you, Sorrel."

The name. Her name, in the soft-speech, the first word she had ever spoken to a human, *stay*, and then the name after. The speaker had lost the memory of hearing it — Sorrel had watched that memory go into the stone-fragment, watched it leave the two-legs' eyes like a bird leaving a snared hand. It hurt her in a place birds did not have names for. But the speaker still said the name right. The name lived in the tongue even when the remembering was gone, and Sorrel decided that this meant some things went deeper than memory, deeper than the stones could cut.

She preened the speaker's hair once, roughly, the way she'd have done for a mate, and took off again into the gray morning.

Below, the Great Hill swelled out of the earth, crowned in gold light, drinking.

* * *

**BRAMBLE**

The horse remembered mud.

Not the road-mud, the ordinary kind that sucked at hooves and let go. Other mud. Old mud, deep under the eastern fields, where long ago — before the quiet, before the chains, before the muting-spell had sat on her throat like a second collar — she had stood in a churned battlefield and pulled a dead soldier's sledge, and the mud had been red, and no one had ever told her what war was because horses are not told things.

But she remembered it. That was the joke of it, the terrible joke: the magicians muted the animals' voices and never thought to mute their memories. Everything the old war did, the beasts still carried. Every field, every burning stable, every rider who never came back to the gate. The land's humans had bought and sold their pasts clean away, but the horses remembered. The dogs remembered. Even the crows remembered, and crows remember everything.

So when the company came down off the hills and Bramble smelled the aqueduct's old stone and the mine-shafts breathing beneath it, she planted her feet and shook her head and spoke, loud, for the first time in many days:

*I know this ground. Bad ground. Blood under it. My blood-line's blood. Listen to me: the doors below are not all doors.*

Zephyrine came to her head at once, hand on her cheek, listening the way they listened, wholly, with their whole ruined self. "Tell me."

*There is a way in that is not a way in,* Bramble said. *A crack where the river went through before the aqueduct. My dam's dam foaled in it, in the old days, hiding from the war-men. Beasts know it. Men forgot it. The gray-shells watch the gates, but nothing watches the water-crack, because men think beasts' paths are nothing-paths.*

Zephyrine stared at her a long moment. Then they laughed — short, cracked, real — and turned to the others.

"Cassian. Your maps end below the Churn. Bramble says they don't have to."

* * *

**ZEPHYRINE**

They went in at moonset, single file, through a seam in the aqueduct's foundation that no engineer had drawn and no warden guarded, because it had been kept, all these centuries, in the keeping of animals.

Cold water to the knee. Stone older than the Order, older than the sigil, older — Zephyrine realized, running their fingers along marks in the rock — older than the war itself. Claw-marks. Hoofprints fossilized in ancient lime. A bear's scratch-sign, repeated in a spiral, meaning something in a language of paths and safety that no human had ever been fluent in.

*Follow the spiral,* said a voice in the dark water, many-voiced, eel and rat and blind-fish together. *We kept it. As promised. Long ago, a promise was made to our grandmothers' grandmothers, by a woman with a kestrel on her shoulder.*

Zephyrine went very still, chest-deep in the freezing dark.

"A covenant," they whispered.

*Kept,* said the dark. *Until you came to keep yours.*

Ahead, below, beneath half a mile of hungry stone, the Churn glowed like a swallowed sunrise, and the sigil's heart beat once — slow, vast, patient — and Zephyrine felt every fragment in their pack answer it, humming, like chicks answering a hawk.

They waded on.
