## 18. Malvane's Bargain

**Zephyrine**

The vault's heart sang to them before they saw it.

Zephyrine had felt sigil-fragments like teeth in their pocket for weeks now — three shards of charred stone, each one humming a different note of an old, broken covenant. But here, in the cavern beneath Ossuar where the memory-mines ended and the dark began, the half-assembled sigil hung in a cradle of iron and light, and it did not hum. It *called*. A low, patient sound, like a mother calling a child home across a field at dusk.

They should not have answered it. They knew that even as they stepped out of the mine-shaft's shadow and onto the vault floor, Sorrel a tight weight on their shoulder, Bracken somewhere behind them with Cassian holding the door against the Regent's wardens.

The sigil was beautiful. That was the worst of it. Three-quarters whole now, its missing quarter a wound of empty air, it turned slowly above its iron cradle like something sleeping — or pretending to sleep. Its light was the color of the valley at harvest, gold going amber going dark, and Zephyrine's chest ached looking at it, the way you ache at a song from childhood you can't name anymore. Couldn't name anymore. The fragments had taken that too.

"You came all this way," said a voice behind them, "to look at my work."

Zephyrine turned.

Regent Malvane stood alone at the edge of the vault floor. No guards. No wardens. She wore no crown — just a plain gray coat, travel-stained, as if she'd walked down through her own mines rather than be carried. Her hair was iron-gray too, cropped short, and her face was the face of a woman who had once been beautiful in the way winter is beautiful, before the cold got into everything. In her hands she carried nothing but a small tin cup, steaming.

"Tea?" she said. "It's a long climb down. I find people are always thirsty after the mines."

Sorrel's claws tightened on Zephyrine's shoulder. *Trap,* the kestrel said, the word sharp as a talon. *Walk away, walk away—*

"I know why you're here," Malvane went on, setting the cup on a stone shelf as if Zephyrine had accepted. "You think I don't. You think I'm a fool in a tower, hoarding other people's yesterdays because I can't bear my own. Everyone thinks that. It's very convenient for them." She looked up at the turning sigil, and for a moment — one unguarded moment — her expression was so nakedly tired that Zephyrine's breath caught. "I watched the old war burn my family when I was nine years old. Do you know what that war was? Not armies. Not even magicians, mostly. It was *covenants* breaking. Promises made by powerful people to each other, and then unmade, and every unmaking fell on someone like me. Someone small. Someone whose village was a footnote in a treaty."

"I'm sorry," Zephyrine said, and meant it, and hated how small the words sounded.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." She turned from the sigil to face them fully, and her eyes were dry and clear and terribly sane. "You've been carrying the fragments. I've felt every step. Do you know what they cost you?"

Zephyrine's hand moved without permission to their breast pocket, where the three shards lay wrapped in wool.

"Your mother's face," Malvane said softly. "Your ninth name-day. The first word your kestrel ever spoke to you." She counted them off like coins. "I have all of those. Every memory the fragments took from you passed through the engines on its way to me — that's how the sigil works, it doesn't destroy, it *routes*. Everything you've lost is sitting in my reliquary right now, labeled and shelved, waiting." She took a step closer. "Give me the fragments. Help me finish the Rite. And I will give you back every single thing it took. Not copies. The originals. You'll remember your mother's face so completely it will take you a week to stop crying."

The vault was very quiet. Somewhere far above, muffled through miles of stone, Ossuar went about its evening — the bells, the carts, the ten thousand citizens trading their pain for pleasant pasts.

"And the speakers?" Zephyrine heard themselves ask. Their voice sounded far away. "The beast-speakers. The ones your wardens hang."

"Protected. By law, written into the new covenant itself." Malvane spread her hands. "Not just tolerated. *Honored.* You of all people know what the valley did to you — hiding your gift like a sickness, teaching you to flinch every time a bird opened its mouth. I can end that. Not in a generation. Tomorrow. The sigil binds covenants, Zephyrine Ravensworth. Real ones. Unbreakable ones. That's what it was *for*, before the old magicians broke it and fought over the pieces. A covenant of protection for speakers, sealed in the sigil itself, stronger than any tyrant who comes after me — including me. I couldn't break it if I wanted to."

"And peace."

"And peace." For the first time her voice roughened. "The old war never ended. Everyone knows it. It just went underground and got patient. The sigil finished properly ends it — ends *all* of it, the feuds, the blood-debts, the border raids, the warden-hunts. One age closes and a new one opens, and children born after won't even have a word for what we're afraid of." She stopped, three paces away now, close enough that Zephyrine could see the fine lines around her eyes, the human exhaustion under the iron. "I am offering you everything you have bled for. Everything you've *paid* for. Your memories. Your people's safety. An end to the hanging of speakers. All I ask is the stones in your pocket and your help finishing what I started."

*Sorrel's voice in their ear, urgent:* *She's lying. They always lie.*

But Zephyrine thought: *what if she isn't?*

That was the trap, they realized later. Not the guards, not the warden-alarm, not any cage of iron. The trap was that Malvane wasn't lying — or wasn't only lying — and some part of them knew it, and that part leaned toward her like a plant toward light.

For one breath — one long, suspended breath, in which the sigil turned above them and the tea steamed on its shelf and Sorrel's claws bit their shoulder — Zephyrine wanted to say yes.

Wanted it the way the drowned want air. Their mother's face. Their name-day, candles and sugar and the smell of the festival fires. *Stay,* Sorrel had said to them once, in the kestrel's bright clipped voice, and Zephyrine could not remember hearing it, only being told they'd heard it, and the gap where the memory should be ached like a pulled tooth. Give her the stones. Help her finish it. Wake up tomorrow whole, and safe, and legally real, in a world where no child ever had to hide what they were.

One breath.

Then Zephyrine looked past Malvane's shoulder, up at the sigil, and saw what hung inside its golden light.

Memories. Thousands of them, strung through the lattice like flies in a web — little drifting motes of other people's lives. A wedding. A funeral. A dog running through wet grass. A woman's last sight of her burning house. All of it harvested, routed, *owned*, feeding the beautiful machine so that one woman's idea of order could be imposed on everyone who'd never been asked.

"You'd give me back my memories," Zephyrine said quietly. "Taken from a reliquary full of everyone else's."

"They sold them," Malvane said. "Or lost them. Or—"

"The valley didn't sell anything." Their voice didn't rise. It got *flatter*, which was worse. "The wards ate us for two hundred years and nobody asked us either. I grew up on stolen ground, Regent. I know exactly what it looks like when someone builds peace on top of a theft and calls the theft a foundation." They drew out the wool-wrapped bundle, and Malvane's eyes fixed on it with a hunger she was too disciplined to hide. "You watched your family burn, and you decided no one would ever burn again, and you were willing to become the fire to make it so. I understand. I almost understand. But I've touched these fragments. I've seen what the covenant actually says."

Something flickered in her face. "You've seen nothing. Fragments lie."

"Fragments remember." Zephyrine closed their hand over the bundle. "And what I saw wasn't a weapon, Regent. It was a promise — the same promise you're making me, except the old version didn't need a jailer. It didn't need engines. It didn't need anyone to hold the power, because the power wasn't held. It was *shared*. Your version has a throne built into it. Even if the throne is yours, and even if you're kind — and I think, gods help me, that you might be — the next one won't be. The sigil doesn't end wars. It just decides who wins the next one in advance."

Malvane was silent for a long moment. When she spoke again, all the warmth had gone out of her voice, and what remained was worse: respect.

"I offered you everything," she said. "No one refuses everything."

"Some things aren't mine to accept." Zephyrine lifted their chin. "My memories were taken without my leave. I won't buy them back with everyone else's. And the beasts—" their voice caught, just once, thinking of Sorrel's first word, gone, gone, a door bricked over in their own head, "—the beasts never got a vote in your covenant at all. So no. No bargain. No throne. No peace bought with a prison."

Malvane exhaled slowly. Then she reached to the stone shelf, picked up the untouched tea, and poured it out on the floor — a small, deliberate, almost ceremonial gesture, the way you spill wine for the dead.

"Pity," she said. "I genuinely liked you."

The sigil's light changed.

It had been gold, drowsy, harvest-colored. Now it went white, and the hum became a shriek, and the iron cradle began to turn, and Zephyrine understood, with the terrible clarity of the moment too late, that the offer had never been an offer at all. It had been a *measurement*. How much would the speaker bend? Where were the fault lines? What did love cost?

Now she knew. Now the machines along the vault walls woke, one by one, their memory-lamps flaring, and the great engines began to drink.

"Guards will be here in ninety seconds," Malvane said, already walking away, unhurried, toward a stair Zephyrine hadn't seen. "Your thief is holding the door with the defector, and they are losing. Take whatever time you have left to reconsider." At the foot of the stair she paused, without turning around. "For what it's worth — I hope you do. I hope it until the very end. That's the tragedy of people like us, Ravensworth. We always want the same thing. We just can't stand each other's methods."

She climbed. The white light swallowed her shadow.

* * *

**Malvane**

From the top of the stair, in the cold gallery above the vault, Sera Malvane watched the speaker below through the floor's iron grating, and permitted herself thirty seconds of grief.

The girl — no, not a girl, a grown person with a kestrel on their shoulder and three stolen miracles in their pocket — stood very straight under the sigil's white glare, and did not run. That was the infuriating thing. That was the thing Sera would remember afterward, in the years when there were years. Most people, offered everything, broke immediately and thanked her for the privilege. This one stood in the light of absolute power and said *no one got a vote*, as if votes had ever kept a single village from burning.

"Report," she said.

Captain Ilsen materialized at her shoulder, armor dull, face carefully blank. "Vault sealed at every approach except the mine-shaft. Wardens massing at the north door. The thief and the defector are pinned at the lower gallery — Vey is wounded, the boy is bleeding, but they've wrecked two of the memory-engines and they're working on a third."

"Let them work. The engines are replaceable. The delay is useful." Sera folded her hands behind her back. Below, the speaker had begun to move — fast now, toward the sigil's cradle, the kestrel launching upward into the rafters with a cry that even the muted birds of Ossuar seemed to answer. "Seal the shaft behind them. Nobody leaves. And Ilsen — the Rite begins at moonrise whether the fourth fragment is seated or not. We have enough stored memory to force the binding. It will be... rougher. Cruder. More will be lost."

"How much more, Regent?"

She watched the small figure below press both hands against the iron cradle, watched the sigil's light bend toward them like a flame toward wind, and felt the old arithmetic settle into place, the one she had carried since she was nine years old standing in the ashes of everything.

"All of it, if necessary," Sera Malvane said. "Every memory in Ossuar. Mine included. Order is worth more than the one who orders." She turned from the grating. "Sound the horns. And bring me the valley elder. If the speaker won't take my bargain—" 

Below, faintly, through stone and iron, the first horn began to sound.

"—they can watch what refusal costs."
