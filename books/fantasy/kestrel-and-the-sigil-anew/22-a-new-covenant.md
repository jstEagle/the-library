## 22. A New Covenant

The valley gate had never been open in living memory. Now it stood ajar every morning, and an old woman named Maren kept the key on a string around her neck mostly out of habit.

She had voted to seal the passes, that terrible first night. She remembered raising her hand among the elders while the sky was still black where stars should have been, remembered saying *we are safe if we stay small*, and she had believed it with her whole heart. She had believed it for sixty years, since she was a girl watching her grandmother bar the shutters against travelers who might be anything at all.

Now she stood at the gate each dawn and watched people come through it.

They came slowly at first — a tinker with a limp, two sisters from the gray country beyond who had never seen an unwarded sky. Then more. A family of potters whose kiln-city had burned in the last days of the Regent's war. A young man who walked with his eyes down until he reached the valley's first orchard and heard, for the first time in his life, birdsong that had not been muted by law.

Maren watched him stop beneath the apple trees and simply stand there, listening, tears running into his collar, and she thought: *this is what we locked away. Not danger. This.*

She had gone to Zephyrine Winterbourne's door in the spring, when the borders opened, and made her apology standing in the yard like a penitent. Zephyrine had listened without interruption, which was their way, and then had said only: "You were afraid. Fear is honest. It's what people build on top of fear that isn't." And then they had asked her in for tea, because holding a grudge was another kind of wall, and Zephyrine was done with walls.

Maren turned the key now out of ceremony rather than necessity. The lock hadn't worked since midsummer anyway; someone — Bracken, everyone suspected, though he denied it with a grin too pleased to be innocent — had quietly oiled it into uselessness.

*Let it rust,* she thought, and left the gate wide behind her as she walked up into the village, where the Founding Festival banners were going up again, this year bearing no sigil at all. Just cloth. Just color. Just a valley deciding, one morning at a time, to be a place instead of a fortress.

* * *

Cassian Vey still woke before dawn. Some things did not unlearn themselves.

He no longer wore the Order's gray. He owned exactly three shirts and a coat with a mended shoulder, and he slept in a room above the valley's farrier shop because the sound of horses being shod reminded him of hounds he would never call again. He told himself he didn't miss them. He was lying, and he knew it, and some mornings he let himself lie about it all the way to breakfast.

The Order had sentenced him to the harvest chairs for his false report — for the report he'd filed saying the speaker-taint trail went cold at the border, when every hound at his heel had known otherwise. He'd escaped with stolen ledgers under his coat and a certainty in his chest that had taken him thirty years of service to earn: that the thing he'd served was built on theft, and that he had been a thief's hand for most of his life.

The ledgers sat now in the valley's new archive, copied out in fair hands, read aloud in the square on market days so that no one could ever again claim they hadn't known. Names of the harvested. Dates. Prices paid for other people's pasts. Cassian had read the first page himself, voice steady until it wasn't, and Zephyrine had stood beside him and said nothing, which was somehow the kindest thing anyone had ever done for him.

What surprised him — what he still hadn't found words for, months later — was that they'd let him stay. The valley had every reason to put him out. He had hunted speakers. He had hunted *Zephyrine*. He had followed their scent across half the gray country with murder in his commission, and only the mutiny of his own hounds had stayed his hand.

But the valley, it turned out, had decided to try something the old world never attempted: judging people by what they did after they knew better.

So he taught now. Self-defense, border-craft, how to read a trail, how to stand between the frightened and the frightening. Children called him Warden Vey for the first month, half in mockery, and he'd corrected them once, flatly — "Just Vey" — and they'd obeyed, and then immediately begun calling him Vey-the-Warden anyway behind his back, and he'd decided that was close enough to grace.

This morning he walked the ridge road above the valley with a boy of nine named Tam, who wanted to learn tracking and had the patience of a stone, which was to say none.

"Sign here," Cass said, crouching. "What do you see?"

"Deer."

"What else?"

Tam squinted. "It's... walking slow? Not scared?"

"And why isn't it scared?"

Tam thought hard, tongue between his teeth. "Because nothing hunts it here anymore?"

"Because," Cass said, "it has learned that this ground is safe. That took time. Safety isn't a wall, boy. It's a habit. You build it the way you build any habit — same ground, same kindness, over and over, until even the wild things believe it."

Tam nodded solemnly, then ruined it by asking if they could get breakfast.

Cass laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound that startled a jay off the fencepost — and said yes. On the walk back, the jay followed them tree to tree, scolding companionably, and Cass found himself talking to it in a low voice, not expecting answers, just talking, the way everyone talked to animals now. Not understanding them. Just... including them.

That was the covenant, wasn't it. Not speech. Company.

* * *

Bracken had never lived anywhere longer than a season, and the valley was going to ruin his record.

He'd meant to leave in the summer. Then the autumn. Then there was the archive to help build, because nobody else could pick a lock worth trusting and somebody had to make sure the records of what Malvane's order had done were kept somewhere honest. Then there was winter, and winter was a bad season for roads, obviously, and then it was nearly festival season again and leaving before the festival would just be rude.

He told himself these stories in order, and he almost believed them, and meanwhile he had a room with his name chalked on the door — his actual name, or the name he used as one, which after everything amounted to the same thing — and a workshop where he taught children the fine arts of picking locks, lifting purses, and spotting a lie, on the grounds that these were survival skills and the valley could either teach them safely or learn them badly later.

The magistrate had objected. Once. Bracken had picked the magistrate's lock, left a note inside reading *see how easy that was*, and the objection had quietly died.

Today he sat on the schoolhouse steps in the late-morning sun, ostensibly supervising recess, actually whittling, actually watching Zephyrine through the open window.

They were doing the listening lesson. No magic in it — there was no magic left in anyone, not like before — but Zephyrine insisted the gift had never really been the hearing. It had been the willingness to hear. Anyone could learn that. Everyone should.

"Close your eyes," Zephyrine was saying, in that level, patient voice. "What do you hear?"

"A bird," said a small girl.

"Which bird?"

Silence. Then, wonderingly: "There's more than one?"

"There are eleven birds singing right now, and a dog two lanes over, and the creek, and someone chopping wood, and your own heartbeat, which counts too. The world is always speaking. Most people just decide it isn't saying anything to them." A pause. "Open your eyes. Now listen with them. What do you see that you weren't seeing?"

Bracken watched the children's faces do the thing faces did during Zephyrine's lessons — that slow opening, like shutters coming off windows. He'd seen it in adults too. In Cass. In Maren. In himself, if he was honest, which he tried to be at least weekly.

Zephyrine had given away the thing that made them singular, and had ended up less alone than anyone Bracken had ever met. There was a lesson in that he hadn't finished learning.

Sorrel was on the schoolhouse roof, as she usually was during lessons — a kestrel-shaped shadow at the ridge of the slates, head cocked, one amber eye on the classroom below. Watching over. Bracken had stopped finding it eerie months ago. Now it just looked like devotion wearing feathers.

He went back to his whittling. He was making a little wooden kestrel for the smallest kid in class, whose birthday was tomorrow, and he was better at locks than carving, so it looked more like a lumpy pigeon, but love didn't require craftsmanship.

*Stay,* he thought, testing the word against the strange weight of it. *I might.*

* * *

Zephyrine had lost four memories to the fragments, and in the months since the Unmaking, they had learned the shape of those absences the way a tongue learns a missing tooth.

Their mother's face: gone. They knew she had loved them; they knew her hands had been strong from kneading dough; they knew she had hummed while she worked. But the face itself was a smooth white space, and when neighbors spoke of her kindly, Zephyrine smiled and grieved privately and did not correct them, because grief needed no correcting.

Their name-day: gone. They celebrated it now on the anniversary of the night the stars went out, on the theory that they had been reborn that night whether they liked it or not.

Sorrel's first word to them: gone. This was the one that ached worst, because Sorrel remembered it perfectly, and sometimes Zephyrine caught her looking at them with that tilted head, and he knew she was remembering a moment they could no longer share, and he knew she knew it too, and neither of them minded, quite. Love, it turned out, did not live only in memories. It lived in returning. In the window sill. In the three taps.

And the fourth — the memory of the drowned chapel, the final truce, the stranger's death that had flooded into them when they touched the third fragment — that one they had given up willingly, poured into the unbinding along with everything else. Sometimes they missed it, oddly. It had been someone's whole ending. But endings belonged to the ones who lived them, and Zephyrine had carried enough of other people's deaths.

The strangest loss was the silence itself. For their whole life, the world had spoken to them — wind carrying gossip, worms complaining about rain, the deep slow thoughts of oaks. Now the world was quiet, magnificently, ordinarily quiet, and Zephyrine had discovered that ordinary quiet was its own kind of language. You had to lean in. Everyone did. That was the new covenant: not that beasts and humans shared words, but that humans had finally stopped assuming the silence meant nothing was there.

Teaching helped. Teaching helped enormously. Every child who closed their eyes and counted eleven birds was, in some small way, continuing what Zephyrine had given away. The gift hadn't died. It had been sown.

After the lesson, they walked home through the orchard, and the valley did its evening work around them — smoke from chimneys, the farrier's hammer, Maren locking nothing at the gate out of pure sentiment. Zephyrine's cottage sat at the lane's end with its garden gone autumnal and its window sills worn smooth by a kestrel's feet.

They were cutting bread when they felt it — not heard, never heard anymore, but felt, a familiar displacement of air — and turned to find Sorrel on the sill, feathers ruffled from a long flight, eyes bright as coins.

She looked at them. They looked at her.

Then she lifted one foot and tapped the glass. Once. Twice. Three times.

*Stay.*

The old signal. From the first weeks, when she'd been too injured to fly far and too proud to admit it, and had invented a code so she could tell him, wordlessly, imperiously, *do not go anywhere.*

"I'm not going anywhere," Zephyrine said aloud, and their voice cracked on it, and Sorrel ruffled her feathers as if embarrassed on their behalf, and stayed on the sill while the bread cooled and the light went gold and the valley, habit by habit, kindness by kindness, taught itself to be safe.

Outside, the first stars of evening came out — every single one of them — and Zephyrine lit the lamp, and stayed.
