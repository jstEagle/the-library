## 1. The Bone That Sang

The bone had been singing all morning.

Not aloud—never aloud—but in the way that a kettle hums before it boils, a frequency felt in the teeth more than the ears. Mirabel Montrose held the femur up to the gray light of her shop window and turned it slowly, reading it the way other people read letters. Cow bone, cured three years in lye and lime, dense and pale as winter milk. A good bone. An honest one. It had never lied to her yet.

"You'd better not start," she told it.

The bone said nothing, which was its habit, and she set it back on the workbench among the shavings and the little knives with their handles worn to the color of honey. Around her, the shop of Montrose & Daughter—there had never been a son; there had been a brother, but the sign had been carved before he was born, or after, she didn't let herself think about which—smelled of beeswax and dust and the faint mineral sweetness of old death. Charms hung from every rafter on twine: knucklebones for safe travel, rib-bells for honest sleep, small carved vertebrae strung like pearls for mothers who could not conceive. The city of Vellamere called them trinkets. Called them *keepsakes*. The word *magic* had not passed legally through anyone's lips in twenty years, and so the whole trade of it had gone quiet and sideways, hiding inside grief where no censor thought to look.

That was the genius of memorial work, Mirabel thought, picking up her finest knife. Nobody regulated mourning. The Sigil Accords had sealed the old gods behind a binding carved into the crown itself, had outlawed hedge-craft and priestcraft and the speaking of names into vessels, but they had not dared outlaw weeping. Grief was too useful to the state. It kept people obedient and tired.

So the bereaved came to her, and she carved, and sometimes—if the dead were willing, if the bone remembered—the dead spoke.

She had heard them since childhood. Not ghosts, exactly. Nothing so dramatic. More like voices carried through water, arriving late and softened, saying small true things: *the door was blue*, *I forgave him years ago*, *tell her the garden needs thyme*. The dead were mostly concerned with small true things. They had let go of the large ones.

For six years, since Tam—since the silence where Tam used to be—she had kept her gift folded up small as a moth's wing. She listened only when a charm required it. She answered only what needed answering. She had made herself a woman of narrow, careful habits: shop opened at eight, closed at dusk, no questions asked of the Crown, no candles lit for gods whose names she still knew and did not say. Invisible. Safe. Twenty years of quiet, worn smooth as a river stone.

Then the mother had come in at half past nine with rain still beaded on her shawl, holding a lock of hair wrapped in linen, and asked for a charm for her boy, and everything had begun to crack.

* * *

"He was nine," the woman said. She sat in the customer's chair by the counter, hands folded around the linen bundle as though it might warm them. "Nine in the spring. He fell from the granary roof. He was—I keep telling people he was flying a kite, because that's what he was doing, but when I say it now it sounds like something I invented. Does that happen? Does grief make you invent things?"

"Grief makes everything sound invented," Mirabel said. "It doesn't mean it isn't true."

"His name was Perrin." The woman said it carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "Perrin Aldous Vey. I want his name in the bone. I want—" Her voice snagged. "Everyone keeps telling me how strong I am. Nobody says his name at all anymore. My sister visited yesterday and talked about the weather for an hour and left, and I realized she never once said *Perrin*. As if saying it would hurt me. As if forgetting is kindness."

Mirabel had heard this before. More and more, these last months, she had heard versions of it—the fraying, the names going unspoken, the strange collective gentleness around absence. She had told herself it was epidemic grief, some contagion of sorrow moving through Vellamere's poorer quarters the way fevers moved through them. She had told herself a great many convenient things.

"I'll carve his name," she said. "Into the charm itself, and into the heart of it, where it will hold."

The woman's breath went out of her like a knot slipping loose. "You can do that?"

"It's what the bones are for." Mirabel rose and took down the femur. "Bone holds what flesh cannot. Flesh forgets on purpose—it has to, to keep living. Bone has no such mercy. Bone keeps everything, if you ask it properly."

She worked while the mother watched, because watching was part of the medicine. First the shaping: the knife whispering off thin curls, coaxing the femur toward the form it wanted, which was always a bird. Every memorial charm she made became a bird—a kestrel, usually, small hawks of the hedgerows, hunters with patient eyes. She had never decided this. The bones simply curved that way under her hands, as if flight were the shape memory took when it was freed from a body.

The curls fell. The bird emerged: wings folded, head turned, a breast smooth as an egg. Then the fine work, the part that looked like decoration to any Crown inspector who might peer through her window—geometric flourishes, knotwork, the harmless filigree of folk craft. And threaded through the filigree, invisible unless you knew the grammar, the true carving: lines that were not lines, angles that leaned against each other like whispered words, a name nested inside a name.

*Perrin Aldous Vey,* she carved, and beneath it, in the older script, the one the Accords had forbidden and her father had taught her anyway: *who flew.*

"Now," she murmured, mostly to herself, partly to the bone, partly—to her own surprise—to whatever might be listening beyond it. "Now, little one. If you're anywhere, if anything of you is anywhere—your mother kept your hair. Your mother kept your name. Come and see."

She held the charm to her ear.

This was the ritual no customer ever saw. Most charms, most days, gave her nothing: silence, or the bone's plain mineral muteness, and she would tell the grieving mother honestly that some souls went out clean as snuffed candles, and there was no shame in it. But sometimes—maybe one charm in ten—the bone sang. A voice would arrive through the marrow like light through frosted glass, and Mirabel would listen, and carry back whatever small true thing the dead wanted said.

She waited. The shop ticked and settled around her. Rain began again against the window, soft as sifted flour.

And then the bone sang—but wrong.

* * *

Later, trying to describe it to herself—and failing, because there was nothing in her life to compare it to—she would reach for the image of pressing her ear to a door and hearing, instead of a room, the ocean. The voice that came through the femur was not Perrin's. It was not any child's. It was not even, strictly, a voice. It was pressure. It was vastness leaning against the thin wall of the world, the way a bull leans against a fence post just to feel it give.

The dead's whispers had edges. They were small, human-sized, frayed at the borders the way all mortal things are frayed. This thing had no edges. It pressed outward against the inside of the sound itself, and for one vertiginous moment Mirabel understood that she was not listening through the bone to the far side of death. Something on the far side of death was listening *back*—and had noticed the hole she'd drilled.

Her hand shook. The knife clattered against the bench.

*Mirabel,* said the vastness, without words, in the way that cold says its own name. *Carver.*

She dropped the charm.

It hit the floorboards and rolled, wings up, and lay there looking absurd and small, a little hawk of bone on a dusty floor, and the pressure receded—not gone, she could feel that, not gone at all, but withdrawn the way a fisherman feels the line go slack and knows the thing on the end has merely turned. Somewhere out past the walls of the world, something enormous had tasted her listening and found it interesting.

"Madam?" The mother was standing, alarmed, one hand pressed to her collarbone. "Are you ill? You've gone white as your wares."

Mirabel bent, picked up the charm. Her fingers did not want to close around it. She made them. "A cramp," she said. "Carpenters' cramp. It passes." She set the bird on a square of linen, wrapped it with the lock of Perrin's hair tucked against its breast, and tied the twine with hands she commanded the way one commands soldiers in a rout—loudly, repeatedly, with doubtful results. "Keep it near your bed. Speak to it when you think of him. Say his name out loud, madam. Say it often. Names are—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Names are how we hold on."

"And will he hear me?" The woman's eyes were wet and fierce. "Through the bone. Will he hear?"

What could she say? *Something heard me. Something with no face pressed its mouth to the keyhole of the world.* She thought of the censors, the white-cloaked Ministry men who had come down Copperlane last month burning "resonant goods" in a barrel outside the chandlery, laughing the way boys laugh at bonfires. She thought of the twenty years of quiet she had built plank by plank around her life.

"He'll know he's loved," Mirabel said. "That's what the bone carries. That's all it ever needs to carry."

The mother paid in copper counted twice, thanked her three times, and left with the charm held against her sternum like a second heartbeat. The bell over the door sighed. The shop settled back into its dust-and-wax silence.

Mirabel locked the door behind her.

* * *

She stood in the middle of her shop for a long time, listening to the rain.

Twenty years ago, she had been eleven years old, and the sky over Vellamere had filled with light the night King Aldric signed the Accords. She remembered—she was allowed to remember this; everyone remembered it; it was the one memory the Crown encouraged—the bells ringing backward, the statues of the old gods veiled in gray cloth by morning, the priests walking out of their temples with their hands empty and their faces blank as eggs. The Binding, the criers called it. Carved into the crown itself, they said, into the iron circlet the king wore, sealing the gods behind it so that magic died in the world like frost dying at sunrise.

Her father had burned his carving tools that night. All but two knives, which he hid inside the chimney flue, and which Mirabel still used, though she had never told a living soul they existed. *Small work only,* he had said, gripping her shoulders hard enough to bruise. *Memorial work. Grief-work. No god will miss a grieving mother's charm, and no king should want to. But you listen to me, Mira—you hear anything vast, anything hungry, anything that knows your name before you give it—you put the knives down and you walk away. Promise me.*

She had promised. She had been eleven, and frightened, and her father's hands were shaking, and she had promised.

Six years ago, when Tam—when the silence came—she had kept the promise harder than ever. Folded smaller. Listened less. Made herself so quiet that even she sometimes couldn't hear herself.

And today, for the first time in twenty years of careful, invisible, law-abiding grief-work, something on the other side of the binding had heard *her*.

*Carver,* it had said. It had known the word. It had known her.

She walked the shop slowly, touching things as she passed, the way you touch furniture in a house after a burglary—not checking for loss, exactly, but relearning what ownership felt like. The hanging charms stirred faintly in the draft from the window, knocking together with small dry clicks, dozens of little bones conversing in a language of percussion. She had always found the sound companionable. Tonight it sounded like nervousness.

At the workbench she stopped, and looked at the femur's twin still lying in its box of sawdust, waiting to become someone else's kestrel.

"Don't you dare," she told it again. But her voice wasn't steady, and the bone, being bone, kept everything—including, perhaps, its opinion of her.

She should put the knives away. She should take tomorrow's appointments, carve the two infant wristlets and the widow's ring, and put the knives away, and go back to the narrow careful life, and tell herself the vastness had been a trick of exhaustion, a fever of the inner ear, anything.

Instead she found herself doing what she always did when the world tilted: talking to the dead. Not listening—just talking, one-sided, the way her father had prayed before prayer was illegal. "All right," she said to the dim shop, to the rafters, to the small dry chorus of hanging bones. "Whatever you were. Whatever pressed against my little door. You should know something about me."

She picked up Perrin's shavings from the bench, cupped them, carried them to the stove, and fed them to the coals. They flared green-white and were gone.

"I don't scare," she said, which was a lie, and then, because lies sat badly in a house built of truth: "I scare very well. But I don't stop."

The fire settled. Outside, the rain thickened toward evening, and somewhere across the rooftops of Copperlane the cathedral bells rang the hour—five strokes, dull and toneless, bells that had sounded wrong ever since the Binding, as though their tongues had been shortened.

And underneath the bells—so faint she would spend the night convincing herself she'd imagined it—came another sound. Not through a bone this time. Through the walls themselves, through the wet stone and the old timber of Vellamere, rising from the direction of the river like a smell.

A child's voice. Thin, drowned, patient.

*Please,* it said. *Please, somebody. The king ate my name.*
