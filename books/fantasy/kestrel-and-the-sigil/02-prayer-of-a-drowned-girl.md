## 2. Prayer of a Drowned Girl

The charm would not stay finished.

Mirabel Montrose had carved four hundred and eleven memorial pieces in her nineteen years at the bench—she kept the count in a ledger hidden beneath the floorboard under the coal scuttle, because the count itself was contraband—and not one of them had ever behaved like this. A memorial was supposed to go quiet once it was done. That was the whole art of it: you took the grief the living carried, you gave it a shape in bone or antler or river-ivory, and the dead who clung to that grief got somewhere to stand. Then they settled. Then they slept.

This one kept waking up.

She set down her burin and looked at it. It lay on the leather pad like something stranded—a small oval of cow bone, no longer than her thumb, strung on a cord of undyed hemp for a girl named Wren Halloway, aged nine years, drowned in the Vell three weeks past. The mother had come in with wet eyes and dry hands, the way they did when they'd already cried themselves empty somewhere private, and asked for *something*, she didn't know what, just something that would hold her girl a little.

Mirabel had heard Wren while she carved. Of course she had. Children came through loudest—they hadn't learned yet how to be dead politely—and this one had been all river-sounds and skipping-rope rhymes and a running commentary on her own funeral that would have been funny if Mirabel had let herself laugh, which she hadn't. She'd finished the carving, spoken the closing words under her breath, wrapped the charm in linen.

And Wren had said, clear as a bell over water:

*She can't hear me.*

Mirabel had gone still with the linen half-folded.

*She's looking right at it and she can't hear me. Why can't she hear me?*

That had been yesterday. Since then, Mirabel had unwrapped the charm six times, held it, listened, heard nothing but the ordinary faint murmur of the city's accumulated dead—the rain-on-roof sound of them, always there, always ignorable—and rewrapped it. Each time, the moment her attention slid away, the voice came back, thinner than before.

*Please. Please, I'm going under again. I don't remember my middle name. I had one, didn't I? Tell my mum my middle name.*

Now, in the grey hour before dawn, with the shop shutters still latched and the stove ticking as it cooled, Mirabel picked the charm up and did something she had not done since childhood, something dangerous, something she'd spent two decades teaching herself never to do.

She opened all the way.

It was like opening a door onto a gale. The dead of Vellamere rushed in—not the polite murmuring rain-sound but the full chorus, thousands upon thousands of voices layered like sediment, and beneath them, beneath everything, that vast wrong pressure she'd felt the night before, the thing at the edge of hearing that was not a voice at all but an absence shaped like one, pressing inward, patient as ice.

And through all of it, small and drowning: *the king ate my name.*

Mirabel's hand shook so hard the charm chattered against her ring.

*He ate it,* Wren said, and now there was terror in the child-voice, the specific animal terror of a nine-year-old who has understood something no nine-year-old should have to understand. *I went down and I couldn't find it anymore, my name, it was like the mud took it, and there was a man standing on the bank, only he wasn't a man, he was tall like a steeple and his face was a hole, and when I tried to say Wren nothing came out because—because—*

The voice frayed. Static, if static could grieve.

*Because he ate it. He ate my name and now my mum looks at my picture and she has to check the back to remember which girl I am. There were two of us. Wasn't there? Wasn't there another little girl?*

There had been. Mirabel knew it without knowing how she knew it—a sister, older, twelve, who had stood on the bank screaming until men waded in after the body. The sister's face was a smudge even in Mirabel's secondhand knowing, and that was wrong, that was deeply wrong, because the dead gave you everything, every detail, whether you wanted it or not.

Something was eating the dead out of the world. From the inside of the world's remembering.

Mirabel put the charm down very carefully, walked to the basin, and was quietly, thoroughly sick.

* * *

She told herself, while she washed her face and relit the stove and opened the shutters to let the morning in, that she would do nothing. This was the sensible resolution, the survival resolution, the one she'd made a hundred times before in twenty years of hearing things the Accords said could not be heard. You did nothing. You carved your charms, you kept your ledger under the floorboard, you stayed so small and so dull that the Crown's censors walked past your window without a glance. Magic was outlawed. Hearing the dead was magic, whatever sophistry she used to pretend otherwise. The purges had taught the city that lesson in blood and bonfire smoke, and Mirabel had survived them by being nobody.

But the charm sat on her bench, and every time she turned her back she felt it like a hand tugging her sleeve.

*Tell my mum my middle name.*

At noon she closed the shop early—first time in three years—and walked down to the river.

* * *

The Vell ran brown and slow through the southern quarter, wide as a street where Wren Halloway had gone in, below the old weir where the current folded back on itself and held what it caught. They called the pool the Keeper. Fishermen hated it; children were warned about it in the flat tone adults used when the warning was really a prayer. There was a post on the near bank with ribbons tied to it—one ribbon, bright blue, new, the silk still crisp. Someone had written on it in careful ink: *WRN.* Just the three letters. As if whoever tied it had started to write the name and found it wasn't there.

Mirabel stood looking at the ribbon for a long time.

Then she knelt, laid her palm flat against the muddy grass, and listened downward.

The riverbank was loud. Rivers always were—the drowned did not leave, they simply stopped being able to leave, and the Keeper had been collecting them for two hundred years. But today the chorus was strange. Today it was full of gaps. Voices that should have been there weren't, and voices that were there kept losing their thread mid-word, like singers forgetting their own songs.

*I had a red coat,* someone said, over and over, a man's voice, worn smooth as a pebble from repetition. *I had a red coat. I had a—I had a—*

*A boat,* said another. *We went out in a boat. Whose boat? We went out in a boat.*

And underneath, faint, fading, threaded through the rest like a single hair pulled through cloth: *please tell my mum my middle name, please, please, it was—it was—it started with—*

Gone. Like a fish slipping a net.

Mirabel sat back on her heels, cold to the bone despite the mild afternoon, and that was when the woman spoke to her.

"You're the carver."

Mirabel turned. A woman stood a few paces off, mid-forties maybe, dressed in the good-but-mended wool of someone who owned exactly one good dress. She had the look of a person who had stopped sleeping some weeks ago and was now moving through the days on pure stubbornness. In her arms she carried a bundle: a small shoe, a hair ribbon, a tin cup with a dent in it.

"Mrs. Halloway," Mirabel said. "I haven't finished—"

"I know." The woman's voice was flat. "I know what you haven't finished. I came to ask you to stop."

Mirabel blinked. "To—"

"Stop." Mrs. Halloway's composure cracked down the middle. "Every time I touch that thing you sent word about, I lose more of her. Do you understand? Three weeks ago I could tell you the exact weight of her when she was born. Seven pounds, two ounces. Last week I had to ask my sister whether Wren liked plums. *Whether my own daughter liked plums.* And yesterday—" her voice dropped to a whisper, horrified, "—yesterday I looked at her drawing on the wall above the hearth and I thought, *whose is that?* For a whole minute. A whole minute, and then it came back, but it's coming back slower each time, like—like tide going out."

"That isn't the charm," Mirabel said, before she could think better of it. "Mrs. Halloway, the charm doesn't take anything. It holds. If anything it's the only thing still holding what's left of—"

She stopped. Too much. Far too much. But Mrs. Halloway had grabbed her wrist with both hands, grip desperate, and was searching her face with the ferocious hope of the bereaved.

"You hear her." Not a question. "They said—you're the one they say about, the bone-woman on Cooper Lane, that things speak to you. I thought it was superstition. I *prayed* it was superstition." Her hands tightened. "Is she there? Is my girl still anywhere?"

And Mirabel, who had spent nineteen years perfecting the lie of the blank face, the shrug, the *I'm just a carver, madam*, heard herself say:

"Yes."

The word cost her everything and she paid it anyway.

"She's frightened," Mirabel went on, and the dam inside her simply broke open, twenty years of silence pouring out into the mud by the river where a child had died. "She's frightened and she's fading and she keeps asking me to tell you her middle name, and I can't give it to her, Mrs. Halloway, because she's lost it. Something has taken it. Something has taken it out of her and out of you and out of everyone who ever loved her, and I don't know what it is yet, but I heard her say—" Mirabel swallowed. "I heard her say the king ate it."

For a moment the woman just stared. Then, slowly, she released Mirabel's wrist and stepped back, and her face did something terrible: it went from desperate to *resigned*, the way a face goes when a mad hope confirms itself and turns out to be worse than despair.

"So it's true," she said. "It's happening to everyone."

"What do you mean, everyone?"

"The Ketterings." Mrs. Halloway's voice had gone distant, reciting. "Their boy fell from the rope bridge last autumn. Tammas, his name was—Tammas Kettering. Except last month his father called him *Tomas*, and when his mother corrected him he looked at her like she was the one who'd forgotten. And the widow Sorrel, on Tanner's Row—her husband drowned in the flood year, and now she sets two places at table and weeps because she can't remember why there are two plates. And the miller's family, and the ferryman's daughter, and—" She pressed the bundle of relics hard against her chest. "Everyone the river took. Everyone the river took lately, it's like they're being... erased. Unwritten. My sister says I'm grieving myself confused. My priest says grief does strange things. But grief doesn't eat *names*, carver. Grief doesn't make a mother check the back of a portrait to know which daughter she buried."

"No," Mirabel said softly. "It doesn't."

Down in the Keeper, the drowned murmured their fraying fragments. *I had a red coat. We went out in a boat. It started with—*

And beneath them all, vast and patient, that pressure at the edge of listening shifted slightly, the way a sleeper shifts, the way something very large turns its attention a few degrees toward a very small sound.

Mirabel felt it notice her noticing.

* * *

She walked home the long way, through streets she barely saw, Wren's unfinished charm in her pocket with its cord wound tight around her fingers like a tether.

She should burn it. That was the sane act. Melt the bone, scatter the ash, tell Mrs. Halloway the piece had cracked in the making, close the door on the whole affair. Twenty years of survival said: *burn it, burn it, burn it.*

Instead, at her own doorstep in the failing light, she took the charm out and held it up to the last of the sun, and listened one more time—carefully, narrowly, the way you listen when you suspect something is listening back.

Wren's voice was weaker now. A candle down to its last inch.

*Are you still there?* the dead girl whispered. *The bone lady. Are you still there?*

"I'm here," Mirabel said aloud, to an empty street, like a madwoman, like a hedge-witch, like a fool.

*It's coming back tonight,* Wren said. *The tall man with the hole face. He comes along the bank when it gets dark and he eats a little more. He's saving me for last, I think. He says—* the voice shuddered— *he says names taste better when everyone who loved them has forgotten them first.*

The sun went down. Somewhere across the city, in a palace on a hill, something wearing a king's body turned its face toward the river district, and smiled with a mouth that was not the king's.

Mirabel Montrose stood on her own doorstep, holding a dead girl's name in a scrap of cow bone, and understood with perfect clarity that her quiet life had ended three weeks ago at the bottom of a brown river, and that she had simply been too afraid to attend the funeral.

She unlocked her shop, lit every lamp she owned, and reached beneath the floorboard for her ledger.

There was work to do.
