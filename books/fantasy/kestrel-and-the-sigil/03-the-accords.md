## 3. The Accords

The rain had been falling on Vellamere for three days, and Sef Rennick had spent all of them walking streets he no longer trusted.

He told himself it was patrol discipline. A captain of the Crown Guard walked his routes; a captain knew his districts; a captain noticed when shopfronts changed hands and when lamplighters stopped lighting certain lanes. But the truth was simpler and uglier: he was looking for a reason to be near Bonecutter's Row, and he had been looking for one for six days, ever since the summons came down from the Ministry of Records with its new vocabulary printed in careful black ink.

*Resonant objects.* That was what they called them now. Not charms. Not relics. Not the little carved bones that grandmothers wore against their hearts because their mothers had worn them before the Accords. *Objects.* The word had a sound like a door closing.

The notice had been posted in the guardhouse at dawn, sealed with the king's sigil — the crown with the binding worked into its circlet, twenty interlocking lines that scholars said represented the twenty years of quiet. Rennick had read it twice while the younger guards pretended not to watch him. It ordered the confiscation of resonant objects from commercial premises. It instructed captains to compile lists of premises where such objects were "habitually produced." And at the bottom, in smaller type, it instructed captains to note any persons who demonstrated "anomalous sensitivity" during questioning.

Anomalous sensitivity. Rennick had turned his cup over in his hands for a long time after reading that, staring at nothing.

He knew exactly one person who would fit that phrase, if anyone in Vellamere did. He owed her more than he could say out loud, which meant he owed her everything, which meant he had been carrying her name like a stone in his boot for twenty years — sometimes forgotten, never absent.

So on the third day of rain he gave his lieutenant the river district and told her he'd be inspecting the Row himself, and he pulled his hood up, and he went.

* * *

Montrose Bone-Carving looked the same as it always had: narrow-fronted, deep-shadowed, its window crowded with memorial charms and buttons and knife-handles and the small pale animals children begged for and parents bought only when they could afford sentiment. Rain ran off the sign in silver threads. Inside, through the glass, he could see her at her bench, hunched over a vise, her good lamp burning even in daylight because Mirabel Montrose had never trusted daylight to show her what bone wanted.

Rennick stood in the rain a moment longer than he needed to. Then he pushed the door open, and the bell above it gave its small cracked note, and she looked up.

She didn't smile. He hadn't expected her to. Mirabel greeted the world the way she handled bone — carefully, testing for flaws.

"Captain." She set down her graver. On the bench between them lay a half-finished charm, a teardrop of femur no bigger than his thumbnail, and he made himself not look at it too long. "You're far from your district."

"I own the whole city this morning," he said. "Ministry paperwork." He let the door swing shut behind him. The shop smelled as it always smelled — dust and oil and something faintly sweet, like old ivory, like the inside of long quiet rooms. "Mirabel. I need you to listen without arguing until I'm finished. Can you do that?"

Her chin came up a fraction. "I've never argued in my life."

"You argue constantly. You just do it silently, which is worse." He stripped off his wet gloves, finger by finger, buying time, arranging the words. Twenty years a guard had taught him how to deliver bad orders. It had never taught him how to warn someone he loved about them. "There's an initiative out of the Ministry of Records. Sealed with the king's own sigil, which means nobody below the Chancellor dared soften it. They're calling it an inventory. It isn't an inventory."

"What is it?"

"They're collecting resonant objects. Charms, relics, anything carved from bone or stone that holds — " he gestured vaguely, hating the vagueness — "*charge*. Anything that sings, in the old talk. Shopkeepers get receipts. The receipts say the items are held for study. There is no study. There's a furnace behind the Ministry courtyard; my sergeant saw the ash."

Mirabel was very still. Stillness was her armor; he'd learned that early. Most people flinched or fussed when frightened. She went quiet as a held breath.

"And the second part," she said.

Of course she'd heard the shape of it under his words. She always heard the shape underneath. "They want names. Carvers. Anyone whose work might resonate. Captains are to submit lists." He met her eyes. "My list has one name on it."

"Then take me off it."

"It already has one name on it, Mirabel. It's had one name on it for twenty years, and every year I write the same report saying the bone-carver on Bonecutter's Row is a dull woman with dull goods and no ear whatsoever, and every year some clerk files it, and every year we both pretend that's a life." His voice came out rougher than he intended. He lowered it. "But there are new men in the Ministry now. Young ones. They don't know my handwriting. They've started re-reading old reports, and last week one of them asked me why a carver with no ear kept a lamp burning at odd hours. They're watching the Row. I needed you to hear it from me and not from a knock on your door."

For a long moment the only sound was the rain on the window and the small tick of the shop's clock, which had been wrong since before the Accords and which Mirabel refused to fix because, she said once, a lie you keep wound tells the time better than a truth you neglect.

Then she said, quietly: "Why now? Twenty years, Sef. The quiet's held for twenty years. What changed?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. That was the question he'd been turning over all week like a coin worn smooth.

"I don't know," he said. "But I'll tell you what I've seen. Three weeks ago the Ministry recalled every record older than the Accords for 'archival restoration.' Whole shelves gone from the Hall of Deeds. Families can't prove land titles anymore. Last month a woman came to the guardhouse in tears because she couldn't find any record of her mother's burial — not the plot, not the stone, nothing. The clerk told her, very politely, that no such person appeared to have existed." He shook his head. "And the king hasn't held a public audience in two months. The ministers speak *for* him now. In his voice. Word for word, they say, so precisely it makes the chambermaids cross themselves."

Something moved behind Mirabel's eyes — not fear exactly. Recognition. He saw her hand drift toward the half-finished charm on the bench and stop, deliberately, halfway.

"Sef," she said slowly. "When did the woman's mother die?"

"How should I know? Years ago, presumably."

"No. When? Ask yourself. You're a guard; you notice things. Did anyone in that family remember the date? The season? Could the daughter tell you what her mother's voice sounded like?"

He opened his mouth to say it was grief, that grief frayed memory, that he'd seen it a hundred times. But standing there in the warm dim shop with the rain coming down, he found he couldn't make the answer come. Because he'd spoken to that woman herself, briefly, while her complaint was logged. And he remembered — now, suddenly, with a cold sliding feeling — that she had described her mother three times in the space of ten minutes, and each description had been of a different woman. Different hair. Different hands. As if the memory were a garment she kept putting on wrong.

"The records first," Mirabel said softly, almost to herself. "Then the memories. Or maybe — " She stopped. Pressed her lips together.

"Maybe what?"

"Nothing. Old stories." She picked up her graver and put it down again, restless, which was unlike her. "Sef. If they come here — "

"They won't come yet. I'll slow them. I've slowed them twenty years; I can slow them a while longer." He said it firmly, and then, because lying to her had never once worked: "But I can't slow them forever, and I can't protect you from what's driving this, because I don't know what's driving this. That's the truth of it. Something's changed at the top of the city, and it's reaching downward, and I'm starting to think the Accords aren't a wall anymore. I think they're a net. And somebody's drawing it in."

* * *

He had been a young guard the night magic fell.

Twenty years, and he still could not walk past the Grand Plaza without his body remembering it — the cold, first of all. People expected fire for a night like that, torches and bonfires, but what Rennick remembered was cold: a cold that came up out of the ground itself, as though the stones had exhaled twenty years of stored winter all at once. The plaza had been packed shoulder to shoulder, forty thousand people, and every breath hung white, and nobody spoke. Even the children were silent. Later, people would claim they'd been awed. Rennick knew better. They had been *held*. Held the way a rabbit is held by the shape of the thing circling above it.

King Aldric had stood on the steps of the Hall with the new crown on his head — a crown most of them were seeing for the first time, rushed from the royal smiths in the final weeks, its circlet worked with those twenty interlocking lines. He had been a young king then. Handsome in a tired way. Rennick had been stationed close enough to see his face, and what he remembered — what he had spent twenty years telling himself he misremembered — was that Aldric had looked *frightened*. Not solemn. Not resolute. Frightened, the way a man is frightened on a scaffold, and reading from a paper placed in front of him by a minister whose name Rennick had never learned, because the man stood slightly behind the king's shoulder, slightly out of the torchlight, and left the city within the year.

*Let it be known,* Aldric had read, and his voice had cracked on the fourth word, and the minister's hand had steadied the page, *that the age of the gods in Vellamere is ended.*

The binding had gone in like a nail driven through the sky. Rennick had no other way to describe it, then or since. There was a sound — not loud, worse than loud, a sound felt in the teeth and the breastbone, a single deep note as if the world were a bell struck once — and every candle in the plaza guttered flat, and every prayer died on every lip mid-word. He had watched a priestess on the plaza's edge open her mouth in blessing and simply stop, swaying, her face going slack with a loss so total she didn't even weep. Forty thousand people had felt something leave them. Nobody could afterward say what.

That was the cruelty of it, Rennick thought now, watching Mirabel turn a charm slowly under her lamp. That was the genius of the thing. You cannot mourn what you cannot recall. The city had woken the next morning lighter and could not say what weight was missing. Within a year, people spoke of the old gods the way you speak of a dream — embarrassed, vague, already doubting it happened. Within five years, the temples were granaries and record offices. Within ten, the purges.

The purges. He made himself go there, because Mirabel deserved the whole truth tonight, and the whole truth had a night attached to it he had never told anyone entire.

He had been nineteen. Third-year guard, proud of his boots, assigned to cordon duty on Tanner's Lane because the Crown had decided hedge-witches were resonant persons and resonant persons were to be detained. He had stood at the mouth of the lane holding a pike he did not know how to use, watching older guards drag weavers and herbalists and one ancient man who sold love-charms out of their homes, and he had done nothing, because doing nothing was the order, and he was nineteen, and obedience fit him like a uniform two sizes too large.

Except once. Once, at the end of that long ugly night, a cart had come down the lane with a girl in it — a carver's apprentice, seventeen maybe, wrists bound with cord, sitting very straight among the confiscated goods. And as the cart passed Rennick's post, she had looked at him. Not pleading. *Assessing.* The way she probably looked at bone.

And then she had said, clearly, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as his ears and no farther: "Your sergeant's list is in his left glove. The real one. He burned the copy."

Rennick had not understood. Not then. He understood three days later, when the detention rolls were posted and the girl's name — Montrose, M. — was marked *released, insufficient evidence*, while eleven others from her street were transported north. He understood years later, when he finally worked up the courage to find the shop on Bonecutter's Row and discovered that the apprentice had become the carver, and that she had known — somehow, impossibly, through whatever sense the Accords had failed to carve out of her — that a corrupt sergeant had planted a resonant object in her master's stock, and that she had saved not only herself but her deaf old master, whose name was on the same list, by getting word to the one guard in the cordon whose conscience was louder than his career.

She had used him. That was the joke he never told. She had reached into a crowd of armed men and picked out the one soft heart, and squeezed, and walked free. And he had spent twenty years being grateful for it, which was either devotion or the longest con ever run on a single guard, and he had long ago decided he didn't care which.

Loyalty, he had learned, was both shield and cage. Her silence had shielded him from his own worst night. And the same silence had caged them both ever since — two people who knew what the other was, bound by a debt neither could repay and neither could name aloud, pretending across a shop counter for two decades that they were merely a captain and a tradeswoman.

* * *

"Say it plainly," Mirabel said now. "What happens if they come."

He dragged his mind back to the warm shop, the rain, the present tense. "Confiscation first. Then questions. Then, if the answers displease them — " He didn't finish. She nodded as if he had.

"And you?"

"I falsify reports. I lose paperwork. I assign myself elsewhere on the nights it matters." He flexed his bare hands, feeling the rain-damp cold in them. "It's not much. I know it's not much. But it's what I have, and I'm telling you so you can decide what you need to do with the time I buy. Bury the resonant pieces. Move them. Whatever it is you actually do down here, Mirabel — " and he watched her face very carefully as he said it — "do less of it for a while."

Something flickered across her expression. Not guilt. Confirmation. She had never admitted to anything, not once in twenty years, and she didn't now. But she looked at him the way you look at a lock you've finally found the key for.

"There's a thing I've been hearing," she said at last, quietly. "In the work. Something wrong. Something big. I thought it was me — grief does strange things to the ear. But you're describing a city that's forgetting itself, Sef. Records vanishing. Names unraveling. A king who speaks in a minister's voice." Her fingers had found the half-finished charm again, turning it, and he saw that her knuckles were white around it. "I don't think your net is political. I think something is drawing it in from the other side."

A log shifted in the little stove. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the sign, running silver down the glass.

"That," Rennick said slowly, "is the kind of sentence that gets a captain reassigned to a border fort."

"I know."

"It's also the kind of sentence I've been half-thinking for a month and refusing to say."

"I know that too." She set the charm down and looked up at him fully, and for a moment he saw not the careful shopkeeper but the girl in the cart, straight-backed, assessing, brave in a way that had nothing to do with comfort. "Then we're both in trouble. Go home, Captain. Walk a different route tomorrow. And Sef — if the Ministry sends someone to the Row before you can warn me again — "

"I'll be the one at the door," he said. "That much I can promise."

He believed it when he said it. He would believe it for another eight chapters of his life, through the market fires and the White Cells and the night he finally chose, openly, irreversibly, between the law he wore and the truth he owed. But that evening, pulling his hood up against the rain outside the darkened shop, he felt the first thin tremor of doubt pass through the promise like a crack through ice — because somewhere south of the river, a bell he had never heard before rang once, deep, felt rather than heard, in the teeth and the breastbone.

And every dog in Vellamere began, softly, to howl.
