## 4. What the Regent Wears

The summons came on a Tuesday, folded inside a delivery of calf bones that Mirabel hadn't ordered.

She knew at once it wasn't a summons in any official sense—no seal of the Crown, no wax, no threat dressed in calligraphy. It was a printed broadsheet, the kind hawkers sold for a copper outside the Guildhall: *By Grace of the King, a Public Audience Shall Be Held Upon the Feast of Saint Vessia, That the Loyal Subjects of Vellamere May Present Their Petitions and Grievances Before the Throne.* Beneath that, in smaller type, the usual litany of conditions. No weapons. No banners. No objects of resonance.

That last phrase was new. Two years ago it had been *no enchanted items*, and before that *no witchwork*. The language kept changing because the thing it described kept not existing, officially, and yet everyone in Vellamere knew exactly what it meant. They knew the way you knew weather coming—not through evidence but through the animal weight behind the sternum.

Mirabel read the broadsheet three times at her workbench, her hands still dusted with bone meal, and told herself she would not go.

Then she went.

* * *

She could not have said, afterward, what decided her—or rather, she could have said it perfectly well and chose not to, because the truth was a girl's voice saying *the king ate my name* in a riverbed two weeks gone, and a mother in her shop yesterday who had brought a lock of hair to carve into a charm and then stood blinking at it as though she'd forgotten why she was holding it. *I'm sorry,* the woman had said. *Whose is this? I know I loved someone with this hair. I can feel the shape of loving them but there's nothing inside the shape.*

Mirabel had carved the charm anyway. She always carved the charm. But when the woman left, Mirabel had sat very still among her shelves of little deaths and little comforts and understood that the fraying was spreading, that it moved outward from the drowned like rot through wet wood, and that every road out of that rot led uphill to the palace.

So: the Feast of Saint Vessia, and the long walk from Tanners' Row up through the Weavers' Quarter, past the censors' depot where men in grey aprons were feeding a bonfire with books and painted icons and—at least one crate, by the smell—carved bone. Mirabel did not look directly at the fire. Looking felt like volunteering.

The crowd thickened as she climbed. Palace Square had been built to hold forty thousand and held it now, a sea of wool coats and breath-steam under a sky the color of old pewter. Guards lined the perimeter in their slate-and-silver, and above them, on the balcony of the Hall of Audiences, a canopy of white silk stirred without wind.

Mirabel worked herself toward the front with the patience of twenty years' practice at being unremarkable. She was good at crowds. Crowds were the safest place in Vellamere for a woman who heard the dead; grief was so dense here that one more whisper vanished into it like a drop into a river. She kept her hands in her pockets, curled around the smooth blank pebble she carried on days like this—a stone with no carving, no voice, nothing to listen through. A plug against the world's noise.

It didn't help today.

* * *

The king appeared at midday.

He came onto the balcony alone, which was itself strange—the protocol demanded chamberlains and heralds and at least a captain of the guard—and the crowd's roar rose and then, oddly, thinned, as though the sound itself wasn't sure it wanted to touch him.

Aldric looked well. That was the first wrongness. He was fifty-eight years old and had ruled through plague year and famine year and the purges, and he should have been bent by now, silvered and stooped. Instead he stood straight as a lance, his dark hair barely touched with grey, his face smooth in a way that made Mirabel think of fruit kept too long in a cellar—preserved, not young. He wore the crown, of course. He always wore the crown now; even the broadsheets noted it, joked gently about it, *our king who loves his father's circlet more than his own bed.* The Sigil Accords were carved into that circlet, ring within ring, and no one had seen the king bareheaded in six years.

He raised his hands and the crowd hushed.

"Children of Vellamere," he said, and his voice carried impossibly far, reaching the back rows without effort or echo. "I see your faces. I hold your sorrows."

And here was the second wrongness, the one that stopped Mirabel's heart and restarted it sideways: the dead began to answer him.

Not aloud. Not visibly. But all around her, in the packed press of living bodies, Mirabel felt the thin presences she spent her life politely ignoring—the residue of grief that clings to places where the dead were loved, the faint cold fingerprints on the shoulders of mourners—all of it turning, orienting, flowing toward the balcony like iron filings toward a lodestone. The king stood in the white silk light and the city's dead leaned toward him the way wheat leans after a reaper.

*He hears them too,* Mirabel thought. *Or something in him does. Something is drinking.*

She had heard the vast wrong pressure once before, two weeks ago, pressing at the edges of a drowned child's whisper. Then it had been distant, oceanic, a whale-song heard through fathoms of water. Now she stood close enough to feel its attention move across the crowd, slow as a searchlight, and she understood with total clarity that what wore Aldric was not a ghost, not a god, not anything the old priests had names for. It was patient. That was the worst of it. Gods hungered loudly; this thing hungered the way winter hungers, without malice, without hurry, absolutely certain of spring.

"Bring me your petitions," the king said, smiling, and the smile fit his face the way a glove fits a hand that isn't quite the right size. "I remember each of you. I will never forget you."

Around Mirabel, people wept with relief. An old man near her sobbed, *he remembers us, he says he'll never forget us,* and pressed forward with his petition, and Mirabel watched the guards admit petitioners one by one up the marble stair, and she watched each of them come back down changed. Not visibly. Nothing you could point to. But they came down lighter, emptier-eyed, comforted in a way that had had something scooped out of the middle of it. A woman descended clutching a paper and murmuring, *he knew my son's name, he said my son's name,* and when Mirabel caught her elbow and asked, gently, what the son's name was, the woman smiled up at her with shining eyes and said, "Isn't it wonderful? He knew it."

She couldn't say it. The name was gone. The king had given it back to her as wonder and taken it away as fact.

Mirabel let go of her arm and stepped back into the crowd, and her whole body had gone cold, and the pebble in her pocket might as well have been air.

* * *

She should have left then. She would tell herself later, many times, that she should have left then. But the line of petitioners was moving and the crowd surged and carried her half a dozen steps along the barrier before she could fight free, and in those steps the king's gaze swept the square again—that slow, tasting sweep—and passed over her—

—and stopped.

It was nothing. It was a fraction of a second, less than a heartbeat, the sort of pause a man gives a face he almost recognizes. But Mirabel had spent her whole life listening to things that were almost nothing, and she knew an ear turning toward her the way a deer knows a bowstring.

The pressure came. Not the diffuse ocean-hunger of before but a point of it, a needle, threading through ten thousand living minds to find the one that was listening back. She felt it touch the edges of her like cold water rising around the ankles. *Here is one who hears,* the needle said, without words, without language, in pure appetite. *Here is one full of names.*

Mirabel did the only thing she could think to do. She opened her mouth and prayed.

Not to a god—the gods were sealed, the gods were behind the binding, praying to them was like shouting down a well—but the way her grandmother had taught her, the hedge-prayer, the small honest words meant for whatever might still be minding the world. *I am nobody. I am a bone-carver on Tanners' Row. I carve charms for mothers. I am thinking about dinner. I am thinking about nothing.* She poured the thoughts out flat and gray and dull, a puddle of ordinary, and wrapped it around herself like a cloak, and into the center of the cloak, hidden deep, she folded the one true thing: *the king ate my name, the king ate my name, the king ate my name.*

The needle hovered. Pressed. Somewhere above her, on the balcony, King Aldric tilted his head—a small motion, curious, a dog hearing a whistle below the range of human ears—and for one suspended moment Mirabel stood in the sight of the thing that had whispered the Accords into existence, the thing that had eaten a kingdom's memory one comfortable forgetting at a time, and it considered her the way a diner considers an unfamiliar dish.

Then a bell rang somewhere in the palace, marking the hour, and the audience was declared at its end, and the needle withdrew, and the king raised his hand in blessing and turned back into the silk-shadowed interior, and the crowd roared its love at his retreating back.

Mirabel walked. She did not run; running was memorable. She walked with the tide of dispersing thousands, down the great avenue, past the burning depot, past the censors in their grey aprons, her face arranged into the mild blankness of a woman thinking about dinner, while inside her everything screamed.

At the bottom of the avenue, where the road forked toward Tanners' Row, she stopped at a water-trough as if to splash her face, and looked at her reflection instead. A middle-aged woman. Bone dust still in the creases of her knuckles. Nobody.

"He noticed me," she whispered to the water. "It noticed me."

The reflection said nothing, but beneath the trough, beneath the cobbles, beneath the whole buried nervous system of the old city, something answered—a murmur in the stones, faint as a pulse through a wall. Not the Regent. Something else. Something older, thinner, starved, the way a voice goes after twenty years of disuse.

*Bone-carver,* it said. *You heard us in the ossuary dark. You heard the girl in the river. Come to the tannery when the lamps are lit. Come to the ones who keep the names.*

Mirabel straightened, heart hammering, scanning the square—but the crowd flowed on indifferent, and the voice was already fading, already sinking back beneath the silence the Accords had laid over the world like snow.

The tannery. Odile Ferran's ground, if half the rumors were true—the illegal chapel, the congregation of the half-forgotten. The place she had spent twenty years making certain she never had a reason to visit.

She looked back up the avenue, once, at the palace rising pale against the pewter sky, at the balcony where a dead man's smile had blessed a city it was eating. *I will never forget you,* the thing had promised, and the crowd had wept for joy, and not one of them had asked the obvious question, the question Mirabel now carried home through the gathering dusk like a coal cupped in both hands:

What use was a promise of remembrance—from a thing that fed on nothing else?

She turned down the lane toward Tanners' Row, and the lamplighters were just beginning their rounds, and she followed the lights.
