## 5. The Lamplighters

The drowned girl's name was written in tallow smoke.

Mirabel had followed her for three days now—through the fish market where a mother paused over a basket of eels and wept without knowing why, past the schoolhouse where the register had a gap in it shaped like a child, down to the river stairs where the water still remembered small feet slapping stone. The voice came to her in fragments, mostly at dusk, when the bone charm she'd carried home from the riverbank warmed against her sternum like a held breath.

*He ate my name. The king ate my name. Please. Somebody has to say it.*

But Mirabel couldn't say it. That was the horror of it. She had tried, standing on those wet stairs with her eyes closed and her hands cupped around the little carved bird she'd made from a gull's wing-bone, and what came out of her mouth was nothing. A blank. A smooth place in her own mind where a syllable should have been, worn away like a river stone.

The dead did not lose their names when they died. She had known that since childhood, since the first whisper came up through a grandmother's knucklebone: *I am Maren, I am Maren, tell them I am Maren.* Names were the last thing the dead carried, the one coin they kept in their pockets crossing over. If this child's name was gone—if it had been eaten out of the world so thoroughly that even Mirabel's gift slid off the absence like rain off oiled leather—then whatever had taken it was not death, and not grief, and not any of the ordinary violences a city does to its children.

It was something that fed.

The trail ended—or rather, thinned to a thread—at the Tanners' Reach, the crooked lane behind the rendering yards where the smell of lye and hides hung so thick you could chew it. The charm grew warm here. Warmer than it had ever been, warm enough that Mirabel tucked it inside her glove and pressed her fist against her ribs as though holding a wound closed.

And there were the lamps.

They hung from the eaves of the tannery—a long, low building of blackened brick squatting against the city wall—and they were wrong for the neighborhood. Every other window in Vellamere's poor quarters went dark at curfew bell, because oil cost coin and coin was watched. But these lamps burned steady and low, seven of them along the eave, hooded in tin so their light fell straight down and pooled on the cobbles like spilled honey. Lamplighters' lamps, she thought at first. But lamplighters worked the main streets, and these were set too close together, too deliberate. Not lighting a way.

Marking a door.

The door itself was unremarkable—iron-banded oak, scarred by decades of hides being dragged through. What was remarkable was that Mirabel could not remember passing it before, though she had walked the Tanners' Reach a hundred times in twenty years of careful, invisible living. She knew every shopfront on this lane. She knew which step wobbled outside the chandler's, which drain grate sang in the rain. This door sat in her memory the way a tooth sits in a gum after it's been pulled: a socket, an ache, no tooth.

*They've hidden it,* she thought. *Or something has.*

The charm in her glove pulsed once, twice, like a small heart deciding.

Mirabel Montrose had survived the purges by being no one. By carving silence into herself the way she carved flourishes into bone—patiently, deeply, until the quiet became part of the material. Twenty years of keeping her head down and her gift folded up small and her shop smelling only of glue and dust to anyone who might sniff too close. Twenty years of hearing the dead whisper and saying nothing, doing nothing, being nobody.

And now she stood in front of a hidden door in a forbidden chapel, following a dead child's plea, about to knock.

*Somebody has to say it.*

She knocked.

* * *

The door opened before the third knock finished, which meant someone had been standing on the other side listening—which meant, in turn, that whoever lived here expected visitors of exactly her kind, or feared them, or both.

The woman who opened it was sixty if she was a day, broad as a barrel and built like one too, with forearms that spoke of decades hauling hides and a face that had stopped pretending to be pleasant somewhere around the second King Aldric festival it hadn't believed in. Her gray hair was bound up in a kerchief spotted with tallow. She looked Mirabel over once, top to bottom, the way a butcher looks at a carcass.

"You're not from the Reach," she said.

"No."

"Your shoes are good but your hands aren't." Her eyes narrowed at Mirabel's right hand, at the fine scars and the callus ridge along the thumb where a carver's blade sits. "Bone-worker."

"Carver," Mirabel said carefully. "Memorial charms. Legal ones."

"There's no such thing." But the woman stepped back anyway, opening the door wide enough to show a slice of candlelit interior, and the warmth that rolled out was not just heat. It was thick with voices—the dead kind, dozens of them, murmuring under the floorboards and inside the walls like bees in a hive. Mirabel's breath caught. In twenty years she had never stood in a place so *loud*.

"You hear it," the woman said, watching her face. It wasn't a question.

"I—" Mirabel's training clamped down, the old reflex, deny and shrink. Then the charm in her glove flared hot, and from somewhere below—beneath the floor, beneath the foundations—a child's voice rose thin and clear through the murmur:

*She came. You said somebody would come and she came.*

The woman heard nothing, obviously. But she saw everything: saw Mirabel sway, saw her hand press flat against her breastbone, saw the tears come up fast and unwanted.

"Odile Ferran," the woman said, extending a forearm rather than a hand, the grip of trade, of work. "I render fat and I tend souls, and both trades are illegal after dark. Come in before the street sees you standing there crying."

* * *

Down was the only direction. Odile led her through a hide-curing room rank enough to make her eyes stream, past vats of lime solution breathing their chemical fog, to a stair cut into the bedrock and lit by the same hooded lamps she'd seen outside. The walls sweated. The air tasted of tallow and old stone and, underneath it all, faintly, sweetly, of incense that had no business existing in Vellamere—frankincense and dried yarrow, the old funeral blend, the one the priests used to burn before the Accords outlawed the burning.

The stair opened into a vaulted cellar that had been a chapel once and probably had been a crypt before that. And it was full of people.

Not many—thirty, perhaps forty—but arranged with a care that made them look like more. Benches of rough plank faced a plain stone altar on which burned three candles and a lamp with a lens of horn. The congregation was the poorest-looking crowd Mirabel had ever seen gathered anywhere, and also the most orderly: men and women in patched wool, a few children sitting unnaturally still, an old soldier missing an arm who sat with his remaining hand flat on his knee like a man at attention. Some dozed. Some stared at the candles. Several turned toward the stair as Mirabel descended, and their faces—

Their faces were what stopped her on the bottom step.

They were *smooth*. Not young-smooth, not peaceful-smooth. Smooth the way a statue is smooth, the way a thing is smooth when detail has been sanded off it. She looked at the old soldier and could not have said whether his hair was brown or gray. She looked at a woman in the front row and knew instantly that she was a mother, and could not have said of whom. Their faces kept sliding, like names on the tips of tongues.

"They can't hold themselves," Odile said quietly beside her. "That's why they're here. The chapel holds them a while longer. The lamps, the rites, the incense—it's scaffolding. Props a memory up while it falls."

"What happened to them?"

"The same thing that happened to your drowned girl." Odile's voice was level, but Mirabel heard the effort of the levelness, the way you hear the effort in a person carrying something heavy downstairs. "Come and sit. What I'm going to tell you goes against twenty years of law, and I'd rather you were seated."

* * *

She told it in pieces, between questions, the way you tell a thing you've told yourself many times alone at night.

The Regent. That was the name the old texts gave it—not a god, though it had passed among gods once, and not a demon either, though it hungered like one. A thing that lived on memory the way ticks live on blood. It had come to Aldric twenty years ago wearing the shape of counsel, whispering in the king's ear during the worst year of the plague-riots, offering him peace, order, quiet. All it asked was a binding. A sigil carved into the crown, sealing the old gods behind it—for gods, Odile explained, are made of memory, sustained by it, and a god sealed away is a feast left covered on a table.

"But it didn't stop with the gods," Mirabel said. Her voice sounded far away to herself.

"No. Gods were the meal. People are the *anchoring*." Odile leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the deep lines of her face. "A thing like that, feeding on memory in a world that remembers—it thins. Stretches. Starts to come apart at the edges like old cloth. So it takes names. Little ones, at first. A dead child here, a forgotten widow there—people whose grip on the world was already loose, whose names were carried by few mouths. Each name it swallows gives it weight. Density. Something to be *made of*, since it isn't made of anything itself." She gestured at the congregation. "These are the ones it's been eating longest. Half-forgotten already when they came to me. I keep them lit a little longer. That's all I can do. Keep them lit."

"And the drowned girl?"

Something moved through Odile's face then—grief, yes, but underneath it a guilt so worn it had gone smooth as the congregants' features.

"Nesta," she said, and the name came out of her like a splinter coming out of flesh, slow and painful and relieving. "Her name was Nesta. She was six. Fell from the mill bridge two winters back. Her mother brought her boot here afterward because the mother had started forgetting—couldn't remember the color of her hair, whether she'd learned her letters. The chapel held what was left of Nesta for a long time. Held her name, even." Odile closed her eyes. "Three weeks ago the Regent found us. Took the name out of my keeping like a thief lifting a purse. Left the rest of her, the little ghost of her, wandering the riverbank begging anyone who'd listen to *say it, please, somebody say it.*"

"That's why she called me." Mirabel's hand had found the charm again without her noticing. "Through the bone. Dead things reach me. Always have."

"Then you're the first new ear this chapel has had in twenty years." Odile studied her with those butcher's eyes, but softer now. "You understand what I'm telling you. There's a thing wearing the king of Vellamere. It eats the past to keep its shape. And it has just learned that a bone-carver in Tanner's Reach can hear the crumbs it leaves behind."

Mirabel felt the fear arrive, cold and complete, and was surprised to find that beneath it, wedged down deep like a coin in a pocket, was something else.

Relief.

Twenty years of whispers she'd refused to answer, of dead voices she'd soothed and sent away and never once followed. Twenty years of *no*. And here, in a tallow-smelling cellar full of the half-forgotten, sat forty people who needed exactly what she had spent her whole life hiding, and a dead child upstairs in the river-dark who had begged her by name—by the *absence* of her name—to come.

"I never wanted this," she said aloud.

"No one does," Odile said. "Fellowship finds you the way drowning finds a swimmer. You can fight it or you can float." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and held out her forearm again. "Stay for the rite. We light the lamps for the eaten at moonrise. You'll want to see it. And afterward—" the butcher's look returned, appraising, decided, "—afterward, I think you and I need to talk about what a bone-carver can build."

Above them, muffled through thirty feet of stone and earth, the curfew bell rang across Vellamere, and in the dark of the river, a small voice that no longer had a name began, patiently, to hope.
