## 6. Twenty Years of Quiet

The shop was quiet in the way Mirabel had made it—deliberately, carefully, the way other people made beds or said their prayers. Quiet was not an absence here. It was a craft.

She had built it bone by bone. The walls were lined with cedar shelving that swallowed sound; the floorboards had been packed with wool and ash beneath; even the window shutters were carved from a wood she'd never learned the name of, bought from a peddler who swore it drank noise like a drunk drinks coin. Customers stepped inside and lowered their voices without knowing why, the way people do in churches and sickrooms. They thought it was reverence. It was architecture.

Because Mirabel Montrose heard the dead, and for twenty years she had taught herself to hear them quietly.

*You're doing it again,* Tam's voice said, from the chair by the stove where he always sat in her memory, boots up on the rungs, whittling something he'd never finish. *Building your little coffin and calling it a shop.*

"It's not a coffin," she murmured to the empty room, to the voice that lived only behind her eyes now. "It's a life."

*It's a very nice life,* Tam agreed, amiable as ever, dead these nineteen years and still impossible to argue with. *Very tidy. Very small. You know what Father used to say about small things.*

"He said good things come in them."

*He said small things rattle.* The knife whispered along the stick in his hands—a sound she had invented for him, really, reconstructed from a hundred evenings until it was more real than any true memory could be. *Rattle, Mira. Like bones in a box.*

She put down her chisel and looked at him. He was seventeen forever, all elbows and grin, a smear of charcoal on his jaw because he'd been drawing sigils on the kitchen table when the soldiers came—or so she remembered it, though she had learned long ago not to trust the details. Memory was soft clay. She had pressed her thumbs into it too many times.

The purges had begun in the spring of the Accords, and they had lasted three years, and they had not been called purges. They had been called *harmonization*. The word had a gentle sound, like tuning an instrument, and that had been the cruelty of it: men in grey coats going door to door with tuning forks of cold iron, listening for resonance, for the hum of magic in walls and wells and children. When the fork sang, they took what sang.

They had taken the hedge-witch Marta Voss from the house two streets over, and after that no one spoke of Marta Voss at all—not because they were forbidden, exactly, but because speaking her name felt like holding a lit match in a room full of straw. Names became dangerous things. Grief became contraband.

And Mirabel, who had heard the dead since before she could write, who had learned her letters from a gravestone because the voices taught her which marks meant *here lies*, understood very quickly what harmonization would mean for a girl like her.

So she carved silence into herself.

That was how she thought of it, then and now. Not suppression—that sounded passive, like holding your breath. This was work. Every morning she sat at her bench and she cut, into small pieces of bone, the shapes of quiet: a spiral with no opening, a knot with no end, a line that returned to its own beginning. She wore them against her skin. She slept with them under her tongue like coins for a ferryman. And the dead, who had always crowded her ears like sparrows fighting over crumbs, drew back—not gone, never gone, but muffled, distant, polite. A murmur instead of a choir.

*You know what you actually did,* Tam said one evening, when the shop was closed and the lamp was low and she was oiling the cedar shelves just to have something to do with her hands. He didn't look up from his whittling. He rarely did, when he was saying the true things. *You didn't carve silence. You carved yourself out of the world. There's a difference.*

"Thank you, philosopher."

*I'm serious, Mira. You turned hearing into hiding. You had a gift—*

"I had a death sentence." Her voice came out harder than she intended, and the dead in the walls stirred faintly, restless, and she pressed her palm flat against the spiral charm at her throat until they settled. "Do you remember what they did to the Kestrel boy? Eleven years old. His grandmother taught him a blessing for sore throats. One blessing. They burned him in the market square and his mother wasn't allowed to scream, and afterward—" she stopped. Swallowed. "Afterward nobody could tell me what his mother's face looked like. Because grief was illegal, and faces forget faster than names."

Tam set down his knife. In her memory he crossed the room, and in her memory he sat beside her, and in her memory he said, gently, the thing he had probably never said at all:

*Then we'll be careful together. That's all. We'll be careful, and we'll be quiet, and someday—*

"Someday," she finished with him, because she always did, because the sentence had worn a groove in her like water in stone. Someday the Accords would fall. Someday the king would wake up—if he'd ever been asleep—and unmake what he'd signed. Someday she could open every window in her skull and let the dead sing themselves hoarse.

Nineteen years of somedays. Nineteen years of careful.

* * *

There were rules, and she had written them herself, and she had kept them the way monks keep vows.

Rule the first: never carve for the living. A charm for a living person carried their voice in it, and voices resonated, and resonant objects were the first thing the iron forks found. So Mirabel worked only for the dead—for memorial charms, grave-goods, the little carved tokens mourners tucked into coffins. The dead didn't resonate. The dead had already given everything up except their names, and names, carved properly, went silent as snowfall.

Rule the second: never let them speak through you in public. In private, in the shop, with the cedar and the wool and the ash, she could listen—briefly, carefully, the way you lean over a well. But the street was iron-fork country. On the street she was plain Mirabel Montrose, bone-carver, a woman of no particular gifts, whose hands were skilled and whose ears were ordinary.

Rule the third—the rule beneath all the others, the keystone of her whole careful life: *never learn anything worth dying for.*

She had broken it last week, and she knew it, and some traitorous part of her was glad.

The drowned girl's plea still moved through her like weather. *The king ate my name.* Mirabel had spent six nights telling herself it was nothing—a fragment, a fever-dream of the river, the babble of a child's ghost who hadn't yet learned the shape of what had happened to her. Children's ghosts babbled. She'd heard enough of them.

But she had also gone back to the riverbank twice, and both times she had felt it again: that vast wrong pressure at the edge of the whispers, patient as ice, the sense of something enormous turning its attention through the dead like a man searching a drawer. And she had seen the mother—the one from the drowning, the one who'd bought the half-finished charm—and watched her struggle, genuinely struggle, to describe her own daughter's face. Watching a mother lose a child twice was a thing Mirabel would carry for the rest of her life, however long the Crown allowed it to be.

*You know what this is,* Tam said. He was sitting on the bench beside her now, legs dangling, watching her hands move over a blank piece of shinbone she hadn't decided what to make yet. *You've known since the river. Say it out loud. Saying it makes it real, and you hate things being real.*

"The binding is failing," she said quietly. To the bone. To him. To the dark. "Whatever the king locked away twenty years ago—it's getting out. Or it was never locked away at all. Maybe it's been wearing the lock like a coat." She turned the bone in the lamplight. "And it eats memory, Tam. It eats names. That's why the mother can't see her daughter's face. That's why the drowned girl said what she said."

*And?*

"And I'm a bone-carver with three rules and a very nice quiet life, and if I follow those rules, I will sit in this shop until the day something comes down my street eating everything I love, and I will die tidy." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Small things rattle."

*There she is.* Tam grinned, and it hurt, the way his grin always hurt, because she had built it herself out of spare parts and longing and it fit him perfectly anyway. *Mira. Listen to me. Whatever you decide—you decide it knowing this: I'm proud of you. I was proud of you when you were eight and talking to old Widow Crane's ghost at supper. I was proud of you when you survived. I'll be proud of whatever you do next. Even if what you do next is stupid.*

"Especially if it's stupid."

*Especially then. Stupid runs in the family.*

She wanted to ask him—she had wanted to ask him for nineteen years, and she had never once let herself, because the question was a door and doors opened from both sides—but tonight, with the binding failing and the dead whispering wrong, the question climbed up her throat on its own.

"How did you die, Tam?"

The whittling knife stopped.

In her memory, he looked at her, and for the first time in nineteen years of invented evenings, his expression was not quite the one she had given him. Something older moved underneath it. Something tired, and fond, and sorry.

*Mira—*

"You were there when the soldiers came. I remember the charcoal on your jaw. I remember the door. But I don't remember after. I don't remember the funeral. I don't remember—" Her hands had stopped moving. The bone lay blank between them. "I've checked, Tam. I've gone through everything, every year, every season, and there's a hole where you should be. A smooth place, like a scar. Like something was *carved out*."

The lamp flickered. In the walls, the dead had gone utterly, unnaturally still—the way birds go still before a storm, the way a forest holds its breath around a wolf.

*Some questions,* Tam said softly, and his voice was fading, thinning, like a tune played further and further down a street, *you shouldn't ask while the walls have ears. Some holes are load-bearing, little sister. You pull one out and the whole house—*

"TAM."

But he was gone, or the shape of him was, and the shop was quiet again—her quiet, her crafted quiet—and it had never felt less like safety and more like a lid.

Mirabel sat alone at her bench for a long time. Then she did the thing she had trained herself out of for twenty years: she reached up, and she touched the knot-charm at her throat, and she loosened it—one thread, just one—and listened.

The dead of Vellamere came flooding in at the edges, cautious as cats, and beneath their murmur she heard it again: the vast slow turning of the hungry thing, somewhere above her, somewhere central, somewhere crowned. Searching drawers. Eating names.

And threaded through all of it, faint as a hairline crack in glass, a new sound. Boots. Many boots, moving through the night city in formation, stopping at doors.

Stopping, she realized with a cold that started in her spine and spread outward, at doors on *her* street.

The knock, when it came, was three measured blows—and through the cedar and the wool and the ash, through twenty years of carved silence, Mirabel heard it like a bell.
