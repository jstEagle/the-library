## 7. A Guard's Doubt

The order came at the gray hour before dawn, when Vellamere's lamps burned low and honest men were still asleep.

Sef Rennick stood in the garrison antechamber of the Ministry of Records, rain still dripping off his cloak onto polished marble that had no business being in a government building, and read the summons a third time. *By authority of the Crown: raid and seizure of premises beneath the Corvane tannery, Saltrow district. Resonant objects suspected. All persons found within to be detained for assessment.*

Assessment. Everyone in the guard knew what *assessment* meant now. It hadn't meant anything when Rennick was a corporal. It had been invented four years ago, along with the Office of Harmonic Inquiry, and it had a way of turning people into paperwork.

"You'll want to move quickly," said Minister Cael Harn, who had summoned him personally, which was itself unusual. Ministers did not summon captains for routine raids. Ministers sent clerks with sealed orders and went back to their ledgers. But Harn had come down from his office on the fourth floor, into the lamplight, so Rennick could see him properly: a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the patient expression of someone who spent his days deciding what the past was allowed to say.

"Quickly," Rennick repeated.

"The longer these objects circulate, the more harm they do." Harn folded his hands. "You know the doctrine, Captain. Resonance spreads. A charm in one pocket becomes a belief in ten."

"I know the doctrine, Minister."

Something in his voice must have carried, because Harn's eyes sharpened, just slightly, the way a moneylender's do when a debtor says *I'm good for it* one time too many. "You have reservations?"

"I have questions about jurisdiction," Rennick said. "Saltrow is Watch territory. The tannery sits on a contested boundary—"

"It has been resolved." Harn produced a document from his sleeve as if he'd been waiting all morning for exactly this objection. "Redrawn last month. The whole of Saltrow falls under Crown authority now."

Rennick took the paper. The seal was genuine. The date was three weeks old. And he knew—he knew the way a man knows his own street, his own stairs, the number of steps between his door and the corner—that three weeks ago there had been a dispute over that boundary, an ugly one, with the Watch captain threatening to petition the Assembly. He'd heard about it over cards.

There had been no resolution. There had been no hearing. Assemblies didn't meet in midwinter.

He looked up. Harn was watching him with pleasant, bottomless patience.

"Efficient," Rennick said.

"We live in efficient times." Harn smiled. "Dawn, Captain. I'd hate for the city to wake to disorder."

* * *

Rennick did not go quickly. He went slowly, deliberately, in the manner of a man whose horse had thrown a shoe, and by the time his squad assembled at the mouth of Tanner's Row the sun was fully up and half of Saltrow was awake to watch them come.

Twelve guardsmen. He'd requested twelve because a smaller force looked like confidence and he wanted no part of looking confident. They clattered down the row past tanning yards reeking of lime and hides, past apprentices staring from doorways, and stopped at the low door set into the tannery's foundation—the door Odile Ferran's congregation used, the door Rennick himself had walked through twice in the past year, once carrying a stolen writ of detention and once carrying nothing but his own shame.

He owed Mirabel Montrose a debt older than both visits. Nine years ago, during the purges, when he'd been a young sergeant ordered to sweep the carvers' quarter, she had hidden three families in her cellar under shelves of bone dust and said nothing while he searched her shop—and he had known, standing among those shelves, that she knew, and that neither of them would ever say it aloud. She had let him lie to his superiors and carry the lie like a stone ever since. Some debts you repay in coin. This one you repaid in silence, or in whatever came after silence.

"All right," he said loudly, for the record and the watching windows. "Crown business. Open in the name of the Accords."

The door opened on a frightened tanner's foreman who swore, convincingly, that the cellar below held nothing but brine vats and rat traps.

It held a chapel. Rennick could feel it the way you feel weather coming—a pressure behind the ears, a faint wrongness in the sound of his own boots on stone. Twenty years ago every guard had been trained to sense resonance; the training had been allowed to lapse when the threat was declared dead, but the sense stayed in the body like a scar that aches before rain. The cellar below was singing, very quietly, in a register the ear refused.

"Nothing here, Captain," said Sergeant Voss, who had gone down the stairs and come back up too fast, pale around the mouth. "Brine. Rats. A few old bones in the walls—tanneries keep them, for the lime."

"Bones," Rennick said.

"Cattle bones, sir. Obviously."

Obviously. Voss had been with him nine years. Voss had a daughter who wore a carved luck-charm to school every day and no one had ever thought to arrest the child for it. Voss was lying to him in front of eleven witnesses, badly, and Rennick understood that the lie was a gift, and that accepting it made him complicit, and that he had been complicit since the moment he'd read the summons and started inventing delays.

He should have felt worse about it. What he actually felt was tired, and underneath the tiredness, something colder and more alarming: relief.

"Sweep it again," he said. "Thoroughly. Then we log the premises as cleared."

* * *

The falsified report took him until evening, and it was the easiest writing he had ever done, which frightened him more than anything else that day.

*Premises inspected at first light. No resonant objects recovered. No persons detained. Cellar consistent with ordinary tannery use; bovine remains noted, non-anomalous. Recommend no further action.*

Fourteen lines. Clean, dull, forgettable—the prose equivalent of a shrug. He signed it, dated it, and carried it up to the Ministry of Records himself, because if he sent it through channels it would pass through clerks, and he wanted to watch what happened to it.

Harn received him in the same antechamber, under the same lamps. Rennick laid the report on the marble table and watched the minister read it with the flat attention of a man checking a list against a list.

"No arrests," Harn said.

"The premises were clean."

"Were they." Not a question. Harn set the report aside without filing it, which was the first wrong thing. Reports got filed. That was the entire religion of the Ministry of Records—you could burn a report, lose a report, argue with a report, but you did not simply set it aside like a bill you'd decided not to pay. "Captain, walk with me."

They walked. Through corridors lined with shelving that ran back farther than the building had any right to be deep, past clerks who did not look up, past carts of folios going somewhere in the dark. Harn talked as he walked, pleasantly, about the weather, about the coronation renewal ceremony being planned for spring, about how fine the new binding would look carved fresh. And all the while Rennick was counting doors, counting turns, mapping the route in the old soldier's habit that had kept him alive through the purges, and noticing something that turned his blood to well water:

The shelves were labeled by year. He passed 20 Years Ago. 19. 18. And the labels were wrong. Not subtly wrong—wrong the way a face is wrong when you realize it's a stranger wearing a familiar coat. He had served in those years. He had filed reports in those years. He remembered the Great Fire of Candlemas Street in year seventeen, the quarantine riots, the trial of the hedge-priest Anselm Grey. He looked for the fire. He looked for the riots. The shelf for year seventeen held neat, uniform folios, hundreds of them, and not one bore the registry mark of the Fire Commission, because—

Because there had been no Fire Commission. The thought arrived whole and cold, uninvited. Except he had drunk with the fire commissioners. He had helped them drag bodies. He had—

He stopped walking.

"Captain?" Harn turned, mild as milk.

"Year seventeen," Rennick said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "The Candlemas fire. Where are those records?"

A pause. Small, but Rennick had spent twenty years reading pauses the way other men read letters.

"What fire?" Harn said.

And that was the crack. Not in the wall—in Rennick. He felt it open down the length of him, quiet as ice splitting on a pond. Because either he was mad—and he was not mad, he could still smell the smoke if he let himself, could still hear the bells—or the records were being rewritten, not amended, not corrected, but *rewritten*, wholesale, years at a time, and the man walking beside him either didn't remember the difference or was one of the hands doing the rewriting.

"There was a fire," Rennick said carefully. "I may be misremembering the year."

"Very likely." Harn smiled and resumed walking. "Memory is a treacherous servant, Captain. That's rather the point of this ministry. We keep what the mind cannot. Come—I'll have your report entered."

* * *

He did not sleep that night. He sat in his rooms above the guardhouse with a decanter he didn't drink from and laid the pieces out on the table of his mind the way he'd once laid out evidence for a court-martial.

Piece one: the Saltrow boundary, redrawn without any hearing he could find—not in the Assembly rolls, not in the civic calendar, nowhere. When he'd gone looking that afternoon, the dispute simply wasn't there. Three weeks ago it had nearly caused a fistfight between two captains. Now the ledger showed the boundary as settled *eleven years ago*, with signatures he recognized and dates that couldn't be real, because one of the signing officers had died nine years back.

Piece two: the fire. Year seventeen. Gone. Not archived—*unwritten*. And with it, he suspected, everything else that made the last twenty years look like what they had been: a slow, grinding suppression, mistakes and cruelties and dead people, instead of the smooth, inevitable, unanimous triumph the official story required.

Piece three: the drowned girl. Mirabel had told him about her six nights ago, in her shop, in a voice he'd never heard her use—flat and careful, the voice of someone reporting a wound. *The king ate my name.* He had told her to keep her head down. He had told her that the Crown investigates rumors, it doesn't act on them. He had believed it when he said it.

Piece four: dawn raids on chapels full of grieving people, orders signed by ministers who never signed raids, and a report that vanished from a minister's hand like a coin into a conjurer's fist.

He got up, took paper, and began—very quietly, in the shorthand only he could read—to write down everything he remembered. The fire. The riots. The boundary dispute. Names. Dates. Bell-towers he'd climbed, streets he'd swept, the color of the sky the night the Sigil Accords were proclaimed and half the city wept and the other half pretended not to notice.

If they were eating the past, then memory was contraband now. Fine. He had been a smuggler of small mercies for nine years. He could carry this too.

Toward morning he stopped writing and sat with his head in his hands, because the arithmetic finally finished itself. If the records could be rewritten, then his own falsified report—the one he'd written to protect Odile Ferran's congregation—was not protection. It was a single candle cupped in a wind that ate candles. Worse: it was a thread. If anyone pulled it, if anyone compared his patrol logs to his report, if anyone asked why Captain Rennick had taken four hours to search a cellar the size of a kitchen—

A knock at the door. Two soft raps. Voss's rhythm.

Voss slipped in without waiting, closed the door, and stood there holding his helmet like a man at a funeral. Up close, in the lamplight, the sergeant looked ten years older than he had at dawn.

"Sir," Voss said. "You need to hear what I found in the cellar. All of it. Not the cattle-bones version."

Rennick looked at him for a long moment—at the man who had lied smoothly for him in front of witnesses, and was now, at risk of the White Cells, coming to unlie.

"Sit down, Sergeant," Rennick said. "Tell me everything. And then—" he glanced at the pages of shorthand drying on his table, the last twenty years compressed into a captain's private hand, "—then tell me how fast your squad can march at night."

Outside, over the rooftops of Vellamere, the bells began the hour, and for the first time Rennick noticed that they sounded thinner than they used to—as if something were slowly drinking the sound out of the world, one note at a time.
