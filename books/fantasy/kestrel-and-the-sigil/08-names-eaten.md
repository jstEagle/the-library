## 8. Names Eaten

The chapel beneath the tannery smelled of tallow and old hides, of lye soaked into stone, and beneath all of it—faint as a memory of a memory—the ghost of incense that had not been burned legally in twenty years. Odile Ferran knelt at the head of the congregation and arranged the last of the candles with hands that did not shake, because her hands had learned long ago that shaking was a luxury the half-forgotten could not afford.

Thirty-one souls tonight. She counted them the way she counted everything now: twice, then once more, because counting was prayer when you had nothing else to pray with. The tanners who worked above them had gone home hours ago; the vats sat cold and dark, and the only light in the underchapel came from candles guttering in jars, throwing shadows up the walls like drowned things rising.

They had come for a rite. That was what she told herself, anyway. They had come for a rite, and not simply because the alternative was sitting alone in rooms where the furniture no longer fit anyone they could name.

The woman they were remembering had been called—had been called something. Odile stood at her little altar of stacked crates and felt the absence open in her chest like a door into a cold room. There had been a name. She had written it down herself, three weeks ago, in the ledger she kept hidden inside the false bottom of her candle box, because writing names down was the first law of her congregation and the second was never let a written name be found.

She opened the ledger now. The page was there. The ink was there. The letters were there, black and orderly on the yellowed paper:

*Mar—*

And after the *Mar—*, nothing. Not smudged, not scratched out, not torn away. Simply nothing, as if the pen had lifted from the paper and never returned, as if the rest of the word had never existed anywhere in the world at all.

Odile's throat closed. She looked up at her congregation, thirty-one faces in candlelight, and saw the same blankness moving through them like weather. Old Piet, the tanner with the burned forearms, was frowning at his own hands as though trying to remember why he'd brought flowers. He had brought flowers. White ones, cheap market chrysanthemums, clutched in a fist scarred by lime pits. He could not have said for whom. None of them could.

"We're here," Odile said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "We're here for her."

*For whom?* someone whispered—not cruelly, not doubting, just lost, the way a man is lost in fog and asks the question of the fog itself.

"For the one we loved." Odile lit the central candle, the fat one, the one she saved for rites. "Names are how the gods hold us. So we say the name, and the gods hear it, and somewhere in whatever country the dead walk, she is held."

It was a good liturgy. She had built it herself, out of scraps—half a funeral mass, half a hedge-charm, half a mother's bedtime blessing—and it had worked before. It had worked for the cooper's son. It had worked for the ferryman. It had worked, imperfectly, tearfully, but truly, eleven times in six years, and each time the working had done the thing it was made to do: it had caught the departed soul the way a net catches a falling body, and held it in the gods' wide hands until it could be carried wherever the dead are carried.

Odile raised her arms. Thirty-one voices breathed in with her.

"Holy Mother below the river," she began, "Hearthmother, Lantern-Bearer, Keeper of the Door of Names—we bring you one of yours. We bring you—"

She reached for the name.

There was nothing there.

Not a blankness this time. A *wound*. She felt it the way you feel a tooth pulled—the tongue goes to the gap again and again, unable to believe. Somewhere in her mind there should have been a woman: a face, a voice, a laugh or a temper or the particular way she'd held a cup. Odile had known her. Had sat across from her at this very altar. Had taken her confession, which was only this: *I'm starting to forget my daughter, Father—I'm sorry, Mother—and I'd rather die than forget her.*

Her daughter. The woman had had a daughter, and the daughter had died, and the woman had grieved so loudly and so publicly that the Crown's censors had marked her, and then—

Then the Hollow Regent had eaten her. Not her body. Her body had died quietly enough, in a rented room, alone, the way the Crown preferred its problems to end. But her soul, her name, the shape of her in the world's memory—that the Regent had swallowed whole, the way a man swallows an oyster, and now even the gods could not find her, because the gods held their faithful by name, and the name was gone from everywhere. From the ledger. From the minds of the living. From, Odile realized with a horror that dropped through her like a stone through deep water, the gods themselves.

She tried the liturgy anyway. She owed the rite that much.

"We bring you—" Her voice cracked. She tried again. "We bring you—"

Old Piet began to cry without knowing why. A young woman near the back, a laundress Odile half-recognized, said softly, "I think I loved her. I think I sat where I'm sitting now and listened to her talk about her girl." She pressed her fists to her mouth. "I can't see her face. Why can't I see her face?"

"Because it was taken," Odile said. The words came out of her like splinters. "Because something took it."

The candle flame bent sideways, though there was no draft. And in the bending of the flame, Odile heard it—not a voice, nothing so kind as a voice—a pressure, a vast attention turning toward them through the stone overhead, patient as rot, amused as a cat.

*You may keep the grief,* the pressure seemed to say. *Grief is the shape the food leaves behind.*

The central candle went out.

Thirty people flinched. One fainted. And Odile Ferran, who had buried a husband and a daughter and a god's good name in twenty years of quiet, stood very still in the sudden dark and understood, with total clarity, that the ritual had failed. Not stumbled. Failed. The Regent had not merely eaten the woman—it had eaten the *holding*, the gods' ability to catch what fell. It had reached up into heaven's hands and picked them clean.

Somewhere in the dark, Piet said, brokenly, "Mother Ferran, who are we mourning? Please. Tell us who we're mourning."

And Odile, who could not answer, who had led these people into a chapel under a tannery promising them that love left records the Crown couldn't burn, felt something in her that had survived purges and prisons and the loss of her own child finally begin, quietly, to break.

That was when the bone-carver spoke.

* * *

Mirabel Montrose had been coming to the chapel for two weeks, and she still sat apart, near the stair, the way a cat sits apart—present, watchful, unconvinced. She was a narrow woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that seemed always to be listening to a room behind the room, and Odile had not yet decided whether to trust her, only that she could not afford not to.

Now Mirabel rose from her corner and crossed the dark with a candle of her own, unlit, and set it on the altar beside the dead one.

"You can't say her name," Mirabel said. It wasn't a question.

"No one can." Odile's voice was ash. "I wrote it down. The writing's gone. The gods can't hold what has no name, and the name is—" She stopped, because finishing the sentence meant admitting what it meant. "It's nowhere. She is nowhere. We're standing in a chapel mourning a hole in the world."

"No," Mirabel said. "You're standing in a chapel mourning a woman. The hole is just where they cut her out."

She turned to the congregation. In the candlelight her face was grim and young and terribly certain, the face of someone who has already paid more than she will ever say.

"I heard her," Mirabel said. "Three nights ago. Through a charm I was carving. She came through the bone the way the dead do—but wrong, thin, like a voice through a wall. She wasn't praying to be remembered." Mirabel's jaw tightened. "She was praying to be *kept*. There's a difference. Remembering is for the living. Keeping is for the dead. And everything that keeps her has been eaten—except one thing."

She lifted her satchel and drew out a length of bone, pale and dense, carved over with fine lines that caught the light like frost on a window.

"The Regent eats memory," Mirabel said. "Memory lives in minds, in ink, in the gods' keeping—all soft places, all places it can reach. But bone isn't soft. Bone doesn't remember the way a mind remembers. Bone *holds*. My whole life I've used it to pass whispers along—messages, really, small things, a dead man's greeting to his widow. Small vessels for small cargo." She laid the carved bone on the altar, next to the extinguished candle, and her hand did not quite tremble. "But a vessel is a vessel. And a vessel can be built large enough, and warded deep enough, and given freely enough, that even a thing that eats names cannot dig the name out of it. Not because the name hides. Because the name becomes part of the walls."

Silence. The kind of silence that is either a tomb or a seedbed.

"That's craftwork," someone whispered. "That's forbidden craftwork. They hanged a carver in Corrow's Field for less."

"They hanged him for selling charms," Mirabel said flatly. "I'm proposing something else. I'm proposing we build her a house the thief can't enter, and we put her name in the foundation stones, and we do it openly, with consent, with every one of you giving what you still carry of her—your grief, your fragments, the shape of the hole she left. Grief is the shape the food leaves behind." She glanced at Odile, and Odile started, because those were the Regent's own words, turned inside out and handed back like a stolen coat reclaimed. "Well. Then grief is also evidence. Grief is the negative space of a person, and negative space can be carved."

Piet wiped his face with a hand like a slab of salt beef. "Will it work? Will she—will she be somewhere? Will she know?"

"She'll be held," Mirabel said. "Not saved. Not sent on. Held—safely, wholly, until the day comes when holding turns into freeing. I don't know when that day is. I only know that right now the choice is between a locked room and being eaten, and I know which one I'd take."

"And the cost?" Odile asked. She had been a priestess long enough to know that every vessel was bought with something. "Bone-craft of that depth costs the carver. Everyone knows the old stories. What does it cost you, Mirabel Montrose?"

Mirabel was quiet a moment. Her thumb moved over her ink-stained fingers, worrying at something invisible—a habit, Odile thought, of a woman touching a wound she kept under wrappings.

"Time," Mirabel said at last. "And hearing. Every name I carve into bone, I have to listen to first—really listen, hold the whole of them in my head while I work. Some of them I won't put down afterward. Some of them stay." Something moved behind her eyes, brief and private as a fish under ice. "That's my cost. It's mine to pay. Don't make it bigger than it is."

"It's big enough," Odile said softly.

Outside, far above, the tannery clock struck the hour—ten heavy strokes rolling down through the floors like a fist on a coffin lid—and beneath the strokes, faint but unmistakable to ears like Odile's, trained by twenty years of listening for boots on stairs, came another sound. Voices. Many voices. Moving through the streets above with the particular organized rhythm of a patrol that is not patrolling but *searching*.

The congregation froze. Thirty faces turned toward the ceiling.

"They're early," Odile breathed. "The sweep isn't due for—"

"They're not sweeping," Mirabel said. All the certainty in her face had gone hard and cold. She was looking at the extinguished candle, at the bent flame's aftermath, at the pressure in the air that Odile had felt and she, apparently, had felt too. "They're following the same wound we are. The Regent knows we gathered around its meal. It sent them."

Odile snuffed the remaining candles with her bare fingers, one two three four, and the dark came down like a mercy. In the blackness she heard Mirabel's voice, low and steady, close to her ear:

"How many ways out of here?"

"Two. The stair we know, and the drain under the vats that we pray we know."

"Then get them moving, Mother Ferran. And take the ledger." A pause, and then, quieter, with an edge Odile would remember for years: "Every name in that book is a person the Regent hasn't finished eating yet. Tonight we learned it can starve them out of heaven itself. Which means from tonight on, we're not a congregation anymore."

"What are we?" Odile whispered into the dark.

She felt rather than saw Mirabel turn toward her, felt the bone-carver's answer arrive like a blade being drawn slowly from its sheath.

"We're an ark," Mirabel said. "And we sail tonight."
