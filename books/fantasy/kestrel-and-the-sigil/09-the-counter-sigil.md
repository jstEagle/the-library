## 9. The Counter-Sigil

The ossuary map came to them folded inside a psalter.

Not a real psalter—Odile would never have risked a true one—but a clever forgery, its vellum pages painted with the words of the Nine in a hand so faithful it made Mirabel's teeth ache. Between the pages of the Litany of Quiet Hands, someone had pressed a sheet of butcher's paper covered in cramped architectural notes: stairways, load-bearing arches, the old priest-door beneath the cathedral that had been bricked over during the purges and bricked over again when the first bricks were found loose.

The map was Rennick's contribution. He had not given it to them himself. He had left it in the false bottom of a flour barrel at the tannery, wrapped around a note that said only: *I was never here. Burn this after.*

Nobody burned it. Odile smoothed it flat on her worktable and weighted the corners with candle stubs and a jawbone she used for measuring, and for three days the underground chapel smelled of tallow and old paper while Mirabel studied the binding the way she had once studied grain and growth rings—looking for the place where the wood wanted to part.

The problem was that nobody alive knew what the binding looked like.

The Sigil Accords had been signed twenty years ago, but the sigil itself—the carving set into the crown Aldric wore—was seen by perhaps forty people in all of Vellamere, and most of those were dead now or had been erased. The crown went onto the king's head at his coronation and came off only in the sealed chamber behind the throne room, where two ministers of the Crown's Office of Resonance kept watch. Mirabel had seen it once, from thirty feet away, at Aldric's coronation. She had been eleven years old. She remembered a gleam of gold, a murmur like bees, and her mother's hand clamping over her eyes because even then—especially then—a bone-carver's daughter heard too much.

So they reconstructed it.

* * *

It began with the drowned girl.

Sefa—though Mirabel still could not say the name aloud without the chapel air going strange, as if the syllables were borrowed goods—had been pulled from the Velle nine days after she drowned, and what remained of her family's memory of her was thin as spring ice. But bones held. Bones always held. Mirabel had built her whole quiet life on that truth: flesh forgot, flesh forgave, flesh let go, but bone was the ledger the body kept, every year of it written down in layers.

She asked Odile for the girl's remains. Odile went pale in the lamplight.

"You know what you're asking."

"I know what I'm asking." Mirabel turned her carving knife over in her fingers, watching the fire move along its edge. "A vessel. Just a small one. A finger-bone charm, nothing more. If there's anything of her name left in the bone, I can read it."

"The Crown burns resonant goods, Mirabel. If they find human bone in this chapel—"

"They'll find it anyway," Mirabel said. "They're already looking. That's what the market burnings are. They're not destroying magic, Odile. They're destroying evidence."

That silenced the room. The half-forgotten congregation sat in their pews of stacked hides, listening with the particular attention of people who had learned that being forgotten was not an ending but a process, slow as rot, and that you could feel yourself going through it.

Odile brought the bones up from under the floor.

* * *

Mirabel worked through the night, alone, because what she did next was not a thing she could do with witnesses.

She chose the girl's left ring finger—not the largest bone, not the strongest, but the one that had been nearest the charm Sefa had been buried holding, a cheap tin locket with a saint stamped on it whose face had been worn smooth by a thumb. Proximity mattered. Mirabel had known that since childhood, the way other children knew that stoves were hot. Things that touched held each other.

She cleaned the bone. She said no prayer, because prayers had been outlawed and also because she had never needed one; the dead spoke to her without intermediaries and always had. She set the bone in her vice, took up her finest knife—the one with the whalebone handle her mother had ground down for her when her hands were still too small for a full grip—and began to carve.

And the bone sang.

Not the way living whispers sounded, not the thin thread of voice she'd been hearing since she was four years old, the voices of the newly dead saying their unfinished things. This was older. Deeper. The sound a bell makes underwater. And underneath it, threaded through it like wire through wax, was a pattern.

Mirabel stopped cutting. Her hands were shaking.

Because the pattern in the bone was the sigil.

Not the whole of it—of course not the whole of it; a finger-bone held a fragment the way a shard of mirror holds a fragment of a face—but enough. The binding that King Aldric wore on his head, the great sealing carved into the crown itself, had been cut into bone before it was ever cut into gold. She could see it in the girl's finger: the same interlocking strokes, the same grammar of closure, scaled down a thousandfold and sleeping in the marrow like a song learned in childhood.

"Oh," Mirabel said aloud, to the empty chapel, to the dark. "Oh, you fools. You didn't build it. You *copied* it."

* * *

The original had been carved from a person.

She should have seen it sooner. Everyone in Vellamere knew the story they told children: that the Sigil Accords had been written by King Aldric's own hand, negotiated with the gods themselves, a peaceful settlement after the Godswar. The gods would withdraw behind the veil; the crown would hold the door; magic would pass out of common use and into history. A mercy, the story called it. An ending without more burning.

But stories told by victors are always furniture with the drawers glued shut, and Mirabel had spent her life opening drawers.

She went back through everything Odile's congregation remembered—and memory was a strange archive, full of gaps where the Regent had fed, but gaps have shapes, and the shape of a removed thing can be read from what remains around it. There had been a priestess. Every account circled her like water circling a drain. The last high priestess of the Nine, the one who had stood in the square at the signing. People remembered her robe. People remembered that she did not struggle. People remembered—some of them, the ones whose memories still had edges—that the crown had been finished *afterward*. Finished quickly. Finished in private.

And people remembered that no one had ever been able to recall her name.

"Vessa," whispered an old woman named Maren, three nights later, gripping Mirabel's wrist across the worktable with fingers like bird claws. "No. Vesna? No—" Her face crumpled. "You see? You see how it goes? It's like reaching into a drawer and finding it lined with velvet and empty. Something was *taken out*, girl. Something was taken out and the drawer was made soft so we wouldn't hear it rattle."

Mirabel held the old woman's hand and did not say what she was thinking: that the Regent had eaten the priestess's name first, before any other, because the name was the keystone of the working. That the binding on the crown was not merely carved *in* bone. It was carved *of* bone. Of hers. And a thing carved of a person can be answered by a person, the way a lock answers its key.

The binding could not be broken. She was beginning to understand that. Bindings of that depth didn't break; they *answered*—call and response, question and counter-question, the oldest grammar bone knew. To unmake the Accords, she would need to carve a counter-sigil. And to carve a counter-sigil—

She did the arithmetic that night, and the arithmetic was brutal.

* * *

She laid it out for Odile and the core of the congregation on the fourth night, chalk on the slate floor of the chapel, because paper could be seized and slates could be wiped.

"Every name the Regent has eaten is gone," she said. "Not hidden. Not stored. Eaten—it's *made of* what it takes, that's how it stays anchored in him, in the crown, in the city. We can't get those names back from it directly. Nothing taken comes back whole." She drew a circle. "But the dead hold echoes. Bone holds echo. The binding was carved from one freely given life—one name, surrendered with consent, freely given. One keystone." She tapped the center of the circle. "The counter-sigil has to answer it in the same grammar. Which means it needs the same material. Names. Freely given. From the erased."

"How many?" Odile asked. She already knew, Mirabel thought, from the flatness of her voice. Mothers always did the arithmetic fastest.

"That depends on how much of the binding we're answering." Mirabel drew lines radiating from the center, and hated herself with every stroke. "The crown-sigil is a single keystone holding a vast weight. To answer it fully—to pull the whole door open—we'd need a counterweight of equal mass. One name won't do it. Ten won't. The Regent has eaten thousands, Odile. Whole parishes. Whole trades. There are streets in the Low Quarter where half the doors belong to families no one can name."

Silence. Somewhere above them, through the floorboards, the tannery's night shift moved vats, and the smell of lime and hide seeped down like weather.

"So it's impossible," said a man near the back. Tammas—no, that wasn't right, that name had frayed too, he was the cooper, he was the cooper, that was all anyone had. "Thousands of names. Thousands of bodies, freely given. It's impossible."

"It's worse than impossible," Mirabel said quietly. "It's all or nothing. The grammar doesn't allow partial answers. A counter-sigil with too few names doesn't weaken the binding—it *feeds* it. It's a question the binding can swallow. We'd be handing the Regent a meal and telling it where we live." She wiped her palm across the chalk circle, blurring it. "Many names, or none. Those are the options."

"And the names have to be *given*," Odile said. "Freely. Consenting. Like her." She nodded at the small bundle of linen where Sefa's finger-bone lay. "The dead can't consent, Mirabel. That's the cruelty of it. The ones the Regent ate are beyond asking."

"No." Mirabel had been turning this over for two days, turning it like a stone with something glinting in its seam. "They're beyond asking *now*. But the Regent hasn't eaten everyone. Not yet. There are the half-forgotten—people like your congregation, people whose names are fraying but not gone. People who can still choose." She looked around the chapel, at the pews of the fading, at faces she had begun, against all her instincts, to care about. "And there are the dying. And there are the ones who will die soon, in a city where the Crown burns resonant goods and the Regent grows hungrier by the month. If they give their bones willingly—if they give their *names*, spoken into the carving while they still own them—"

"You're talking about building an army of the erased," Odile said.

"I'm talking about building a chorus," Mirabel said. "One voice couldn't answer the binding. The priestess's voice couldn't, in the end—that's why it worked, why it *held*, because it was one name holding a door against everything behind it. But a chorus—" She thought of the bone singing under her knife, that deep underwater bell. "A chorus might."

Maren, the old woman, began to laugh. It was not a happy sound, but it was not despair either; it was the laugh of someone who has finally been shown the size of a thing she'd been fighting blind.

"You'll need a workshop," she said. "You'll need bone. You'll need time. And you'll need every soul in this city who remembers losing someone to stand up in front of the Crown's censors and say *take mine too.*"

"Yes," Mirabel said.

"And if the Regent learns what you're doing—if it hears even one whisper of a counter-sigil—it will simply eat faster. Empty the city before you finish. Starve your chorus."

"Yes," Mirabel said again, and her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack, because these people deserved truth the way they deserved names.

* * *

She walked home through the pre-dawn streets with her hood up and the girl's finger-bone charm warm against her sternum, and she let herself think about the cost properly, the way a carver counts the grain before the first cut.

Thousands of names. Thousands of givings. Years of work compressed, somehow, into whatever time they had—and they had less than they thought, because the Regent was not idle, because the burnings in Ash Wednesday Market were accelerating, because the Crown's Office of Resonance had begun keeping lists.

And beneath all of it, the thought she had been refusing to look at directly, the splinter working its way toward the surface:

*A counter-sigil needs a keystone. The binding's keystone was a name given with love and surrendered with consent. And somewhere in this city, at the center of everything the Regent has hidden, there is a name it guards deeper than all the others. A name that matters to me.*

She didn't know why she was sure of that. She only knew that when she imagined the Regent's hoard—a dark granary of stolen names—there was one shelf in it that felt warm, the way a stove feels warm through a wall. One name her mind slid past every time she reached for it, smooth as a drawer lined with velvet.

Empty. Soft. Silent.

She stopped on Wexler Bridge, in the grey hour when the river mist comes up off the Velle and makes ghosts of the lamplighters, and she put both hands on the cold stone rail, and she said, aloud, to the water, to the dead, to whatever was listening:

"What am I forgetting?"

The river said nothing. The river had forgotten how.

But far away, in the sealed chamber behind the throne room, in a crown of gold set with a carving of bone, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep—and smiled, the way a landlord smiles at a tenant who has finally started reading the lease.
