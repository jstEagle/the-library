## 10. Ash Wednesday Market

The pyres went up before dawn.

Mirabel heard them from her window—axes biting wet wood, the creak of cart wheels on frost-stiffened cobbles—and knew, before she looked, what day it was. Ash Wednesday. The Crown's oldest theater. Every year since the Accords, the censors had built their bonfires in Vellamere's market square and fed them with anything that *sang*: charms, relics, prayer-flags, bone-work, the letters of the dead read aloud too lovingly. Every year the crowd came to watch, because a crowd will come to anything that promises warmth in February.

She dressed slowly. Her hands were steady. She made herself notice that they were steady, the way you make yourself notice a wall still standing after an earthquake.

*They don't know about you,* she told herself. *You are invisible. You have been invisible for twenty years.*

But the drowned girl's name was carved into three charms now, hidden in the false bottom of her workbox, and Odile Ferran's chapel beneath the tannery smelled of her visits, and last night Rennick had stood in her doorway with rain on his shoulders and said, *Mirabel, there's a list. Your street is on it.*

She opened the shop anyway. That was the whole point of being invisible—you kept your shutters open and your hands moving and you gave the world nothing to look at.

By midmorning the square was full. She could hear it through the front glass: the crier's voice, flat as slate, reading the Proclamation of Resonance. *Objects which retain echo of unlicensed utterance are hereby declared contraband.* Somewhere a child laughed, and the sound cut through the proclamation like a knife through cloth.

Mirabel was wrapping a plain birch rattle—a baby's toy, no magic in it, nothing but wood and love—when the knock came at her door.

Three knocks. Even. Official.

She did not answer fast enough, so they let themselves in, because they were the Crown's men and doors were only suggestions to them. Two censors in grey, ash already dusting their sleeves from the morning's burning, and behind them a clerk with a ledger and a long silver tuning-fork of the kind used to test bells.

"Proprietor?" the elder censor asked, though his eyes had already begun to move along her shelves, cataloguing.

"Montrose," she said. "Bone-carver. Buttons, combs, memorial work. All licensed."

"All licensed," the clerk repeated, without inflection, writing it down anyway, because writing things down was how the Crown made the world obedient.

The younger censor drifted toward the back room. Mirabel's heart performed a slow, deliberate act of treachery against her, hammering so hard she could feel it in her teeth. The false bottom of the workbox. Three charms. A drowned girl's name, sung into cow-bone by candlelight.

*Keep your face empty. Empty as a swept floor.*

The tuning-fork sang.

It was a small sound—the clerk struck it against his knuckle and held it up, and it hummed a single clean note—but the room answered it. Mirabel felt the answer in her own bones first: every piece of worked bone on her shelves shivering into resonance, whispering its little histories, the mother's grief in the memorial charm, the old man's laughter in the walking-stick grip. Twenty years of quiet craft, all of it suddenly audible, all of it suddenly *guilty*.

"Well," said the elder censor, almost gently. "Well, well."

"It's bone," Mirabel said. "Bone remembers. Everyone knows that. It's why people buy memorials—that's not resonance, that's grief, that's—"

"Madam." The censor picked up the memorial charm, turned it in his fingers with the tenderness of a man handling something he intended to destroy. "Everything remembers. That is rather the problem."

From the back room, the younger censor's voice: "Found something."

* * *

Rennick saw the smoke from four streets away and made himself walk toward it at a guard's pace, neither hurrying nor dawdling, because a captain who hurries is a captain with a conscience and consciences get reported.

He had put on the uniform that morning the way a man puts on a coat he knows has been cut too small every year for two decades. In the barracks mirror he had looked at himself and thought: *today I am furniture. Today I stand where I am told and see nothing.*

The market square was packed shoulder to shoulder, breath steaming, faces lit orange by three pyres. The censors had been efficient. He could see the shapes of what burned even when he couldn't bear to identify them: a rocking horse collapsing into flame, a wall-hanging of saints unraveling into sparks, and—God help him—a crate of children's grave-goods going up all at once, so that for one terrible moment the fire roared with a sound almost like singing, and the crowd made a noise he would hear for the rest of his life, a low collective *oh*, half hunger and half shame.

He found his post at the square's east corner, beside Lieutenant Vasq, who was warming his hands and looking pleased with the weather.

"Good turnout," Vasq said. "His Reverence the Lord Censor will be happy. Numbers matter."

"Numbers matter," Rennick agreed, and hated himself cleanly, thoroughly, with the efficiency of long practice. That was the trick he'd learned in twenty years of service: hate yourself *cleanly*. Don't let it smear. Don't let it show. Do the thing, then fold the hatred up small and put it in the drawer with the other folded things—the night of the purges, Mirabel Montrose's face through a doorway, the debt he'd never once named aloud—and lock the drawer, and stand straighter.

Then he saw the second cart.

It wasn't goods this time. It came through the north arch under escort, and the crowd parted for it the way water parts, and in the back of the cart, wrists bound with regulation cord, sat a broad woman in a tanner's apron with iron-grey hair coming loose from its braid.

Odile Ferran looked, if anything, bored. She surveyed the pyres like a woman inspecting inferior stitchwork. When her gaze swept the square and passed over Rennick's uniform, it paused—not on him, he told himself, not on him specifically, just on the uniform, just on what the uniform meant—and then moved on, and she lifted her chin at the censors as if daring them to find her impressive.

*Don't,* Rennick thought, with a desperation that surprised him. *Don't look at me. Don't make me real to you. I can't be real today.*

Because here was the arithmetic he could not escape: he owed this woman's network nothing directly. But he owed Mirabel everything, and Mirabel loved this chapel, these half-forgotten souls, this hedge-witch who kept a congregation of the erased like a woman keeping candles lit in a wind. And Odile was in a cart, and Rennick was in uniform, and between those two facts lay the entire rotten architecture of his life.

"Prisoner manifest," said the clerk at the cart, reading aloud for the record. "Ferran, Odile. Charge: maintenance of an unlicensed assembly. Charge: possession of resonant materials, quantity—" he checked the page, and something flickered across his face, some small human horror quickly suppressed, "—quantity extensive."

"What quantity?" someone shouted from the crowd.

"Seventy-one objects," the clerk said. "And eleven names."

Eleven names. The crowd murmured. Names were worse than objects; everyone knew that now. Names were what the Accords had been built to govern.

They took her down off the cart and walked her past the pyres, close enough that the heat pushed at her clothes, and Odile Ferran did not flinch, and Rennick stood at his post and did nothing, and hated himself cleanly, and locked the drawer.

Except the drawer didn't lock anymore. He noticed that, standing there in the orange light. He noticed that somewhere in the last month the drawer had stopped closing, and everything he'd folded up small was unfolding all at once behind his ribs, and the only thing keeping it contained was the discipline of standing still.

Vasq elbowed him. "That's the tannery witch, isn't it? The one your raid missed?"

"My report was accurate," Rennick said. "The cellar was empty."

"Mm." Vasq smiled at the fire. "Funny thing, Captain. I pulled the archive copy of your report last week. Interesting reading."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in it, something small and wooden surrendered.

"Funny how?" Rennick said.

"Funny like, the cellar inventory lists forty items, and your report says the cellar was empty, and yet here's the witch with seventy-one objects seized from a cellar that was empty." Vasq's voice stayed pleasant, conversational, pitched under the crowd-noise. "Almost like someone rewrote it. Almost like someone's been rewriting a lot of things. You should see what happened to the purge records, Captain. Half the execution orders from twenty years ago have different dates now. Different *names*, sometimes. Like the paper itself is being eaten."

Rennick kept his eyes on the pyre. "Paper rots."

"Paper does rot," Vasq agreed cheerfully. Then, quieter, almost kind: "Watch yourself, Captain. There's a new auditor coming through the ministries. Things are getting *tidied*."

* * *

They took everything.

Mirabel stood on the cobbles outside her own shop—it was not hers anymore, apparently; there was a seal on the door now, red wax over the latch, and the seal had a sigil on it that made her teeth ache to look at—and watched four censors carry her life out into the street in crates.

The memorial charms went first. Then the combs, the buttons, the rosary-beads, the crib mobiles, the walking sticks, the little carved saints she'd never blessed because blessing them would have been a crime and she'd never been able to decide whether that made her a coward or a survivor. The tuning-fork clerk walked the shelves one last time, striking his note, listening, and each time the room hummed back he nodded to the censors like a man confirming a harvest.

At the end, the elder censor came out carrying her workbox.

"No," Mirabel said, before she could stop herself.

The censor stopped. Looked at her. For a moment—one strange, suspended moment—his face did something complicated, and she thought absurdly: *he's going to give it back. Under all that grey, there's a man who once owned something.*

"The false bottom," he said quietly. "Clever work. My daughter had a music box like that, before." He handed the box to the younger censor. "Inventory it separately. Some of these pieces may merit study rather than burning."

*Study.* The word landed like a sentence of transportation. Study meant the White Cells' laboratories, meant resonant objects wired to instruments and questions, meant the three charms with the drowned girl's name passing under eyes that understood what they were looking at.

Mirabel did not follow the crates. That was the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than carving silence into herself, harder than twenty years of invisibility: she stood on the cobbles and let them take the girl's name away, and she did not run after it, because running after it would tell them exactly what they had.

Instead she went home, and sat in the dark, and listened.

The dead were quieter than usual tonight. That frightened her more than shouting would have. She pressed her palm flat against the windowsill, against the old house-bone of Vellamere itself, and reached out the way Odile had taught her—not grasping, just *listening*, the way you listen for a heartbeat under a blanket.

And there, far off, thin as thread: the drowned girl.

Not words. Not yet. Just a feeling, carried on whatever current ran between the dead and the bone-carvers who served them—a feeling like a hand gripping hers in dark water.

*Still here. Still here. Come find me.*

And underneath it, fainter, wronger, vast as a cathedral and hollow as a drum: something else listening too. Something patient. Something that had noticed, at last, the one listener in Vellamere who could hear it back.

Mirabel took her hand off the sill.

She had one ally left in uniform, one debt, one door she hadn't knocked on in twenty years because knocking would burn the bridge either way. Tomorrow the city would sweep the ash off the cobbles and pretend the day had been warm and necessary. Tonight—

Tonight she wrapped her cloak around her shoulders and went out into the cold to find Sef Rennick, and ask him which side of himself he intended to be, once and for all.
