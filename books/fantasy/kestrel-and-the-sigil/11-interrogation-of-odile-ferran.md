## 11. Interrogation of Odile Ferran

They took her shoes first.

That was the detail Odile Ferran would remember afterward—not the manacles, not the hood, not the long descent through stairwells that smelled of chalk and cold iron, but the moment a bored clerk with ink-stained fingers held up her boots and said, "Laces go in the inventory," as if she were a woman checking a parcel at a coaching inn. As if she would be coming back for them.

The White Cells earned their name honestly. Everything down there was white—whitewashed stone, white wool blankets, white robes on the wardens, white bread on white plates. No color anywhere. No wood, no cloth dyed anything warmer than bone. Odile understood the design within an hour of arriving. Color was memory's handmaiden. You remembered the red of your mother's shawl, the blue of a river in summer. Take away color and you took away the hooks on which a mind hung its past. The Crown had built a room that starved remembering.

Clever, she thought, lying on the narrow cot with her hands folded over her sternum like a woman already laid out. Cleverer than the old kings ever were.

She had been in the cells four days when they came for her properly.

* * *

The interrogator was called Warden-Cleric Anselm Voss, and he was not what she expected. She had imagined a brute, or worse, one of the pale ministers with their ledgers of confiscated names. Instead he was small and neat, sixty perhaps, with kind creases around his eyes and the gentle voice of a man who had spent decades delivering bad news to families. He brought tea. He set it before her on the white table and waited until she drank, and only then did he sit.

"Mother Ferran," he said. "May I call you that? I understand you were never ordained."

"You may call me whatever helps you sleep."

The creases deepened. "Honesty. Good. I find honesty goes faster." He unfolded a single sheet of paper, though Odile noticed his eyes never dropped to it. "You were taken at Ash Wednesday Market in possession of forty-one resonant objects. You were taken from a chapel beneath the Corvane tannery, where you had conducted unsanctioned rites for a period of—" he paused delicately, "—some years. Eleven persons were detained with you or subsequently identified through your effects. The Crown wishes to know who else came to your chapel. Names, Mother Ferran. That is all we want. Names."

"And what does the Crown give?"

Voss smiled, and it was the smile that told her everything—the smile of a man about to offer something he believed irresistible. "Before I answer that," he said softly, "I wonder if you'd tell me about your daughter."

The cell went very quiet. Somewhere above them, water moved through pipes, a sound like a slow swallow.

"I don't have a daughter," Odile said.

"No," Voss agreed gently. "You don't. Not anymore." He turned the paper toward her. On it was written nothing—a blank space where a name should be, surrounded by dates, by a parish record number, by the notation *record expunged per Accords, ref. 7-114*. "A fever, in the year after the binding. You buried her in the churchyard of Saint Ellowen's, which no longer exists. You mourned her for three years. And then, one morning, you woke and could not recall her face." He folded his hands. "We have her records, Mother Ferran. Her birth entry. Her burial entry. Every document the censors missed. We can restore them to you. We can give you back her name."

Odile looked at the blank space on the page and felt the old wound open in her chest, the one she had learned to breathe around, the way you learn to breathe around broken ribs. She thought: *they know exactly what they're doing.* She thought: *of course they do. They've had twenty years of practice.*

And beneath both thoughts, quieter, colder, the thought she refused to let surface: *how do they know so precisely what was taken?*

Because only the eater knows the shape of what it has swallowed.

"Her name," she said aloud, testing it. It came out steady. Good. Grief was a muscle too, and hers had been training for eleven years.

"Lise," Voss said.

The word landed in her like a stone dropped into a well. She heard it fall and fall and never touch bottom. Lise. Was that true? Was that the sound of it? She had named a child once, had whispered that name into small ears, into prayers, into the dark of a sickroom—and now a stranger in a white robe handed it back to her like change from a market stall, and she could not tell if it rang true or false. That was the cruelest arithmetic of the Hollow Regent's feeding: it didn't just take the name. It took your ability to recognize it.

"That isn't it," she said.

"It is."

"It isn't." She met his kind eyes. "You want me to trade living people for a word. And I don't even know if the word is real. If I say yes, Warden-Cleric, what have I sold? Eleven souls for a sound that may be nothing but bait shaped like my grief."

Voss regarded her for a long moment. Then he leaned back, and something in the quality of the lamplight changed—or Odile imagined it changed—and when he spoke again, his voice had another speaker underneath it, the way a bell has the hammer's strike inside its ring.

"You doubt the gift because you cannot verify it," the voice said. It was still Voss's voice, pitched the same, but it came out wrong, evenly, without breath behind it, each word placed like stones laid by a mason. "Wise. So let me verify it. Your daughter liked honey on salted porridge. She was afraid of geese. On the last night of her fever she asked you whether the saints could hear through walls, and you told her yes, and she said *then I will talk to them until I am one of them*, and you laughed, because you thought it was a child's joke, and it was not a joke. It was a prayer. And I ate it, Odile Ferran. I ate it out of the air between you and her, and I have kept it, warm, these eleven years, the way a miser keeps a coin no one remembers minting."

The tea in Odile's cup had gone cold. Her hands were steady around it. Inside, everything was screaming.

*It knows. It truly knows. Which means it truly has.*

"There is no version of this," the Regent said through Voss's patient mouth, "in which you leave this place without giving me names. There is only the question of what you receive. The girl's name, whole, restored to every record, returned to your tongue so that you will wake tomorrow knowing it as you knew it before me. Or nothing. Or less than nothing." A pause, precise as a knife finding a joint. "Your congregation is already being questioned. Some of them are not strong. Old Tomas wept within the hour. The cooper's wife has already given us the tanner's boy. You cannot protect them, Mother Ferran. You can only decide how much they lose."

*Old Tomas wept within the hour.* Odile let that settle. Let herself feel the spike of fear for him, the guilt, the terrible arithmetic beginning in her chest. And then she looked at it, the way she had taught her congregation to look at their own memories during the rites: hold it up to the light. Turn it. Ask who benefits from your believing it.

Because here was the thing the thing in the king did not understand about hedge-witches, about women who had kept illegal candles lit under a tannery for a decade: they lied the way other people prayed. Continuously, sincerely, and with their whole hearts.

Tomas had a spine of oak; she'd seen him refuse to give his late wife's name to a census-taker with a cudgel in hand. The cooper's wife hadn't been able to pick Odile out of a lineup of two. Whatever the Regent claimed its puppets had confessed, it wanted her to believe the net was closing fast—wanted her frightened, hurried, trading cheap. Fear made sellers. Patience made liars.

"You're right," Odile said.

Voss blinked. The Regent, for the first time, hesitated—a hitch in the evenness, a stone slightly mislaid.

"I'm an old woman," she went on, letting her shoulders curve, letting her hands tremble just enough. Trembling was honest work; she'd earned it. "I've sat in this white room four days with no color and no shoes, and you come offering me my dead child, and I find that I want to say yes. God forgive me, I want it so badly my teeth ache." She looked down at the blank space on the page. "But I'm not the one you want. I keep the chapel, yes. I light the candles. But I don't choose who comes. There's someone above me. Someone who found *me*."

Silence. The lamp hissed.

"A bone-carver," Odile said, and watched the stillness in Voss sharpen into attention so intense it was almost audible. "In the Lamplighters' Row. She hears the dead through what she carves—I've seen it, I swear it on my daughter's grave, which is more than most can swear to anymore. She's the one the drowned girl spoke to. She's the one who taught me the vessel-work. If you want the network, the whole root of it, you don't want my little congregation of grievers. You want Mirabel Montrose."

Every word true. That was the beauty of it, the discipline of it. The best lies are built entirely of true things, arranged in false order. Yes, Mirabel heard the dead. Yes, Mirabel had carved the vessels. Yes, the drowned girl's plea had reached her first. All of it true—and none of it a betrayal, because the Regent already knew Mirabel's name. It had seized her shop. Its ministers had inventoried her goods. Odile was handing the hunter a map of ground it already owned, dressed up as treasure.

What she withheld was everything that mattered: the ossuary, the counter-sigil, the priestess's bones, Rennick, the plan taking shape somewhere above her head in rooms she would never see again. Those she buried under the one name she could afford to spend.

"Mirabel Montrose," Voss repeated. He wrote it down—actually wrote it, the first time his pen had touched the page, and Odile understood that the Regent valued confirmation more than discovery. It needed its knowledge witnessed. It was building itself a ledger of certainty, because certainty was the one food it could not take.

"She'll run," the Regent said through him. "When your arrest reaches her ears—"

"She won't run." Odile allowed herself a dry, tired almost-smile. "I've known her two years. She runs toward. It's her worst quality. Give her a day and she'll come looking for me, and you can take her at the shop, or the tannery, or wherever her guilt drags her." *Three days,* she thought. *Give me three days. Long enough for the frame to be carved. Long enough for Rennick to move. Long enough.* "But I'll want proof of good faith. Records restored. My daughter's entries, given to me, in my hands, before I say another word about anyone."

"The name first."

"The paper first. I'm not a fool, Warden-Cleric. Words are your currency and you print them yourself."

Something flickered across Voss's face—amusement? admiration? the Regent's cold appraisal of a bargaining opponent?—and then smoothed away. "Three days," he said. "Bring me the bone-carver's location, her associates, her intentions. In exchange, Lise Ferran lives again in every record in Vellamere, and you will hold her name on your tongue until you die of old age in a warm bed." He rose, gathered his tea things with the tidiness of a lifelong clerk. At the door he paused. "For what it is worth, Mother Ferran—and it is worth little—I hope you choose her. Mothers should have their children back. It is the world's oldest debt."

The door closed. The lock turned. Odile sat alone in the white silence with her cold tea and her shaking hands, and only then, only when there was no one to see, did she put her forehead down against the white table and weep—not for the lie, and not for the truth of it either, but for the name. *Lise.* Whether it was real or bait, she would carry the sound of it out of this place or die carrying it, and either way the thing in the king would learn what hedge-witches had always known:

You cannot eat a woman's memory of her daughter. You can only borrow the shape of the hole it left, and holes cut both ways.

* * *

They gave her the papers the next morning, as promised. Two brittle pages, parish seals intact: a birth, a burial. *Lise Ferran,* born in the spring of the last free year, dead of fever at six years old. Odile read them forty times. She pressed them flat beneath her cot's thin mattress and lay with her palm over the place where they hid, and she practiced saying the name silently, over and over, fitting it into the hollow in her chest like a key into a lock that might or might not be its lock.

On the second day, a warden brought her supper and, setting down the tray, murmured a sentence that meant nothing to any listener but her—one of the chapel's old cant phrases, smuggled in through a guard whose sister sang in Odile's congregation. *The kestrel flies at dusk. The frame holds. Hold fast, Mother.*

Hold fast. Three days, then. Perhaps less.

Odile closed her eyes and began, very quietly, to pray—not to the sealed gods, and not to the saints, but to the dead themselves, to the great unnumbered chorus pressing against the thinning binding, the erased and the unmourned and the half-forgotten. *Listen to me,* she prayed. *One of you is dreaming in a crown tonight. One of you is still a king, somewhere under the hunger. Find him. Tell him to hold on. Tell him help is coming wearing a carver's apron, and that an old liar bought him three days with the only coin she had left.*

Above her, far above, in a palace she would never see, a window scratched with desperate letters caught the last of the evening light.

And in the dark beneath the tannery, in the dark beneath the palace, in the dark beneath everything, something vast and patient turned its attention toward a sleeping mind—and found, to its surprise, that the mind was awake.
