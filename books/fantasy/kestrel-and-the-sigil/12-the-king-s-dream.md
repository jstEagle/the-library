## 12. The King's Dream

He was drowning in a room he knew.

That was the first thing Aldric understood, or the shape of understanding, because understanding came to him the way light comes through deep water—bent, delayed, arriving already broken. The room was his. He knew it by the smell of beeswax and cold stone, by the particular way the wind worried the leaded glass of the east window, by the sound his own boots made on the flagstones when he walked it at night because sleep had become a country he could no longer enter.

Except he could not feel his boots. He could not feel anything. He was standing in his own chamber in Vellamere's high tower and he was made of smoke, or of memory, or of nothing at all, and somewhere below him—or above him, or inside him—something else was breathing.

*You are dreaming,* said a voice that might have been his own.

*I am always dreaming now,* he answered, because he had learned this much: that arguing with the voice gave it weight, and weight was what it wanted. It fed on weight. On years. On the dense, warm mass of a life lived forward.

The chamber assembled itself around him in pieces, like a painter working from a description given secondhand. Bed first—the great oak bed with its carvings of kestrels, his grandfather's vanity rendered in wood. Then the hearth, unlit. Then the window, and beyond the window the city, gray under a sky the color of old pewter, and beyond the city the river, and beyond the river—

Nothing. There should have been hills. He was almost certain there should have been hills. He could remember *hills* as a word, as a category, as something a kingdom contained, but when he reached for the sight of them the reaching hand closed on air.

*There were hills,* he said to the voice.

*Were there?*

*West of the river. Green in spring. My father took me—* And there it stopped, the memory, sheared off clean as a bone sawn through. Not faded. Faded things left edges, ghosts, the ache of a shape. This was cut. This was a page removed from a book so carefully the binding showed no gap, and only a man who had read the book a thousand times would know that somewhere between one line and the next, whole chapters had gone.

*You took them,* Aldric said.

The voice did not answer, which was its own kind of answer. In twenty years he had learned its silences the way a sailor learns weather. When it was silent and amused, it was feeding. When it was silent and still, it was listening for other listeners. When it was silent like this—patient, vast, indifferent as stone—it was sleeping, and Aldric was walking through the house of his own skull while the thief slept in the master bedroom.

He moved toward the window. Walking without legs was a thing he had gotten used to, the way one gets used to grief, which is to say never entirely but enough to keep moving.

* * *

The dream changed around him the way dreams do, without transition, without permission.

He was in the throne room, and it was the day of the Accords.

He knew this day better than any day of his life, because it was the day they had taken from him most thoroughly, and what remains after theft is the knowledge of the theft itself. Twenty-two years old. Crown not yet warm on his head—he had been king for eleven days. The hall full of lords whose names came to him in fragments: Lord Asbury, yes; the fat one with the rings, yes; the woman with the gray braid who wept throughout and whom he had loved like an aunt and whose name was—

Gone. A hole where a name should be, smooth-walled, painless. That was the worst of it, he thought, watching the scene assemble: the holes didn't hurt. Pain would have been a mercy. Pain would have meant something in him still fought. The hollow places were comfortable. They had been worn smooth by twenty years of use, like a path through grass, and some mornings—in the rare, smuggled minutes when he surfaced—he caught himself walking down those paths gladly, filling the gaps with whatever the Regent poured in, and calling it peace.

In the dream-throne-room, the priestess stood before him.

He had signed her death. That was the sentence his mind returned to, over and over, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Not ordered—signed. Her name was on the warrant in his own hand, though even now he could not summon the name, only the motion of writing it, the drag of the nib, the small blot where his hand had hesitated. She had knelt. She had looked up at him with an expression he had spent two decades trying to recover and could not—not forgiveness, not hatred, something more terrible than either—and she had said:

*It cannot be broken, Majesty. Only answered.*

And then the guards had taken her away, and Aldric had sat on his father's throne with wet ink on his fingers, and the thing that had whispered the Accords into his ear for eleven days had settled into him like a cat settling into a chair, turning once, twice, making itself comfortable, and closed its eyes.

*Why do you show me this?* he asked the sleeping voice, though he knew the voice did not choose his dreams. His dreams were the last territory it had never managed to hold. It ate memory while he woke; it could not police what he dreamed. So the dreams were all salvage, all wreckage washed ashore, and he walked them nightly like a man combing a beach after a shipwreck, gathering what the sea had not kept.

The priestess turned her head. In the dream, impossibly, her eyes found his across twenty years and a hall full of erased faces.

*Answered,* she said again. *Not broken. Remember that. Whatever you lose, remember that.*

Then the throne room folded up like paper and he was falling through dark, and the dark smelled of tallow and river water, and somewhere very far away a girl was praying.

* * *

He surfaced once, briefly, into waking.

This was rarer than the dreams and more dangerous. The Regent slept in rhythms he had spent years mapping—a long stillness after the evening meal, when the body sat by the fire and stared at nothing and ministers learned not to disturb it; a deeper stillness in the smallest hours, when even the thing's hunger seemed to slow. In those hours, if he was lucky, he rose through the layers like a diver rising through cold strata, and for a handful of minutes the eyes were his.

The hands were always his. That was the strange mercy of it. The Regent wore his body but had never bothered to learn it properly—it moved the way a puppet moves, competently, without history. It did not crack the knuckles of the left hand the way his father had taught him. It did not rub the thumb along the scar on his palm, the scar from the falcon, from the bird named Kestrel he had flown at twelve before his father decided kings did not fly hawks. Small treasons of habit. He hoarded them.

He was at the window. He did not remember crossing the room—that was usual; the body went where the sleeper's will drifted, and sometimes his will, surfacing, found itself already somewhere, like waking mid-journey in a carriage. The east window. The city below, lampless now by decree after the ninth hour, streets black except for the watch-fires, and beyond the walls the river running silver under a half moon, and beyond the river—

Hills. For one heartbeat, one impossible heartbeat, he saw them. Green. Spring-green. And on the nearest hill a boy on a pony with his mother laughing beside him, and the laugh arrived whole, undamaged, ringing in his chest like a struck bell, and he thought: *it missed one. Twenty years of eating and it missed one.*

His mother's face. He had not been able to hold it for nineteen years. He had woken a hundred times in the night-quiet reaching for it and found the smooth-walled hole, the comfortable nothing. And here it was, returned entire—the strong nose, the crooked smile, the way her left eyebrow rose higher than the right when she was deciding whether to scold him. Queen Isolde. *Isolde.* The name came with the face, and he pressed both against the inside of his skull like a man pressing flowers, knowing how they browned, knowing how they crumbled.

Because he understood, with the clarity that only the stolen are granted, why it had come back tonight. The binding was thinning. He felt it in the tower stones, in the river-smell, in the way the dead—*the dead*, and when had he begun to sense the dead, that was new, that was the binding failing too—murmured beneath the city like water under ice. The Regent was hungry. It was feeding harder, digging deeper, and everything it loosened in its feeding shook loose other things, things buried so deep even it had forgotten them.

His mother's face had been buried deepest of all. He understood that too, suddenly, the way one understands a thing in a fall. It was not random loss. The Regent kept certain memories the way a miser keeps coins—not spent, not lost, *kept*. His mother. The priestess's face. The boy on the hill. The Regent held them because they were the anchors of him, the roots, and a puppet needs its strings anchored somewhere.

Which meant they could be cut from the other end.

His hand was moving. He watched it move with the strange detachment of the surfacing dead-man he had become, his own fingers lifting, finding the diamond pane of the window, and beginning to scratch. The ring on his finger—his signet, which the Regent wore because kings must wear their seals—caught the moonlight and bit into the glass with a sound like a small animal in distress.

Scratching was slow. Scratching was loud. Somewhere behind his eyes, in the deep warm dark where the thing slept, something shifted its weight.

*Quickly,* he told himself. *Quickly, quickly.*

He wrote what mattered. Not plans—he had no plans; he was a ghost giving dictation to a window. Not pleas—he had passed beyond pleas years ago. He wrote the only thing he had left, the coin he had just been handed back and could feel already growing cold in his palm, fading even as he carved it, the green hill dimming, the laugh going thin, his mother's eyebrow sinking back beneath the water of forgetting:

*I REMEMBER MY MOTHER'S FACE,* the diamond point gouged. *DO NOT LET IT TAKE THAT TOO.*

And then, because the shifting behind his eyes had become a stirring, and the stirring a cold current flowing up through his chest like river water flooding a drowned man's lungs, he added four words in a hurry, badly, the letters slanting:

*THE PRIESTESS KNEW HOW.*

* * *

The Regent woke the way winter wakes: everywhere at once, without mercy.

Aldric went under. There was no struggle anymore—he had learned that struggling was like a drowning man fighting the sea, spending strength to be moved a foot and pulled back ten. He folded himself down into the cellars of his own mind, into the places he had dug over twenty years, the hidden rooms behind false walls where he kept what he could keep: the scar on his palm. The sound of kestrel wings. Eleven days of being a king before the whispering began, eleven days he replayed like a miser counting the same coins.

But tonight, going down, he carried something new. Tonight he had seen the hills. Tonight he had heard his mother laugh. And the fact of it—the bare, stubborn, physical fact that a memory could survive down there, could surface after nineteen years intact and ringing—was a coal he cupped in both hands as the dark closed over him.

*It can be survived,* he told the dark. *Things you bury deepest can come back. You missed one. You missed one, and someone will find the rest.*

The dark did not answer. But far off, at the edge of his sinking awareness, he felt the Regent rise from the fireside chair, cross to the window, and stand before the scratched words for a long time. He felt it read them. He felt—this was the worst part, this was the thing he would carry down into every cellar of himself—he felt it *consider*, with the patience of a creature that had eaten centuries, whether the message was a wound to punish or a clue to follow.

Then it laughed. Not aloud. The body never laughed aloud anymore. It laughed inside, in the place where his laughter used to live, and the laugh was almost fond.

*Let them come,* it said to no one, to the window, to the city, to whoever might be listening in the dark below. *I have eaten kings before this one.*

And in the morning, the ministers would find the window broken and replaced by noon, and the record of it struck from the household rolls by evening, and none of them would remember that anything had ever been written there at all.

But windows were not the only things that remembered.

Down in the city, in a shop that smelled of tallow and bone-dust, a carver was listening to the dead—and the dead, for the first time in twenty years, were saying the king's name.
