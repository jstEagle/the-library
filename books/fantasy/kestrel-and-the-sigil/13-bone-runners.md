## 13. Bone Runners

The route to the ossuary did not exist on any map in the Guardhouse. That was the first thing Sef Rennick had understood about it, twenty years ago, when he was still a corporal with ink-stained cuffs and a sergeant who liked him for reasons neither of them examined. The royal ossuary lay beneath the Citadel's oldest foundation—older than Aldric, older than the Accords, older than the city's habit of pretending it had always been quiet. The only way down was through the bone runners' corridor: a service passage built for carters hauling plague dead in the years before Vellamere had walls worth the name.

Rennick had walked it once, as a young man, escorting an archivist who never came back up. He had told himself then that the man had simply resigned and left by the river gate. He was done telling himself things like that.

Now he stood at the mouth of the passage at the fourth hour of the morning, lantern hooded to a coin of light, watching Mirabel Montrose look at the dark like it owed her money.

"This is a guard route," she said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"It's a dead route," Rennick said. "Which is better. Dead routes don't get inspected." He tapped the wall beside him, where a hairline seam of masonry ran floor to ceiling. "Carters' access. Sealed after the last plague year. Unsealed twice since—for funerals nobody talks about. I unsealed it a third time tonight."

"With whose permission?"

"Mine." He let that sit. "You wanted to know what defection looks like, carver. It looks like this. It looks like a man forging his own signature on his own authorization because there's no one left above him honest enough to sign it."

Mirabel's face in the hooded light was all angles and exhaustion. She'd barely slept since the market—since they'd taken Odile, since Rennick had stood in his uniform in that burning square and done nothing while censors fed charms into the flames like they were feeding a god. He could see she remembered that too. She hadn't said anything about it. That was worse than if she had.

"The runner meets us below," he said. "Name's Pell Wick. Young. Fast. Knows every maintenance crawl under the Citadel better than the masons who built them. He thinks we're moving documents."

"And when he finds out we're moving me?"

"Then he'll have a decision to make," Rennick said, "and I'm hoping he makes it before someone offers him more than I can pay."

She looked at him a long moment. "That's not hope, Captain. That's arithmetic."

"It's both. Come on."

* * *

The passage swallowed them in stages. First the smell—the deep mineral cold of stone that had never learned sunlight, cut with old lime and older death. Then the sound: their boots, and beneath their boots, faintly, water moving through channels no one maintained. Then something Rennick didn't have a word for, a pressure against the ears, like standing near a struck bell after the sound has gone.

Mirabel felt it too. He watched her hand drift to the charm-bag at her hip, the one that clinked now with a weight it hadn't carried a week ago.

"You hear them?" he asked quietly.

"Not yet. But they know we're coming." Her voice had the careful flatness she used when she didn't trust what her voice might do otherwise. "Twenty years, Captain. Twenty years the binding's been pressing down on every dead thing in this city, and now it's thinning, and they're—" She stopped. Started again. "It's like walking toward a house where everyone's been asleep behind a locked door, and you can feel them starting to wake up. All of them. At once."

"How many?"

"I don't know." A pause. "More than the living."

They walked. Rennick counted junctions by habit—left at the flooded stair, right at the collapsed arch, straight where three tunnels met in a Y of ancient brickwork—and Mirabel followed without asking how he knew, which was its own kind of trust, and one he hadn't earned cleanly. He kept thinking about that. He suspected he would be thinking about it for the rest of his life, however long the Regent allowed that to be.

At the second hour, she spoke again.

"The debt," she said. "From the purges. You've never told me what it actually was."

He'd known this was coming. He'd been dreading it anyway.

"You were nineteen," he said. "Your brother had just been taken. Your shop was on the list for seizure—you didn't know that part; the list went out at night and the raids came at dawn. I was on the raid roster. Third name down, your street." The lantern swung gently with his stride. "I crossed your shop off. Wrote *premises cleared* in my own hand. If anyone had checked—if anyone ever checks—I'd have hanged next to the hedge-witches."

"Why?"

He almost laughed. It came out as breath. "Because I'd seen the list that week. Four hundred names. And I realized I couldn't remember the face of a single person on it—not one—and I understood that wasn't an accident. Someone had made sure the list meant nothing to us. Names without faces. You can burn anything if you make it nothing first." He turned the corner at the flooded stair, wading shin-deep through black water, and heard her follow. "Your brother had a face. Everyone in this district said so. Tam Montrose, who could make a whole tavern laugh with half a sentence. So I saved the shop, and I told myself that was the whole debt paid, and then I spent twenty years not coming back to check whether it was."

"It wasn't," Mirabel said softly.

"No," Rennick agreed. "It wasn't."

* * *

Pell Wick was waiting at the third junction, perched on an overturned mortar trough with his knees drawn up, eating a boiled egg in the dark like a boy at a fence. He couldn't have been more than twenty-two. He had the runner's build—all tendon and nerves—and when he stood and saw Mirabel properly, the egg stopped halfway to his mouth.

"That's not documents," he said.

"No," Rennick said.

Pell looked from Rennick to Mirabel and back, and Rennick watched the arithmetic run behind the boy's eyes—the same arithmetic Rennick had been running himself for days, except Pell ran it faster, because youth runs everything faster. Bone carver. Resonant goods seized at Ash Wednesday Market. The Ferran woman in the White Cells. The renewal ceremony announced for coronation day, when the binding would be re-carved and made permanent, and every whisper of the old gods strangled forever.

"They say she talks to dead people," Pell said.

"They say a lot of things," said Rennick.

"Do you?" Pell asked Mirabel directly.

"Yes," she said.

Something in her voice—no performance in it, just the plain tired truth—made Pell put the egg away. "Right," he said. "Where are we going?"

"The ossuary," Rennick said. "Under the old chapel. There's a crawl behind the cistern that comes up inside the bone vault."

Pell went very still. "Nobody goes to the ossuary."

"Somebody does tonight."

"Captain—" The boy's voice dropped, urgent. "There's a reason runners don't take contracts down there. Denny Corr took one eight months ago. Just to the outer gallery, in and out, two hours' work. He came back up wrong. Kept saying he'd forgotten why he went. Then he forgot he'd gone. Then—" Pell swallowed. "Then he forgot his mother's face. His own mother, Captain. He sat in the Lamplight tavern crying because he could see her across the room and she was a stranger. Three days later he walked into the river."

The silence in the tunnel had texture. Rennick felt Mirabel go rigid beside him.

"The Regent feeds down there," she said. "Of course it does. Its bones are buried there. It grazes its own graveyard."

"So you understand why—" Pell began.

"I understand," Rennick cut in, "that in four days the binding gets re-carved, permanent, and everything Odile Ferran bought with three days in the White Cells dies with it. I understand that the counter-sigil needs the priestess's bones, and the priestess's bones are in that vault. And I understand, Pell, that you're free to walk away right now and I will falsify your contract myself so no one knows you were ever here."

He expected the boy to take it. Any sensible person would take it.

Pell looked at Mirabel instead. Looked at her the way you look at a door you've been walking past your whole life without knowing it was a door.

"Denny Corr's mother," Pell said finally, "still lights a lamp in her window for him. Every night. Doesn't matter that he's dead. She says the lamp's not for finding him—it's so that wherever he is, something remembers the way home." He shouldered his pack. "Show me the crawl."

* * *

They lost the day at the fourth junction.

Later, Rennick would replay it a thousand times and find the exact moment—the hinge on which everything swung. It was the sentry post. A maintenance shaft with a ladder and a grate opening onto a lower gallery of the Citadel proper, and there, posted where no sentry should have been, a pair of guards in Crown livery, checking papers against a ledger by lamplight.

A ledger. In a sealed service tunnel.

"Records audit," Rennick murmured, drawing Mirabel and Pell back into the shadow of the arch. "They're mapping the routes. Marking which ones are still passable." His stomach had gone cold and stayed cold. *Someone told them where to look.*

"We go around," Pell whispered. "There's a drain channel—old, pre-walls—runs parallel maybe forty yards east. Tight, but it connects to the chapel cistern same as this does."

"Can you lead it blind?"

"In my sleep." Pell was already moving, and Rennick noted—with the detached part of his mind that never stopped taking notes—that the boy moved too fast, too confidently, for a man improvising. He noted it and filed it and hated himself for filing it, because Pell Wick had lit no lamps for strangers and asked nothing.

The drain channel was exactly what Pell promised: tight, foul, and passable. They crawled it for an hour in the dark, Mirabel's breathing steady behind him, and Rennick began—foolishly, criminally—to believe.

The ambush came at the cistern.

Not Crown guards. Worse. Two men in the grey of the Regent's ministers' household, waiting with the patience of furniture, and between them, held by the arm, Pell—his face grey, his lips moving around words that wouldn't come, because they had already taken them. Rennick saw it instantly, the hollowed look, the same look Denny Corr must have worn. They'd started eating the boy's memory before he even arrived. Leverage. A withdrawal already in progress.

"Captain Rennick." The taller minister had a voice like paper folding. "Warden Vess extends his compliments. You're expected to stand down."

"Am I."

"The bone carver walks with us. You walk back to your barracks, and tomorrow you're promoted, and none of this ever—" The minister smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes, because there was something looking out of them that had never once seen a human eye from the inside. "—happened. You understand *happened*. We're very good at that now."

Rennick's sword was out before the sentence ended. He'd like to say he thought about it. He didn't. Twenty years of standing in rooms doing nothing collapsed into one motion, and the motion was forward.

What saved them wasn't his sword. It was Mirabel.

She'd pulled a charm from her bag—one of the new ones, carved in the days since the market, carved from bones freely given by the half-forgotten congregation—and she shattered it against the cistern wall, and the sound it made was not a sound. It was a *name*, spoken at a volume names aren't meant to hold, and both ministers flinched as though scalded. In that flinch—a full heartbeat of vacancy, the puppet-strings going slack—Rennick's blade took the tall one's legs out from under him, and Mirabel drove a carving awl—her small sharp everyday awl, her bread-and-butter tool—into the other's shoulder, and they ran.

They ran, and Pell ran with them, weeping without sound, and behind them the shorter minister screamed in a voice that was not entirely his own: *"YOU CANNOT CARVE WHAT WE HAVE ALREADY EATEN."*

* * *

They didn't stop until the outer gallery of the ossuary closed over them like a hand—pillars of stacked skulls, alcoves of femurs laid in herringbone, and the air thick with that bell-after-sound pressure until Mirabel staggered and caught herself against a pillar of the dead and gasped.

"They're here," she whispered. "All of them. Rennick—they're *awake*."

And Pell sat down hard on the gallery floor, in the house of the city's oldest dead, and put his face in his hands.

"He led us true," Rennick said quietly. "In the end. Whatever they took out of him—he brought us here."

"I know." Mirabel knelt beside the boy. "I know. Pell. Look at me. What's your mother's name?"

The boy's head came up. For a moment his eyes were clear, terrified, present. "Marisol," he said. "Marisol Wick. She lights a lamp."

"Then she's safe," Mirabel said. "Names given freely can't be eaten. Only taken. Hold onto the difference."

Rennick turned to watch the gallery entrance—the way they'd come, the dark they'd outrun—and that was when he saw it, scratched into the plaster of the pillar beside him in a shaking, desperate hand, in letters half a century old:

*THE BINDING IS A QUESTION. DO NOT BREAK IT. ANSWER IT.*

Below it, smaller, newer, the ink barely faded:

*I remember my mother's face. Do not let it take that too.*

"Aldric," Rennick breathed. "He's still in there. He's been leaving messages." He looked at Mirabel, and for the first time since the purges, since the list of four hundred nameless faces, since the crossing-off of a nineteen-year-old's shop, Sef Rennick let himself feel the thing he'd been refusing to feel: not fear. Certainty.

"They know we're here," he said. "We've got hours, maybe less. Tell me what you need from these bones, carver. Tell me fast."

Mirabel was already unwrapping her tools, and all down the gallery, in the stacked dark, the dead were leaning closer to listen.
