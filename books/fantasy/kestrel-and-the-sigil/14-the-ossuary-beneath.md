## 14. The Ossuary Beneath

The stairs went down farther than the city went up.

Mirabel counted them because counting kept her hands from shaking—two hundred and eleven steps, spiraling into the dark beneath the royal quarter, each one worn to a shallow bowl by feet that had stopped coming here twenty years ago. Rennick walked ahead with a shuttered lantern, its light leaking through the slats in thin gold blades that swept the walls as he turned. The walls were bone.

Not decoration. Not metaphor. Bone, laid in courses like brick, femurs and ribs and skulls packed so densely they had fused into a single pale stone that seemed to breathe when the lantern light passed over it.

"Nobody comes down here," Rennick said quietly. "Officially, it doesn't exist. The ossuary was sealed the same week the Accords were signed. I've seen the orders. *Seal and forget.* Those were the actual words."

"Forget," Mirabel repeated. The word tasted wrong in her mouth, like meat gone off. "That's not an instruction. That's a feeding instruction."

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. They had both spent three days learning what the Hollow Regent did with forgetting.

The stairs ended at a door of black iron so old it had rusted into lace, and beyond it the dark opened out the way a cathedral opens—not a room but a held breath. Rennick opened the lantern's shutters all the way, and light poured down onto a floor of interlocking bones arranged in patterns too regular for accident: spirals, sigils, a great ring of skulls set crown-to-crown around a central dais where a single sarcophagus of white marble sat open and empty.

And the dead began to speak.

It started at the edges of hearing, the way rain starts—a hiss, a murmur, a sound you could convince yourself was wind through stone. But Mirabel had heard wind her whole life, and this was not wind. This was voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Rising together, not in words at first but in that pre-verbal pressure she knew from her carvings, the feeling of meaning pressing against the inside of silence.

She gripped the doorframe. Twenty years of discipline told her to close her ears, to carve the quiet back over herself the way she'd done every day since Tam—

But there was no quiet to carve. The binding was thin here. She could feel it like a scar stretched over a wound, and beneath it the dead were not whispering.

They were singing.

"Oh," Mirabel said, and it came out very small. "Oh, they're still *here*."

"They never left." The voice came from the empty air above the sarcophagus, and it was a woman's voice, dry as page-leaves, patient as erosion. "We were only made illegible."

* * *

The priestess stood where the light pooled thinnest, and she was not quite solid and not quite absent—the way a face is present in a window when the sun is behind you. She wore robes that might once have been white and were now the color of old ivory, which is to say the color of bone, which is to say the color of everything in this place. Her hands were folded. Her eyes were closed. When she opened them, they were the gray of ash.

"You are the carver," she said. Not a question. "I felt your work on the girl from the river. Crude. Loving. Correct in every particular that matters."

"The drowned child." Mirabel stepped forward before fear could stop her. "Is she—"

"Here. They are all here. Every name the Regent has eaten goes somewhere, carver. Nothing is destroyed. It is only swallowed." The priestess turned her head, and the chorus swelled around her, and now Mirabel could pick words out of it—fragments, names, half-prayers, a mother saying *supper*, a boy saying *again, again*, a hundred people saying their own names like anchors thrown into deep water. "Twenty years ago I sealed the gods behind a binding carved into the crown, and I used my own bones to do it, and I believed—I *believed*—that I was building a wall. I was not building a wall. I was teaching the city a grammar it could not refuse."

Rennick had gone very still near the door, hand nowhere near his sword, which Mirabel understood was the only respectful place for it. "You're the last priestess. Vessa Anhour. They burned your effigy in Ash Wednesday Market for six years running."

"They burned straw," the priestess said. "I was already here. A binding needs bones freely given, Captain. Mine were the first. The gods' last servant, holding the door shut from the inside." Her mouth curved, and it was not entirely a smile. "I thought I would be the lock. Instead I became the hinge—and hinges swing both ways."

Mirabel moved closer, drawn the way water is drawn downhill. Up close, the priestess flickered like a candle in a draft, and through her, faintly, Mirabel could see the ring of skulls behind her, each one incised with a mark she recognized from her study of the Accords' text—the original sigil, the one carved into Aldric's crown, repeated ten thousand times in miniature until the whole room hummed with it.

"It's failing," Mirabel said. "The binding. We've seen it failing everywhere. The dead getting through. Memories fraying. Why?"

"Because a binding is a sentence, and sentences need a speaker." The priestess lifted one translucent hand toward the ceiling, toward the palace above, toward the throne. "Aldric spoke it. Aldric's living will has held it taut for twenty years, the way a rope holds a bridge. But something has been eating the speaker. Bite by bite. Year by year. And a sentence whose speaker forgets it—" she spread her hands, and the chorus rose like a tide, "—becomes a question."

* * *

They made camp in the ossuary because there was no going back up before dawn—the guard rotations changed at the fourth bell, and Rennick's forged route would not hold twice. He built no fire; firelight traveled. They ate cold bread and harder cheese, and Mirabel sat cross-legged on a floor of other people's ancestors and tried to feel the sacrilege and couldn't, because the bones under her felt less like remains than like an audience waiting for the play to start.

The priestess drifted—there was no better word—around the central ring, and wherever she passed, the chorus bent toward her like wheat toward wind.

"Show me the sigil," Mirabel said finally. "All of it. Not the version in the legal texts. The real one."

"You have been studying the legal texts." It was not a compliment. "The Crown's copy of the Accords is a translation, and translations are how empires lie politely. Come. Look with a carver's eyes, not a scholar's."

She led Mirabel to the innermost ring of skulls, and Mirabel knelt, and Rennick held the lantern high, and she saw it.

The marks on the skulls were not decorative repetitions. They were *conjugations*. Each skull bore the same root-form, but inflected differently—one in what her hands understood instantly as future tense, one in conditional, one in a tense she had no word for except *consenting*. The sigil wasn't a lock. It was a verb. A vast, distributed, ten-thousand-part verb, conjugated across every bone in the room, and the crown above was merely its subject.

"It's not broken from outside," Mirabel breathed. "It can't be. You can't smash a sentence."

"No," said the priestess. "You cannot break a binding. You can only *answer* it. Every binding is a demand laid on reality, and reality retains the right of reply. The Accords say: *gods, be silent.* The answer must come in the same grammar, spoken by the same kind of voice, or it is only noise." Her ash-gray eyes fixed on Mirabel. "You brought bones. Show me."

Mirabel unwrapped the bundle from her pack with hands that had stopped shaking somewhere on the two-hundredth stair and hadn't started again. Bones freely given—she had spent weeks gathering them, and every one had cost her a conversation with a grieving family, a promise, sometimes a lie she hated. The river-girl's finger bones. Odile's congregation's offerings. A rib from a woman no one could remember, donated by the woman herself through a charm Mirabel had carved blind.

The priestess looked at the bundle for a long moment.

"These are good," she said. "These are true. And they are not enough."

"We know the mathematics," Rennick said from the shadows. "Many names or none. We've done the arithmetic. We keep arriving at numbers we can't reach in time."

"Your arithmetic is wrong because your premise is wrong." The priestess crouched beside Mirabel, and being crouched near her was like standing near an open cold-storage door in summer. "You think the counter-sigil must outweigh the binding. Force against force. Bone against bone. But bindings do not answer force. They answer *truth*. One truth, perfectly spoken, in the binding's own grammar, will unravel what ten thousand bones cannot outweigh—because the binding was built on a lie, and lies are structurally unsound. They hold only while no one looks at them directly."

"What lie?" Mirabel asked.

"That the gods consented." The priestess's voice dropped, and the whole ossuary seemed to lean in. "The Accords were written as if the old gods agreed to be bound—for peace, for order, for the city's good. Beautiful language. Ratified language. But I was there, carver. I held the pen. The gods were not asked. They were *sealed mid-sentence*, and the Regent whispered the words into Aldric's ear so smoothly that even Aldric believes to this day that he negotiated rather than imprisoned." She straightened. "Find the truth the binding was built to bury. Speak it back in the binding's grammar. The whole structure will answer—or collapse. With sentences, those are often the same thing."

* * *

Later—deep in the dead hours, when Rennick slept sitting up with his sword across his knees out of twenty years of habit—the priestess came to Mirabel alone.

The chorus had softened to something like breathing. In the lantern's low light the ghost was barely visible, more temperature than figure, and when she spoke it was almost gentle.

"You want to ask me about the keystone."

Mirabel's chest tightened. "I haven't told anyone about the keystone."

"You didn't have to. It's written all over you. Carvers carry their unfinished work in their faces." A pause, long as a held note. "Every binding has a keystone—the element without which the sentence means nothing. Mine was my own name, given willingly. Do you know what the Regent's keystone is? What it hides deepest, guards most jealously, builds its whole architecture around?"

"I think so." Mirabel's throat was sand. "A name. Given with love. Surrendered with consent."

"Yes." The priestess let the silence sit between them like a stone on a grave. "And you know whose name it is."

"I don't *know*." Mirabel pressed her palms flat against the bone floor, as if she could draw steadiness up out of it. "I know what the chorus keeps circling. I know what the Regent hides deepest. I know that when I reach for my brother in my memory, some of the edges are—smoothed. Worn. Like a coin handled too long." Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Tam died in the purges. Everyone says so. Everyone who remembers him at all."

"Everyone who remembers him at all," the priestess repeated softly. "What a strange phrase. Think about it, carver. Sit with it. The chorus will tell you the rest when you're ready to hear it, and not one hour before. That is mercy, not cruelty. Some truths arrive too early and kill the person who needs to carry them."

Above them, far above, the palace slept, and in it something hollow wore a king and dreamed of permanence. Mirabel felt it even here—even through two hundred and eleven steps and a sealed door and twenty years of law—as a faint pressure at the edge of her mind, patient and vast, the way winter presses on a window.

But the dead were singing. For the first time in twenty years, the dead were singing, and the binding that had silenced them was, tonight, thin enough to see through.

"Teach me the grammar," Mirabel said. "All of it. Every conjugation. If the plan has to change, then I'll carve the new plan myself—but I need to know what a sentence can do before I write one that has to end a god."

The priestess smiled then, fully, and for a moment she looked young—looked like a woman who had once believed walls were enough.

"Then we begin at midnight," she said, "with the tense they never taught you. The one called *answering*."

And from the ring of skulls around them, soft as snowfall beginning, the chorus started to teach.
