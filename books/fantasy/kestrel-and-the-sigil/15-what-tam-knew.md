## 15. What Tam Knew

The ossuary did not sleep.

Mirabel had thought it would—the way graveyards slept, the way her shop slept at night with its little carved faces watching the dark. But the dead beneath Vellamere were awake now, all of them, and they had been waiting twenty years for someone to listen.

She sat cross-legged on the cold flagstones with the priestess's bones arranged before her in a crescent, and she listened.

The chorus was not like the whispers she had known all her life. Those had been singular voices, one grief at a time, each dead soul speaking through bone as if through a keyhole. This was a door flung open. Hundreds of voices braided into one long tone, low as a bell heard through water, and inside that tone Mirabel could feel individual threads if she reached for them—a washerwoman who had died in 812, a soldier of the old border wars, a child too young to have finished learning her letters. They pressed against the thinning binding like moths against glass. Not angry. *Eager.* That was somehow worse.

"You're shaking," Rennick said from the stair. He had not come down past the third step since they'd arrived. He stood with his hand on his sword hilt out of habit, though there was nothing down here to fight except truth.

"I'm fine."

"You're lying."

"I'm a bone-carver, Captain. Lying is what we do to bone so it will hold still." She flexed her fingers, stiff from three days of carving the counter-sigil's frame on the priestess's femur. "The chorus wants something. It's been circling around a thing all morning, the way birds circle a chimney. I can't make out what."

"Then ask it."

Ask it. As if the dead were a neighbor you could hail over a fence. But Odile's voice came back to her—*the binding is thinning, girl, and thin things let more through*—and Mirabel understood that this was exactly why the priestess had brought her here, why the ghost had spent two nights teaching her the grammar of the sigil in dreams that left her waking with chalk-dust on her lips. The binding was failing. And everything that leaked through it leaked toward her.

She set down her knife. She laid both palms flat on the flagstones, on stone older than the Accords, older than Aldric's grandfather, and she spoke aloud because the dead seemed to prefer it, because sound gave them something to hold onto.

"I'm listening," she said. "All of you. Whoever has something to say about Tam Montrose—I'm listening."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted four heartbeats. She counted them against her own pulse, an old habit from the purges, when counting heartbeats was how you knew you were still alive enough to be afraid.

Then the chorus changed.

It was subtle at first—a shift in pitch, the way a room changes when one person leaves it. Then a single thread pulled free of the braid and hung there, distinct, and Mirabel felt her whole body go cold, because she knew that thread. Not its words. Its *weight*. Twenty years of carving charms for the grieving had taught her the weight of every kind of voice: the guilty, the peaceful, the confused, the vengeful. This one had a weight she had only ever heard once before, in a half-finished charm she'd burned rather than finish, in a workshop that smelled of tallow and cedar, when she was nineteen years old and the world had just ended.

"Tam?" Her voice cracked on the name. It always cracked. She'd built her whole life around names that didn't crack, and this one never obeyed.

The thread did not answer in words. It answered in *pressure*—a sensation behind her eyes like a memory trying to surface through deep water. And then, slowly, reluctantly, the chorus gathered itself and spoke, hundreds of voices in terrible unison, and what they said was:

**He was not taken.**

"What?" Rennick had come down three more steps without seeming to notice. "What did they say?"

"He wasn't taken." Mirabel's hands had gone numb against the stone. "During the purges. He wasn't—" She stopped. Started again. "I've spent twenty years believing they took him. The King's men came for the hedge-witches and the listeners and anyone who could hear, and Tam hid me in the cellar and went up to answer the door, and he never came back down. Everyone said the Crown took him. I *said* it. For twenty years I've said my brother was purged, and I built every charm I ever carved on top of that sentence like it was a foundation stone."

**He was not taken,** the chorus said again, gentler now, the way you repeat a hard thing to a child. **He walked up those stairs knowing. He opened the door knowing. He climbed into the wagon knowing where it went.**

"No." She was on her feet without remembering standing. "No, he was protecting me, that's different, that's not the same as—"

**He made a bargain, Mirabel Montrose.**

The chorus split then—not into chaos but into clarity, each voice taking a phrase, passing it along like buckets in a fire-line, and the story assembled itself in her skull piece by piece while she stood frozen in the dark:

*A guard captain came to the door, yes—but not to arrest him. To deliver terms.*

*The first binding was failing even then. Carved into the crown, sealed by the Accords, but a binding needs an anchor, and anchors are names. The thing that whispered the Accords into existence—it needed a keystone. A name given freely, held deepest, hidden best. A name no one would ever think to look for.*

*Tam Montrose, aged twenty-two, listener, brother, offered himself. He asked one condition: that his sister be forgotten by every record, every ledger, every list. That she be erased from the Crown's attention so thoroughly that no minister would ever say her name aloud.*

"That's why they never came for me." The words came out of Mirabel flat and strange, someone else's voice. "Twenty years. I told myself I was careful. I told myself I was good at being invisible. I carved silence into myself and called it survival, and the whole time—" Her knees buckled. Rennick caught her arm, and she shook him off, and caught herself on the crescent of ancient bones. "The whole time I was invisible because my brother *bought* it. With himself."

**He is the keystone,** the chorus said. **His name is the lock's heart. The Hollow Regent hides it deeper than anything else it holds—deeper than the drowned girl's name, deeper than the woman your congregation could not remember. Because if the keystone is spoken, freely, with love, the whole binding answers to it.**

* * *

Rennick made her sit down. She let him, mostly because her legs had opinions of their own now. He crouched beside her on the flagstones, and for once he didn't reach for his sword or his rank or any of the other things he used as armor. He just stayed.

"Tell me what you know about the night he was taken," he said quietly. "Everything. I was a corporal then. I may have been there. I need to know if I was there."

"You weren't." She almost laughed. "You were in the north quarter that night. I checked, years ago. I checked every roster I could steal a look at, trying to find which squad took him. You were elsewhere. I remember being relieved." She stared at her hands. They had bone dust in every crease; they always would. "I remember everything about that night, Rennick. That's the joke of it. The Regent eats memories, and mine are the one thing it can't touch, because Tam paid for them. Every memory I have of him is the payment itself. He bought my whole life with his name, and now you're telling me the price of freeing everyone is—"

She couldn't finish it. The chorus finished it for her, softly, without cruelty, because the dead have no talent for cruelty—they've seen too much of the living kind.

**To speak the keystone, you must give what he gave. A name surrendered with consent. His consent must come through you, his blood, his last kin. And you cannot carry his memory and speak his surrender at once. The working will not bear the weight of both.**

"You'll forget him," Rennick said. Not a question. He'd done the arithmetic faster than she had, or maybe he'd simply been braver about looking at it.

"I'll forget everything." Her voice was steady. That surprised her. Somewhere underneath the steadiness she could hear screaming, but it was very far away, like a riot in another district. "Not just the night. Everything. Every summer. The scar on his chin from falling off the tannery roof when he was nine. The way he whistled when he lied, which was constantly, badly, and with tremendous confidence. The charm he tried to carve me for my sixteenth birthday—he was hopeless at it, all thumbs, and I kept it anyway, I kept it for years until—" She stopped. Pressed her palms against her eyes. "Until I burned it. After the purges. Because keeping it felt like digging a wound open every morning. So I burned it, and I told myself that was strength, and now it turns out it was just the first small rehearsal for this."

"Mira."

"Don't call me that. Only he called me that." She lowered her hands. "Sorry. Sorry. I'm—give me a moment. I'm renegotiating the terms of my entire existence and I'd like a moment to do it in."

Rennick gave her the moment. He was good at that. In all the weeks since the tannery chapel, since the market burning, since Odile's escape from the White Cells with blood on her sleeves and three days' worth of beautiful lies behind her—through all of it, Rennick had learned when to speak and when to simply be a solid shape in the dark that hadn't left. She suspected it was the same skill that had made him falsify his first report, and his second, and finally walk away from his whole career with his sword still on his belt because some debts don't fit in ledgers.

"The priestess," he said at last. "Does she confirm it?"

"She's known since I arrived." Mirabel looked at the crescent of ancient bone. The ghost was there—heavier than air, patient as sediment. "She's been teaching me the sigil's grammar for two days, and she never once mentioned that the keystone was sitting inside my own head. Why? Why not tell me immediately?"

The ghost's answer came not as words but as a feeling, and Mirabel translated it aloud because Rennick deserved to hear it:

"Because I might have run. Because a person who learns the price in advance has time to find a reason it doesn't apply to them. She wanted me to arrive at it already committed—already holding the knife I'd carved, already halfway down the road." A breath. "She wanted me to choose it the way Tam chose it. Knowing. Freely."

**Yes,** said the chorus, and the priestess's presence within it nodded, and somewhere in the vast braid of the dead, Mirabel was almost certain she felt a thread that weighed like her brother's voice draw close and hold very, very still.

* * *

They climbed back up through the ossuary levels as the bells rang terce—muffled bells, these days; the Crown had ordered the carillons dampened after the Ash Wednesday fires, as if grief were a noise complaint. At the second landing Mirabel stopped, because there was a niche in the wall there, an old burial alcove with its occupant long since moved to the great charnel stacks, and in the niche someone had scratched words into the plaster decades ago. She'd passed it twice without reading it. Now she read it.

*I was here. My name was Ansel. I mended shoes. Tell someone.*

Just that. A dead man's whole testament, compressed to eleven words by someone who understood that names were the only currency that survived.

"Rennick." She didn't turn around. "When this is done—if it works—you'll remember him for me. Tam. Everything I tell you between now and the ceremony. You won't write it down, you won't record it, nothing the Regent can eat. You'll just hold it. Can you do that?"

He was quiet for a moment. "That's a heavy thing to ask a man to carry."

"I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm asking anyway."

"Then yes." He said it the way he said everything that mattered—flatly, immediately, without decoration. "But Mira—Tam. There's something you should know first. Before you spend the next three days telling me your brother's whole life so I can keep it safe. Something I found in the archives, back when I was still pretending to be loyal. I didn't understand it then. I think I do now."

She turned. His face in the lamplight was gray, and not from the underground air.

"There was a requisition order dated the week after the purges ended. One line item. Bones, human, quantity: sufficient for one binding, source: voluntary donation, authorized under seal of the Accords." He met her eyes. "Voluntary, Mirabel. The archive clerk thought it was a clerical error. Voluntary donations didn't happen during the purges. Nobody volunteered." His voice dropped. "Except somebody did. Somebody signed. And the signature on that requisition—the witness signature, the one who stood surety for the donation—"

"Say it."

"It was your brother's handwriting. Tam Montrose witnessed his own offering. He didn't just climb into the wagon. He signed the paperwork." Rennick swallowed. "And Mira—there was a second page attached to the requisition. A letter, sealed, marked *to be opened only by the one who comes to undo it*. The Regent has kept it locked in the crown vaults for twenty years. Addressed to whoever finally came to answer the binding."

The chorus, far below them, hummed through the stone like a struck bell.

"They knew," Mirabel whispered. "Even then. Tam knew someone would come. He wrote ahead." Her hand found the wall, the cool plaster, the dead man's scratched testament. "Twenty years ago my brother sat down and wrote a letter to me—to the me who would come—and the thing wearing our king has been sitting on it ever since."

She pushed off the wall and started up the stairs, fast, the fear in her chest transmuting as it rose into something harder and brighter.

"Three days to the coronation," she said. "We're going to need a way into the crown vaults."

Behind her, faint through the stone, the dead began—very softly, in hundreds of voices—to sing.
