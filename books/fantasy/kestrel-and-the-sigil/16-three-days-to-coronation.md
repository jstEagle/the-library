## 16. Three Days to Coronation

The heralds went out at dawn, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern towers, every bell in Vellamere was ringing a rhythm no one could name but everyone's bones remembered.

*Renewal,* the proclamations said, nailed to doors and pasted over the burned scars in Ash Wednesday Market. *By grace of His Majesty King Aldric, the Sigil Accords shall be renewed and made perpetual. Let all faithful subjects gather in the Coronation Square three days hence, that the binding may be carved anew upon the crown, and the peace of twenty years sealed forever.*

The peace of twenty years. Mirabel read those words twice, standing in the doorway of her seized shop with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves so no one would see them shake. The peace of twenty years was a mouth full of stolen names. And now the thing wearing her king meant to make it permanent.

She had heard it announced before she'd seen it. Of course she had. All morning the dead had been restless—not whispering their usual fragments, not pressing their small griefs against her palms through bone and wood, but keening. A low, continuous note like wind through a cracked window, rising from every grave within earshot of the square. The dead knew what a renewal meant. The dead had been at the first carving.

*Three days,* said the girl who had drowned, whose voice lived now in the half-finished charm against Mirabel's sternum. Her name was still gone—eaten past recovery—but her voice remained, thin and stubborn as a reed. *They always did it fast when they were afraid.*

"Are they afraid?" Mirabel murmured into her collar.

A pause. Then, with something that might have been grim satisfaction: *It's eating faster than it can digest. You're making it hungry and full at once. It wants it finished before you finish.*

Mirabel looked down the street toward the palace, where the banners of the renewal hung already, white sigils on grey cloth, and felt the vast wrongness behind the king's eyes turn its attention, briefly, like a lighthouse beam sweeping the water.

Three days.

* * *

In the White Cells there were no windows, and so Odile Ferran had learned to measure time by other instruments: the rattle of the meal slot, the changing pitch of the pipes in the walls, the slow migration of cold along the flagstones from the outer wall inward. By these measures, she judged it was morning of the second day—or perhaps the third. Hunger blurred the arithmetic.

She sat cross-legged on the pallet with her hands open on her knees, the way she sat for prayer, except that she wasn't praying. Prayer required someone to receive it, and the Regent had seen to it that the gods couldn't hold even her whispers. So instead she recited. Names. Her congregation's names, the ones she'd gathered across eleven years of illegal candlelight: Maren Ostwick, who'd forgotten her own wedding but not the smell of her bride's hair. Tammas Cole, who wept at doorways he couldn't explain. The woman with no name at all, whose rite had failed, whose absence had become the cell's most sacred wound. She recited them the way other people counted coins, and if the Regent wanted to listen, let it choke on how many of them there were.

The lock turned without warning.

Odile was on her feet before the door finished opening, weight forward, ready to spend what little strength she had on one good scream—one sound loud enough to bring the whole block awake, because a riot was a wall and walls could be climbed. But the figure in the doorway raised a hand, and the hand wore a guard's glove, and under the helmet brim were eyes she knew.

"Sef Rennick," she breathed. "You absolute fool."

"Probably." He slipped inside, closed the door, and stood with his back against it, listening to his own courage fail and hold anyway. He looked terrible. He had looked terrible since the market, since the burning, since the moment he'd stood in uniform and done nothing while they fed Odile's chapel into the flames. She could see all of it written on him—the self-hatred worn smooth now, polished by repetition into something almost like resolve. "I have maybe forty minutes before the shift changes. Less if the warden does his rounds early."

"Then talk fast or unlock faster."

"Both." He pulled a ring of keys from under his cloak—guard-issue, unremarkable, the kind of theft that would cost him his post and possibly his hands—and knelt at her ankle irons. "Here's what I know. The renewal ceremony is in three days. Two, now, by the time you're out. The Regent means to re-carve the binding permanently, and it needs the city present for it—all of them, every soul it can pack into the square, because memory is easier to eat out of a crowd. It also—" the irons clicked open, and he moved to her wrists— "it has doubled the watch on the ossuary road. Which means Montrose is close to finding what she needs, which means we're out of time."

"The others?"

"Gone to ground. I moved them myself, two nights ago, before the market heat reached them. They're at the tannery's old curing loft. Odile—" He hesitated, keys stilling in the last lock. "Your congregation kept your candles lit. Every night. Right where the chapel used to be. Just stubs in a circle on scorched stone. I didn't have the heart to sweep them away."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Eleven years underground teaches a person to weigh mercy precisely, and this was real coin. "Thank you," she said, and meant it more than any prayer she'd ever failed to send.

"Don't thank me yet." He straightened, pressed a bundle into her arms: a guard's spare cloak, plain boots, a loaf of black bread still warm enough to steam. "There's a laundry cart leaves the cells at the change of shift. You'll be under the linens. The gate sergeant owes me a debt I intend to collect tonight, and after that you walk south two streets and knock twice at the tannery's river door. Can you run?"

"I'm fifty-three years old, Captain."

"Can you run?"

"I can run," she said, "if what's chasing me matters enough."

"It does." He held her gaze, and beneath the guard's discipline she saw the crack he'd been carrying since the purges, the debt he owed a bone-carver who had once hidden him from men exactly like the man he'd become. "When this is over—if it's ever over—I'm going to stand in whatever passes for a court and say everything. Every falsified report, every record rewritten, every name I watched them take. But that's after. Right now my job is getting you out, and then getting myself to the ossuary road before the double watch notices I'm the only guard walking it alone."

"Why alone? Where's Montrose?"

"Carving." Something crossed his face—awe, and fear, and the particular tenderness of a man watching someone do an impossible thing correctly. "You should see it, Odile. What she's building. It's not a weapon. I thought it would be a weapon. It's more like—" He searched. "Like an answer. To a question the binding asked twenty years ago and never let anyone reply."

Odile shouldered the bundle. In the corridor beyond the door, boots rang on stone, unhurried, routine. Forty minutes had just become thirty.

"Captain Rennick," she said quietly, "when the gods come back—and they will come back, whatever that thing believes—you'll want to be somewhere they can see you."

He opened the door. "I'm counting on it."

* * *

The curing loft above the tannery smelled of salt and old leather and, faintly, of tallow—a smell that had once meant sanctuary and now meant hurry. Mirabel had claimed the corner beneath the broken skylight because it gave her moonlight until midnight and gray daylight after, and because the floorboards there had been bleached silver by years of sun, and silver-colored things seemed to sit still for her.

The counter-sigil's frame lay across four planks she'd lashed together: a lattice of bone, and not bone of any single kind. The priestess's fragment from the ossuary formed its heart—a curl of yellowed ivory that hummed when Mirabel's fingers came near, that sang when she slept. Around it, in a spiral like a nautilus shell, she had set the freely given pieces: a knucklebone from Maren Ostwick, surrendered with a kiss to the scarred hand; a sliver of rib from Tammas Cole; teeth, vertebrae, a child's finger-bone worn smooth as a river stone. Each one warm. Each one willing. That was the grammar the priestess's ghost had taught her, the true grammar underneath the Accords' brutal architecture: *the binding was taken; the answer must be given.* Force had built the cage. Only consent could build the key.

But consent was slow work, and the frame was only the frame.

Mirabel braced the lattice on her knees and began the day's carving, and the dead gathered around her the way they always did—she felt them as warmth at her back, a congregation invisible and patient. The drowned girl's charm pulsed against her chest, keeping time.

"You should rest," said Odile from the doorway. She had arrived at dawn, smelling of laundry soap and river water, thinner than the chapel had ever let her look, and she had spent the day doing what she did best: organizing the desperate. The congregation took shifts bringing food. Rennick's runner network carried messages folded into fish deliveries. Even the disgraced priest, old Father Anselm, had crept up from wherever he hid and left a bundle of consecrated thread on the stairs without knocking.

"After the frame."

"You said that yesterday."

"And it was true yesterday." Mirabel didn't look up. Her knife moved in strokes fine as eyelashes, cutting the answering lines into the priestess-bone: not the binding's closed geometry, its locked circles and devouring spirals, but open forms—doorways, thresholds, the shape a hand makes when it opens. "Every hour I carve is an hour the Regent has to wonder what we're doing. Fear makes it hasty. Hasty things make mistakes."

"Fear also makes it cruel." Odile came closer, lowered herself stiffly to the floorboards. Up close, Mirabel could see what the White Cells had taken: the tremor in her hands, the new hollows under her eyes, the way she flinched at the skylight's rattle. "It spoke to me. Through the interrogators' mouths. It offered me my daughter."

The knife paused.

"I never told anyone here I had a daughter. I couldn't have—there was nothing left to tell. It reached into me and found the hole where she'd been and it put a name in the hole and asked me to walk through the cell door holding that name." Odile's voice stayed level, which cost her visibly. "I lied to it, Mirabel. I lied beautifully, and it bought three days, and I've been wondering ever since whether lying to a thing like that is faith or suicide."

"What name did it give you?" Mirabel asked softly.

"That's the worst part." Odile laughed, a dry, cracked sound. "It was right. Or it was close enough that some traitor part of me believed it. *Liesel.* It said *Liesel*, and something in my chest turned over like a key." She shook her head hard, as if dislodging water. "So carve fast. Because it doesn't need to beat us, Mirabel. It only needs to offer each of us the one thing we can't refuse, and wait."

Mirabel bent back over the frame. But her hands had gone quiet, and in the silence the dead pressed closer, and among them—faint, familiar, unbearable—she caught the ghost of a laugh she hadn't heard in fifteen years. Tam's laugh. The way he'd snorted when he found something truly funny, undignified, boyish, gone.

*Two days,* whispered the drowned girl. *And it knows about brothers.*

Mirabel's knife resumed. Outside, the bells began again—that rhythm no one could name, ringing the city toward the square, and far off across the rooftops, in the palace where a king slept uneasily inside his own stolen body, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to count the crowd it intended to eat.

Down in the curing loft, the counter-sigil's frame drank the lamplight, and waited for its heart to be finished—and for the name at its center, the keystone, the one the Regent hid deepest, the one Mirabel still did not know she would have to surrender.

Two days.
