## 17. The Price Named

The ossuary had a way of making time feel like something carved rather than lived. Mirabel felt it now as she sat among the stacked bones of the last priestess, the ghost's presence settling over her like cold silk. Three days. That was what they had. Somewhere above them, the Crown was hammering scaffolds into the coronation square, and the thing wearing King Aldric was sharpening its chisels for a binding meant to outlast the world.

"You have not slept," the priestess said. Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls of bone themselves, from the marrow of the city. "You should sleep."

"I'll sleep when it's done." Mirabel spread her tools across a flat slab of limestone—the ossuary's own floor, worn smooth by centuries of kneeling mourners. Her knives caught the lamplight: the hooked blade for joints, the fine burin for letterwork, the little spoon-shaped gouge she'd used since she was nine years old, when Tam had stolen it from a shipwright's shop and given it to her wrapped in brown paper. *For the girl who makes dead things sing.*

She flinched away from the memory before it could finish blooming. Not yet. She couldn't afford to spend them yet.

"The mathematics," she said. "Tell me again. All of it. I want to hear it wrong at least once so I know I've understood it right."

The ghost was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the particular gentleness of someone who has delivered death sentences before and learned that softness is the only mercy left to offer. "The original binding was carved from my bones because I gave them. Do you understand? The Sigil Accords did not take my body. I knelt in this room and I opened my own veins and I sang while I died, because a binding made from unwilling bone is only a cage, and cages rust. A binding made from given bone is a covenant. It holds because every part of it *agrees* to hold."

"And the Regent found the flaw in that."

"The Regent *is* the flaw." The lamplight guttered though there was no draft. "It whispered the Accords into Aldric's ear. It knew that a covenant can be answered but never broken—so it has spent twenty years making sure no one alive remembers how to answer. It ate the grammar. It ate the priests. It ate me, name by name, until even I had to learn myself back from the bones I left behind." A pause, and then, almost wry: "It is thorough. You must grant it that."

Mirabel picked up the burin and set it down again. Her hands wanted work; her mind refused to give them any. "The counter-sigil. The frame is done—or near enough. Odile's congregation gave their names freely, Rennick brought the records of the purged, the chorus of the dead has been teaching me the answering-grammar for two days straight. But you said the frame isn't enough. You said it needs a keystone."

"Yes."

"And you said—" Her voice cracked on the second word. She started over. "You said the keystone must be a name. Given with love. Surrendered with consent."

"That is the shape of it."

"Whose consent?" Mirabel looked up at the darkness where the ghost gathered itself, all of her attention narrowed to a point. "The erased are dead. The eaten are gone. Whose consent is left to give?"

The silence stretched until Mirabel thought the ghost might simply refuse—and then she understood, with the sick lurch of a floor giving way, that the silence *was* the answer.

"No," Mirabel said.

"His name is the keystone." The priestess's voice was very quiet now, the way one speaks in a sickroom. "The Regent hides it deepest because it is the strongest thing it ever took. Your brother walked into the first binding willingly, Mirabel. He gave himself as the anchor-point, the nail on which the whole covenant hangs. Every other name the Regent has eaten was taken. His was *given*. And a given thing cannot be re-given by anyone but the giver."

"He's dead. He's been dead twenty years. Dead men don't consent."

"Dead men do not. But he did not die whole, did he?" The ghost drifted closer; the lamplight bent around her like light around a hand held near a flame. "A piece of him went into the binding. A piece of him remains wherever the Regent keeps its hoard. And between those two pieces stands the only living soul who loved him enough, and knows him well enough, to speak his consent through the veil. There is exactly one door into Tam Montrose's will, child. That door is you."

* * *

She walked after that. She didn't decide to walk; her legs simply took her, up the ossuary stairs and out through the tannery's reeking yard and along the canal path where the drowned girl had once begged through a half-finished charm. Evening was coming down over Vellamere, the lamps being lit one by one by the lamplighters who were also smugglers and also congregants and also, tonight, soldiers in a war fought entirely with memory. The city looked so ordinary. Bakers closing their shutters. Children running home ahead of the dark. A woman beating a rug off a balcony three stories up, the thump-thump-thump carrying across the water like a slow heart.

Twenty years ago, Tam had walked these same streets. Twenty years ago, he'd kissed the top of her head and said *keep the shop, keep your hands busy, keep your head down*, and gone to the palace to do whatever it was the resistance had asked of him, and never come home. She'd spent two decades believing he'd been dragged. Believing he'd been afraid. Believing that if she had been braver, or louder, or less careful, she might have saved him.

He hadn't been dragged. He'd *chosen*. And the choosing had made him the keystone, and the keystone had made him the Regent's most guarded treasure, and now—

Now the price.

She stopped at the railing where the river ran dark beneath her. In chapter upon chapter of her life she had imagined this water taking things. It had taken the drowned girl whose prayer had started everything. It had taken, in smaller ways, pieces of everyone in Vellamere—a birthday here, a face there, whole neighborhoods' worth of shared history dissolved like sugar in rain. The Regent ate memory to stay anchored, Odile had told her. It ate, and the city forgot, and the forgetting made room for more eating.

And now it would eat her too. Not all of her. Just him. Just everything.

"Show me," she said aloud, to the priestess who had followed her without being asked, present as a pressure at her shoulder like weather. "Show me exactly what surrendering means. Don't soften it. If I'm going to choose grief, I want to know its weight before I lift it."

The ghost was silent for a moment. Then the world changed.

* * *

It was not a vision. Visions had edges. This had none—it simply *was*, and she was inside it, standing in the kitchen of her childhood with flour on the table and Tam's laugh coming through the wall from the room where he was supposed to be doing sums. She was perhaps ten years old. She could smell the bread. She could hear the specific creak of the third stair, the one their father always swore he'd fix.

*This,* the priestess said, from nowhere, from everywhere. *This is what surrender means. Not the loss of facts. The loss of the rooms.*

Mirabel reached for the table and her hand passed through the flour.

*Every memory of him is a room you have lived in,* the ghost said. *When you surrender his name, the rooms do not empty. They cease. You will not remember laughing with him and feel an ache where the laughter was. You will not stand in this kitchen and grieve. You will stand in a kitchen that was never anything but yours alone, and the space where he stood will not be a wound, because there will be no shape left to wound. Grief requires a contour, Mirabel. He will leave no contour.*

"That's worse," Mirabel whispered. "That's so much worse."

*Yes.* No apology in it. Only truth, which was the only currency the dead had ever carried. *And there is more. Consent cannot be coerced—not his, not yours. The working will ask you, at the final moment, whether you give freely. If one thread of you gives falsely—if you say yes while meaning no—the counter-sigil fails, and the Regent will know your face forever. So you must decide now. Tonight. Before the ceremony. You must choose grief the way one chooses a blade: fully, in daylight, knowing the cut.*

The kitchen flickered. Tam came through the doorway—fourteen, gangly, ink on his fingers, grinning at her with that crooked half-smile that had gotten him out of every trouble he'd ever fallen into. *Look what I found,* he said, and pressed the little spoon-shaped gouge into her hands, wrapped in brown paper. *For the girl who makes dead things sing.*

Mirabel screamed. Or she didn't—she couldn't tell, inside the memory, whether sound was hers to make. She stood in the flour-smelling kitchen and watched her brother give her the tool she still used every day, and she understood at last what the priestess had come to tell her: that the Regent had offered her this already, once, in the coronation square of some future she was trying to prevent. *Tam back, whole, if you stop.* And she had refused. She had thought refusing was the hard part.

Refusing was nothing. Refusing was easy. The hard part was this: to hold every room he lived in, every stair-creak and flour-dust afternoon and brown-paper gift, and carry them deliberately to the altar of her own mind, and lay them down, and let go—and mean it. To choose the amputation while the limb was still warm. To grieve in advance, completely, so that the surrender at the end would be not a theft but a gift, freely given, the way he had given himself, the way the priestess had given her bones.

Consent answering consent. That was the grammar. That had always been the grammar.

"How do I mean it?" she asked, when the kitchen had dissolved and she was back on the canal path with wet eyes and aching hands. "How does anyone mean a thing like that?"

*You practice,* the priestess said. *Tonight. You spend your last hours with him the way one sits vigil over the dying—not clinging, not begging, but blessing. You look at each room until you have thanked it. And then tomorrow, when the working asks, you will be able to answer honestly: I have already said goodbye. What remains is only the letting-go.*

* * *

Rennick found her at midnight, back in the ossuary, sitting before the half-carved counter-sigil with her knives laid out and untouched. He came down the bone-lined corridor quietly, as he did everything, a big man made careful by twenty years of walking on the law's thin ice. He carried two cups of chicory and a blanket, and he set them down without a word and sat beside her, his shoulder a warm wall against the cold rising off the stone.

"It's true, then," he said finally. "What the ghost told Odile. The keystone."

"It's true."

"Tam." He said the name carefully, the way one handles a relic. "I only met him the once. The night of the purges. He carried a wounded hedge-witch past my checkpoint on his back and I let them through because he looked at me like—" Rennick stopped. Rubbed his jaw. "Like he already knew how the story ended and had decided to be kind about it anyway."

"That sounds like him." Her voice held. She was surprised by that.

"Mirabel." He turned to face her, and in the lamplight his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "There has to be another way. There's always another way. We could—"

"There isn't." She said it gently, because he needed gentleness, because he had spent twenty years believing the law could be bent toward mercy and here was the proof that sometimes it couldn't—that sometimes the only honest arithmetic was the brutal kind. "The binding can't be broken, only answered. An answer needs a keystone. The keystone needs a name given with love and surrendered with consent. His name. My love. My consent." She picked up her cup of chicory and found, absurdly, that her hands were steady. "I've been a bone-carver my whole life, Sef. Do you know what I've learned? That the dead don't resent us for burying them. They resent us for pretending we haven't. I'm not going to pretend. I'm going to bury him properly, with my eyes open, and then I'm going to finish the work."

Rennick was silent for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "Then I'll hold witness. Whatever you need remembered afterward—whatever you want written down, kept, spoken over your shop door—I'll keep it. I owe him that much. I owe you more."

"Not written down." She shook her head. "If it's written, the Regent can find it. Eat it. Turn it against us." She looked at the counter-sigil, at the thousands of tiny names spiraling inward toward the empty keystone-space at its heart, a space shaped like nothing, shaped like an absence waiting to become a presence. "Just hold it. In your head, in your heart, the way the old priests held the gods. Be my memory, Sef. For as long as it takes me to build new ones."

"I will," he said. "On my life."

"Don't say that." A ghost of a smile. "People who say that in this city tend to end up in my workshop."

* * *

Dawn was still hours away when Rennick finally slept, curled against the ossuary wall like a man guarding a gate even in dreams. Mirabel stayed awake. She had decided that she would stay awake until the ceremony, that sleep was a country she could visit again after, that every hour of unconsciousness was an hour of Tam she'd lose without having chosen to lose it.

So she sat with him.

Not with his body—he had none, not anymore, not anywhere she could reach. She sat with the rooms. She walked through them one by one, the way the priestess had taught her, and she blessed each one. The kitchen with the flour. The third stair's creak. The summer he taught her to swim in the canal and she swallowed half the river and he laughed so hard he fell in too. The winter of the purges, the checkpoint, the wounded witch on his back, the look he'd given a young guard named Sef Rennick. The last morning, the kiss on her head, *keep the shop, keep your hands busy, keep your head down*—and how she had obeyed him for twenty years, and how she was about to break every rule he'd ever given her, gloriously, in his name.

*Keep your head down,* she thought. *Forgive me, Tam. I'm about to raise it higher than it's ever been.*

Toward morning, the priestess's presence returned, cool and patient at her shoulder. *Have you chosen?*

Mirabel looked down at her hands—her carver's hands, scarred and capable, the hands that had heard the dead since childhood, the hands that had made a drowned girl's prayer into a revolution. She thought about the empty space at the heart of the sigil. She thought about rooms ceasing rather than emptying. She thought about a brother who had chosen once, freely, and deserved to be answered in the same coin.

"I chose the moment you showed me the kitchen," she said. "Everything since has just been the goodbye."

*Then it is enough,* the ghost said softly. *More than enough. It is the rarest thing in any age, Mirabel Montrose: a grief chosen in advance, with love, with open eyes. The binding was built on such a choice. Yours will unbuild it.*

Far above, through the ossuary's throat of stone, the bells of Vellamere began to ring the hour—and then kept ringing, wrong, discordant, a pattern no bell-ringer had ever written. Mirabel stood, heart hammering, and Rennick woke with his hand already on his sword.

From the direction of the palace, faint but unmistakable, came a sound neither of them had heard in twenty years: a choir. Thousands of voices, singing the opening rite of the Renewal.

They had a day. They had less than a day.

Mirabel picked up her knives.
