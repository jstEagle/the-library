## 18. Vigil

The safehouse above the old chandler's shop had one window, and the window faced east, and Mirabel had chosen it for that reason without admitting to herself why. Tomorrow she would need the dawn. Tonight she needed only the dark it came out of.

She had cleared the worktable of everything but a single candle and the bones.

They were laid out on linen in the order she would carve them: the priestess's radius, pale as riverstone; Odile's daughter's finger-bones, given back from the rite that failed; nine small vertebrae from the drowned girl, dredged up by Rennick himself with his guard's gauntlets still on because he could not bear to touch them bare-handed; and others—thirty-one pieces in all, each one a name spoken into them by someone who still half-remembered, each one humming faintly when the candle flame leaned toward it, which it did now, all at once, like grass bending under wind.

The counter-sigil's frame was done. The keystone was not. The keystone was her.

Rennick came up the stairs the way he always did, announcing himself with his weight—one heavy tread, then a pause, then two more. Twenty years of guard discipline had worn grooves so deep in him that even betrayal moved along them. He carried bread, hard cheese, and a bottle of plum wine he set down without ceremony, as if provisioning a watchpost rather than keeping vigil with a woman about to amputate her own past.

"You should sleep," he said.

"After."

"That's what you said last night."

"And I slept beautifully," Mirabel said, "for an hour."

He almost smiled. She watched him decide not to, and something in her chest went loose and grateful, because tomorrow there would be no one left who remembered her well enough to make that particular face at her, and she wanted to bank it now, the way you bank a fire before a long cold.

"Sit down, Sef."

He sat. The chair complained. He looked at the bones, then away from them, the way he'd learned to look at the dead since this began—with respect and a soldier's refusal to flinch, both held at once.

"The frame holds?" he asked.

"It holds. It answered me tonight." She turned one of the vertebrae with a fingertip; it sang, very faintly, a note like a wet glass rimmed. "The grammar is right. The priestess checked my work twice." The ghost did not stay long these days—the binding thinned everywhere now, and the dead were being pulled through it like leaves toward a grate—but she had stayed long enough to say *yes* in the old tongue, and Mirabel had wept over a syllable.

"And the keystone?"

Mirabel poured the wine. Two cups. Her hand was steady, which surprised her; she had expected shaking by now, and instead there was only a terrible calm, the calm of a bone after the marrow is drawn.

"The keystone needs a name given with love and surrendered with consent," she said. "You know that."

"I know that." He took his cup but didn't drink. "I'm asking if it needs to be tonight. You could wait until you're standing in front of him. Say the name, give it up right there—"

"No." She said it too fast, and heard herself do it, and made herself slow down. "Because if I wait, I'll bargain. Standing in front of that thing, holding the last of him in my mouth—I'll try to keep some corner of it. A birthday. The way he whistled. One thing, just one, and the sigil will feel the lie and fail, and everyone dies, and the Regent gets twenty more years of eating us." She looked at the candle. "It has to be clean, Sef. It has to be whole. So it has to be now, while I'm not afraid yet, or while I'm afraid enough that I can't afford to lie."

Rennick nodded slowly. He was a man who understood orders best when they were also true.

"Then tell me how to help."

* * *

What she asked of him was nothing, and it was everything.

"Don't write it down," she said. "Don't carve it, don't memorize it, don't hold it for me. That's the temptation, isn't it? Someone keeping a copy. But the Regent eats memory wherever it lives—in heads, in paper, in bone. If you keep him somewhere, you're just a second door for it to break." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "I need a witness. Not a record. A witness. Someone who stands there while it happens and can say afterward: it was real. She didn't imagine him. Even if neither of us remembers what we're attesting to."

"That's a strange kind of testimony," Rennick said. "Swearing to a fact we'll both forget."

"All testimony is," Mirabel said. "You swear to what happened before it fades. This just fades faster."

He thought about that for a long moment, and then he did something she hadn't seen him do since the purges: he took off his sword belt and laid it against the wall, out of reach, the way a man puts down a tool when he's come to do something no tool can help with.

"Tell me about him," he said. "Start anywhere."

So she started at the beginning, because there was nowhere else, and because the beginning was the part she trusted herself least to keep accurate, and she wanted it said aloud while saying it could still be checked against the feeling underneath.

"His name was Tam. Tam Montrose. Our mother called him Tam-tam when he was small and he hated it so much he kept hating it even after she died, which I think was his way of keeping her alive. He was four years older than me. He had a gap in his teeth you could fit a straw through, and he could whistle through it, two notes at once, and he did it constantly, appallingly, during prayers, during school, during our father's funerals—" she stopped. "Both of them. Father died twice, in a sense. Once when he fell off the ferry dock, and once when the Accords took the river-god he'd been praying to since boyhood, and Tam said the second death was worse because at least the first one left a body to be angry at."

The candle leaned. The bones hummed their low chord. Outside, Vellamere lay quiet in the way of cities holding their breath before a coronation—banners going up in the dark, criers already rehearsing the Regent's proclamation in their sleep.

"He knew," Mirabel said. "That's the thing I have to say out loud tonight, Sef, because tomorrow I won't know it, and someone should hear it while it's true. He knew what he was doing. When the binding was carved, when they needed a name willing enough to sit at the center of it—he volunteered. My brother walked into the crown of King Aldric and let them seal him in, and he did it to keep the gods from being slaughtered outright, and I spent twenty years believing he'd been dragged away in a purge wagon because that was easier than believing he chose it and never told me why."

"Did he ever tell you?"

"Once." Her voice caught on the word and she rode past it. "The night before. He came to my room—he was nineteen, I was fifteen—and he sat on the end of my bed and taught me a carving pattern. Just a simple border knot, the kind you put on spoons. And he said, 'If you ever forget me, Mira, carve this, and your hands will remember even if you don't.' And I laughed at him. I said, 'Why would I forget you, idiot?' And he looked at me—" she pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes— "he looked at me like I was the one being foolish, and he said, 'Because forgetting is the only mercy anyone will offer either of us.' And then he whistled through the gap in his teeth, that awful double note, and went downstairs, and that was the last time I saw my brother's face."

The silence after was long and full, the silence of a room where something enormous has been set down carefully.

Rennick didn't touch her. She noticed that—noticed it and understood it. He was holding witness, and a witness doesn't alter the scene. But he slid his chair six inches closer, so that his shoulder was near hers, near enough to feel the warmth of him, and that was its own kind of speech.

"What else," he said quietly. "Keep going."

* * *

She kept going until the candle had burned down two thumb-widths and the wine bottle stood empty and her voice had gone rough as unpolished bone.

She told him about the summer Tam taught her to swim by throwing her in the Kestrel and letting her nearly drown exactly once, and how furious she'd been, and how he'd fished her out and said, "Now you're a swimmer forever, because fear only works once." She told him about the winter he apprenticed illegally to a hedge-carver in Salt Row and brought home charms shaped like sleeping birds, and how their father burned them, and how Tam rebuilt every single one from memory and gave them away to neighbors whose children coughed all winter, and not one child coughed. She told him about the girl he'd loved—a cooper's daughter named Brey, or Bray, or maybe Bren, and Mirabel stopped, horrified, testing the name against the feeling beneath it and finding the feeling intact but the name already fraying at the edges, the Regent's hunger reaching even here, into this night, into this room—

"Sef. What was the cooper's daughter's name?"

"Bren," Rennick said at once. "You said Bren an hour ago. Bren Halloway. Her father made the barrels for the customs house."

Relief hit her like cold water. "Bren. Yes. Bren Halloway." She gripped the table edge. "It's starting already. It's eating backward from the edges. That's why it has to be tonight."

"Then finish tonight," he said. "Tell me the rest."

She told him about the argument—their last real conversation, three days before the binding, when she'd begged him to run south with her, over the mountains, anywhere the king's writ thinned out, and he'd refused, and she'd said things she would give anything to unsay, except that tomorrow she wouldn't remember saying them, and wasn't that the Regent's whole economy, sin without penance, grief without a grave?

"And the last thing I said to him," she finished, "was 'I hope whatever they take from you is worth it.' And he said—" her voice broke cleanly, like a snapped wire— "he said, 'Mira. Everything I love is worth it.'"

She put her head down on her arms on the table, among the singing bones, and cried the way she had not cried at either funeral: hugely, gracelessly, with her whole body, while Rennick sat beside her and did not write a single word of it down.

* * *

Near dawn, gray light finding the east window, she lifted her head.

"Do you have it?" she asked. Her voice was wrecked. "Not the details. Do you have—him? Enough of him?"

Rennick considered the question with the seriousness he gave to tactical ground.

"I have a brother," he said slowly. "Four years older. Gap in his teeth. Whistled two notes at once. Chose something terrible to save people he loved, and let his sister believe the worst of him to protect her from the truth of it." He met her eyes. "I couldn't draw his face. But I know him. Is that enough?"

"That's more than enough." She laughed, wet and cracked. "That's the whole point. Memory isn't the record, Sef. It's the shape the record leaves. You're holding the shape."

"Then I'll hold it," he said. "And tomorrow, when you look at that list of names in my handwriting and feel nothing where he should be—you come find me, and I'll tell you the shape again. As many times as it takes."

She reached across the table and covered his hand with hers, the first time she had touched him outside of necessity, and he turned his palm up and held on.

"One more thing," she said. "Before it goes. Tell me what I sound like right now. So I'll know what I lost."

Rennick thought. "Like someone telling the truth," he said finally, "who's been practicing lying for twenty years and just quit."

She closed her eyes. *Tam Montrose,* she said inside, one last time, in the privacy behind her own eyes where no god or regent could follow yet. *Tam Montrose, who chose. Who whistled. Who threw his sister in the river. Who was worth it.*

The candle guttered. Down in the city, the coronation bells began their first rehearsal, and the bones on the table sang back to them, all thirty-two voices, waiting for the thirty-third.

Mirabel opened her eyes, picked up her knife, and set its edge against the keystone bone.

"Hold witness, Captain," she said. "I'm ready."

But before the blade could fall, boots hammered up the stairwell—three runners' worth of panic arriving at once—and Odile Ferran's voice came through the door, ragged with news that would burn the whole night's stillness to ash:

"Mira. The ceremony's been moved. It starts at sunrise."
