## 19. The Renewal

### Rennick

They dressed the city in white for the renewal, which was the cruelest thing the Regent could have done. White banners on every lintel. White chalk lines down the processional way. White robes for the ministers of the Crown, so that when they walked through Vellamere they looked like a procession of mourners who had forgotten whom they were mourning.

Sef Rennick stood at his post on the eastern steps of the Coronation Hall in a guard's uniform he had not truly worn since the night he falsified his first report. The uniform fit the same. That was the horror of it. Twenty years of small betrayals and the wool still sat on his shoulders like it belonged there.

Below him, the square filled. Ten thousand people, maybe more—fishwives and clerks and children hoisted onto shoulders, all of them come to watch their king be bound again. They did not know that was what they were watching. The proclamation had called it a *Renewal of Gratitude*, a ceremony of thanksgiving for twenty years of peace without sorcery. There would be hymns. There would be bells. There would be the king, radiant in the morning light, holding up the crown so the whole city could see the sigil carved into its band.

And there would be Mirabel Montrose, somewhere in that crowd, carrying a working made of stolen bones and borrowed names, walking toward the brightest light in Vellamere with nothing but her hands between her and the thing that wore the king.

Rennick scanned the faces below him and found nothing. She was good at this. Better than he'd ever been. He told himself that was comfort and knew it was a lie.

A minister touched his elbow. "Captain. His Majesty's party approaches. You'll take your position at the dais."

"Yes, Minister."

He took one last look at the crowd—at a woman near the fountain holding a bundle wrapped in cloth against her chest like a child, though Rennick knew it was not a child—and went to do his duty, whatever his duty turned out to be today.

### Aldric

He surfaced like a man coming up through dark water, and for a moment he did not know where he was, only that his hands were cold and someone else's thoughts were moving through them.

*The crown,* the Regent was saying, in the voice Aldric had long ago stopped being able to distinguish from his own. *Feel how it fits.*

It fit. It always fit. That was the trap of it—the binding had been carved into the crown itself, and the crown had been carved into his skull by twenty years of wearing, and now there was no clean place where the metal ended and the king began.

*Aldric.* The voice was almost gentle. Almost paternal. It had been gentle with him for two decades, the way a farmer is gentle with an ox. *Today we finish it. Today the seal is permanent, and you will never again feel them pressing at the edges. No gods. No whispers. No dead girls praying through river water. Only quiet. I am giving you quiet.*

*I gave you my kingdom,* Aldric thought, or tried to think. It was hard to know which thoughts were his anymore. Some mornings he woke and could not remember his mother's face, and he had learned not to grieve it, because grieving it made the Regent curious about what else might be spared.

*You gave me permission,* the Regent corrected. *There is a difference. Kings are good at signing things they don't read.*

The doors opened. Light poured in. Somewhere beyond it, ten thousand voices were singing the Accords Hymn, the one about the blessed silence, and Aldric felt himself being carried forward on the sound like a leaf on floodwater.

And then—briefly, in the last few steps before the dais—he felt something else. A thread of wrongness in the crowd, thin as wire, singing a note that did not belong to any hymn.

The Regent felt it too. Aldric felt the thing inside him go very still, the way a spider goes still when it feels the web tremble.

*Ah,* said the Hollow Regent. *There she is.*

### Odile

Odile Ferran had escaped the White Cells three days ago with a broken rib, Rennick's route-keys, and no plan worth the name, and now here she stood in the Coronation square with half her congregation pressed around her like a shield of the half-forgotten, and she thought: *this is either the stupidest or the holiest thing I have ever done, and I have done both in quantity.*

Her ribs screamed when she breathed. She breathed anyway.

"Look at them," murmured Tam's sister's friend's widow—a woman whose name Odile kept having to re-learn, because the Regent had eaten it twice already. "They're smiling."

"They are," Odile said. "That's the trick of it. It doesn't hurt, what he does. That's why no one fights. You can't raise a mob over something that feels like peace."

Above them, the king emerged onto the balcony, and the square roared, and Odile watched the crowd's joy with the flat professional attention of a woman who had spent twenty years tending things the law said did not exist.

She saw it begin before anyone else did.

It started at the edges of the square, subtle as frost forming. A fishwife mid-laugh forgot the punchline of her own joke and stood blinking. A boy on his father's shoulders asked *why are we here?* and the father opened his mouth and produced nothing. An old man near the fountain looked down at his own hands as if he'd never seen them before, then smiled, because the smile was easier than the question.

"It's starting," Odile said. "Everyone hold your charms. Hold your names. Say them under your breath—your name, your mother's name, the street you grew up on. *Say them.*"

Around her, thirty ragged voices began whispering like wind through dry grass. *Maren. Maren Ferran. The Tannery Row chapel. My daughter's name was—*

My daughter's name was—

Odile's breath caught. Three days in the White Cells, and the Regent had offered her the name back, and she had lied beautifully and bought time, and she had thought the price was paid. But standing here under the widening shadow of the re-carving, she reached for Maren and found the shape of her like a footprint in sand with the tide coming in.

"No," she whispered. "No, you don't. *Maren Ferran. Born in the spring. Laughed like breaking glass. Mine.*"

She gripped the bone charm at her throat until it cut her palm, and held on.

### Mirabel

Mirabel had spent her whole life learning how to be unseen, and it turned out that all of it—the hunched shoulders, the dull clothes, the shop that smelled of dust instead of power—had been training for this: walking through ten thousand people carrying the loudest object in Vellamere and looking like nobody at all.

The counter-sigil rode against her chest in a sling Odile had sewn from altar cloth. It was the size of a loaf of bread and it weighed like grief. Inside its frame of priestess-bone and freely-given bone, forty-one names were carved, each one given by someone the Regent had tried to erase, each one singing so faintly that only another carver could hear it. Forty-one names out of hundreds. It should not have been enough. The mathematics of the working were brutal, and the mathematics said *many names or none.*

But the priestess's ghost had taught her the true grammar, and the grammar said something the Accords had never allowed anyone to say: the binding could not be broken. It could only be *answered*. And an answer did not need to be bigger than the question. It needed only to be true.

Around her, the crowd dimmed.

She felt it happen the way you feel weather change—not seeing it, but knowing. The whispers of the dead, which had been a low tide-murmur at the edge of her hearing all morning, began to thin. Names peeling off gravestones. Faces sliding off portraits. She passed a woman clutching a locket and heard, inside the locket, a small voice repeating *please remember me, please remember me* faster and faster, like a prayer losing its words.

Mirabel's hands shook. She made them stop.

*Forty-one names,* she told herself. *Say yours with them.*

*Mirabel Montrose. Daughter of the Tannery Quarter. Sister of—*

Sister of—

The word came back blank. Not empty—*blank*, like a page removed from a book, the remaining pages still bearing the dent of what had been pressed against them. Her chest seized around a hole shaped exactly like a laugh she couldn't hear anymore.

*Tam Montrose,* she said silently, fiercely, to the hole. *Who chose. Who chose. Who chose.* She didn't know yet what he had chosen—that knowledge waited for her past the binding—but she knew he had chosen, and she held that like a rope over a drop.

Ahead, the dais blazed white. The king raised the crown above his head, and the crowd hushed, and the Regent's voice rolled out over the square wearing Aldric's throat:

"People of Vellamere. For twenty years you have lived in peace, because your king loved you enough to bind away the things that prey upon you. Today I renew that gift. Today the silence becomes eternal."

He lowered the crown toward his own head.

And the sigil carved into its band began to glow, and the glow began to spread, crawling down the white banners like fire down a fuse, and everywhere it touched, memories went out like lamps.

Mirabel stepped out of the crowd into the open.

### Rennick

He saw her the moment she moved, because he had been looking for her the way you look for a wound—you find it by the pain.

She walked into the aisle the crowd had left before the dais, small and straight-backed, gray-haired, carrying nothing visible at all. Every guard on the steps would see her in seconds. Every minister. The thing wearing the king.

Rennick's hand went to his sword out of pure trained reflex, and he looked at his own hand as if it belonged to a stranger, and he made a choice he had been making in pieces for months and made whole in that instant.

He drew the sword—and pointed it, not at her, but down the steps at the advancing line of ministers. "HOLD!" he bellowed, in the parade-ground voice that twenty years of drills had built into his chest. "By authority of the Captain of the Guard, the square is sealed! No one advances!"

It bought nothing but seconds. Seconds were what he had to sell.

The nearest minister turned to him with an expression of mild surprise, and behind the mildness Rennick saw the other thing looking out, patient and vast, and it *recognized* him—it had read his falsified reports, it knew his handwriting, it knew every debt and cowardice in him—and it smiled with the minister's mouth.

"Captain," it said pleasantly. "You look unwell."

"I'm fine," Rennick said, and set his feet, and meant to die there if dying was required, and discovered—to his enormous surprise—that he wasn't afraid. He had been afraid his whole life of this exact moment, and now that it came, it felt like setting down a weight.

Across the square, Mirabel Montrose lifted the bundle from her shoulder and unwrapped the counter-sigil to the morning light.

### Mirabel

The bones sang.

Not faintly now. The moment the sun struck the frame, all forty-one names sounded at once—not a discord but a chord, a sound like a cathedral organ played by the dead, and the crowd heard it too, ten thousand people flinching as if a bell had rung inside their skulls. And in that ringing, things came back. Small things, first: the fishwife's joke, the boy's reason for being there, the old man's own name. The Regent's spreading glow stuttered like a candle in wind.

On the dais, the king's head turned. Aldric's face looked down at her, and it was not Aldric looking out of it. The Hollow Regent regarded her across the square with eyes gone pale as river ice, and its voice, when it spoke, did not bother with the king's warmth anymore. It came out layered, many-voiced, the sound of a choir with everyone removed from it.

"*Little carver.*" The word fell over the square like ash. "*You brought me a gift. Bones. How thoughtful. Do you know what I do with bones?*"

"You eat what they remember," Mirabel said. Her voice was small against the square and it carried anyway; the charm made sure of it. "I know. I've heard them. All of them. Every name you took—they've been talking to me for weeks." She took a step forward. Another. Terror sat in her stomach like swallowed stone and she walked with it, carried it, let it ride along the way she had once carried grief. "And I've been listening. That's the difference between us. You take. I listen."

"*I take so that they will not suffer,*" the Regent said, and for one flickering instant it sounded almost sincere, almost wounded, and Mirabel understood with a lurch that it believed this—that it had whispered the Accords into existence believing it was mercy. "*Memory is pain. I have carried the pain of a kingdom for twenty years. I am tired, little carver. Let me rest. Let them rest. Give me the frame and I will give you back the brother. Whole. Warm. Laughing. You could have him back before the bells finish ringing.*"

The square went silent. Ten thousand people, most of them not understanding what was being offered, and one woman at the center of it understanding completely.

For a heartbeat—for one entire human heartbeat—Mirabel let herself want it. Tam's laugh. Tam's terrible whittled birds. Tam at seventeen, alive, arguing with her about the proper way to salt fish. She let the wanting fill her all the way up, and it was the sweetest thing she had ever tasted, and it was poison, and she knew it, because anything the Regent gave you was made of what it had eaten, and what it had eaten of Tam was not Tam. It was the memory of Tam, worn like a coat.

"You don't have him," she said quietly. "If you had him, you wouldn't offer him. You keep him hidden deepest of all, because he's the keystone, and keystones are for keeping." She raised the counter-sigil above her head, arms burning, terror and resolve fused into a single bright unbearable thing. "His name is Tam Montrose. He chose. And I'm here to answer you."

The Regent screamed.

It was not a kingly sound. It was the sound of a hollow place trying to be full, of hunger discovering it could be refused, and the crown on the dais flared white-hot as the old binding surged to defend itself—and Mirabel felt the counter-sigil wake in her hands, felt the forty-one names lean into hers like soldiers leaning into a door, and she spoke the grammar the priestess had taught her, the answer to the question carved in the crown:

"The binding stands," she said, "and we answer it: *you were never asked. We ask. We choose. We remember.*"

Light met light above the Coronation Hall, and the world held its breath—

—and deep beneath the dais, in the royal ossuary, in the dark where the keystone slept, something that had chosen to be forgotten twenty years ago began, at last, to stir.
