## 20. Kestrel

The light in the coronation square was the color of old ivory.

Mirabel walked into it with her arms bare and her sleeves rolled back the way she did at her workbench, as if this were only another commission, another grieving family, another name to hold. The counter-sigil hung against her chest on a cord of braided hair—Odile's, Rennick's, a strand of her own—and beneath it, strung along both forearms like terrible bracelets, sang the charms.

Two hundred and eleven of them. She had counted them the way you count your own heartbeats when you cannot sleep: not for comfort but for proof that the counting is still possible. Each charm held a name the Regent had eaten. Each one was carved from bone freely given—the priestess's femur at the center of the frame, the congregation's offerings around it, the drowned girl's small finger-bone set at the apex where the keystone would go. They sang now, all of them together, a sound below hearing, a pressure more than a pitch, and Mirabel felt them the way she felt Tam's voice once through a half-finished charm: *there, there, there.*

The crowd did not see her at first. That was the Regent's doing. It stood on the coronation dais in Aldric's body, in Aldric's crown, and it was pouring itself into the binding carved into the gold, and the pouring took everything it had, so for one merciful moment its attention was elsewhere. Around the dais the crowd stood too still. Their faces were smooth as river stones. As Mirabel passed through them she saw memory leaving them visibly—a woman forgetting mid-smile why she had smiled; an old man's hand rising to touch a wedding ring he no longer recognized; a child opening her mouth to ask for her mother and finding, briefly, no word for *mother* at all.

*Hurry,* said the chorus of the dead, everywhere inside her skull, gentle as snowfall. *Hurry, carver. He is drinking them.*

She was hurrying. Her legs were shaking. She was hurrying.

The dais steps were white marble, twenty of them—she counted those too, because counting was a rope and she refused to let go of either end. Halfway up, the Regent noticed her.

The change was subtle. Aldric's head turned by degrees, the way a weathervane turns when the wind decides. The king's face was still the king's face, lined and tired and human-shaped, but behind the eyes something vast leaned close to the glass, and the glass fogged with its breath.

"Bone-carver," it said. Its voice was Aldric's voice used badly, stresses in the wrong places, a man's instrument played by something that had learned music from watching drowning men. "You are late to your king's coronation."

"It isn't a coronation," Mirabel said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "It's a re-carving. You're renewing the Accords. You're making yourself permanent."

"I am making *quiet* permanent." The Regent spread Aldric's hands, and the crowd sighed as one, and somewhere in the third row a man sat down heavily on the cobbles because his legs had forgotten what they were for. "Twenty years I have kept this city from tearing itself apart over gods who never loved it. Twenty years of peace. You would trade that for whispers."

"I would trade it for names." She climbed two more steps. The charms on her arms were singing louder now, and she could feel the Regent hearing them—it flinched, a small ugly twitch at the corner of Aldric's mouth, each time the chorus swelled. "You ate a drowned girl's name. She was seven. Her mother kept setting a fourth plate at supper for a month before she understood why the table looked wrong."

"The girl was noise," the Regent said. "The mother's grief was noise. All of it is noise, and I am the quiet after." Aldric's eyes fixed on the counter-sigil at her throat, and Mirabel watched hunger move across the king's face like weather. "That is beautiful work. Give it to me and I will let the city keep its dreams."

"No."

"Then let us speak of dreams worth keeping." And the Regent smiled, and it was the worst thing yet, because the smile was warm. It was kind. It was the exact smile of a brother about to share a secret. "Mirabel Montrose. Bone-carver. Daughter of the tanner's row. Sister."

The word went through her like a blade slipped between ribs.

"I know what you lost," the Regent said softly, and began to descend the steps toward her, unhurried, a king greeting a subject. "You think I eat memory whole, but that is wasteful. I *savor*. And I have been saving yours. Every one of them. Do you want to hear how he laughed? I have it. Do you want the sound of him whittling in the dark while you pretended to sleep? I have that too. The way he said your name—*Mira*, always *Mira*, never Mirabel—I have kept it safe for fifteen years, polished it every day like a jewel, waiting."

It stopped three steps above her. Close enough that she could smell the myrrh of the ceremony oil on Aldric's skin.

"Put down the sigil," the Regent said, "and I will give him back to you. Not the memory. *Him.* Whole. I have worn ten thousand dead and I can wear one more, and he will laugh the way he laughed, and say your name the way he said it, and he will sit across from you at supper every night for the rest of your life, and you will never have to be the sister of a ghost again. You will have a brother."

The square was silent except for the crowd breathing in unison, slow as tide.

And Mirabel stood in that silence and let herself want it.

That was the trap and she knew it was the trap and she walked into it anyway, deliberately, because Odile had taught her that the only way past a hook is to look at the bait until you understand exactly what you are refusing. She wanted it the way the drowned want air. She saw it whole: Tam at her workbench, complaining about her dust; Tam stealing the good knife for the hundredth time; Tam grown older than he ever got to be, gray coming in at his temples because she had imagined it so often it had become real to her. A life with the hole filled. A life where the purges had never taken him, where the night the guards came had gone some other way, where she had not spent fifteen years carving silence into herself one careful stroke at a time.

"You're crying," the Regent observed, with something almost like tenderness. "Good. Grief is the handle by which I lift you."

"I know," Mirabel said.

She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, the way she did when bone dust got in her eyes, and she lifted the counter-sigil from her throat, and she held it up into the ivory light.

"But here's what you don't understand about carvers," she said. "We don't work in what things are. We work in what they *were*. And my brother—" her voice cracked, and she let it crack, she let the whole square hear it— "my brother was not a thing you could offer me back. Because he wasn't taken. He *chose*. And a choice isn't a possession. It's a door, and it only opens one way."

The Regent went very still.

"You hid his name deepest," Mirabel said. "Keystone of your whole binding. Fifteen years I thought the guards took him and I was too late to save him, and the truth is he walked to the altar himself, and gave himself willingly, and his love for this city is the nail you hang on. You couldn't break his consent. So you buried it. But consent doesn't rot, Regent. It waits."

"*Stop,*" the Regent said, and Aldric's voice broke in the middle of the word, and for one instant underneath it Mirabel heard another voice, thin and human and terrified—*I remember my mother's face*—and then the vastness closed over it again.

"The priestess told me the grammar," Mirabel said. She was shaking now, openly, and she did not care. "A binding cannot be broken. Only answered. And an answer must be given the way the question was asked—freely, with love, surrendered with consent. His consent comes through me. Through the last person who remembers him." She looked up into the king's stolen face, and her heart was breaking, and she let it break, because broken ground is where you set the post. "So I'm going to say his name. And when I do, I'm going to give you every memory I have of him. All of them. Willingly. With love. And you will have to *hold* them—you'll have no choice, that's how the grammar works—and my grief for my brother will sit inside you beside his sacrifice, and they will be the same shape, and they will not fit, and you will come apart at the seam."

"I will give him back," the Regent said, and now the warmth was gone and the hunger was naked, and the crowd moaned as it pulled harder at their memories, drinking fast, panicking. "I will give you the *boy*, Mirabel, I will carve him out of my own substance, he will be warm, he will be real—"

"He will be a puppet wearing my love," Mirabel said. "I know. I've seen you wear a king."

She set the keystone—the drowned girl's finger-bone—into the socket at the counter-sigil's apex. It fit with a small click that she felt in her teeth. Two hundred and eleven charms rose off her arms, drifting, singing, arranging themselves in the air around her in the pattern the priestess's ghost had traced in the dark of the ossuary: the answer to the question the Accords had asked twenty years ago.

The Regent raised Aldric's hands and the sky over Vellamere went the color of a scar.

Mirabel filled her lungs.

"His name," she said, "is Tam Montrose."

The Regent screamed without a mouth.

"And he chose," Mirabel said, and the memories began to leave her.

They left the way blood leaves a wound—not all at once, but in pulses, in tides, and she stood in the tide and named them as they went because naming was all she had left to give. *Tam teaching me to hold a knife. Tam's laugh through the shop wall. Tam the winter of the fever, Tam asleep in the chair, Tam saying Mira, Mira, wake up, the guards are coming, and me pretending to sleep because I was afraid, God forgive me, I was afraid—*

The Regent seized the memories as they poured out of her, gulping them, trying to anchor itself in them, and that was the answer working, that was the grammar closing: every memory of Tam was soaked in his choice, and his choice was consent freely given, and consent freely given was the one thing the Hollow Regent could not digest. It swallowed and starved. Swallowed and starved. The binding in the crown began to sing back at the counter-sigil, note answering note, question answering answer, and the space between the notes grew wider and wider—

"—who chose," Mirabel said.

And then there was a boy whittling in the dark, and she could not say why the image made her chest hurt. There was a word, *Mira*, hanging in the air with nothing attached to it. There was a shape of sunlight where a person used to be, and she reached for it and her hands closed on warm empty air, and she understood, distantly, from very far away, that she had paid, that the price had been paid, that it was done—

The Regent unraveled.

There was no better word. It came apart like smoke deciding to be honest, like thread giving up its cloth, like a lie that has been spoken aloud in front of everyone and finally, gratefully, stops pretending. The vastness poured backward out of Aldric's body, out of the crown, out of the binding, drawn up and away and thinner and thinner until it was a seam of darkness against the scar-colored sky, and then a scratch, and then a cold spot in the air, and then nothing at all except the ordinary morning wind moving through the square.

The binding in the crown cracked. Not shattered—cracked, a single clean line through the Sigil Accords, the way ice cracks before it becomes water.

King Aldric fell.

Mirabel caught him. She did not decide to; her knees simply folded and her arms went out and there he was, heavier than a king should be, lighter than the thing that had worn him. His eyes were open. They were wet.

"My mother," Aldric whispered. His hand found the front of her shirt and gripped it like a drowning man. "Her face. I remember her face. Twenty years—I remembered nothing, I remembered *nothing*—" He was weeping the way men weep who have forgotten how, badly, loudly, with his whole ruined body. "What did it take from you? Carver. What did it take?"

Mirabel opened her mouth to answer and found the place where the answer lived, and the place was full of sunlight, and the sunlight had no name.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "Something I agreed to give."

Around them the crowd was waking like a field after frost. The woman who had forgotten her smile remembered it and pressed both hands to her mouth. The old man turned his ring slowly around his finger, reading it like a letter from a stranger who turned out to be himself. Somewhere a child shouted *Mama!* and a woman answered, and the two sounds met in the middle of the square and held on to each other.

The charms drifted down around Mirabel and the king like slow snow, two hundred and eleven small bones settling onto the marble, still singing, softer now, softer, the song of work finished.

Rennick reached them first, sword drawn, uniform torn, running as though the running were the only prayer he knew. Then Odile, gray-faced from the White Cells, leaning on a young runner's shoulder. Then the congregation, the half-forgotten, spilling into the square from every street, touching their own faces, saying their own names aloud like passwords to a country they thought they'd lost.

Mirabel knelt on the dais steps with a weeping king in her arms and a hole in her chest shaped like sunlight, and she did the only thing a carver does when a piece is finished.

She looked it over for flaws.

The binding: cracked, not broken. The crown: loose on Aldric's brow, and he did not seem to want it. The Regent: gone—but gone the way a debt is paid, not the way a debt is forgiven, and somewhere in the marrow of the world she could feel the difference, faint as a scar under a sleeve.

And herself. She checked herself last, the way carvers always do.

She was missing something. She knew she was missing something the way you know a room is missing a chair—by the clean patch of floor where it should stand. She searched inward and found a feeling like sunlight with no window to explain it, and a word—*Mira*—that meant nothing and hurt everything.

"Mira," she said aloud, testing it.

It echoed. It echoed as if the square itself had learned the word from someone who had once shouted it through a wall, and had kept it, all this time, waiting for her to need it back.

Above the dais, the crack in the binding widened by a hair's breadth, and through it—for the first time in twenty years—Vellamere heard its gods breathe.

* * *

They carried Aldric down the steps on a bier made of guard cloaks, because he refused to be carried any other way and refused also to walk alone. Mirabel walked beside him. She could not have said why he wanted her there, except that twice on the long road to the palace he looked at her hands—bone-dusted, nicked, a carver's hands—and wept again, quietly, as if recognizing a debt he could not find in any ledger.

At the palace gates he stopped. The crowd had followed them the whole way, growing, filling the avenue from wall to wall, and now they waited, breath held, to learn what a king was when the hollow thing was out of him.

Aldric turned to face them. He looked smaller. He looked, Mirabel thought, like exactly what he was: a man who had spent twenty years locked inside his own body watching it be used.

"The Accords are repealed," he said, and his voice carried because the square carried it for him, mouth to mouth, faster than any herald. "As of this hour. And I will stand trial—" a roar went up and he raised a hand, and waited it out, and went on, quieter, "—for what was done in my name during the years I could not stop it. All of it. Let the record be true, whatever else is true."

He looked down at Mirabel then, in front of everyone, and did not lower his voice.

"This woman bought your names back," he said. "Ask her what it cost. She won't tell you. She can't." His jaw worked. "Neither can I. But I saw it. I saw it from inside, at the end, and I will spend what's left of my life repaying it."

"There's nothing to repay," Mirabel said.

"That," said Aldric, "is what worries me."

He was escorted inside. The crowd did not disperse. It did the other thing, the thing Mirabel would remember even when she'd forgotten why it mattered: it turned, all of them, slowly, toward her, and an old woman near the front—the one with the wedding ring—said, in the hush, "What do we do now?"

And Mirabel Montrose, standing in the wreckage of the most important day in twenty years with a nameless sunlight aching in her chest, heard herself give the only answer a carver ever gives.

"Bring me your dead," she said. "We have a great deal of work to do."

Behind her, unnoticed by anyone but her, the cracked binding in the crown hummed—one low, patient note, like a door left very slightly open.
