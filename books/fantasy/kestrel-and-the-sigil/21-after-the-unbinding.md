## 21. After the Unbinding

The silence afterward was the loudest thing Sef Rennick had ever heard.

He stood in the coronation hall among three hundred people who had just watched their king wake up screaming, and no one made a sound. The Regent's unraveling had left a residue in the air—like the moment after a bell is struck, if the bell had been ringing for twenty years and everyone had forgotten what quiet was. Dust hung in the light from the high windows. Somewhere in the rafters, a pigeon shifted its weight.

Aldric lay on the steps of the dais, crumpled like a man set down by someone who no longer cared how. His crown had rolled free and come to rest against the base of the throne, spinning slowly, wobbling, dying. Rennick watched it stop. He thought: *that's it, then. That's the whole kingdom, stopped.*

Mirabel Montrose knelt beside the king with her hands hovering over him, not touching. She looked hollowed out. Not weeping, not triumphant—just emptied, the way a house looks after the furniture's been carried out and you can finally see the shape of the rooms. Rennick knew that look. He'd worn it for years without knowing why.

Later he would learn what she had paid. Later she would tell him, in her flat bone-carver's voice, about a brother named Tam, about choosing, about the word *chose* being the last thing she said before the memories went out of her like a snuffed lamp. But in that first hour, all he knew was that she had walked into the light carrying charms that sang, and the thing wearing the king had come apart like wet paper, and now she knelt there with nothing in her face at all.

"Captain." A guardsman—Voss, young, still holding his ceremonial halberd as though the Regent might come back and require stabbing—was at his elbow. "Orders?"

Rennick looked at the hall. The ministers of the Regency were pressed against the far wall, pale as tallow. Chancellor Veyne, who had signed the arrest orders, who had rewritten the census rolls with his own careful hand, was trying to edge toward a side door with the dignity of a man who believed doors would still open for him.

Rennick had spent twenty years learning which orders to follow slowly. This was not one of those moments.

"Nobody leaves," he said. "Seal the doors. Veyne stays where he is."

Voss blinked. "On whose authority?"

"The king's," Rennick said. "He's awake. Ask him."

* * *

Aldric woke the way a drowning man wakes—badly, in stages, each one worse than the last.

Rennick was there when the physicians finished with him, because someone had to be, and because he did not trust anyone else in that palace to stand in the room without flinching or kneeling. The king sat propped against pillows in a bedchamber that smelled of camphor and old incense, and he was smaller than Rennick remembered. Not physically—the man had always been lean—but somehow diminished, the way a sword is diminished when the smith grinds away the rust and you see how much metal is gone.

"You're Rennick," Aldric said. His voice was rough, underused. "You were at my father's funeral. You held the banner when the wind took it."

"I was sixteen, Majesty."

"I know." The king looked at his own hands, turned them over, studied them as if checking for someone else's fingerprints. "I know a great many things I have no right to know. It's been sitting in me like stones. Twenty years of watching through glass." He closed his fists. "How long?"

"How long since you were yourself? We don't know, sire. Possibly since the beginning."

Aldric laughed—a terrible sound, dry and cracked, like a door opening on a room no one had entered in years. "Since the beginning. Yes. I remember signing the Accords. I remember the quill moving. I don't remember deciding to move it." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and human, and Rennick felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched so long he'd mistaken it for his skeleton. "There was a woman. A priestess. Her bones are in my crown."

"They were, Majesty. They aren't anymore."

"Then I owe a debt to whoever took them out." The king's gaze sharpened. "And I owe a longer one to everyone who suffered while I slept in my own skull. Bring me the Accords, Captain. The original text. And bring me paper."

* * *

The repeal was read aloud in the coronation hall nine days later, because Aldric refused to let it be merely published. "It was done in public," he said. "It will be undone in public. Let them watch this time instead of forgetting."

Rennick stood at the foot of the dais in full dress uniform while the king's herald unrolled the proclamation. Aldric had written most of it himself, in a hand that shook and then steadied, and Rennick had watched him cross out the word *pardons* three times before settling on *restitution*.

*"Let it be known,"* the herald read, *"that the Sigil Accords, signed in the twentieth year of the old reckoning, are hereby void in their entirety. The binding carved into the crown of Vellamere is dissolved. The gods of this city, banished by fear and fed upon by treachery, are free to return—or to withhold themselves, as their judgment falls. No law of this realm shall compel worship, nor forbid it. And let it be known further that the Crown acknowledges its crimes—"*

Here the herald paused. Aldric nodded once, curtly, like a man swallowing medicine.

*"—its crimes against its own people: the purges, the burnings, the erasures of name and record. These were not policy. They were predation. They will be investigated, recorded, and answered—not by forgetting them, which has been our sickness, but by remembering, which will be our cure."*

Three hundred people listened. Some wept. Some stared with the flat suspicion of people who had learned, over twenty years, that proclamations were things that happened to other people. An old woman near the front—Rennick recognized her distantly as one of Odile Ferran's congregation, one of the half-forgotten—stood perfectly still until the herald finished, then said, in a voice pitched to carry:

"And who answers for it? The dead can't testify."

"No," Aldric said, and his voice carried too, unassisted. "But the living can. And they will be heard." His eyes found Rennick's. "Starting with those who enforced it."

The old woman turned. Looked at Rennick. Looked at his uniform, the silver braid, the captain's insignia polished that morning.

Rennick did not flinch. It cost him.

* * *

They offered him the palace.

Not in those words, of course. Chancellors and lords never say anything in words; they say it in corridors and pauses. Lord Protector Cassel caught him outside the council chamber two days after the repeal, in the marble passage that smelled of beeswax and cold stone, and put a hand on Rennick's shoulder like an old comrade.

"Aldric needs a strong hand at the Guard," Cassel said. "Someone the men trust. Someone who kept order through the hard years." A pause, weighted. "Someone who understands that a city recovering from twenty years of fear doesn't need more fear added to it. There will be inquiries, Captain. Inquiries can wound a fragile peace. A wise man knows which truths serve and which ones only bleed."

Rennick looked at the hand on his shoulder. He thought about the night of the purges, nineteen years ago, when he had been a sergeant with a squad of six and orders to clear a carver's row, and he had found a girl of maybe nineteen standing in the doorway of her shop with a bone charm in her fist and terror all over her face—and he had looked at her, and looked at his men, and said, *This row's already cleared. Move along.* And Mirabel Montrose had gone inside and shut the door, and neither of them ever spoke of it, and he had carried it anyway, every day, like a stone sewn into his coat lining.

He thought about the tannery chapel. About the raid order he'd stalled, the report he'd falsified, the congregation of half-forgotten souls singing hymns to gods who couldn't hear them. About Odile Ferran lying beautifully in the White Cells to buy three days with her life.

About Ash Wednesday Market, and himself standing in uniform doing nothing, hating himself cleanly, precisely, in the way he'd been trained to hate—efficiently, without consequence.

"There's going to be an inquiry," Rennick said. "Into the purges."

"If it must be. Limited in scope, naturally—"

"I'll be testifying."

Cassel's hand tightened fractionally on his shoulder. "Captain. Think about your career."

"I've thought about it every day for twenty years," Rennick said. "That's the problem." He removed the lord protector's hand with two fingers, gently, the way you move a sleeping cat off a document you need to sign. "Thank you for the offer, my lord. I'll take the truth instead. It pays worse, but the hours are honest."

Cassel's face went through several small weathers. "You'll destroy yourself."

"Probably," Rennick agreed. "But I'll do it facing forward."

* * *

The inquiry convened in the autumn assize hall, a chamber built for grain disputes and boundary quarrels, which somehow made it worse—worse and better. No cathedral grandeur to hide behind. Just wooden benches, a raised table for the commissioners, and a clerk with a fast hand recording everything, which was the entire point, which was the whole revolution of it: that things said here would be *written down* and *kept*, and that the keeping would be the punishment and the mercy both.

Rennick testified for four days.

He told them about the purge lists and how they were generated—not by evidence but by quota, by ministers feeding names to the Regent the way you feed a fire. He told them about resonant objects burned in Ash Wednesday Market, and named the officer who gave the order, and watched that officer's face across the room go gray. He told them about the falsified report, his own, and entered it into evidence with his own signature attached, and heard the clerk's pen scratch it into permanence.

He told them about the night magic fell. He had been seventeen, a raw recruit on the walls, and he had seen the sky over the temple district flicker like a guttering lamp, and heard—not with his ears, with something deeper—a chorus cut off mid-note. Twenty years later, saying it aloud in a room full of clerks, he finally understood that what he'd heard was the gods being locked out of their own city.

"Why did you not report these matters earlier?" asked Commissioner Hale, a dry stick of a man with ink-stained fingers.

"Because reporting them would have destroyed me and changed nothing," Rennick said. "Because the records were being rewritten faster than testimony could be filed—I have documents proving that, exhibits fourteen through thirty-one. Because I was afraid." He let that sit. "I'm including the fear in my testimony because I think it belongs there. Half the city stayed silent out of fear. If the record shows only heroes and monsters, it will be a lie, and lies are what got us here."

Hale made a note. "And Captain Montrose's associate—the hedge-witch Ferran. You assisted her escape from the White Cells."

"I did."

"That was a crime under the Accords."

"The Accords were repealed before I did it," Rennick said, and then, because the truth was the whole point: "No. That's not honest. I decided to do it before the repeal. I would have done it regardless. I'm prepared to answer for that."

The commissioner regarded him for a long moment. "For the record, Captain, why?"

Rennick had expected the question. He had an answer prepared, something measured about legality and legitimacy. What came out instead was simpler.

"Because a woman in a cell was being offered her dead daughter's name as bait, and the thing making the offer wasn't human, and somebody had to act like one." He folded his hands. "The law is how we hold each other, Commissioner. When the law stops holding people—if it becomes the thing you need to be held *against*—then keeping it isn't duty anymore. It's just obedience. I stopped confusing the two somewhere around the third falsified report."

The scratch of the clerk's pen filled the hall. Nobody moved. And Sef Rennick, who had spent twenty years being exactly the right amount of silent, sat in the noise of his own recorded voice and felt the stone in his coat lining, for the first time, begin to dissolve.

* * *

The gods came back the way frost comes—not all at once, not everywhere, but unmistakably.

First it was small things. The shrine niches in the old walls, bricked over during the Accords, began to feel warm to the touch. A fisherman on the Vell swore the river god had taken his offering again after two decades of indifference; three days later, the whole waterfront claimed the same, and half of them were liars, but not all. Rain fell on the temple ruins and did not pool—it sank, straight down, as if the ground had remembered how to drink.

Odile Ferran wrote to Mirabel about it—Rennick saw the letter pinned above the workbench in the reopened shop, its edges curled from handling. *They're cautious,* she'd written. *Like cats returned to a house where they were beaten. They sit in the thresholds and watch us. Give them time. Give them names.*

Names. Everything came back to names. Rennick had spent four days testifying to a hall full of clerks whose entire function was the preservation of names, and he understood now that this was the true repair—not the repeal, not even the king waking, but the slow, stubborn re-inscription of ten thousand erased lives into the record of the world. Justice, it turned out, was mostly bookkeeping done by people who cared.

He visited the shop on a grey Thursday, ostensibly to deliver the final inquiry schedule, actually because he had nowhere else he wanted to be. Mirabel was at her bench, carving. She looked up when he came in, and her eyes went to the papers in his hand, and he saw her brace herself—he'd learned to read that in her, the way she braced for bad news the way other people braced for cold water.

"Testimony's concluded," he said. "Findings next month. Your name came up."

"It usually does."

"As a witness, not a defendant." He set the schedule on the bench. "Montrose—they want you to describe the counter-sigil. For the record. The grammar of it, the priestess taught you. They think it should be preserved."

Mirabel was quiet a moment. Her thumb moved over the half-carved charm in her hands—an unremarkable piece of riverbone, except that Rennick had learned to notice the way her pieces hummed faintly against her skin, like held breath.

"The priestess's grammar," she said at last, "isn't mine to give away for free. But some of it should be written down. Before I forget the parts I was allowed to keep." She looked up. "Will you be there? At the findings?"

"Front bench," Rennick said. "I'm listed first. 'Primary witness for the prosecution of state erasures.'" He tried the phrase out loud and found it fit better than any rank he'd ever worn. "Cassel resigned rather than face the inquiry, incidentally. Veyne didn't get the chance. He's in the White Cells now, in the cell Odile used. The irony is apparently lost on him."

"Nothing's lost on him," Mirabel said. "That's what makes it justice instead of revenge. He'll understand every day of it."

She turned the charm in her fingers, and Rennick saw—carved into the curve, small enough to miss—a kestrel in flight, wings spread, and beneath it a second mark he didn't recognize: a simple spiral, like a shell, like a name curling around an empty center.

"For whom?" he asked.

Mirabel looked at the little carving for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was steady and very soft, the voice of a woman reading from a list she could no longer see.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But someone loved him. And I intend to find out who."
