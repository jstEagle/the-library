## 22. Charms for Forgotten Names

The shutters came off the shop on a morning that smelled of rain and river-mud, the way Vellamere smelled before the Accords, if the stories could be believed—and now they could be, which was the strangest part of everything.

Mirabel Montrose stood in the doorway of her own shop with the key still warm from her pocket and looked at what two years of seizure had left behind. Dust lay over everything like grey snow. The workbench was where she'd left it, scarred with twenty years of knife-cuts, but the drawers had been rifled and not closed properly, and someone had stacked her blanks—calf bone, river-ox rib, the pale offcuts she'd never had the heart to burn—in a corner as though they were evidence of something.

They had been evidence of something. That was the whole trouble.

She crossed the floor and opened the shutters one by one, and light came in the way it does after a long illness: gradually, then all at once, filling the room to its corners. Outside, Ash Wednesday Market was rebuilding itself around her. A carpenter was fitting new boards over scorch marks on the cobbles where the censor fires had burned. The baker's stall had reopened under a striped awning, and a woman was arguing cheerfully about the price of honey-bread, and neither of them flinched when a bell rang somewhere in the temple district—the old gods' bells, recast, ringing again for the first time in Mirabel's lifetime.

She had heard bells like that once, she thought. Or she had been told she'd heard them. There was a difference now between the things she remembered and the things she knew, and she was learning to live in the space between.

* * *

The first charm took her most of the day.

Not because it was difficult. Her hands remembered their work even when the rest of her did not; the knife found the grain of the bone the way water finds a channel. It took the day because she made herself go slowly. She made herself read the name on the slip of paper three times before she carved it, and say it aloud twice, and hold the finished thing against her palm until it warmed.

The list lay open on the bench beside her. Forty-one names, written in Captain Sef Rennick's blunt, unlovely hand—the hand of a man who had learned to write reports that would survive inspection, every letter squared and defensible. He had compiled it from the chapel records Odile Ferran had kept in code beneath the tannery, from parish books the censors had missed, from the testimony of families who had come forward after the Unbinding with scraps and fragments and half-heard prayers. Forty-one people the Hollow Regent had eaten so thoroughly that even the gods, newly returned, could only find smudges where they had been.

*We can hold them,* the river-god had said, in the voice of moving water, when Odile asked. *But only if someone gives us somewhere to hold them.*

So: charms. Bone-carved vessels, freely given, freely received. Mirabel had designed the first one herself, working from the priestess's grammar, and the design had come out of her hands almost whole, as though some part of her that no longer had a name attached to it had been waiting years to make exactly this.

She carved the name into the curve of the bone. She carved the answering sigil beneath it—not the binding's grammar of *sealed, silent, kept*, but the counter-grammar the ghost in the ossuary had taught her: *spoken, held, returned.* And when she set down the knife, the charm did what the old memorial charms had never quite done. It sang.

Not loudly. Not the vast wrong pressure she'd felt two years ago pressing at the edges of the dead's whispers. This was small and clear, a note like a wet finger on a glass rim, and inside the note was a person—a woman named Brey Ostrewe, who had sold ribbon-dye by the canal and laughed too easily, according to a sister who remembered the laugh but nothing else. The sister would come tomorrow. The sister would take the charm home and hang it above the door, and every time the wind moved it, Brey Ostrewe would be spoken into the world again, and the gods would hear, and keep.

Mirabel wrapped it in cloth and wrote the sister's name on the outside, and sat for a moment with her hands flat on the bench, letting the silence settle.

This was the work now. This was what she had chosen instead of the thing she'd lost, though *chosen* was too clean a word for it; you did not choose grief the way you chose bread at market. You chose what to do inside it. Rennick had said that to her once, standing in this very doorway with his guard's coat finally hanging loose on his shoulders, no longer armor, just cloth. He'd said it awkwardly, the way he said everything true.

*You gave up a brother to save a city. Most people don't get a trade that matches the size of the wound.*

She hadn't answered him then. She wasn't sure she could answer him now. But she picked up the next blank, and the next name, and began.

* * *

They came to the shop the way water comes back to a dry channel—hesitantly at first, then in flood.

The first week it was the families from the list, one or two a day, arriving with the careful faces of people who have rehearsed their grief and are afraid it will look wrong. A man whose wife had been erased so completely that he had remarried and then, after the Unbinding, spent a month unable to explain to his new bride why he wept at certain songs. An old soldier who had fought beside someone whose face he could no longer picture, only the fact of loyalty, huge and sourceless, sitting in his chest like a stone. A child of perhaps nine who came alone—which should have frightened Mirabel, and did—and put a copper on the counter and said, "Mama says you can make the dreams stop being empty."

Mirabel carved for all of them. She taught herself to interview without wounding: *Tell me anything. A smell. A habit. What they did with their hands.* Because a name alone was a thin vessel; the sigil needed texture, the grain of a life, to sing properly. And the families gave what they had, and it was always strange and always enough—the dead turned out to be surprisingly durable in their details, persisting in secondhand anecdotes, in recipes, in the way a daughter still folded her sleeves the way her erased father had.

By the second week there was a queue. By the third, Odile Ferran had appointed herself unofficial doorkeeper, installed a stool by the window, and begun charging a nominal fee for those who could pay—"the congregation tithes, carver, and your rent is real"—while waving through anyone who couldn't. Odile had aged in the White Cells and then somehow un-aged since, as though freedom had been a poultice. She wore her hedge-witch's charms openly now, on the outside of her shawl, little clacking things of wood and shell, and she greeted each visitor with the same outrageous warmth, as if every grieving stranger were an old friend arriving late to supper.

"You're staring at the list again," Odile said one afternoon, catching Mirabel motionless over the forty-one names, thirty-three of them struck through in completed work.

"I'm trying to remember if I knew any of them."

"And?"

"No." Mirabel turned the page over, as if the answer might be hiding on the back. "That's the thing, Odile. I worked this market for fifteen years. Some of these people must have bought thread from me, argued with me about prices, complained about the weather. And there's nothing. Not a face, not an irritation, not a favorite kind of pie. He didn't just take them. He took everyone's *having known* them." She set the page down carefully. "I keep thinking one of these days I'll carve a name and my hands will hesitate, because some part of me will recognize it. And it hasn't happened yet."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then she said, in a different voice than her usual one, "It happened yesterday."

Mirabel looked up.

"Name eleven. Jossan Wray." Odile's fingers moved to one of her shell charms, worrying it. "When you said it aloud—you always say them aloud—I watched your face. You smiled. Just for a breath. Like hearing a joke end well." She shrugged, uncomfortable with her own tenderness. "I don't know what it means. Maybe nothing. Maybe the memory's gone but the shape of it stayed, the way a footprint stays in mud after the foot's gone."

Mirabel looked down at the list. *Jossan Wray.* The ink meant nothing. The smile, apparently, had meant something.

"Leave the charm with me tonight," she said. "I want to sit with it."

* * *

She sat with it, and the shop went dark around her, and she did not sleep so much as drift along the edge of a country she could no longer enter.

There was sunlight in it. That was the only way she could describe it afterward—a feeling like sunlight falling across her shoulders, warm and specific, the particular weight of afternoon light through a south-facing window. Someone was nearby in the drifting place. Someone whose presence she knew the way you know your own hands, without looking. They weren't doing anything remarkable. They were whittling, maybe, or laughing at something she'd said, or just breathing in the same warm room. The feeling had no name attached. It had no face. It was pure relation, relation stripped of its object, love with the beloved's features worn away—and it hurt worse than any memory could have, because a memory at least ends, and this simply *was*, ambient and eternal, sunlight she could not source.

She woke at dawn with tear-tracks drying on her cheeks and the Jossan Wray charm singing softly in her closed fist, and she thought: *this is what he left me. Not the memories. The weather of them.*

She decided she could live on that. Barely, but truly.

* * *

Rennick came on a Thursday, in civilian clothes that fit him badly, carrying bread and a bottle of plum wine and the expression of a man about to deliver a report he'd rather burn.

"The trial transcripts are public," he said, once they'd eaten. "All of them. Aldric insisted—no sealed annexes, no 'for the dignity of the crown.' Every purge order, every name, read aloud in the great hall with the king sitting there taking it." He turned his cup. "I testified Tuesday. Third day. My voice held better than I expected."

"I know. Odile was there. She said half the gallery wept and the other half wanted to arrest you anyway."

"That sounds like Odile." A pause. He was bad at pauses; they sat on him like borrowed coats. "There's talk of a commission. Formal reparations. Restoring seized property—" he gestured at the shop around them, the shelves of finished charms, the light, "—which is how you got this back, technically, though I signed the order myself because I wanted to see the look on the clerk's face."

"Was it a good look?"

"He cried a little. Clerks cry easily. It's the filing."

She laughed, and he relaxed by degrees, and for a while they drank plum wine and listened to the market noise and the far-off bells, and it was nearly easy, the way it had become easy between them over two years—two people who had stood together in the worst light there is and come out the other side bound by something that didn't need naming because both of them had seen what happened to things that got named carelessly.

Then he said, "There's a boy asking after you at the chapel. Been there three days. Odile's been feeding him, but he won't say what he wants to anyone but the bone-carver." Rennick hesitated. "He's maybe twelve. River-family, from the look of him. Downstream wards."

Something cold touched the base of Mirabel's spine, an old reflex, the memory of another drowned child's prayer reaching up through a half-finished charm. But the binding was gone. The Regent was unraveled, thread by thread, name by given name; she had felt it go, had felt the city's past pour back into it like water into cracked ground. Whatever this was, it wasn't that.

"Send him tomorrow," she said. "Morning. I'll be carving anyway."

* * *

He came at midmorning, when the light through the south window was doing its best imitation of the sunlight in her drifting places. He was thin and serious and he stood in the doorway as if permission might be revoked at any moment, and when Odile tried to offer him a stool he shook his head and gripped the strap of his satchel instead.

"You're the carver," he said. Not a question.

"I'm the carver."

"My gran sent me." He dug into the satchel and brought out a bundle of oilcloth, unwrapped it on the counter with the exaggerated care of a child handling something sacred. Inside lay a piece of bone—old bone, yellowed, dense, a fragment of something larger long broken—and a scrap of paper gone soft at the folds.

"She said her mother kept it hidden in the chimney all through the bad years. Said it was from before the Accords. Said—" the boy screwed up his courage visibly— "said it used to whisper. Before. And it stopped for twenty years, and last month it started again, and Gran says it's saying a name over and over, and she's too old to walk this far, and she says if anyone knows what to do with a bone that sings, it's you."

Mirabel looked at the fragment and did not touch it yet.

Bones sang to her. That had always been the gift and the curse of her; she heard the dead through the material they left behind. But this bone was not from the Regent's eating. This bone predated everything. And the binding was gone, which meant—

She picked it up.

The chorus hit her like weather.

Not one voice but many, layered, patient, twenty years deep in the dark of a chimney: the dead of the purges, the ones whose bones had never been found, never carved, never sung home. They had waited in this fragment the way seeds wait in frozen ground, and now, with the binding broken, they were germinating all at once, and the name they were saying—the name the boy's gran had heard—rose out of the chorus and stood in the middle of the shop like a person walking in.

*Tam.*

Not Tam Montrose. She would have known that; some scorched stump of her would have flared. This was *Tam* as a sound the dead were passing hand to hand, a name carried through the chorus because the one soul who had the right to carry it had paid it away—and the dead, who owed him everything, had refused to let it drop. They had caught it as it fell from her. They had been holding it ever since. Waiting for her shop to reopen. Waiting for her hands.

The sunlight-feeling broke over her again, overwhelming, specific, and this time it had a direction: it came from the bone, from the chorus, from the dead saying *he chose, he chose, he chose* in the rhythm of a work-song, and underneath it, fainter, a single voice that might have been his, that was probably only her longing wearing his shape, that said:

*You don't have to remember me. That was the point. Remembering me was never your job. It was mine, and I did it, and it was enough, and look what you built in the space where I was.*

Mirabel set the bone down on the bench among her tools, among her forty-one finished names, and found she was smiling, and found the smile was steady.

"What's your name?" she asked the boy.

"Piet."

"Well met, Piet. Tell your gran she was right to come." She drew a fresh blank toward her—river-ox rib, good density, good grain—and picked up her knife. "And tell her this: the bone isn't haunted. It's *waiting*. There's a difference, and the difference is work, and I'm going to do it." She tested the blade's edge against her thumb, the old familiar sting. "It'll take a while. There are more voices in here than I can count, and every one of them gets a vessel, and every vessel gets a name, and every name gets said out loud until the gods have it by heart."

The boy nodded solemnly, having understood perhaps a third of it, which Mirabel judged sufficient.

Odile appeared at her shoulder with the tactless delight of a woman watching history start in a cluttered shop. "Where will you begin? With the loudest?"

Mirabel considered the fragment. The chorus hummed against her palms, patient as seed, warm as a south window, and threaded through it ran the name she had surrendered and the dead had saved, and the feeling like sunlight that she could not source and no longer needed to.

"With the quietest," she said. "The loud ones can wait. The quiet ones have been waiting longest."

And she set the knife to the bone, and began.
