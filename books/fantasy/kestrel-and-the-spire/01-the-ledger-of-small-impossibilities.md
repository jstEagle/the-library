## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its hours the way a miser keeps coins: carefully, secretly, and never quite in the order you expected. Jonah Crane had learned this on their first night as an apprentice keeper, seven years ago, when Vess Corvane had handed them a candle and said, *Inventory begins at midnight because that is when things are most themselves.* Jonah had not understood then. They understood now, standing in the vault beneath the guildhall with a ledger open across their forearm and a stylus tucked behind one ear, surrounded by shelves that breathed.

Not metaphorically. The shelves actually breathed — a slow expansion and contraction of the old blackwood, which had been cut from trees that grew over a buried god's dream. Everything in the vault was like that. Everything here was slightly more alive than it should have been.

Jonah began, as always, with the echoes.

Row three, shelf two: forty-one bottles of bottled echo, each stoppered with wax pressed from a different century. Jonah lifted the first — a squat green thing no bigger than a fist — and held it to their ear the way a physician listens to a chest. Inside, faintly, someone's grandmother was laughing in a kitchen that no longer existed. The laugh had been sold by the granddaughter after the funeral, three years back; she'd needed coin for passage north, and grief, the Guild had found, weighed less when portioned out. Jonah recorded the bottle's condition — *stable, wax intact, laughter fading at approximately half a percent per season* — and set it back exactly where it had stood. The shelf sighed around it.

*Stable*, they wrote. *Fading. Recommend re-waxing within the decade.*

They moved down the row. A boy's first word, bottled in amber glass. The sound of a door closing on an argument that would never be resolved. Someone's memory of rain on a roof in a city that had since burned — the buyer had wanted to know what home felt like, and had never once opened the bottle, preferring to keep the wanting whole. Jonah catalogued all of it without flinching. That was the discipline of a keeper: you did not judge what passed through your hands. You measured it, dated it, and wrote its truth down so that nothing, ever, could be entirely lost.

It was the writing down that Jonah loved. Not the trade itself — though the trade paid for the roof over their head and the ink in their well — but the ledger work. The world outside Lowmark's walls was full of things slipping loose: names worn off gravestones, rivers that wandered when no one watched them, promises made in childhood and forgotten by thirty. Somebody had to keep the accounts. Somebody had to say, with ink and certainty, *this existed, this mattered, this is where it went.*

Jonah Crane was very good at saying that.

At the far end of row three sat the river.

Officially, the ledger called it *Lot 114: The Aldern Brook (portion thereof, approx. two miles, stolen)*. Unofficially, everyone in the Guild called it the trouble, because it had been sitting in its long stone trough for eleven years while the Guild's lawyers argued with the Guild of Cartwrights about whether a waterway could legally be considered chattel. It had been taken — folded up like cloth, allegedly by a thief who could fold anything — from a valley three provinces east, and it had never stopped trying to go home. At night, if you stood close enough, you could hear it muttering against the stone. Rivers, Jonah had learned, remembered their courses the way old dogs remember doors.

Jonah checked the trough's seals, noted the water level — down a finger's width since last month; it was evaporating into somewhere else, some elsewhere it dreamed of — and wrote:

*Lot 114. Restless. Recommend settlement of dispute or return to source before the specimen exhausts itself. A stolen river is still a river.*

Then they paused, stylus hovering, and added in smaller script, the way they always did, the way Vess had once caught them doing and laughed at: *I'm sorry.*

"You apologize to inventory," Sefa Idris had told them once, arms crossed in the doorway of the vault, her voice flat with a contempt she wore like armor. "You know that, don't you? You apologize to *bottles*."

"Things notice," Jonah had said. "Everything notices."

Sefa had looked at them for a long moment with those gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave less, and then she'd shaken her head and gone back upstairs, and Jonah had understood that she thought them soft, and that she was wrong, and that being wrong about it cost her something she'd never admit.

* *

Midnight inventory took four hours if done properly, and Jonah always did it properly. By the time they reached the last shelf — row nine, the shelf of names — the candles had burned down to stubs and the vault had settled into its deepest quiet, the particular hush of a room full of sleeping valuables.

Names were the most delicate stock the Guild handled. An echo could be resold; a borrowed future could be returned, more or less; but a name, once traded away, rarely came back to its owner. People sold them for debts, for love, for escape. Some buyers collected names the way others collected stamps. Some buyers were the names themselves, hungry, looking for a way back into a mouth.

Tonight there was one new acquisition to log. Jonah unrolled the small lead cylinder — names were stored in lead, which slowed their leaking — and read the accompanying slip in the broker's cramped hand:

*Sold willingly by the bearer, one name, common usage, twenty-two years held. Reason given: "to become someone else." Payment: passage and a new face. Note: bearer wept. Note: bearer smiled while weeping.*

Jonah slid the cylinder into its cradle and wrote the entry with the care it deserved:

*One name, surrendered freely. Held in trust until claimed or until the law of return lapses (100 yrs). May it serve its bearer better than it served them.*

Their hand hesitated over the page. Names were the worst part of the work, worse even than the futures, because Jonah could never quite shake the feeling that somewhere in the city above, a person was walking around with a hole in their mouth where a word used to live. They had asked Vess about it once, early on — why the Guild dealt in names at all, why not stick to echoes and honest hauntings — and Vess had looked at them with that expression she reserved for questions she intended to answer sideways.

"Because if we didn't," she'd said, "someone worse would. Better our ledgers than theirs, little crane. Better our hands than theirs." And she'd rapped her knuckles twice on the ledger's spine, the way she did, as if waking it, and changed the subject.

Vess Corvane was not a woman who answered questions. She was a woman who made you feel clever for having stopped asking them.

* *

Jonah climbed the spiral stair out of the vault a little past the fourth bell, carrying the night's ledger under their arm, and found the guildhall above already lit — which was wrong. The hall was supposed to be dark at this hour. The great chamber with its hanging maps and its long oak tables and its chimneypiece carved with the faces of every guildmaster back to the founding was meant to sleep until dawn, when the day-keepers came shuffling in with their tea and their complaints.

But lamplight spilled across the floorboards, and at the master's table, alone among the shadows, sat Vess Corvane with a ledger open before her that Jonah had never seen before.

She was not a large woman. She had a way of taking up space anyway — elbows planted, silver-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be an actual quill, rings on six fingers, each ring older than the last. She looked up when Jonah's boots creaked on the boards, and her face did the thing it always did: softened by exactly one degree. Never two. One degree of warmth, precisely rationed, like everything else in the Guild.

"Crane." Her voice was smoke and arithmetic. "You're late."

"The vault ran long. Lot 114 is losing water again."

"Rivers are ungrateful." She gestured with her chin at the chair across from her. "Sit. I want your eyes on something."

Jonah sat. Up close, they could see that the ledger before her was old — older than any in the working collection, bound in leather gone the color of dried blood, its corners worn round as river stones. There was no title on the spine. There was no title anywhere. But along the fore-edge of the pages, in faded gilt, someone had lettered a single word, and Jonah's stomach performed a slow, cold turn as they read it.

*Covenant.*

"I've finished the night inventory," Jonah said, setting their own ledger down. "Forty-one echoes stable, two flagged. Lot 114 needs settling or returning. One new name in trust. Nothing unusual."

"Mm." Vess turned the old ledger toward them, and for a moment — a strange, suspended moment — Jonah thought she was going to show them the pages. Thought she was finally going to explain the word that had haunted the margins of their apprenticeship for seven years: *covenant*, appearing in the master accounts like a ghost in a family portrait, always referenced, never defined. Every keeper signed an oath that mentioned it. Every contract bore its seal. And whenever Jonah had asked — and they had asked, politely, persistently, for years — the answer had always been the same sideways smile, the same knuckles rapped on wood.

*Better our ledgers than theirs, little crane.*

Vess's hand rested flat on the open page. She did not turn it toward Jonah after all. Instead she closed the book, slowly, and the sound of it shutting was louder than it should have been, the way a door sounds louder when it closes on a room you will not see again.

"Nothing unusual," she repeated. "Good. That's good, Crane. Keep it that way."

"Master Corvane—"

"Vess." She almost smiled. "How many times."

"Vess." Jonah leaned forward. "I've been keeper of the night vault for three years. I've logged every lot, balanced every account, reconciled every debt the Guild owes and is owed. I have never once seen the covenant ledger. I've never seen *anyone* see it. When the wardens come to audit, it isn't on the manifest. When the founders' portraits are cleaned, the first guildmaster is holding a book that matches that one exactly, and no one will tell me what happened to it." They heard their own voice tighten and did not stop it. "I belong here. I have given this place my nights and my careful hands and — and my loyalty, which you have never once had to ask for. Don't I get to know what I'm keeping?"

The lamplight moved. Somewhere in the walls, the guildhall's old timbers ticked and settled, the building dreaming its slow wooden dreams.

Vess studied Jonah across the table, and for a heartbeat something moved behind her eyes — something Jonah had never seen there before and would spend a long time, afterward, trying to name. It might have been guilt. It might have been grief. It might only have been the lamplight, guttering.

"You get to know what you're keeping," she said at last, quietly, "when knowing it won't break you. That is not an insult, Crane. It's the kindest rule I own." She rose, tucking the covenant ledger inside her coat, close against her body, the way one carries a wound. "Finish your entries. Lock the vault. Go home and sleep, and tomorrow be meticulous, because you are the most meticulous thing I have ever made, and I am proud of you, and one day — one day soon, I think — none of it will matter anymore."

"*None of it*—" Jonah started to stand. "What does that mean? What happens tomorrow?"

Vess paused at the foot of the stairs that led up to her rooms. She did not turn around. The candlelight put her shadow long across the floorboards, stretching toward Jonah like something reaching.

"It means," she said, "that some accounts are coming due. Old ones. Older than the Guild." A pause, and then, softer, almost to herself: "I did try to find another way. Whatever you hear, whatever you see — remember that I tried."

And she climbed the stairs and was gone, leaving Jonah alone in the great hall beneath the painted faces of the founders, heart hammering, mind circling the same three words like a moth around a flame.

*I tried. I tried. I tried.*

Jonah gathered their ledger, snuffed the candles one by one, and stood a moment in the dark before the chimneypiece, where the first guildmaster's carved face gazed out with its stone book in its stone hands. On impulse — an impulse they would remember, later, with terrible clarity — Jonah lifted their candle stub and held its dying light to the carved book's gilt-edged pages.

There, incised thin as a hairline crack, so faint it might have been wear, might have been age, might have been meant to be found only by someone stubborn enough to look: a line of text too small to read by candlelight, running along the bottom of the carved page like a watermark.

Jonah memorized its shape anyway. Keeper's habit. You wrote down what you saw, and trusted the ink to keep it safe until you understood it.

Outside the tall windows, over the rooftops of Lowmark, the stars were burning as they had burned every night of Jonah's life — steady, indifferent, numbered. In the border hills far to the east, the ancient wards hummed their tuneless, eternal hum, holding back whatever they had always held back, and no one in the city gave them a second thought.

By tomorrow midnight, both would be gone.

Jonah blew out the candle and went home through the sleeping streets, unaware, carrying the shapes of words they couldn't yet read, while behind them in the dark the guildhall settled on its foundations like a beast preparing, at last, to wake.
