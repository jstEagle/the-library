## 2. What the Wards Were Holding Back

The wards did not fail all at once. That was the terrible part, Jonah would think later — that the world gave them a moment of warning, a held breath, and no one in Lowmark thought to take it.

Jonah heard it first as a sound from the ledger room, where they had been cataloguing the evening's acquisitions by lamplight: a faint, high note, like silk drawn slowly over a blade. They set down their pen. The ink on the page before them — entry 4,117, *one borrowed summer, lightly used* — was still wet, and it shivered.

Not the ink. The lamplight. The flame leaned sideways as though listening to something far away, and then the sound came again, closer now, unmistakable: the tearing of silk, vast and slow, as if someone were unwinding the sky's hem one thread at a time.

Jonah was out of their chair and at the window before they had decided to move.

Lowmark lay below the guildhall in its usual evening arrangement — chimney smoke standing straight in the windless air, lamplighters working the river district with their long brass poles, the market square emptying itself of carts and noise. It was an ordinary night. Jonah had catalogued four hundred ordinary nights from this same window, and they knew the city's rhythms the way they knew the weight of their own pen.

Then the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Out* — all of them, in the same instant, from horizon to horizon, as though a hand had passed over the sky and closed it like a book. One moment the constellations Jonah had mapped a hundred times hung in their patient arrangements; the next there was nothing above Lowmark but a black so complete it seemed less like absence than like attention. Something looking down. Or something looking *in*.

Jonah's hands found the windowsill without being asked to. Their breath fogged the glass even though the night was not cold.

"Entry four thousand one hundred seventeen," they said aloud, because saying things aloud kept them real, and the guild taught that early. "One borrowed summer. Condition: intact." Their voice sounded thin in the ledger room. "The stars are not catalogued as consumable."

Below, the first scream went up from the river district.

* * *

Jonah took the stairs three at a time, ledger satchel slung across their chest the way Vess Corvane had drilled into every keeper in their first year: *You do not run toward disasters empty-handed. You run toward them with your tools.* The guildhall's ground floor was already in motion — Sefa Idris shouting orders to the junior keepers, her dark braid coming loose, her face lit orange by the emergency lamps someone had unshuttered.

"Sefa!" Jonah caught her arm at the door. "The border wards — the ones along the Grayfen line — when were they last inspected?"

"How should I know? I file requisitions, Crane, I don't walk the borders." But her eyes had gone to the black sky through the open door, and whatever she saw there made her grip tighten on Jonah's sleeve. "That's not weather. That's not anything. What is that?"

"That," Jonah said, "is what the wards were holding back. And we're about to find out why they stopped."

They went out into the screaming dark together.

Lowmark was coming apart in the way cities do — not all at once but in pieces, each piece its own small apocalypse. A lamplighter stood frozen in the middle of Tanner's Row with his pole raised, staring up at the starless sky as though he could shame it back into burning. Doors slammed in waves down the streets ahead of something no one could see. From the direction of the Grayfen hills, far off, came a sound Jonah had only ever read about in the oldest ledgers: a low, resonant humming, like a struck bell that never finished ringing, and beneath it, fainter, something almost like voices.

The memory-fog came off the river first.

Jonah smelled it before they saw it — a smell like rain on old paper, like the inside of a book left shut for years. Then it rolled up Cobble Lane, gray-white and slow, waist-high, moving against the wind. Where it touched, people stopped. A fishwife mid-shout simply... quieted, her mouth still open, her eyes going smooth and pleasant as she forgot, apparently, what she had been angry about. An old man sat down in the middle of the street and began to smile, murmuring a name over and over — *Elenna, Elenna* — with the contentment of someone remembering, though his face said he no longer knew who Elenna had been or whether she was living or dead.

Fog of forgetting. Catalogued. Jonah had catalogued it themselves, two years ago, a vial of it brought down from the northern fens — entry 2,908, *condensed forgetting, fen-variety, handle with gloves*. It was supposed to exist in vials. In fens. Behind wards.

It was supposed to be *excluded*.

A child was standing in it.

Small, maybe six years old, nightshirt pale in the gloom, standing ankle-deep in the rolling gray with the particular stillness of someone who has forgotten they were running. As Jonah watched, the fog curled up around the child's knees, and the child's face began to change — the quick animal alertness draining out of it, replaced by a soft, vacant calm that Jonah recognized with a lurch of horror from the fishwife's face.

Jonah was moving before thought caught up.

They hit the edge of the fog and it closed over them like cold water, and the world went strange. Sounds arrived late. The screaming from the market square seemed to come from years ago, from someone else's memory. And then the fog began doing what fog of forgetting does: it reached into Jonah and started taking.

Their mother's face — the absence where a mother's face should have been, the absence they had carried so long it had become a kind of furniture — shifted. For one vertiginous second Jonah felt the absence *filling*, warm and specific, a woman laughing in a kitchen, and understood with sick clarity that the fog wasn't erasing that void; it was offering to fill it with something false, something borrowed, something that would feel more real than grief—

*Guild techniques,* they told themselves, and clung to the training like a handrail in a landslide.

*Name yourself. Name your anchors. Speak them aloud.*

"My name is Jonah Crane," they said, and their voice worked, barely. "Keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things. Third chair, inventory and acquisition. My anchors: the ledger room smells of cedar and iron-gall ink. Vess Corvane takes her tea bitter and pretends otherwise. Entry 4,117 is a borrowed summer and it is still wet on the page. I am walking toward a child on Cobble Lane. I am walking toward a child on Cobble Lane. *I am walking toward a child—*"

The fog pulled harder. It found the loneliness Jonah kept folded small at the bottom of themselves, the one shaped exactly like a mother they'd never known, and it pressed on it gently, the way you'd press a bruise to see if it still hurt. *I can give you her,* the fog seemed to say, in no words at all. *I have so many mothers. Take one.*

"No," Jonah said, aloud, furious now, and fury was an anchor too. "She isn't yours to lend. Nothing is yours to lend. That is the entire *point* of the covenant—"

The word came out of them unbidden, and the moment it did, the fog flinched.

Jonah felt it — a ripple through the gray, a hesitation, the way a dog flinches from a name it has been beaten with. *Covenant.* The fog knew the word. The fog feared it, or remembered it, or had been *bound* by it once, long ago, and some thread of that binding still ran through the syllables.

They reached the child.

Up close the little girl's eyes were half-lidded, dreamy, her lips curved in the beginnings of that terrible contented smile. Jonah knelt, dragged off their own coat, and wrapped it around her — wool, dense, physical, an anchor you could hold — and took her small face in their hands.

"Look at me. Only me. My name is Jonah Crane and I am going to tell you true things until you can hear them. Your name—" they didn't know it, didn't know anything about her except the shape of her fear before the fog drank it, "—your name is yours. No one has traded it. No one has spent it. You live in Lowmark, on a street with cobblestones, and tonight the stars went out, which is frightening, but it is *outside* you, and you are *inside* you, and inside you is the one place the fog cannot follow if you hold the door."

The child's brow furrowed. Her eyes focused — a fraction, then more.

"Hold the door," Jonah said. "Say it. Hold the door."

"...Hold the door," she whispered.

"Now stand up. Hold my voice like a rope. We are going to walk out of here counting cobblestones, and you will count them louder than the fog, and when we reach the corner where the lamps are, I will find your mother, whose name is also hers, and also not traded, and also hers."

The girl stood. She counted. Her voice was thin and cracked and got stronger with every stone — *four, five, six* — and Jonah matched her step for step, speaking their own anchors over and under her counting like a second melody, and the fog swirled around them, hungry and hesitant, pressing at the edges of them both but unable, somehow, to close. Whether it was the guild techniques or the word *covenant* still hanging in the air between them, Jonah didn't know and didn't stop to learn.

At the corner of Cobble Lane, beneath a lamp that still burned, a woman was tearing through the crowd calling a name — *Perrin, Perrin* — and the child broke from Jonah's side with a cry, and the woman caught her up, and Jonah stood in the lamplight shaking, checking their own hands as though to confirm they still belonged to them.

They did. Mostly. There was a new warmth somewhere behind their ribs, a kitchen, a laugh, a woman's face they had never seen and must never trust — the fog's parting gift, lodged in them like a splinter. Jonah filed the feeling away with the meticulousness that was their whole character: *entry pending, contamination suspected, do not rely upon.*

Then they looked up at the starless sky, and the purpose crystallized.

* * *

Sefa found them there, twenty minutes later, leading a chain of forty dazed citizens toward the guildhall by lamplight and rope. She took one look at Jonah's face and thrust a flask of something medicinal into their hands.

"You saved the Ashdown girl," she said. "Half the street saw it. Fog walked right up to you and backed off like you'd shown it papers."

"I showed it a word." Jonah drank. The medicine burned. "Sefa. Listen to me. The fog of forgetting is catalogued. Entry 2,908, fen-variety, contained behind the northern wards. The humming from the Grayfen — I've read it, it's in the deep archive, third binding, *the excluded chorus*, sealed behind the border wards four hundred years ago. Everything coming out of the dark tonight is something we have an entry for. Every single thing. Which means—"

"Which means the wards weren't keeping out chaos," Sefa said slowly. Her face had gone the color of tallow. "They were keeping out an *inventory*."

"They were holding a ledger closed." Jonah looked up again at the blank sky, and the terror in them turned over, quietly, and became something harder and more useful. "And someone opened it. Wards don't fail, Sefa. They're paid for. Fed. Maintained. Ours were maintained by the Guild — by us — on a schedule I have personally filed the requisitions for. Someone stopped paying. Or someone *sold* the payment to something else."

Above Lowmark, in the absolute black where the stars had been, something moved — a slow, vast displacement, like a shape turning over in sleep, or like a tower beginning, very far away, to consider growing.

And in Jonah's satchel, against their hip, the guild ledger they'd grabbed on the way out fell open to the page they'd been reading when the silk tore: entry 4,118, entered in Vess Corvane's own precise hand, dated that very morning, its description column left deliberately, unforgivably blank.

*Item: the covenant. Status: transferred. Terms: see guildmaster.*

Jonah read it again by lamplight while the city wept around them, and began, very quietly, to be afraid of the woman who had given them a place to belong.
