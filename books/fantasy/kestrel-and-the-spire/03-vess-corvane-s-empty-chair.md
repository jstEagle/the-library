## 3. Vess Corvane's Empty Chair

Jonah Crane had a theory about doors.

Twenty-two years in the Guild of Impossible Things taught them that every door was a promise, and the guildhall's front door — black oak older than Lowmark itself, carved with the sigil of the open hand — promised that whatever waited inside was catalogued, understood, and therefore survivable. Jonah had walked through it ten thousand times. They knew the exact weight of its hinges, the way it sighed shut behind you like a keeper closing a ledger at day's end.

This morning it stood ajar by three inches, and through the gap came a smell that did not belong: scorched vellum and something sweeter underneath, like flowers left too long in a sealed room.

Jonah pushed the door open with two fingers, the way one touches a page suspected of biting.

The entry hall was wrong before they could name how. The coat pegs were bare — Vess's grey traveling cloak gone, Sefa's oilskin gone, even the apprentice hooks stripped of their threadbare scarves. The mosaic floor, which depicted the known realms arranged around an unmapped center, had been pried at; three tiles near the middle were missing, leaving raw mortar like wounds. And the air itself felt thin, the way air feels after a fire has eaten all the good out of it.

"Guildmaster?" Jonah's voice came out smaller than intended. "Vess?"

Nothing answered except the building settling, and buildings only settle when something has disturbed their bones.

Jonah moved through the hall the way they moved through inventory: methodically, hands clasped behind their back to keep from touching anything, eyes doing the work instead. The long reading tables were overturned in a pattern that looked less like struggle than like searching — someone had flipped each table, run hands along the undersides, moved on. The notice board where keepers pinned trade requests had been swept clean, every scrap of paper taken or torn, a single brass pin left gleaming in the cork like an accusation.

The archive door hung off one hinge.

That was the moment Jonah stopped being able to pretend. The archive was the guild's heart, the room where the ledgers lived — not just the Ledger of Small Impossibilities but the deep volumes, the ones bound in hide that had never belonged to any animal, the ones that whispered if you pressed your ear to the spine. Jonah had spent eleven years learning those shelves the way other people learned scripture. Every book had its place. Every place had its logic. The logic was Vess Corvane's, and Vess guarded it the way a dragon guards not gold but the memory of gold.

Now the shelves gaped. Ledgers lay on the floor with their spines split, pages fanned like birds shot mid-flight. Bottled echoes had been swept from their niches — Jonah found one under the reading table, stoppered glass still warm, the echo inside pressing against the curve of the bottle like a moth. A stolen river, kept in a long copper trough since before Jonah's apprenticeship, had been drained; the trough held nothing now but a residue of silt and one small, confused fish that had no water to remember being born in.

And on Vess's desk — Vess's desk, oak as old as the door, scarred with the knife-marks of a hundred years of keepers testing their nibs — sat a single ledger, closed, squared precisely to the desk's edge, with a note weighted on top by the guildmaster's seal.

The seal was cracked.

Jonah had seen that seal pressed into wax more times than they could count: the open hand, the unmapped center, the motto in Old Guildscript — *We Keep What Cannot Be Kept.* The wax this morning was split clean down the middle, half the hand, half the blankness, as though whoever broke it had wanted to be certain the message could never be mistaken for official business.

They picked up the note. Vess's handwriting, unmistakable — the sharp forward slant, the letters that leaned into the future like a woman walking into wind:

*Follow the ink.*

Four words. No signature. No apology. Jonah read it six times, and each time the words rearranged themselves slightly, the ink settling like sediment, as though the sentence itself were trying to remember what it meant.

Then they opened the ledger.

It was one of the deep volumes — *Continuities*, Volume Nine, the register of things the Guild held in trust for the world rather than for any buyer. The covenant entries lived here. Jonah knew because they had catalogued the volume a hundred times without ever once being permitted to read past the index. The index was intact. The pages after it were not: torn out, cleanly, close to the spine, perhaps forty leaves in all, and the stubs that remained were browned at the edges as though the removal had happened recently, urgently, with heat involved.

Forty pages. Forty entries. Gone.

Jonah stood in the ruined archive holding a broken seal and a gutted book, and felt the first cold thread of a thought they would spend the rest of the day refusing to finish: *she took the covenant with her.*

"No," they said aloud, to the empty room, because some thoughts must be contradicted out loud or they take root. "No. She wouldn't."

The fish in the dry trough flopped once, hopelessly. Jonah tipped it into the rain barrel by the window, where it hung in the green water looking at them with an expression of profound grievance, and went to find Sefa Idris.

* * *

Sefa arrived an hour later, and she arrived the way she did everything: fast, loud, and slightly ahead of schedule.

She came through the front door — which Jonah had finally managed to close, mostly to hear it sigh — with her boots still muddy from the night's chaos in the streets, her dark hair escaping its braid in every direction, and a kitchen knife tucked into her belt, which was very Sefa. She was the guild's other senior keeper, and where Jonah catalogued, Sefa acquired. Where Jonah filed, Sefa fought. They had worked together nine years and agreed fully on perhaps four occasions, all of them involving wine.

"You're alive." She said it like an accusation, crossing the hall in six strides and gripping Jonah's shoulders hard enough to leave marks. "I went to your rooms. Your rooms are empty. Do you know what the streets look like? There are people standing in the fountain square who can't remember their own children's names. There's a man selling pieces of yesterday for bread. *Bread,* Jonah. And then I come here and the door's open and—"

She stopped. She had seen the archive over Jonah's shoulder, and Sefa Idris had never in her life been slow on the uptake.

"Oh," she said. Quietly, for her. Then louder, walking past Jonah into the wreckage: "Oh, *no.*"

She moved through the damage faster than Jonah had, reading it differently. Jonah saw loss; Sefa saw evidence. She crouched at the pried tiles, ran her thumb along the mortar. She lifted the split seal from the desk and turned it in the light. She pulled down three of the split-spined ledgers and riffled them like a cardsharp, and Jonah watched her face do something complicated.

"The deep volumes," Sefa said. "*Continuities* Nine is missing its covenant section. What else?"

"*Continuities* Seven and Eleven. The breach registers. Everything referencing the border wards." Jonah heard their own voice go flat and professional, which was how they kept it steady. "Whoever searched the hall knew exactly what to take."

"Not whoever." Sefa set the ledger down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something one wants to throw. "Look at the tables. Flipped and checked underneath, right? Someone looking for a hiding place only a keeper would use. Look at the tiles — third panel, the loose one we've all tripped over for a decade. Pried up, put back badly. Vess always put it back badly; she said a perfect floor made keepers lazy." She turned, and her eyes were bright and hard and terrible. "This wasn't a ransacking, Jonah. This was a *packing.*"

"Don't."

"She's gone, Jonah. Her cloak's gone. The wards fell last night — the same wards she personally renewed every solstice, alone, in the cellar, and never let anyone watch. The stars went out. And this morning our guildmaster is missing along with every record of the covenant, and she left us—" Sefa snatched up the note and read it aloud with savage mimicry, "'Follow the ink.' Follow the ink! That's not a message, that's a *taunt.*"

"That's Vess." Jonah pulled the note back. Their hands were not entirely steady, and they hated that. "You've known her nineteen years. Have you ever known her to explain herself? She sealed a haunted well in Kettering with a note that said 'back Thursday' and came back a month later with a bottled thunderstorm and no explanation. She doesn't taunt. She *directs.* If she wrote 'follow the ink,' then following the ink is what we're meant to do."

"Or," Sefa said, and here her voice dropped into something worse than anger, something careful, "it's what she wants us to do while she's already sold what was in the book."

The word landed between them like a dropped blade.

"Selling the covenant isn't possible," Jonah said. "The covenant isn't a thing you sell. It's not in the trade registers. It's not—"

"It's worth everything, Jonah." Sefa began pacing, boots ringing on the wounded mosaic, gesturing with the kitchen knife in a way that would have alarmed anyone who didn't know her. "Think about what it actually is. It holds the borders. It keeps out everything the wards used to keep out. Last night the wards failed and the stars went out *on the same hour,* and you're telling me that's coincidence? Something bought its way through. Something paid. And the only person in this city with the authority to open that particular till—" She stopped pacing. She pointed the knife at the torn stubs of pages in the open ledger. "She tore them out herself. Look at the burn marks. That's candle-wax spatter, that's a working done in a hurry at a desk, that's *her* desk."

"You don't know that."

"I know her handwriting, and I know her habits, and I know—" Sefa caught herself. Closed her mouth. Opened it again, slower. "I know that last month she asked me for the Idris family records. My family's memory-titles. Said it was routine audit." Her jaw tightened. "Routine audit, Jonah. Three weeks before the sky goes dark."

The cold thread in Jonah's chest finished its thought despite them. *She was gathering. She was taking stock of what she could spend.*

"She audits everyone," Jonah said. "She audited me twice last year. It's what she does. It's what a guildmaster does."

"A guildmaster audits the *guild.* Not my dead grandmother's name-deeds."

They stood there in the wrecked archive, two keepers of impossible things, unable to keep hold of the simplest one: the truth about a woman they had both served, in different ways, for most of their adult lives. Outside, Lowmark's bells were ringing wrong — arrhythmic, uncertain, as though even the bell-ringers had lost count of the hours overnight. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, steadily, endlessly, at nothing.

"We should search the workrooms," Jonah said at last. "All of them. Systematically. If she left 'follow the ink,' there's an ink trail, and I'm the one who reads her filing."

"And if the trail leads somewhere you don't want it to lead?" Sefa asked. But she was already rolling up her sleeves, already moving toward the cellar stairs, because whatever else Sefa Idris was — reckless, tactless, armed with cutlery — she was a keeper, and keepers follow the record.

They searched until the light through the high windows went from grey to gold to grey again. They found the cellar wards unbroken but *unfed* — the great stone basin where the covenant's power had pooled for centuries held only a skin of residue, like the film on milk. They found the map-room untouched, which somehow made it worse, as though the maps hadn't mattered enough to steal. They found, behind the false shelf in Vess's private study, a strongbox containing nothing but a child's wooden bird and a lock of hair wrapped in oilcloth, and Jonah recognized neither, and Sefa pretended not to watch them hold the hair, and neither of them spoke about it.

It was nearly dusk when Sefa found the ledger.

Not the gutted one — a different volume, small, pocket-sized, bound in grey leather Jonah didn't recognize. It was wedged inside the chimney flue of the private study, wrapped in waxcloth, placed deliberately, findable-by-someone-who-knew-where-to-look. Which meant, Jonah thought with a lurch, findable by *them,* because Vess had trained their eye herself.

Inside were pages of Vess's handwriting, dated across the last year. Transactions. Jonah read three lines and felt the floor tilt.

*Received: one covenant-thread (minor), from the Spire, in exchange for continued ward maintenance. Considered: acceptable risk. The alternative is letting the borders fail now rather than later.*

*Received: second thread. The Spire asks increasingly specific questions about the keystone lineage. Answered none. Note: it knows the Cranes are involved. It has always known.*

*Note to self: tell Jonah nothing. Grief makes cartographers imprecise, and I need this one precise.*

Jonah read that line four times. *Tell Jonah nothing.* Not cruelty — the opposite, maybe, or something wearing mercy's coat. Vess had known. Had known about the Cranes, about the keystone lineage, about whatever Jonah was, and had chosen silence as a kind of armor for them.

Or — said the cold thread, the Sefa-colored thread — it was exactly what a seller writes to quiet her conscience while she dismantles the lock she was hired to keep.

"There's more," Sefa said softly. She was watching Jonah's face. "Last page."

Jonah turned to it. One line, dated three days ago:

*I have bought us time. Not much. What comes next cannot be done from inside the guildhall, and cannot be undone by anyone who stays. If you are reading this, the choice is made. Forgive me or don't. Follow the ink.*

The study was very quiet. Downstairs, faintly, the dry copper trough ticked as it cooled, and the riverless fish had gone still in its barrel, and outside the stars were beginning to come out — most of them, the familiar constellations with their familiar gaps, the sky wearing its wound openly now.

"So," Sefa said. She sat down on the edge of Vess's desk, knife across her knees, and looked at Jonah with an expression stripped of all her usual armor. "Your guildmaster traded with the thing that ate the sky. Maybe to buy time. Maybe to buy herself a staircase. Both readings fit the evidence, and I've got a knife and a bad attitude and no way to tell which is true." She tapped the little grey ledger. "But she wrote *follow the ink* twice, and I know you, Crane. You're going to follow it whether I come or not. So I'm coming. Question is whether we follow it as believers or as prosecutors."

Jonah closed the grey ledger and held it against their chest, and realized their arms had made the decision their mind kept refusing: they were carrying it like something rescued, not something seized.

"Believers," they said. "For now. Because if she's guilty, I want to hear her say why. And if she's not—" They looked at the torn stubs in the great ledger, at the forty absences where the covenant used to live. "If she's not, then someone else tore those pages, and followed her out of this hall, and I want to know who's clever enough to frame Vess Corvane."

Sefa snorted. "Nobody's that clever."

"Somebody put out the stars."

That silenced her. She stood, sheathed the ridiculous kitchen knife, and jerked her chin toward the door. "Ink trails fade. If we're going, we go tonight."

They were almost at the stairs when Jonah stopped — stopped so suddenly Sefa walked two paces past before noticing — because the last of the daylight had shifted through the high windows and fallen across the archive floor, and the spilled ink from a shattered inkwell had dried in a long, deliberate line across the flagstones. Not a spill. A stroke. Drawn, Jonah was almost certain, by a finger dipped in living ink, drawn in the old guild shorthand every keeper learns first:

*A head-start. Come quickly.*

And beneath it, smaller, in handwriting that shook in a way Vess Corvane's handwriting had never shaken in nineteen years:

*It remembers me, Jonah. It knows my name. God help me, I think it knows yours too.*
