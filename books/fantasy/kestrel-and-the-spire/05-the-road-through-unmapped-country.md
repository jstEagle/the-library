## 5. The Road Through Unmapped Country

The road out of Lowmark had been, according to every map in the Guild's collection, a straight line.

Jonah knew this the way they knew their own heartbeat: three days' walk to the ferry at Coldharbour, then the long climb through the Ashen Fells where the old surveyors had planted their brass markers like teeth. They had walked it twice before, once as an apprentice carrying Vess Corvane's sealed correspondence and once alone, fleeing a bad winter and a worse argument. Both times, the road had behaved. Roads were supposed to behave. That was the entire covenant between a traveler and a map — you gave the map your trust, and the road gave you its length.

By noon of the first day, the road had broken that covenant.

It began subtly. A milestone Jonah remembered — *Coldharbour, 14 leagues* — appeared twice within an hour of walking, the same chipped numeral, the same bird dropping dried white down its face. Then the birches started repeating: not similar trees, but the *same* tree, with the same lightning scar and the same knot shaped like a closed eye, standing at intervals as though the forest had been printed from a woodcut and the carver had run short of imagination.

"Stop counting," Sefa said, without turning around. She walked ahead the way she did everything, as if the world owed her right of way — boots unlaced at the top, guild cloak worn inside out so the sigil wouldn't show, hair hacked short with what looked like a bread knife. "Counting makes it worse."

"Counting is how I know it's worse," Jonah said. They held their ledger against their chest like a breastplate. "That milestone has passed us four times. Either we're walking in a circle, or the circle is walking past us."

"Both," Sefa said. "Welcome to unmapped country. The land's started forgetting itself, and when land forgets, it repeats. Like an old man telling the same story because he can't remember he already told it." She spat to the side of the road, an act of contempt she performed with real artistry. "Keep your eyes on something that changes. Clouds. Your own feet. If you watch the repeats too long, they start watching back."

"That's not cartographic principle."

"No," Sefa agreed cheerfully. "It's survival."

Jonah wrote anyway — of course they wrote anyway, because a keeper who stopped writing was just a person lost in the woods — recording the repetitions in the margin of their field ledger with the small notation for *unstable terrain, do not trust bearings*. The ink sat strangely on the page, drying slow, as if even the paper wasn't sure this was happening.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a duplicated barn.

It stood in a field of wheat that grew in perfect concentric rings, each stalk identical to its neighbor, and there were two doors facing opposite directions, and both doors were open, and neither led anywhere Jonah was willing to go. Sefa built a fire with the efficiency of someone who had slept rough more nights than she'd slept soft, and Jonah sat with their back against the barn wall and tried to triangulate their position by the stars.

There were no stars. Not gone — the whole sky hadn't emptied, only here, only above this stretch of country, where the heavens had been replaced by a smooth grey nothing like the inside of a closed lid.

"The Spire eats outward," Sefa said, following their gaze. She was cleaning her nails with a knife, which she managed to make look threatening rather than fastidious. "Memory first, then light, then shape. We're in its appetite now. By tomorrow we'll be in its throat."

"You've been here before."

It wasn't a question, and Sefa didn't treat it as one. She turned the knife over in her fingers. "Once. Years ago. I came looking for someone."

"A trader? A debtor?"

"My brother."

The word landed in the fire and burned there. Jonah kept still. In the Guild they had a rule about grief: *let it finish speaking before you answer*. It was one of the few rules Sefa Idris had never mocked.

"Tomas," she said. "Tomas Idris. He was a singer — not the paid kind, the kind who sings because silence makes him itch. He heard about the Spire before anyone else did, back when it was a rumor traders told to frighten each other. Said a tower grown out of memory must have all the songs ever sung in it, and he wanted to hear them." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "He was twenty-two. I told him it was a fool's errand. I told him if he went, I wouldn't come after him, because I don't chase fools."

"But you came."

"I came." She fed a stick to the fire. "Three years late. I found the place where the tower stands — you can't miss it, it leans on the horizon like a debt — and I walked the base for six days calling his name. And on the seventh day something came out of the fog to meet me. Tall. Wearing clothes that used to be Tomas's. With a face like a candle burnt down to the wick." She looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were flat and bright as coins. "It didn't know me. But when I said his name, it hummed. Four bars of a song our mother used to sing. Then it walked back into the fog, and I let it go, because chasing it felt like digging up a grave to argue with the body."

Jonah found their voice had gone thin. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." Sefa pointed the knife at the ledger. "You're the one who reads the old covenants. You tell me — when the Guild catalogued the Spire, when Vess filed her reports, did anyone write down what happens to the people it takes? Are they dead? Are they stored? Is my brother humming somewhere inside that thing with everything else it swallowed?"

Jonah thought of the entry they'd found weeks ago — *the covenant, see restricted index* — and of Vess's face whenever they'd asked, that careful blankness like a door painted onto a wall. "I don't know," they said honestly. "But the ledgers will. Everything ends up in the ledgers eventually. That's the whole point of them."

"Then that's the deal, Crane." Sefa lay back, arms behind her head, staring at the starless lid of sky. "You get us to the tower using your maps and your rules, and I get my brother's name back — even if all that's left of him is a line in some archive. And if it turns out your precious guildmaster sold him along with everything else, I'll burn the guildhall down myself, with or without you in it."

"Fair terms," Jonah said, and meant it, and hated how much they meant it.

* * *

The second day, the villages began duplicating.

The first one was called Brindle, according to its signpost, and it was a pleasant-enough sprawl of slate roofs and drying laundry, where a woman selling apples told them the ferry at Coldharbour had been closed since the wards fell. The second Brindle appeared two hours later, identical down to the woman, the apples, and the exact crease in her apron, and when Jonah asked her the same question she gave the same answer in the same cadence, like a music box rewound.

"They're echoes," Jonah murmured, once they were clear. "Memory-fog given architecture. The land remembers these places and can't stop replaying them."

"Or the places are real and we're the echo," Sefa said. "Ever consider that?"

"Constantly."

The third Brindle was wrong. It stood at a crooked angle to the road, its roofs sagging like a face mid-collapse, and the people in it moved in loops — the apple woman walking ten steps, turning, walking ten steps again, her smile fixed, her basket always holding exactly seven apples no matter how many she handed out. When Jonah took one, out of a horrible curiosity, it weighed nothing. It tasted of nothing. It tasted, specifically, of *the idea of an apple*, which is a taste that leaves you hungrier than before.

Sefa watched them eat it and laughed until she had to hold a fencepost. "You look like a man reading a letter from a lover who's forgotten him."

"It's a perfectly serviceable apple."

"It's a *lie* with a skin on." She wiped her eyes. "Gods, you really are a keeper. Offered a miracle and you critique the portion size."

They argued about the apple for most of the afternoon, and it was, Jonah would later reflect, the first honest conversation they'd had. Sefa maintained that anything repeated by a forgetting land was worthless; Jonah maintained that repetition was itself information — that a map of where the world wore thin was worth more than a map of where it was solid, because thin places were where things got in. Sefa called this "the most depressing sentence ever spoken by a living person." Jonah wrote it down in the margin, underlined twice.

By dusk they reached the river.

Except there was no river. There was the *bed* of a river, wide and stony and dry as an old promise, with rowboats sitting on the stones like beached animals and a ferry landing whose sign read COLDHARBOUR CROSSING in letters gone pale as bone. Jonah stood at the edge of the empty channel and felt something in their chest go quietly, catastrophically wrong.

"This is the crossing," they said. "This is definitely the crossing. I've drawn this landing. I've *archived* this landing."

"And?" Sefa said.

"And rivers don't forget how to be rivers. Water isn't memory. Water doesn't—"

But even as they said it, they understood, and the understanding arrived cold, the way all the true ones did. The stolen river. Entry forty-one in the Ledger of Small Impossibilities, bottled and shelved in the Guild's vaults, traded away decades ago by someone who needed coin more than a landscape needed water. The land below Lowmark had been forgetting in patches for years — everyone had assumed it was natural erosion, the ordinary decay of things. But the patches had edges. And the edges all pointed toward the Spire.

"It's been drinking for a long time," Jonah said aloud. "Not just since the wards fell. The wards weren't keeping it out. They were keeping it *fed*. Rationing it."

Sefa had gone very still beside them. "Then whoever fell the wards—"

"—didn't break the fence," Jonah finished. "They opened the larder."

They stood together on the dry stones while the wind moved dust in patterns that almost looked like current. Somewhere downstream — or upstream, or nowhere — a bell rang in a village that might not exist anymore, and the sound arrived late and slightly wrong, like a voice through a wall.

"We camp upstream," Sefa said finally. "Water or no water, the high ground will show us the fells tomorrow. And Crane—" she paused, and for once the mockery drained out of her face, leaving something older and wearier underneath, "—when we get there. When we see it. Whatever's left of my brother, whatever it's wearing him as — you'll help me find him. Swear it on your ledgers. You people put stock in that, don't you? Swearing on books."

Jonah looked at the empty riverbed, at the boat full of nothing, at the woman in the third Brindle still handing out apples that tasted of the idea of apples. Then they unslung their field ledger, held it flat on both palms the way keepers swore oaths at induction, and said:

"On the ledgers, then. Every name the Spire holds, we bring home or we write down why we couldn't. Keeper's word."

Sefa studied them for a long moment, as if checking the oath for fine print. Then she nodded once, sharp, and turned to climb the bank. "Keeper's word," she echoed. "First sensible thing your guild ever taught you."

* * *

They gained the ridge at dawn on the third day, and the Ashen Fells opened below them like a hand uncurling, and there it was.

The Spire stood on the far side of the fells where no tower should have been able to stand — too tall, too narrow, its proportions arguing with the eye. It rose out of a plain of grey mist like a needle pushed through fabric, and its surface was not stone. Even at this distance Jonah could see it *moving*, slowly, the way a sleeping face moves: walls flickering with images too far away to resolve, light sliding across it in pulses like breath. It looked less built than *condensed* — as if someone had taken a century of remembering and pressed it until it had no choice but to stand up.

Beside them, Sefa had stopped breathing.

"There," she whispered, and her arm came up, finger shaking, pointing not at the tower but at the mist around its base — where figures were moving. Small at this distance, patient, methodical, pacing the perimeter in patterns too regular to be alive. "That's where I saw it. That's where *he*—" Her voice cracked and reformed. "Crane. Your maps. Tell me you have a way in."

Jonah opened the field ledger to a fresh page. Their hands were steady, which surprised them; some part of them had expected to shake forever after seeing it. They began to draw — the tower's bearing, the mist's edge, the pacing figures, the intervals between them — and as the pen moved, the living ink seemed to wake, the lines drinking in what they recorded, the sketch trembling faintly like something newly born.

"Yes," Jonah said. "I think I do."

And far below, at the base of the Spire, one of the pacing figures stopped — and turned, and stood, and looked directly up at the ridge where they stood, across miles of forgetting, as if it had been waiting for them all along.
