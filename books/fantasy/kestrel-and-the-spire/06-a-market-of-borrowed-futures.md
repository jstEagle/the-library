## 6. A Market of Borrowed Futures

Sefa Idris had been to markets where they sold grief by the ounce, and markets where you could buy the last hour of a dying man's life, decanted and stoppered, still warm. She had once, in a younger and stupider year, haggled over a vial of someone else's first love — it had tasted of rain on hot stone and had ruined her for three months. But she had never seen anything like the Gullet.

That was what the smugglers called it: the Gullet, because everything went down and nothing came back up. It lived in the throat of an old quarry two days' walk south of Lowmark's failing wards, a wound in the earth where the land had already begun forgetting its own shape. The quarry walls were terraced with stalls — canvas and bone-lattice and hanging lanterns that burned without flame, lit instead by bottled hours someone had traded away and never reclaimed. The light was strange. It fell like late afternoon no matter the actual hour, because most of the hours burning up there belonged to other people's golden evenings.

Sefa walked with her hood up and her hand near the knife at her hip, which was not a threat but a habit, the way some people touched prayer-beads.

"Stay close," she murmured to Jonah. "And whatever they offer you, don't smell it."

"Why not?"

"Because smelling is half the sale. They compound the futures with scent. Fresh bread means domesticity. Rain means second chances. My brother—" She stopped. Swallowed. "There was a stall once that smelled like our mother's kitchen. I still don't know if that was real or compounded."

Jonah said nothing, but Sefa felt them cataloguing it — she could see the ledger working behind their eyes, that maddening guildkeeper habit of filing every grief under *inventory*. Three days on the road through unmapped country had taught her this about Jonah Crane: they coped by indexing. The world frightened them less when it was cross-referenced.

The Gullet swallowed them gently. Down the terraces, past a stall selling Tuesdays (genuine, certified unspent), past a woman whose face was a blur because she'd sold her reflection years ago and it hadn't grown back, past children too quick and too quiet, running errands for brokers who paid them in borrowed courage.

At the center of the quarry floor stood the Exchange itself: a long table of black wood under a canopy of stitched-together sails, and behind it, the broker.

He was called Master Quillon, though Sefa knew at least four names he answered to and suspected none of them were his. He was small and neat and gray as ash, with hands so soft they looked unused, and eyes that did the one thing no one else's did in the Gullet — they stayed focused. Everyone else here looked slightly past you, at the life you might be selling. Quillon looked *at* you, and it was worse.

"Sefa Idris." His voice was dry paper. "You've been away from my tables a long time."

"I've been away from your tables on purpose."

"And yet here you are." He spread his soft hands. "Everyone comes back eventually. What does the Guild of Impossible Things want today? Bottled echoes? A decade of someone's retirement? I have a lovely stretch of unlived fatherhood, very gently used—"

"Passage," Jonah said.

Sefa winced. Rule one of the Gullet: never name what you want before the broker names his price. But Jonah had never learned rules; Jonah had only learned ledgers, and ledgers were a different animal entirely.

Quillon's attention slid to them like oil finding a crack. "Passage to where?"

"The Spire."

The word dropped into the quarry and the market *flinched*. Sefa felt it happen around them — conversations thinning, a stall-keeper turning her back, a child pulled into a tent by the wrist. Even the flameless lanterns seemed to gutter, though there was nothing to gutter.

Quillon didn't flinch. That was the trouble with men who had sold their fear decades ago; it made them excellent brokers and terrible company.

"No one goes to the Spire," he said pleasantly.

"People do. The Blank go up and come down. Traders go partway. There's a road."

"There's a road the way there's a river of fire. Technically passable. Practically insane." He laced his fingers. "I could arrange it. Guides who owe me. Camels that have forgotten how to die. But passage to the Spire isn't bought with coin, keeper. Coin is for people who intend to keep living ordinary lives afterward. Passage to the Spire is bought with something the Spire might want back."

"I know what it costs," Jonah said.

"You know what things cost," Quillon corrected. "You don't know what *you* cost. Not yet." He tilted his head, and Sefa saw him doing it — the appraisal, the weighing, the terrible arithmetic of the Gullet, in which a person was reduced to inventory. "But you're interesting. You walk like someone keeping accounts. And you came in with Sefa Idris, which means either you trust her judgment or you have none, and your hands say ledger-keeper, and your eyes say—"

He stopped.

Sefa watched it happen. Watched the broker's focused gaze sharpen into something almost like hunger, watched him lean forward across the black wood as if Jonah had suddenly become legible.

"What's your name?" Quillon asked softly. "Your full name. Family name."

"Don't—" Sefa started.

"Crane," Jonah said, because of course they did, because guildkeepers signed everything, because honesty was a reflex they couldn't suppress any more than blinking. "Jonah Crane."

The market went quiet around them in rings, like ice cracking outward from a stone.

Quillon sat back very slowly. He looked at Jonah the way Sefa had once seen a man look at a door he'd believed painted onto a wall, right before it opened.

"A Crane," he said. "Here. Walking toward the Spire. With *her*." A flick of one soft finger at Sefa, who felt her stomach drop through the quarry floor. "Oh, this is a day. This is a day I have waited eleven years for."

"You know the name," Jonah said carefully.

"Everyone in the trade knows the name. Maren Crane is scripture in certain circles." Quillon smiled, and it was the worst smile Sefa had ever seen on him, because it wasn't greedy. It was almost gentle. "The cartographer who mapped the first breach. Who sealed it. Who died doing it — that's the story, isn't it? That's what the Guild tells its little keepers."

"That's what happened," Jonah said. Their voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone holding a page very still so it doesn't tear.

"Maren Crane didn't die." Quillon leaned in again, and now he lowered his voice, and the lowering was somehow louder than shouting. The nearest stalls had gone silent; the whole Gullet was leaning in without appearing to move. "Maren Crane was *taken*. There's a difference, keeper, and the difference has a price, and the Guild has been paying it for twenty-five years in silence."

* * *

They ended up in the back of Quillon's stall, behind a curtain of sailcloth, at a table set with tea that smelled of nothing at all — deliberately, Sefa realized. A neutral cup. A broker's courtesy, or a broker's trap: he wanted them thinking clearly.

She stood by the curtain with her arms crossed, between Jonah and the exit, though she couldn't have said which of them she was guarding.

"Twenty-five years ago," Quillon said, pouring, "a woman came through this market. Not the Gullet as it is now — we were smaller then, meaner, a ditch with awnings. She was looking for maps of the deep reaches. Living ink. She carried a child."

Sefa heard Jonah stop breathing.

"She couldn't have been more than — what, twenty-three? Twenty-four? She traded for passage the same way everyone does. With future. But hers was strange stock. She put a year of her own tomorrow on my predecessor's table, and the year *sang*. Do you understand? Futures don't sing. They sit there, gray and hypothetical. Hers rang like a struck bell. My predecessor took it and resold it three times and every buyer swore they'd lived the best month of their lives inside it." He set the pot down. "A future that sings belongs to someone who is going to matter. We all knew it. We let her go anyway, because the Spire's road pays better than sentiment."

"You're saying my mother stood in this quarry," Jonah said. Each word placed like a stone in a wall. "Traded a year of her life. Carrying me."

"Carrying you, leading you by the hand — I don't know which. I know she asked one question before she left, and the answer is why I remember her at all." Quillon folded his soft hands. "She asked: *if the Spire takes a memory from someone, does it die in them, or does it live in the tower?*"

"And?"

"And my predecessor told her the truth, because she'd paid for truth along with passage. It lives in the tower. Nothing the Spire takes is ever destroyed, keeper. It's *kept*. Filed. Preserved. The Spire doesn't eat memories the way fire eats wood." His eyes were steady and awful. "It eats them the way a collector eats art. Everything it has ever taken is still in there. Every stolen childhood. Every traded name. Every consumed man — your friend's brother among them, I'd wager, from the way she's standing."

Sefa's jaw tightened. She said nothing. Traitorous, kind, unbearable Quillon.

"So when I tell you Maren Crane was taken and not killed," Quillon went on, "I am telling you something with a shape to it. Taken things can be *found*. The Spire keeps. It hoards. Somewhere in that tower — in its walls, its floors, its archive-heart, whatever they call the place where it stores what it steals — there is a shelf, or a room, or a *stone*, with your mother in it. Alive the way a song is alive inside a locked music box. Lucid, maybe. Aware, maybe. Twenty-five years aware."

The silence after that was total. Somewhere above them a lantern-hour burned out and was replaced, and the light shifted like afternoon becoming a different afternoon.

Jonah's hands were flat on the table. Sefa watched them — watched the fine tremor in the fingers, watched the knuckles whiten and ease, whiten and ease, a tide of someone fighting not to feel a thing until they'd finished understanding it. Guildkeeper to the bone. Filing the earthquake.

"You said passage," Jonah said finally, and their voice was almost level. "Name the price."

* * *

Quillon named it in the manner of all good brokers: too casually, while doing something else, so the number would land before the meaning did.

"A year of your future," he said, examining his teacup. "Twelve months, taken from the end of your natural span, or from the middle — your choice, though I'll advise the middle; end-years are brittle stock. Unlived, uncertified, freely given. In exchange: guides to the Spire's road, safe conduct through the shardlands, and my personal word that no one in my network sells your names to the tower's recruiters." He glanced up. "Which is worth more than the guides, incidentally. The Blank don't find people. People get found *for* them."

"A year," Sefa repeated flatly. "Of their *life*."

"Of their *future*, which is not the same thing and is worth far less. No one has ever missed a year they never lived." Quillon waved a hand. "It's the gentlest currency in the building. I've taken decades from men who begged me to take more."

"It's not gentle," Sefa said. "Nothing here is gentle. You dress theft in accounting and call it mercy."

"And you would know, Idris." For just a moment the broker's focus turned on her, and she felt it like a blade laid flat against the skin — not cutting, but reminding. "You've sat where your friend is sitting. You know exactly what a year feels like coming out."

She held his gaze and did not blink, because blinking was conceding, and she had conceded enough in her life to last several lifetimes, most of them unlived.

Jonah spoke quietly. "How is it taken?"

"Gladly. Willingly. With witnesses." Quillon produced, from somewhere in his ash-gray robes, a small glass vessel no bigger than a thumb — empty, or seeming so. "You hold it. You think of the year you're giving. Not the contents — the *fact* of it. A year you will now never have. You speak it aloud: *I give this freely.* And it comes out. It's painless. That's the part people hate, afterward. That it doesn't hurt."

"And then it's gone forever," Jonah said.

"Then it's gone forever, and somewhere a stranger will live a month of it and call it luck."

Sefa should have stopped it. Later, walking the shardlands, sleeping in duplicated villages, she would turn that moment over and over — the exact weight of the teacup in her hand, the exact angle of the sailcloth light — and ask herself why she hadn't reached out and closed her fingers around Jonah's wrist and said *no, not this, find another way, there is always another way.*

She knew why, even then. She knew it the way you know a scar's history without looking.

Because she understood the arithmetic. Because she had done the same sum in the same market eleven years ago and gotten the same answer, and the answer had a name, and the name was Tam, and she had failed to save him anyway. And some part of her — the rotten, practical, survivor part — wanted to watch someone else make the trade and walk away from the table still standing, still themselves, still *possible*. Proof it could be done. Proof the Gullet didn't always win.

So she stood by the curtain and said nothing, and hated herself in the fluent, practiced way of the long-complicit, and watched Jonah Crane reach for the glass.

"One condition," Jonah said.

Quillon raised an eyebrow.

"When this is over — however it ends — I want the year back. On the ledger. Recorded as a debt owed to me by this market. If the Spire falls, if the covenant holds, someone repays it. That's not a request. That's a term."

For the first time since the quarry, Master Quillon looked genuinely surprised. Then — briefly, horribly — delighted.

"Ohh," he breathed. "Now I see the family resemblance. Yes. Done. Witnessed." He snapped his fingers and a boy appeared from behind the sailcloth, pad and stylus already moving; the Gullet ran on children and debts, and it kept both close. "One year of future, lent rather than sold, repayable upon the unmaking of the Spire. Entered. Sealed." He slid the glass across the table. "Whenever you're ready, keeper."

Jonah picked it up.

Sefa watched their face as they held it — watched them close their eyes, watched their lips move around words she couldn't hear, and she thought, absurdly, of her brother Tam at eight years old, holding a caught moth in cupped hands, deciding whether to keep it or let it go. The same careful tenderness. The same terrible gentleness toward small captive things.

"*I give this freely,*" Jonah said.

The glass filled.

Not with light, exactly. With weather — a faint shifting glow, gold going to green going to blue, like a window looking out on a year that would now never arrive. Sefa felt it from across the room, a pressure behind her teeth, a homesickness for a place that didn't exist. Whatever Jonah Crane had just surrendered, it had been beautiful. You could tell. Beautiful futures had a color, and this one hurt to look at.

Quillon stoppered it with reverence and set it among his wares, and Sefa noticed — filed it away, the way she accused Jonah of doing — that he placed it apart from the others. Not in the racks with the common stock. On a high shelf. Alone.

Brokers kept singing futures separate. She'd heard that once and never known what it meant until now.

"We leave at dusk," Quillon said briskly, all business again, the moment sealed and shelved with everything else. "Guides will meet you at the north stair. Don't tell them what you paid; guides get superstitious. And keeper—" He paused at the curtain, half-turned, and his voice dropped into that lower register again, the one that carried. "One more thing. About your mother. Free of charge, because I liked her, and because I have regretted letting her walk up that road for twenty-five years."

Jonah looked up. Their face was pale and composed and utterly wrecked underneath, the way a house looks after an earthquake that spared the facade.

"When you find her," Quillon said, "and you will find her, because Cranes finish their maps — don't expect the woman who left. Expect what the tower made of her. And expect her to know things about the covenant that the Guild would burn its own halls to keep quiet." His eyes flicked to Sefa, then back. "Ask her about the keystone. Ask her what the covenant actually *requires*. And then decide, quickly, whether you're the kind of Crane who pays the family price."

He swept out through the curtain, leaving the tea untouched and the lantern-light trembling.

* * *

They walked up out of the Gullet in the false golden afternoon, past the stall of certified Tuesdays, past the woman with the blurred face, past the quick quiet children. The quarry mouth opened onto ordinary dusk, real dusk, the sky doing its honest work, and Sefa breathed it in like a woman surfacing.

Jonah walked beside her in silence for a long time. The path wound up through gorse and broken walls, toward the ridge where the guides would wait, and beyond it, days away, the shardlands, and beyond those — the tower.

"You gave up a year," Sefa said at last. "What was in it?"

"Nothing. It was unlived. That's the point."

"That's a broker's answer. I asked for yours."

Jonah was quiet long enough that the gorse got loud with wind. When they finally spoke, they didn't look at her; they looked ahead, at the darkening line of the world.

"I tried to think of the fact of it, like he said. Just the fact. A blank year." A breath. "But it wouldn't stay blank. It kept... filling. A kitchen. Someone laughing in another room. Rain on a roof I apparently owned." Their voice stayed level, which was how Sefa knew it was costing them everything. "I gave that away. Freely. And now some stranger gets the kitchen."

Sefa said nothing for a while. The ridge rose before them, black against the last light.

"For what it's worth," she said finally, "the ones who fill in the blanks — the ones who can't help imagining what they're giving up — they're the only ones I ever trusted at that table. The rest are just spending money they haven't counted."

Jonah glanced at her. Something passed between them that wasn't quite forgiveness and wasn't quite alliance, but was load-bearing nonetheless.

Then Jonah stopped walking.

Sefa followed their gaze. On the ridge ahead, against the bruised sky, the guides were waiting — three shapes beside two patient, wrong-eyed camels. Ordinary enough. But below the ridge, off the road, half-hidden in the gorse, stood a fourth figure. Still. Patient. Wrong in a way Sefa's body understood before her mind did: a person-shaped absence, a silhouette with nothing behind the eyes.

A Blank. Far from the tower. Far from anywhere it should have been.

It wasn't moving. It wasn't watching the guides.

It was watching Jonah.

And as the last light died, Sefa saw its mouth open, and heard — thin across the wind, in a voice like a memory played through water — a single word.

"*Jonah.*"
