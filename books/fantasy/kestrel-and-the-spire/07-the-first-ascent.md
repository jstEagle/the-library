## 7. The First Ascent

They smelled it before they saw it.

Jonah had been told, in the way that travelers are told things by people who have never been where they're going, that the Spire could be seen from three days out — a black needle against the sky, taller than anything built by hands. What the travelers had not mentioned was the smell: something like rain on hot stone, something like a book left open in a damp room for a hundred years, and beneath both of those, faint as a whisper through a wall, something sweet. The sweetness was the worst part. It smelled like being six years old and waking to bread baking, like the inside of a coat pocket that still held last winter's chestnuts, like every small good thing Jonah had ever failed to notice at the time.

"That's it," Sefa said, reining her mule to a stop on the switchback road. "That's what it smells like. I'd forgotten." Her face did something complicated and then went carefully blank, which Jonah was learning was how she handled most feelings too large to carry openly.

"Forgotten what?"

"What remembering smells like." She nudged the mule forward. "Come on. If we can smell it from here, we're close enough that it might be able to smell us."

They crested the ridge an hour past noon, and there it was.

Jonah had drawn towers all their life. Towers of glass, towers of bone, a tower in the far north said to be grown rather than built, a tower in the southern archipelago that appeared only on maps drawn during eclipses. They had thought themselves beyond surprise. They had been wrong, and they knew it immediately, completely, the way one knows one has stepped off a stair that isn't there.

The Spire rose out of a plain of grey grass like a wound made vertical. It was not black, exactly — it was *dense*, the way ink is dense, the way a memory you've turned over ten thousand times becomes denser than the day it happened. Its walls were not stone. Jonah's cartographer's eye catalogued the surface even while the rest of them recoiled: striated, layered, compressed, like sediment laid down by centuries instead of seasons. Like paper pressed under impossible weight until paper became something else. And the whole vast face of it — a thousand feet of it visible from the ridge, more climbing into cloud above — *moved*. Not structurally. Not dangerously. But slowly, the way a sleeping person moves, the way light moves across a page when you tilt it.

And it flickered.

That was the thing no traveler had managed to convey. The walls flickered with images, faint and shifting, like reflections in water that hadn't decided what to reflect. Jonah raised their spyglass with hands that were not quite steady and watched a window-shaped patch of the tower's flank resolve into a summer kitchen: copper pots, a woman humming, flour dust hanging in a bar of sunlight. The image held for three heartbeats, then sank back into the grey-brown density of the wall like a stone dropped into deep water. A few feet away, another patch brightened: two children running through a wheat field, laughing at something Jonah couldn't hear, though they found their own mouth had curved toward a smile without permission. That image lasted longer. Then it, too, dimmed, and the wall swallowed it, and somewhere inside the Spire someone's childhood became load-bearing.

"It eats them," Jonah said. Their voice came out flat, which surprised them; they had expected it to shake. "The memories. It doesn't just take them. It *builds* with them."

"Now you understand what I've been trying to tell you since Lowmark," Sefa said quietly. She had not looked through the spyglass. Jonah suspected she didn't need to. "Everyone thinks the Spire is a prison. It's worse. It's a *larder*."

* * *

They made camp that night in a fold of the hills, downwind, with no fire because Sefa insisted and Jonah agreed once they'd seen why. From the ridge in the failing light, they could see the plain around the Spire's base, and the plain was not empty.

"They patrol," Jonah murmured, lying prone at the ridgeline with the spyglass, sketching by feel on a board strapped to their forearm — an old guild trick, drawing blind, letting the hand remember what the eyes recorded. "Regular intervals. Four circuits. No lamps."

"No lamps," Sefa repeated. "Because they don't need them. Look at how they walk."

Jonah looked. The figures moved along the Spire's base in loose files, and their walking was wrong in a way that took several seconds to name: too smooth. No hesitation, no adjustment of footing over the broken ground, no turning of heads. They walked as if the ground were already known to them, as if the entire world were a room they'd memorized long ago and would never need to look at again. They walked, Jonah realized with a slow cold settling in the stomach, the way a person walks through a house in the dark — except these were outside, under open sky, and they still didn't look up.

"The Blank," Sefa said. "That's what the traders call them. People who've given everything. Not killed — the Spire doesn't kill, killing wastes material. It takes and takes until there's nothing left to take, and what's standing at the end gets put to work."

"How many?"

"Nobody counts. Counting implies they matter to somebody, and the Spire likes it that way." She lay back against her pack, arms crossed, staring at the first stars — real stars here, this far from Lowmark, which felt obscene. Stars over that. "There used to be villages on that plain. Farming towns. The soil was good. Now look at the grass."

Jonah looked. The grey grass grew in patches, thin and colorless, and where it grew thickest it grew in perfect circles, unnervingly regular, like rings in felled timber. "What are the rings?"

"Sefa Idris does not know, and Sefa Idris has decided she doesn't want to know, and suggests her colleague adopt the same policy until we're safely past them."

But Jonah was already sketching the rings, because that was the disease they'd been born with and the trade they'd chosen: you see a pattern, you record the pattern, you cannot *not*. And as they drew, they noticed something else, something that turned the cold in their stomach into ice. The rings weren't random. Arranged across the plain, seen from above — and Jonah always saw from above, that was the curse of mapmakers, they read land the way readers read text — the circles formed characters. Old script. Guild script, the formal hand used in covenant ledgers before Jonah's time.

They copied the arrangement into the margin of their working sheet and stared at it for a long moment before showing Sefa.

She sat up very slowly. "What does it say?"

"'We consented,'" Jonah read. "'All of us. We consented.'" They looked out at the grey fields, at the perfect rings, at the smooth-walking figures patrolling the base of a tower built from other people's yesterdays. "It's a lie written in the landscape. Or—" and here came the horror, arriving not like a wave but like water rising through floorboards, slow and total, "—or it's true. Or they consented, one at a time, each thinking they were the only one, each giving a little because someone told them it was needed, until there was nothing left to give and nothing left to know they'd given it. That's how it works, isn't it? That's how it got my mother. That's how it got Vess. Nobody signs a contract for their whole soul. They sign for a year. For a debt. For someone they love. And the interest—"

"Stop." Sefa's voice cracked like a whip. In the dark her eyes were very bright. "You don't get to do that. You don't get to make it make sense. I've spent eleven years making it make sense, Crane, and let me tell you what sense costs." She lay back down, turned away, and said nothing more for a long time, and Jonah, who had learned in four days of travel exactly which doors in Sefa Idris opened and which were painted on, let it lie.

But they kept sketching. And the sketch grew: the patrol routes, four circuits, gaps of eleven minutes at the northern arc where the terrain funneled the Blank through a defile of fallen masonry; the sentry positions, or the absence of them — the Spire posted no watchers above the ground floor, as if nothing that climbed it could possibly matter; the rings of grass; the flickering patches of the wall, which Jonah had begun to chart like weather, noting that memories surfaced most often on the western face at dusk, as if the tower dreamed at the end of the day the way people did.

By midnight the working sheet was full, and Jonah sat with it in the starlight and understood, with the particular loneliness of their profession, that they were looking at the first honest map anyone had ever made of this place. Every other rendering — the woodcuts in forbidden pamphlets, the diagrams in Vess's stolen notes — had shown a tower. A thing. An object.

This was not an object. This was an *organism*, and it was fed, and it was patient, and every line of Jonah's careful ink said so.

* * *

Near dawn, Jonah woke to Sefa's hand clamped over their mouth.

Below the ridge, thirty yards out, stood one of the Blank.

It had come up the slope alone, off its circuit, and it stood perfectly still in the grey pre-light, facing their campsite — facing, Jonah realized with a lurch, *them*, though its eyes were the problem. They were open. They were aimed. But nothing looked out of them, nothing received, the way a window is aimed at a garden without seeing it. Frost had formed on its shoulders. It had been standing there some time.

Sefa's knife was in her other hand. Jonah shook their head minutely. *Don't.* You didn't fight the Blank; everyone who wrote about them agreed on that much, though none of them said why, and Jonah suspected the why was that fighting them meant admitting they had once been people, and the arm does not easily raise itself against that.

The Blank took a step forward. Then another. Its mouth opened — and the sound that came out was worse than any threat, because it wasn't a threat at all. It was a voice worn down to gravel and static, a voice used as a tool until the tool remembered it had been a throat, and it said:

"*...kestrel...*"

Jonah stopped breathing.

The word hung in the cold air. Not a word the Blank should have. Not a word from any ledger, any contract, any language of debt and consent. A word from somewhere older, somewhere personal, somewhere that had no business surviving inside a hollowed thing on a hillside at dawn.

The Blank swayed. For one instant — Jonah would spend years afterward unsure whether they had imagined it — the empty eyes *focused*, found Jonah's face, and behind the emptiness something moved the way the memories moved on the Spire's walls: surfacing, flickering, sinking. A woman's laugh, maybe. Flour dust in sunlight. Hands teaching smaller hands to draw a horizon.

Then it turned, smooth and unresisting as a door on oiled hinges, and walked back down toward the plain, and the frost on its shoulders caught the first light like a shroud catching fire.

Sefa lowered the knife. "Crane," she whispered. "Why did it say that? Why did it say *that word*?"

Jonah looked at their own hands, ink-stained, trembling slightly, holding a map of a tower that ate childhoods. They thought of a hidden workroom in Lowmark, of living ink replaying a dead woman's final walk, of a note that said *follow the ink.*

"I don't know," they lied — or half-lied, because the truth was still assembling itself somewhere below language, cold and certain as the dawn. "But I think," they said, watching the Blank shrink to a speck at the base of the Spire, "something in there remembers me. And I think we have to go in before it forgets again."
