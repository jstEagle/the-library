## 8. The Blank Who Knew My Name

The rope bit into Jonah's wrists before they understood what had happened.

One moment they had been crouched in the bracken below the ridge, sketching the Spire's outer wall by starless moonlight — the Blank patrolled in patterns that were almost, but not quite, regular, and Jonah had spent three hours learning the shape of the almost — and the next, the ground itself had opened beneath them like a mouth. Not a pit. A fold. The land here still forgot itself in places, and something had taught the forgetting to hunt.

Now Jonah hung from a hook of pale stone in a chamber that smelled of nothing at all, which was somehow worse than any smell. No rot, no damp, no smoke. Nothingness had a texture, it turned out: a smoothness against the inside of the nose, like the air had been scoured.

They were not alone.

The Blank stood six feet away, perfectly still, in the way that only things without heartbeats can be still. Up close, the horror of them resolved into something more intimate than horror. They were not faceless. They had faces — soft, unfinished ones, like wax left too near a flame, like a portrait someone had begun and abandoned. This one's features suggested a woman who might have been anywhere between thirty and sixty, her outline blurred as though the world could not quite commit to her.

Her hands were empty. She had not needed a weapon. The fold in the earth had been enough.

"You're going to take me up there," Jonah said, because their voice was the only tool left, and because Sefa was half a mile south with the horses and every minute Jonah stayed alive was a minute the patrol patterns went unlearned. "To the Spire. That's the procedure, isn't it? You bring back anything that maps you."

The Blank tilted her head. It should have been an empty gesture, a puppet's pantomime of curiosity.

Instead she said: "You hold your pencil wrong."

Jonah's breath stopped.

The voice was not a voice so much as a weather — a pressure in the chamber, arriving from everywhere, thin as paper. But under the strangeness, under the hollowed-out hollowness of it, there was something warm. Something that knew.

"Everyone holds a pencil," Jonah managed. "That's not — that proves nothing."

"You choke it too high on the shaft. Like you're afraid of it." The Blank took one step closer. Her feet made no sound; Jonah honestly could not say whether they touched the floor. "Your mother did the same. I remember her laughing at you for it. You were seven. You were drawing a map of the garden, and you'd put the well in the wrong place, and she laughed — not meanly, never meanly — and said, *a cartographer who fears her own instruments will draw fearful maps.*"

"I don't—" Jonah's throat closed. "I never knew my mother."

"I know." And there it was, unmistakable now, threaded through the blankness like gold through grey stone: grief. Real, enormous, human grief, worn smooth by years of erosion but still unmistakably grief. "That is the cruelest part. I know her, and you don't. She is in here—" one hand rose, pressed against the featureless softness of her own chest, "—in pieces. In scraps. The Spire takes people apart and files them, and some of her got filed into me. I have been carrying her for eleven years without knowing whose she was. Until tonight. Until I saw your face in the bracken, and everything she remembered about loving you woke up all at once, and it has been screaming ever since."

* * *

Jonah had read about the Blank in the Guild's oldest ledgers, back when they were still allowed in the restricted stacks. Vess had caught them there once — fourteen years old, lantern held high, reading accounts of the wars before the wards — and instead of punishing them, Vess had sat down beside them and told the stories properly. *The Blank are what's left when the Spire finishes with someone,* she'd said. *It drinks the memory and leaves the body walking. They're not evil, Jonah. They're empty. There's a difference, and pitying the difference is the beginning of wisdom.*

Vess had sounded so certain. Vess had sounded like someone describing weather in a country she'd visited.

But no ledger, and no guildmaster's fireside lesson, had prepared Jonah for this: an enemy quoting a mother's laughter. An enemy who knew how Maren Crane — a name Jonah had learned only days ago, off a map drawn in living ink — had teased her child about pencils.

"What's your name?" Jonah asked. It came out rough. "Before. What was your name?"

Silence. The Blank's unfinished face worked, the way a sleeper's does when a dream turns.

"I don't know," she said at last, and the grief in it could have flooded the chamber. "That's the first thing it takes. It takes the name so you can't be called home. Everything else goes slowly after that — it likes to keep some of us lucid for a while, the ones with useful memories. I was useful. I remembered roads. I remembered which families hid grain during the famine, which wells ran bitter, where the old shrines stood before the Guild catalogued them out of existence." Her head lifted. "I was a surveyor too. Isn't that funny. We were colleagues, once, you and I. In whatever way a dead woman and a stolen woman can be colleagues."

"The Spire keeps you lucid," Jonah repeated. Their mind, traitorous and meticulous even now, was already drafting: *the Blank retain episodic fragments relevant to their former trades; lucidity persists in proportion to mnemonic utility; therefore the tower curates its own garrison—* and then they made themselves stop, because the woman in front of them was not a data point. "For how long?"

"Eleven years. Maybe twelve. Time is difficult, in here." She turned her hand over, studied the palm as if expecting writing on it. "At first I fought it. I used to repeat my name to myself all day, every day, like a prayer. Whatever it was. I've lost the word now, but I remember the shape of saying it — two syllables, the second one rising. Do you understand? I remember the melody of myself and none of the words."

Jonah thought of Sefa's brother, consumed by the Spire years ago. Thought of Sefa saying, around a dying fire, *they don't kill you, Crane. Killing would be a mercy with edges. They keep you.* Thought of the broker in the market of borrowed futures, whispering that Jonah's mother hadn't died — she'd been taken.

"How much of her do you have?" Jonah asked. "My mother. How much?"

"Some. Fragments. The Spire files by feeling, mostly — it finds memories delicious according to their intensity, not their order. So I have her wedding day and not her husband's face. I have a night of terror, running through snow with something wrapped against her chest, and I know — I *know*, the way you know your own heartbeat — that the bundle was you. I have the moment she decided to leave you with the Cranes. Not why. Just the deciding. Her hands shaking. Your fist closed around her finger like you were holding on to the whole world." The Blank's voice frayed. "She sang to you. Badly. She knew it was badly and she did it anyway, and you loved it, you loved it more than anything, and she used to think — she used to think, *when this is over, when the breach is sealed, I'll go back, I'll learn the lullaby properly, I'll sing it right.*"

The chamber swam. Jonah became distantly aware that they were crying, that tears were tracking down into the corners of their mouth, salt on the nothing-smell.

"She sealed the breach," Jonah whispered. "Everyone says so. Maren Crane died sealing the last breach."

"Yes," the Blank said softly. "And the Spire grew from the place where she died. Draw your own conclusions, cartographer. You're better at it than I am now."

* * *

Time passed. Jonah couldn't have said how much. The chamber had no windows, and the Blank did not move except to shift her weight in small, almost-human ways, and the nothing-smell pressed in like deep water.

Eventually Jonah asked the question that had been building since the first words left her.

"Why haven't you taken me up yet?"

The Blank was quiet for a long time.

"Because she doesn't want me to," she said finally. "Because somewhere in the filing system of that tower, Maren Crane's love for you sits in a drawer marked *do not use until the end of the world,* and tonight the end of the world walked into my patrol line wearing her chin and her stubborn shoulders, and the drawer opened." She stepped closer. This time Jonah could see that her eyes, though clouded, were the exact grey-green of the sea off Lowmark in winter. "Do you know what it's like, being a drawer someone else owns? Every thought I have belongs to the tower. Every loyalty. When it commands, I obey — not because I choose to, but because choosing was the second thing it took, after the name. And yet." Her ruined face did something that was almost a smile. "And yet here I stand, disobeying. Or trying to. It may be that I cannot stop myself. It may be that love is the one thing it cannot fully digest — it drinks and drinks and the dregs remain, and the dregs are enough, tonight, to make me treasonous."

"It will punish you," Jonah said. "If it notices."

"It notices everything. It simply doesn't always bother to look." She reached out — Jonah flinched — and her fingers, cool and dry as old paper, found the ropes at Jonah's wrists. For a moment she only held them, as if remembering what ropes were for. Then, with a surveyor's economy of motion, she began to untie them.

"You're letting me go."

"I'm doing no such thing. I am experiencing a malfunction." The knot gave. Blood rushed back into Jonah's hands, prickling, alive. "There is a maintenance door in the western foundation, thirty paces behind the third standing stone. It opens onto the stair that the Blank use when we're rotated up the tower. It is watched, but the watch changes at the hour when the bells would ring, if this country still had bells. Go now. Go quickly. And cartographer—" her hand closed briefly around Jonah's wrist, grip fierce despite its frailty, "—when you make your map. When you find the heart of it. Look for her. She's still in there. Whatever else the Spire is, it is also a prison, and prisons can be broken."

"Maren Crane is alive?"

"Maren Crane is *kept.*" The distinction landed like a stone in still water. "There's a difference, and pitying the difference is the beginning of wisdom. Someone taught me that once. I think it was her."

Jonah's legs nearly failed getting up. They stood in the nothing-smelling dark, wrists raw, eyes streaming, looking at the enemy who had given them back a piece of their mother in scraps and fragments and the melody of a bad lullaby.

"What do I call you?" Jonah asked. "When I write this down. What name do I give you?"

The Blank was silent for so long that Jonah thought she wouldn't answer.

"Two syllables," she said at last. "The second one rising. If you ever find it — write it in your ledger. Names are how we're called home." She turned toward the passage that led up, into the tower's shadow, and her voice came back one final time, thinner now, already dissolving into the weather of the Spire:

"Run, little mapmaker. Your mother died buying this world one more century. Don't you dare waste it."

* * *

Jonah ran.

Through the fold in the earth, which had mercifully forgotten how to be a trap; across the frozen furrows of a field that duplicated itself every hundred yards; past the third standing stone, leaning like a tired sentry, to the maintenance door — iron, unbarred, exactly where she'd said. The watch changed as promised. Jonah slipped through the gap between patrols with a cartographer's precision and a fugitive's heart, and did not stop running until the ridge swallowed them and the Spire was a black needle behind.

Sefa found them at dawn, or what passed for dawn now, under a sky the color of unwashed wool. She took one look at Jonah's face, at the torn ropes still hanging from one wrist, and reached for her knives.

"Who?" she demanded. "Which one of them—"

"A Blank." Jonah's voice cracked on the word. They sat down hard on the cold grass. "A Blank caught me, and she — Sefa, she *knew* me. She had pieces of my mother inside her. My mother's memories, filed away in a hollowed stranger like letters in a drawer, and she read them to me. She sang—" Jonah pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "She let me go. Or she couldn't stop herself. I don't know which, and I don't know if it matters."

Sefa crouched beside them. For a long moment she said nothing, which from Sefa was a kind of sacrament.

"They're in there," she said quietly. "All of them. The taken. I told you they keep you — I told you what it did to Tam, and you looked at me like I was telling ghost stories." Her jaw tightened. "So now you know. The whole army of that thing is made of people somebody loved. That's not a garrison, Crane. That's a hostage wall."

"And it means we can't just burn it down." Jonah lowered their hands. Under the exhaustion, under the grief, something had hardened — the same resolve that had carried them out of the Guildhall ruins, out of Lowmark, up the long forgetting road. But it had changed shape overnight. It was no longer only anger. "Every Blank we destroy, we destroy someone's mother, someone's brother. Someone's *name*, maybe — names are how they're called home, Sefa, she told me that, she begged me to find hers—"

"We'll find it," Sefa said. Simply. As if it were already entered in a ledger.

Jonah pulled out their field book with numb fingers and began, right there in the wet grass, to draw: the chamber, the patrol lines, the maintenance door, the stair. And at the bottom of the page, in careful capitals, they wrote the only description they had:

*TWO SYLLABLES. THE SECOND ONE RISING.*

Then they paused, pencil choking high on the shaft, and added one more line — the coordinates of the western foundation, the third standing stone, the hour of the changing watch. Because the Blank had said *look for her,* and Jonah intended to do exactly that.

"Sefa," they said. "We're not going around the Spire anymore. We're going in. Tonight, through the maintenance door — and Sefa, there's someone inside we need to find. Someone the ledgers say is dead."

Sefa looked at the map, then at Jonah's face, and Jonah watched understanding arrive in her like weather.

"Your mother," she said.

"My mother," Jonah agreed, and folded the page closed over the name they didn't have yet, and the tower waited above them with all its stolen drawers.
