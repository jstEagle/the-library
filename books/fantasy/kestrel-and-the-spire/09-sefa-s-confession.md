## 9. Sefa's Confession

The fire was small, because small fires could be explained and large ones had to be defended. Sefa Idris had learned that distinction years ago, in another life, in a room with better chairs, and she built the flame accordingly: a fist of dry gorse and two split logs, tucked into the lee of a fallen wall that some forgotten village had left behind like a shed tooth. Above them, the sky did its new trick — the one that made her teeth ache if she thought about it too long — where the stars should have been and were not, a darkness so complete it felt less like night than like a held breath.

Jonah Crane sat across the fire with their knees drawn up, turning a strip of dried fish over the flames with the same maddening patience they brought to everything. Cataloguing, Sefa thought. Even now. Even here. They probably had a mental ledger entry for *fish, dried, one strip, condition poor*. They probably had a mental ledger entry for her.

*Companion, temporary. Trustworthiness: pending.*

She almost laughed. It came out as something else instead, a short rough sound that made Jonah glance up.

"You should sleep," Jonah said. "You took second watch."

"I'm not tired."

"You're lying."

"I'm resting my eyes in an upright position. There's a difference." She pulled her coat tighter. The coat had been good once, guild-issue wool with the Keeper's knot embroidered at the cuff, back when she'd believed the knot meant something clean. Now the embroidery was frayed to gray threads, and she hadn't been able to bring herself to pick them out or to repair them. A monument to indecision, worn at her wrist. "Jonah."

"Mm."

"Ask me."

Jonah looked at her across the fire. In the low light their face was all planes and shadow, the cartographer's habit of stillness sitting on them like a second skin. They had that way of waiting — really waiting, the way no one at the Guild ever waited, everyone always three entries ahead of the conversation — and Sefa found, to her fury, that her eyes stung.

"Ask you what?" Jonah said quietly. Which was its own kind of cruelty, or its own kind of mercy. Making her say it whole.

"You've been circling it since the market," she said. "Since before the market, if I'm honest. Since I told you about Tam. You've been very polite about it, which from you is practically screaming. You want to know how I know so much about the Spire. How I knew the Blank patrol in threes. How I knew which floors of the lower archive take bribes and which take blood. You want to know why a Guild keeper can walk through unmapped country like she's reading her own handwriting." She turned her hands over, palms up, as if the answer might be written there. It wasn't. She'd checked. For eleven years she'd checked. "So ask me."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a duplicated owl called from two directions at once — the same note, the same interval, a bird the geography had forgotten into copies — and Sefa flinched before she could stop herself.

Jonah set down the fish. "Sefa—"

"Don't soften it. Whatever you're about to do, don't do it gently. I've had gentle. Gentle is what they give you at the exit interview while they're already unbuckling your name."

That landed. She saw it land — saw Jonah go still in a new way, the way they'd gone still in Vess Corvane's ransacked study, standing over the torn ledger pages. *Follow the ink,* the note had said. Sefa had wanted to burn the note on the spot. Some part of her still did.

"I worked for them," she said.

The words came out flat and small, and she hated how small, so she made herself say the rest at full weight, each piece placed like a stone on a cairn.

"Not the way you're imagining — not one of the Blank, not one of the hollowed ones in the corridors with their eyes like unlit windows. Worse, actually. Better paid. I was a recruiter. Eleven years ago. Before your time at the Guild, before Vess's chair was even warm. The Spire doesn't only take people, Jonah. Taking is crude. What it prefers — what it *runs on* — is being given. A memory surrendered freely binds tighter than one ripped out. Everyone who built the covenant knew that; it's why the sacrifice had to be willing. And so the Spire employs people who are very good at making surrender feel like a choice." She smiled, and felt her mouth do it wrong. "I was very good."

Jonah said nothing. The firelight moved on their face. Sefa kept going, because stopping now would mean never starting again.

"There's a script. Of course there's a script; everything in that tower is procedural down to the marrow. You find people at the hinge-points of their lives — grief, debt, disgrace, love gone wrong — and you don't lie to them. That's the first rule and the deepest one. You never lie. You tell them exactly what the trade costs and exactly what it buys, and you let their own misery do the persuading. A widow who can't carry her husband's face every morning. A debtor who'd trade ten years of Tuesdays to be free. A man whose brother—" Her voice caught on the wire of it. She pushed through. "A man whose brother was dying anyway, slowly, in pain, and who was offered a way to make the ending *useful*. Meaningful. Fed into something ascending. He signed. People always signed. That was the horror of it, Jonah — I never once forced anyone. I just held the door open and described the view."

"And Tam," Jonah said. Not a question. Sefa had told them about her brother on the road, in the duplicated village where every house existed twice — told them he'd been consumed by the Spire years ago, which was true, and had carefully not told them the rest, which was also true, and had watched Jonah notice the shape of the omission without pressing. Rule-keeper, she'd thought bitterly at the time. Waits for you to file the missing page yourself.

"My brother," Sefa said, "was not dying."

The fire had burned down to a red heart. She fed it a stick and watched the sparks climb toward a sky with no stars to catch them.

"Tam was a fool and a dreamer and the best of us, and he'd gotten himself involved with a cell of covenant-loyalists — people who thought the old bargain was sacred and the Guild had grown fat and cowardly around it. He came to me. My own brother, walked into my office in the Spire's outer ministry, because he thought — *he thought* — that I was still his sister first and theirs second. He wanted to warn me off. He'd figured out what I was. Someone had finally told him what the woman who sent money home every month actually did for a living, and instead of cutting me off he came to *save me*." Her laugh cracked in the middle. "He sat where you're sitting. He said, 'Sef, whatever they're paying you, it isn't enough. Nothing is enough. You know what they do with what we give them.' And I —"

She had rehearsed this confession for eleven years, in bathhouses and border inns and the long gray corridor of sleepless nights, and every rehearsal ended at the same door, and every time she reached the door she stood outside it like a debtor.

"I reported him," she said. "I filed the intake. His name went into the ledger — *their* ledger, the black-bound one, the one with the clasp — and within the week the Blank came for him, and I signed the transfer order myself, because if I didn't sign it they'd have asked why a recruiter hesitated, and hesitation gets audited, and I was twenty-four years old and a coward, and I traded my brother's name to save my own position. Not my life. My *position*. My office with the good light and the salary and the lie that I was only holding doors open, never walking anyone through."

The silence afterward was enormous. Out in the dark country, the twin owls called and called, and the dead village kept its double shadows, and Sefa sat with her hands open in her lap like a woman showing she carried no weapon, though the weapon was the whole of her.

When Jonah finally spoke, their voice was careful in the way of someone crossing ice.

"The market broker," they said. "In the tent with the futures hanging like lanterns. When they recognized my name — you went white. You already knew then. About Maren Crane. About what the Spire does with the ones it takes whole."

"I suspected. I'd seen the deep-vault manifests once, by accident, before I ran. Names kept *alive* in storage, memory suspended rather than consumed — the Spire hoards certain lineages. Cartographer lines especially. Mapmakers' memories are dense, structured, *load-bearing*." She looked up. "I should have told you on the road. I told myself I was protecting you. That was the recruiter's sentence, Jonah. We have a sentence for everything. *It's kinder if they choose it themselves.* *They'd have found out eventually.* *Better it comes from me.* Every lie I ever sold, wearing honesty's coat."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because tomorrow we reach the ridge." She nodded east, where the land rose in long dark shoulders toward the thing they had both been refusing to name aloud — the Spire, visible now on clear nights even without stars, as a place where the darkness was somehow *denser*, a hole shaped like a tower. "And because you deserve to decide what walks beside you into that place. I'm not asking forgiveness. Forgiveness is a currency and I'm done trading. I'm asking you to make the call the way you'd make any call in the field: with full information. Do you trust me? Knowing what I am. Knowing what I did. Knowing that the person who filed your mother's world into a vault might have been — could have been — someone exactly like me, holding a door open, describing the view."

Jonah was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Sefa catalogued, helplessly, every possible answer, ranked by how much each would cost her.

Then Jonah reached into their coat and drew out the little traveling ledger they kept — the pocket one, the one Sefa had teased them about, *you alphabetize your bootlaces* — and opened it to a fresh page, and wrote, in their small precise hand, by firelight.

"What are you doing?" she asked.

"Entering it," Jonah said. "Into the record. Your confession, dated, witnessed. Signed by you?" They held out the pencil.

Sefa stared at it. "You want me to sign it."

"It's how my mother's guild worked — the real work, before it rotted. Nothing true gets erased by being spoken. It gets erased by being *unrecorded*." Jonah's eyes lifted to hers, steady, unreadable, firelit. "If you sign it, it exists. Whatever happens tomorrow, whatever you do or fail to do, this page says you chose to put it on the record yourself. That's worth more than anything I could say back to you. So: do you trust *me*, Sefa? With the worst fact about you, in my keeping, permanently?"

Her hands shook taking the pencil. She signed. The letters came out ragged but legible: *Sefa Idris, who held the door.* Jonah closed the book and put it away, and then, at last, answered her.

"I don't forgive you," Jonah said. "Not tonight. Maybe not for a long time. Tam's name deserves better than a quick absolution by campfire." They poked the fire apart, letting the flames stand up taller, braver. "But I trust you. Because you told me anyway, when silence would have served you until the ridge. And because you keep his name — you said you keep it, you say it to yourself every morning. The Spire eats names, Sefa. Anyone who fights to hold one against that current is fighting on the right side, however late they started."

Sefa discovered she was crying, silently, furiously, the way she hadn't since she was twenty-four and signing her brother away. She didn't wipe her face. Let the record show it, she thought. Let the ledger show all of it.

"Get some sleep," Jonah said, rising for first watch, and then paused, looking east past her shoulder. "Sefa."

"What?"

"Look at the ridge."

She turned. Against the starless dark, the horizon had changed. Where yesterday there had been only the denser-than-dark suggestion of the tower, tonight there was a thin seam of light running up the Spire's flank — pale gold, flickering, like ink catching flame along a page — climbing floor by floor, as if something inside were lighting lamps. Or as if something inside were *reading*.

And beneath the light, faint but unmistakable, carried on a wind that shouldn't have reached this far, came a sound neither of them could mistake: a bell, tolling once, twice — the recall-chime of the Guild of Impossible Things, rung from a tower that had stolen it.

Vess Corvane knew they were coming.
