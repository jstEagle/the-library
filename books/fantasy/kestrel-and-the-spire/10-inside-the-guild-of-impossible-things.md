## 10. Inside the Guild of Impossible Things

The Spire's base did not have doors so much as it had mouths.

Jonah had mapped enough thresholds in their life to know the difference. A door was a courtesy — a thing built by people who understood that walls were agreements between inside and outside. A mouth was a presumption. The entrance before them now was a tall arch of grey stone that seemed to have grown rather than been carved, its edges smooth as a tongue, and it opened onto a corridor lit by lamps that burned without flame, giving off light the color of old milk.

"Smile," Sefa murmured beside them. "Traders smile."

Jonah smiled. It felt like wearing someone else's coat.

They had spent two days at the ridge camp preparing for this, and most of those days had gone into their disguises, which were less clothing than argument. Sefa had argued them into it piece by piece: the dust-grey cloaks of the salt-road caravans, the lead tokens traders carried to prove they'd paid the road-tolls, the particular slouch of people who had walked a long way carrying something heavy and valuable and none of anyone's business. Jonah's own contribution had been subtler. They had taken a page from one of Vess's living-ink maps — a blank margin, nothing more — and learned to let it sit against their skin beneath the cloak, where it drank their fear and gave back, faintly, the smell of an ordinary life: bread, ink, rain on slate. To anything that tasted memory in passing, they would read as no one worth tasting.

It was the closest thing to armor either of them owned.

"You're doing the thing," Sefa said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you catalog everything so you don't have to feel any of it." She adjusted her hood, and beneath it her eyes were sharp and dark and unfooled. "I do it too. It's a guild habit. We should both be worried about how much we have in common."

"I'm not feeling anything I need to avoid," Jonah said, which was a lie so flat it barely qualified as speech, and Sefa had the grace not to say so.

They joined the line of traders shuffling toward the arch. That there was a line at all had been the day's first shock. Jonah had expected the Spire's approaches to be empty, guarded, terrible — a fortress of forgetting with nothing human near it. Instead there was commerce. Carts. A woman selling roasted chestnuts from a brazier, her face pleasant and slightly too still, like a portrait of a woman selling chestnuts. Traders came up the salt road in twos and threes with goods Jonah could identify and goods Jonah couldn't: jars of something that glowed faintly amber; bolts of cloth that seemed to shift pattern when looked at directly; small locked boxes that ticked, softly, like clocks counting down to nothing.

"They feed it," Sefa said quietly. "That's what the trade is. You bring it memories, it pays you in — " she gestured vaguely at a trader ahead of them who was examining his own hands with an expression of mild, contented confusion — "in whatever you've forgotten you wanted."

"And the Blank?"

"Patrol the upper levels. The lower floors are for business. Business doesn't need hollow men." She paused. "Mostly."

Two of the Blank stood flanking the arch, and Jonah made themselves look at them properly this time, because looking away was a luxury they'd already spent. They were tall and grey-cloaked and moved with a smoothness that was worse than stiffness — the smoothness of things that had once known hesitation and had it removed. Their faces were not blank, exactly. They were *averaged*. Features present but unresolved, as if a hundred faces had been laid over one another and only the agreement between them remained. One of them turned its head as Jonah passed, and for a moment its gaze rested on them with something that might, in a living face, have been called attention.

Then it looked away, and Jonah breathed again, and hated how much effort breathing had become.

* * *

Inside, the Spire's lower floors were a market, and the market was a wound dressed as a festival.

The great entry hall rose through three open tiers, balconies ringing the central space, staircases spiraling up into light that came from nowhere and illuminated everything evenly, without shadow, which Jonah found more disturbing than darkness would have been. Stalls lined every tier. Voices layered over voices — haggling, laughing, calling out wares — and under all of it ran a sound Jonah took several minutes to identify because it was so quiet and so constant: a hum, deep as a held breath, coming up through the floor. The Spire itself, digesting. Or dreaming. Jonah had stopped pretending those were different things.

A stall on the second tier sold childhoods by the season. Another sold last words. A broker with silver rings on every finger called out prices for unlived lives — *one summer of the sea, gently used, owner never went!* — and the crowd laughed, and Jonah's stomach turned over, because they knew exactly what it cost to buy a year of your own future here, and they had paid it, and the bill was still outstanding somewhere in this building.

"Don't stare at the stalls," Sefa said. "Stare at the architecture. Traders gawk at buildings. It's safer."

So Jonah gawked at the architecture, which was its own kind of horror once they started truly seeing it. The walls were stone the way a pearl is stone — layered, compressed, grown around some irritant at the center. And the layers moved. Not visibly, not when watched directly, but Jonah kept catching the walls out of the corner of their eye flickering: a flash of a kitchen, a garden, a girl running through wheat, a man dying badly in a doorway — images surfacing in the stone like fish rising in a pond and sinking again. The Spire was built of compressed memory. Every block was somebody's everything, pressed until it forgot it had ever been a life.

And then, on the third tier, past a curtain of grey beads, Jonah saw the sign.

They stopped walking. Sefa bumped into them, swore under her breath, followed their gaze — and went very still.

The sign was modest. Carved wood, painted letters, the kind of thing you'd see above a counting-house in Lowmark. It read:

**GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS — LOWMARK & ASSOCIATED CHARTERS**

Below it, smaller: *Appraisals daily. All trades final.*

"That's not possible," Jonah said.

"It's a guild of impossible things," Sefa said. "You'd think impossibility would be more of a feature than a bug."

But Jonah wasn't listening, because they were already moving toward the door beneath the sign, and everything in them that wanted to run the other way had been outvoted by the single fact that the door was *familiar*. The wood was Lowmark oak. The hinges were the same make as the guildhall's — forged by Anselm Pratt, who charged double and deserved it. Someone had brought home here. Someone had built a branch of the Guild inside the thing eating the world, and there was only one person alive with the authority and the arrogance to do it.

Vess Corvane.

* * *

The office beyond the door was a perfect replica of Vess's study in the guildhall, down to the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a leaping fish, and that perfection was the most frightening thing Jonah had seen since the stars went out. Because the guildhall study had a water stain because the roof leaked because Vess refused to pay for repairs, and this room had been built *deliberately*, stain included, by someone who understood that authenticity lived in flaws. This was not a copy. This was a transplant. A piece of the Guild, roots and all, sunk into the body of the Spire.

The desk was tidy. Vess's desk was never tidy. But the ledgers stacked upon it were Guild ledgers — Jonah knew the binding, the paper, the particular iron-gall ink that smelled faintly of cloves because Vess insisted on clove oil against moths. Jonah put their gloved hands on the topmost volume and felt their heart hammering against their ribs like something trying to get out.

"We shouldn't be here," Sefa said. She had posted herself at the bead curtain, watching the market through the gaps. "This is a private office. People notice private offices."

"People notice traders who steal," Jonah said. "We're appraisers today. We're evaluating stock." Even as they said it they were opening the ledger, because the truth was that no disguise or excuse mattered anymore. Some doors you walk through knowing you can't walk back.

The ledger was old. Older than Vess's tenure — the early entries were in a hand Jonah didn't recognize, formal and cramped, and the dates went back further than Jonah had ever seen a Guild ledger go. Back past the founding of Lowmark. Back past the charter Jonah had memorized during their apprenticeship, the one that said the Guild traded in *memories, names, and borrowed futures* and owed fealty to no crown and no god.

Back to the covenant.

Jonah had asked about the covenant exactly once, years ago, an eager apprentice with an inventory question. Vess had looked at them for a long moment with an expression Jonah had never been able to classify, and had said: *"Some entries are load-bearing, Crane. You don't knock on them."* And Jonah had never knocked again. They had told themselves it was discipline. Now, reading, they understood it had been obedience, and obedience is just fear that's been given a filing system.

The entries began plainly enough. *Year One of the Covenant.* The first guildmasters — three names, all unfamiliar, all written with titles after them like honors: *Keeper of the Threshold, Keeper of the Ledger, Keeper of the Debt.* And then the terms, set out in the careful language of people who know they are writing something that will outlive them:

*Let it be agreed between the realms and that which stands between them: that the borders shall hold, and the excluded things shall stay excluded, and the night sky shall keep its fires. In exchange, once each hundred years, the Guild shall bring to the threshold one willing soul, who shall give of their remembered life freely, wholly, and without coercion, and this gift shall renew the covenant for another century.*

*A willing soul,* Jonah read again. *Freely, wholly, without coercion.*

Every hundred years. For as far back as the ledger ran, the pattern repeated. A name entered. A date. A short paragraph in each hand — and the hands changed every century, generation after generation of guildmasters, and Jonah read their words and felt the weight of them accumulate. Some entries were serene. One keeper wrote simply: *I go gladly. The wheat needs rain and my children are grown.* Some were terrified and honest about it: *I am afraid. Do it anyway. Do not let them take me unwilling — the terms matter, the willingness IS the terms.* One, near the middle, was almost angry: *To whoever reads this: it is a fair bargain and a hard one, and if you are reading this hoping to find a loophole, shame on you. There is no loophole. There was never supposed to be one.*

And then, ninety-nine years ago — no. Jonah checked the dates twice, hands shaking. Ninety-nine years ago, the last sacrifice. A name: Odile Ferrance. A note in a later hand: *Given willingly. Covenant renewed.*

And then a gap. A hundred-year gap, stretching forward to a date that had come and gone — Jonah counted backward from the night the wards fell, and the arithmetic landed with a sound like a bone breaking. The covenant had fallen due. This year. This autumn. The wards hadn't failed randomly. They had expired, the way a lease expires, the way a promise expires when the payment doesn't come.

And there was no new name. Where the next sacrifice should have been entered, there was instead a different hand — Vess's hand, unmistakable, the sharp downstrokes Jonah had watched ten thousand times — and Vess had not written a name.

She had written a plan.

*No volunteer has come forward,* the entry began, and even in ink Jonah could hear the tightness in her voice. *The Council says wait. Waiting is what the last Council said, and Odile Ferrance died for them while they drafted resolutions. I will not preside over the unraveling of everything because no one has the courage to be decent.*

*I have found another way. The Spire takes memory. Very well — let it take memory in quantity. Let it take the covenant itself, consume the whole of the old bargain, and in consuming it, dissolve the debt. What is eaten cannot be owed. I will give it the covenant entire, and it will have swallowed its own terms, and the realms will stand free of this bloody tithe forever.*

*If I am wrong, may whatever reads this forgive me. If I am right, burn this page and thank me.*

Jonah sat down. There was a chair behind them — Vess's chair, replicated down to the worn left armrest — and their legs simply stopped being interested in standing.

"No," they said aloud. It came out very small.

Sefa crossed the room fast, read over their shoulder, and Jonah watched her face do what their own must have done: the narrowing, the denial, the slow collapse. "She didn't sell it," Sefa said. Her voice was strange, stripped. "Everyone said she sold it. Sold us out, took payment, betrayed — " She stopped. Started again. "She tried to *cheat* it. She tried to cheat a bargain older than the city, and instead of dissolving the debt she — "

"She fed it an appetizer," Jonah said. "The covenant is the biggest memory there is. The oldest. She gave the Spire a taste of it, and the taste woke it up, and the wards fell because the covenant is broken now — torn, half-eaten — and the Spire doesn't want to settle for one meal anymore. It wants the whole herd." They heard their own voice going flat and precise, the cataloguing voice, the voice that kept feeling at arm's length, and this time they let it, because the alternative was screaming in Vess Corvane's replicated office inside the tower that was eating the world.

Because that was the vertigo of it, the thing that made the floor feel theoretical. Jonah had built their whole life on the Guild. On its ledgers, its rules, its small mercies — the bottled echoes returned to grieving families, the stolen river traced and given back, the name someone traded away kept safe in case they came back for it. The Guild had been the proof that impossible things could be handled responsibly. And the Guild had been, all along, the mechanism. The collection agency. Once a century it had walked someone to the threshold and called it duty, and Jonah had catalogued the covenant's entry a hundred times without knocking, and the whole beautiful careful institution existed to keep one appointment — and when the appointment came due and no one kept it, the guildmaster hadn't broken the system.

She had tried to game it. Which was somehow worse. Betrayal you could hate. This you had to *understand* — Vess, who kept clove oil against moths, who paid double to Anselm Pratt because his hinges deserved it, who had taught Jonah that every entry deserved care — Vess had looked at a hundred years of willing sacrifices and decided she could find the loophole everyone before her had sworn didn't exist. Corruption dressed as mercy. Arrogance dressed as love. And the realms were paying the difference.

"There's more," Sefa said. She had pulled a slimmer volume from under the stack — recent, this one, Vess's current working ledger. "Look at the accounts. Look where the money goes."

Jonah looked. Entries in Vess's hand, meticulous as always: payments to brokers. Payments for passage. Payments — Jonah's breath caught — *payments to the Spire itself*, recorded as trades. Small ones. Careful ones. Over months, Vess had been feeding the Spire measured doses, calibrating, negotiating. And interleaved with the payments, notes:

*It listens better than they said. It remembers what it eats — that's the horror of it, it keeps everything, it just can't give it back.*

*Asked it about Maren Crane. It showed me. Gods forgive me, it showed me, and she's — *

The sentence stopped there. Ink blot. A pressed-down period, hard enough to nearly tear the page.

Maren Crane.

Jonah's mother. The name Jonah had carried all their life like a stone in a pocket — the mother who died sealing the last breach, the hero of the story everyone told and no one detailed, the absence that had raised them as much as the Guild had. Vess had asked the Spire about her. Vess had *seen* something. And Vess had written *she's* and could not finish it, and had blotted the ink, and had gone on making payments, month after month, and Jonah understood with cold certainty that whatever Vess had seen was the reason for all of it — the cheating, the bargaining, the ascent. Vess hadn't just tried to save the realms.

She had tried to save a specific person inside them, and had decided the price was acceptable, and had never asked the price's family.

"Jonah." Sefa's hand was on their shoulder. Through the bead curtain, the market noise had changed — not louder, but organized, the way a crowd changes when it starts moving in one direction. "Someone's coming up the stairs. Grey cloaks. Two of them. They're asking the brokers questions."

"About what?"

Sefa's jaw tightened. "About a cartographer."

The hum in the floor seemed, just then, to deepen — or perhaps Jonah only imagined that the Spire had noticed them, the way you imagine the sea noticing a swimmer. They closed the ledgers, gently, the way you close the eyes of the dead, and stood.

"Back door?" Sefa asked.

Jonah looked at the office — the water-stain ceiling, the clove-smelling ledgers, the chair with the worn armrest where they had just learned that everything they trusted was the teeth of a trap. There was a door behind the desk. Of course there was. Vess always had a back way out; it was practically guild doctrine.

"Yes," Jonah said. "And Sefa — whatever's behind that door, we're about to understand why she really built all this."

They opened it, and the hum rose to meet them like a tide.
