## 13. What We Trade Away

The alarm began as a sound Sefa felt in her teeth before she heard it.

It came up through the Spire's floors like a bell rung underwater, a low thrum that shivered the marrow of the tower itself, and then it climbed — up and up and up, gathering harmonies as it rose, until the whole archive-heart rang with it. Not a siren. A *name*. She understood that much in the first terrible instant: the Spire did not shout for its guards the way any ordinary fortress would. It called them by name, all of their names at once, every stolen syllable of every stolen person, woven into one vast chord of summons.

And somewhere beneath that chord, threaded through it like a wire through wax, was Vess Corvane's voice.

"*Find them.*"

Sefa grabbed Jonah's sleeve. "Move. Now."

They ran.

The archive stretched around them in shelves that went up farther than lamplight could follow, and each shelf held not books but *memories* — glass vials, lead-sealed boxes, scrolls that whispered when the air moved past them. Sefa had walked these corridors once before, years ago, on the other side of the transaction table, and she had told herself then what she told herself now: *keep your eyes forward, keep your hands still, don't look at what they're selling.* But running past those shelves at speed was worse than walking. The memories knew she was there. They turned toward her. She could feel them leaning out of their containers like flowers toward sun, hungry to be held, to be remembered by anyone at all.

One vial caught her ankle with a thread of longing so sharp she stumbled. Inside it, someone's wedding day. She didn't know whose. That was the horror of this place — it didn't matter whose. Every memory here had been somebody's whole world once.

"Sefa!" Jonah was ahead of her, map clutched against their chest, ink-stained fingers white-knuckled on the paper. "Left here — the corridor slopes down—"

"How do you know?"

"I mapped it." Their grin was wild, terrified, brilliant. "I'm *mapping it right now*. Every turn we take, I'm drawing it. If we get out, we can get back in."

She wanted to kiss them or strangle them, possibly both. "Then draw faster."

They burst through an archway into a gallery where the walls were made of compressed recollection — Sefa could see faces moving in the stone, flickering, half-formed, people dreaming inside the architecture of the tower. And standing in the gallery, motionless as furniture, were three of the Blank.

They had no eyes to track with, but they tracked anyway. Their heads turned in unison, smooth as drawer-slides, and the alarm-chord bent itself around them, feeding them direction. One of them raised an arm — not threateningly, Sefa realized with a lurch; *imploringly*, palm open, the way you reach toward something you've lost.

"Don't," Jonah breathed. "Don't look at its face."

But Sefa looked. She always looked. It was her curse and her craft, looking at things other people flinched from.

The Blank's face was unfinished, like a portrait someone had abandoned halfway. Features suggested rather than rendered. But around its mouth, faint as breath on cold glass, there was an expression — and it was *grief*. Pure, uncomplicated grief, worn like a garment that almost fit.

"Corridor," Jonah said. "There's a service stair behind the east wall — I saw it in Maren's memories, the maintenance ways the builders used—"

"The wall's solid stone."

"It's *memory* stone. Everything here is memory. And memory has doors." Jonah pressed their palm flat against the wall and closed their eyes, and Sefa watched the living ink on the map in their other hand begin to move, crawling off the page and into the stone like water finding cracks. "Maren walked these halls. Her memories are in the walls themselves. I just have to ask politely."

The wall *shivered*.

For one heartbeat nothing happened, and Sefa was certain they were dead — certain the Blank would close their unfinished hands around them both and carry them up to whatever Vess Corvane had become. Then a seam opened in the stone, hairline-thin, widening like a waking eye, and cold air poured through smelling of rain and distance and the outside world.

"Go," Jonah said.

They went.

* * *

The service stairs were narrow, steep, and old in a way nothing else in the Spire was old. Here the walls were plain grey rock, unhaunted, and Sefa felt her shoulders drop half an inch for the first time since they'd entered the tower. Below them, muffled by stone, the alarm-chord continued its endless calling of names.

Jonah paused on a landing, chest heaving, and looked back up the way they'd come. "Vess sounded the alarm herself. Not the Spire. *Her.*"

"She's part of it now. Hard to say where one ends and the other begins."

"No." Jonah shook their head slowly. "I saw her face, Sefa. When she showed me my mother in the wall — there was something underneath the ascension. Something scared. She's not the Spire. She's a woman who made a bargain she doesn't know how to escape, and she's dragging the whole world up the tower with her because she can't admit she was wrong."

"You defend her even now."

"I defended her when I thought she'd betrayed us. I'm not defending her now. I'm *diagnosing* her." Jonah started down again. "There's a difference. Betrayal is a choice. Fear is a cage. She built the cage herself, but she's still in it."

Sefa followed, and said nothing, because the truth was she recognized the cage. She'd lived in one herself for six years — the cage of a thing done that couldn't be undone, a name traded, a brother erased, all of it justified in the moment and unjustifiable ever after. She knew exactly how a person could build their prison brick by brick and call it survival.

They descended three flights, four, five. The alarm faded below hearing. And then, at the sixth landing, the stairs simply stopped.

Not collapsed. Not blocked. *Stopped* — the steps ending mid-air at a wall of darkness that wasn't shadow. Sefa put her hand near it and felt it pull, gently, insistently, the way sleep pulls at a tired mind. Forgetting. A pocket of pure forgetting, pooled in the stairwell like water in a cellar.

"That's new," Jonah said quietly. "That wasn't in Maren's memories. The Spire's growing."

"It knows we're coming. It's filling the exits with itself." Sefa backed up a step. "Is there another way?"

Jonah consulted the map, and Sefa watched their face do something complicated — hope curdling into arithmetic. "Two. The long way around through the lower galleries, which puts us back among the Blank. Or—" They stopped.

"Or?"

"Or we go through it." Jonah touched the edge of the darkness. "Forgetting isn't a wall, it's a *current*. You can cross a current if you anchor yourself to something. Something it can't dissolve."

"And what in all the realms can't be dissolved?"

Jonah looked at her. And Sefa felt the answer arrive before it was spoken, felt it settle over her like a shroud, because she had known — some quiet, honest corner of her had known since the market, since the broker whispered about her brother — that this debt would come due. That the Spire took payment in the only coin it respected.

"My brother," she said.

* * *

They sat on the top step, the pool of forgetting lapping softly at the stair below them like a tide against a seawall, and Sefa told Jonah everything, because if she was going to do this, they deserved to hear it whole.

His name was Tam. Tam Idris. He'd been two years younger than her and taller by fourteen, with a laugh that started in his belly and worked its way out slow, like he was savoring it. He'd been the one who believed in the Guild — properly believed, not the way she'd believed, which was mostly in the wages. He'd joined the recruiting arm because he thought the trade in memories could be made honest. He used to say, *Sef, imagine it — nobody has to lose anything forever. We just hold things for each other. Like a lending library for souls.*

He'd been twenty-three when the covenant's tithe came due and the recruiters came to their village, and he'd been on the list, and Sefa — who was senior, who knew how the lists worked, who knew which names could be moved and which couldn't — had moved his name onto another list. A list marked *taken*. And she'd kept her own name off both.

"They didn't kill him," she said. Her voice came out flat, which was how she'd trained it, which was the only way she could carry it. "They *unmade* him. Took his name first — that's the anchor, you see, the name is what holds a self together — and then everything else unraveled after. His wife stopped knowing him. His children—" She stopped. Started again. "The Spire keeps what it takes. Somewhere in that tower is a vial with Tam Idris written on it, and inside is everything he ever was. I've walked past it. Twice. On guild business. I walked *past my brother* and signed the receipt."

Jonah's hand found hers. Said nothing. Just held on.

"So here's what I have left of him." She drew a breath. "Six years of scrubbing my own memory, hiding him, burying him deeper every year so the guilt wouldn't eat me alive — and in doing that I lost most of him anyway. I have maybe a dozen memories left. His laugh. Him teaching me to skip stones on the Merrow. The night he told me he'd joined the Guild and I lied to his face and said I was proud of him. And his name. I kept his name separate, wrapped it in guild-craft, sealed it — because the name is the anchor, and as long as I hold his name, some part of him is still *held*. Still real. Still owed."

"And the pool down there eats memories."

"Eats everything except what you refuse to let go. And you can only refuse for one thing. One anchor per crossing." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you see the joke of it? Six years I've been hoarding him like a miser. Twelve memories and a name, guarded like crown jewels. And it turns out the only way out of this tower is to spend him."

"It doesn't have to be the name," Jonah said quickly. "Keep the name. Give it the twelve memories. Cross anchored to the name alone—"

"And arrive on the other side holding a word for a man I can't remember. A name with no one attached. That's not keeping him, Jonah. That's keeping a *receipt*." She shook her head. "No. I've been thinking about this wrong for six years. We all have. The Guild thinks of memory as property — you hold it, you spend it, you lose it. But that's not what Tam believed. He said we hold things *for each other*. Lending library for souls." She stood, and her legs were steadier than she expected. "So I'm not going to lose him. I'm going to *lend* him. To the crossing. To the current. Let the forgetting carry him for a while — and when this tower comes down, when the covenant is reforged and everything the Spire swallowed goes home, he'll come back to me. All of him. Even the parts I gave away. Because I'll have written it down."

She nodded at the map in Jonah's hands. At the living ink.

"You're the keeper of ledgers, Crane. So write it. Write it exactly: *Sefa Idris lends her brother Tam — his laugh, the stones on the Merrow, the lie she told him, and eleven more she won't itemize — to the passage between the fifth and sixth landings, to be returned in full upon the unmaking of the Spire.* Witnessed and countersigned." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she let it. "Because that's the difference between sacrifice and loss, isn't it? Loss is when nobody writes it down."

Jonah's eyes were wet. They uncapped the ink without a word, and wrote, and their handwriting shook, and Sefa loved them for it. Then they held out the ledger and the pen.

"Countersign," Jonah said. "It's your entry. Your hand belongs on it."

She signed. *Sefa Idris*, in her careful clerk's script, the same script she'd used to sign away his name six years ago — and this time the pen felt like a key instead of a knife.

* * *

The crossing took less than a minute and lasted a lifetime.

She stepped down into the dark with Tam's name held behind her teeth like a coal, and the forgetting rose around her, patient and total, and it *took*. She felt it happen — felt the stones on the Merrow go, the weight of them gone from her hand, the water-glint gone from her eye. Felt his laugh lift out of her chest like a bird leaving a branch, and oh, she chased it, she chased it down through years of memory and it slipped her grasp and was gone, and she was still falling, still being unwound—

—and she held the name.

*Tam. Tam Idris.*

The current pulled at it and could not move it, because it was not a memory anymore. It was a *debt*. Written, witnessed, countersigned, owed. The Spire could devour anything a person merely remembered. But a thing entered in a ledger, promised back, spoken aloud in the dark by a woman who refused to release it — that was not food. That was law.

The dark parted.

She came out the far side on her hands and knees on plain grey stone, gasping, and Jonah was there, hauling her up, saying her name over and over like a charm, and she pushed them back to arm's length and checked herself the way you check your pockets after a cutpurse works a crowd.

Her brother's face: gone. She reached for it and found smooth blankness, a door painted on a wall. His voice: gone. The stones, the Merrow, the lie, the pride, the shame — gone, gone, all of it, swept clean as a winter field.

But the name remained. *Tam Idris.* She turned it over in her mind and found it warm. Found it *occupied*, somehow — not an empty word but a full one, a vessel with cargo waiting at the far end of a voyage. Not a receipt.

A promise.

"Well?" Jonah asked softly.

"He's not smaller," she said, and was surprised to find she meant it, and surprised more to find she was smiling through the wreck of her crying. "All these years I thought remembering him was carrying him. But it was just — clutching. Hoarding. This—" she pressed her fist to her sternum, where the name sat like a banked fire "—this is lighter and heavier at the same time. I lent him. He's not lost. He's *out on loan*."

Jonah hugged her then, quick and fierce, ink and sweat and fear between them, and Sefa let it happen, because some alliances are made at tables over contracts and some are made in the dark under a tower that eats the world, and the second kind holds better.

"Come on," Jonah said, wiping their eyes with the heel of their hand, already turning to the map, already the cartographer again, because grief was a country they both knew how to walk and neither of them could afford to stand still in. "The exit shaft Maren showed me is two levels down and east. And Sefa — when we get out, when this is over, I'm going to help you collect. Every memory in that tower comes home. That's not a promise, it's an *entry in the ledger*."

"Countersigned?"

"Countersigned."

They went down together, and the stairs were only stairs, and the dark behind them stayed dark and empty, holding what it had been given, keeping it safe the way a river keeps a stone — not possessing it, only carrying it toward the sea.

They were one landing from the exit shaft when the alarm changed.

It had been a chord of names, vast and impersonal. Now it narrowed, sharpened, became a single voice threaded through the tower's bones — Vess Corvane's voice, but thinner now, frayed, almost pleading:

"*Jonah. Jonah Crane. You don't understand what you're taking from me. Come back and I'll show you — I'll show you everything your mother chose over you—*"

Sefa saw Jonah stop. Saw the map tremble in their hands.

And then, above them, through the stone, through the tower, through the whole ascending spine of the Spire, came a sound like a thousand pages turning at once — and every light in the stairwell went out.
