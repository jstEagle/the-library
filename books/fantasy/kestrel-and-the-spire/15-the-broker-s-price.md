## 15. The Broker's Price

The broker came at dusk, when the light through the ruined chapel's broken windows turned the dust to drifting gold, and Jonah was mending a hole in their coat with thread pulled from the hem of Sefa's spare cloak.

They knew before they looked up. The air in the chapel changed the way it did in the guildhall when someone opened a ledger that had been sealed — a pressure, a hush, the sense of something being *entered into record*. Jonah's needle stopped mid-stitch. Their hands had learned, over years of trade and inventory, to feel the moment a debt came due.

"You're early," Jonah said, without turning around.

"I'm never early." The broker's voice was exactly as Jonah remembered it: dry as old vellum, amused in the way of someone who has seen every kind of fool part with every kind of treasure. "Time-shares are paid on schedule. But schedules bend for emergencies. And you, cartographer, have become an emergency."

Jonah set down the needle and the coat and turned.

The broker stood in the chapel doorway as though standing in a drawing room. She wore the same grey traveling clothes from the market beneath the hills, the same silver chain with its hundred small keys, each key opening some drawer of unlived life somewhere in her inventory. Her face was pleasant and forgettable, which Jonah now understood was not a natural quality but a professional one — a face kept deliberately unmemorable so that clients would remember only what she sold them.

Behind her, the dusk gathered. Somewhere on the ridge above them, the Spire stood against the darkening sky like a needle pushed through the fabric of the world, and even from this distance Jonah could feel it — a faint pull behind the eyes, the sensation of memories leaning toward something, the way water leans toward a drain.

Sefa was out scouting the eastern approach with the last of Maren's fragments folded into oilcloth against her chest. Jonah was alone. They suspected the broker had known that.

"Is this about the year I traded?" Jonah asked. They kept their voice level. They were proud of how level it was. "I understood passage bought passage. I didn't expect change back."

"Change back." The broker smiled thinly and stepped inside, her boots leaving no prints in the chapel dust. Jonah noticed that. Filed it away. A person who left no prints was either very skilled or not entirely present, and either way worth watching. "No one has ever accused me of giving change back. I'm here because the Spire has called in all outstanding debts. Every future mortgaged within three hundred miles is being collected tonight. It seems your tower wants fuel, and it wants it *now*."

"The tower isn't mine."

"No," the broker agreed. "But your year is. Or rather — it was. It's been liquidated. Consolidated into the collection. Which means, by the terms of our trade—" she produced, from somewhere in her grey coat, a small ledger bound in leather the color of dried blood, "—you're entitled to witness the disposition of your asset. Standard practice. Most clients decline." She looked up, and her forgettable face held something that might, in a less careful woman, have been pity. "You strike me as a client who won't decline."

Jonah should have declined. Some part of them — the part that had spent years cataloguing bottled echoes and stolen rivers, the part that believed in careful ledgers and closed covers — knew that watching would be a wound they'd carry the rest of their life, however long or short that proved to be.

But another part of them, the part that had followed torn pages across unmapped country, the part that had found their mother's name written in living ink on a map of a tower that ate memory — that part needed to know. Needed it the way a starving person needs bread, without dignity, without negotiation.

"What did I give up?" Jonah asked quietly. "Exactly. You brokers deal in specifics. So give me the specific."

The broker opened the blood-colored ledger to a page marked with a ribbon, and read aloud, in the flat cadence of inventory:

"*One standard year, forward-dated, drawn from the account of Jonah Crane, keeper, Guild of Impossible Things. Contents: one household, coastal, two rooms above a chandlery. One spouse, unnamed at time of deposit — the client had not yet met them, but the client's heart had already furnished the rooms.*" She paused there, just slightly. "*One child, imagined in sufficient detail to constitute collateral. One garden, salt-tolerant. One dog, disreputable. Peace, ongoing. Duration: the whole of the client's remaining life, compressed into a single representative year for purposes of valuation.*"

The chapel was very quiet. The gold dust drifted.

"You saw all that?" Jonah said. Their voice came out strange. Smaller than they intended. "In my year?"

"I see everything in every year we hold." The broker closed the ledger gently, as one closes a door on a sleeping room. "It's how we price them. Yours was... unusually complete, for a client so young. Most people mortgage vague futures. Promotions. Inheritances. You had already hung pictures in your mind. Do you know how rare that is? Most people can't furnish a room they've never lived in. You'd built the whole house." She tilted her head. "The chandlery below smelled of beeswax and brine. Your child had a gap where her front tooth should be, and she was missing it because she'd climbed the garden wall to watch the kestrels and fallen off, and refused to cry about it."

Jonah's breath went out of them slowly, like air from a punctured thing.

She hadn't known. She hadn't known any of it — that they wanted that, that they had *built* that, secretly, in the locked room of themselves where keepers keep the things too tender for inventory. They had never written it down. Never spoken it. And yet it had been in them, whole and waiting, priced and filed, and now—

"Now," the broker said, and drew a second object from her coat.

It was a lantern. Small, brass, ordinary — except that the glass panes were not glass but something thinner, brighter, like the membrane of an egg held up to sunlight. Inside, faintly, something moved. Something warm.

"This is your year," she said. "Condensed for transport. The Spire will drink it tonight with all the others. But first — the terms of your trade entitle you to view the asset. Once. Fully. Would you like to?"

Every prudent instinct Jonah possessed said no.

"Yes," Jonah said.

* * *

The broker set the lantern on the chapel's cracked altar, struck no match, spoke no word — simply turned the little brass key that hung nearest her heart among the hundred, and the membrane-panes bloomed with light.

And the chapel fell away.

Not literally — Jonah knew, distantly, that they still stood on cold stone in a ruined chapel in unmapped country with the Spire's hunger pressing at the edge of the sky. But the light took them the way living ink takes a mapmaker, and they were *elsewhere*, and it was not memory, because Jonah had never lived it. It was the future. The unlived year. Playing out around them in colors more vivid than anything remembered has a right to be, because nothing remembered has ever been this clean.

Morning, in a room above a chandlery. Two rooms, low-beamed, whitewashed, a window that caught the sea. The smell of beeswax and brine rising through the floorboards exactly as the broker had said, and under it the smell of bread, because someone downstairs was baking and the heat carried it up through the joists. Jonah stood in the doorway of the bedroom — their own body, older, softer around the eyes, hair going grey at the temples in a way that suited them — and watched themselves sleep.

Beside them slept a stranger Jonah did not know and recognized instantly. Dark-haired, broad-shouldered, a faded scar along one forearm, hands that were calloused in the pattern of someone who worked rope or rigging or nets. Their spouse. The one whose name had been *unnamed at time of deposit*. Jonah looked at that sleeping face and felt a grief so specific it was almost physical — grief for a person who did not exist, who had never existed, whom they had somehow loved anyway, in advance, in secret, in the locked room.

*I made you up,* Jonah thought, standing over the bed. *I made you up out of nothing but wanting, and you're the truest thing I've ever seen.*

The scene moved the way dreams move but with none of a dream's fog. Morning: bread and honey, the dark-haired stranger laughing at something, catching Jonah's older self around the waist, flour on both their faces. Then the garden — salt-tolerant, wind-bent rosemary and sea-pink and one stubborn fig tree against the south wall — and the child.

The child came over the wall the way the broker had said, scraped and triumphant, six years old maybe, with Jonah's own dark eyes and a gap where her front tooth should be, holding a fledgling kestrel cupped in both hands with the enormous care of the very young.

"It fell," she announced. "Out of the nest on the point. It's fine. I'm fine. Nobody has to tell Da."

Older-Jonah knelt in the dirt and looked at the bird and then at the girl, and the look between them was so full, so *known*, that Jonah-the-watcher had to put a hand against the garden wall to stay upright. This was the love. This was the whole of it, distilled: a scraped knee, a wild bird, a parent kneeling in the dirt choosing, again and always, to be exactly here.

The year unfolded. Not every day — the important ones. A storm that kept them all indoors playing cards by lamplight while the sea threw itself against the harbor wall. The dog — disreputable, enormous, half wolf by the look of him — stealing an entire ham and lying in the middle of the kitchen wearing an expression of magnificent innocence. The child's first day at the village school, walking away down the lane with her spine straight and her chin up, refusing to look back, and older-Jonah standing in the doorway with a hand pressed flat over their heart as if holding something shut that wanted to burst out and follow her.

And once — only once — the vision showed Jonah something else. Older-Jonah alone at night, at a desk by the window, drawing. Maps. Careful, patient maps of the coast, of the harbor, of the kestrels' cliff on the point. But these maps were different from guild work. No borders claimed. No territories marked. Just the world, drawn lovingly, *kept* — and in the corner of each one, small and neat, a signature that was not a signature but a dedication: *For the house above the chandlery.*

Peace, ongoing. Duration: the whole of the remaining life.

Jonah understood, watching, why the broker had offered the viewing. Not cruelty. Precision. This was what a year cost. This was the exchange rate. And some deep bookkeeping part of them — the part that had spent a lifetime keeping honest ledgers — insisted on looking at every entry, even the ones that burned.

Especially those.

* * *

The last scene was evening. Autumn, by the leaves. The garden gold and going brown. The child — taller now, eight perhaps, the gap-tooth grown in — sitting on the kitchen step with the kestrel, fully fledged and free, perched on her wrist by mutual agreement rather than training. The dark-haired spouse leaned in the doorway. The dog snored. The sea did what the sea does, endlessly, beyond the wall.

Older-Jonah sat down on the step beside the girl and said something too quiet for the vision to carry, and the girl laughed, and the kestrel shifted its feet, and the light went amber and long and gentle across everything, and the vision held there — held, and held, the way a hand holds yours at the end of something —

— and began to burn.

There was no other word for it. The edges of the world went brown first, like paper near a flame. The amber light curdled to orange, to red. The garden curled. The sea hissed into steam. The child looked up directly at Jonah — directly, impossibly, her dark eyes finding theirs across the impossible distance between the living and the unlived — and did not cry, because she was made from Jonah's own imagining and Jonah had imagined her brave.

Then she was ash, and the ash was sparks, and the sparks went up the chimney of the lantern and out into the dark above the ruined chapel, where the Spire waited, and Jonah felt them go. Felt the year leave the world. Felt the specific gravity of it — the bread-smell, the rope-calloused hands, the gap-toothed grin, the peace, ongoing — drain out of possibility forever, drawn north and up, into the walls of a tower built from everyone's everything.

The lantern glass went dark. An ordinary lantern. Empty. Worthless.

Jonah stood in the ruined chapel with their hand still pressed flat over their heart, holding shut the thing that wanted to burst out, and found there was nothing left inside to hold.

* * *

"Well," said the broker softly.

Jonah did not turn. "Get out."

"I'll leave the ledger copy. Terms require—"

"*Get out.*"

A pause. Then, unexpectedly, gently: "Keeper Crane. Look at me."

Jonah looked. The broker's forgettable face had settled into something realer — tired, old, human. She held out the empty lantern.

"Burn this," she said. "Or keep it. Clients do both. But hear me first, because I've watched ten thousand years burn and I'll tell you what I told the last one who stood where you're standing: the Spire didn't take that from you tonight. *You* gave it. Months ago, at my market, freely, for passage toward your mother. Everything since — the road, the Blank, the archive, the woman in the wall — grew from that trade. That child existed because you chose her over safety. She burned because you kept walking." The broker's voice dropped. "Grief is the receipt. Don't you dare misfile it."

She set the lantern on the altar beside the ledger copy, and walked out into the dusk without prints, and was gone.

Jonah stood a long time. The stars were coming out overhead — the ones that remained, the wounded remnant of the sky — and far to the north the Spire glowed faintly, fatter, brighter, fed.

When Sefa returned after midnight, she found Jonah sitting on the chapel steps with the empty lantern in their lap, cleaned of ash, polished, its little brass door open. Inside, folded small, lay a scrap of parchment in Jonah's own handwriting:

*A household, coastal. A spouse with rope-scarred hands. A daughter who climbs walls for kestrels. Salt-tolerant rosemary. Peace, ongoing. Recorded so that it cannot be unwritten, and cannot be forgotten by the one who paid for it.*

Sefa sat down beside them without asking. After a while she said, "What did it cost?"

"A year," Jonah said. "The wrong year. The right one." They closed the lantern's little door. "Sefa. When we go up that tower tomorrow — I'm not climbing it to save the realms anymore. I understand Vess now. I understand the temptation from the inside. I'm climbing it so that no one else ever has to stand at a market and buy their way toward the people they love with pieces of the people they'll become."

Sefa was quiet a moment. "That's a worse reason," she said. "It's also a better one."

Far to the north, the Spire pulsed — once, slow, like a heartbeat, like a swallow. And in the chapel behind them, on the blood-colored ledger the broker had left behind, fresh ink was seeping up through the page unbidden, writing itself in a hand none of them recognized:

*Collection complete. All assets consolidated. The tower is nearly full. Come and see.*
