## 1. The Ink That Bled

The trouble with mapping a place you have loved your whole life, Noor Nakamura thought, wedging her boot into a crevice of the eastern ridge, is that you stop seeing it. You stop seeing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, or your own name, or the particular way the wind comes down off the Kestrel Stones smelling faintly of iron and old rain.

She had been surveying the ridge for six days. Six days of triangulation points and breakfasts of cold millet cakes and evenings spent arguing with her mule, Bramble, who had opinions about elevation that Noor found frankly unscientific.

"Higher," she told him now, from halfway up a scree slope. "You're a pack animal. Ambition is part of the job description."

Bramble flicked one ear, which was his way of filing a formal complaint.

The eastern ridge was the last unmapped corner of the Hollow Valley — not truly unmapped, of course; Noor had drawn it a dozen times since childhood, first in her mother's careful hand copied out badly, later in a style entirely her own, all fine lines and patient hatching. But her atlas of the valley was eleven years old in its eastern sheets, and rivers, even dead rivers, even *magic-dead* rivers, had the discourtesy to move.

That was the whole point of the valley, after all. That was the promise stitched into its founding and repeated in every schoolroom from Ashford to the Millwright's Bend: *nothing changes here.* No miracles. No hauntings. No god's finger reaching down to redirect a river out of spite. The Hollow Valley was the one place in the world where a map, once drawn, stayed true. That was why Noor had given her life to it. That was why, if she was honest — and she tried to be honest only in the privacy of her own skull — she had never once left it.

She reached the crest by midmorning, unrolled her field sheets against a boulder, and began to work. And it was not until the sun stood high and her ink had gone the consistency of honey that she noticed the river.

The Sere, which drained the eastern moors, was drawn on her sheet in a long, lazy curve — a curve she had verified personally, on foot, twice. On the paper, the curve was *moving*. Not the ink. The ink sat where it sat. But the line of the river beneath it, the truth of it, had shifted a finger's width west, and her drawing now sat beside it like a guest who had arrived at the wrong house.

Noor stared at it for a long moment. Then she did what any sensible cartographer would do: she blamed the paper.

She pulled a fresh sheet from her satchel, weighted it with stones, and redrew the Sere from her own memory, which was flawless and which she was prepared to defend in any court in the valley. The new line matched the old line exactly.

By late afternoon, the new line had drifted too.

* * *

She did not sleep well that night, which was unusual; Noor could sleep on scree, in rain, and once, memorably, standing up against Bramble during a hailstorm. But she lay in her lean-to listening to the valley breathe and thinking about the river, and around midnight she gave up, lit her lamp, and took out the finished maps — the good ones, the fair copies she'd spent the spring inking at her desk in Ashford, the ones that were supposed to be *done*.

The Sere had moved on all of them. A full mile west, in the oldest sheet, and the ink there had gone strange — not faded, not smeared, but *wet*, beading along the riverline as though the paper had begun, quietly, to bleed.

She checked the other sheets with hands that were almost steady. The North Road, which had run straight as a sermon for as long as anyone remembered, now showed a branch she had never drawn, peeling off toward the Kestrel Stones and fading into the margin like a path someone had started to walk and thought better of. A village she had mapped as four houses now showed five. In the corner of the western sheet, in a hand that was not hers — smaller, older, slanting left — someone had written a single word in ink the color of dried blood:

*Soon.*

Noor sat back on her heels. Outside, the valley lay silent under the stars, every stone exactly where it had always been, every boundary holding, holding, holding.

"Right," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "Either I've gone mad, which is possible, or the valley has, which is worse, or—" She stopped. There wasn't an *or*. She'd been saving up *or*s her whole life and now, at the worst possible moment, she was out of them.

* * *

She came down off the ridge two days early, driving Bramble hard, and it was on the moor road below the Kestrel Stones that she found the pilgrim.

He was lying in the heather at the exact spot where the valley's border ran — not a wall, not a fence, just a line Noor knew the way other people knew their own pulse, a line her grandmother's generation had walked and blessed and *sealed*, though the schoolbooks were vague on what the sealing had cost. The man had crawled the last stretch. His feet were ruined. His coat had come from a country Noor knew only as shapes on other people's maps, and his lips were cracked, and when he saw her he laughed — a small, terrible sound, like a hinge in an empty house.

"The cartographer's daughter," he said.

Noor crouched, keeping her weight on her heels, the way you did with injured strangers and strange dogs. "I'm the cartographer," she said. "No daughter. Just me. Can you walk? There's a farmstead a mile on, they'll have water."

He caught her wrist. His grip was nothing, a bird's grip, and still she did not pull away.

"Answering," he whispered. "They're answering again. Forty years of silence and now — everywhere, everywhere, prayers going *through*." His eyes were wet and bright and full of something that was not quite hope and not quite terror, some alloy of the two that Noor had no name for. "I walked a year to find this valley. The only place they can't hear. The only place a person can be *unheard*." He laughed again, and this time it broke in the middle. "And they sent me here anyway. They sent me here to tell you. They want the cartographer's daughter."

"I told you," Noor said, and her voice came out thinner than she intended. "There's no daughter."

The pilgrim smiled. It was the kindest and worst thing she had ever seen a dying man do.

"There's always a daughter," he said. "There's a daughter, or a son, or a grandchild. The debt doesn't care what you call yourself. The debt comes down the blood like water down a hill." His hand tightened, once. "She broke it, you know. Your grandmother. Everyone thinks she sealed the valley to keep the gods out." His breath rattled. "She sealed it to keep *them* in the dark. To keep them starving and blind and furious. And now the veil is thin, and they remember, and they are asking for her by name."

The wind came down off the Kestrel Stones, smelling of iron and old rain, and for the first time in her life Noor heard something in it — a low, vast attention, like an eye opening.

"Her name," Noor said. Her mouth had gone dry. "What name are they asking for?"

The pilgrim looked at her with enormous pity.

"Yours," he said. "They're asking for yours."

He died an hour later, at the farmstead, with water on his lips and a priestess from Ashford reading the quiet rites, and Noor sat outside in the yard with her satchel of bleeding maps and did not go in.

* * *

That night, in her own house for the first time in a week, Noor unrolled every sheet she owned across the floor until she stood in a paper country of her own making. The ink had dried to brown. The phantom road to the Kestrel Stones had darkened, grown certain, as if it had been walked by many feet. And in the corner of the western sheet, beneath the word *Soon*, new writing was surfacing — slowly, the way a body surfaces in a pond — in that same small, slanting, unfamiliar hand.

Noor knelt and read it, and the floor seemed to tilt beneath her knees, because the hand was unfamiliar and the words were not; they were the words her mother used to say over her cradle, words Noor had never written down for anyone, never spoken aloud since she was six years old:

*Come and find me, little mapmaker. The borders are opening.*

Noor sat in the middle of her paper country for a long time. Then she got up, found her traveling satchel, the good one, the one she had never once used, and began — with the steady hands of a woman doing something entirely reasonable — to pack.

Bramble, watching from the yard, filed a formal complaint.
