## 2. What the Valley Forgot

The pilgrim died at dawn, which was inconvenient for everyone involved.

Noor had buried him herself, in the thin soil behind the surveyor's hut, because the valley's one gravedigger was eighty and believed firmly that strangers who arrived bleeding were the earth's business, not his. She said a few words over the mound. She wasn't sure what faith he'd held, so she covered the bases: something about rest, something about rivers, something about being answered. That last one made her throat tighten, because it was what he'd kept whispering as the light went out of him — *they're answering again, they're answering* — with his cracked hand pressed flat against her map of the eastern ridge as though the paper were a door.

She had not slept. Instead she'd done what she always did when the world misbehaved: she laid out every map she owned on the floor of her workroom and looked for the lie.

There were forty-one of them. Forty-one years of surveys compressed into vellum and ink, the whole Hollow Valley rendered in her careful hand — every stream, every sheep track, every boundary stone her grandmother had supposedly placed "for tidiness." And now, in the gray light coming through the shutters, Noor could see that the maps were wrong. Not damaged. *Wrong.* The River Sallow on the 12-year survey ran where no river ran anymore. A road appeared on the northern sheet that she had never drawn, never walked, never seen — and yet its lines were hers. Her own hand. Her own ink, gone slightly brown with age, as if she'd drawn it decades ago.

"No," she told the maps conversationally. "Absolutely not."

The maps declined to comment. But when she leaned close to the northern sheet, she noticed the road's margin bore a tiny annotation in handwriting she did recognize — a spidery, confident script she'd spent her childhood imitating:

*Do not let them find the third piece.*

Her grandmother's handwriting. On a map Noor had drawn herself, in a year her grandmother had been eleven years dead.

Noor sat down on the floor among her life's work and laughed, once, the way you laugh when a floorboard gives way beneath you. Then she got up, because there was exactly one place in the valley where answers lived, and it was the Founders' Archive under the meetinghouse, and it opened at eight.

* * *

The archive smelled of dust, tallow, and the particular stubbornness of people who write things down so they don't have to remember them. Noor had been in it perhaps six times in her life; the archivist, Master Odo, guarded it the way a hen guards an egg she suspects is rotten.

"Cartography section," she said. "Covenants, borders, founding-era documents."

Odo peered at her over his spectacles. "Founding-era? Nobody reads founding-era. It's all sermons and seed inventories."

"Then you won't miss it."

He let her in — partly because she was the Nakamura girl, and the Nakamura name still carried weight in the valley like a coin nobody could quite spend, and partly because he wanted his lunch. She heard him shuffling off upstairs, muttering about pickled herring, and turned to the shelves.

The records were worse than sermons. Three hours in, her fingers gray with dust, she had learned the valley's founders had strong opinions about drainage and weak ones about everything else. But the ledgers were signed, all of them, by the same nine names — and one name appeared more than the others, in that spidery, confident hand:

*M. Nakamura, Cartographer-General.*

Her grandmother. Whom Noor had known only as a smell of cedar oil and a voice saying *measure twice, love, the world lies once.*

And then, in the bottom of the last drawer, wrapped in oilcloth against nothing in particular, she found the thing the drawer had clearly been built to hide: a journal, and inside the journal, a drawing.

It was not a map. Or rather, it was a map the way a wound is a kind of geography. Circles within circles, lines crossing lines, and at the center three broken shapes — shards, she thought, pieces of something shattered — each marked with a symbol she didn't know and a word in a language she almost did. Beneath it, her grandmother's writing, cramped and fast:

*The covenant is not a wall. I told them it was a wall because they needed walls. It is a promise, and promises have two ends. I broke it into three so that no one hand could hold it whole — not theirs, not mine, not the gods'. One sleeps in water. One I traded for a lie. The third I hid where no god would think to look: in a living memory, freely given. If you are reading this, then the veil is failing, and I am sorry, and you are almost certainly my blood.*

*Forgive me. I did it to keep you safe. Safety is just a cage someone else designed, but I built it anyway, because I loved you before you existed.*

Noor read it four times. On the fourth reading, her hands had stopped shaking enough to notice the last line, added later, in ink of a different color — an ink that shimmered faintly, the way the pilgrim's blood had shimmered on her map:

*He knows I'm dead. He will come looking for what I took. Be kinder to him than I was.*

* * *

That night, the voice came back.

It had started two nights ago — a low sound like wind through a keyhole, arriving not through her ears but through the maps, rising off the paper the way heat rises off stone. She had told herself it was exhaustion. Exhaustion, however, did not usually address her by name.

*Nakamura.*

"I'm ignoring you," Noor said to the empty workroom, quill poised over a fresh sheet. Ignoring things was a professional skill. You could not map a haunted ridge if every rustle of grass made you flinch.

*You are drawing my prison,* the voice observed. It sounded tired the way old iron is tired — not weak, just heavy. *Every line you lay down, I feel it. Your grandmother drew this valley onto my skin. Do you know what that is like, little cartographer? To be written over?*

"I imagine it's like having your family's secrets published," Noor said. "I'm experiencing something adjacent."

A pause. Then, unexpectedly, something that might have been a laugh — a dry, grinding sound, like a millstone remembering how to turn.

*You have her mouth. Insolent, both of you.*

"You knew my grandmother."

*I knew her the way a debtor knows a creditor.* The voice darkened. *She came to me when the realms were tearing at each other's seams. Magic here was a flood; magic there was a famine. She promised balance. Then she took the covenant — the living law we forged together — and broke it into three, and buried the pieces, and sealed your valley into silence, and called it peace. For sixty years I have starved on the far side of her wall while your side forgot I exist. Prayers fall on deaf air. Crops fail. My realm eats itself. And now the wall is cracking, and what leaks through leaks *sideways*, wild and hungry, and your dying pilgrims are only the first of it.*

Noor set down her quill. "What do you want?"

*What I have always wanted. What was promised. Reforge the covenant. Find my three pieces and make us whole again — or watch both realms starve of each other, slowly, politely, while everyone insists it isn't happening.* A pause, and when the voice resumed it was quieter, almost gentle, which was somehow worse. *I will be honest with you, since your grandmother was not honest with me. The third piece is the worst of the three. It lives in a memory. Freely given, or not at all.*

"What memory?"

*Yours. Whoever carries it. And you carry one that glows like a coal in the dark, little cartographer. I can see it from here.*

Noor looked at her hands. Ink-stained, steady, her grandmother's hands almost exactly. She thought of her mother — of laughing in a kitchen, of flour on a cheek, of a face she had never once needed to describe because it was simply *there*, the way north is there, the fixed point every other direction quietly agreed upon.

"Not tonight," she said.

*Not tonight,* the god agreed. *But soon. The wall has maybe a season left in it. Less, if it rains.*

* * *

She packed at first light, which took longer than expected, mostly because packing for a journey out of reality's good graces is not covered by any standard satchel inventory. In the end: her good pens, three bottles of ink, the blank atlas she'd been saving for something important, dried fish, a flint, her grandmother's journal, and — after a long moment — the 41-year map of the valley, folded small. If she was going to walk out of the only safe place she'd ever known, she wanted proof of it in her pocket.

Master Odo caught her at the meetinghouse steps, herring-breathed and suspicious. "Going somewhere, Nakamura?"

"Surveying," Noor said. "Beyond the ridge."

"Nothing's beyond the ridge."

"That's rather the point. Someone should map it." She smiled at him, and he blinked, and by the time he'd assembled a rebuttal she was already walking.

The border stones stood where they had always stood, gray and unremarkable, along the eastern fold of the valley. Her grandmother's "tidiness." Noor stopped before the nearest one and put her palm against it. It hummed, very faintly, like a struck bell long after anyone remembers ringing it.

*Last chance to stay,* said the voice in her bones, not unkindly. *The cage is comfortable. Cages usually are.*

"My grandmother wrote that safety is a cage someone else designed," Noor said aloud. "Well. I'm redesigning it."

She stepped past the stone.

The world on the other side was green and wrong — grass too bright, sky too deep, the light itself seeming to lean toward her with interest. Magic hung in the air like weather. Somewhere ahead, water was calling itself a shrine, and a drowned god's first fragment waited at the bottom of it.

Noor opened her atlas to the first blank page, uncapped her pen, and began, in her careful hand, at the very top:

*Realm Beyond the Valley — surveyed by N. Nakamura, last heir of M. Nakamura, who owes everybody an apology.*

*Day one. Everything is trying to kill me. Drawing boundaries anyway.*
