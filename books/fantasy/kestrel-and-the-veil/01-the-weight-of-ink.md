## 1. The Weight of Ink

The thunder arrived on a Tuesday, which Paloma Ashgrove considered typical. Impossible things never came on days when a man had time for them.

He sat in the Receiving Room of the Ledger Guild of Vess with a quill behind his ear, three more in the pot, and a crate of bottled weather open at his elbow. The bottles were dark green glass, each stoppered with wax the color of a bruise, and inside them the storm turned over and over like something dreaming. When he lifted one to the light, it grumbled against his palm. Not loud. Just a low complaint, the sound a sky makes before it decides.

"Item forty-one," he said, and wrote: *Bottled thunder, provenance unknown, seven vessels, contents volatile. Do not shelve near mirrors.*

The last instruction was not superstition. He had seen what happened to mirrors.

Around him the Receiving Room did its slow work. Clerks moved between trestle tables with armloads of manifest sheets, their shoes whispering on floorboards worn into shallow valleys by two hundred years of exactly this. The Guild of Vess took in everything the world produced that it could not explain, and it explained none of it. It only recorded. Weight, count, condition, provenance where provenance existed — which was rarely — and then it shelved the thing in the deep stacks below, where the impossible gathered dust alongside tax rolls from dead harbors and the correspondence of merchants no one remembered.

Paloma had been doing this for eleven years. He was very good at it. That was the whole of his ambition now, though once — he could barely locate the memory, it was so far back, like a room in a house he'd moved out of before he could walk — once he had wanted to write something true.

He wrote truths all day now. They just weren't his.

"Forty-two," said the porter, hauling the next crate onto his table and cracking its lid with a pry-bar. "Careful with this one, scribe. It followed me up the stairs."

"They all follow you up the stairs," Paloma said. "It's the stairs."

"No." The porter, a broad woman named Doss with forearms like mooring rope, wiped her face and stepped back a deliberate pace. "This one *followed* me."

Inside the crate, packed in straw, lay a shadow. It was not attached to anything. It lay flat against the straw the way shadows lie against walls, but it was shaped like a person — a small person, narrow-shouldered, hands folded — and it was darker than the darkness around it, the way ink is darker than night. As Paloma leaned over it, it shifted. Turned, if a shadow can be said to turn, to keep its face away from him.

"Widow's second shadow," Doss read off the tag. "Surrendered by the widow herself, gods rest her. Said she couldn't afford to feed it anymore."

Paloma picked up his quill. His hand was steady. Eleven years will do that; eleven years will make a man's hand steady through almost anything.

"Item forty-two," he said. "*Second shadow, human origin, detached. Condition: grieving.*"

"It's got a condition?"

"Everything has a condition." He wrote a little more, because it was true and because the ledger deserved truth even when the world didn't bother with it: *Detached shadows retain the habits of their grief. Recommend isolation shelving. Recommend no lamps.*

Doss watched him write. "You always talk to 'em like they're listening."

"They're always listening. That's most of what they are." He set down the quill. "Shelve it in the west annex, third gallery, next to the weeping hourglass. They'll keep each other company."

"That's not how shadows work, scribe."

"No," Paloma agreed. "But it's how widows work."

She grunted, resealing the crate, and carried it off toward the stair that went down instead of up. Paloma watched her go, then pulled the audit ledger toward him and did the thing he did at the top of every hour, the thing that had become as involuntary as breathing: he calculated.

Eleven years served. His indenture had been signed at nineteen, fifteen years against a debt of four hundred crowns — his mother's debts, inherited, plus the fee the Guild charged for the trouble of inheriting them. Interest compounded quarterly. Wages credited against principal at a rate the Guild called generous and arithmetic called insulting. He had done the sums so many times he could do them in his head while cataloguing a haunted mirror, and he did them now, lips moving slightly, quill tapping:

Nine years remained. Nine years, less whatever the winter bonus came to, more whatever the infirmary charged him for the cough he'd been carrying since spring. If nothing went wrong. Nothing ever went wrong, in the Guild; that was nearly the definition of the place. Nothing went wrong, and nine years passed, and Paloma Ashgrove walked out of these doors at thirty-five with nothing owed and nothing owned and the best working years of his life pressed flat between ledger covers like a flower nobody had wanted.

He found, examining the feeling the way he examined inventory, that he did not mind. That was the trick of it. That was the thing he'd learned in year three, after the escape attempt, after the penalty years were added: ledgers were safer than lives. A ledger balanced. A ledger forgave nothing but forgot nothing either, and there was a strange mercy in that. People lied, left, died, changed the shapes of themselves until you couldn't find the person you'd known anywhere in them. Ink stayed where you put it. If you kept your head down and your columns straight, the world would pass over you like weather over stone, and one day you'd walk out unscratched, unloved, and free.

He believed this completely. It is worth recording that he believed it completely, because belief of that kind is a load-bearing wall, and later, much later, walls would come out of it.

* * *

The afternoon brought the mirror.

It came up from receiving wrapped in three blankets and a chain, and the clerk who escorted it would not touch it directly, so Paloma ended up unwrapping it himself, which was how he preferred things anyway. Other people's caution made him nervous. His own caution he could manage.

The mirror was oval, silvered, old. Its frame was carved with vines whose leaves had been picked out in gold so fine it looked like pollen. And its surface did not show the Receiving Room. It showed the Receiving Room as it had been some sixty years ago — he knew this because a brass plaque screwed to the frame read *PROPERTY OF THE GUILD, RECOVERED FROM THE HARROW ESTATE, 1143* — and in the reflected past, a young woman in Guild gray stood at a table cataloguing something, glancing up every few moments as though she could feel him watching.

She could. They always could. That was the trouble with the Harrow mirrors.

"Item fifty-eight," Paloma said quietly. "Mirror, retrospective. Depth approximately six decades. Subject within reflection: aware." He hesitated, quill hovering. Then, in the margin, in the small cramped hand he used for notes he wasn't supposed to make: *She looks tired. Tell someone to check whether reflections sleep.*

He had written variations of that note for years. No one ever checked. The Guild did not consider the contents of its impossible inventory to be *inhabitants*. Contents were contents. You weighed them, counted them, shelved them, and if a bottle of thunder rattled at night, you noted the rattle in the maintenance log and moved along.

Paloma worked until the lamps were lit, and the lamps being lit meant the day shift draining out through the front doors and the night shift coming up from the dormitories, yawning, and the great bronze doors of the Guild of Vess being barred for the evening — first the bolt, then the chain, then the word, spoken by the door-warden in the flat ceremonial voice that drained the words of meaning through sheer repetition: *Closed. Closed. Closed.*

Paloma stayed late. He always stayed late. It was not devotion; it was strategy. Every hour worked past the bell was an hour credited at time-and-a-half, and time-and-a-half, compounded across a year, shaved weeks off the far end of nine years. He had a whole private mathematics of hours. He could tell you to the day what an evening was worth.

So he was alone in the Receiving Room at half past ten, trimming a lamp, when he noticed the oldest ledger.

It sat on its lectern in the corner where it had sat for as long as anyone could remember — the Founders' Ledger, they called it, though no founder's name appeared in it. It was the guild's first book, its charter and its master record both, and it was kept out not for use but for reverence, the way churches keep bones. Nobody wrote in it. Nobody had written in it for a century and a half. Its pages were vellum gone the color of weak tea, and its script was a hand so old that the modern alphabet was only a rumor of it.

The pages were moving.

Not fluttering — the windows were shut, the lamps still. Moving the way a sleeping animal moves: a slow stir, a settling, a stir again. Page turning toward page. The whole book seemed to be leaning, very slightly, toward the night door.

Paloma stood with the lamp snuffer in his hand and told himself the obvious things. Drafts. Old bindings settle. Vellum remembers humidity. He was a man who trusted explanations the way other men trusted rope, and he reached for them now, hand over hand.

Then the knocking started.

Three blows on the night door, down at the bottom of the receiving stair. A pause. Three more. Small blows. Fists, not a knocker — fists too small to reach the iron ring, striking the wood itself.

And every page of the Founders' Ledger rose at once, all together, like a flock startled off water, and hung trembling in the lamplight, and turned — every leaf, every tea-colored page — toward the sound.

Paloma Ashgrove had spent eleven years learning what fear looked like in objects. He knew how cursed things trembled and how haunted things waited and how the truly dangerous items did nothing at all, which was worse. He had never seen a book *answer*.

The knocking came again. Three blows. Pause. Three.

And beneath it, faintly, muffled by oak and distance, a voice — a child's voice, saying the same few syllables over and over, a phrase, a question, a plea in a language Paloma did not know and had never heard and yet which the oldest ledger in Vess clearly understood, because it strained toward the door like a dog hearing its name.

Paloma set down the lamp snuffer.

Later — much later, when he understood what he was walking toward — he would try to remember whether he had hesitated. He liked to think he had. He liked to think some part of him, the part that believed in ledgers and straight columns and the safety of keeping one's head down, had stood in that lamplit room and argued sensibly for going to bed.

If it did, he never found it again. He crossed the Receiving Room, took the ring of night keys off their hook, and went down the stair toward the knocking, while behind him the Founders' Ledger turned its pages one by one, following.
