## 2. A Knock in the Old Tongue

The night door of the Ledger Guild of Vess had three locks, and Paloma Ashgrove knew the sound of each one the way a mother knows her children's footsteps. The first was iron, heavy and honest. The second was brass, and it stuck in damp weather. The third was bone-white and older than the guild itself, and nobody — not Corvane, not the archivists, not the oldest ledger-keeper with his milky eyes and his forty years of service — knew what metal it was or who had forged it.

It was that third lock that spoke to him now.

Not literally. He wanted to be clear about that, even alone with his own thoughts at half past the dead hour, because clarity was the one thing a scribe could afford. It *hummed*. A low note, felt in the teeth rather than heard, like a tuning fork pressed against the jaw. In six years of night shifts, Paloma had heard it twice. Both times, something in the deep archive had been disturbed. Both times, he'd written an incident report, filed it under Anomalies, Subsection Nine, and told himself that impossible things were simply inventory he hadn't been assigned to count yet.

The humming stopped. Then came the knock.

Three strikes, uneven, small. Not the fist of a drunk or the boot of a watchman. Knuckles, maybe. Or the flat of a child's hand, too tired to make a proper fist of it.

Paloma set down his pen. On the desk before him lay the night's work: a manifest of incoming acquisitions, each item described in his own careful hand. *One jar, sealed, containing thunder (approx. 3 seconds' duration). One shadow, female, widowed, docile.* The guild bought such things from desperate people and sold them to worse ones, and Paloma recorded the transactions because recording was what he did, because ink on a page was at least *true*, which was more than could be said for most of what passed through Vess after dark.

He took up the lamp and went to the door.

Through the grate, by lamplight, he saw mud. Mud first, because there was so much of it — caked grey-brown, dried in ridges, as if the boy outside had waded through clay up to his chest. Under the mud: a boy, perhaps eight years old, hollow-cheeked, lips cracked, eyes so dark they seemed to hold no reflection at all. His clothes were wrong for Vess. Wrong cut, wrong weave, wrong everything — a tunic of some rough cloth dyed a blue so deep it was nearly black, stitched with patterns Paloma's eye kept sliding off of, the way your tongue keeps finding the gap where a tooth used to be.

"Guild business is concluded until dawn," Paloma said through the grate. Standard words. He'd said them a hundred times.

The boy answered him.

The sounds were nothing Paloma knew. Not Low Vessian, not High, not the merchant pidgin, not the river-tongue of the bargemen, not the clipped syllables of the northern trade cant that he'd picked up cataloguing imports. This language moved differently — it rose where languages fell, held vowels long past comfort, and somewhere inside every sentence was a sound like wind moving through a stone corridor, a sound that seemed to come from behind the boy's ribs rather than his throat.

"I don't understand you," Paloma said.

The boy said it again, slower, and this time Paloma caught the shape of desperation underneath the strangeness — the universal grammar of someone asking for help. The boy pressed both palms flat against the grate. His hands were shaking.

Behind Paloma, in the dark of the entry hall, a book turned its own page.

He didn't turn around. Turning around would have made it real, and he needed another moment of unreality, just one, to finish being afraid in private. But the sound came again — the soft dry whisper of vellum against vellum — and then again, faster, a fluttering like birds trapped in a room, and Paloma made himself pivot with the lamp raised.

The reading stand by the wall held the guild's oldest ledger. That was all anyone called it: *the oldest ledger*. It predated the guild's founding, had been found in the building when the building was bought, and no scribe was permitted to touch it without a Guildmaster's seal. Its cover was grey leather gone smooth as river stone. For six years it had sat on that stand, inert as any book, and Paloma had dusted around it with the particular reverence of a man dusting around a sleeping dog.

Now its pages were turning themselves, back and forth, back and forth, like something breathing hard. And every time the boy outside spoke, the pages stopped — paused on some spread Paloma couldn't read from this distance — then resumed their restless searching.

"Stop that," Paloma told the book, and hated how thin his voice sounded.

The boy knocked again. Three strikes, weaker now.

Paloma looked at the door. At the three locks. At the rules, which were explicit: admit no one between dusk and audit; report all anomalies; above all, above everything, *the ledgers are safer than lives* — that was the first maxim taught to every indentured scribe, drilled into them before they learned to cut a quill. Ledgers outlast lives. Ledgers don't lie. Ledgers don't drag you into alleys or sell you to buyers or leave you in a ditch with your debt unpaid and your name struck from the rolls.

But the boy's voice cracked in the middle of whatever he was saying, and the oldest ledger went still, open, waiting, and Paloma thought: *a book doesn't knock.*

A book doesn't walk through the mud to find you.

He threw the iron lock. The brass stuck; he cursed it loose. The bone-white lock he touched last, and it was warm. Warm as skin. It turned without resistance, the way it never did, and Paloma decided — filing it, already, under Anomalies, Subsection Nine — that he would think about that later, if there was a later.

The night air came in smelling of rain and river and old clay. The boy swayed on the step. Up close, the mud on him wasn't mud. It was finer than mud, greyer, almost powdery, and it smelled faintly of chalk and ash, like the ruins of something burned a very long time ago. His dark eyes fixed on Paloma's face with an intensity that made the scribe step back.

Then the boy looked past him, into the hall, at the open ledger on its stand, and his whole body changed. The exhaustion fell away like a coat dropped from shoulders. He said one word — one long, rising word full of that corridor-wind sound — and crossed the threshold without waiting for permission.

"Hey — you can't—" Paloma reached for him.

The boy was fast. He slipped past, bare feet slapping stone, and ran to the reading stand, and put both muddy hands on the oldest ledger's open pages, and began to read aloud.

That was the only way Paloma could describe it. The script on those pages was nothing — he'd glimpsed it once, years ago, a dense unbroken text in no alphabet used by any living nation, and the guild's scholars had pronounced it decorative, ornamental, meaningless. But the boy read it the way Paloma read Vessian: fluently, easily, his finger tracking line to line, his voice gathering strength with every phrase. And as he read, the hall changed.

Not visibly. That was the horror of it, and the wonder. The lamp burned the same. The stones were the same. But the *air* thickened, went golden and slow, and Paloma smelled — impossibly, unmistakably — woodsmoke and baking bread and flowering vines, smells that belonged to no street in Vess, that belonged to a summer evening in some place that had never existed within a thousand miles of here. Somewhere overhead, though the ceiling was solid stone, he heard a bell. A small bright bell, ringing the hour with a cheerfulness no bell in Vess possessed.

And the ledger's pages, under the boy's hands, were *filling in*. Where before there had been dense unreadable script, now margins bloomed with annotations in the same tongue, diagrams, small precise drawings — a bridge, a tower, a tree with leaves like coins. As if the book had been waiting two hundred years for someone who could read it properly, and had been holding its breath.

The boy stopped. Looked up at Paloma. And asked him something — a question, clearly, the intonation unmistakable — while the golden air slowly drained back to ordinary lamplight and the smell of bread faded to nothing and the bell's echo died in stone that had never let it in.

Paloma stood with his lamp and his fear and his six years of training, and understood, with the cold certainty of a man balancing a column that will not sum, that he was standing in front of the single largest anomaly in the history of Subsection Nine.

"You're asking me something," he said slowly. "I don't know what."

The boy's face crumpled. He tried again, gesturing — at himself, at the book, at the door, at the world beyond it — and the gestures told a story Paloma couldn't parse but couldn't ignore: *me, lost; book, mine? home, gone; you, help.*

Then the boy's gaze snagged on the manifest still lying on Paloma's desk, on the neat columns of his handwriting, and something kindled in his expression — recognition, of a sort. Ink. Numbers. The shape of record-keeping. He pointed at the page, then at himself, then pressed his muddy hand flat over his own chest, and said a short phrase, twice, insistently, until Paloma understood it was a name.

"Tam," Paloma repeated. Or something close to it. The actual sound had a click in the middle that his tongue couldn't find.

The boy nodded so hard the mud on his hair flaked off. *Tam.* Then he pointed at the ledger, at the whole grey mass of it, and at the hall, and at Paloma, and said a longer phrase — and this time, though the words meant nothing, the tone was plain as arithmetic. It was the tone of a person telling you something true and terrible and expecting you to do something about it.

The oldest ledger, on its stand, turned one final page and lay open, quiescent, satisfied. Waiting for the rest of whatever it was waiting for.

Paloma closed his eyes briefly. Behind them he saw the incident report he would have to write, and the forms, and Corvane's office with its tall windows and its patient smile, and the first maxim of the guild, *the ledgers are safer than lives*, and he understood suddenly, completely, that the maxim had never once mentioned whose life was supposed to be spent keeping them safe.

"All right," he said to the boy, to Tam, to the warm bone-white lock and the breathing book and the ghost-smell of bread. "All right. You stay tonight. In the morning—"

In the morning, he had no idea. But the boy was already curling himself onto the bench beside the reading stand, pulling his muddy knees up, one hand still resting on the open ledger as if it were a rope thrown to a drowning man. Within moments his breathing slowed. He slept the way the truly exhausted sleep — instantly, totally, like falling.

Paloma stood over him a long time.

Then he went to his desk, took up a fresh sheet, and wrote, in his best careful hand:

*Incident report, night of the 14th. One (1) child, male, approx. eight years, unknown origin, unknown language, admitted to premises under emergency provision. Subject appears able to read Folio Zero. Folio Zero responsive. Recommend immediate review by—*

His pen stopped.

Because the oldest ledger had turned, gently, of its own accord, to face him. Open to a fresh page. Blank. Waiting.

And at the top of that blank page, in ink that had not been there a moment ago, in a hand that was not the boy's and was certainly not Paloma's, someone had begun to write.

* * *

The letters were Vessian. That was the first shock — that the impossible thing should be legible, courteous, as if the universe had decided to observe proper form. The second shock was the handwriting itself: a fine, antique cursive, the kind Paloma had seen only in documents two centuries old, the kind no living scribe was taught anymore.

The page read:

*He speaks the truth-tongue. Do not let them take the book from him. Do not let them take him from the book. You have until the audit.*

Paloma read it four times. Each pass, the ink looked fresher, as though the words were still deciding to exist. He glanced at the sleeping boy. He glanced at the door, triple-locked, unbreached. There was no one else in the hall. There was no one else in the world, as far as the lamp's light reached, except a scribe, a child, and a book that had opinions.

*You have until the audit.*

Dawn audit was in four hours. Corvane herself sometimes attended the morning review — not always, but often enough, and always on the days when something valuable had come in overnight. Paloma looked down at the boy curled around the oldest ledger like a hearth around a fire, and did the arithmetic he was trained to do, the only arithmetic that had ever mattered to his survival.

A child who could read Folio Zero was not a child. He was an acquisition. He was provenance. He was the single most valuable item ever to walk through the night door of the Ledger Guild of Vess, and by morning, if Paloma filed this report, if he wrote one honest word about what he had seen, the boy would be catalogued, appraised, sealed, and sold — and Paloma's own debt would remain exactly as long as it had ever been, because the guild did not reward honesty; it merely noted it.

Or.

Or he could take a clean sheet, write *no anomalies, all quiet*, and commit the first fraud of his carefully honest life.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the rain had stopped. Somewhere in the walls, pipes ticked as the building settled, and beneath the ticking, faint as memory, Paloma thought he heard the bone-white lock humming again — contentedly now, like something fed.

Tam murmured in his sleep, and the word he murmured was the same one he'd said at the door, the long rising word full of wind and corridors, and this time, hearing it half-dreamed and unguarded, Paloma caught its shape at last. It wasn't a plea. It was a place. It was the sound a person makes when they say *home* and mean a country.

He pulled a fresh sheet toward him. Dipped his pen.

*All quiet,* he wrote. *No anomalies.*

The lie sat on the page looking exactly like the truth, which was, he supposed, the most important thing a scribe ever learns about ink.

Above the reading stand, the oldest ledger turned its own page — softly, patiently, like a clock counting down.
