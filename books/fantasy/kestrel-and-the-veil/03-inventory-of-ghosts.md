## 3. Inventory of Ghosts

The night door had a bolt on the inside and a rule about it: *no unregistered persons past the threshold after the ninth bell.* Paloma broke that rule with his shoulder, one hand fisted in the boy's collar, the other hauling the door shut behind them as if he could bolt out the whole world.

He couldn't. The boy came with him anyway — mud, silence, and those eyes that kept doing something eyes shouldn't do, catching lamplight like water catches a stone.

"Walk," Paloma whispered. "Don't speak. If anyone asks, you're my nephew and you're simple."

The boy looked at him with an expression that needed no translation. It said, quite clearly: *I understood none of that, but I understand you're afraid.*

Good. Fear was honest. Fear was a language they shared.

Paloma took him down through the counting floor, past the long desks where night-scribes bent over their columns like penitents, past the great brass scales that weighed nothing but were calibrated anyway, down the iron stair into the deep archive. The air changed at the bottom of the stairs. It always did. Cooler, drier, smelling of vellum and tallow and something older underneath — the smell of a room that had been holding its breath for two hundred years.

The deep archive was where the Ledger Guild of Vess kept what it could not explain and could not afford to destroy.

* * *

Paloma had worked the deep archive for six years and had never once been comfortable in it.

Other scribes hated it for the dark or the dust or the superstition that the folios down here listened. Paloma disliked it because it made his ledgers feel small. Upstairs, ink was ink. A debt was a number, a number was a fact, and a fact could be balanced. Down here, the inventory defied balancing. He had catalogued, over the years:

- One bottle of thunder, vintage unknown, which hummed when it rained.
- The second shadow of a widow from Harrow Lane, folded into a box no larger than a prayer book.
- Fourteen keys to doors that no longer existed, each still warm.
- A ledger page that wept saltwater at high tide, cause unrecorded.

Impossible things. The guild's term for them was *anomalies*. The guild's policy on anomalies was simple: catalogue, contain, and — where a buyer could be found — sell.

Paloma had told himself for six years that this was fine. That the guild was a warehouse, not a graveyard. That things without owners needed somewhere to go, and better a shelf than the fire.

Tonight, with a mud-caked boy pressed into the gap between the sealed folio cases and the back wall, breathing like a small frightened bellows, that story curdled in Paloma's mouth.

"Stay," he said, and pointed at the floor, and then pointed at himself, and then made walking fingers. *Stay. I come back.*

The boy nodded once, slow, the way you nod at someone speaking through water.

Paloma lit the reading lamp at his desk, pulled the current anomaly index toward him, and did the thing he did best in all the world: he began to cross-reference.

* * *

Here is what Paloma knew about the boy's language, which was nothing, except that it wasn't anything.

Not Vessic, obviously. Not Low Vessic, not Harbor Cant, not the clipped merchant dialects of the coast. Not Old Imperial, though Paloma had enough of that to read tombstones. Not the river-tongues, not the hill-whistles of the eastern shepherds, not the trade-pidgin that every dockhand in Vess spat half of daily. And it wasn't nonsense — that was the thing that had stopped Paloma's heart at the night door. The boy's words had grammar. They had weight and shape and repetition, the way real language does. When the boy had spoken, the oldest ledger in the receiving room had answered him. Pages had stirred. Paloma had seen it. He would spend the rest of his life being glad and horrified that he'd seen it.

So: a language with grammar, spoken by a child of perhaps eight, found alone in the mud outside the guild at midnight, recognized by a book.

There was exactly one place in the guild where a language could be matched against records going back further than living memory, and Paloma was sitting in it.

The sealed folios lived in the inner vault behind the archive proper — twelve cases, each locked, each requiring a countersignature from a guildmaster to open legally. Paloma had no countersignature. What Paloma had was six years of knowing which hinges Sefa-the-locksmith's apprentices had oiled lazily, and a thin steel pick he'd bought off a debt-collector named Brannic in exchange for overlooking four lines in Brannic's brother's account.

He told himself, working the first lock, that this was an audit. Unregistered language, unregistered person, unregistered response from registered inventory. All of it fell under his remit. All of it required documentation.

He was lying, and the lock knew it, and it opened anyway.

* * *

The sealed folios were organized by century, which meant the earliest ones were organized by decay. Paloma started at the far end, where the vellum had gone the color of old teeth and the ink had faded to the brown of dried blood, and he worked forward, fast, by feel more than sight, running his fingers down columns of entries looking for—

Looking for what? He didn't know. A word. Any word that matched the sounds the boy had made. Paloma had only heard maybe thirty of them, but language leaves hooks in you. There had been one word the boy repeated three times at the door, three times with rising desperation, and Paloma's ear had snagged on it the way a sleeve snags on a nail.

It sounded like *Kes-tral.* Or *Kess-rell.* Or something near it.

He found it in the fourth folio of the third case, in an entry dated two hundred and eleven years ago, written in a hand so cramped it might have been fleeing across the page.

*Item received: one (1) door, oak and iron, bearing inscription in the Kestrine tongue. Door does not open. Door does not open from either side. Door does not open.*

Paloma read it twice. Then a third time, slower, because his eyes wanted to skip ahead and some instinct older than training held them.

*Kestrine tongue.*

Below the entry, in a different hand — later, official, the flat bureaucratic script the guild used for verdicts — someone had written:

*Provenance: KESTREL (see Erasure Order 9). Struck from record per treaty. Retain physical holdings. Do not delete.*

Struck from record. But there it was, in the same ink, on the same page, in the same hand: *Kestrel.* You cannot strike a word from a record without writing it. The erasure order required its own exception. Every deletion was also a preservation. Paloma felt something turn over in his chest, slow and enormous, like a ship changing course.

He turned the page.

* * *

They were everywhere, once he knew to look. Not everywhere — the guild would never permit everywhere — but threaded through the early folios like a vein of ore through gray stone. Entry after entry, each one logged, weighed, assessed, and retained.

*A crate of Kestrine coinage, minted under the Assembly of Nine. Currency non-negotiable; nation of issue nonexistent. Retain.*

*One (1) streetlamp, Kestrine make, which lights at dusk without oil or flame. Mechanism unknown. Origin: city of Vel Sarane, Kestrel. City of origin nonexistent. Retain.*

*A jar of Kestrine honey. Does not spoil. Tastes, per tester's note, 'of a summer I have never had.' Retain.*

And then, deeper in the fifth folio, an entry that made Paloma set the lamp down because his hand had begun to shake and shaking hands spill lamp oil and burning archives helped no one:

*Consolidated register, Kestrine holdings, post-Erasure. Summary: total recovered mass of nation approximates 61%. Remaining 39% presumed dispersed, destroyed, or held privately. Note: the country itself is not recoverable. The country is not a thing. The country is a name, and the name has been broken into pages. See Name-Folio Index, restricted.*

Paloma sat very still.

Somewhere above him, through three floors of stone and timber, the guild slept on — clerks in their bunks, the Guildmaster in her rooms, the great brass scales gleaming in the dark, weighing nothing. Two hundred years of quiet bookkeeping. Debts and deposits, acquisitions and sales. A respectable institution. He had given it six years of his life against a debt he hadn't chosen, and he had believed — he had *needed* to believe — that the worst thing the guild did was charge interest.

The country was a name, and the name had been broken into pages.

He thought of the anomaly index upstairs. Of bottled thunder and widow's shadows and fourteen warm keys. Of the buyers who came on the first Tuesday of every month, escorted through the back galleries, their faces carefully not recorded. He had assumed they were collectors. Eccentrics. Rich fools buying curiosities.

What do you call a man who buys a piece of a country?

He didn't have a word yet. He wrote in the margin of his own working notes, in his neat audit-hand: *provenance shards?* and hated how tidy it looked. How sellable.

* * *

The boy made a sound.

Small. A breath caught and released — the sound a child makes turning over in sleep, except the boy wasn't asleep. Paloma rose, lamp in hand, and crossed to the gap between the cases, and found the boy sitting up with his knees drawn in, staring at the open folios spread across the desk with an expression Paloma had no translation for and didn't need one.

The boy slid out of the gap, crossed the archive floor barefoot and silent, and stood over the pages. His lips moved. Reading? No — Paloma realized with a lurch — *recognizing.* The way you recognize your own house from a distance. The way you recognize, in a stranger's window, the shape of a lamp you grew up under.

The boy touched the entry about the streetlamp. His finger rested on the word *Vel Sarane*, and he said something soft, four syllables, and this time Paloma heard it clearly enough to catch the shape of it, and the shape was grief.

"That's a place," Paloma said quietly, uselessly, in Vessic. "That was a place."

The boy looked up at him. And then he did the thing that turned the night from strange to terrible: he reached out and turned Paloma's working notes toward himself, found the word Paloma had written in the margin — *Kestrel* — and put his finger on it, and tapped it twice, and then pressed his palm flat over his own chest.

*I am from there. I am that.*

"Yes," Paloma said, in a voice that barely worked. "I know."

* * *

He should stop. He knew he should stop. Every rule he had, every survival instinct six years of indenture had ground into him, said: close the folio, wake no ghosts, log nothing, tell no one, get the boy out before dawn and let the world keep its secrets. Curiosity was how scribes became cautionary tales.

But the entries kept unfolding under his hands like something blooming, and Paloma had spent his whole adult life believing that facts were safer than people, and here was a fact the size of a nation, and he could not look away.

Erasure Order 9. He found the reference in the seventh folio, in a summary so dry it read like a weather report:

*Treaty concluded following the Kestrine Succession Crisis. Terms: full territorial dissolution. The signatory powers agreed that Kestrel should not be conquered but forgotten — conquest breeds martyrs, forgetting breeds nothing. The Kestrine Assembly consented. Method: the breaking of the True Name into constituent pages, dispersal of same, and the striking of all records. Physical remnants to be collected and administered by the Ledger Guild of Vess in perpetuity.*

*In perpetuity,* Paloma read, *or until such time as the Guild determines disposal.*

Disposal. He thought of the buyers again. First Tuesday of the month.

The Assembly consented. He read that line five times, trying to make it mean something other than what it meant. A country had voted itself out of the world. Had handed over its own name to be broken apart like a stolen watch. And the guild — his guild, his debtors, the institution that owned the next nine years of his life — had been the drawer they'd locked it in. And then, somewhere across two centuries, the drawer had become a shop.

The thrill that had carried him through the folios — the pure, singing thrill of discovery, of *knowing*, the feeling he'd chased since he was a boy copying letters in the dirt — curdled now, slowly, thoroughly, into dread. Because knowledge in Vess was never free. Knowledge was inventory. And Paloma had just inventoried himself into danger.

If the guild had sold pieces of a country, then the guild had buyers who knew what they were buying. Buyers who would want the rest. And a boy who spoke the dead tongue — a boy whom the oldest ledger in the building answered like a bell answering a struck note — was not an orphan. He was provenance. He was the last living shard of Kestrel, and he was standing barefoot on Paloma's archive floor, and Paloma had broken a lock and falsified nothing yet but was already guilty of everything.

Footsteps, above. Distant, on the counting floor. The night-watch making rounds.

Paloma closed the folios. He moved the boy back into the gap between the cases, wrapped him in his own coat, and knelt so they were eye to eye.

"Sleep," he said, and mimed it, head on folded hands.

The boy lay down. Before his eyes closed, he said one word to Paloma — the desperate word from the door, the one that sounded like *Kes-tral* — but softer now, and Paloma understood, finally, that the boy hadn't been saying a place-name at the door at all.

He'd been saying *help*. In the only country's tongue he had. To a country that no longer existed.

Paloma sat at his desk until the lamp burned low, and he did not sleep, and near the end he took out a fresh sheet and began, because his hands needed work or they would shake, to draft an inventory. *Items of interest, unlogged:* and then he stopped, because the first item on any honest list would be *one (1) child, origin Kestrel, status: known to the undersigned,* and any honest list was a death warrant with neat margins.

He burned the draft in the lamp flame and watched the ash curl.

Which was when he saw, through the archive's high grate of a window, lanterns moving in the courtyard above. Three of them. Moving not with the patrol's lazy drift but with purpose, converging — on the night door. On the door he'd come through with a mud-caked boy whose footprints were almost certainly still drying on the stair.

A knock echoed down through the floors. Measured. Patient. The knock of someone who already knew what they'd find.

Then, muffled but unmistakable, a woman's voice, smooth as a closed ledger, calling down the stairwell:

"Scribe Ashgrove. The Guildmaster will see you now."
