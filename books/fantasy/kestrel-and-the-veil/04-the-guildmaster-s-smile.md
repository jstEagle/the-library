## 4. The Guildmaster's Smile

The summons came at the ninth bell, folded into a slip of vellum that smelled of clove oil and old money.

*Paloma Ashgrove. Upper Archive. Now.*

No reason given. No reason needed. In eleven years of indenture I had been summoned to the Upper Archive exactly twice — once to witness the signing of a shipping contract worth more than my debt, once to identify a forger who was dragged out in irons before I finished speaking. The Guildmaster did not call scribes upstairs for small things.

I looked at Tam first.

He was asleep in the gap between the map cabinets where I'd hidden him three nights ago, curled around the ledger folio like it was a warm animal. He slept the way children sleep in war zones, I imagine — lightly, one hand always touching something that mattered. His mud-stained boots were tucked under my spare coat. My supper sat half-eaten beside him because he'd refused to eat until I ate first, and I had learned in three days that this was not a negotiation you could win.

*Upper Archive. Now.*

They knew. Of course they knew. The night door had been watched since the boy arrived; I'd simply been too tired, too fascinated, too *stupid* to assume otherwise. A man who believes ledgers are safer than lives will eventually learn what ledgers are actually for.

I stood, straightened my guild sash, and did the arithmetic I always did when fear arrived: four years, two months, and some odd days left on my debt. Eleven years served. A body can survive almost anything if it knows the number.

Then I climbed the stairs to the Upper Archive, and met the woman who owned all of them.

* * *

Guildmaster Corvane kept her office at the top of the Ledger Hall, behind doors of black oak carved with the Guild's motto: *ALL THINGS ACCOUNTED*. Inside, there were no shelves. That was the first thing that unsettled me, every time. A guild built on paper, and its master kept no books of her own. Just a desk of pale stone, a window overlooking the counting floor, and walls hung with framed documents so old their ink had gone the color of dried blood.

She was feeding her bird when I entered. A shrike — of course a woman like Corvane kept a shrike — spearing mealworms on a thorn fixed to its perch.

"Ashgrove." She didn't look up. "You've been working late."

"Quarter-end audit, Guildmaster."

"Mm. Impossible inventory is heavy work. Bottled thunder doesn't catalog itself." She offered the last worm to the bird on her bare palm, an intimacy that made my skin crawl. "Tell me about your week."

"I completed the audit of Vault Six—"

"Paloma." My name, said gently, the way you'd say it to a dog that had wandered onto a road. She turned from the perch at last, and gave me the smile that had made junior clerks resign rather than be summoned again. It was a good smile. Warm, even. That was the horror of it. "Let's not spend the whole afternoon pretending. There's a boy sleeping in your archive."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below us, three hundred quills scratched against three hundred pages, the sound of the Guild's heart beating.

"He came to the night door," I said. "Mud to the knees. Speaking gibberish. Protocol says unknowns go to the wardens—"

"Protocol says nothing of the kind, and we both know it. Protocol says unknowns go to the wardens *immediately*, not after three days of hiding them among sealed folios." She crossed to her desk and drew out a single sheet — I recognized the stock instantly, archive-grade rag paper, and my stomach dropped through the floor. "You logged him, Paloma. Tuesday night. Entry forty-one-forty. 'One male child, approx. eight years, language unidentifiable, responds to Folio K.' You filed him. You filed a *child* as an item of impossible inventory."

Because that was the only way to keep him safe, I wanted to say. Because anything in the ledgers belongs to the Guild, and things the Guild owns don't get thrown out — they get *stored*. But I said none of it, because she was watching me with those patient gray eyes, and every excuse sounded worse aloud than it had in my head.

"He answered to Folio K," she continued, tapping the page. "Do you know what that means?"

"No, Guildmaster."

"Liar," she said pleasantly. "But we'll come back to that." She set the sheet down and smoothed it flat with both hands, and I realized with a fresh wave of cold that it wasn't my entry at all. It was a bill of sale. Blank except for the buyer's seal, which I didn't recognize — a tower inside a circle, stamped in silver wax. "Three nights ago, before your boy ever knocked, a client approached me. Very old money. Very particular tastes. They collect provenance, Ashgrove. Origins. First causes. Things that were somewhere before they were anywhere." Her fingers rested on the blank line where a price would go. "And three days ago, a child walked up to our door speaking a tongue that died two hundred years ago, and the oldest book in our vaults *answered him.*"

"You think he's connected to—"

"I think," she said, "that my client would pay more for that boy than this entire building is worth. More than the building, the vaults, and every soul indentured inside them." She let that sit. Let me do the math she knew I couldn't help doing. "More than your debt, Paloma. Your debt, dissolved. Your papers, burned. Your name struck from the obligation rolls with a ceremony and a bonus, if you like bonuses. Walk out of here tomorrow a free man with a purse full of crowns."

The words landed in my chest like coins dropped down a well. I heard them fall and fall and never hit bottom.

Four years, two months, and odd days. Four years of cold rooms and thinner suppers, of watching free men cross the square while I inked their shipping manifests. Four years of being a number in someone else's column. And all I had to do was hand over a child I'd known for three days to a buyer whose seal I didn't recognize, for purposes described only as *collecting origins*.

"That's not a delivery," I said. "That's a sale. Of a person."

"It's a transaction." Corvane returned to the shrike and stroked its head with one finger; the bird tolerated it the way employees tolerate her. "Everything is a transaction, Ashgrove. You above all people should appreciate that. You've spent eleven years learning that lives balance better than feelings. Here's a chance to practice what the Guild taught you." She glanced back over her shoulder. "Or have I misjudged you? Have you gone sentimental over a stray?"

"No, Guildmaster."

"Good." The smile again, wider now, showing teeth. "Because I should tell you how the alternative goes. If you refuse — and you may refuse, I'm not a monster, refusal is a legal right of every scribe in this hall — then I deliver the boy myself, your debt stands, and your file acquires a note. *Scribe Ashgrove concealed Guild property for personal reasons. Judgment impaired. Not recommended for sensitive inventory.*" She picked a thread off her sleeve. "There are perhaps six positions in Vess a man with that note can hold. None of them involve paper. Do you know what a man who loves ledgers becomes when no one will let him touch one?"

I knew. I'd seen one begging outside the Grain Exchange the winter I arrived, a former archivist with shaking hands who talked to the crows about filing systems.

"And the boy?" My voice came out flatter than I intended. "What happens to him either way?"

Corvane tilted her head, genuinely considering whether to answer, which was itself an answer.

"The client has been very clear about condition," she said. "Alive. Intact. Cooperative." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "How they achieve cooperation is the handler's concern. Which is why they want a handler who has already established rapport. Three days of rapport, Ashgrove. He eats when you eat. He sleeps when you're near. You've done the expensive part of the work already." She spread her hands, presenting the bargain like a merchant presenting cloth. "All I'm asking is that you finish the job you started when you filed him under inventory. Deliver the goods. Settle your account. Everyone balances."

*Everyone balances.* Except the boy, who balanced nowhere, weighed nothing, belonged to no column but mine now, apparently, whether I liked it or not.

"How long do I have?"

"Sunset tomorrow. The client keeps evening hours." She opened a drawer and slid a key across the desk — iron, heavy, stamped with the same tower-in-circle seal. "Vault Nine. You'll find a transport case fitted to specification. Silk lining. Air holes." She said *air holes* the way another woman might say *fresh flowers*.

I took the key because refusing to take it was also a choice, and I wasn't ready to make any choice in that room. The metal was cold and heavier than a key had any right to be.

"One more thing." Corvane's voice followed me to the door, soft and pleasant and absolutely certain. "Folio K. The old ledger that answers him. You've been reading it. Don't insult us both by denying it — the dust tells stories, and I employ people who listen to dust." The shrike screamed once, a bright terrible sound. "Whatever you think you found in those pages, Paloma, whatever story the dead country tells about itself — remember that countries are just debts that got too large to collect. We foreclosed. That's all erasure is. Foreclosure." She smiled her warm, horrible smile. "Don't fall in love with collateral."

* * *

I went down through the counting floor without seeing it. Three hundred heads bent over three hundred desks, and not one of them looked up, because looking up in the Ledger Hall was how you acquired obligations.

In the stairwell between floors I stopped, put my hand against the wall, and was quietly, thoroughly sick with the shape of the thing she'd offered. Not the threat — the threat I understood, threats were just invoices with worse manners. What sickened me was how long the offer had taken to become monstrous. For three full stairs, some part of me had been *calculating*. Four years against one child. Eleven years of service against three days of acquaintance. The arithmetic had run automatically, the way it always ran, before the rest of me caught up and understood what was being counted.

She hadn't offered to buy the boy. She'd offered to buy *me*, using him as currency, and the worst part — the part I would carry longer than the fear — was that the price had been fair. By the Guild's lights, it had been generous.

Tam was awake when I reached the archive. He was sitting up in the cabinet gap with Folio K open across his knees, and he looked at my face and went still in the way of children who have learned to read adults the way scribes read contracts.

He said something in his dead language. Soft. A question.

"I don't know," I told him, in Vessic, uselessly, and then, because lying to him had somehow become harder than lying to the Guildmaster: "She knows you're here. She wants me to give you away. Tomorrow, at sunset. And if I do, I go free."

He considered this with a gravity no eight-year-old should own. Then he looked down at the folio, turned it toward me, and tapped one line near the top of the page — a line I had read a dozen times in three days and never understood, struck through but not deleted, in that looping antique script:

*Item: one (1) nation, remaindered.*

Below it, in a different hand, newer, smaller, someone had added a note in the margin. I'd assumed it was a cataloguer's mark. Tonight, with Corvane's key burning cold in my pocket, I finally read it properly.

*Not for sale. Held in trust. Payment pending.*

Tam watched my face as I read it, and nodded slowly, as if I'd finally said something intelligent.

Outside, the bells rang the tenth hour. Twenty-one hours to sunset.
