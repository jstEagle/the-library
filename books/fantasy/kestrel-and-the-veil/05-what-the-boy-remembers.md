## 5. What the Boy Remembers

*Paloma*

The archive at night was the only place in Vess where Paloma felt honest.

By day he lied constantly — to creditors, to junior scribes, to himself, mostly in numbers. *Three years, four months remaining on the debt.* A lie so well-kept it had become a kind of truth. But down here, among the sealed folios and the smell of cold vellum, there was no one to perform for. Just him and the ledgers, which never pretended to be anything but what they were: records of things that had happened, or were supposed to have happened, or — in the Guild's special category, filed under *Provenance Pending* — things that had happened somewhere no map would admit to.

He'd hidden the boy behind the folio racks on the third tier, under a dust sheet that smelled of tallow and old mistakes. The boy — Tam, he'd finally gotten out of him, though the name came out sounding like two stones knocked together — had not slept. He sat cross-legged with his knees drawn up, watching Paloma the way a watchman watches a fire that hasn't decided yet whether to spread.

"You're hungry," Paloma said, in Vessic, then again slower, then again with his hands shaping the word for bread.

Tam answered in the other language. The dead one. The one that shouldn't exist.

Paloma had spent the day doing what scribes do when they can't understand something: cross-referencing. He'd pulled every lexicon fragment the Guild owned, every grammar of every tongue from the Salt Marches to the Amberglass Coast, and none of them matched. And yet the oldest ledger in the building — Ledger One, chained to its lectern like a dog that had bitten someone — had understood the boy perfectly. When Tam spoke, its pages stirred. Not turned. *Stirred.* Like a sleeper hearing a familiar voice through a wall.

So now Paloma sat on the floor of the third tier with a candle stub, a slate, and a boy who spoke a country that had been struck through.

"Let's try it this way," he said. He drew on the slate: a house. Simple. Roof, door, window.

Tam leaned forward. His eyes went bright in a way that made Paloma's chest hurt before he understood why.

"*Sennet,*" Tam said, touching the roof. Then a flood of words, fast, urgent, his hands moving too — pointing up, out, wide, as if the slate were far too small for what he was describing.

Paloma wrote the sound phonetically: *SEN-net.* Then he drew a row of houses. "Sennet?" he asked, gesturing at all of them.

Tam shook his head, frustrated, and grabbed the slate. His chalk-work was astonishing — quick, sure strokes, a whole street blooming under his fingers: terraced houses with stepped gables, a canal running between them, bridges arched like drawn bows, lanterns hanging from iron brackets shaped like herons.

"That's not possible," Paloma said quietly. Because it wasn't. He knew the architecture of every city within six hundred miles; it was part of his trade, cataloguing provenance. Stepped gables like that existed nowhere. Heron-brackets existed nowhere. The canal ran *uphill* in Tam's drawing, gently, confidently uphill, and the boy had drawn it without hesitation, without even noticing.

Tam tapped the drawing. "*Kestrel,*" he said — and this time the word arrived almost intact, almost pronounceable, because it was the same word in both languages. Some words survive translation the way bones survive fire.

Then he said it again, and again, tapping each house, each bridge, each impossible uphill stretch of water, and Paloma understood: *all of it. All of this is Kestrel.*

Behind them, unchained now — Paloma had unhooked it an hour ago and told himself he'd rehook it later — Ledger One rustled its pages like a man turning over in bed.

* * *

*Tam*

The man-scribe did not speak properly. None of them here spoke properly. Their mouths were broken, all of them, full of flat sounds and missing music, and when Tam tried to say *good evening* they looked at him like he'd coughed up a fish.

But the man-scribe could draw. That was something. In Kestrel everyone could draw — you learned it before letters, because letters in Kestrel moved and you had to be able to catch them first. Tam's mother said he drew better than he spoke, which she claimed was a high compliment and which his sister insisted was not.

His sister. He pressed the thought down where he kept it, in the box under his ribs, next to the last thing she'd said to him. *Run until the doors stop being doors.* He had run. The doors had stopped being doors. Now everything here was walls.

The man-scribe drew a house and Tam felt the box under his ribs rattle open, just a crack, and he couldn't help it — he took the chalk and gave the man back his street. All of it. Sennet Row, the whole crooked length of it, from the Weepers' Bridge to Aunt Ivelle's bakery with the blue door that argued with you if you knocked rudely. He drew the canal going uphill toward the Lantern Fields because that was simply where the canal went; water in Kestrel was ambitious. He drew the heron-lanterns because his mother had hung one outside their window and told him it would keep bad dreams from landing, the way herons keep fish.

"*Kestrel,*" he said, and the big book behind them woke up, and that frightened him less than it should have. Books in Kestrel were half-asleep things anyway, always dreaming about whatever was written in them. This book dreamed hard. This book dreamed like it had been waiting two hundred years for someone to talk to.

He told the man-scribe about the festival. He didn't know why — maybe because the man's face had gone strange and sad looking at the street, and sad faces needed feeding. He stood up so he could use his whole body.

"The First Unwriting," he said, and acted it: the whole city gathering on the rooftops at dusk, everyone holding a candle and a small white lie written on paper. You told your lie to the flame — *I am taller than my brother, I once swam the sea, I do not miss my father* — and burned it, and the smoke rose straight up in windless air and became, briefly, a second city above the first, made of everyone's small dishonesties, glowing gold, and children tried to name the streets of the sky-city and whoever named a new one got a coin.

The man-scribe watched him with an expression Tam couldn't read in any language. It wasn't disbelief. Disbelief he knew how to fight. This was worse. This was the face of a man who believed him completely and was grieving about it.

Tam stopped acting. The rooftop feeling drained out of him and left the other feeling, the one from the night the doors stopped being doors: the smell of burning paper everywhere, his mother's hands shoving him toward the alley, her voice doing the thing voices do when they're pretending not to break.

"Mama," he said. Just that. In the true language, where the word meant also *home* and also *stay*.

The man-scribe reached out — slowly, the way you reach toward a bird — and touched the top of Tam's head, once, and then wrote something on the slate and showed him.

It was a drawing of a woman. Badly done, lopsided, the face a smudge. But above it the man had drawn a question mark, and pointed at Tam, and waited.

Tam nodded. Yes. Yes. *Yes.*

And the man closed his eyes for a moment, and when he opened them they were wet, which made no sense at all, because it was Tam's mother who was lost, not his.

* * *

*Paloma*

Later — much later, past the hour when even the night auditors went home — Paloma sat alone at the long table with the slate drawings arranged in front of him like evidence at a trial.

That was exactly what they were, he realized. Evidence. Of what, he couldn't yet say. But he'd been a ledger-man too long not to recognize a pattern assembling itself out of noise.

He laid them out in sequence. The street. The uphill canal. The heron-lanterns. The stepped gables. The festival of the sky-city, which Tam had performed with his whole body, laughing and then abruptly not laughing. A fruit Tam had drawn and then mime-eaten, making a face of such specific bliss that Paloma had caught himself salivating for a taste of something that did not exist. A woman's figure, drawn over and over, more carefully each time — the last version had a mole under the left eye, a particular way of folding her arms, a heron-feather tucked behind one ear.

Every detail consistent. Every detail mutually reinforcing. Every detail verifiably false.

That was the thing that kept snagging him. Paloma's entire profession rested on a simple axiom: *records agree with reality or they are fraudulent.* He had spent nine years detecting fraudulence. He knew its textures. Liars embellish inconsistently; frauds over-specify; forgers always reveal themselves in the seams. But Tam's account had no seams. The boy remembered a city the way you remember a city — patchily, emotionally, with the wrong things vivid (the exact argumentative creak of a blue door) and the right things vague (he could not say how many bridges crossed the canal, only that "the middle one wobbled"). No forger forgets like that. No liar remembers door-hinges.

Which left only the impossible conclusion, the one Paloma had been circling all night like a moth around a lamp he suspected would kill him:

The boy wasn't lying. The boy was remembering. Which meant the city had existed. Which meant —

He pulled Ledger One onto the table. The chain clinked. He'd found the entries yesterday, in the sealed folios: *Kestrel*, struck through in the old red strike-line the Guild used for dissolved contracts, but — and this was the irregularity that had kept him awake — *not deleted*. Everything else the Guild dissolved got expunged, scraped, rewritten. Kestrel's entries remained legible beneath the strikes, hundreds of them, spanning decades: census fragments, tax rolls, a shipping manifest for "ambitious water," a complaint from a lantern-keeper about herons.

Struck through, but preserved. Like something you couldn't quite bring yourself to throw away. Or like something you were keeping *for* something.

Paloma turned to the final entry. Dated two hundred years ago, in a hand he recognized — every scribe did — as the Founder's own. It was short. It read:

*Item: one nation, self-surrendered. Disposition pending. Do not mourn it. It is not destroyed. It is held.*

Held. By whom? Held *where?* The Guild's vaults held bottled thunder and widows' shadows and, apparently, the geographic remains of a country that had chosen to stop existing rather than be conquered — and the Guild had been selling it off, shard by shard, to buyers whose names were recorded only as initials and sums.

And a boy had walked out of that arithmetic and knocked on the night door speaking its language, and the oldest ledger in Vess had *stirred* to hear him, and Paloma had hidden him instead of reporting him, which was treason, which was — he did the calculation automatically, hated himself for doing it — another eleven years added to his debt if caught. Twelve. Corvane's mood dependent.

Twelve years for a child he'd known three days.

Paloma looked at the slate drawing of the woman with the heron-feather, and thought about the moment Tam had said *mama*, and how the word had meant three things at once, and how Paloma's own mother had been dead eleven years and he had never once, since her funeral, allowed himself to think of her voice for longer than a breath, because thinking of it fully hurt too much, and grief deferred accrues interest like anything else.

Neither of us ever had it, he thought. The boy has lost a country. I've only lost the nerve to remember mine.

From the third tier came a small sound. Tam, asleep finally, curled under the dust sheet, murmuring in the dead language — and the murmur wasn't distressed. It was the rhythm of a lullaby, half-remembered, sung to himself by the last citizen of nowhere.

Ledger One, still open on the table, turned a page by itself.

Paloma looked down. New ink was surfacing in the margin of the final entry, grey at first, then darkening, in handwriting that was not the Founder's and not the Guild's and not, he realized with a cold sliding feeling, entirely human:

*The boy is the key. The buyer comes at week's end. Bring him, scribe, and walk free — or hide him, and learn what we hold.*

Below it, a signature: a single letter, stylized, elegant.

*C.*

Corvane.

Paloma sat very still. Somewhere above him, the Guild's night bell rang the hour, and under the dust sheet on the third tier, a sleeping boy sang a lullaby to a country that no longer existed, in a language that only one book in the world still understood — and that book had just told on him.
