## 6. Buyers and Borders

Corvane left the guild at dusk, and she left like a woman who did not believe in being followed.

She took no carriage. She dismissed her footman at the corner of Ledger Street with a flick of two fingers, and then she walked — fast, upright, her gray coat buttoned to the throat — into the kind of evening that made honest people hurry home. Paloma gave her half a block of head start and then followed anyway, because he had spent eleven years learning that the only thing more dangerous than disobeying the Guildmaster was not knowing what she did when she thought no one was watching.

He told himself it was about Tam. It was mostly about Tam. The boy was locked — *hidden*, he kept correcting himself, *archived* — in the sub-basement among the sealed folios, with a jug of water, half a loaf, and instructions written in three languages, one of which was apparently close enough to the boy's dead tongue to make him laugh on first reading. Laugh. As if being smuggled into the heart of an institution that erased countries were a game of hide-and-seek.

But it wasn't only about Tam. It was also about the folio he'd found, the one with KESTREL struck through in red across every page but never deleted, never released, held in the guild's memory like a debt nobody would admit to owing. And it was about the way Corvane had looked at him yesterday over the audit table and said, mildly, *You've been pulling sealed records, Ashgrove,* and then smiled and let him go.

Letting him go was worse than stopping him. Letting him go meant she wanted him moving in a direction she could predict.

So he followed her through Vess as the lamps came up one district at a time, from the counting-houses to the silk quarter to the river terraces where the city's money went to be seen spending itself. He wore his second-best coat, the plain brown one, and carried a scribe's satchel because nothing in Vess looked more invisible than a man carrying paperwork for someone richer than him. Twice Corvane stopped without warning and turned, scanning the street behind her, and both times Paloma became fascinated by a shop window — once a chandler's, once a display of mourning jewelry, which felt like an omen he chose not to read too closely.

The house she entered stood on Amberfall Terrace, and it was less a house than a statement: white stone, black iron gates, windows tall enough to walk through. No nameplate. Places like this didn't need names; the people inside already knew whose house it was, and everyone else was expected to pretend they didn't care.

Paloma cared. He circled the block twice, found the servants' gate propped open for a delivery of wine, and walked in behind the crates with his satchel hugged to his chest and the expression of a man who had been sent ahead to check the vintage.

Nobody stopped him. That was the thing about wealth — it assumed it had already met you, or that you weren't worth meeting.

* * *

The auction was in the ballroom.

He understood that immediately and then spent a full minute refusing to understand it, because the room was wrong in a way his mind kept trying to file under *expensive* when it belonged under *impossible*. Chandeliers, yes. String quartet, yes. Forty or fifty guests in velvet and gilt, champagne in flutes thin as promises. And down the center of the ballroom floor, where dancing should have been, a runway of black cloth, and along it, on pedestals of polished basalt, the lots.

Lot one was a stone. Just a stone, knee-high, rough-hewn, sitting under a glass dome — except the air around it bent slightly, the way air bends above a summer road, and the guests nearest it kept leaning in and breathing deep, and their faces went soft and stupid with pleasure.

Lot two was a jar of water. Lot three was a length of rope, coiled neat as a sleeping snake. Lot four was a door — an actual door, freestanding, painted a faded blue, its frame still hung with a scrap of curtain.

And each lot bore a small ivory card, lettered in the Guild's own house hand, which Paloma recognized because it was *his* hand, or near enough — the standardized script every indentured scribe was drilled into until their own handwriting died of neglect.

**PROVENANCE SHARD 41 — RIVERSTONE, EAST BANK. ORIGIN VERIFIED.**

**PROVENANCE SHARD 44 — WELLWATER, THE LOW QUARTER. ORIGIN VERIFIED.**

**PROVENANCE SHARD 12 — HARBOUR ROPE, TANNER'S STAIR. ORIGIN VERIFIED.**

**PROVENANCE SHARD 3 — DOOR, MILL LANE. ORIGIN VERIFIED.**

Origin verified. Verified against what? Against ledgers. Against ledgers written in his hand, audited at his desk, filed in the archive where a boy who spoke a dead language could make the pages stir like leaves in wind.

A country's borders are lines on paper. Everyone knows that. What nobody tells you is that the paper is load-bearing.

Paloma stood at the edge of the crowd with his heart going like a trapped bird and watched a duchy get sold off in door-sized pieces.

* * *

He found Corvane near the front, standing beside a heavyset man with rings on every finger, and she was doing the thing he had seen her do a hundred times across an audit table: listening with total attention while appearing entirely bored. The heavyset man was talking about yield. Paloma drifted close enough to hear fragments.

"—retention's the whole question, Guildmaster. A shard keeps its character for a generation, maybe two, if you anchor it right. My colleague bought the orchard piece last spring. His trees fruit out of season now. Out of *season*. Do you know what a peach commands in winter?"

"More than your discretion, I hope," Corvane said, and the man laughed too loudly, and Paloma slipped past them toward the lots.

Up close, the stone under glass was worse. Not because it looked strange — it looked like any riverstone you'd skip across any water — but because it looked so completely ordinary, so *finished*, like something that had been somewhere for a very long time and expected to stay. There was moss in its crevices. Moss doesn't grow on inventory. Moss grows on places.

He leaned toward the dome and the bending air touched his face, and for half a second he smelled it: cold water, green banks, woodsmoke from somewhere downstream, bread. A whole landscape folded into a smell, the way a whole life folds into a name.

His eyes stung. He straightened up fast, checked that no one had seen a scribe weeping over a rock, and moved on.

The blue door was worse still. Someone had scratched heights into its frame — little horizontal lines with dates beside them, the way families mark their children growing. The tallest mark was dated in a calendar Paloma had never been taught, in numerals that matched the ones in the sealed folios. He put his fingertips against the paint, lightly, the way you touch a wound to learn how much it hurts.

Under his hand, faint as a pulse through a wrist, the door *hummed*.

"Careful, friend."

He turned. The speaker was a steward in livery the color of dried blood, smiling with professional warmth. "Lots aren't to be handled before sale. Resonance, you understand." He said it the way you'd say *delicate antiques*, and then, seeing Paloma's satchel, softened further. "Guild man? You'll be here for the verification table, then. Through the far arch. They're always short-staffed on auction nights."

They were. The verification table sat behind the arch in a service corridor, manned by a single harried junior factor surrounded by ledgers — ledgers Paloma knew, ledgers he had *written*, stacked open to pages bearing his own careful columns. The factor glanced up, saw the satchel, saw the guild pin on his lapel that he'd forgotten to remove, and visibly relaxed.

"Thank the Ledger. You're from Corvane's office? We need the cross-checks recopied before the third bell — the buyers want fresh abstracts with each lot."

"I'm—" Paloma began, and then heard himself say, "Yes."

Because this was the door open wider than any he'd ever kicked, and he was not the sort of fool who looked a gift breach in the mouth. He sat down at the verification table, pulled the top ledger toward him, and began to work, and working meant reading, and reading meant understanding exactly what he had been doing with the last eleven years of his life.

The abstracts were simple. Each shard, it turned out, was more than a fragment. Each shard was a *deed*. When a buyer purchased Provenance Shard 41, they purchased not the stone but the stone's *standing* — its legal-magical claim to be part of a real place. And a place, a true place, a nation, was nothing but the sum of such claims, held together by a name. Strike the name from the world's memory, scatter the claims into portable pieces, and what remained was not a destroyed country.

It was an estate in probate.

Kestrel hadn't fallen. Kestrel had been *liquidated*.

Paloma copied figures with a steady hand and let the horror move through him in slow tides, because a scribe's hands don't shake, that was the first thing they taught you, ink forgives nothing. Two centuries ago. Two hundred years of auctions, presumably — annual, seasonal, whenever the market could bear another door, another well, another stretch of riverbank. Somewhere in this room, in these forty strangers sipping champagne, was ownership of every street Tam remembered. Every festival ground. The bench where his mother had sat. Sold, resold, inherited, walled into gardens, sunk into private lakes.

And the boy was the last citizen. Which made him, legally speaking, the heir to everything in this room — and heirs, Paloma knew from eleven years of probate work, were always best dealt with before they came of age.

* * *

The auction began at the third bell, and the auctioneer was Corvane.

That shouldn't have surprised him and did anyway. She mounted the small stage at the end of the runway wearing the same gray coat she'd worn out of the guild, as if to say that this, too, was guild business, merely conducted in better lighting. The room hushed the way rooms hush for her — he'd seen it happen over budget disputes, over disciplinary hearings, that particular silence of people deciding in advance to agree.

"Honored collectors," she said. "Tonight we offer fourteen lots, all origin-verified, all clean title. You know our standards. Nothing leaves this house that isn't *true*."

Nothing leaves this house that isn't true. Paloma stood at the back of the crowd with a recopied abstract in his hands and thought: *you erase a country and call the erasure truth.* The Guild's deepest magic, if the sealed folios were anything to go by, had never been fire or armies. It was bookkeeping. A line drawn through a word, done correctly, witnessed properly, and the word stops meaning. The country stops having been. Only the ledgers remember, because someone has to hold the debt — and the Guild held it, and charged interest, and sold the collateral.

Lot one, the riverstone, opened at four hundred crowns and closed at two thousand, bought by a wine merchant's widow who clapped her hands like a girl.

Lot four, the blue door, went for nine. Nine thousand crowns for a family's height-marks. The buyer was a young lord with a bored mouth who said he'd have it installed as a garden feature, "somewhere the ivy can do something charming with it."

Paloma wrote none of this down. His hands were steady. Everything else was not.

Between lots, servants rotated the pedestals, and Paloma noticed what he should have noticed earlier: gaps. Fourteen lots tonight, but sixteen pedestals. Two stood empty at the far end, draped in black cloth, and when a servant passed them, the cloth *moved*, subtly, as if what lay beneath was breathing, or straining, or listening.

He drifted toward them during the bidding on a lot described only as SHARD 7 — CHURCHBELL, UNCONSECRATED. Up close, under the cloth, the empty pedestals hummed the same note the blue door had hummed under his fingers, but louder, strained, a note held too long. He lifted the edge of one cloth an inch.

Beneath it was nothing. Not emptiness — *nothing*, a patch of dark that his eye slid off like rain off oilcloth, a hole shaped like whatever used to be there. And around the rim of the nothing, scratched into the basalt pedestal, tiny anchor-runes in the Guild's house hand, holding the absence in place the way a splint holds a broken bone.

Two shards had escaped. Or been taken. Or were *trying* to go home.

"Ashgrove."

Corvane's voice, directly behind him, mild as milk.

He turned. She stood with a champagne flute she was not drinking, and her eyes went from his face to the lifted cloth to the satchel, unhurried, cataloguing.

"The verification table," she said, "is through the arch. This wing is for bidders."

"I was recopied forward," he said. "Fresh abstracts. The factor asked—"

"The factor asks for many things." She took a slow sip, watching him over the rim. "You look pale, Ashgrove. Auctions disagree with some temperaments. All this... history changing hands." Her gaze flicked, briefly, to the humming pedestals. "Some people find it distasteful. They tend to be people who haven't yet learned what things cost."

"I know what things cost," Paloma said. "It's most of my job."

Something moved behind her eyes — approval, or amusement, or the exact expression of a woman confirming a figure she'd already calculated. "Doesn't it just," she said. "Eleven years, isn't it? Fourteen months remaining. Such discipline. Most men in your position would have done something desperate by now." She set the untouched flute on a passing tray. "Desperation is expensive, Ashgrove. Remember that, whatever else you take from tonight."

She walked back toward the stage. Lot eight was called. A folded map, creased white with age, sold for eleven thousand to the ringed man from earlier, who whispered to his neighbor that it was "the capital district, or what's left of the paper of it."

The capital. Tam's streets. Tam's mother's street, probably, though Paloma couldn't know that, wouldn't let himself guess which shard held which grief.

He retreated through the arch, returned the abstracts to the factor, thanked him, and let himself out through the servants' gate with his heart hammering and his mind running cold and fast along a single track:

Fourteen months on his debt. Fourteen lots tonight. The arithmetic of it sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone: the Guild was selling Kestrel faster than any scribe could ever buy his way free of it, and the boy asleep in his archive was the last unclaimed asset in the entire estate.

Which meant Corvane hadn't summoned him yet about Tam because she didn't need to. She was letting him find the edges of it himself. She wanted to see what he'd do when he understood — whether he was the kind of man who got desperate, and therefore cheap, or the kind who got useful, and therefore valuable.

Either way, she'd priced him.

* * *

He was three streets from Amberfall Terrace, walking fast with his collar up, when he realized he was being followed.

Not Corvane's people — he knew their tread, heavy and official. This was lighter, quicker, stopping when he stopped. He cut down Tanner's Stair, past the shuttered dye-works, into the knot of lanes where the undercity leaned against the respectable one, and the footsteps followed, and he spun with his satchel raised like a shield—

—and a small mud-caked shape dropped off a wall and landed in front of him, breathing hard, eyes enormous in the lamplight.

Tam. Out of the archive. Out of the guild. Out of the locked sub-basement whose lock Paloma had personally oiled last month because the mechanism stuck, a kindness that was going to get them both killed.

"How," Paloma said.

Tam grinned, breathless, and held up one hand. In his dirty fist, wrapped in a scrap of black cloth torn from a pedestal drape, was something that hummed — that hummed loud, that hummed like a held breath finally let halfway go, that made the lamplight bend toward it the way grass bends toward sun.

Paloma's stomach dropped through the street, through the undercity, through the bedrock of Vess itself.

"That's lot thirteen," he said hoarsely. "That's — boy, do you know what you've done? There will be men with rings on every finger tearing this quarter apart by morning—"

Tam didn't understand the words. Tam understood the tone, and answered it the way he answered everything: by opening his fist a crack, so the humming spilled out warm as bread, and pointing down the lane — and for one impossible heartbeat Paloma *saw* it, saw cobbles that weren't there laid over the ones that were, saw a stair rail of green-painted iron ghosting through the Vess brickwork, saw a street sign in curved letters he could almost, almost read.

Home, said the phantom street. Home, said the shard in the boy's fist. And Tam's face, lit by borrowed lamplight, was the face of a child hearing his mother's voice from two rooms away.

Somewhere behind them, back up the Terrace, a bell began to ring — not the third bell, not any scheduled bell, but the quick ugly clangor of an alarm.

"They know," Paloma said. "Of course they know. Run."

Tam ran. Paloma, ledger-scribe, debtor, coward by his own long accounting, ran after him — into the undercity, into the dark, carrying nothing but a satchel of other people's paperwork and the sudden, terrifying knowledge that he had just become a thief of geography.

Behind them, the alarm bells multiplied, and the phantom street glowed faintly ahead like a road only they could walk.
