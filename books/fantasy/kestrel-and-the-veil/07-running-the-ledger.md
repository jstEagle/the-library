## 7. Running the Ledger

Tam had stolen things before. Bread, mostly. A coat once, in the winter after the world forgot him. He knew the shape of stealing: the cold pinch of it behind your ribs, the way your ears listened for boots that weren't there yet.

This was different.

The shard sat in his palm like a piece of evening. It was no bigger than a coin — a sliver of grey stone shot through with veins of gold that moved when he wasn't looking directly at them — and it was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. The auction house below him had smelled of candle fat and old money and, underneath it all, faintly and wrongly, of home.

He'd heard them say its name while he crouched in the rafters where Paloma had told him *do not move, do not breathe, do not exist*. Lot forty-one, provenance shard, recovered from the Vess deep-archive. *A fragment of the Kestrel capital district, north bank.* And then a man with rings on every finger had paid more coin than Tam could count for one small piece of the street where he'd been born.

Tam did not remember deciding to move. His body simply understood, before his mind caught up, that some things were not for selling.

So now he ran.

* * *

The undercity of Vess was a country of its own — the Guild called it the Roots, and it was all drainage tunnels and collapsed wine cellars and staircases that went down to doors that opened onto other staircases going down. Tam had lived in it for three days once, when he first arrived, before the Guild's night door and the tired scribe with ink-stained fingers who had looked at him like Tam was a sentence he couldn't parse.

Paloma was behind him. Tam could hear him — the scribe's breathing, ragged, and the slap of his shoes on wet stone, and every few strides a word hissed between them: *"Slow down— Tam— slow—"*

Tam didn't slow down. The shard was pulling.

He felt it the way you feel a hand on your collar. Every few dozen steps the stone in his fist would shiver, and the dark tunnel around him would *lean*, and for half a heartbeat the walls weren't walls anymore.

It happened first on the long stair by the tannery outflow. The shard warmed, the gold veins flared, and the stair became — briefly, impossibly — a street. Cobbles under his feet instead of steps. Lamplight the color of honey instead of torch-smoke. A smell of frying dough and river water and something floral he couldn't name but his body wept at recognizing. Shop signs hung overhead, painted in letters that curved like birds in flight, and Tam read them without trying, the way you read your own name.

Then it was gone. Steps again. Dark again. The tannery stink flooding back in like a tide returning to a beach.

But for that half-heartbeat, Tam had been standing on Marrowgate Street, in the city of Kestrel, two hundred years after it died.

He laughed. It came out of him cracked and wild, and he heard Paloma stumble behind him.

"What," the scribe gasped, "was *that*."

"Keep up!" Tam shouted over his shoulder, and ran faster.

* * *

They burst out of the Roots through a coal chute into the Lantern Ward, which was Vess showing off: gas lamps in wrought-iron cages, cafés with glass fronts, well-dressed people strolling as if the world were safe and always had been. Tam skidded on the cobbles, pulled Paloma into an alley between a milliner's and a moneylender's, and pressed his back against cold brick, chest heaving.

"They'll have guards on the doors," Paloma whispered. He had his ledger clutched against his chest like a shield, and there was ink on his cheekbone from where he'd pushed his hair back with dirty fingers. "By now they'll know exactly what's missing. Tam, do you understand what you've done? That shard is worth more than this entire ward. More than—"

"It's mine." Tam hadn't meant to say it out loud. It came out low and certain, in trade-tongue, and then again in the old language, because the old language was the only one big enough to hold what he meant. *Mine. Ours. It belongs to us.*

Paloma stared at him. In the lamplight leaking into the alley, the scribe looked young and terrified and stubbornly, stupidly kind, and Tam thought — not for the first time — that he still didn't understand why this man kept helping him. Debt, Paloma said. Ledgers. But Tam had watched men work off debts before, and none of them had ever hidden a child in an archive and taught him card games with a deck missing the queens.

"The buyers will come for it," Paloma said. "Corvane will come for it. We have until dawn, maybe less, and then every enforcer in Vess—"

The shard pulsed.

Tam felt it before he saw it — warmth blooming up his wrist — and then the alley *changed*. Not vanished. Overlaid. For one glorious, unbearable second, the brick wall to his left was a plastered wall with a blue door, and someone had chalked a hopscotch grid on the stones at his feet, and above them laundry lines crossed the gap between buildings strung with shirts and sheets that snapped in a wind that wasn't blowing in Vess. Somewhere just out of sight, a woman was singing. Tam knew the song. He knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat, and he could not have written down a single word of it if you paid him.

He turned toward the singing. Of course he turned toward the singing.

"TAM." Paloma grabbed his shoulder, hard, and the alley snapped back — brick, gaslight, the moneylender's sign creaking. The singing cut off mid-phrase. "What is it doing? What did you see?"

"My mother," Tam said. His voice came out strange and flat. "Not — I mean, I didn't see her. I heard her. She used to sing on washing day. Everyone hung their lines across the alleys and sang so the work went faster." He looked at his closed fist. Through his fingers, the gold veins glowed softly, patient as a pulse. "It's not showing me ghosts. It's showing me *where I am*. Where I really am. Underneath here."

Paloma went very still, the way he did when a number came out wrong in an audit. Tam had learned to read that stillness. It meant the scribe's whole understanding of the world had just been quietly rearranged.

"The shard remembers being part of Kestrel," Paloma said slowly. "And when it moves... it tries to put itself back."

"It wants to go home," Tam said. "So do I."

"That's not — Tam, Kestrel doesn't *exist*. There is no home to go to. Whatever those flashes are, they're echoes, they're—"

"Then why can I smell the bread?"

Paloma had no answer for that. Tam watched him reach for one anyway — watched him open his mouth, close it, and finally press his lips together in the expression of a man adding a figure to a column and hating the total.

Above the alley mouth, a whistle sounded. Two notes, sharp. Then answered by two more, further off. Paloma's face drained.

"Enforcers. Grid pattern — they're sweeping the ward." He grabbed Tam's sleeve. "Roots again. Now. Move."

* * *

They went down through a cellar hatch Paloma seemed to know about, though he wouldn't say how, and the Roots swallowed them. Tam ran with the shard fisted against his heart and let it pull him, because the pull was never wrong. When the tunnels forked, the stone warmed toward the left. When a passage choked with rubble, the warmth said *right, right, down*, and every time he obeyed it, the phantom overlays came faster and lasted longer, as if the shard were pleased with him, as if it were waking up.

A sewer junction became a plaza with a fountain. A flooded culvert became a canal lined with moored boats, lanterns doubled in black water. Once — only once, and it nearly stopped Tam's heart — the overlay held for five full seconds, and in those seconds he stood in a market square where vendors called out prices in the old tongue and a boy his own age chased a dog between the stalls, and everything was so *solid*, so loudly, rudely alive, that Tam reached out to grab a passing stranger's sleeve just to prove it was real.

His hand closed on empty air and cold water to the elbow.

After that he didn't reach anymore. He ran with his fists clenched and his eyes hot and let the dead city flicker around him like light through shutters.

Paloma caught up at a junction where three tunnels met, bent double, hands on knees. "You're — following — it," he wheezed. "Aren't you. You're letting a rock navigate."

"It knows the way."

"To *where*? There's nothing there!"

"There's everything there!" It tore out of Tam louder than he intended, echoing down all three tunnels. He lowered his voice. "You saw the folios. You read the entries. You know it was real. You told me — you said the ledgers are its borders, you said—" He stopped, because Paloma had gone rigid.

"I said that in the archive," Paloma whispered. "In the archive, where no one could hear. How do you—"

"You talk in your sleep. You talk in your sleep in *their language*, did you know that? You've been reading those folios so long it's gotten inside you." Tam stepped closer. The shard pulsed between them, and for a moment both their faces were lit gold, and behind Paloma's shoulder the dripping tunnel wall briefly showed peeling yellow paint and a child's height-marks scratched into a doorframe. *TAM, AGE 5. TAM, AGE 6.* He didn't look at it. If he looked at it he would stop moving, and if he stopped moving they would catch him, and if they caught him they would take the last piece of his country and sell it to a man with too many rings. "You're the only person in this whole rotten city who believes me. So believe me now. It's not leading us anywhere. It's leading us *back*."

* * *

The shard took them east, deeper than Tam had ever been, through passages where the Roots gave way to something older — pre-Vess stonework, blocks the size of carts fitted without mortar, and carvings worn nearly smooth. Paloma ran his fingers along one as they passed and made a sound like a man being punched.

"This is foundation work," he breathed. "This is *old* foundation work. This predates the city charter by—" He stopped walking. "Tam. Stop. Stop, look at this."

The passage opened into a vaulted chamber, dry despite the season, floored in flagstones. And the flagstones were wrong. They were laid in the pattern Tam had walked every market day of his earliest life — he knew it in his ankles before he knew it in his head — and set into the center of the floor, mortared into Vess masonry like a tooth in someone else's jaw, was a single slab of Kestrel cobblestone. Grey, gold-veined, unmistakable.

The shard in Tam's hand blazed.

The chamber filled. Not faded in, not flickered — *filled*, wall to wall, ceiling to floor, with the north bank of the Kestrel capital at golden hour. Stalls. Crowds. Gulls screaming off the river. The fountain with the leaping fish statue that children climbed on when the wardens weren't looking. The air was warm and smelled of spice and horse and bread, and it was so complete, so mercilessly complete, that Tam heard Paloma make a small broken noise beside him and felt the scribe's hand find his shoulder and grip it, whether to steady himself or Tam, impossible to say.

"How long has this been down here," Paloma whispered. "How long has Vess been sitting on top of—"

A gull landed on the fish statue and screamed at them, and Tam, sobbing without any permission from the rest of his body, started to laugh.

Then the vision guttered — the crowd thinned like smoke, the gull went translucent, the golden light drained — and in the last instant before the chamber returned to darkness and drip and cold, Tam saw figures at the edge of the fading square. Three of them. Standing very still amid the dissolving crowd, watching him. Watching *him*, not the market, not the shard. One raised a hand — not waving. Pointing. Down the eastern passage.

And a voice, thin as paper, spoke in the old tongue:

*"The mapmaker. Find the mapmaker. Before they find you."*

The chamber went dark. Tam stood shaking in the black, the shard cooling against his chest, Paloma's grip tight on his shoulder.

"Tam," the scribe said quietly. "Tell me you saw them too."

"The eastern passage," Tam said. "They said to find the mapmaker." He swallowed. "I don't know any mapmakers."

Paloma was silent a moment. Then, slowly, in the voice of a man reading an entry he'd hoped never to audit: "Sefa Ilun. Disgraced court cartographer. Struck from the rolls twenty years ago — her file was in the sealed folios, next to Kestrel's. I thought she was a footnote." He exhaled. "She mapped Kestrel. Before the end. She's the only one who ever drew it whole."

Somewhere far above them, muffled through two hundred years of Vess stone, a whistle sounded. Then another. Closer than before.

Tam tightened his fist around the shard, and together they ran east, into the dark, chasing a ghost's directions and a dead woman's maps — while behind them, patient as ink, the Guild came pouring down into the Roots.
