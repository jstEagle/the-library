## 8. The Cartographer's Debt

The shard of Kestrel rode in my breast pocket like a coal that had forgotten how to burn.

It was warm. It had been warm since Tam pulled it off the auctioneer's velvet tray three nights ago, and it hadn't cooled once — not through the sewers beneath the Saltmarket, not through the two days we'd spent sleeping in shifts in a coal cellar that smelled of old fires, not now, as I stood on the crooked doorstep of the only person alive who might know what to do with it.

Tam stood behind me, one hand fisted in my coat hem. He'd learned enough Vessian in a week to say *hungry* and *hide* and *don't let them take me*, and he said none of these now. He just held on and watched the door with the flat, patient stare of a child who has already seen the worst thing a world can do.

The house was a cartographer's house even before I read the brass plate. You could tell by the windows — each one slightly different in size, as if the builder had been paid per pane and had decided, out of spite or principle, that no two views of the street deserved identical frames. The plate read **S. ILUN, SURVEYS & CHARTS**, and beneath it, scratched into the paint by some passerby with time and a grudge: *liar*.

I knocked.

Nothing. I knocked again, harder, and heard something inside shift — not footsteps. Paper. A slow avalanche of it, someone wading through a sea of their own work.

"Shop's closed," said a voice like gravel steeped in tea. "Closed eleven years. Closed permanently. Go away."

"My name is Paloma Ashgrove," I said. "I'm a scribe with the Ledger Guild of Vess."

The silence that followed had texture. It was the silence of a woman deciding whether to fetch a knife.

"The Guild," she said at last, "can go away faster."

"I'm not here for the Guild." I pressed my palm flat against the door, against the warmth in my pocket. "I'm here about Kestrel."

I have heard doors open angrily. I have heard them open fearfully. I had never heard one open the way hers did — slowly, with a kind of terrible care, as though the woman behind it were afraid the word itself might spill out onto her step and she'd have to shovel it back inside.

Sefa Ilun was smaller than I expected. Cartographers, in my imagination, were tall — people who needed height to see over horizons. She was barely past my shoulder, sixty-odd years old, with ink stains up both forearms like old bruises and eyes the color of a winter sky deciding whether to snow. She looked at me. Then she looked down at Tam, and everything in her face changed at once, the way a map changes when you realize the river you've drawn for forty years has moved.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor little—"

Tam stepped behind my leg.

"He doesn't speak Vessian," I said. "Not properly. Not yet."

"What does he speak?"

"You already know."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them again, she was older by a decade, and she stepped back from her doorway like a woman opening a tomb.

"Get inside," she said. "Both of you. And if anyone followed you, don't tell me until they're dead, because I don't want to spend my last good hour being afraid."

* * *

Her shop was a drowned ship. Charts hung from every surface, layered so thick the walls had disappeared — coastlines over coastlines, mountain ranges stacked like sediment. And every chart, I realized within ten steps, was wrong. Deliberately wrong. Rivers bent where no rivers ran. Cities sat on coasts that owned no harbors. It took me a moment to understand what I was looking at, and when I did, my stomach turned over.

"They're decoys," Sefa said, watching me see it. "Forty years of work, all of it lies. Every accurate map I ever drew, I burned or buried. What's left is bait. If the Guild comes for me, they'll ransack this place and leave believing they found everything." She smiled without any humor in it at all. "They did come, once. Eleven years ago. They left with three carts of fiction and my license. Best trade I ever made."

"Why keep lying maps at all?" I asked. "If you're done with it—"

"Because a lie you maintain is a wall," she said. "And I have spent eleven years maintaining one very large lie, scribe, and I am very tired, and you are going to sit down and tell me exactly how a Guild man came to be standing on my step with a piece of a dead country in his pocket and its last living child hiding behind his coat. Start at the beginning. Do not skip anything. I will know if you skip anything."

So I told her. The audit. The boy at the night door. The sealed folios, the struck-through entries, Corvane's smile and her offer, the auction, the provenance shards, the flight through the undercity while phantom streets bloomed around us like flowers made of memory. I told it badly and quickly, because I kept expecting her to stop me, and she never did. She only grew quieter, and stiller, until by the end she was sitting with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral, which, in a sense, she was.

When I finished, she was silent for a long time.

"Show me the shard," she said finally.

I took it out. Up close, it was less impressive than it had been under the auction lights — a rectangle of something between glass and stone, cloudy, shot through with faint lines that might have been roads. Tam leaned toward it the way a cold child leans toward a hearth.

Sefa didn't touch it. She held her hand above it, palm down, and closed her eyes, and her lips moved — counting, I thought, or praying, or reading something written in a language that lived in the air rather than on paper.

Then she snatched her hand back as if burned.

"That's the Weeping Quarter," she whispered. "Northeast district. The tanneries, the terraced gardens, the long stair down to the river. I drew those streets when I was twenty-three years old." Her voice cracked clean through. "I measured them. I walked every one of them with a chain and a compass and a boy named Oren carrying my stakes. This—" she gestured at the shard, furious suddenly, tears standing in her eyes without falling, "—this is a *piece*. Someone cut my city into lots and sold it by the parcel."

"Yes," I said.

"And you want me to help you put it back together."

"I want to get him out," I said. "There's a door. I think there's a door out of Kestrel — out of whatever's left of it, wherever it's been kept. To open it I need the country's true name, and the name is scattered across ledger pages and shards and God knows what else. I need someone who can read the geography. Who can tell me which piece is which. Who remembers enough to recognize what I find."

Sefa laughed. It was an awful sound, dry as old vellum.

"Recognize," she repeated. "Scribe. Do you know why they struck me from the rolls? Why 'liar' is painted on my door? It wasn't the decoy maps. Those came after. It was because, two hundred years too late, I stood up in a Guild assembly and said the words *Kestrel existed*, and I said it with citations." She pushed herself up from her chair and began moving through the hanging charts, pulling pins, unhooking frames, her hands shaking. "I was young. I'd found references — shipping manifests, a treaty fragment, a hymnal with place-names that matched nothing on any living map. I thought I'd discovered something. I thought they'd *thank* me." She yanked a chart down hard enough to tear it. "They erased my license instead. They erased my name from the guild rolls, which, for a cartographer, is its own small death. You spend your life putting places on the map, and then one day you learn what happens to the person who tries to put a whole country back."

"So you won't help," I said. My chest felt strange saying it — half relief, half grief. "You'll give us food and a night's floor, and in the morning we'll—"

"I didn't say that." She stopped, her back to me, one torn chart hanging from her fist. "I said you'd pay."

I should have known. Everything in this business of impossible things had a price; I'd spent seven years cataloguing other people's payments. Bottled thunder, a widow's second shadow — every entry in the vault had cost somebody something they couldn't buy back.

"Name it," I said. "Coin. I have almost none, but—"

"Not coin." She turned around. Her eyes went to my hands, and I understood before she spoke, the way you understand thunder before the sound arrives. "Your mother's ring. The one you've been turning on your finger since you walked in. The one you touch whenever you're frightened, which, I note, has been often."

I looked down. She was right. I'd been worrying the band the whole time I talked, the way I had since I was nine years old and she pressed it into my palm along with her last words: *keep something of mine, so I'm somewhere.*

"It's just a ring," I said. "Brass. It's not worth—"

"It's not about worth." Sefa crossed back to me and sat down across the table, and her voice gentled into something almost kind, which was somehow worse. "It's about *material*. Everything true about Kestrel runs on inheritance — on things handed down, carried, kept. That ring is the oldest thing you own and the only thing you love. It's saturated. Forty years of your mother's hands, twenty-five of yours. If I'm going to do what you're asking — if I'm going to open my old surveys, call up streets I swore never to look at again, teach a Guild scribe to read a country that powerful people died forgetting — I need an anchor. Something real to hold the work to. Something loved." She extended her open palm across the table. "That ring is the down payment, Ashgrove. On me. On my help. On my life, if Corvane finds out I've touched any of this — and she will find out, because she always does."

"She'll kill you," I said.

"She'll try. She's tried before." The winter-sky eyes didn't blink. "The ring, scribe. Or take the boy and go, and I'll wish you luck from behind my locked door, and in six months some buyer will wear his mother as a brooch, and I'll add another lie-map to the wall and try to sleep. Your choice. I've made mine twice already tonight; I'm only waiting on yours."

Tam tugged my sleeve. He pointed at the ring — he'd noticed it weeks ago, the way children notice the small fixed stars in an adult's orbit — and then he pointed at himself, and then, with terrible clarity, he pointed at the shard in my pocket, and then at the floor, and then at his own chest. *This buys me home.* He didn't have the words. He didn't need them.

My mother had been a washerwoman who died owing four months' rent. The ring was brass because brass was what she could afford, and she'd worn it every day of her life anyway, and when she gave it to me she'd said, *it's not much, but it's proof I was here.* Keep something of mine, so I'm somewhere.

If I gave it away, she would be nowhere. That was the arithmetic of it, plain and cruel. One dead woman's somewhere, traded for one living boy's everywhere.

I thought about the ledgers. I thought about how I'd spent seven years believing that ink was safer than lives — that if you wrote things down carefully enough, in straight lines, in fair columns, the world couldn't hurt you. And I thought about how completely, how catastrophically wrong I had been, because the ledgers were the wound all along. The ledgers were the knife.

Maybe that was the lesson of the whole rotten trade: nothing is safe. There is only what you keep, and what you give, and which one you can live inside afterward.

I twisted the ring off my finger. It resisted — rings do, at the end, as if they know — and came free, leaving a pale band of skin that looked, absurdly, like a border.

"For my mother," I said, placing it in Sefa Ilun's palm. "However this ends — whatever happens to me — she was here. Her name was Maren Ashgrove. Write it down somewhere. Somewhere true."

Sefa closed her fingers over the brass, and something passed through the room — a pressure change, a hush, the feeling of a scale settling. The shard in my pocket pulsed once, warm as a heartbeat, and went cool for the first time in three days.

"Done," she said quietly. "Witnessed and done." She tucked the ring into a leather pouch at her belt, and then she was moving, suddenly all business, hauling charts down by the armload, tearing the beautiful lies off her walls. "Now listen, because we have very little time and a great deal of country to rebuild. My true surveys are buried under the tannery floor in Harrow Lane — yes, the same tannery, don't look at me like that, the universe has a sense of humor and it's a bad one. Maps, field notes, the master gazetteer. Two hundred and eleven pages, if the damp hasn't taken them. Between those and your Guild folios and whatever shards you can steal, we might assemble enough of the name to open your door."

She paused, one arm deep in a heap of decoy charts, and looked at me over the wreckage.

"But there's something you need to understand first, Ashgrove, before you commit to this road any further than you already have. The reason the Guild seals those folios isn't secrecy." Her voice dropped. "It's mercy. Or what passes for it. Because every page of Kestrel's true name takes something from the one who reads it. A memory. A real one, gone forever, in exchange for the country's. I lost—" she stopped, jaw working. "I lost things, the first time I mapped it. Things I only know I lost because the gaps have gaps. So before dawn, before we dig up my grave of paper, you're going to sit down and decide what you're willing to forget. Because the price is coming either way. It always comes. The only question is whether you pay it blind or choose it."

Outside, somewhere down the crooked street, a church bell began to count the hour — and stopped halfway, struck silent mid-note, as though the bellringer's hand had been caught around the rope.

Sefa went very still.

"That's not the bell," she said. "Kestrel's bells used to stop like that. Half-rung. Half-remembered." She turned to the shuttered window, and the winter-sky eyes had gone flint-hard. "Something just crossed into my street wearing a piece of that country. Get the boy into the back room. Now."

From my pocket, faint as a dying coal, the shard had begun to glow.
