## 9. Pages That Bleed

The shop smelled of old glue and older grief, which was the only honest smell in Vess.

Sefa Ilun kept her shutters closed even at midday. Light was a luxury for people whose work the Guild hadn't burned, and she had learned to draft maps by candle stub and memory — though memory, these days, was a currency running low.

She had the boy sit on the good stool, the one with the unbroken leg, because he was small and because he kept looking at her hands like they might do something holy. The scribe — Ashgrove, he'd said, Paloma Ashgrove, in the flat careful voice of a man reading his own obituary — stood by the door with his coat still buttoned, as if ready to bolt. Good instinct. She wished she'd kept hers.

"You said you mapped it," Ashgrove said. "Before."

"I said I mapped Kestrel." She laid her palm flat on the counter, over the scar in the wood where she'd once driven a stylus through it in fury or fear; she no longer remembered which. "I don't say *before*. There is no before anymore. That's rather the point of erasure."

Tam spoke then, that river-stone language of his, three syllables that landed in the room like coins dropped down a well. Sefa's breath caught. Two hundred years since anyone had spoken it aloud in her hearing, and her body answered before her mind could — her mouth shaped the reply before she'd decided to give it.

*You are welcome here,* she had almost said. In Kestrelish: *the door knows your name.*

"What did he say?" Ashgrove asked.

"He didn't say anything yet." She turned to the boy. "He asked if this was the place where the pages sleep. He can feel them. Can't you, child?"

Tam nodded slowly, the way children nod when an adult has finally stopped lying to them.

Sefa went to the back wall and pressed the loose brick above the third shelf. Behind it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay what remained of her life's work: not the maps the Guild had confiscated and burned in the square — those had been copies anyway, pretty ones, the kind you show to magistrates — but the true sheets. The ones drawn from walking. From counting. From listening to a country tell you its own name.

She unwrapped them on the counter beside Ashgrove's ledger pages, the ones he'd smuggled out under his coat, struck-through entries in Guild ink. Eleven sheets of hers. Nine of his. Together they made something neither half could be alone.

"Here's what nobody tells you about erased countries," Sefa said. "You cannot erase a thing completely. Erasure is expensive. It takes power, and power always leaves residue. So the erasers do the next best thing. They break the name into pieces and scatter the pieces into objects — ledgers, rings, bottled thunder, whatever will hold an impression. Then they sell the pieces, because selling is how you make sure no one ever gathers them back. A thief might steal a whole treasure. No one steals four hundred fragments held by four hundred owners who each believe theirs is the only one."

Ashgrove was very still. "The Ledger Guild."

"Was founded on the proceeds. Yes." She watched him absorb it. He was quicker than most Guild men; his face did the arithmetic without being told. "Your ledgers aren't records of impossible inventory, scribe. They're the country's borders, filed and catalogued. And every time Corvane sells a page, Kestrel gets a little smaller. Or it would, if it were anywhere at all. Instead the pieces just get... lonelier."

Tam touched one of her sheets, feather-light, and the ink moved. Not smudged — *moved*, the way water moves when something alive passes beneath the surface. Roads shifted a finger's width toward him. A coastline leaned in.

"Stop," Sefa said softly, and pulled the sheet away. "Not yet. You'll understand why in a moment, and I'd spare you the moment if I could."

She took out her knife — the mapping knife, short-bladed, for cutting quills and trimming vellum and, twice, for less gentle work — and pricked her thumb. One bead of blood, no more. She pressed it to the corner of the first sheet, where the title should have been and wasn't.

The paper drank it. And then, in ink the color of dried roses, letters surfaced across the page like fish rising:

*KESTREL, WHOSE NAME IS—*

And stopped. Cut off at the margin, continuing onto the next sheet, and the next, the name broken across twenty pages the way a sentence is broken across a shout.

"The true-name manuscript," Sefa said. "One name, many pages. Spoken whole, it opens the door — restores the country, wakes what sleeps, returns what was taken. Spoken in part, it does nothing but cost you." She looked at Ashgrove. "Do you know what it costs?"

"No," he said. But he knew. She could see him knowing and hating it.

"Blood opens the page. Memory pays for it. Every page you read, truly read — every fragment of the name you take into yourself — the page takes something back. A memory. It chooses. You don't get to choose." She flexed her cut thumb. "That's the toll. That's why the name was safe for two hundred years. Nobody wants to pay it. Nobody sane."

"How much?" Ashgrove asked. "Per page."

"It varies. Small pages take small things. The color of a particular afternoon. The taste of a soup. You barely notice, at first. That's the trap — it feels affordable." She began laying the sheets in order, edge to edge, the rose-inked letters flowing across the seams. "But the name is long, scribe. Twenty pages, and the last ones are the deep ones. The ones that hold the country's heart. Those don't take afternoons."

Tam was watching her face. Children always read faces first and words second, and this child had been reading faces professionally since before he could walk, probably. He said something quiet in Kestrelish, and Sefa heard the question in it before she understood the words.

*How much have you paid?*

She could lie. She had lied to magistrates, to buyers, to herself, for two decades. But the boy had walked out of a dead country carrying its last living breath, and lying to him felt like spitting in a well.

"Sit down," she said to both of them. "This takes telling."

* * *

Her daughter's name was Mirele.

She checked this every morning, the way other women checked their reflections. *Mirele.* It was still there. Some mornings it came easily, warm as bread. Other mornings she had to dig for it, and once — only once, years ago — she had dug and dug and come up with a shape, a smallness, a weight that had slept against her chest, and no name attached, and she had sat on the floor of this shop until dark until it surfaced again like a swimmer.

Mirele. Born in the spring of the year the Guild first came asking about her maps. Died — no. Not died. Sefa's hands went still on the sheets.

Died was simple. Died she could have carried.

What happened was this: when the erasure came, when the sky over Kestrel went the color of a closed eye, there were ways out for some. The cartographers knew them — they had mapped the borders, and borders, even dying ones, have doors. Sefa had a place on the last road out. One place. Mirele was six, and feverish, and the crossing required the traveler to remember their own name perfectly, all the way through, because the erasure ate names first and asked questions never.

A six-year-old with a burning fever could not hold a name steady for the length of a crossing.

So Sefa had done the arithmetic that mothers do in the worst hour of the world, and she had sent Mirele through anyway, holding her hand, feeding her the name syllable by syllable — *Mi-re-le, again, again, don't let go of it, my love, don't let go* — while Sefa herself stayed on the wrong side, holding the door, buying time for others, because someone had to hold it and because she believed — she had *believed* —

She had believed she would follow.

She hadn't followed. The door closed. The country ended around her like a sentence cut off mid-word. And when she woke in Vess, in this city that had never heard of Kestrel, she found that the erasure had taken its tithe from her too, quietly, in the night: not Mirele's name — she'd guarded that, spent decades guarding it — but everything else. The color of her eyes. Whether her laugh came high or low. Which parent she favored. The exact weight of her asleep.

Sefa had the name and nothing behind it. A hook with no coat.

"And so," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved, "when I tell you the price of these pages is memory, understand that I am not speaking theoretically. I have read four of the twenty. Over the years. Carefully. Each time telling myself I'd stop after one more." She tapped the fourth sheet, where the rose-ink letters waited. "The first page took the smell of my mother's kitchen. I noticed for a week and then forgot I'd ever noticed — that's how it works, you see, you can't mourn what you can't recall missing. The second took my wedding day. I had to be told I'd been married. The third took—" Her throat closed. She opened it by force. "The third took the year Mirele was born. All of it. I know she was born. I know I loved her from the first moment, because I know myself, or what's left of me. But the year itself is a hole, and things keep falling into it."

"And the fourth?" Ashgrove asked. Quietly. Like a man approaching a wound.

"The fourth took her face."

The candle guttered. Neither man moved.

"I have her name. I have the fact of her. I have a shape — small, warm, a weight against my chest. But her face—" Sefa spread her hands, empty. "I could pass her in this street, scribe. I could stand in front of her, and she could say *Mother*, and I would feel it here—" she pressed her fist below her ribs "—and I would not know her. That is what the pages cost. That is what remembering costs. And I am telling you now, before we begin, so that you can choose with your eyes open: there are sixteen pages left, and the last ones go deeper than mine did. The name doesn't want blood. Blood is just the knock. What it wants is *you*. All the way down."

Silence. Tam slid off the stool and came to her and did the thing that undid her more than any question: he took her scarred hand in both of his small ones and spoke in Kestrelish, slow, formal, the cadence of a ritual she half-remembered from a country that no longer existed.

*What is forgotten is not lost. What is lost is not gone. The country keeps what the keeper gives.*

"That's a mourning verse," she managed. "Where did you learn a mourning verse?"

He looked at her with eyes too old for the face, and answered in the same tongue: *Everyone in Kestrel knew it. We sang it on the last night.*

* * *

They worked until the candles burned to pools.

It went like this: Sefa bled a page, read the risen letters, spoke them aloud once so Ashgrove could transcribe the sound into Guild notation — his idea, and a clever one, the scribe hiding inside the rebel — and then sat with her hands flat on the counter while the page took its payment, watching her own face to see what left it.

Page five took her sister's voice. She learned this because she reached for it, testing, the way you probe a missing tooth, and found smooth nothing where thirty years of teasing and quarreling and comfort had been.

Page six took the name of the street she grew up on.

Page seven took—

Page seven she felt go, and couldn't identify what it had been, and that frightened her worse than any named loss, because the toll had begun taking things she didn't know she owned.

Ashgrove transcribed with a jaw like iron and sweat at his temples. Twice he stopped her. "Rest. The pages will keep."

"The pages will keep," she agreed. "We won't. Corvane knows you're gone by now, scribe. Every hour we spend being careful is an hour her enforcers spend being thorough." She pricked her thumb again. "Besides. You want to know the worst part? The obscene part?" The eighth sheet drank her blood and the rose-ink rose. "It's beautiful. Reading the name is beautiful. Each fragment is like — like hearing one instrument of an orchestra you'd forgotten existed. Even knowing what it's costing me, some traitor part of me *wants* the next page. That's how it was designed, I think. The erasers wanted the pieces to be pleasant to hold. Easier to sell."

She read the eighth fragment aloud. Ashgrove wrote it down. The page drank.

And this time it was Ashgrove who swayed, who put a hand on the counter, who went gray — because the toll, she realized too late, did not distinguish reader from hearer. He had listened to eight fragments now, spoken aloud, taken into himself through the ear instead of the thumb. His memories were bleeding too, slower, silently, a man hemorrhaging from a wound he hadn't agreed to.

"Ashgrove." She caught his elbow. "Say something. Tell me something true, right now, so I know what's still in you."

"My mother," he said. Then, longer, feeling for it: "My mother used to sing while she worked. I think. I think she sang." His eyes came up, and they were the eyes of a man standing at the edge of a hole in himself. "Sefa. I can't remember whether she sang or whether I only ever wanted her to."

"There's a way to shield it," she said quickly. "Wax in the ears, certain salts — I should have — I'm sorry, I've been paying alone so long I forgot the toll collects from crowds—"

"Don't apologize." He straightened. Something in his face had set, the way wax sets, the way decisions set. "Just tell me honestly. When the name is whole — when we open the door — does the toll stop? Does everything come back?"

Sefa looked at the twelve remaining sheets laid out like the skeleton of a sentence, at the boy asleep in the good chair with his cheek against a phantom coastline, at the candle stubs drowning in their own wax.

"No," she said. "What the pages take, they keep. That's the design. Remembering isn't a loan, scribe. It's a sale."

Ashgrove nodded slowly, and she watched him do the sum she'd done twenty years ago at a closing door: the total cost, weighed against the total need, and the terrible discovery that the columns balanced.

"Then we keep reading," he said.

Which was when the bell above her shuttered window rang — once, soft, the signal of a customer who knew the shop was closed and didn't care — and through the gap in the boards Sefa saw lantern-light, and boots, and the grey livery of the Ledger Guild, and heard a voice she knew from burned-map days say, pleasantly, through the wood:

"Mistress Ilun. Open the door. We know you have the scribe, and we know you have the boy, and Guildmaster Corvane sends her regards — and her offer."
