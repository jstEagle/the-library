## 10. Corvane's Hand

The knock came at the wrong hour, in the wrong rhythm, and Paloma knew before he opened the door that the night had turned against them.

Three sharp raps. A pause. Two more. Sefa's signal for *come now, come quietly* — except she was already inside, bent over the worktable with Tam's shard pinned under a brass frame, tracing the phantom streets it threw across her floor like light through water. She wouldn't have knocked on her own door.

Paloma set down the pen he'd been using to copy coordinates from the bleeding pages — his hand cramped, his head thick with the fog of a memory spent an hour ago, something about a river, he thought, or a boyhood dog; the loss had gone soft at the edges already, which frightened him more than the losing — and crossed to the shop's front window.

Down the crooked lane of Inkrow Row, lanterns moved. Not the loose, swaying lanterns of watchmen on their rounds. These traveled in formation. Four lights, evenly spaced, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who had never once been told *no* by a locked door.

Guild enforcers wore gray oilcloth over their armor so their chains wouldn't catch the light. Paloma could see the shapes of them now, could see the fifth man who walked ahead without a lantern at all — a reader, then. Someone who could feel pages the way sharks felt blood.

"Sefa." His voice came out low and wrong. "Sefa, they're here."

She didn't look up. Her stylus kept moving, laying a fine line of silver ink along the edge of the shard's projection, and where the line fell, the ghost-street beneath it sharpened — cobbles rising through Vess's mud like bones surfacing in shallow water. "Two more minutes. The name won't hold unless the boundary's closed."

"There is no *two more minutes*. There's a reader and four enforcers and they are thirty seconds up that lane."

That got her attention. She straightened, looked past him to the window, and Paloma watched something move across her face that he would spend the rest of his life trying not to remember: not fear. Calculation. The same look she'd worn when she'd taken his mother's ring, turning it in the lamplight, weighing what it cost him against what it bought.

"Back room," she said. "There's a hatch under the dye shelf. It drops into the tannery culvert. It smells like a crime against God, but it runs east two hundred paces before it surfaces."

"You're coming with us."

"I'm finishing the boundary." She was already back at the table, pulling drawers open, and now Paloma saw what she was doing — she wasn't packing. She was *stacking*. Every map she owned, every chart, every half-finished reconstruction of a country that no longer existed, gathered into a single pile with the efficiency of a woman who had rehearsed this moment in her head for years. "Tam. Wake up, love. Time to run."

The boy sat up from his nest of blankets behind the counter, hair wild, eyes immediately clear in the way of children who had learned that sleep was a thing you could be robbed of. He looked at the window. He looked at Sefa's pile of maps. And Paloma watched him understand, in the wordless way children understand, exactly none of the details and all of the meaning.

"The hatch," Sefa said, jerking her chin at Paloma. "Take him. Go."

"Not without—"

"*Go.*" For the first time since he'd known her, Sefa Ilun raised her voice, and it cracked the room like a whip. Then, softer, without looking at him: "Paloma. I've been waiting twenty years for them to come. I made my peace with this door a long time ago. You haven't even found yours yet."

The lanterns were closer. He could hear boots now, and the low murmur of the reader giving some instruction, and underneath it all — faintly, horribly — the sound of the sealed folios in Sefa's back room stirring on their shelves, pages shivering like leaves, answering the approach of someone who knew how to listen to them.

Tam grabbed Paloma's sleeve. His other hand was wrapped around the shard, knuckles white.

"She stays?" the boy asked, in his broken, accented Vessic. Three words he'd learned fast because they mattered: *stay*, *go*, *run*. "She stays?"

Paloma couldn't answer. That was the truth of it. He stood there with the hatch open at his feet and the smell of the culvert rising up around him like a held breath, and he could not make his mouth form a lie, and he could not make it form the truth either.

Sefa solved it for him. She crossed the room, took Tam's face in both hands, and spoke to him in Kestrel's tongue — the dead language that was not dead, that lived in this boy's mouth and in the ledgers of the Ledger Guild of Vess and, as of three nights ago, in the bleeding pages spread across Sefa's worktable. Paloma didn't understand the words. But he understood the shape of them, the cadence, the way the boy's chin trembled and then stilled. It was the shape of *be brave* and *remember me* and *this is not your fault*, all pressed flat into one sentence.

Then she kissed his forehead, turned Paloma bodily by the shoulder, and pointed at the hole in her floor.

* * *

The culvert was everything she'd promised and worse. They waded through muck that sucked at their boots, Tam riding Paloma's shoulders with the shard clutched against his neck, its cold seeping through both of them. Behind them, muffled by brick and earth, Paloma heard the front door of the mapshop give way — not broken, but *opened*, which meant someone had picked the lock rather than force it, which meant Corvane hadn't sent brutes. She'd sent professionals.

He counted his steps to keep from thinking. Forty. Sixty. The ghost-light of the shard bled faintly through Tam's fingers, and where it touched the culvert walls, Paloma saw things that weren't there: a drainage channel becoming a canal, brick becoming pale Kestrel stone, a grate overhead framing not Vess's smoky sky but a clean blue morning with gulls in it. The shard remembered streets. Even underground, it remembered.

At step one-ninety the culvert ended in a ladder and a trapdoor, and they surfaced in a coal yard behind a chandler's shop, filthy to the knees, breathing air that tasted of tallow and river.

And then Paloma made the mistake of looking back.

From the coal yard he could see, over the rooftops, the upper windows of Sefa's shop on Inkrow Row. One of them lit — orange, flickering, growing.

"No," he said. "No, she said she'd finish the boundary, she said—"

But even as he said it, he knew. He'd seen the stack of maps. He'd seen the way she'd built it, not packed it. She hadn't been preparing to surrender her life's work. She'd been preparing to *burn* it.

Flames crawled up through the window frames, and Paloma understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in, what she had done. Every map in that shop was a piece of Kestrel. Every chart was a fragment of the true name they'd spent days reconstructing. If the Guild took the maps, they took the country — again, the way they'd taken it two centuries ago, page by page, border by border, sold off to buyers who wanted erased power. So Sefa had fed it all to the fire first. Twenty years of work. Her daughter's name, somewhere in those folios, in her own handwriting. Gone in the time it takes paper to catch.

She'd burned the country to keep it out of their hands. And she'd burned it *loudly*, dramatically, a beacon on Inkrow Row — because a fire draws every eye in the district, and every enforcer in the building, and gives two fugitives in a coal yard the one thing money can't buy: a head start.

"She did it for us," Tam said quietly. Not a question. Children always knew.

Paloma's throat worked. "We have to keep moving."

"We can't leave her."

"We don't have a choice."

"Yes we do." The boy's voice hardened, and for a moment the accent fell away entirely and he sounded like something older, something that had watched a country choose its own ending. "You told me. Everyone who helps us pays. Your ring. Your memories. Now her. When does it stop being everyone else?"

Paloma had no answer for that either. It seemed to be the shape of his whole life lately: questions with no answers, doors with no keys, debts he hadn't known he was signing.

So he did the only honest thing left. He took Tam's hand, and they ran.

* * *

They ran until dawn grayed the sky, through the undercity Paloma had only ever known as lines on guild manifests — the smugglers' warrens under Tanner's Reach, the drowned chapel where the river thieves held their markets, a rope bridge across a sewer channel that Tam crossed laughing, actually laughing, high and wild, the shard's ghost-light making a second boy beside him made of blue morning and gulls. Wherever the shard went, Kestrel followed, thin as breath on glass. Once, crossing a market square, an entire street bloomed around them for three heartbeats — awnings, fountains, a woman calling a child home in the old tongue — and a dozen Vessic pedestrians stopped mid-stride and stared at nothing, and one old man began, very quietly, to weep.

By full light they were holed up in the drowned chapel's vestry, wrung out, filthy, alive. Tam slept almost instantly, curled around the shard like it was a hearthstone. Paloma sat with his back against wet stone and tried to inventory what remained.

His mother's ring: gone, paid to Sefa. His childhood: mostly gone, spent page by page, though he could no longer be sure which parts were spent and which simply faded, and that uncertainty was its own kind of loss. Sefa Ilun: gone, taken or burned or both — he didn't know which, and not knowing was a splinter under every thought. And the pages they'd recovered, the ones Paloma had copied onto his own body of notes before the fire — those remained. Folded against his chest, in oilcloth, next to his skin. Most of a name. Not all of it.

He should have felt despair. Instead he felt something colder and more useful: arithmetic. Sefa had paid in maps and freedom. He was paying in memory. The ledger of Kestrel's restoration balanced only if someone kept paying, and the Guild would keep sending collectors, and the final page — the capital, the heart of the name — was still out there in some buyer's vault, and every hour they waited, Corvane's people closed another street, bought another witness, tightened another border.

A sound woke Tam near noon: boots on the chapel steps, many of them, moving with formation discipline. Paloma was on his feet with a knife he didn't remember drawing, positioning himself between the boy and the door—

—and the door opened on a girl of perhaps sixteen, soaked to the waist, soot-streaked, carrying a sealed guild dispatch case in both hands like something consecrated.

"I was in the culvert," she said, in the flat voice of someone reciting words she'd rehearsed the whole way. "Under the shop. I heard everything. I'm the one who picked the lock for them — they pay me, I pick locks, it's what I do, I didn't know whose shop it was—" Her voice cracked. She thrust the case forward. "The reader kept one thing out of the fire. This was in the reader's satchel. I stole it when they weren't looking, because I heard what the tall one said about the boy, about what they'd do to him, and I—" She swallowed. "I'm Sefa's daughter's daughter. My grandmother burned herself a name once. I want to pay some of it back."

Paloma opened the case with hands that shook.

Inside, wrapped in guild-gray cloth, lay a single page — old, heavy, dense with struck-through script that still shifted and breathed when he looked at it directly. And across the top, in the Guildmaster's own precise hand, a routing note:

*Recovered from the Ilun premises. One item survived the fire. Deliver to Lord Ambrose Vey — he will know which piece completes his purchase.*

The capital. The final page. The heart of the name, riding in a courier's satchel toward the man who had bought it — and now, briefly, impossibly, in theirs.

Tam leaned over his shoulder, read the note slowly, and looked up at Paloma with eyes that held no homesickness at all now. Only direction.

"Vey," the boy said. "We go to Vey."
