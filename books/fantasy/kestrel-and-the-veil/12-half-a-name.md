## 12. Half a Name

The pages had a smell now. Paloma noticed it on the third night in Sefa's ruined back room, among the scorched shelves and the smell of ash that would never fully leave the walls. The recovered folios of Kestrel's name smelled like rain on warm stone — like a place after a storm, though no such place existed to smell of anything.

He'd catalogued stranger things for the Guild. Bottled thunder. A widow's second shadow, folded like linen. But those had been objects, curiosities, inventory with prices and provenance lines. These pages were something else. These were *geography*. He could feel it when he held them too long: a faint pull behind his sternum, as if his body were being gently tugged toward somewhere it had never been.

"Stop reading them," Tam said from the doorway.

"I'm not reading them."

"You're reading them with your face." The boy crossed the room and sat on the crate that served as his chair, his knees drawn up, his chin on them. He was nine years old and had the posture of an old man waiting for bad news he'd already memorized. "Sefa said every page costs you something. You've read four."

Paloma set the folio down carefully, aligning its edge with the others. Eleven pages lay before him on the trestle table, each one sealed in wax paper Sefa had scavenged from her own ruined stock. Eleven fragments of a true name. He knew what four of them had cost him without having to reach for the memories — reaching was the problem; the gaps were smooth-edged now, like river stones. He knew he'd had a childhood. He could no longer find the house it happened in.

"There are nineteen," Tam said. "Sefa counted. Nineteen pages make the whole name."

"Nineteen." Paloma looked at the eleven. "We have eleven."

"And one more thing." Tam reached into his shirt and drew out the shard — the provenance shard he'd stolen at the auction, the sliver of Kestrel's geography that had carried them through the undercity with phantom streets blooming under their feet. It had gone quiet since. It lay in Tam's small palm like a chip of grey glass, and even now, even dormant, Paloma could feel it pulling. "It wants to go home. I can feel it wanting."

* * *

Sefa Ilun came back at dawn, moving through her own shop like a ghost taking inventory of its haunting. She had burned her life's maps two nights ago to cover their escape from Corvane's enforcers, and she kept catching herself reaching for charts that were no longer there — her hand rising, turning, finding nothing, falling. Each time, her face did something complicated and then settled into nothing at all.

She spread the eleven pages across her worktable, weighted at the corners with brass map-weights, and stood over them for a long time without touching anything.

"The name is a sentence," she said finally. "Not a word. People think a country's true name is like a person's — one sound, one identity. It isn't. A country's name is everything it agreed to be. Every border it drew, every road it committed to, every promise its founders signed. That's why the Guild could sell it off in pieces. A sentence can be cut into clauses."

"How many clauses?" Paloma asked.

"Nineteen." She touched the edge of the nearest page without lifting it. "You have eleven. Which means eight are still out there, in the collections of people who bought pieces of a dead country because they wanted its power and didn't understand they'd bought a corpse's fingers." Her mouth twisted. "Except it wasn't a corpse. Was it? It was sleeping."

Tam said nothing. He had gone very still in the way he did when Kestrel came up — the stillness of a boy holding a door shut inside himself.

"Eight pages," Paloma said. "In eight collections. We know where three of them are. The auction records Sefa pulled list buyers for the river province, the western forests, the salt marshes. Those are recoverable. Hard, but recoverable."

"And the last one?"

Sefa looked at him. In the grey light coming through the broken shutters, her eyes had the flat shine of someone who has already done this arithmetic and hated the answer.

"The capital," she said. "Vey bought the capital."

* * *

Lord Ambrose Vey. Paloma had seen him once, at the auction, from the back of the room — a tall man in dove-grey who bid rarely and never lost, whose face had shown nothing at all while lesser nobles sweated over provenance shards like men gambling for organs. He hadn't cheered when he won the capital. He hadn't smiled. He had simply nodded, once, as if confirming an appointment he'd made years ago.

The Guild's records on Vey ran to four pages, and Paloma had read all of them before his memory of reading them could be taken from him. Ambrose Vey, third son of a minor house, elevated by marriage, enriched by trade concessions along the coast. Collector. Patron of cartographers — which was how Sefa had known his name before the auction, how she'd known to warn them. And one more line, in the cramped hand of some Guild clerk two decades dead:

*Purchased the Kestreline capital (Aurelin) entire, incl. residual civic structures. Buyer declined standard severance rites. Requested the city be delivered "intact and inhabited."*

*Intact and inhabited.* Paloma had turned that phrase over for two days. Cities couldn't be delivered intact and inhabited unless the inhabitants went with them. And the inhabitants of Aurelin had scattered themselves into objects and orphans on the country's final night — Tam had told them that much, in his broken translation, weeping over words neither Paloma nor Sefa could parse. Scattered. Folded into things. Into *ornaments*, if Vey's collection was what Sefa feared it was.

"He has people," Tam said quietly, when Paloma told him. They were on the roof of Sefa's shop, because Tam slept badly indoors now, and because the roof was where he did his hardest thinking, lying on his back watching a sky that belonged to some other country. "In jars. Or frames. Standing very still. My mother used to tell me about the scattering — she said some people wouldn't scatter all the way. Some people held on to too much of themselves and got caught between. She said they'd be asleep but not asleep. Waiting for the name."

He rolled his head to look at Paloma. "If Vey has the capital, he has the ones who got caught. He has them standing in his house like candlesticks."

Paloma felt something in his chest go hard and cold and very clear. It was not quite anger. Anger was hot and stupid and burned out. This was colder. This was the feeling he got during audits when he found a column of numbers that balanced perfectly because someone had made them balance — the feeling of fraud recognized, of a lie so well-kept it had become architecture.

"We need the page," he said. "The nineteenth page. The last clause of the name. Without it, the other eighteen are half a sentence — a door that opens onto nothing."

"Then we get the other seven first," Tam said. "And then we get his."

"Yes." Paloma watched the sky lighten over Vess's rooftops, over a city built on the proceeds of other people's erasures. "But understand what we're doing, Tam. We're not stealing a page. We're walking into the house of a man who owns a city, and asking him — one way or another — to give up the only thing keeping his collection intact. The moment the name is spoken, everything he bought stops being his. Every shard, every citizen, every clause. The name doesn't just open a door. It dissolves the sale."

Tam sat up. For the first time since Paloma had known him, the boy smiled, and it was not a child's smile at all. It was old and thin and fierce, and it belonged to a country.

"Good," Tam said.

* * *

They spent six days planning, and the planning changed Paloma in ways he didn't notice until later.

Sefa taught him Vey's world. She had mapped for the man once — before the erasure, before her disgrace — and she remembered his estate the way she remembered coastlines: precisely, obsessively, with the affection stripped out. "Ashcourt," she said, sketching it from memory on the back of a burnt chart. "North of the city, past the vineyards. The house is older than his title; he married into it and pretends otherwise. Public rooms on the ground floor, gallery above, private wing behind the orangery. He shows the collection by appointment only. Appraisal visits, acquisition consultations. He likes experts. He likes being the most knowledgeable man in any room, which means he'll test you."

"Test me how?"

"By knowing more than you pretend to. So don't pretend to much. Come in as a Ledger Guild appraiser — your credentials are real, your debt is real, and Corvane will assume you're running, not scheming. Let her think that. Running men are predictable. Scheming men get audited." Sefa tapped the sketch. "You go in, you appraise something, you find the page. Pages have a weight to them — you know that now. You've been carrying eleven of them around like a stone in your chest. Trust the pull."

"And if the pull leads somewhere I can't walk out of?"

"Then walk out anyway." She said it without looking up from the sketch, and Paloma heard what was underneath it: *I walked into Vey's orbit once. Look what it cost.* He thought of the daughter whose face she could no longer remember, the price she'd paid reading pages of her own reconstruction, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a debt he couldn't cover.

Tam trained too, in his way. He practiced going still — truly still, the stillness of the caught-between citizens, hands loose, eyes soft-focused, breathing shallow. "If they put me in a frame," he said, "I want to be good at it." Paloma had refused, flatly, to bring him anywhere near Ashcourt, and Tam had refused, equally flatly, to accept the refusal, and they had compromised the way refugees compromise: Tam would stay with the pages, hidden in the cellar Sefa had walled off generations ago, guarding eleven clauses of a country's name with a stolen shard in his fist and instructions to run if anyone knocked.

"You'll come back," Tam said on the last night. Not a question. A term of contract, offered in the old tongue first and then repeated in Vessic, as if saying it twice made it binding in both languages.

"I'll come back," Paloma said, and heard, in his own voice, the ledger-clerk's cadence — the tone of a man recording a liability he intends to honor.

* * *

On the seventh morning, Paloma dressed in his Guild greys, pinned the appraiser's badge to his collar, and looked at himself in Sefa's cracked mirror. The man who looked back was thinner than the one who had started this, harder at the jaw, with shadows under his eyes that no ledger ever recorded. Four memories gone. He inventoried them the way he inventoried everything: he knew the count, not the contents. A childhood home. A winter. A teacher's name. Something else, something warm, that left a shape like a hand.

He gathered the eleven pages, wrapped in oilcloth, and gave them to Tam through the cellar door.

"Hold the country," Paloma said.

Tam took the bundle against his chest the way children hold things they mean to keep forever. "Hold the door," the boy answered, in Kestreline.

The pull led him north. He didn't need a map. Eleven clauses of a true name tugged at him from inside his own ribs, and Lord Ambrose Vey's estate sat at the end of the tug like a knot at the end of a thread, and Paloma followed the thread through the vineyards with the morning fog burning off around him, and the house rose up out of the mist exactly as Sefa had drawn it — old stone dressed in newer money, cypress rows, an orangery glowing pale green at the eastern wing.

At the gate, a footman in dove-grey livery asked his business.

"Appraisal," Paloma said. "Ledger Guild of Vess. By appointment."

The footman consulted a list he did not appear to need. "Lord Vey expects you."

*I expect you too,* Paloma thought, and followed the footman up the drive, counting doors, counting windows, marking exits the way Sefa had taught him, the way a scribe marks margins — small notes in the white space of a page, waiting to be read.

The front doors opened onto a hall that smelled of beeswax and, beneath it, faintly, unmistakably, of rain on warm stone.

Somewhere in this house was the last page of Kestrel's name. Somewhere in this house, if Sefa was right, were the caught-between citizens of Aurelin, standing very still, waiting for the word that would wake them.

Paloma squared his shoulders, adjusted his badge, and stepped across the threshold into the collection of Lord Ambrose Vey — carrying half a name in his chest and a plan sharpening itself with every step, like a blade laid slowly against a whetstone.

Behind him, the doors closed without a sound.
