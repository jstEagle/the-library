## 13. An Invitation in Gilt

The invitation arrived the way all dangerous things arrive in Vess: beautifully, and with a bill attached.

Lord Ambrose Vey required an appraiser of provenance for a private viewing. Discretion assured. Compensation generous. The Guild's placement office sent three names, and Corvane sent mine — which told me everything. She wasn't offering me a job. She was offering me a leash with a very long rope, and she wanted to see how far I'd run before it went taut.

I went anyway. A man who has watched a country get sold off piece by piece does not decline an invitation to the buyer's house.

* * *

Vey's estate sat above the city like a held breath — white terraces cut into the cliff at Silverrest, where the fog never quite committed to arriving. The carriage that collected me from the gate had no driver I could see, which meant either automation or a servant paid enough to be furniture. Both, probably.

I had dressed the part: good grey coat, scribe's ink-stain scrubbed as far from my fingers as soap would allow, the forged credentials Sefa's last contact had built for me in four fevered hours before the raid took her. *Maren Quill, freelance provenance assessor, retained by the Ashcombe Trust.* The name was invented; the trust was real; the paperwork was better than mine.

At the door, a steward examined my letters with the delicacy of a man handling something that might still be alive.

"Master Quill. Lord Vey is eager to begin." He paused, and his eyes did a small, terrible thing — they flicked down to my hands, then back up, cataloguing. "You'll want to wash first. His lordship prefers clean hands on old things."

*Old things.* I filed the phrase away and followed him through halls that were less decorated than curated. Every object stood alone on its plinth or behind its glass, lit like a saint. A clock that ran backward. A sword with no edge and no shadow. A jar of what looked like weather.

And everywhere, the same quiet. Not silence — houses have silence. This was *held* quiet, the hush of a room where something is listening.

* * *

Vey received me in the long gallery, and he was not what I expected. I had built him out of Corvane's smile: fat, cruel, jeweled. Instead he was thin and grey-eyed and gentle-looking, the kind of man who apologizes to chairs when he moves them. He wore no rings. That frightened me more than jewels would have.

"Master Quill." He shook my hand himself, which stewards everywhere would have fainted over. "You come recommended by people who don't exist. I admire that. It takes craft."

"Lord Vey is kind."

"I'm not, actually. But I am curious, and curiosity looks like kindness from the inside." He gestured down the gallery. "You've seen the pieces?"

"Some."

"Then you know they're not curiosities." He began walking, and I fell into step because refusing would have been louder than any question. "Everyone who handles them feels it. Weight without mass. The appraisers call it resonance. I call it *provenance*. These objects were somewhere. They belonged to somewhere. And that somewhere was removed from the world so thoroughly that these are the only evidence it ever stood."

"Kestrel," I said, carefully bored.

His step didn't falter, but the temperature of his attention did. It settled on me like frost forming.

"You know the name."

"It appears in sealed folios," I said. "Struck through. A scribe notices struck-through things. It's the whole trade."

"Mm." He resumed walking. "Yes. The Guild keeps its ledgers the way a butcher keeps knives. Sharp, oiled, and never discussed at dinner." He stopped before a tall case and touched the glass with one finger, an oddly tender gesture. "Do you know what I bought here, Master Quill? Not objects. *Geography.* A bridge. A hillside. Eleven feet of a street called Lantern Row. When I hold this—" he tapped the case, inside which lay a flat shard of grey stone veined with gold, "—I can smell bread baking. There is no bakery within forty miles. There is no *street* within forty miles that ever smelled of anything but tallow and fish. And yet."

He turned to me, and his gentleness was suddenly the worst thing about him.

"The Guild sold me a country in installments and called it salvage. I intend to own all of it. Which is why you're here." He smiled. "You're going to tell me what the remaining pages are worth, and then you're going to help me buy them."

My heart performed a slow, controlled collapse. Corvane hadn't sent me to test my loyalty. She'd sent me because Vey wanted a Guild insider, and she was willing to spend me to keep him sweet.

"I'm an appraiser," I said. "Not a broker."

"Everything is a broker if the price is right. Yours, I'm told, is seven years of indenture." He said it lightly, the way one mentions the weather in someone else's country. "Shall we work? I'll show you the collection. You'll tell me what I have. We'll both pretend this is commerce."

* * *

It was commerce the way a vivisection is medicine.

He walked me through sixty pieces, and I did the thing I do — the weary, competent thing, the only armor I have ever owned. I dated shards by their ledger-marks. I identified a fragment of Kestrel's western wall by the mason's tally scratched into its underside, a tally I had personally catalogued two years ago in a folio I was never supposed to open. I gave him numbers. He nodded as though I were confirming things he already knew, and perhaps I was.

And all the while, the resonance pressed against me like a crowd pressing against a door.

Because it wasn't metaphor. Sefa had taught me that much before the enforcers came for her shop: erased things don't vanish, they *displace*, and displacement leaves pressure. Each shard in Vey's gallery was a wound in the world wearing a display case, and standing among sixty of them was like standing in a room where sixty people were trying to remember their own names.

Piece thirty-one stopped me cold.

It was small — a door, an actual door, oak, iron-banded, no taller than a child. It stood freestanding on a plinth under glass, and the label read *Threshold Fragment, Provenance Verified, Lot 88.*

A door. From a country that had been folded out of existence. My mind went where it always went now: to Tam, asleep in Sefa's burned-out back room with a stolen shard under his pillow, dreaming streets onto streets. To the name we were assembling page by page, each page costing me a memory I'd never get back — I had already lost the smell of my mother's kitchen, and I grieved it the way you grieve a tooth.

"That one interests you," Vey observed.

"Doors interest appraisers," I said. "They complicate valuation. Is it functional?"

"No. Believe me, I've tried." Something moved behind his mildness — hunger, quickly sheathed. "It opens onto nothing. Or rather, it opens onto *absence*, which is worse. Whatever it once opened onto has been... elsewhere, for two hundred years." He studied me. "You look pale, Master Quill."

"Long stairs," I said. "Rich air."

He laughed, genuinely, and moved on, and I made myself move with him, and I hated how easily my legs obeyed.

* * *

The garden was his idea. "Appraisers judge better outdoors," he said, which was nonsense, and I understood it was nonsense the moment we stepped out, because the garden was where he kept the ones that couldn't be cased.

Statues, the steward had called them earlier, in the tone servants use for things they've been ordered not to call anything else.

There were nineteen of them along the rose walk. Marble-white figures in lifelike poses — a woman leaning to smell a flower she could not possibly smell; a man mid-stride, coat tails carved flying; a girl seated on a bench with her hands folded, chin lifted as if listening to music. The craftsmanship was impossible. Skin with pores. Fabric with weave. Eyes—

Eyes that were wet.

I stopped walking. Vey kept walking, three steps, four, then turned, patient, expectant, like a host presenting the wine cellar.

"They're beautiful, aren't they," he said. "My ornement garden. The centerpiece of the collection. You see, Master Quill, when Kestrel was erased, most of its people chose scattering — they poured themselves into objects, into the land itself, which is why the shards resonate. But some few..." He touched the stone shoulder of the striding man, gently, the way he'd touched the case downstairs. "Some few were caught whole. Preserved mid-erasure. Alive, aware, and fixed. The finest provenance in existence: not a piece of a country, but a citizen of it, intact."

The woman at the roses. Her stone fingers were curled around the bloom, and there — there, where a tear would track — the marble shone faintly, freshly, as though something inside was still weeping on a schedule no one could see.

"They can hear," Vey said conversationally. "I believe they can hear. They don't respond, of course. Two hundred years of practice and none of them have managed so much as a blink. But the eyes stay damp, which suggests the machinery of grief still runs." He smiled at me, mild as milk. "I find it restful, gardening among them. They never disagree."

Cold went through me, and it did not stop going through me. This was not the dread of the auction house, the horror of ledgers and lots. This was older and simpler: a man showing me prisoners and calling them flowers.

"How do you feed them?" I asked. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it.

"Feed them?"

"Resonance degrades. Your shards downstairs are dimmer than their ledger entries describe — I checked the marks. Whatever keeps these nineteen *aware* costs something. Either you're feeding them, or you're spending them."

For the first time, Lord Ambrose Vey looked at me with actual interest, the way a chess player looks at an opponent who moves an unexpected piece.

"You *are* good," he said. "Yes. Awareness draws on the surrounding provenance. I rotate the garden near the stronger pieces. It's why the girl on the bench sits where she sits — the Lantern Row shard is directly beneath her bench, set into the flagstones. She was fond of that street, I'm told." He tilted his head. "Told by whom, I've never determined. The Guild's records on individual citizens are poor. Perhaps you could help with that too, in time."

I looked at the girl on the bench. Young — younger than Tam. Her stone hands were folded in her lap, and her face was tilted up and slightly to the left, toward a sound that had ended two centuries ago, and her cheeks gleamed.

Somewhere under my ribs, past the fear, past the appraiser's arithmetic, something closed its fist.

I had spent my whole life believing ledgers were safer than lives. Numbers didn't weep. Columns didn't get planted in gardens by men who found them restful. But I had been wrong about what the ledgers *were* — they were borders, Sefa said, they were the country's bones — and I had been wrong about what safety cost, and standing in that garden I finally understood the shape of the bill.

Corvane sold geography. Vey bought people. And the difference between them was only which end of the transaction you found restful.

"You're angry," Vey said softly.

"I'm appraising," I said.

"Yes. That's anger, in your profession." He walked on, and I walked with him, because the leash was long but it was a leash. "Nineteen citizens. Sixty-one shards. Three rooms of documents, including, I'm told, several pages of a certain manuscript the Guild has been careless about." His grey eyes rested on me with terrible gentleness. "Pages that have been *leaving* it. One gathers."

The garden was very quiet. Somewhere in the white rows, marble cheeks shone with tears that had nowhere to fall.

"If you find whoever is collecting those pages," Vey continued, strolling, admiring his roses, "you might mention that I pay better than anyone. And that I already own the final page — the capital itself, the keystone of the name. Whoever assembles the rest will need to come to me for it." He turned, and his smile was warm and bottomless. "So really, Master Quill, whoever they are — they're already invited."

* * *

I left at dusk with my fee in a purse too heavy and my hands steady in the way that costs the most.

In the carriage, descending through the fog line toward the city, I took out my notebook and wrote nothing about shards or valuations. I wrote: *Nineteen alive. Girl on the bench, young, dark hair, listening posture. Bench over Lantern Row shard.*

Then I wrote: *Tam's mother. Last seen running toward the erasure with him in her arms. If any citizen was caught whole—*

I couldn't finish it. Some columns refuse to balance.

But Tam would finish it. Tam, who remembered festivals and bread and a voice singing in a dead tongue; Tam, who had crossed half of Vess on the strength of a stolen street. Tomorrow I would have to sit across from a nine-year-old boy and decide whether to show him a garden.

Below me, Vess lit its lamps one by one, and above me, on the white terraces, nineteen people wept in the dark where no one was supposed to notice — and one of them, I was suddenly, horribly sure, was waiting for exactly that.
