## 14. The Ornament Garden

The garden was behind glass.

Tam had expected a lot of things from Lord Ambrose Vey's estate — dogs, guards, the smell of money like old meat. He had not expected a greenhouse the size of a church, and he had not expected it to be full of people.

They stood between the orange trees and the camellias, spaced out like furniture, each one on a low marble plinth. Men in fine coats. Women in dresses gone out of fashion two hundred years ago. A child holding a wooden bird. All of them still. Not dead-still — Tam knew dead, had slept in ditches beside it once in Vess's undercity — but *held*-still, the way water holds when you freeze it mid-splash.

He pressed his palm flat against the cool glass wall and forgot how to breathe.

Because he knew them.

Not their names — not all of them — but the *shape* of them. The way you know the shape of your own street with your eyes shut. A woman by the third plinth wore her shawl the way Kestrel women wore shawls, folded twice and pinned at the left shoulder so the right arm stayed free for work. A man near the far wall stood with his weight on one hip, thumbs hooked in his belt, exactly the way ferry-men stood waiting for the last crossing of the night.

"Kestrel," Tam whispered, and the word came out in the old tongue without asking his permission. *Kestrel. Kestrel. Kestrel.* His own language sounded small in this huge glass room, like a bird let into a cathedral.

Paloma's hand landed on his shoulder. "We shouldn't be here."

"We're always somewhere we shouldn't be." Tam didn't look away from the glass. "That's just where we live now."

It was true. Three days ago they'd been nobody — a runaway ledger-thief and an indentured scribe with ink stains to his elbows, sleeping in Sefa Ilun's map shop above a chandler's. Then Sefa had burned her life's work to cover their escape and been taken by Corvane's enforcers, and Paloma had gone quiet in a way that scared Tam more than shouting would have, and they'd come here, to Vey's estate, because the final page of Kestrel's name lived behind these walls and there was no other way to get it.

Paloma had forged himself an appraiser's commission. It had taken him one night and most of his remaining dignity. He looked wrong in the borrowed coat — too thin, too watchful, hands that kept wanting a pen — but the gate guards hadn't looked closely. Rich houses never do. They see the coat and stop seeing the man.

So here they were. Paloma upstairs appraising silverware with shaking hands while Tam, who was small and quick and officially did not exist, slipped down through the service corridors to find whatever the ledgers said Vey kept in his private conservatory.

Tam had thought: pages. Maybe a locked case. Maybe another shard of country, like the one he'd stolen from the auction house, the one that had shown him phantom streets all the way through the undercity until his chest ached with homesickness for a place that wasn't there anymore.

He had not thought: people.

* * *

There was a door in the glass wall. Of course there was — Vey liked to walk among his collection, apparently. Tam tried it and found it unlocked, because what kind of thief breaks into a garden of statues? Nobody steals what everybody thinks is decoration.

The air inside was warm and green and wrong. Under the smell of oranges and wet soil there was something else, something faint and sweet like flowers left too long in a closed room. Tam had smelled it before. He'd smelled it the night Kestrel ended — though he couldn't remember that night, not really, only flashes, only his mother's hand closing around his wrist and her voice saying words he'd carried across two centuries without understanding them until recently:

*Run. Forget your name if you have to. Live.*

He walked down the row.

Up close, the preserved citizens were worse. Their skin had the waxy sheen of fruit polished by a merchant. Their eyes were open. That was the worst part — the eyes were open, and they were *looking*, or almost looking, focused on some middle distance where maybe they could still see something. Their chests didn't move. Their hair had grown, some of them, a finger's length of grey coming in at the temples, as if time touched them only grudgingly, only enough to remind them it existed.

Tam stopped in front of the woman with the shawl.

She was young. Younger than his mother should be, after two hundred years — but of course, that was the point, wasn't it? Time hadn't been allowed to touch them properly. She had dark circles under her eyes and flour dusted in the creases of her knuckles and a burn scar on her forearm shaped like a crescent moon, and Tam knew that scar. He didn't know how he knew that scar. He knew it the way he knew the shape of streets he'd never walked: from the inside, from the fragments the erasure had left buried in him like shrapnel.

His mouth went dry. "Mama?"

The word came out tiny. In the old tongue it meant more than mother — it meant *the-one-who-holds*, the-one-the-house-turns-around. He said it again, louder, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy half his age.

The woman didn't move.

But — Tam leaned closer, close enough to fog the wax of her cheek with his breath — her eyes moved. Just slightly. Downward. Toward him. And in them, deep down under two hundred years of held stillness, something *flickered*. Like a lamp behind a shuttered window. Like someone hearing their name called from very far away and turning toward the sound even though they can't rise, can't answer, can't do anything but turn.

"No," Tam whispered. "No, no, no — you're awake. You're awake in there."

Her eyes stayed fixed on him. The flicker didn't fade. If anything it brightened, a candle catching after long rain, and Tam felt it like a hook behind his ribs.

"Mama, it's me. It's Tam." He grabbed her hand. It was warm. Warm! Not cold like the dead, warm like bread, like a hearth, like everything home had ever meant, and it didn't move, didn't squeeze back, lay in his grip like a sleeping thing that couldn't wake. "I got out. I got out and I came back, I came back for you, I—"

He was crying. He noticed distantly, the way you notice weather. Tears dripped off his chin onto her frozen fingers and he wiped them away with his sleeve, furious at himself, because what if she could feel that, what if she could feel everything and just couldn't say?

*Awake. Aware. Unable to move.*

For two hundred years.

* * *

He sat down on the floor of the greenhouse, cross-legged at the foot of her plinth, the way he used to sit at her feet in a kitchen he could barely picture anymore, and he talked. Because what else do you do? What else had he ever done, in ditches and doorways and the grey halls of the Ledger Guild, except carry the sound of her voice forward through the dark?

"I found the ledgers," he told her. "The Guild had them. Pages of the country, cut up and sold like — like cloth. Like meat. There's a man named Paloma, Mama, a scribe, he's — he's finding the pieces. We're putting the name back together. When we finish, the door opens. The country comes back. You'll be able to move. You'll be able to *breathe*."

Her eyes flickered. He chose to believe it was yes.

"There's a bad woman named Corvane. And a man named Vey who owns this house. They sold the country in pieces, Mama. Streets. Rivers. I saw them bid on a piece of the harbor like it was a horse." His fists curled on his knees. "I'm going to help stop them. I stole a shard already. Did you know that? Your son is a thief. You'd box my ears."

The flicker again. Steadier now. And then — Tam's heart slammed against his teeth — her little finger twitched. Once. Barely. A movement smaller than a moth's wingbeat, but a movement, real and deliberate, in a body that had been marble for two centuries.

"You can hear me." His voice shook apart. "You can hear me the whole time."

The finger twitched again. Twice. Slow. Deliberate. And Tam, who had spent months learning to read the Guild's cramped notation, who had learned to read Paloma's silences and Sefa's smoke signals of mood, understood with a lurch that she was trying to speak. Trying to tap out words the only way left to her.

One twitch. Pause. Two twitches. Pause. One.

He counted. He made himself count, made himself be the scribe's student instead of the sobbing child, and the pattern repeated, patient as falling snow, and it took him four repetitions to understand, and when he understood he wished he hadn't.

*No. Run. Now.*

"Mama—"

Two twitches. Harder. *Run. Now.* And then her eyes moved — actually moved, dragging themselves sideways with visible effort, toward the far end of the greenhouse, toward the shadowed corner past the camellias where the light didn't reach.

Tam turned and looked.

At first he saw nothing. Then his eyes adjusted, and he saw that the corner wasn't empty. There was a chair there. An ordinary chair, cushioned, angled to face the garden — a viewer's seat, the kind of chair a man sits in to enjoy his possessions. And on a small table beside it lay a book, bound in green leather, its cover stamped with a sigil Tam knew better than his own heartbeat now: the interlocked rings of the Ledger Guild.

And the chair was not empty.

A man sat in it. Old, heavy, dressed in a dressing gown worth more than the whole archive Paloma had served his debt in. He held a glass of wine and he was watching Tam with the mild, pleasant expression of a man watching a bird land on his fountain.

"Well," said Lord Ambrose Vey. "It speaks."

* * *

Tam was on his feet before he knew he'd moved, putting himself between the chair and his mother, arms out, the way you'd guard a doorway. His whole body was screaming *run* — his mother's tapped warning still pulsing in his ears — but his feet had grown roots. Because behind him was the only piece of her he'd found in two hundred years, and he would not turn his back on it.

"How long," Tam said. His voice came out low and rough, older than he was. "How long have you been sitting there."

"Since the boy came down the corridor humming a song no one has hummed in two hundred years." Vey sipped his wine. "You'd be amazed what carries. Walls are thin in old houses. Songs thinner." He gestured lazily with the glass at the rows of still figures. "Do you know what they are, boy? Truly?"

"They're people."

"They're *provenance*." Vey said the word the way priests said holy names. "Every one of them is a citizen of record. Do you understand what that means? A country is not land. Land is cheap. A country is its people — their habits, their speech, their songs. These—" he waved the glass at the frozen woman with the flour on her hands, and Tam wanted to break every finger on that soft white hand, "—are the last living texts of Kestrel. Each one is worth more than your entire nation was, when it was merely a nation. Alive, they appreciate. Dead, they're just corpses with good stories. So they stay exactly as they are. Forever."

"Forever," Tam repeated. The word tasted like ash and glass.

"It's a mercy, if you think about it." Vey leaned forward, and his eyes were pale and interested and utterly empty of cruelty, which was somehow the most horrifying thing about him. "War would have killed them. Erasure nearly did. Here they wait, safe, perfect, until a buyer comes along who wants a *complete* Kestrel — and oh, buyers do dream of that. Whole countries are the rarest vintage there is." He smiled. "And now one walks into my garden on its own legs. Do you know how much you've just saved me in acquisition costs?"

Tam's hands were shaking. He thought about the shard hidden in his shirt, the fragment of harbor street warm against his skin. He thought about Paloma upstairs, surrounded by guards, wearing a forged commission and a face that had never once managed to lie successfully. He thought about his mother's finger tapping *run* against two hundred years of silence.

"You won't get us," Tam said. "Any of us. We're opening the door. We're bringing all of them back — every single one — and when we do, men like you will have nothing left to sell."

Something changed in Vey's face. Not anger. Worse. *Delight.*

"The door," he said softly. "So that's what the scribe is after. The true name." He set down his glass and rose from the chair, unhurried, a big man unfolding, and behind him, at the edge of Tam's vision, the greenhouse door opened and two servants stepped through — no, not servants; they moved like enforcers, wide-shouldered, hands loose. "Boy, I've spent forty years and three fortunes assembling this collection. Do you imagine I'll let a mud-lark and a debt-scribe unmake it with a bedtime story?"

Behind Tam, faint as a moth's wing, his mother's finger began tapping again. Faster now. Frantic.

*Run. Run. Run.*

Tam ran.

He went over the camellia bench, through the service door, into the corridors, lungs burning, and behind him he heard Vey's voice, calm and carrying, giving orders that were not shouts because men like Vey never shouted: "Lock the grounds. Find the scribe. And bring me the boy *unmarked* — he's the crown jewel."

Tam ran, and the shard against his chest grew warm, and ghost-streets flickered at the edges of the real ones — cobbles overlaying marble, a lantern that belonged to no century swinging overhead — as if the stolen piece of home were trying to shelter him, trying to hide him in itself.

He burst out through a kitchen door into the grey afternoon and nearly collided with Paloma, who was already running too, appraiser's coat flying, terror written plain on his honest face.

"They know," Tam gasped. "Paloma, they know, and my mother — my mother's *alive*, she's in there, she's been awake the whole time—"

Paloma caught him by the shoulders, steadied him, and Tam watched the exact moment the news landed — watched the scribe's face go through horror and grief and arrive somewhere hard and bright.

"Then we don't run," Paloma said quietly. "We go get the last page. Tonight. Before Vey decides a living heir is worth more than a preserved one."

Above them, in the windows of the great house, lights were coming on one by one, like eyes opening.
