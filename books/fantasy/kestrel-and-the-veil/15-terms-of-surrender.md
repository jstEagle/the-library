## 15. Terms of Surrender

The trap closed at the moment Paloma thought he'd won.

He had been in Vey's collection room for less than an hour. Long enough to catalogue it, because cataloguing was what his hands did when his mind was screaming. Fourteen glass cases along the eastern wall, each one climate-sealed and softly lit, each one holding a person.

Not statues. Not effigies. People — preserved in some amber of the Guild's making, mid-gesture, mid-breath, their eyes open. A woman with her hands cupped as if catching rain that had stopped falling two centuries ago. A man in a soldier's coat, frozen turning toward a door. An old woman seated in a chair that had been carried here from somewhere else, her lap empty, her face tilted upward as though listening for a voice.

And on the smallest case, set apart on its own pedestal like something precious: a young mother, dark-haired, her mouth shaped around a word.

Tam's word. The boy had said it a hundred times in the archive's shadows, the only word of Kestrelish Paloma had learned to recognize by shape alone. *Mama.*

Paloma's pen had stopped moving. That was his first mistake. In the Ledger Guild you never stopped moving; stillness was how they knew guilt.

The second mistake was reaching for the case.

His fingers had barely brushed the glass when the lamps came up all at once, every wick turned to full, and Lord Ambrose Vey stepped out from behind a screen of pierced brass as if he'd been waiting there all evening, which he almost certainly had.

"There he is," Vey said warmly. "The appraiser."

* * *

They did not bind him. That was the worst part, later — that they didn't need to. Two house guards took up positions flanking the door, and Vey poured wine with the unhurried grace of a man who owned everything in the room including the hour, and gestured Paloma into a chair across from the pedestal where Tam's mother stood sleeping with her eyes open.

"You're wondering how I knew," Vey said.

"I'm wondering why the Guild sends an appraiser who doesn't know provenance when he sees it." Paloma kept his voice level. His heart was not cooperating. "Your cases are mislabeled. These aren't Vessic funerary figures from the Third Dynasty. They're citizens of—"

"Kestrel." Vey said the name the way other men said the names of racehorses. "Say it again. Slowly. Do you know how rare it is to hear someone say it without flinching?"

"Corvane flinches?"

"Corvane *counts*," Vey said. "It's a different kind of flinching. She counts every syllable of that name for its market value, and it terrifies her, because value is the only language she speaks and that name keeps refusing to be priced correctly." He handed Paloma a glass. "Drink. You've had a long night of trespassing."

Paloma didn't drink. He set the glass on the table between them, precisely centered, the way he'd center a ledger line, and Vey smiled like a man watching a horse he'd bet on behave exactly as expected.

"The Guildmaster sent you," Paloma said. "She knew I'd come."

"She hoped you would. There's a difference, and the difference is you. Corvane is thorough but she lacks imagination. I supplied the invitation — the gala, the hiring notice, the little fiction of needing an appraiser for the autumn collection. All of it built so that one specific man would walk through my door believing he'd picked the lock himself." Vey settled back. "You have a talent, Ashgrove. You always think you're escaping. It's very useful in collateral."

The word landed like cold water.

"Collateral," Paloma repeated.

"Mm." Vey examined his own wine against the lamplight. "You know, I've purchased eleven pieces of Kestrel over the years. The western hills. A river — the Erren, it was called; lovely river, gone now, or rather *here*, folded into a casket in my cellar. The capital itself, which was the crown of the collection until Corvane found something better." He looked up. "Do you know what the best piece of a country is? Everyone assumes it's the land. Land is bulky. Land requires lawyers, wardens, endless maintenance to keep it from bleeding back into the world. No. The best piece of a country is its *name*. One page of the true-name manuscript will hold more power than forty acres of phantom geography, and it fits in a breast pocket."

Somewhere in the walls of the house, a clock ticked with terrible patience.

"Fifteen years ago," Vey continued, "the Ledger Guild fell upon hard times. Bad speculation in memory-futures, a scandal involving a Guildmaster's predecessor, debts called in by creditors who are not the sort you refuse. Corvane needed liquidity. I needed a page of Kestrel's name — specifically the page bearing the syllables of the capital's founding line, which is the spine of the whole manuscript. She had the page. I had the money. But there was a complication."

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. He was aware, distantly, of Tam's mother watching him from inside her glass with her unblinking preserved eyes, aware of the rain-catcher woman, the soldier, all of them attending this conversation in the only way left to them.

"The true-name pages don't transfer cleanly," Vey said. "Magic is a bureaucracy too, Ashgrove. Every transaction needs a signature, a witness, and — critically — *collateral*. Something pledged that ensures performance. A page of a country's name can't simply be sold. It has to be anchored to something that can be reclaimed if the buyer defaults." He spread his hands, gracious, almost apologetic. "So we asked the ledger what it wanted. And the ledger, being a ledger, answered honestly."

*No,* Paloma thought. *No.*

"Your indenture," Vey said. "Your debt to the Guild. It isn't a debt, Ashgrove. It never was. Your father didn't borrow money your family couldn't repay — your father didn't exist in that story at all, I'm afraid; I've checked the older records. Fifteen years ago the Guild needed a human anchor for the sale of a page, and the ledger selected you. You were seven years old. You were walking home from a scribe-school placement exam with the highest marks in your cohort. The ledger likes high marks. Anchors should be precise."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a ship tilts when it leaves harbor.

"My mother," Paloma heard himself say.

"Was told a story about debts, and paid it in the only currency she had: her son. She signed papers she couldn't read. Most people can't read them — they're written in contract-ink, which rearranges itself after signing. She believed she was securing your apprenticeship." Vey's voice softened into something that might have been pity in a better man. "She died believing you were one payment away from coming home. That part wasn't necessary for the magic. I paid for it separately, out of what I'd call kindness and Corvane would call client management."

Paloma stood up. He didn't remember deciding to stand. The guards shifted; Vey raised two fingers and they stilled.

"Fifteen years," Paloma said. "I counted days. I kept a private tally in the margin of my own life — four thousand eight hundred and some days, minus Sundays, recalculated whenever I earned a remission credit. I planned my whole existence around a number that was *invented*. There was no debt. There was no end date. I was—" His voice cracked and he hated it, hated it in front of this man, in front of these silent witnesses. "I was furniture."

"You were *collateral*," Vey corrected gently. "Which is better than furniture. Furniture depreciates. Collateral appreciates, if it's chosen well. And you were chosen very well indeed." He rose, walked to the small case on the pedestal, and rested his fingertips on the glass above the dark-haired woman's frozen face. "Because here is what Corvane never fully understood, and what you are only now beginning to. When the ledger chose its anchor, it chose someone *syllable-deep* in Kestrel already. You've spent fifteen years keeping that country's borders in ink. Every entry you ever made — the bottled thunder, the widow's shadow, the thousand impossible things — those weren't inventory, boy. Those were fragments that drifted loose when Kestrel was erased, and the ledger has been quietly reabsorbing them through you. Through your hand. Your handwriting is one of the last living things that remembers how Kestrelan letters join."

Paloma thought of the night door. The mud-caked boy. The oldest ledger stirring on its shelf like something waking, answering a child's dead tongue while every trained scribe in the building stood helpless.

"It wasn't the boy it answered," he said slowly. "It answered through me."

"Now you're learning." Vey turned. "The page I bought anchors to you. The pages Corvane has been selling — every provenance shard, every scrap of geography that went under the hammer last week — they all trace their chain of custody back to the same original transaction. To you. You are not a man who stumbled onto Kestrel's secret, Ashgrove. You are the *load-bearing wall* of its dismemberment. Pull you out, and fifteen years of sales begin to unravel. Which is why—" and here his pleasantness finally dropped away, replaced by something older and hungrier, the look of a collector regarding the one piece missing from a complete set, "—you are going to finish the manuscript for me."

"I'm going to do no such thing."

"You misunderstand your position." Vey crossed to his desk, unlocked a drawer, and laid a single sheet of paper on the blotter. Even across the room, Paloma felt it — a hum in his teeth, in the scar tissue of old ink stains, in whatever strange organ he'd grown over fifteen years of writing a dead country's borders. The paper was old. The script on it was Kestrelish, and Paloma could read perhaps a third of it now, thanks to a boy and a cartographer and pages that cost memories to read.

"This is the founding line," Vey said. "The spine. The page whose sale created you. And this—" he drew out a second document, fresh vellum, dense with contract-ink that crawled faintly at its edges, "—is your amended indenture. Your terms of surrender, if you like the dramatic phrasing. Sign it, and the following occurs. One: your debt is dissolved — genuinely dissolved, the ledger itself will strike the line. Two: the boy is transferred to my ownership, alive and unfrozen, as befits a citizen of such quality. Three: you will serve as principal appraiser of the Kestrel recovery, reconstructing the full true-name under my direction, at generous salary, for the rest of your natural life."

"And if I don't sign?"

Vey's smile returned, thinner now.

"If you don't sign, I exercise default. The clause no one reads because no one thinks it applies to them. Default on an anchor means *forfeiture*, Ashgrove. The Guild forfeits you to me entire — history, handwriting, memories, all of it becomes my property, and I extract the name from you the way I extracted the river from the hills. It's slower. It hurts more. And the boy goes into a case beside his mother, which, I'll note, is where he'll end up eventually regardless, because a preserved citizen of Kestrel is worth more than a running one." He tapped the vellum. "Sign, and he stays flesh. Refuse, and he joins the garden. Either way, I get my country back. The only variable is how much of you survives the transaction."

Paloma looked at the pen Vey held out. Black lacquer, silver nib. Probably worth more than his mother's ring had been before he'd traded it to Sefa Ilun — and Sefa was in a Guild cell now because of him, burned maps and broken hands and a daughter's name she'd paid a memory to restore.

He looked at Tam's mother. At the word her mouth was still shaping after two hundred years.

*Think,* he told himself. *You're a scribe. Read the document. There is always a margin, there is always a footnote, there is always a line item nobody notices because nobody reads like you do.*

So Paloma took the pen. Vey's eyebrows rose, pleased. The guards relaxed a fraction.

And Paloma pulled the amended indenture toward him and began, deliberately, ostentatiously, to read — line by line, lips moving, the picture of a beaten clerk checking his chains. Let Vey enjoy it. Let Vey think the reading was submission.

Because the contract-ink rearranged itself after signing. Vey had said so himself, proud of it.

But it rearranged *after* signing. Before signing — for the few minutes a document sat unexecuted on a desk, live and legible — it held still.

And Paloma Ashgrove had spent fifteen years learning to find the truth inside a ledger's fine print, and he had just seen, tucked into clause nine among the forfeiture terms, three syllables of Kestrelish that even Vey's lawyers hadn't recognized as words.

Three syllables. From the founding line.

The final page wasn't in Vey's drawer at all.

It had been in front of Paloma since he walked in — pinned to the wall of the law itself, hidden in the one place no thief would ever think to steal from.

He reached the bottom of the page. He lifted the pen. And above the signature line, in a small, steady, appraiser's hand, he wrote a single question mark — and looked up at Ambrose Vey with the first honest expression he'd worn all night.

"Lord Vey," he said, "before I sign, I wonder — do *you* actually know what you bought?"
