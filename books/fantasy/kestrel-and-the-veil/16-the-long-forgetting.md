## 16. The Long Forgetting

They took my boots first.

I don't know why that detail matters, but it does. Everything else about the cell was what I expected — stone, dark, a door with no handle on the inside — but the boots were a small cruelty I hadn't planned for. Bare feet on cold floor. A scribe's feet, soft from forty years of sitting. They wanted me uncomfortable in a way that had nothing to do with pain.

The cell had one furnishing: a lectern. Carved from pale wood, angled like the ones in the Guild's reading hall, where apprentices learned to copy contracts without smudging. Someone had thought of everything. Someone had known exactly what I would do.

On the lectern lay the pages.

Not all of them. Eleven of the nineteen. The rest were still scattered across Vess — in Corvane's vaults, in buyers' cabinets, and one, I was certain, somewhere in Lord Ambrose Vey's estate, sewn into whatever gilded horror he called his collection. But eleven pages is enough to begin. Eleven pages is enough to lose yourself in.

I sat on the floor for a long time before I touched them. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is about to become the only thing I have left. I sat with my back against the wall and my bare feet drawn up and I told myself every reasonable argument against what I intended to do.

Reasonable argument one: I could wait. Tam would come. The boy had dragged me through half the undercity of Vess; he'd stolen a shard of a dead country off a table full of armed men; he had not once, in all the weeks I'd known him, abandoned anything he loved. He would come.

Reasonable argument two: memorizing the name without the final page was like learning a song missing its last note. You could hold it in your mouth forever and never resolve it. Worse — you might forget how it ended, and then all this suffering buys nothing.

Reasonable argument three: Sefa Ilun had warned me. *Each page takes what it takes,* she said, her hands flat on her maps like a woman steadying herself against wind. *It doesn't ask which memories you'd rather keep. It takes the ones closest to the surface. It takes what you love.*

All true. All good reasons.

And none of them mattered, because through the wall, faint as breath, I could hear the boy crying. Not loudly. Tam didn't cry loudly. He cried the way Kestrel must have died — quietly, completely, so that no one would notice until it was done.

So I got up. I put the first page on the lectern. And I began to read.

* * *

You should understand what reading these pages is like, because no one who hasn't done it can explain it, and by the time anyone else does it, they won't remember enough to warn you.

The words are not words. That's the first thing. Oh, they look like words — an alphabet older than any script the Ledger Guild teaches, curves and strokes that seem to rearrange themselves when you blink — but they aren't language the way Vessic is language. They're more like instructions. Or wounds. Each character is a small decision the country once made about itself: *this river bends here; this festival falls on the longest night; this mother sings this song to this child.* Reading them isn't comprehension. It's ingestion.

And the price comes due immediately.

The first page cost me the smell of bread.

I don't mean bread in general. I mean the specific smell of the bakery on Cordwainer Street where I grew up, the one run by the Halloway sisters, whose ovens ran hot at four in the morning and whose windows fogged gold in winter. I knew it was gone the way you know a tooth has come loose — not by absence but by the strange new smoothness where it used to be. I reached for the memory and found the street, the sisters' faces, the bell over their door, everything except that one warm smell. A hole shaped like yeast and heat, cut clean.

I sat down again after that page. I'll admit it. My hands were shaking and I pressed them flat against the cold floor and I said, out loud, to nobody: *Eleven pages. Eighteen to go. You can stop whenever you want.*

That was a lie, and I knew it even as I said it, because stopping meant the memories I'd already paid were wasted, and I have never in my life tolerated waste. Forty years in the Ledger Guild will do that to you. Every entry balanced. Every column summed. Waste was the one sin Corvane's guild actually punished.

So I read the second page.

* * *

By the fourth page I understood the shape of the theft.

It doesn't take memories at random. It takes them in order of intimacy — or perhaps in order of how often you've held them, the way a river wears down the stones it touches most. The common things go first. Then the dear things. And last, I suspected, the things so deep you don't even know you carry them.

Page four took my childhood.

Not all at once. It went in pieces, politely, like a guest gathering a scattered coat and gloves. The name of my childhood street went first — I could see the houses but couldn't have told you where they stood. Then the game we played with chalk lines and a knucklebone, rules dissolving even as I grasped for them. Then the faces of the other children, blurring into pleasant anonymous shapes, and finally the knowledge that there had been other children at all.

What remained was a fact, dry as a ledger line: *Paloma Ashgrove was born in Vess, in the Cordwainer quarter, to a mother whose name was—*

Whose name was—

I gripped the lectern until the wood creaked. Her name was there. Her name was still there. *Maren. Maren Ashgrove.* I said it aloud three times, the way you'd check a figure twice before sealing a ledger, and the relief was so violent it frightened me.

Because here is what I learned on page four, kneeling on a stone floor calling my mother's name like a prayer: the magic doesn't take everything. It leaves you the facts. It strips the living tissue off the bone and hands you back the skeleton and expects you to be grateful. I would always know I'd had a childhood. I would simply never feel it again. Somewhere in me there was now a room with all the furniture removed, swept clean, and a small card on the door reading *occupied once.*

I think that's worse than forgetting outright. Forgetting at least is peaceful. What the pages leave you is grief without an object — mourning for something you can prove existed but cannot find.

* * *

Between pages, I kept track. Of course I did. I'm a scribe; inventory is the only prayer I know.

I scratched a tally into the wall with the stylus they'd left me — another courtesy, another confirmation that they knew. Seven marks for seven pages read. Beside each mark, a word, so I could audit my own losses:

One. Bread.
Two. Rain on the guild roof — I noticed its absence during a storm I could hear but somehow no longer felt.
Three. The taste of tea. All tea. Every cup I would ever drink again would be hot brown water.
Four. Childhood.
Five. The color of my own front door.
Six. My father. Not his death — his existence. There was a woman named Maren who had raised a son alone, and the arithmetic of that had a gap in it shaped like a man I would never miss because I no longer knew he'd been missed.

Seven.

Page seven was the mother's voice.

I want to describe this properly and I find I cannot, because describing it requires the very thing it removed. Here is what I can say: Maren Ashgrove sang. I know this the way I know the entries in a ledger I copied decades ago — as information, verified, filed. She sang while she worked. There was a song about a lark, or a river, or a girl crossing water; the subject is gone but the fact of singing remains, and with it the fact that I used to fall asleep to it, and the fact that I loved it.

Loved. Present-tense love, past-tense object. That's what page seven left me. A verb with nothing to hold.

I stopped after seven. I had to. I sat in the dark of that cell and I did something I hadn't done since I was small enough to be carried: I wept, silently, thoroughly, with the discipline of a man balancing accounts, and when it was done I wiped my face and looked at the remaining four pages on the lectern and hated them with a purity I have never managed to feel toward any person.

Corvane came that night. I heard her key in the lock, smelled the clove oil she used on her hands, and I didn't stand. Let her find me on the floor among my tallies like an animal in its den.

"You're further along than I expected," she said. She sounded almost fond. "Sefa Ilun told us you wouldn't dare. She said you were the most careful man she ever met."

"Sefa Ilun," I said, "is in one of your cells too."

"For now." Clove oil and the rustle of her coat as she crouched. In the dark I could just make out her face, that patient smile she wore like a guild seal. "Do you know why I'm letting you do this, Paloma? Why the pages are on a lectern instead of locked away?"

"Because you need the name assembled."

"I need the name *readable.* There's a difference. A name in pieces is merchandise. A name in one mind—" she tapped my forehead with one finger, lightly, almost kindly, "—is a vault. And a vault can be opened." She rose. "Read, scribe. Read all of it. When you're finished, you'll open the door out of Kestrel for Lord Vey, and your debt will dissolve, and everyone will get exactly what they paid for."

She paused at the door.

"One piece of professional advice," she said. "From one keeper of ledgers to another. Whatever you're holding onto — whatever you're saving for last — the pages will take it anyway. They always take the anchor last. It's how they make sure you finish."

The door closed. The key turned.

I sat for a long time in the dark, doing the arithmetic she'd handed me. If the pages take the anchor last, then the thing I was clutching — *Tam, Tam, the boy through the wall, the reason for all of this* — was scheduled. Already invoiced. The magic had marked him in its ledger as *final payment due upon completion.*

Unless I finished differently than she planned.

Unless the last page never reached me, and the name stayed broken in my head, and the door stayed shut — unless I became not a vault to be opened but a vault welded closed, a man walking out of the world carrying eleven-eighteenths of a country and no key.

But that was a plan for a man with all nineteen pages. And four of mine were still on the lectern, waiting, patient as creditors.

* * *

I read page eight at dawn, if the gray seeping under the door could be called dawn.

It took the harbor. Vess's harbor — the masts, the gulls, the fish-stink and tar, the bells of the tide-wardens. I had walked it ten thousand times. Now there was a city with a southern edge and a blankness beyond it, and I had to trust the blankness had once been beautiful.

Nine took my apprenticeship: Master Ellery's cough, the weight of my first seal-ring, the pride. Pride goes early, I discovered. Pride is shallow water.

Ten took snow. Just — snow. The whole sensation, gone, leaving the word *snow* like a coin from a country that no longer minted it.

And eleven—

Eleven took Maren's face.

Her name survived. Her voice was already gone, spent on page seven. But her face — I had been hoarding it, I realize now, guarding it behind the smaller losses, rationing my visits to it the way a poor man rations candles. And the pages knew. Corvane had told me they'd know. They saved it, let me believe I was winning, and then they took it in a single stroke, and what remains is a woman I loved, described entirely in facts: brown hair, a scar on the left eyebrow, hands ink-stained from mending ledgers to make ends meet. A portrait written by a stranger. Filed under *mother, deceased, beloved, unverified.*

There are eighteen marks on my wall now. Wait — no. Eleven. Eleven marks, eleven pages, eight still outstanding, scattered through Vess in the cabinets of people who bought geography by the pound.

I am running out of self. I can feel the edges of me going thin, like paper read too many times. Soon there will be very little Paloma Ashgrove left — a competence, a habit of tallying, a stubbornness with no origin story. A vessel. Which is, I understand at last, exactly what both Corvane and I require. She wants a vault. I need a country.

Through the wall, faintly, the boy has stopped crying. Instead I hear humming — tuneless, half-remembered, a child trying to reconstruct a song from a country that died before he learned all its verses.

I know the feeling intimately now.

Eight pages to go. Somewhere out there, a disgraced cartographer is sitting in a cell of her own, and I find myself praying — to whom, I no longer know; perhaps to the country itself — that she remembers what she promised me. That she gets free. That she brings me the rest.

Because I have counted, and recounted, and the ledger is clear:

Everything remembered costs everything owned.

And I am nearly out of things to sell.
