## 17. What Sefa Paid

They had taken her hands first.

Not cut them off — Corvane was more elegant than that. She had simply asked Sefa to draw the map of Vess's undercity from memory, and when Sefa refused, the Guildmaster had opened a ledger on the table between them and begun reading aloud. Names. Dates. A daughter called Wren Ilun, born in the spring of a year Sefa could no longer quite see.

"Tell me what you remember about her," Corvane said, turning pages. "Take your time."

That was the cruelty of it. The Guild's ledgers did not record facts; they recorded debts, and memory was currency like any other. Every hour Sefa sat in that chair, Corvane read a little more of her life back to her in the flat voice of an auditor, and every reading took something. Not erased — spent. Transferred into the Guild's accounts, where it would sit until some buyer wanted a cartographer's love for her child, or a woman's grief for a city, or the particular ache of having drawn Kestrel's coastline from a rowboat while the country still existed to be measured.

Sefa had held out for three days. On the fourth she gave up the location of Sefa Ilun's shop, and on the fifth she gave up the hiding place beneath its floorboards where two fugitives and a stolen shard of a dead nation had slept. She told herself she was buying time. She told herself Paloma and the boy would be gone by now, that they'd seen the smoke and run.

She did not tell herself anything at all after the sixth day, because telling herself things required remembering what she'd promised, and promises were among the first entries Corvane had liquidated.

* * *

On the ninth night, the door of her cell opened and did not close behind whoever left it.

It was not mercy. It was arithmetic. Corvane had emptied her of everything useful — the shop, the maps, the routes through the undercity, the name of the ferryman who owed her a favor — and what remained was a husk in a chair, too hollow to ransom and too expensive to feed. Somewhere in the Guild's ledgers there was probably an entry closing out the account marked *SEFA ILUN*, balanced to zero, filed away.

But Corvane had made one mistake, and it was the same mistake powerful people always made: she had assumed that a person stripped of her memories was a person stripped of her self.

Sefa stood up. Her legs remembered how, though she didn't remember learning. Her body was a map of itself, older than any ledger, and it knew the way down the corridor, past the sleeping clerk, out through the coal chute into the alley behind the Hall of Accounts. She moved through Vess like water finding a crack, and if you had asked her *why* she was running, or *where*, she could not have answered. But her feet turned east, toward the river, toward the tannery district, and something in her chest — some entry too deep to read, written in ink older than the Guild — pulled her along like a current.

She stole a coat off a drying line. She stole bread off a windowsill. She walked all night on roads she had mapped once, in another life, and near dawn she came to a drainage culvert where the brickwork bore a chalk mark: a circle crossed by a single line. She did not know the mark. She did not know why her hand rose to touch it.

Her hand knew. That was enough.

* * *

She found them in the flooded crypt beneath the old customs house, because of course they were there — the scribe and the boy, huddled over candle stubs and scattered pages, and the sight of them hit her like a wave hitting a seawall, breaking over something solid she hadn't known she had.

Paloma came up off his crate so fast he knocked the candles sideways. "Sefa — gods — they said—"

"They said I was spent." Her voice came out rusted. "They weren't wrong." She held up her hands and turned them over, palms, knuckles, the ink stains in the creases that no longer meant anything to her. "I don't remember my shop. I don't remember drawing Kestrel. I don't remember—" and here the current in her chest lurched, painful as a hooked fish, "—there was someone. Wasn't there? Someone small. Someone who—"

"Wren," Tam said quietly.

The boy sat cross-legged on the stone floor with the recovered pages spread around him in a careful spiral, and he looked at her with eyes far too old for his face. He spoke the dead tongue first, softly, and then, in his halting trade-speech: "Your daughter. Wren. You told me once she laughed like rain on a tin roof. You said I should hear it someday."

Sefa stared at him. The words went into her like keys into locks she'd forgotten she owned. Rain on tin. A laugh. A small girl standing in a doorway in a city that no longer existed, laughing at the weather.

"I have no daughter," Sefa said. It was true. It was also the most complete lie she had ever spoken, and both truths lived in it at once, and she understood suddenly, fully, what the Guild had done to her — not theft but amputation, cutting the memory away whole and leaving the wound shaped exactly like the missing thing.

"You do," Tam said. "You just can't carry her right now. There's a difference."

Paloma crossed the room and took her by the shoulders, and his hands were shaking. "Why did you come? If they let you go, it's because you're bait. They'll be following you. You know that."

"I know it." She reached inside the stolen coat and drew out the thing she had carried for nine days without knowing she carried it, sewn into the lining by hands whose reasons she no longer possessed: a folded scrap of paper, soft as cloth from handling. "And I know this. My body wouldn't let me leave it behind. Whatever I was before they emptied me, apparently she planned for this."

Paloma unfolded it with the reverence of a man handling scripture. His breath stopped.

"What is it?" Tam crawled closer.

"The final page," Paloma whispered. "Or — the key to it. Coordinates. A description." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and burning at once. "This is Lord Vey's estate. The west wing. The vault beneath the aviary. Sefa — how—"

"I don't remember." She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "Isn't that funny? The one useful thing left in me, and I can't even tell you where I got it. Some version of me bled for this, scribe. Try to be worth it."

He gripped her arms harder. "We can get you out. Upriver, past the toll bridges — Tam knows the ways the shard opens—"

"No." She said it gently, and watched the word land on him like a dropped stone. "Listen to me, Paloma Ashgrove. I've been read like a ledger for nine days. I know exactly what I'm worth now: nothing. An empty account. If I come with you, I'm weight. If they catch me again, they'll start reading me backward, pulling threads to find which way you ran." She smiled, and it cost her everything, and she paid it anyway. "But an empty woman walking alone in the fog? No one follows a ghost. Let me be your ghost. Go to Vey. Finish the name."

"And you?" Tam asked. His voice was very small.

She crouched down to the boy's level. Around them the crypt dripped, and somewhere above, the river muttered against the pilings, and she thought: *somewhere there was a tin roof, and rain, and a girl who laughed.*

"I'm going to buy something back," she said.

* * *

The Guild kept a depository in the Saltcellar district — a bonded warehouse where provenance shards and confiscated memories waited in lead-lined drawers for their buyers. Sefa knew this the way she knew the chalk mark on the culvert wall: not in her mind, but in her marrow. Her feet took her there across half of Vess, through fog thick as wool, past the toll bridges where lamplighters were snuffing the dawn flames.

She did not have a key. She did not need one. She walked up to the night clerk — a boy younger than Paloma, bored, cold, half-asleep over his register — and she said, in the crisp diction of a Guild professional, "Audit. Corvane's authority. Account 114, transfer pending, open the drawer."

Authority was a language, and she still spoke it fluently even if she couldn't remember learning. The clerk opened the drawer.

Inside, in a velvet-lined slot, sat a small glass vial, and in the vial, swirling like ink in water, was a memory. The label read: *ILUN, S. — LOT 7 — MATERNAL, ASSORTED.*

She held it up to the lamp and watched the light move through it, and inside the swirl she saw — briefly, terribly — a flash of yellow: a dress. Small shoes. A doorway.

"Sign here," the clerk said, pushing the register toward her.

She signed *Sefa Ilun* in a hand that shook only a little, and it was the first time since the cell that the name had felt like hers, and then she thanked him politely, walked out into the fog, and did not stop walking until the Saltcellar was far behind her and the river lay black and wide before her, and she was certain — as certain as a gutted woman could be — that no one had followed.

Then she unstopped the vial and drank.

* * *

Memory does not return gently. It comes home the way floodwater comes home, finding every low place, filling every room.

Wren.

Wren Ilun, born on a morning when the plum trees along Kestrel's southern terraces bloomed out of season, as if the country itself had hurried to greet her. Wren at three, chasing gulls off the sea-wall. Wren at seven, sitting on Sefa's drafting table and "helping," which meant dragging a blunt pencil across the margins of the great coastal survey and declaring each scribble a new island. Wren at nine, demanding to know why the capital's streets bent in the middle, and Sefa explaining that the city had grown crooked on purpose, the way a tree grows around whatever it loves, and Wren considering this with enormous seriousness and saying, "Then who does the city love?"

Wren at eleven, on the last night. Standing in the square with ten thousand others while the elders unbound the country's name from the world. Wren's hand in hers. Wren saying, not afraid, never afraid, "Will it hurt, Mama?" and Sefa lying, "No, my heart," and the light coming down like a curtain, and the terrible peace of it, the chosen ending — a people deciding that if they could not be free they would at least be *unfindable*, scattering themselves into objects and orphans and ornamental gardens, folding their whole nation into a scream and a silence—

Sefa came back to herself kneeling on the riverbank, the empty vial in her fist, weeping without sound.

They had taken everything else. Her craft, her history, her maps, her years. But they had sold this lot cheap — *maternal, assorted* — because to the Guild a mother's love was inventory, and inventory was priced by weight, and love weighs nothing until it's gone.

She got up. She wiped her face. And she began to walk toward Vey's estate, because Wren was there — Wren was in that garden, that was what the coordinates meant, that was why some braver version of herself had sewn the page's location into her own coat: not only to save the name, but because the name, spoken whole, would wake the garden, would give her daughter back her body, her motion, her morning.

Sefa Ilun had lost everything twice. She intended to make the second loss count.

* * *

The fog was lifting when she reached the estate wall, and the sun was coming up gold behind the aviary, and that was when she heard the horses.

Three riders on the river road, moving fast, and even at this distance she knew the gray in the lead — Corvane rode grays, always, because she liked her animals the way she liked her ledgers: uniform, disciplined, interchangeable. They had followed her after all. Or they had simply come to Vey's on their own errand, converging on the final page the way all the hungry things in this story were converging, and it no longer mattered which.

What mattered was time.

Sefa put her hands on the wall and climbed. Her body protested — nine days in a chair, nine days of being read apart — and she overruled it, joint by joint, the way she had once overruled rivers and ravines when a map demanded a crossing. She went over the top and dropped into the garden beyond, into rows of figures standing among the hedges like statuary, and there —

There.

Third row, beside a sundial. A girl of eleven, frozen mid-turn, mouth open on a word, hands raised as if to catch something falling. Eleven forever. Preserved by a buyer's greed the way flowers are pressed in books: perfect, flat, alive.

"Wren," Sefa said, and her voice worked, and her legs worked, and she ran.

Behind her, hooves on gravel. Shouting. The crack of the estate gates. None of it mattered. She reached her daughter and pressed her palms to the girl's frozen cheeks and felt, beneath the stillness, the faint impossible warmth of a person waiting, and she said, "I drank it back, my heart. I have all of it now. Every year. The gulls. The islands you invented. Who the city loves." She was crying again and she let it come. "Hold on. The scribe is coming. He's going to say your country's name, and when he does, you're going to finish that turn, and I will be right here — do you hear me? Right here —"

A hand closed on her shoulder and spun her around, and Corvane stood there with the sunrise behind her, flanked by enforcers, breathing hard from the ride, and her face wore the expression Sefa had learned to dread more than any other: the look of a woman discovering an unbalanced column.

"You were supposed to be nothing," Corvane said. "I closed your account."

"Then reopen it." Sefa stepped in front of her daughter, spreading her arms, making of herself a wall between the Guild and the girl. "Because I remember everything now, Corvane. All of it. Kestrel's coasts, its crooked streets, its last night — and I remember *you*. I remember when you were a junior auditor with ink on your cuffs, before you learned to price people. I remember it all, and I am going to stand here and hold every piece of it in my head until the door opens, and there is nothing you can take from me that I won't have already given."

Corvane studied her for a long moment. Somewhere across the grounds, glass broke — Paloma, in the vault, or running from it — and the Guildmaster's eyes flicked toward the sound, calculating.

"You understand," Corvane said quietly, "that I can't allow the name to be completed."

"Then you'd better be faster than a mother," Sefa said, "because I have been slow my whole life, and I am done with it."

She threw herself at the nearest enforcer, all ninety pounds of ruin and fury, and bought with her body the seconds that could not be bought with anything else — and as the world slammed into her, as the gravel came up to meet her, she kept her eyes fixed on her daughter's frozen face and her mind fixed on the whole bright country she carried, street by street, name by name, refusing to drop a single page of it.

*Remembering has a price,* she had told Paloma once, in a shop she could now recall plank by plank. *Pay it gladly.*

She paid.
