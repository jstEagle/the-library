## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The trouble with keeping a ledger of impossible things, Yara had learned over ten years, was that the impossible had terrible manners about arriving on schedule.

It was a quarter past midnight, the kind of night at Hollowgate where nothing happened so thoroughly that Yara had begun to suspect the nothing of doing it on purpose. Rain ticked against the gatehouse roof in a rhythm that was almost, but not quite, musical. The candle had burned down to a stub shaped vaguely like a disappointed turnip. Yara sat at the great oak desk with a pot of cold nettle tea and the Ledger open to a fresh page, and for three hours the only entry had been:

*Hour of the Wolf. Nothing traded. Nothing offered. Nothing seen. — Y.W.*

They had written it in their smallest, neatest hand, the hand the guild had trained into them, the hand that took up as little room in the world as possible. Yara Whitlock was very good at taking up as little room as possible. It was, in fact, the only thing anyone had ever praised them for.

*You're a shadow, Whitlock,* Guildmaster Corvane had said at their binding ceremony, and had meant it as the highest compliment the guild could pay. *A good keeper is a door that no one remembers opening.*

Yara had spent a decade being a door no one remembered opening. It was lonelier than it sounded. Lonelier even than it sounded when Yara said it out loud to the kettle, which they did sometimes, on nights like this, because the kettle never repeated anything to anyone.

The knock came at the gate's wicket door just as Yara was considering whether it would be a scandal or merely a tragedy to fall asleep face-first in the Ledger.

Three knocks. Pause. Two knocks. The old pattern. A client.

Yara straightened their collar, smoothed their expression into the particular blankness the guild required—*polite as fog,* Corvane called it—and went to the door.

The man on the step was soaked through, thin as a bundle of kindling, and holding a lantern that had long since surrendered to the rain. He was nobody. That was the first thing Yara noticed, the way they'd been trained to notice: no guild marks, no realm-sigils, no sign of anything that would require the guild to care about him afterward. He was the sort of man the world misplaced on a regular basis and never thought to look for.

"You're open," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The gate is always open," Yara said. "That's rather the trouble with it. Come in before the rain does."

He came in. He dripped on the flagstones with the apologetic thoroughness of a man who had spent his whole life dripping on things. Yara seated him by the fire, poured him the last of the tea—it had gone bitter, and he drank it like it was a kindness, which in a way it was—and set out the trade-scales, the ink, and the small iron box in which the guild kept its questions.

"You know how this works?" Yara asked.

"I know how it works." He turned the empty cup in his hands. "My wife died in the spring. There's a memory I keep of her that I can't— I can't carry it anymore. I want to sell it. All of it. The whole day."

Yara nodded. Grief-trades were common. Half the ledger's early pages were grief, pressed flat like flowers in a book. "The guild will weigh it, set a price, and you'll be paid in what you asked for when you made your appointment." Yara glanced at the intake slip pinned to his coat. "You asked for forgetting. That's the standard rate for a whole-day memory. You'll walk out of here lighter, and you won't remember walking in. Do you understand that part? Most people say they do, and then they weep anyway. Both are allowed."

"I understand." But he didn't take off the memory. He sat there, turning the cup, and the rain filled the silence, and finally he said, "Keeper. Before I sell it. Can I tell you about it? Just—tell. Not trade. Tell."

Yara should have said no. The guild's rules were clear: keepers did not converse. Keepers recorded. A keeper who listened too closely began to want things, and a keeper who wanted things began to be seen, and a keeper who was seen was no use to anyone.

"Tell me," Yara said, because it was midnight, and the kettle was the only other soul available, and because some small stubborn thing in Yara's chest had never once obeyed the guild's rules, it had merely gotten very good at hiding.

* * *

The memory, as he told it, was of an ordinary day. That was what he kept coming back to. *An ordinary day.* His wife had spent it mending a net in the garden, and the light had come through the holes in the net and made lace on her hands, and she had laughed at something—a neighbor's goat, of all things, a goat with opinions—and nothing had happened. Nothing at all. And he had looked at her hands in the lacy light and thought, without knowing why, *remember this. Remember exactly this.*

And now she was gone, and he remembered exactly this, every day, perfectly, and it was killing him by inches.

"I thought selling it would be a mercy," he said. "But now I'm here and I can't make myself let go of it. Isn't that a stupid thing?"

"It's the least stupid thing I've heard all year," Yara said. "But the night's young."

He laughed—a wet, cracked laugh—and then he reached into his coat and drew the memory out to set it on the scales.

Yara had seen ten thousand memories drawn for trade. They came up as light, mostly. A soft gold glow, the color of candlelight through honey, warm and small and portable as an egg. Grief-memories ran gray-silver. Joy ran gold. Fear ran a color Yara had never found a name for and had privately decided not to.

The man's memory came up black.

Not dark. Not shadowed. *Black*—a lightlessness with weight to it, sitting on the scale like a stone dropped in a well. The candle flames in the room bent toward it. Yara felt the hairs on their arms lift, and the gate—Hollowgate itself, the old stone arch at the heart of the gatehouse, which had been silent as furniture for ten years—gave a single low hum, like a struck bell heard through a wall.

Yara had never heard the gate hum. Keepers were told the gate *couldn't* hum. It was a door. Doors did not have opinions.

"That's not right," the man said, staring at it. "That's not— keeper, that's my wife mending a net. Why does it look like that?"

"I don't know," Yara said honestly. "Put it away. Slowly."

He did. The black light sank back into his cupped hands and the candles straightened and the gate went quiet, and Yara sat very still and did the arithmetic every keeper dreads: a memory that shouldn't exist, in a client who shouldn't matter, on a night when nothing was supposed to happen.

The guild would want it recorded. That was the whole of a keeper's life: record, seal, send up the mountain to the guildhouse, and never ask what happened to the pages after. Corvane had told Yara once, early on, with a fondness that had always sat strangely in Yara's stomach: *The ledger keeps us honest, Whitlock. So long as everything is written down, nothing can be hidden.*

Yara had believed that for about four years.

Then they had started noticing which pages came back from the guildhouse with the ink faded to nothing. Which trades vanished from the copies. Which clients were never spoken of again. The guild's ledgers did not keep the guild honest. The guild's ledgers kept the guild *clean*, and there was a difference, and Yara had learned it alone, in this room, at this desk, the way they learned everything.

"Keeper," the man said. "Do you still want it?"

Here is what Yara should have done: weighed the black memory, recorded it, sealed it in the night-post, and let the guild decide what a memory that bent candlelight and woke a sleeping gate was worth.

Here is what Yara did instead.

"I want you to listen very carefully," Yara said, "because I'm only going to say this once, and then we're both going to forget I said it, in the sense that we will never speak of it again. Something is wrong with that memory. Not with you—with it. It's too heavy. Memories of ordinary days weigh almost nothing; that's their whole trick. This one weighs like it's been carrying something inside it for a long time." Yara turned to a fresh page. "So I'm going to write it down. All of it. Your wife, the net, the goat, the light through the holes. Every word you told me. And I'm going to keep the page here."

"Not send it up?"

"Not send it up."

"Is that allowed?"

"Almost certainly not." Yara dipped the pen. "Now. Tell me about the goat. Start with the goat."

So he told it again, better this time, and Yara wrote it all down in the smallest, neatest hand—the hand that took up no room—every detail, the lacy light and the laughing and the way he'd thought *remember exactly this*, and at the bottom of the page, where the guild's form required the keeper to certify *Nothing unusual observed*, Yara wrote instead:

*The memory is black. The gate hummed. I don't know why. I'm keeping this page.*

Then they tore the page out—carefully, along the stitch, so the ledger's binding wouldn't show the loss—and folded it into quarters, and hid it in the one place in Hollowgate no one had looked in ten years because no one remembered Yara had put it there: the hollow spine of the keeper's own chair, beneath the seat cushion worn into the shape of Yara's sitting.

The man sold his memory after all. Yara weighed it, and paid him, and watched him walk out into the rain lighter by one ordinary day, and if the blackness went with him or stayed behind in the room, Yara honestly could not say. The gate did not hum again. The candle burned down. The rain kept its almost-music.

Yara sat back down at the desk, opened the Ledger to a fresh page, and wrote:

*Hour of the Wolf. One grief traded, standard rate. Nothing unusual observed. — Y.W.*

Then they sat in the quiet and felt the shape of the lie settle over them, and found—to their considerable surprise—that it fit like a coat they'd been measured for all their life without knowing it.

That was the thing about being invisible, Yara thought, blowing out the candle. Nobody sees what you keep.

The trouble, they thought, on the edge of sleep, with the rain still going and the gate silent and the black memory folded six feet away in a chair that no one had ever once offered to let them keep—

The trouble was that somebody, somewhere, always came looking for the things that went missing.

They were right. They had no idea how right.

The stars went out three nights later.
