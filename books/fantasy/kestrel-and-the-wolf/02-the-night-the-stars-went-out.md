## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with keeping a gate to another world, Yara had learned over ten years of nights, was that it was mostly *waiting*. Waiting for clients. Waiting for trades. Waiting for the small, terrible moments when something impossible crossed the threshold and Yara had to write it down in a hand so steady it looked like someone else's.

So on the night the stars went out, Yara was doing what they always did between transactions: sitting at the keeper's desk with a cup of tea gone lukewarm, mending a fraying cuff with thread the color of old honey, and listening to Hollowgate breathe.

The gate did breathe. That was the first thing they'd tell you about it, if keepers were allowed to tell anyone anything. The great arch of black stone set into the borderland hillside wasn't dead rock but something closer to a sleeping animal — its surface warmed and cooled in slow rhythms, and on quiet nights like this one, Yara could almost hear it exhale. The wards hummed along the arch's curve in a key that no musician had ever managed to write down. Yara had spent a decade pretending not to love that sound.

They were on the last knot of the cuff when the humming stopped.

Not faded. Not faltered. *Stopped*, the way a heart stops — all at once, mid-beat, leaving a silence so complete that Yara heard their own pulse stumble to fill it.

They looked up. The tea sat untouched. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if the whole room were holding its breath.

"No," Yara said quietly, to the gate, to the night, to nobody. "No, you don't get to do that."

They rose, and the floorboards announced them the way they always did — creak by familiar creak, the house's own ledger of every step Yara had ever taken through it. At the outer door they paused, hand flat against the wood, and made themselves count to five. A keeper did not run. A keeper observed, recorded, and reported. A keeper was a hinge, not a hand.

Yara pushed open the door and stepped out under the sky.

And the sky was *wrong*.

Every star was gone. Not clouded — there wasn't a wisp of cloud from horizon to horizon — not dimmed, not hidden. Gone, as though someone had reached up and unpicked them one by one from the hem of the night, leaving only a vast unbroken black. Even the Wanderer, the bright stubborn star that sailors swore by and that Yara privately thought of as the only reliable colleague they'd ever had, had been taken too.

The dark between the hills was absolute. It pressed down like water.

Yara's hands were shaking. They noticed this with a strange, distant interest, the way they noticed everything: *record this later. Client unknown. Transaction: theft, possibly. Item stolen: the entire sky.*

Then the gate screamed.

There was no other word for it. The wards, which had gone silent, came back all at once in a shriek of discord that rattled the lamp glass inside the house, and along the arch of black stone cracks of light split open like frost spreading across a window — except the light was wrong too, a bruised violet, the color of a bruise on the world itself. And through those cracks, before Yara could move, before Yara could even decide whether moving was permitted, something fell through.

It hit the ground at the foot of the arch with a sound Yara felt in their teeth. Then the violet light guttered out, the wards died for good, and the ordinary darkness rushed back in — thicker now, hungrier, as if it had tasted something.

Yara stood very still on their own doorstep, which suddenly felt like the last lit room in a drowning house.

"Right," they said to the darkness, because talking steadied them, and because there was no one left to forbid it. "I'm going to go look. And I'm going to regret it. Noted."

* * *

The thing at the foot of the gate was a wolf.

Or it had the shape of a wolf — huge, far bigger than any wolf had any right to be, big as a pony, its coat the grey of ash and rain. But it lay wrong, sprawled and broken-edged, and as Yara's eyes adjusted to the starless dark they saw why. There was a wound along its flank, long and deep, and the blood welling from it was not red. It was silver, catching what little light existed and throwing it back, glowing faintly like spilled moonlight.

Yara should have gone back inside. Every rule, every page of every ledger, every instinct honed across ten invisible years said *go back inside, bar the door, record nothing, report nothing, be no one.*

Instead Yara knelt in the frost-wet grass beside it.

"Hello," they said, absurdly. "You're bleeding."

The wolf's head lifted. Its eyes opened — amber, luminous, and unbearably *tired*, the way nothing animal is tired. It looked at Yara for a long moment, and then, in a voice rough as gravel under a cartwheel, unmistakably human, it spoke.

"You're the keeper."

Not a question. Yara's mouth went dry. "I'm the — I'm nobody. Night caretaker. The keeper isn't—"

"Don't." The wolf's ears flattened. "Don't do that. I've watched your kind do that for three hundred years. 'I'm nobody, I'm just the hands, I'm just the pen.' You're the keeper. You're the one who writes it all down." Its breath hitched, and more silver welled between its ribs. "Which means you're the only one who can write down what happens next."

"What happens next," Yara repeated. Their voice sounded very small under the empty sky. "Which is what, exactly?"

"The covenant broke tonight." The wolf said it flatly, the way you'd report a death. "The seal your guild helped forge — the thing holding both worlds apart and together — it shattered. I was carrying half of it. Now I'm carrying half of it and dying, which is inefficient of me." It tried to rise; its forelegs buckled; it caught itself, snarling at its own weakness. "The stars didn't go out, keeper. They went *home*. They belong to the other side of that gate, and without the covenant to anchor them here, they've been called back. What comes next is the dark that was always waiting underneath them."

Yara looked up at the hole where the sky used to be, and felt something in their chest crack open — terror, yes, but underneath it, terrifyingly, something that felt like relief. Ten years of recording impossible things, and here at last was the impossible thing itself, lying in their yard, asking for help.

"Can you walk?" they heard themselves say. "Inside. Before—"

"Before what?"

As if summoned, light bloomed over the southern ridge. Lantern-light, many lanterns, moving fast and in formation. Yara knew that formation. They'd recorded its arrivals often enough: the Guild of Quiet Hands did not send enforcers unless something needed to be erased.

"They can't find you," Yara said, and heard their own voice change — lower, quicker, decided. "If they find a wounded — whatever you are — at a broken gate, they won't heal you. They'll file you."

"File me?"

"Under *resolved*." Yara was already moving, shrugging off their coat. "Can you make the woodshed? There's a root cellar behind it. Old smuggling cache, predates the guild. They don't know it exists because—" a wild, giddy laugh escaped them, "—because I never wrote it down. First useful thing my silence has ever done."

The wolf stared at them. "You're lying to your own guild. You've never lied to them."

"How would you know that?"

"Because I could smell it on you from thirty feet. You reek of honesty. It's disgusting." But it heaved itself upright, swaying, silver blood dripping a slow bright trail, and limped toward the shed as Yara walked — deliberately, casually, heart slamming — back toward the front of the house to meet the lanterns.

* * *

There were six of them, led by Warden Corvane, whose face Yara had seen perhaps four times in ten years and who had never once remembered Yara's name. That, tonight, was a mercy.

"Caretaker." Corvane's lantern swept the yard, the gate, the dark stain of silver already soaking into the grass. He dismounted slowly. His gaze snagged on the stain and stayed there. "Report."

"The wards failed," Yara said. "Near midnight. The gate cracked — violet light — something came through. Then the wards died entirely."

"And the something?"

Here it was, then. The moment. Yara had rehearsed lies their whole life in a way — the careful blankness, the practiced invisibility — but never an actual lie, never words shaped to misdirect, aimed at the people who owned their bread and their bed and their name in the guild rolls.

"It went north," Yara said. "Fast. On four legs. Big. It was bleeding — silver, if that means anything to you." *Let them chase north,* Yara thought. *Let the trail go cold in the river shallows.* "It ignored me. It seemed — afraid."

Corvane studied Yara for a long moment, and Yara understood, with cold clarity, that they were being weighed — not as a suspect, but as furniture. Furniture did not lie. Furniture reported.

"North," Corvane said at last. "Two of you ride north. The rest secure the gate. Nothing crosses in either direction. If the caretaker sees anything—" his lantern swung back to Yara, "—caretaker?"

"I'll write it down," Yara said. "That's what I'm for."

He almost smiled. "That's what you're for."

They rode off — two north, four to the gate — and Yara stood alone in the yard until the lanterns shrank, then turned and walked, unhurried, a keeper's measured pace, around the side of the house to the woodshed.

In the root cellar below, lit by one shielded candle, the wolf lay curled around its own wound, watching the ladder-hole with those amber, human-tired eyes.

"You lied," it said.

"Yes."

"To your own guild. For me. A stranger who fell out of a broken sky."

Yara climbed down the last rung and sat on the cold earthen floor, and found they were smiling, and couldn't stop, and didn't especially want to. "Ten years I've written down everyone else's trades," they said. "Tonight I finally made one. My silence for your life. I don't even know your name."

The wolf was quiet a moment. When it answered, its voice had changed — softer, wary, as if the word cost it something.

"Kestrel," it said. "My name is Kestrel. And keeper, before your guild comes back — we need to talk about what your ledgers were really built to erase."

Above them, through the earth and the floorboards, the empty sky pressed down like a held breath, and somewhere far to the south, in a clean white hall, a ledger opened itself to a fresh page.
