## 3. What the Wolf Carried

Pain was an old acquaintance of mine. We had dined together in the borderlands for eleven years, and I knew all its table manners. But this pain—the one sitting behind my ribs like a guest who had decided to move in—was new, and it had brought friends.

I woke on a floor that smelled of ink and lamp oil and dust that had been left alone too long to be called mere dust. Dust with *tenure*. The floorboards were dark wood, scarred by centuries of boots and crates and, judging by one long gouge near the wall, something with claws that had once been dragged across the room against its will.

I knew the feeling.

"You're awake." The voice came from above me, and it belonged to the keeper of this place—the thin, quiet human who had hidden me from the hunters with a lie so clumsy it had nearly broken my heart. "Don't get up yet. Or do. You'll ignore me either way."

They were right. I got up.

The room tilted politely, waited until I had finished being dignified about it, then tilted again. I caught myself on a desk stacked with ledgers, and the wolf's body did what the wolf's body always did: it knocked three of them to the floor with my tail.

"Those are in order," Yara said, in the tone of someone reporting a death.

*I'm sorry,* I said, and heard my voice come out of the wolf's throat—that strange doubled sound, growl and word braided together. It always startled humans. This one only blinked, retrieved the ledgers, and began reordering them while looking at me sideways, the way one looks at a storm cloud that has apologized.

"You speak," they said. "Wolves don't speak."

"I'm not a wolf."

"No," Yara agreed dryly. "You're a bleeding hole in my floor is what you are. There's blood on the threshold, blood on the rug, and blood on my second-best quill, which I want you to know was a gift."

I looked down at myself. They were right, of course. The crossing had torn me open along the old wound, the one I carried from the night everything cracked, and I had been leaking my way through their gate like a punctured wineskin. In the other realm we would have called this *shameful*. Here, apparently, it was a matter of quills.

"Sit," Yara said. "Lie down. Whatever wolves do. I have salve, bandages, and a great many questions, and I intend to use all three."

* * *

They worked by lamplight with the brisk tenderness of someone who has tended animals before and found them better company than people. Their hands were careful. Their mouth, I would learn, was not.

"So," they said, pressing a cloth to the gash along my flank. I flinched. They pressed anyway. "You came through a dead gate on the night every star in the sky resigned simultaneously. Hunters from my own guild tried to kill you. And you talk. Shall I keep going, or would you like to interrupt?"

*You ask questions like a debt collector.*

"I *am* a debt collector. Of a sort. I record trades at this gate. Impossible things. Memories, shadows, years off the end of lives. Ten years of it." They tied off the bandage with a small vicious knot. "So believe me when I say I know what it looks like when something crosses over carrying more than itself."

I should have lied. Lying was my craft; I had been made for it, shaped into it, exiled for failing at it exactly once. But their hands were warm, and the lamp was warm, and I had been cold for eleven years in the places between worlds where nothing is warm and nothing has a name.

*I carry half of a stone,* I said.

Yara went very still. Even their breathing stopped, which in a human is a considerable feat.

"What kind of stone?"

*The kind that holds a door shut.* I reached—not with the wolf's clumsy paw but with the thing inside me, the part that was never a wolf—and let it surface. Half a seal-stone, no bigger than a coin, older than any kingdom in either realm, humming faintly with a light like the memory of dawn. Yara stared at it the way the devout stare at relics, or the way thieves stare at unlocked windows.

"That's—" They swallowed. "That's covenant-work. That's *the* covenant. But the guild says—"

*The guild lies,* I said, and the words came out harder than I meant, with more growl in them than word. *Your guild says the gate was built to keep our realm out. Your guild says the wards are yours. Your guild says there was never any pact, any signing, any promise. I know, because I've read your ledgers' reflections in the dark, and I know what erasure looks like. Erasure was my last duty before it became my punishment.*

Yara sat back on their heels. For a long moment the only sound was the lamp's small flame doing its patient work.

"Tell me," they said quietly. "All of it. I write things down. It's rather my whole personality."

* * *

So I told them, and because they asked, I told it truly—which is a discipline, like fasting, and twice as painful.

*There were two realms,* I said, *and between them a border that was not a wall but a handshake. Long ago—before your guild had a name, before it had hands, quiet or otherwise—the two realms made a covenant. Not conquest. Not separation. A *promise*. Each realm would keep a side. Each realm would send signatories to seal it: three from yours, three from ours. Six names written into a single stone, split in two halves so that neither realm could break it alone.*

"The seal-stone," Yara said. "And the gate—"

*Was never a gate. It was an altar. A meeting place. Things crossed freely—traders, healers, songs, rain that fell in one world and smelled of the other. Your guild began as its custodians. Keepers of the ledger of crossings.* I looked around the room, at the shelves, the ledgers, the careful dust. *This room was a place of welcome once. Can you feel it? Underneath the ink?*

Yara's hand had drifted to the desk, resting flat on the wood as though taking a pulse. They didn't answer, but their face did something complicated.

*Then, ninety years ago,* I said, *your founders wanted the border for themselves. Trade without sharing. Power without promise. They broke the covenant from your side—unmade the signatories' names, rewrote the ledgers, sealed the altar and called it a gate. Our realm's magic began to starve. Ours broke back, badly, in grief. And the space between the realms—the dark that isn't a place, that eats names and unmake things—began to seep through the crack they'd made. The wards your guild builds aren't keeping us out, keeper. They're patching a wound your founders opened, and lying to everyone about whose knife it was.*

"And you?" Yara said softly. Too softly. "Where do you fit in this history?"

I should have stopped there. Instead I looked at the lamp flame, because it was easier than looking at them.

*I was a covenant-keeper. One of ours. When the betrayal came, I was the one sent to warn your side—and I arrived too late, because I hesitated, because I loved someone among your founders and could not believe she would do it. By the time I spoke, the stone was shattered and six names were ash. My own council judged me negligent. I judged myself worse. So I took exile, and I took the largest piece of the broken stone, and I have spent eleven years carrying it through the dying places, looking for the other half and for anyone left alive who signed.*

Silence. The good kind and the bad kind, tangled together.

"You hesitated," Yara said at last, "because you loved someone."

*Yes.*

"And you've regretted it for eleven years."

*Every hour.*

"Hm." Yara stood, walked to the shelf, and pulled down a ledger—one of the oldest, its spine cracked, its corners soft with handling. They set it on the desk between us but did not open it. "Ninety years ago," they said. "The founding era. These early volumes are... strange. Gaps. Pages cut out. Entries where the ink has been scraped and rewritten so many times the paper's gone thin as skin. I always thought it was poor record-keeping." Their jaw tightened. "It wasn't poor record-keeping, was it."

*No.*

"They erased it. They erased it and then they made *me*—" Yara stopped. Laughed, briefly, without any humor in it at all. "Ten years I've kept those books. Ten years thinking I was guarding the truth. I was guarding the *cover story*."

I watched them stand in the middle of the room they had given their whole life to, holding the shape of their own betrayal the way I held the stone. Something in my chest, some frozen old thing, cracked slightly. Not painfully. More the way ice cracks before a river remembers how to move.

*Now you know how it feels,* I said. *I'm sorry. It's a terrible gift.*

"Don't apologize." Yara crouched again, checked my bandage with fingers that had gone a little unsteady, then steadied them. "Apologizing is my job. You've bled on enough of my possessions for one night." They paused. "Kestrel. That's your name? The hunters said it."

*It was. Names are complicated, where I come from. Mine was struck from our rolls when I was exiled. What remains is... what remains.*

"Well, Kestrel-what-remains." They tore a strip of linen with their teeth. "If half a covenant-stone is real, and the other half exists, and even one signatory still breathes—then this isn't a story anymore. It's a task. And I don't know yet if I'm going to help you or turn you in, so don't look at me like that."

*I wasn't looking at you like anything.*

"You were. Wolves have exactly one look and it's *pathetic and sincere*, and I won't fall for it." But their hands, binding the last fold of cloth, were gentle as anything. "Get some sleep. If enforcers come back, I'll lie again. Apparently I'm gifted."

I lay down on the floor that smelled of ink and old welcome, and for the first time in eleven years, sleep came easily, carrying no dark with it.

I woke near dawn to lamplight and the scratch of a quill. Yara sat at the desk, the ancient ledger open before them, writing fast, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on their face.

They had found a name in the margins. A name that had survived the scraping.

A name I recognized.

*That's a signatory,* I breathed.

Yara looked up at me across the little lamplit room, eyes wet and furious and alight.

"I know," they said. "It's my predecessor. And according to this—they never left the gate."
