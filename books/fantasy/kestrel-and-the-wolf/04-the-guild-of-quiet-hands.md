## 4. The Guild of Quiet Hands

The summons came at dawn, folded inside a ledger page that had no business being in Yara's office.

Yara found it tucked between the record of a woman who'd traded her shadow for a second chance and the receipt for three hours of borrowed courage, and it was written on paper so fine it seemed embarrassed to be touched. The guild seal pressed into the wax was a closed hand. It always was. The Guild of Quiet Hands did not knock, did not announce, did not explain. It simply appeared in your morning like weather.

*Keeper Whitlock. Attend the Master. Today. Alone.*

Yara read it twice, then a third time, because their hands were shaking and the words kept sliding out of focus. Ten years at Hollowgate, and they had never once been summoned to the Spire. Keepers were not summoned. Keepers were *kept*, the way one keeps a tool: oiled, sharpened, stored where it can be found, never brought to the table.

They thought of Kestrel, asleep — if wolves slept — curled behind the false wall in the archive where Yara had hidden them two nights ago, breathing slow and careful around cracked ribs. They thought of the memory they'd recorded and hidden, the one that shouldn't exist, the one with the covenant's name written in it like a scar.

*Alone,* said the summons.

"Right," Yara told the empty room, in the voice they used for impossible trades. "Of course."

* * *

The Spire rose from the center of the capital like a needle through cloth, all white stone and windows that reflected nothing. Yara had seen it a thousand times from a distance and never once up close; now, walking its long entry hall, they understood why. The floor was polished to a mirror shine, and every reflection that looked back up at Yara was slightly wrong — a half-second late, a shade too pale, wearing an expression Yara wasn't making.

Guild architecture. Designed, Yara realized with a cold settling in their stomach, to make you doubt yourself before anyone even spoke to you.

A servant with kind eyes and no other features worth noting led them up seven flights of stairs — no elevator; elevators were for people who mattered enough to be seen arriving — and announced them to a door that opened before she finished speaking.

The guildmaster was smaller than Yara had imagined. That was the first shock. Everyone imagined the Master of Quiet Hands as something vast, a monument in robes. Instead there sat a woman perhaps seventy, silver-haired, sharp-faced, with ink stains on her fingers that no amount of scrubbing would ever remove. She looked less like a spymaster than a grandmother interrupted at her correspondence.

"Whitlock." She didn't look up from the page before her. "Sit."

Yara sat. The chair was uncomfortable in a way that felt deliberate.

"You've kept Hollowgate for ten years," the guildmaster said. "Cleanest ledgers in the guild. Not one irregularity flagged. Do you know how rare that is?"

"I try to keep things boring, Master."

That earned a glance. Dry, assessing. "Boring is the highest art. Most keepers can't resist. They annotate. They editorialize. They fall in love with their own observations." She turned the page. "And yet. Two nights ago, a client sold you a memory that does not appear in any index of permitted recollections. And last night, something crossed the border wards that our instruments say could not cross. Both events passed through your gate. Both events are absent from your reports."

Yara's mouth went dry. "I filed—"

"You filed nothing." The guildmaster set down her pen at last and folded her hands. "Which is the only reason you're sitting in that chair instead of a cell. A keeper who lies is useless. A keeper who hides things has *judgment*. Judgment can be negotiated with." She slid a ledger across the desk. "So let us negotiate."

It was a master ledger — one of the founding volumes, bound in leather gone soft as skin. Yara knew its age by the stitching pattern alone. Their hands itched to touch it, the way a musician's hands itch near a fine instrument.

"What am I looking at?"

"The truth," the guildmaster said, "or rather, the shape we gave it. Open it to the third marker."

Yara opened it. The page was dense with entries in a dozen different hands, centuries of them, and every single entry concerned the same thing: erasures. Notations of what had been removed. *Covenant reference struck, folio 12.* *Signatory names struck, folios 30–44.* *Border accord struck entire.*

"It's a ledger of deletions," Yara said slowly.

"It is the guild's first and finest work." Something moved across the old woman's face — pride or grief, worn smooth by time into something indistinguishable. "When the founders made their bargain, they understood that a covenant is only as strong as its memory. So they built the Quiet Hands not to guard the border, child. We were built to *forget it on purpose*. Every ledger since, every training, every ritual of silence — designed so that no keeper would ever know what the gate truly was. You have spent ten years guarding a door without knowing it was a door."

"The wards fell," Yara said. "The stars went out. Something came through."

"Yes." The guildmaster's voice went very quiet. "And the other side will come asking for the signatories. And when they do, this guild will tell them, honestly, that it has none. Because we erased them. Thoroughly. Lovingly. For four hundred years." She leaned forward. "Which brings me to you, Whitlock. The wolf. Where is it?"

The room seemed to tilt. Yara kept their face still — ten years of practice, keeping still while impossible things changed hands. "I don't know what you mean."

"Don't." The guildmaster sighed, and for a moment she looked every year of her age, and tired down to the marrow. "I watched you walk in here. I read your file. You hid a forbidden memory rather than destroy it. You are a keeper who *keeps*, even when told to burn. So I will offer you the only honest trade left in this building." She pushed a fresh ledger toward Yara. Blank pages. Pristine. "Write your report. Write that the wards failed due to natural decay. Write that the intruder was destroyed at the border. Write that no memory of any covenant exists in your records. Do that, and you keep your post, your rooms, your life. Refuse—" she spread her ink-stained hands, "—and I cannot protect you from what happens next. There are those above me, Whitlock. There always have been. Even I am mostly a hostage to old oaths. But today, in this room, I am offering you the choice I never got."

Yara looked down at the blank ledger.

They thought about their rooms at Hollowgate. Small, warm, smelling of lamp oil and old paper. The kettle with the chipped handle. The shelf where they kept the trades nobody else wanted — the griefs people couldn't carry, the hopes too fragile to spend. Ten years of being essential and unseen, and it had been *home*, hadn't it? The only home they'd ever had.

And it was a cage built out of forgetting. It had always been. They had simply been comfortable enough not to notice the bars.

"If I write that," Yara said quietly, "then everything the wolf told me is true. About the founders. About the betrayal. About what happens when the covenant stays broken." They looked up. "You know it's true. You just told me yourself. Four hundred years of erasure, and the dark is coming through the cracks anyway. Writing a pretty lie in this book won't stop it. It'll just make sure no one's ready when it arrives."

The guildmaster held their gaze for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled — small and crooked and sad.

"There it is," she said. "Judgment." She drew the blank ledger back. "You understand what you're refusing?"

"No," Yara admitted. "Not all of it. But I understand what I'm choosing."

"Then run, Keeper Whitlock. Run well. And when you find the others — the ones we were made to forget—" she paused, and her ink-stained hand trembled, just slightly, against the desk, "—tell the river I still owe her a ferry fare."

* * *

Yara walked out of the Spire at a pace that was almost calm, which took more discipline than running would have. Down the seven flights. Past the mirrors that showed reflections half a beat behind. Out into streets that suddenly felt full of eyes.

They didn't go home. Home was the first place they'd look — *they*, whoever sat above the guildmaster, whatever quiet hands reached further than hers. Yara had planned for this without knowing they were planning: a decade of invisibility meant knowing every unwatched stairwell in the capital, every market crowd, every cart of turnips big enough to hide behind if one were inclined to hide behind turnips, which Yara now discovered one absolutely was.

By dusk they stood at the edge of the city with sore feet and a satchel containing, in order of importance: the hidden memory-folio, the founding ledger page the guildmaster had slipped into their coat during the handshake she'd offered at the door (a handshake! from the Master of Quiet Hands! Yara was still reeling), a change of clothes, and a jar of pickled plums.

Priorities mattered.

Hollowgate was two days' travel by road and half a day by the old drover paths nobody remembered. Yara took the paths, walking through country that grew stranger the farther they went — hedgerows that hummed, stones that cast shadows pointing the wrong way. The borderlands. The dying edge of the world, where the broken covenant leaked.

Kestrel found them before they found the gate. Of course the wolf did; Kestrel had a talent for appearing exactly where Yara least wanted an audience, usually bleeding.

"You smell like fear and pickled plums," the wolf said, materializing from a stand of trees that had no business standing that close together. One ear was torn; the ribs were still wrong. "And you're walking the wrong direction for a keeper going home."

"I'm not going home." Yara set down the satchel, dug out the plum jar, and offered it. Kestrel regarded it with deep suspicion, as though it might be a trap. Everything was a trap, to Kestrel. "I've been dismissed. Possibly condemned. Definitely hunted. I told the most dangerous woman in the guild that her life's work was a lie, and she gave me a handshake and a stolen page of evidence, and I have decided not to examine either of those things too closely until I've slept."

Kestrel took a plum. Chewed. Swallowed. Yellow eyes studied Yara with an expression that on a human face would have been called reluctant respect, and on a wolf was just a long, unnerving stare.

"You refused them," Kestrel said at last. "Your own guild. Your whole life."

"It turns out my whole life was a forgery." Yara tried to make it sound light. It came out sounding like what it was. "Someone wrote me into a story where I guard a door and ask no questions, and I've gone off-script, and now there's no page left with my name on it."

Something shifted in the wolf's posture — a lowering of the great head, the way another creature might have put a hand on a shoulder.

"Then we'll write you onto a new one," Kestrel said gruffly. "Come on. If they're hunting you, they'll reach the gate by tomorrow. And there's someone you need to meet before they do — someone who remembers what your guild paid four hundred years to erase."

"The signatories," Yara said. "They're real."

"One of them is real. The rest are ash and rumor." Kestrel turned, limping slightly, toward the darkening hills. "She lives in a river now. She'll want something from you before she helps. They always do."

"What kind of something?"

Kestrel glanced back, and there was — unmistakably — humor in it, dry and tired and warm.

"The expensive kind," the wolf said. "The kind you have to say out loud."
