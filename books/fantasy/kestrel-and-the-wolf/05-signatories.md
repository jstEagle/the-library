## 5. Signatories

### Yara

The river found them before they found the river.

They had been walking the borderlands for three days—Kestrel loping ahead in wolf-shape when the ground was open, walking on two legs when it wasn't, because as Kestrel put it, "A wolf carrying a bedroll looks like a thief. A man carrying a bedroll looks like a fool. Fools are left alone." Yara had begun to suspect that everything Kestrel said was a small philosophy wrapped around a practical complaint.

Then the mist thickened, and out of it came water—the sound of it first, a low patient chuckle over stones, and then the river itself, wide and slow and so black it seemed to have swallowed the missing stars and kept them for itself.

At its edge sat a ferry. Not moored. *Waiting*, the way a cat waits by a door it has decided you will open.

"That's her," Kestrel said quietly. The wolf-shape rippled away, leaving a tall figure with grey-shot hair and eyes the color of deep ice over running water. "Maren of the Undervale. First signatory. She held the covenant's western clause."

"You're certain?"

"I carried her half of the seal-stone once." Kestrel touched their chest, where the broken fragment hung against skin beneath the shirt. "It still remembers her weight."

Yara looked at the boat, at the black water, at the shape now rising from the stern—a woman in oilskins, silver-haired, holding an oar she clearly had no intention of using until she was good and ready.

"Keeper," the woman called. Her voice was the river's voice, that same patient chuckle. "And a wolf in a coat. I haven't seen either in a hundred years, and I can't say I've missed the combination."

Yara's mouth went dry. They had rehearsed this. On the road they had rehearsed it: *Honored signatory, we come seeking—* formal, humble, precise. But standing in front of Maren of the Undervale, all the rehearsed words evaporated like breath on glass.

"We need your help," is what came out instead.

"Mm." Maren stepped down into the boat and sat, oar across her knees. "Everyone does. That's rather the point of rivers. Everything ends up in us eventually, wanting to be carried somewhere else." She looked at Yara a long moment, and Yara felt it like being read—not skimmed, but read closely, the way one reads a ledger looking for a falsified line. "You're the Hollowgate keeper. The little one who writes everything down."

"Yara Whitlock."

"No," Maren said pleasantly. "That's what's written. I asked who you are."

* * *

### Kestrel

Kestrel had forgotten how exhausting goddesses were.

Not powerful—that went without saying—but *exhausting*. Maren had a way of asking questions that were not questions, of answering questions that had not been asked, and of doing both while looking at you as though you were weather she was mildly disappointed in.

They crossed the river in silence. It was not an ordinary crossing; Kestrel felt the water taking inventory of them as they passed, every old wound, every old guilt, weighed and noted and filed somewhere in the dark beneath the hull. When they reached the far bank, where a low stone house leaned against a hillside with smoke curling from its chimney, Maren finally spoke again.

"You'll eat first. Negotiations on an empty stomach are just hunger wearing a costume."

The house smelled of bread and lamp-oil and something older underneath, something like wet stone and green growing things. Maren set out food without ceremony—bread, hard cheese, stewed apples—and watched them eat with the frank appraisal of someone who had fed pilgrims for centuries and had opinions about all of them.

"The guild sent no one after you?" she asked.

"They think we went north," Yara said.

"Do they?"

"I wrote it in the duty ledger before we left. In my hand. It will be believed." A pause. "I've never written anything untrue in that ledger before. It felt—" Yara frowned, searching. "Like lying down in someone else's bed."

Kestrel nearly smiled. Trust a keeper to find the honest way to describe dishonesty.

"And you," Maren said, turning those river-grey eyes on Kestrel. "Exile. Covenant-keeper of the eastern reach. You let the wards crack, and you ran, and now you've come back carrying half a stone and a story." She sipped from a clay cup. "Why should I spend what's left of my oath on either of you?"

"Because the dark between worlds is eating the borderlands," Kestrel said. "Because places are unraveling. Because people are forgetting their own names—I've seen it. A village where every door stood open and every hearth was cold and not one soul could tell me what they'd been about to do."

"Yes," Maren said. "I know. My river runs through there. Lately it comes back lighter than it went in. Rivers aren't meant to come back lighter." She set down the cup. "So. You want me to reforge the covenant. Three names, three halves of the seal-stone, freely given. Fine. I'll give mine."

Hope leapt up in Yara's chest so fast it was almost audible, and Kestrel saw it happen, saw the keeper's whole face change—

"—if," Maren continued, "the keeper answers one question. Honestly. Aloud. And I will know if it isn't, because I am a river, dear, and rivers are where truth goes to be carried."

* * *

### Yara

Yara put down the spoon.

Of course. Of course it would come to this. Every step of this journey had been a peeling-away—the guild's clean ledgers peeled back to show erased lines, the gate's history peeled back to show a broken promise, Kestrel's wolf-shape peeled back night by night to show someone tired and funny and grieving. Yara had thought themselves prepared for further peeling.

But not this. Not aloud.

"Ask," Yara said, because there was nothing else to say.

Maren folded her hands. "You kept that gate for ten years. Alone, unseen, unthanked. The guild could not have picked a better keeper if they'd built one. So tell me, little writer of impossible things—" her voice gentled, which somehow made it worse, "—what does the gate mean to you? Not to the covenant. Not to the realms. To *you*. And don't tell me what you think I want to hear, because I have heard every version of that answer ever composed, and most of them rhyme."

The fire popped. Kestrel had gone very still across the table, watching, and Yara found they couldn't look at them while answering—or couldn't look anywhere else. Both, somehow.

What did the gate mean?

"It means—" Yara started, and stopped, and tried the rehearsed thing: "It means the border holds. It means people sleep safely on both sides."

"Rhymes," said Maren.

"It means duty."

"Rhymes."

"It means—" Yara's hands curled around the edge of the table, wood grain pressing into palms. And then, quietly, in the voice they used for entries never meant to be read:

"It means I mattered. For ten years, I was the only one who knew what came through that gate, what it cost, what it was *for*. Nobody knew my name. I thought I didn't mind. I told myself invisibility was a discipline, a craft." A breath that shook slightly on the way out. "But when the stars went out and the wolf fell through the gate bleeding, do you know what I felt, under the terror? I felt *found*. Someone finally saw the post I'd been standing at all these years. Someone needed the ledger. Someone needed *me*." Yara looked up, eyes hot. "The gate means I'm not invisible. That's the ugly, selfish truth of it. I want to matter to someone specific. Not to history. To—" 

The word arrived before permission did.

"To him."

Silence. The fire popped again. Somewhere outside, the river chuckled over its stones, and Yara had the mortifying certainty that it was laughing at them.

Kestrel made a small sound—not quite a word—and Yara finally turned to look at them properly. The exile was staring with an expression Yara had no entry in any ledger to describe. Something between a man watching a door he'd given up hope of opening and a man realizing it had been unlocked the entire time.

"Well," Maren said into the quiet, briskly, as though Yara had recited a shopping list. "Finally. An answer that doesn't rhyme." She rose, went to the mantel, and took down a small wooden box worn smooth by centuries of handling. "Do you know why I demanded that? Not cruelty. Necessity. The covenant reforges around willing hearts, and a heart that doesn't know what it wants makes a seal with a crack in it. We had cracks enough the first time." Her jaw tightened, just briefly—the first crack in her own composure Yara had seen. "Freely given means knowing what you're giving it *for*."

She opened the box. Inside lay a shard of pale stone, veined with faint silver light, humming softly like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

"The western clause," Maren said. "Mine since before your guild had a name. I give it freely—to the keeper who wants to matter, and the exile who wants to stop paying." She pressed it into Yara's hands and closed Yara's fingers over it. "Two halves now. One name remains."

"The third signatory," Kestrel said hoarsely. "We thought they were dead. The records say—"

"The records say a great many things," Maren said dryly. "Your guild wrote some of them." She looked at Yara, and something almost tender moved through the river-grey eyes. "The third name isn't dead, children. The third name has been inside the gate longer than you've been alive. Your predecessor, keeper. The one they told you died in service."

The shard pulsed in Yara's hands, warm as a heartbeat, and outside the window the river suddenly ran silver-bright under a sky that had no stars yet—but would, Yara thought, or die making it so.

"Then we go back," Yara said. "To Hollowgate."

"Yes," said Maren. "And you'd hurry. My river's been coming back lighter every hour."
