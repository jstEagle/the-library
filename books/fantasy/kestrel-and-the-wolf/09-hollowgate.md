## 9. Hollowgate

The gate had never looked like much. That was the joke of it, Yara thought as they climbed the last ridge with the ledger strapped to their back and Kestrel's blood-warm fur pressed against their leg. Ten years of keeping the most important door in two worlds, and it was a stone arch in a hillside, half-swallowed by ivy, the kind of thing a traveler would piss against without a second glance.

Tonight there was nothing casual about it. The sky above Hollowgate was a wound. Where stars should have been there was only a smooth black absence, like ink spilled across glass, and it was spreading—Yara could see the edges of it creeping down toward the horizon on both sides, eating the borderlands from above and below at once. The dark between worlds had found its door.

And everyone they knew was already here.

The guild had come in force: forty enforcers in their grey quiet-hands livery, arranged in a crescent around the arch, lanterns hooded so that only slivers of light escaped. At their center stood Guildmaster Corvane, and beside her—not a prisoner, not quite an ally—the ferrywoman, who had insisted on being called by her river-name even though saying it made the air taste like rain. *Vess*. She stood with her bare feet in the mud and her arms folded, looking profoundly unimpressed by forty armed guildsmen, which Yara supposed was the privilege of having been worshipped for a thousand years.

"You're late," Vess said.

"We stopped to save a village," Kestrel said. "Forgive us."

"The village is fine, then?"

"It's unraveling slightly less."

"Good enough." Vess turned back to the gate. "It wants to be opened. Can you feel it? The whole hillside is holding its breath."

Yara could feel it. The wards were dead—that had been true since the night the stars went out—but the gate itself still hummed, deep and patient, like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. It wanted what it had always wanted: to be a door again. And behind it, pressing against the seam of the world, was the other thing. The unlit dark. Patient as hunger.

Corvane stepped forward. Up close, without the guildmaster's mask of office, she looked older than Yara had ever allowed themselves to notice—tired in the bones, the way a person gets when they've been holding a door shut for thirty years.

"Whitlock," she said. "You came back."

"You didn't think I would?"

"I hoped you'd be smarter than that." But there was no cruelty in it. There was something worse: grief. "I read your predecessor's hidden pages, after you fled. All of them. I've known about the final clause for twenty years, Whitlock. I built my whole guildmastership around making sure no one would ever have to pay it."

"And instead," Yara said quietly, "you let the whole sky start paying it instead."

"Yes." Corvane's jaw tightened. "I chose wrong. I know. I'm standing here in the mud with forty knives and a broken covenant, so I trust we can agree I'm aware." She exhaled. "So. The reforge needs three signatories and a life freely given. We have the goddess. We have me—I signed it, before I understood what signing meant; my oath has been a leash ever since, and I will stand here and give whatever is asked. And we have the third name."

She looked past Yara, up the slope, to where a small figure had been waiting all this while at the edge of the arch's shadow—a thin, grey-haired person in a keeper's coat gone soft with age, whom Yara had last seen ten years ago handing over the ledger and saying *don't get attached to anything, least of all yourself*.

Maren. Yara's predecessor. Alive, hidden inside the gate's hollow, keeping the last warm room in the world.

"The clause says any keeper may seal it," Maren said now, voice dry as old paper. "Any keeper. Not any wolf. Not any goddess. A keeper—one who has held the ledger, one whose name the book knows." Their eyes found Yara's, and there was no apology in them, only a terrible gentleness. "I'm sorry, child. I meant to do it myself tonight. I've been dead for years in every way but the walking. But the book won't take me. It stopped knowing my name the day I handed the ledger to you."

The words landed like stones into still water. Yara felt the ripple go all the way through.

*The book knows your name.*

They had known, hadn't they. Some part of them had known since the ferry, since Vess made them say aloud what the gate meant—and what they'd said, trembling on the river, was not *duty*, not *the guild*, not *the world*. They had said *him*.

"No," Kestrel said.

Everyone turned. The wolf had gone very still, hackles lifted, eyes fixed on the ledger on Yara's back as if it were a knife pointed at someone they loved. Which, Yara thought absurdly, it was.

"No," Kestrel said again, louder, and the word came out half in the human voice and half in something older, a sound like winter deciding something. "I carried the seal-stone across a dying world. I have been penance walking on four legs for a hundred years. This is mine. This was always going to be mine. Yara—" and the wolf looked at them, and there was so much naked fear in that gaze that Yara's chest hurt, "—step away from the book."

"The book isn't asking you," Yara said. Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "It's asking a keeper."

"I'll become a keeper," Kestrel snarled. "I'll hold the damn ledger for ten silent years, I'll learn the trade of impossible things, I'll—"

"You hate paperwork."

"This is not the time for humor, Yara."

"It's exactly the time for humor. It's the only weapon I've got that doesn't require opposable thumbs." But their hands were already moving, unbuckling the straps, drawing the ledger around in front of them. The book was warm. It had always been warm, faintly, the way a sleeping animal is warm, and Yara had spent ten years telling themselves it was just the lamplight. It wasn't. It had been waiting too.

Kestrel lunged—not at Yara, never at Yara, but at the book, jaws closing on the spine to drag it away, and forty enforcers started forward and Vess put out one hand and the river rose out of the ground, a thin silver ribbon of it, and said, very calmly, "No one moves. This is a covenant matter. Everyone gets to watch them be idiots together."

"*Vess*," Kestrel growled through a mouthful of ledger.

"Hush, wolf. You had your century of choosing alone. Let them choose."

Yara knelt in the mud with the book open on their knees. Around them the dark pressed closer; the black absence overhead had begun, very softly, to make a sound—not a roar, nothing so honest, but a kind of un-sound, the noise of things forgetting themselves. Somewhere down the valley a farmhouse lost its name and became a shape. Yara could feel the ledger drinking in the moment, wanting it, hungry for exactly this page.

"Yara." Kestrel had let go of the book. The wolf crouched in front of them, close enough to touch, and the human voice cracked clean through. "Ten years invisible. Ten years recording everyone else's trades, everyone else's lives, and taking nothing for yourself. Don't you dare spend the first thing you ever chose on me."

"That's precisely why I'm spending it on you," Yara said. "Everything else I ever did was duty. This is the first thing that's mine." They smiled, and found, to their surprise, that the smile was real. "You told me once that in your realm, names are debts. That to give your name is to owe yourself to the thing you name. Well. I've been owing myself to this gate for a decade. Time somebody collected."

They took out the pen. Keeper's ink, mixed with gate-dust and grief, the recipe passed hand to hand down a hundred silent years. And beneath the last entry—the memory that shouldn't exist, the trade that had started everything—Yara wrote:

*Received, this night, of the dark between worlds: one gate, sealed. Paid in full: Yara Whitlock.*

Their own name. Given freely. The ledger shivered under their fingers like a living thing offered water.

"No—" Kestrel was shaking, the whole wolf, head to tail. "There has to be another way, there's always another way, I'll find it, give me a night, give me an hour—"

"Kestrel." Yara set down the pen and put both hands in the thick ruff of the wolf's neck, the way they'd done on cold nights in the borderlands, the way that meant *here, still here*. "Look at me. I spent ten years learning every trade that comes through a door like this. Do you know what I learned?" Their voice softened. "Nothing given under compulsion holds. Nothing spent in despair buys anything worth having. The clause doesn't want a death. It never wanted a death. It wants a *gift*—a life freely given, gladly, by someone who knows exactly what they're giving up and chooses anyway." They leaned their forehead against the wolf's. "I know. I choose. And I am not doing it in despair, you enormous dramatic animal. I'm doing it because loving you is the first thing that ever made me visible, and I would rather be remembered as yours than forgotten as the guild's."

For a long moment Kestrel said nothing. Then, very quietly, in the smallest voice Yara had ever heard from them: "Will it hurt?"

"Probably."

"Then I'm staying right here."

"Wouldn't dream of stopping you."

Yara picked up the pen again. The final line. The ledger opened itself to the covenant page, the old signatures glowing faintly—three hands, three realms, a promise written before either world had learned to count its years. Two of the names flickered as their bearers stepped forward: Vess, laughing softly, sadly, laying one wet hand on the page; Corvane, kneeling in the mud in her fine grey coat, pressing her palm flat beside a signature she'd been fleeing for thirty years.

And Maren, who had walked down from the arch's shadow, put their hand over Yara's on the pen.

"Together, then," the old keeper said. "One last trade. Best I ever brokered."

They wrote the name together. And the gate—

The gate *woke*.

Light poured out of the arch like water from a broken dam—but not the cold ward-light of the old seals. This was warmer, messier, gold shot through with green, the color of a hearth seen through a window on a winter night. The covenant reforged itself around the willing heart at its center, threads of light stitching sky to sky, and Yara felt the ledger take their name the way a river takes a leaf—gently, completely, without malice. Something went out of them. A weight they hadn't known they carried. Their name, their small careful invisible self, folded into the book forever.

And something else stayed. Warmth. Hands in fur. Forehead against forehead. The smell of rain and woodsmoke and Kestrel.

The black absence overhead broke apart like smoke in wind. And through the tearing dark, one by one, the stars came back—changed, Yara would swear forever after, changed and burning brighter, as if the sky had learned something from almost dying.

The light faded to a glow. The gate stood open—not sealed shut as it had been for a thousand years, but open, a doorway between worlds with a keeper's cottage beside it and a fire lit inside.

Yara sat in the mud, empty-handed, nameless in the old ledger, and laughed until they cried.

Kestrel shifted—human-shaped for the first time in weeks, kneeling in front of them, gripping their shoulders, furious and relieved and undone. "You absolute— you *clerk*—"

"Keeper," Yara corrected. "Formerly. Currently unemployed." They wiped their face. "So. I appear to have traded my name and gotten a gate, a goddess, a reformed guildmaster, and a wolf. Terrible exchange rate. I demand a review."

Vess was already wading toward the cottage, inspecting it with the air of a woman assessing real estate. "The river runs past here," she announced. "I'm staying. Someone has to keep you two fed."

Corvane rose slowly, brushing mud from her knees, and looked at the open gate, and at the stars, and at Yara, and said the hardest word in the guild's entire vocabulary: "Thank you."

But it was Maren who spoke the thing that mattered, the old keeper standing at the threshold of the cottage with the ledger tucked under one arm, smiling like someone finally allowed to rest.

"Come inside, child," they said. "Both of you. The book kept your name—but books are only ledgers, and ledgers can be amended." They tapped the cover, and beneath Yara's crossed-out, surrendered name, fresh ink was already blooming, patient as spring. "Someone once told me the next chapter is always written by whoever stays."

Kestrel's hand found Yara's in the dark.

Inside, the fire was waiting.
