## 10. Everything That Stays

The stars came back on a Tuesday.

Yara would remember that detail forever, because it was so absurdly ordinary. Ten years of ledgers full of impossible trades—memories sold by the pound, shadows pawned against bad harvests, a man who once paid for safe passage with the color of his eyes—and the sky's great resurrection happened on a night when Yara was doing inventory and complaining about a shipment of lamp oil that smelled like burnt hair.

Kestrel noticed first. The wolf had been dozing by the hearth in what remained of the gatehouse, chin on paws, one ear twitching at Yara's muttering, when they went suddenly rigid and lifted their head toward the window.

"Yara."

"You can't have my dinner. I counted the—"

"Look."

Yara looked.

Through the cracked pane, above the black teeth of the Hollowgate ruins, the sky was filling. Not all at once—the stars returned the way a wound heals, from the edges inward, faint points kindling in the east while the west still gaped dark. But they were coming back, and they were *wrong* in the most beautiful way: some burned blue where they'd been gold, some pulsed slow as sleeping hearts, and one near the horizon glowed a deep green no star in either realm had ever worn.

"They're changed," Kestrel said quietly. They had shifted to their human shape without seeming to decide to—their body did that more easily now, as if the covenant's reforging had loosened something in them too. They stood at the window barefoot, silver-haired, scarred hands pressed to the glass. "The old stars were anchors. These are... invitations."

"That's very poetic for someone who ate an entire wheel of cheese yesterday and blamed the ferrywoman."

"The ferrywoman *did* eat half of it."

"Brinn is a river goddess, Kestrel. She has a divine metabolism. You have a guilty face."

But Kestrel wasn't listening anymore. They were watching the green star, and their expression had gone soft and wondering in a way Yara had only seen once before—in the moment just before Yara wrote their own name into the ledger, when Kestrel had understood what was happening and hadn't been able to stop it.

Yara crossed the room and stood beside them, shoulder to shoulder against the cold glass. Below, in the courtyard, Brinn's lantern-lit ferry was just pulling up to the ruined dock, and the guildmaster Maren Voss could be seen picking her way down the hill path with a satchel of tools and the expression of a woman who intended to argue with the universe and win.

"Everyone's coming," Yara said.

"Of course they are." Kestrel glanced sideways, mouth curving. "You gave your name to a ledger to save two worlds. People tend to show up for that."

"I mostly did it so you wouldn't do anything noble and fatal."

"Mostly?"

"Ninety percent," Yara said. "The other ten percent was spite toward the old guildmasters. Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation for quiet dignity."

Kestrel laughed—that real laugh, unguarded, still new enough that it startled them both sometimes—and outside, under the returning stars, the rebuilding of Hollowgate began.

* * *

It took a season, because they refused to rebuild it as it had been.

Maren argued for walls first. "A gate needs defenses," she said, standing in the rubble with her guildmaster's seal finally broken and hanging loose around her neck like a weight half-removed. "Wards, watchtowers, a garrison—"

"It had all of those," Yara said, "and it became a prison with a gift shop. No walls. Doors instead. As many doors as people need."

Brinn, sprawled on a half-sunk barge with her feet in the water, raised a cup of something that steamed even though it was cold. "I second the door proposal. Walls are just doors that failed their examinations."

So they built doors. Doors of river-stone that Brinn sang awake, each one opening onto a different crossing point between the realms. Doors of ash-timber salvaged from the borderlands, which Maren insisted on inspecting personally, running her fingers along the grain and murmuring apologies to the wood in the old covenant tongue she'd been forced to forget for forty years. One door was simply a frame with nothing in it, and when Yara asked what it was for, Kestrel said, "For people who aren't ready to go through yet but want to stand in the doorway and look," and Yara had to turn away and pretend to check the mortar for a while, because their throat had gone tight.

The ledger stayed. Of course it did—it lived now in the heart of the gatehouse, open on its stone plinth, and Yara's name was gone from it forever, surrendered to the sealing. But here was the thing nobody had expected, the thing the predecessor's final clause had never mentioned: a name given freely didn't vanish. It *spread*. Every traveler who passed through the gates went home speaking of the keeper of Hollowgate, and though none of them could say the name—because there was no name to say—they said things like *the one who chose* and *the keeper who stayed*, and slowly, stitch by stitch, the worlds began sewing Yara back into existence out of story instead of ink.

"You're becoming a legend," Kestrel observed one evening, watching a merchant from the far side of the gate describe Yara—with wild embellishment—to his awestruck apprentice.

"I'm becoming *embarrassing*. He says I fought the dark single-handedly. I wrote in a book. The book did the fighting."

"He also says you're seven feet tall."

"That part's accurate. I'm imposing."

"You come up to my shoulder."

"Imposingly."

* * *

Winter came gently, for once, to the borderlands. The places that had unraveled were knitting themselves back together—not identical to what they'd been, but whole. A village that had forgotten its own name woke one morning remembering it wrong, decided it liked the new version better, and threw a festival about it. Brinn attended. There were incidents involving the river and a pie-eating contest that Yara recorded in a brand-new ledger, because if Hollowgate was going to be a meeting place, then it needed records of everything that crossed: not just trades in impossible things, but visits, reconciliations, letters carried between realms, a wolf-shaped exile teaching human children to howl at the mended moon (badly; the children loved it).

Yara kept that ledger by hand, every entry, and found that the work felt nothing like the old invisibility. Before, recording had been erasure—writing things down so the guild could make them never have happened. Now it was the opposite. Every entry was a small act of witness. *This happened. This person mattered. This stays.*

One night in deep winter, Kestrel found Yara asleep at the plinth, cheek on the open page, ink smudged across one cheekbone. Instead of waking them, Kestrel sat down across the table and simply watched the firelight move over them, and thought about names.

They had lost theirs twice, Kestrel reflected. Once to exile, when the covenant broke and they'd walked out of their realm carrying half a shattered stone and a guilt the size of a sky. And once—no. Not lost. Given. On the last night, when Yara had written their own name instead of letting Kestrel's be taken, something strange had happened: the ledger had refused to erase Kestrel entirely. A willing sacrifice averted, it seemed, left a residue. Somewhere in the old pages, in handwriting that flickered between visible and not, Kestrel's name still existed—half-real, held in trust, waiting.

Waiting for what, they hadn't known until tonight.

"Yara," they said softly.

Yara surfaced from sleep with the grace of a startled cat. "Wha—I wasn't sleeping. I was reviewing entries with my eyes closed."

"Of course you were." Kestrel slid the fresh ledger aside and turned the old one around, tapping the page where their own half-name shimmered. "I've been thinking. My name is stuck in here. Half-given, half-withheld. It doesn't belong to me anymore, exactly—but it doesn't belong to the ledger either. It belongs to the moment we made together. Which means—" they took a breath, and found their hands weren't steady, and decided not to mind, "—which means I think I get to choose what happens to it. And I choose to give it. Not to the covenant. To you."

Yara sat up very straight. "Kestrel—"

"Not as payment. Not as sealing. Just as—" Kestrel laughed at themselves, helpless. "Ten years keeping a gate alone, and I still can't find the word. As a *gift*. Names should be able to be gifts. So: take mine. Keep it however you want. Say it when you're angry with me, say it when I track mud through your immaculate gatehouse, say it in the middle of the night if you wake up frightened. I just want it to live somewhere warm."

The fire popped. Outside, the green star burned steadily above the doorless door.

Yara looked down at the shimmering name for a long moment. Then they picked up the pen, and instead of writing, they leaned across the table and kissed Kestrel—thoroughly enough that the pen rolled off the plinth and neither of them moved to catch it.

"There," Yara said when they finally drew back, slightly breathless. "That's how I'll keep it. It lives in that. Everything else is just spelling."

"That's terrible record-keeping," Kestrel said, and pulled them closer. "Future scholars will be furious."

"Future scholars can take it up with the seven-foot legend of Hollowgate."

* * *

Spring came, and with it the dedication—or rather, the non-dedication, because Yara refused to hold a ceremony and everyone ignored them.

Brinn came upriver with barges of flowering branches. Maren came down the hill with forty former enforcers who had, one by one, traded their silence-oaths for honest work, and who now formed the world's least intimidating honor guard. Travelers from both realms crowded the courtyard, and someone had strung lanterns through the empty doorframe, and a child from the far side of the gate tugged Yara's sleeve and asked, with the devastating directness of the young, "Is this your house?"

And Yara—who had spent ten years being essential and unseen, who had hidden a forbidden memory in a false ledger bottom, who had lied for the first time to save a bleeding wolf, who had written their own name into darkness and come out the other side nameless and more known than they had ever been—crouched down to the child's level and said:

"It's everyone's house. But yes. Mine too."

The child considered this, nodded with tremendous gravity, and ran off to inform the wolf, who was already surrounded by children demanding howling lessons.

Later, when the lanterns were lit and the doors stood open on their different worlds, Kestrel found Yara on the gatehouse steps, watching the changed stars come out.

"Penny for them," Kestrel said.

"They're not worth a penny. I was just thinking that the old keepers got it backwards. They thought guarding meant closing. Keeping things out. Keeping things *in*." Yara leaned their head against Kestrel's shoulder. "But that's not what a gate is. A gate is a thing that decides, moment to moment, what passes and what stays. And right now—" they gestured at the courtyard, the lanterns, the goddess arm-wrestling a blacksmith, the wolf patiently demonstrating a howl that made three children fall over laughing, "—right now, everything I love is on the staying side of the door."

Kestrel was quiet a moment. Then: "You know the stars are still sending signals. Changed ones. Brinn thinks the green one is trying to communicate."

"Mm. Tomorrow problem."

"She thinks it might be an invitation. From whatever's beyond the changed sky."

"Tomorrow problem," Yara said again, and smiled, and reached up to pull Kestrel's head down to theirs—

—which was when the empty doorframe, the one built for people not ready to cross, filled with light from a direction that had no direction, and a voice like the green star given breath spoke through it, in a language older than either realm, saying clearly enough that every soul in the courtyard went silent:

**"We heard a name was given freely here. We have been looking for such a thing for a very long time. May we come in?"**

Yara looked at Kestrel. Kestrel looked at Yara. In the courtyard, Brinn quietly set down her drink and said, "Well. *That's* new."

Yara took a breath, stood, and stepped forward to answer the door.
