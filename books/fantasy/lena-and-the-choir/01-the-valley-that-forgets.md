## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The whetstone sang a low note against the steel, and August Ishikawa listened to it the way other girls listened to birdsong.

She worked on the porch of her grandmother's house in the blue hour before dawn, when Hollowmere was still more shadow than place. The valley held its mist close this time of year, pooling it in the low fields like milk poured into a bowl, and the mountains that ringed them—Mount Sorrow to the north, the Twins to the east and west, and old Bald Crag squatting to the south like a sleeping shepherd—caught the first grey light and turned it silver at the tips. Everything else was dark. Everything else was quiet.

Everything else was safe.

August had been told that last part so many times, by so many mouths, for so many years, that it had stopped sounding like a blessing and started sounding like a wall. *Safe.* The word sat over the valley the way the mist did, thick and white and total, hiding whatever lay beneath it.

She turned the blade beneath the stone. The sword was old—not heirloom-old, not museum-old, but old the way a workhorse is old, used and cared-for and unloved in any way that mattered. Its grip was wrapped in leather gone dark with the oil of four generations of hands. Its crossguard bore no maker's mark. And its name—

Its name was a locked room in her own house. She walked past it every day. She was forbidden to open the door.

*You do not speak its name,* her grandmother had said, when August was six years old and had found the word scratched into the tang of the blade where the grip met the guard, half worn away, in letters she hadn't yet learned to read. *Not here. Not anywhere. Not ever, if you can help it. A name is a door, little one, and doors swing both ways.*

So August sharpened an unnamed sword in the dark and let the whetstone sing instead, because the stone could say anything it liked and nothing would come of it.

The rhythm of the work steadied her. Long stroke, short stroke. Heel to tip, tip to heel. Her grandmother said you could tell an Ishikawa's mood by how they maintained their edges, and August supposed hers must have been saying something lately, because she'd been sharpening every morning for two weeks now, though the blade had been keen enough to shave with since the first day. There was no practical reason for it. There was only the ache—that low, wordless ache that lived somewhere behind her sternum, the one that flared when she watched a hawk turn on the wind above Bald Crag with the whole sky available to it, or when she heard the miller's daughter laugh too loudly, or when she woke from dreams she never remembered but always woke from angry, her fists clenched, her jaw sore, as if she'd spent the night shouting something important at someone who wouldn't listen.

Restraint, her grandmother called it. Discipline. The family virtue.

August was nineteen years old, and she was beginning to suspect it was just another word for hunger.

* * *

"You'll wear the edge to nothing, doing that."

Her grandmother stood in the doorway with a lamp in one hand and a shawl dragged over her shoulders, a small woman bent now into the shape of a question mark, her white hair braided tight against her skull in the old style. Maren Ishikawa had eyes the color of river ice and a voice that could have cut glass without trying, and she looked at August across forty years of mornings exactly like this one and saw, August was sure, far too much.

"The edge is fine," August said.

"The edge is fine because you keep it fine, which is not the same thing." Grandmother lowered herself onto the bench beside her with the careful economy of motion that age had taught her, set the lamp between them, and held out her hand. August gave up the sword. It was easier than arguing. Most things were easier than arguing with Maren Ishikawa; she had once argued the miller's widow out of a decade of unpaid debts in the space of a single afternoon, and the widow had thanked her afterward.

Grandmother turned the blade in the lamplight, reading it the way Tam Osgood's old books claimed scholars read manuscripts. "You've been dreaming again."

It wasn't a question either. August looked out at the mist. "I don't remember them."

"No," Grandmother said. "I expect you don't. Your mother didn't either, at your age. Or my mother. Or mine." She ran her thumb along the flat of the blade, gentle, almost tender. "The gift doesn't die in the valley, August. It sleeps. And sleeping things dream."

"Then why put it to sleep at all?"

The question came out sharper than she'd meant it, edged like the sword, and she heard it land and winced. But Grandmother didn't bristle. She never did. She just went very still, the way she did whenever the oath came up, stillness like deep water, like the moment before a stone finished sinking.

"Because we promised," she said.

"We promised *what?* That's what I'm asking. You've told me everything else a hundred times. The valley forgets magic. The valley unmakes it. Our family came here three hundred years ago and swore an oath and gave up—" August gestured at the dark horizon, at everything beyond the mountains she had never crossed, "—all of it. Every story ends the same way. *We swore an oath.* Nobody will tell me what we swore, or to whom, or why it needed three hundred years of granddaughters keeping it."

"It needed keeping because it was given."

"That's not an answer. That's a fence."

For a long moment Grandmother said nothing. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere down in the village, the first dog of the morning started up, and then thought better of it. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed—lower, older, carrying something August had never quite heard in it before. Not anger. Something closer to grief wearing patience as a coat.

"Do you know what happens to magic when it crosses the ridgeline?" she asked. "Truly happens?"

"It dies. You've said. It comes apart."

"It comes apart," Grandmother agreed. "Like sugar in rain. No pain, no violence. It simply stops being itself. A witch who walks into Hollowmere is only a woman by the time she reaches the mill. A spell unravels mid-word. Even the old roads—the ones that were laid with intention, the ones that hummed—you can stand on them here and feel nothing at all." She turned the sword so the lamp caught along its full length, fire running edge to point. "Three hundred years ago, our family walked into this valley carrying the most dangerous thing in the kingdom. And they laid it down in the one place in all the world where it could never be picked up again."

"The gift," August said. "The swordspeaker's gift."

"The swordspeaker's gift," Grandmother confirmed. "And the oath that keeps it asleep. Both of them. Together. They are not separate things, little one, however much you wish they were. The gift is the key and the oath is the lock and the door they belong to—" she paused, and August watched her choose, deliberately, to stop, "—the door is not yours to worry about. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. Sleep well tonight, and mind the edge tomorrow."

She rose, took the lamp, and left August alone in the dark with a sword that had no name and a heart full of questions shaped exactly like keys.

* * *

Hollowmere woke around her the way it always did: slowly, reluctantly, as if the valley itself had to be talked into each new day.

August carried the morning's water up from the well, two buckets on the yoke across her shoulders, and greeted the village in the order the village expected. Old Hollis at the smithy, already red-faced at his forge despite the cold, who nodded at her the way he'd nodded at her mother and her grandmother before her, a nod that meant *you're ours and we're yours and neither of us needs to say it.* The Fenwick twins hauling nets up from the mere, arguing about whether the pike were running shallow this year the way they argued about everything, including, once, memorably, whether the sun came up in the same place every day. Widow Marsh sweeping her step with a broom worn down to a whisk, who pressed a heel of yesterday's bread into August's hand without breaking her sweep, because that was how affection worked in Hollowmere: passed hand to hand like tools, unremarked, assumed.

This was the whole world. Two hundred souls, a mill, a smithy, a chapel with a bell cracked so badly it rang flat, terraced fields climbing the lower slopes where the soil was thin and stubborn and so were the people who worked it. August knew every face, every gate, every dog. She knew which roof leaked and whose husband drank and which children swam in the mere when they thought no one watched. She had helped birth half of them and would likely help bury the rest.

And every single one of them knew what she was. That was the strange arithmetic of Hollowmere: everyone knew, and no one spoke of it, and the silence was itself a kind of vow, renewed daily, village-wide. The Ishikawas kept the oath, and Hollowmere kept the Ishikawas. It had been that way for longer than the chapel, longer than the mill, longer than memory. Outsiders came sometimes—a tinker, a tax-man, once a year a wool merchant from over the southern pass—and they stayed a night or two and left, and none of them ever seemed to remember much about the place afterward. Travelers said the road through the valley felt like walking through deep water. They said they lost hours. They said, laughing a little uneasily, that they could never quite recall the faces of the people they'd spoken to there.

The valley forgot them. And they forgot the valley.

That was the other half of the bargain, the half nobody explained because everybody understood: Hollowmere did not merely fail to hold magic. Hollowmere failed to hold *anything*, if the anything was not born here. Which meant that whatever the Ishikawas had buried under three hundred years of discipline was safe not because it was hidden, but because the whole valley was a forgetting, and the forgetting was the walls, and the walls had no cracks.

August believed that. She had believed it her entire life the way she believed in winter, absolutely and without examination.

She was thinking about it anyway that morning, standing at the well rope with the water dripping off the yoke, when she noticed the bird.

It was a crow—or it wore a crow's shape—but it sat on the ridgepole of the chapel and it did not move, and crows in Hollowmere always moved, always squabbled and strutted and stole the washing, because that was what crows did everywhere. This one sat perfectly still and faced south, toward the pass, and it had been facing south, August realized with a small cold start, since before dawn. Since she'd been on the porch. She had taken it for a shadow.

She watched it for a long moment. It watched the pass.

"Hollis," she said, passing the smithy on her way back, "has that bird been there long?"

Hollis squinted up from his anvil, wiped his hands on his apron. "What bird?"

But when August turned back, the ridgepole was empty, and the chapel bell—cracked, flat, faithful—began to ring the hour, and the sound of it went out over the mist-covered fields like a stone dropped into still water, and somewhere far beyond the southern mountains, August would think later, the ripples were still traveling.

* * *

That evening, Grandmother called her to the hearth.

It was not unusual, exactly. Winter evenings belonged to the fireside, to mending and shelling beans and the long slow business of sitting together. But there was a quality to the summons tonight—*August. Come sit.* No task attached, no errand folded into it—that made her cross the room slower than usual.

Grandmother had the sword across her knees.

She almost never touched it anymore. Her hands, August knew, ached in the cold, and the blade lived on two pegs above the mantel where it had hung since before August was born, a black shape against the plaster, present the way furniture is present, invisible the way furniture is invisible. Tonight it lay bare across the old woman's lap, and the firelight moved along it, and Grandmother was looking at it with an expression August had no name for. Reverence, maybe. Or apology.

"Sit," she said, and August sat.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Grandmother said. "And I want you to understand, before I begin, that I have told this story perhaps five times in my life, and each time I have told less of it than I knew, and tonight I am going to tell you more than I have ever told anyone. Because you asked me a question this morning that deserved better than a fence, and because—" she stopped. Her thumb moved along the flat of the blade, back and forth, a small unconscious motion, the way a person might touch a scar. "Because I think the fences are going to matter less soon. I don't know why I think that. Old women learn to trust the parts of knowing that don't come with reasons attached."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind had come around to the south, which it almost never did, and it worried at the shutters like a stranger testing a door.

"Ask me what you asked me this morning," Grandmother said.

August's mouth had gone dry. "Why did we swear the oath?"

"Mm. And underneath that?"

"What does the oath *lock?*" The words came out barely above a whisper. "You said the gift is the key. You said the door isn't mine to worry about. Whose door is it?"

Grandmother closed her eyes. For a moment she looked so old that August was frightened—not of her, but *for* her, frightened the way you're frightened watching someone carry something heavy down stairs.

"There was a king," Grandmother said at last, "three hundred years ago, who made a bargain he should not have made. And the bargain got inside him, the way rot gets inside timber—from the crown down—and it wore him, August. It wore him the way a glove fits a hand. And it would have worn every king after him, forever, because that is what bargains like that do; they do not end, they only change hands. Unless." She opened her eyes, and the firelight caught the ice-color in them and made it burn. "Unless someone outside the bargain holds the one thing that can undo it, and refuses, generation after generation, to use it. Refuses even to look at it. Refuses to so much as speak its name."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a floor tilts in a dream.

"The sword," August said.

"The sword," Grandmother agreed softly. "And the voice that wakes it. Three hundred years of Ishikawa women, little one, walking into a valley that eats magic, carrying the only key in the world to a door in the capital of the kingdom. We did not come here to hide from our gift." Her hand closed, trembling slightly, around the grip. "We came here to bury it alive. On purpose. In the one grave in the world deep enough."

The wind rose. Down in the village, a dog began to bark and did not stop, and other dogs joined it, one by one, until the whole dark valley below was ringing with a sound August had never heard there before—not alarm exactly, not fear exactly, but animals smelling something on the wind that had no business being on the wind.

"And now," Grandmother said, very quietly, mostly to herself, to the fire, to the sword, "now I find I am wondering whether the grave has held as long as we were promised it would."

August opened her mouth—to ask *what promise, whose promise, promised to whom*—and that was when they both heard it, faint and far and utterly impossible, threading up the southern pass through the wind and the dogsong:

A horn. More than one. And beneath them, thin as wire drawn tight across the whole distance of the dark, the beat of many hooves, coming down into the valley that forgot.

Grandmother was on her feet faster than August had seen her move in ten years, snuffing lamps, her voice stripped suddenly of every year she owned.

"August. Take the sword. Take the cellar stair, and don't stop for anything—not for me, not for the house, not for *anything*—"

"Grandmother—"

"*Now,* child." And in the last light of the hearth, Maren Ishikawa smiled at her granddaughter, and it was the saddest thing August had ever seen. "The valley is about to remember us."
