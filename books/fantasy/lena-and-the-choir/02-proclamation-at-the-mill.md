## 2. Proclamation at the Mill

The bell in the mill tower had not rung in eleven years.

August knew this the way she knew the number of steps to the spring, the way she knew which floorboards in her grandmother's house sighed before they creaked — by living inside the knowledge until it became part of her bones. The bell had last rung when old Marta Hollis died, and the whole valley had climbed the hill to carry her down together, because that was what Hollowmere did with its dead: it carried them, all of them, all the way.

So when the bell began to swing on a gray morning in late harvest, August's hands went still on the whetstone, and the sword beneath her palms seemed to hold its breath with her.

She was in the smithy behind the house, as she was every morning before the light came fully up, working the edge of the blade she was never allowed to name. It was an old ritual, older than she was, older than her grandmother's grandmother: oil, stone, cloth, silence. The steel drank the lamplight and gave nothing back. It was a good blade. It was, her grandmother always said, *too* good, the way a locked door is too good if you have nothing worth stealing.

"August." Her grandmother's voice from the kitchen doorway, thin as wire. "You hear it?"

"I hear it."

"Finish your work. Then we go. We go like everyone else."

There was something in the old woman's voice that August had heard only twice before in her life — once when the fever took her father, once the night the eastern ridge slid in the rains and half the terraces went with it. It was not fear exactly. It was the sound of someone measuring a distance they had hoped never to measure.

* * *

Hollowmere gathered at the mill the way water gathers in a low place, without anyone deciding it. By the time August and her grandmother came up the cart track, the yard was full — the Aldous family with their flour-white hands, the Perrys with their three loud boys finally quiet, Widow Cade leaning on Bram Oakes' arm though she'd sworn for years she needed no one. Two hundred souls, maybe more, come out of their houses on a working morning because a bell told them to, because that was the covenant of small places: *when the bell rings, we come.*

The mill itself rose above them, its wheel still turning in the stream even now, patient as it had been for a hundred years, grinding grain no one had asked it to stop grinding. On the loading platform stood a man August had never seen.

He was not impressive, and that was somehow worse. She had expected — she didn't know what. Armor. A herald's tabard blazing with the crown's stag-and-sun. Instead there was only a narrow man in a traveling coat the color of wet slate, holding a scroll, flanked by two riders in crown livery who sat their horses with the loose, bored competence of men who had done this forty times and would do it forty more. Their horses were wrong for the valley — tall, glossy, valley-bred ponies tossing their heads at the strangers' smell.

The crier unrolled his scroll. He did not shout. His voice was dry and administrative, the voice of a man reading a list of tariffs, and that dryness made every word land like a nail.

"By decree of His Majesty Aldric the Third, Sovereign of the Crownlands, Protector of the Faith, and Warden of the Purity of the Realm—" he paused, wet his lips, "—the following Edict of Purity is proclaimed throughout the shires, to be read in every town, village, and holding, and to be obeyed upon pain of law."

The mill wheel turned. The stream said its one long syllable under it.

"Let it be known that sorcery, witchcraft, and the unsanctioned exercise of gift are abominations before crown and faith alike. Let it be known that any person possessing such gift, whether by blood, by bargain, or by birth, shall surrender themselves to the officers of the Inquisition within thirty days of this proclamation, to be examined, registered, and bound to lawful use."

Widow Cade made a small sound, like a stitch pulling tight.

"Any person so surrendered who is found harmless shall be released to their livelihood under bond. Any person found harboring, concealing, or aiding a gifted person shall be judged as gifted themselves. Any gifted person who does not surrender within the term shall be declared *hunted*, and any subject of the crown may take them, dead or living, for the reward of ten crowns."

Ten crowns. August watched the number move through the crowd like a fish through still water. You could see it pass from face to face — not greed, not yet, just the arithmetic of it, the way a farmer's eye measures a field. Ten crowns was a winter's feed. Ten crowns was a new roof. Ten crowns was the Perry boys' apprenticeships, was medicine for Widow Cade's lungs, was everything the valley spent its whole year scraping toward and never quite reaching.

"The term begins today," the crier said, and rolled up his scroll.

For a moment nobody moved. The wheel turned. Somewhere a cow complained behind a fence, absurd and ordinary, and August thought: *this is how it ends, then. Not with fire. With a man reading a list.*

Then Tam-under-the-hill — no, that wasn't right, that was the story-name; it was Tamlin Aldous, the miller's brother, home from the coast these five years — Tamlin stepped forward and said, "And what if there's no gifted folk here? What then? We're meant to stand in a line and be counted for nothing?"

The crier looked at him with mild, terrible patience. "Then you have nothing to fear," he said, "and the counting will cost you an hour."

But that wasn't true, and everyone knew it wasn't true, and the knowing moved through the yard like cold air through a cracked window. Because the counting was not the point. The counting was the *net*. And somewhere in Hollowmere, standing in the crowd with oil still faintly shining on her hands, was a girl whose family had come to this valley four generations ago precisely so that no net would ever find anything to catch.

Her grandmother's hand found hers. The old woman's fingers were dry and strong and colder than the morning.

"Stand straight," Maren Ishikawa murmured. "Look bored. Boredom is armor, child. Wear it well."

* * *

They counted them in the mill yard, after all. Not a line — the crier hadn't demanded that, not here, not yet — but something worse in its way: a walking of the rows, rider and clerk, while a man from the county seat read names off a tax roll and each household answered *present* or sent up a hand to say why not. August stood between her grandmother and the Aldouses and watched the riders' eyes.

They were young, both of them. That surprised her. She had imagined witch-hunters as old and cruel, faces like dried riverbeds. These were boys, nearly, with sunburns and chapped lips, and one of them sneezed violently when the mill dust caught him, and half the children laughed, and for one bright moment the yard was only a yard again, full of people laughing at a sneeze.

Then the other rider's gaze crossed August, and stopped.

It didn't linger. That was the skill of it — she could see that it was trained not to linger, the way a fisherman doesn't stare at the water where he means to cast. But it touched her the way a hand touches a doorframe in the dark: checking the width. Measuring her shoulders against some idea it carried. Her hands, still faintly greased with blade-oil that would not wash entirely away no matter how she scrubbed, because the oil got into the creases of her knuckles and stayed. Her grandmother beside her, spine straight as a fence post, face arranged into the mild irritation of an old woman kept from her chores.

"Household of Ishikawa," the clerk read. "Maren, widow. One dependent, August, aged nineteen. Occupation?"

"Smithing," her grandmother said. "Blades, tools, mending. As her father before her, and mine before him."

"Mm," said the rider who had measured her. "A smithing family. In a valley with no road worth the name."

"We make what the valley needs," Maren said. "The valley needs little. It suits us."

Something passed across the rider's face — not suspicion exactly, or not only. Recognition, almost, as if he'd been shown a picture once, long ago, and was comparing it to the original. August felt the hair rise along her arms, under her sleeves, where no one could see.

"Present and accounted," the clerk said, and made his mark, and they moved on.

That should have been the end of it. It should have been an hour of boredom, as promised, and then the wheel and the grind and the ordinary day swallowing the extraordinary one whole, the way Hollowmere swallowed everything, gently, over time.

But when the riders rode out — down the cart track, past the spring, over the low bridge — August saw the second rider turn in his saddle and look back. Not at the mill. At her house, small and smoke-stained on its rise above the smithy. He looked at it the way a man looks at a thing he has been told to remember.

* * *

The change came slowly, which is to say it came within a single afternoon, which is to say it came faster than anything had ever come to Hollowmere before.

It started with the Perrys' youngest, Joss, seven years old and fearless, who had followed August around since he could walk, who had once sat under her forge bench for a whole day just to watch her work. He came to the fence while she was stacking split wood and stood there with his fists in his pockets, and when she grinned at him he didn't grin back. He looked at her hands.

"August," he said, very seriously, "are you a witch?"

"No," she said, and it was even mostly true, and the mostly sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. "I'm a smith."

"My da says the edict says anyone can be taken for helping. Even kids." He kicked the fence post. "My da says I'm not to come round anymore."

"Joss—"

"I'm not s'posed to say I came either." And he turned and ran, small and fast and already gone, and August stood with an armful of split ash wood and understood that the first door had closed, and that it was a seven-year-old's hand that had shut it, and that none of it was his fault, and that none of that helped at all.

By evening it was everywhere. It was in the way conversation stopped when she came into the common room at the inn to fetch the flour her grandmother had traded for — stopped, then restarted too loudly, about rain and turnips. It was in Widow Cade crossing the lane rather than pass close to her, though the lane was wide enough for six. It was in Bram Oakes, who had taught her to fish when she was nine and gutted her first trout with her, meeting her eyes for one stricken moment across the yard and then looking away with the expression of a man watching a flood come toward something he loved and finding his feet would not move.

Not everyone. That was the thing she held onto, climbing the hill in the blue dark with the flour against her chest. Not everyone. Tamlin Aldous had nodded to her, slow and deliberate, in front of God and everybody, the nod of a man planting a flag. Old Sorrel the shepherd had spat, loudly, in the direction of the departing riders and said the crown could count his sheep without counting his conscience. There were hands that still reached out.

But safety, August was learning, was not a wall with a few loose stones. Safety was a weave, and every neighbor's fear was a pulled thread, and you could not know which thread, pulled, would undo the whole of it. Four generations her family had lived in this valley believing that distance was protection — that magic could not follow them here, and neither, therefore, could anything that hunted magic. Her grandmother had built her whole life on that belief the way you build a house on bedrock.

And in one gray morning, a man with a scroll had walked into the yard and shown them all that the bedrock was paper.

* * *

Her grandmother did not light the lamps that night.

August found her in the kitchen sitting in the dark, hands folded on the table, and for a long time neither of them spoke. The fire was banked to embers. Outside, the valley lay quiet in the way it always lay quiet, the stream and the wind and the far-off complaint of a dog, sounds so constant they had become a kind of silence.

"They'll come back," August said at last. "The riders. They'll write something down about us."

"Yes."

"You saw it too. The way he looked at the house."

"I saw." Her grandmother's voice was level, but her thumbs were moving against each other, a tiny restless motion August had never seen in her before. "August. Come here. Sit."

She sat. In the ember-light her grandmother's face was all planes and shadow, and suddenly August could see the young woman inside it, the one who must have sat at this same table sixty years ago, learning whatever lesson was coming now.

"There are things I have not told you," Maren Ishikawa said. "Things I swore to your grandfather I would tell you when the time came, and prayed every night I would die before the time came." She drew a breath. "You have been raised to believe our oath keeps you safe. Keeps the gift asleep, keeps the hunters uninterested, keeps this valley a hiding place. That is the half-truth we fed you, and it was a kindness, and kindnesses are often lies with better manners."

The embers ticked. Somewhere out in the dark, the mill wheel turned on, grinding nothing now, the day's grain long finished, turning anyway because that was what it was made to do.

"What else does the oath keep?" August asked. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

Her grandmother looked at her for a long moment — looked at her the way the rider had, measuring, but with love instead of arithmetic, which was somehow more frightening.

"Not *what*," she said. "*Who.* And not only yours." She rose, joints cracking, and went to the pantry, and moved aside the crocks and the winter squash, and August heard — for the first time in her life — the click of a lock she had never known existed in the house she had lived in for nineteen years.

"Tomorrow," her grandmother said, opening the pantry's false back onto darkness, "you will learn what our family actually swore, and to whom, and why the king's men have come to a valley that magic cannot follow." She glanced back, ember-light catching one eye. "Because they did not find us by accident, child. Someone gave them the road. And it was drawn by an Ishikawa's hand."

Below the house, in the cellar August had never been allowed to enter, something that was not quite a draft moved the cold air upward, carrying the smell of old iron and older paper.

The oath was waking. And it was hungry for its heir.
