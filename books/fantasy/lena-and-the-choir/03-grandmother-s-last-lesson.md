## 3. Grandmother's Last Lesson

The night after the crier came, my grandmother did not sleep, and neither did I.

I heard her moving through the house the way I had heard her move through it all my life—slow, deliberate, each footfall placed like a word in a sentence she had rehearsed. The floorboards of Hollowmere houses were cut from black pine that remembered every weight ever laid on them, and Oba's steps had a particular sound, a creak-and-settle, creak-and-settle, that I could have picked out of a hundred houses blindfolded. That night the sound did not go to her room. It went to the hearth, and stayed there.

I lay in my loft under the eaves and watched the rafters and listened to the fire being fed, log after log, until the heat rose up through the boards and pressed against my back like a hand. Somewhere below, metal touched stone. Not my whetstone. A heavier rhythm. Something being taken down from a high shelf, or out of a locked box, or up from beneath a floor.

I should have gone to sleep. The Edict had been nailed to the mill door at noon; by morning the whole valley would be talking, and by evening someone would be talking to someone else about me, because that was how fear moved through Hollowmere—not fast, but thorough, like water finding every crack in a wall. I knew what was coming the way you know weather in your knees. But I could not sleep, and after an hour of pretending, I gave up the pretense, dressed in the dark, and went down the ladder into the smell of woodsmoke and tallow and the particular clean iron scent that had hung around our family for as long as there had been a family.

Oba sat in her chair with the sword across her knees.

Not my sword. Mine was upstairs, wrapped in oilcloth under my bed, the one I had been sharpening since I was nine years old without once being told its name. This was the other one—the old one, the one I had seen perhaps six times in my life, always on the same occasions: a death in the family, a birth, and once, unforgettably, the night a stranger knocked at our door asking directions and Oba had stood between him and the hearth for the whole of his visit with her hand resting on nothing at all, as if something invisible hung at her hip where this sword would have been.

It was shorter than mine. Older. The scabbard was plain grey leather gone stiff as bark, and the fittings were iron, not silver, and there was no ornament anywhere on it except a single line of script burned into the throat of the sheath, letters so worn they were less writing than memory of writing. The firelight moved along it and did not quite catch. Every other thing in that room caught the firelight—the copper pots, the window glass, the wet wool drying by the door—but the sword took the light and kept it, the way a well takes a stone.

"Sit," Oba said, without looking up.

I sat. On the bench opposite her, the one with the burn mark shaped like a crescent moon that I had been told since childhood was from a dropped coal and which I now, watching her hands, suddenly doubted.

"You didn't sleep either," I said.

"I slept sixty years ago." She turned the sword over in her lap, slowly, the way you turn over a letter you already know by heart but read again anyway. "Tonight I'm done sleeping."

* * *

She began, as she began everything, with bread.

There was still dough rising under the cloth by the hearth—I had set it before dawn, we both had, because the world had ended at noon and bread does not care—and while she talked she worked the dough with her knuckles, folding and turning, folding and turning, and I understood even then that this was not distraction. This was the lesson itself. My grandmother taught with her hands. When she wanted you to remember something, she made your body do it while your mind heard it, so that the knowledge lived in muscle and not just in the soft, rewritable paper of the head.

"The story you know," she said, "is that our family came to this valley to hide."

"I know it," I said. "You've told it every year at First Frost. We came north to keep the gift asleep. The valley forgets magic, so magic forgot us. Four generations of Ishikawas keeping quiet."

"Good." She folded the dough. "Now forget it. Not entirely—it's true the way a fence is true. It keeps things in and it keeps things out and it is not the reason anything was built." Her knuckles pressed deep into the pale mass, and flour rose off it in a small ghost-cloud that drifted toward the rafters. "August. What do you think an oath is?"

"A promise."

"That's what children think. What else?"

I thought about the oath. I had never seen it written; there was nothing written. It lived in Oba's mouth and in the words she made me repeat every year on my naming-day eve, kneeling on the cold hearthstones with my palms flat against the floor: *I hold the gift asleep. I speak no name. I keep the lock.* Nine words. I had said them so many times they had worn smooth as river stones, and smooth things stop being noticed.

"A promise that costs something," I said finally.

"Better. And who pays the cost?"

"The one who swears it."

"No." She looked up at me then, and her eyes in the firelight were the exact color of the iron fittings on the old sword—dark, and catching nothing. "That's the lie we tell ourselves so we can live with it. An oath is a promise that spends other people. Every oath. The man who swears to his lord spends his wife's widowhood. The priest who swears silence spends the truth of everyone he refuses to tell. You have spent your whole life paying a debt you never borrowed, child, and tonight, because the crown has come knocking, I am going to tell you what the debt bought."

The dough went into the pan. She wiped her hands on her apron, and then, deliberately, folded the apron and set it aside, as if putting away a tool whose work was done.

"Come here. Bring the lamp."

* * *

We knelt together on the hearthstones, and she made me put my palms flat against the floor the way I did every naming-day eve, and I thought, absurdly, *it isn't naming-day*, and then I thought, *she knows that*, and then I stopped thinking and started listening, because Oba began to speak in the oath-voice—the low, level voice she used only for the nine words—and what she said was not the nine words.

"There was a king," she said, "two hundred years ago, who was not one man."

The fire popped. I kept my palms flat on the stones. They were warm through my skin, warm the way a living thing is warm.

"There was a king who wore a crown that was older than the kingdom, and the crown was not empty. There are things in this world, August, that eat. Not meat. Not grain. They eat *holding*. They eat the shape a thing keeps. A cup holds water; they drink the holding. A grave holds a name; they drink the name. And a crown—" her voice did not waver, but her hands, flat on the stones beside mine, had gone very still, "a crown holds a kingdom. Do you understand? A crown is the largest single holding a mortal thing can carry. One object that contains the shape of a hundred thousand lives, their laws, their seasons, their debts, their dead. To such a thing, a crown is not a symbol. It is a feast laid out on a silver dish."

"And the king—" My mouth had gone dry. "The king two hundred years ago. He wore it."

"He wore it because if he did not, someone worse would have. That part of the story is true in every version, and you will need it later, so hold onto it: the crown cannot be destroyed. It can only be *worn*. It must sit on some head, held by some discipline, or it goes looking. Our king chose to be the dish rather than let it choose its own meal." She drew a breath. "But a man is a small vessel for so large a hunger. He lasted eleven years. Then the thing inside him began to reach past him—to use his voice, his seal, his armies—for its own appetite. And the men and women who loved him, who could not kill him and could not free him, went looking for a way to lock the door they could not close."

My palms were sweating against the warm stone. "The oath."

"The oath." She nodded slowly. "Seven families. Seven swordspeakers, in the days when there were many, when the gift was common as brown eyes and twice as dangerous. Each family took a seventh of the binding. Each family swore a lock, and each lock needed a key-shaped absence—a gift held asleep, a name left unsaid, generation after generation, until the debt was paid or the world ended, whichever came first. Six of the seven families broke or died or scattered within a century. Their locks failed quietly, one by one, like teeth falling out of a jaw. Ours held. Ours was the last tooth, August. The final lock. The one that, if it fails, the whole jaw opens."

The fire had burned down to a red heart. I became aware, distantly, that the house around us felt different—thicker somehow, as if the walls had leaned in to listen, as if the black pine floors were holding their breath along with me.

"So the valley," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, young and thin. "The valley isn't hiding us."

"The valley is the *sheath*." Oba's hand came down over mine, pressing my palm flatter against the hearthstone, and beneath my skin I felt it then—a hum, faint as a bee behind a wall, deep as bedrock. I had knelt on these stones ten thousand times and never felt it. "Magic cannot follow here because we asked the land itself to forget us, and the land, being generous and old, agreed. Inside this valley, the gift sleeps so deeply it might as well not exist. Outside—" she turned my hand over, palm up, and traced one line across it with her thumb, a line I would feel for the rest of my life, "outside, the moment you speak your sword's name, the last lock turns. And what is locked does not stay locked politely. It wakes, and it reaches, and the first thing it reaches for is the hand that opened the door."

I looked at the old sword across her knees. The firelight still would not catch on it.

"Why show me this one?" I asked. "Why tonight?"

"Because tomorrow, or the day after, hunters will come to this valley, and they will not leave, and you will have to make a choice I hoped you'd die of old age never facing." She lifted the old sword and held it out across the space between us, and I saw that her hands were shaking—not with age, or not only with age. "This blade remembers the binding. It was forged in the first year of the oath, from the same steel as yours, by the same hand. Yours is the key. This one is the witness. If the day comes when you must break the oath, you break it before this blade, so that something older than your fear can see it done properly."

"Break the oath," I repeated. The words felt like handling a snake—smooth, cold, wrong-directioned. "You spent my whole life teaching me to keep it."

"I spent your whole life teaching you to *carry* it." For the first time that night her voice cracked, just barely, a hairline fracture in old glaze. "Keeping is easy. Carrying is knowing what it weighs. And August—" she closed my fingers around the scabbard of the witness-blade, and her grip was fierce, and her eyes were wet and furious and proud all at once, "if the day comes, you do not break it out of fear, and you do not break it out of anger. You break it the way your great-grandmother meant it broken: *by choice, for justice, with witnesses.* Anything less and the lock doesn't open. It shatters. Do you hear me? There is a right way and a wrong way to end the world's longest promise, and the wrong way leaves the door hanging open."

Outside, far off down the valley, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then a third, farther away, and then the ugly chorus of them all together, the sound Hollowmere dogs made only when strangers were on the roads at night.

Oba's head came up. She was seventy-eight years old and she moved like a girl getting out of a cold river—fast, total, no transition. The witness-sword went back into its hiding place beneath the floor with three movements of her hands, and the board settled over it seamless, and she was standing, and she was blowing out the lamp, and she was saying, calm as Sunday:

"Cellar. Now. Take the lamp relight and the bread. Don't argue, don't ask, and August—" she caught my wrist in the dark, and her thumb found the line she had traced there, and pressed it, hard, like sealing a letter, "whatever you hear above you tonight, whatever you hear tomorrow, you stay asleep. The gift stays asleep. Promise me the nine words."

*I hold the gift asleep. I speak no name. I keep the lock.*

I said them. She kissed my forehead, and her lips were hot, fever-hot, though her hands were ice, and she pushed me toward the cellar door, and the dogs were barking everywhere now, all down the valley, a ring of them, closing.

Later—much later, pressed into the dark behind the preserve shelves with the witness of two hundred years humming faintly through the floorboards above me—I would understand that she had known. Not guessed. *Known.* Known that the map existed, known that the map was coming, known that the lesson could not wait for a kinder night because there were no kinder nights left.

And I would understand, too, what she had not said, what she had folded into the bread and knelt into the stones and sealed into my palm with her thumb: that the first half of the history ends with a question she could not answer.

*Who drew the map?*

Only an Ishikawa could have.
