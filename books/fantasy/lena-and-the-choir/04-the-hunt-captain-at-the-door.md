## 4. The Hunt-Captain at the Door

The knock came at dusk, and it was wrong.

August knew the sound of every knuckle in Hollowmere. Old Wren Halloway rapped twice and waited, always, because his hands ached in cold weather. The miller's boys pounded like they were threshing. The tanner's daughter knocked with her elbow because her arms were full of hides half the time. But this was three strikes, evenly spaced, hard enough to shiver the latch in its bracket — the knock of someone who expected doors to open for them, not the other way around.

Grandmother was already moving.

August had been sitting by the hearth with the sword across her knees, whetstone whispering along its edge in long, patient strokes. It was the evening ritual of her whole life: sharpen a blade she had never been permitted to name, hone an edge for a war that was never supposed to come. The steel drank the firelight and gave nothing back. She looked up, and saw her grandmother standing very still in the middle of the room with her head tilted, listening the way August had seen her listen to storms — as if counting something in them.

"Whetstone down," Grandmother said quietly. "Sword on the table. Not in your hand."

"Who is it?"

"Someone who knocks like the door owes them." Grandmother crossed to the window and put one finger against the shutter's seam, parting it the width of a hair. Whatever she saw out there made her face go smooth and empty, which was worse than any expression August had ever seen her wear. "Three riders at the gate. Six more in the lane. Grey cloaks. Silver pins shaped like open eyes."

The words landed in August's stomach like stones dropped into a well. *Witch-hunters.* She had heard the word all her life the way you hear the names of diseases — distant, clinical, belonging to other places. The Edict had been read at the mill nine days ago; the crier's voice still rang in her ears some nights, *all gifted persons to be surrendered or hunted*, and the neighbors' eyes sliding toward her afterward like water finding a crack. But Hollowmere forgot things. That was the valley's whole nature. Magic could not follow them here; surely the crown's long arm would forget them too, the way the valley forgot everything.

"They can't find anything," August said. "There's nothing to find. I've never—"

"Hush." Grandmother turned from the window. In the firelight her eyes were bright and terribly awake, and August understood, suddenly, that the old woman had been waiting for this knock her entire life. Not fearing it. *Expecting* it. "Listen to me now, child, and don't waste breath arguing. There's a hatch behind the flour bin. Stairs down to the cellar, and past the cellar, the tunnels your great-grandfather cut through the limestone. They run north under the ridge line. You follow them until you smell pine, and then you climb, and then you keep walking until the valley is a story someone told you."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are." No anger in it. Just the flat certainty of bedrock. "Because if you stay, I have to spend my last hours watching two of us instead of guarding one, and I am too old to guard two." She was already pulling up floorboards near the pantry, dragging out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth: dried meat, a skin of water, a small clay pot sealed with wax. "The pot is salve. Burn-leaf and marrowfat. You'll know why when you need it, and I'm sorry for that."

"Why would I need—" August stopped. Her grandmother's hands were shaking, and her grandmother's hands never shook. "You knew this was coming. Before the Edict. Before any of it. You've known."

For a moment — just a moment — the old woman's composure cracked, and August saw underneath it something vast and exhausted and old beyond counting, a woman who had spent sixty years carrying a secret the way a mule carries stone, uphill both directions.

"The Ishikawas have always known," she said. "That's what the oath *is*, August. Knowing. Now go. Down the stairs, left at the second fork, and whatever you hear above you — whatever you hear — you do not come back up."

August set the sword on the table as she'd been told, then picked it up again. Her grandmother watched her do it and, after a heartbeat, nodded once — permission, or surrender, or both.

The hatch was exactly where she'd been told, though she'd lived in this house all seventeen years of her life and had never once suspected it. The stairs went down farther than the house stood tall, into air that tasted of limestone and old water. Behind her, faintly, she heard her grandmother close the hatch, slide the flour bin home over it, and then — this was the sound August would carry longest — begin to lay another log on the fire. Calmly. As though preparing for company.

She meant to go straight to the tunnels. She told herself she did.

But the cellar had a gap in its north wall where the mortar had crumbled, a gap August had peered through since childhood, and it looked out across the yard toward the front door. And so instead of running, August pressed her eye to the cold stone and watched three grey-cloaked figures dismount in her grandmother's yard, and watched the door open before they knocked a second time, and watched Captain Sera Vane step into the light of her family's hearth.

* * *

She was younger than August had imagined a hunt-captain would be. That was the first wrong thing. August had pictured some grey-bearded butcher, some man built like the gallows he served. Instead: a woman perhaps thirty, broad-shouldered, dark hair cropped short, wearing her captain's insignia the way other people wore old scars — without apology and without comfort. She carried her helm under one arm rather than on her head, and she stamped the road mud from her boots on the mat, and August thought, absurdly, *she wiped her feet.* Witch-hunters didn't wipe their feet.

Two hunters flanked her inside the doorway, but Vane held up a hand and they stayed at the threshold, which meant she wanted the conversation private, which meant it wasn't going to be a simple arrest.

"Mistress Ishikawa," Vane said, and her voice carried clearly through the gap in the wall, low and even, a voice trained to be believed. "I'll be plain with you, because I think you're owed plainness. I have a warrant for the inspection of this household. Names, bloodlines, any objects of power. You may cooperate, or my men will make it thorough. Thoroughness is ugly. I'd spare us both."

"Young woman," Grandmother said, and her voice was warm, almost amused, the voice she used for telling fortunes at midwinter festivals, "you are standing in the kitchen of a dying woman who has baked bread every week for forty years. Look around you. What object of power do you see? The butter churn?"

"I see a valley that shouldn't exist," Vane said. "That's the thing about Hollowmere, mistress. I've read the surveys. Every gifted bloodline in the kingdom has a geography, and yours is the only one with no record at all. No census. No tithe. No witch-pricings, no burnings, no trials — nothing, for six generations, in a kingdom that has burned *hundreds*. A hole in the ledger that clean isn't luck. It's maintenance." A pause. "Someone paid a great deal, for a very long time, to make sure no one ever looked here."

August's mouth went dry. Above her, through the floor, she could hear her grandmother's silence doing work.

"And yet," Vane went on, "here I am. Which means someone finally looked. Do you know how I found this valley, mistress Ishikawa? Not from your ledgers — you haven't got any. Not from informants — you don't have those either. I found it because a map came to my desk in the capital with this house marked on it. This exact house. A map of Hollowmere so precise it shows the well, the millrace, the footpath to the ridge shrine. Drawn in a hand I'd swear was trained in the royal scriptorium." Paper rustled. "And there's a mark on it. A seal. Can you tell me what this seal means?"

Silence. Long enough that August's own heart seemed loud in the stone.

"No?" Vane said softly. "Then let me ask it differently. Who in your family draws maps?"

And August felt the world tilt, because she knew — everyone in Hollowmere knew — that no one drew maps. That was the oldest rule after the oath itself. *We do not name the blade, we do not draw the land.* Maps were how hunters found people. Maps were how the world outside reached in. Her grandmother hadn't drawn so much as a garden plan in August's lifetime.

"There is no one," Grandmother said at last, and there was something in her voice August had never heard there before. Not fear. Something colder. "Captain, that seal — describe it to me. Precisely."

"A circle broken by a vertical stroke," Vane said. "With a dot in the upper quadrant."

Another silence, longer, and when Grandmother spoke again her voice had changed entirely — flattened, careful, a voice walking on ice.

"That is not an Ishikawa seal."

"No," Vane agreed. "It isn't. I checked. I spent three weeks in the royal archive checking, which is how I came to learn that the archive's catalog of heraldic seals has pages missing. Cut out. Recently." Another rustle of paper. "So here is where we are, mistress. Someone drew a map of your house in a royal hand, sealed it with a mark that isn't yours, and sent me to collect a family that the crown's own records say doesn't exist. Either you are the greatest concealment in the history of the hunt — or someone very powerful wants you found, and doesn't care whether I understand why." A chair scraped. "Which is it?"

"Captain Vane." Grandmother's voice was gentle now, terribly gentle. "How old is the king?"

A pause. "What has that to do with—"

"Nineteen years on the throne, and never once seen to eat in public. Nineteen years, and no heir named. Nineteen years, and the hunts began in his seventeenth year — the year he came of age, the year a king begins to rule *himself*. Ask yourself who gives the orders you carry, Captain. Ask yourself whose hand holds yours when you hold the warrant." A breath. "You seem an honest woman in a dishonest trade. So I'll give you honesty: search the house. Turn over every stone. You will find an old woman, a dead hearth, and nothing else — because everything else left this valley before you were born, and the valley itself will help you forget you ever came."

Above, boots moved. Drawers opened. The hunters worked through the house while August stood frozen in the dark with her grandmother's bundle clutched to her chest, and she should have been moving — *left at the second fork* — but she couldn't stop watching through the gap in the wall, couldn't stop seeing Vane stand in the middle of her family's kitchen turning slowly, reading the room like a page.

It was Vane herself who found the hatch.

Not by searching — by standing still. August watched her stop in the middle of the room, head tilting, the same listening posture her grandmother had worn an hour before. Then she walked — not to the flour bin, but to the corner where the draft ran wrong, where the cellar air breathed up through a seam in the boards — and knelt, and pressed her palm flat to the floor.

"Mistress," she said quietly, "there's cold air coming up through your kitchen floor."

And August's grandmother said, in a voice like the closing of a book:

"Then you'd best take me instead."

* * *

The words dropped through the floorboards and broke over August like cold water.

She watched, through the gap, as her grandmother walked — unhurried, spine straight as a plumb line — to where Vane knelt, and stood over her with the firelight gilding one side of her face.

"This house has stood two hundred years," Grandmother said. "It has a cellar, and the cellar has a root cellar, and the root cellar has a crawl space, and none of it concerns you. But you will not put your hands on my granddaughter, Captain, because my granddaughter is not here. Check the beds. Check the wardrobes. She is not in this house, and if you tear it apart board by board you will only prove how badly your masters want a girl who sharpens swords for a living, and how little proof they actually have."

"Grandmother." Vane said it carefully, testing the word. "Your neighbors tell me the girl lives here. Your neighbors tell me she carries a sword everywhere, even to church. Your neighbors tell me—" the captain paused, and something flickered across her face, distaste or doubt, "—that she talks to it."

"She does talk to it," Grandmother said. "Aloud, sometimes, when she thinks no one hears. The way lonely children talk to anything. If that is a crime now, Captain, then arrest every widow in the kingdom."

"It's not the talking that concerns me." Vane rose. She was taller than Grandmother by a head, and she did not loom, which somehow made it worse — she simply stood, patient as winter. "It's the map. Because I've been doing this work for eleven years, mistress, and I know what a coerced drawing looks like, and I know what a willing one looks like, and the map on my desk was neither. It was *practiced*. Whoever drew it has drawn this valley a hundred times. From memory. In detail no surveyor could match." She looked around the kitchen slowly. "An Ishikawa drew that map. Or someone who loved an Ishikawa enough to know this house down to the grain of its floors. And whoever they were, they didn't send it to warn you. They sent it to *find* you."

"Then your quarrel is with them," Grandmother said, "not with me."

"My quarrel," Vane said, "is with whoever is using the crown's hunt as a private errand. And I am beginning to suspect that person signs their orders with a broken circle and a dot." She turned to her men in the doorway. "Search the cellar."

August was already moving.

She went left at the second fork, as instructed, down a passage so low she had to bend double, the bundle strapped tight across her back, the sword's scabbard scraping limestone overhead. Behind her — muffled, distorted, coming through the stone like voices underwater — she heard the flour bin scrape aside, heard the hatch bang open, heard boots on the stairs.

And she heard her grandmother's voice, one last time, pitched to carry down through the floor the way it had once carried across festival crowds:

*"Left at the fork, August! Left, and don't look back! Whatever you hear —"* and then the voice broke off, because a hunter must have reached her, and the last thing that came down the tunnel was not words at all but a single sound, sharp and furious and utterly unafraid —

— and then the tunnel swallowed everything, and August ran.

* * *

She ran until the air changed.

That was the marker her grandmother had given her — *until you smell pine* — and the limestone gave way to packed earth, and the packed earth to roots, and the roots to night sky opening above her like a wound in the hillside. August climbed out into a stand of pines on the north ridge, lungs burning, legs shaking, and turned to look back.

Hollowmere lay below in its bowl of hills, lamplit and small, the millpond holding a smear of orange firelight. Nothing was burning. Not yet. Smoke rose thin and ordinary from chimneys. Somewhere down there, in her grandmother's kitchen, an old woman was trading herself for a head start, and the valley went on forgetting, as it had always forgotten, as it had been built to forget.

August stood among the pines with the sword heavy on her hip and her grandmother's bundle digging into her shoulders, and she made herself look at the truth she'd been outrunning since the knock at the door.

Someone had drawn that map. Someone who knew this house down to its bones. Someone with a royal hand and a seal that wasn't theirs, and there were perhaps six people alive who had ever stood in that kitchen — and August could count them on one hand, and every one of them was either below her in that valley right now, or dead, or—

Or she didn't know. That was the worst of it. Seventeen years of restraint, of the unspoken name, of the whetstone's nightly liturgy, and it had all been undone not by magic, not by the gift, not by anything August had done — but by a piece of paper in a hand she might have known, sealed with a mark she didn't.

Betrayal, she thought, doesn't come through the walls you guard. It comes through the ones you never thought to watch.

Below her, a light flared in her grandmother's kitchen window — brighter than hearthlight, brighter than it should be, throwing shadows that leapt. Then another, in the barn. Then a third, at the mill, far across the water, where the crier had read the Edict nine days ago to neighbors who had known her all her life and had, apparently, known her well enough to sell her.

The valley was beginning to burn.

August gripped the sword's hilt — the unnamed, patient, sleeping thing — and felt, for the first time in her life, it feel less like a burden and more like a question. *What would you have me do?* she almost asked it. She caught herself just in time. Some questions, her grandmother had taught her, you do not ask unless you are prepared to live inside the answer.

She turned north, away from the firelight, away from the only home she had ever known, and began to walk.

Behind her, faint across the miles, she thought she heard singing — high and clear and wrong, a voice like a struck bell, rising from somewhere far off along the south road, where no choir had any business being.

She did not stop. But she listened, all that night, as she walked.
