## 5. What the Fire Took

*Later, she would remember it in pieces. That was the mercy of it — and the cruelty. Memory broke the way the valley broke: not all at once, but along old fault lines, so that what remained was never whole again.*

The first piece was smoke.

August stood on the ridge above Hollowmere with her lungs full of it, and the smoke tasted like everything at once — like pine resin and thatch, like the miller's rye bread, like the tallow candles in the shrine where her grandmother had prayed to no god in particular for sixty years. It tasted like the inside of her own throat, which had gone raw from screaming a name down into the valley that no one had answered.

*Grandmother.*

She had screamed it until the word stopped meaning anything. Until it was just a shape her mouth made. And the valley below had answered with fire, and with the small bright sounds of things collapsing, and with nothing else.

* * *

Here is how the pieces came, in the weeks after, when she let herself take them out and look at them:

Piece one: the cellar door closing.

Her grandmother's hands on her shoulders, those hands that had kneaded bread and bound sword-hilts and pulled splinters from August's palms for nineteen years, pressing her down the ladder into the dark. The smell of earth and cold stone and turnips. The lantern held high so August could see the tunnel mouth, the low crawl of it going north under the root-cellars of three houses, dug by great-grandfathers who had never said why.

"You go north," Grandmother had said. Her voice was very calm. That calm should have told August everything. Grandmother was only ever calm before a storm or a lie, and there was no storm coming that August could see. "You don't look back. You don't come back. You find the river and you follow it, and you don't speak to anyone wearing the crown's colors, and you *don't*—"

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are." No anger in it. Just the flat certainty of a woman stating the weather. "Because I am asking you to, and because you have spent your whole life doing as I ask, and because if you stay, they will have both of us, and then the oath dies in this valley with nothing carried out of it. Do you understand? The oath doesn't live in me, child. It lives in whoever walks away carrying it."

"The oath is a lie," August had said, because of the lesson, because of last night, because of the first half of the true history still sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone. "You told me. It's a lock on something larger. You told me it wasn't protection—"

"It is both," Grandmother said. "Most heavy things are." And then she had kissed August's forehead, once, hard, like pressing a seal into wax, and closed the trapdoor, and August had heard the bar drop into its brackets on the other side, and had beaten her fists against the underside of it anyway, uselessly, the way children beat on doors, though she was nineteen years old and had never once been a child who beat on doors.

Piece two: the tunnel.

Crawling through the dark with dirt in her mouth and the sword wrapped in sacking across her back, the unnamed blade knocking against her spine with every movement like a second heartbeat. Above her, muffled by six feet of earth, the sounds of the village. Boots. Voices with the crown's cadence, clipped and certain. Captain Vane's voice — Sera Vane's voice — giving orders in that level tone August had heard only once before, at the mill, reading the Edict aloud while neighbors looked at her sideways.

And then, threaded through all of it, thin as wire: her grandmother's voice, raised for perhaps the third time in August's entire life.

"—entered this house without warrant, and you will leave it the same way—"

A crash. Laughter, ugly and young. The particular sound of a table breaking, which August knew intimately, having broken two of them herself in her time.

She had crawled faster. She had crawled until her elbows bled, and the tunnel went on, and the sounds above faded, and that was the worst part, that fading — the way the world could simply diminish behind you, volume by volume, until your whole life was a rumor in another room.

Piece three: the ridge at dusk.

She came up out of the tunnel's end in the bracken half a mile north, in the gray light between afternoon and evening, and turned around, and saw it.

Hollowmere burned the way a body burns: slowly at the edges first, then everywhere at once. The smithy went first — she would learn later it went first, though in memory everything seemed to burn simultaneously — and then the mill, and then the row of houses along the water where the Aldous family lived, where Tam Osgood's mother had lived before she moved to the capital, where August had learned to swim in the millpond by nearly drowning four separate summers running. The church bell was ringing. Someone was ringing the church bell, over and over, and August thought absurdly, *they'll put it out, someone will put it out, the bucket line, we've done it a hundred times for chimney fires* — and then she saw the riders moving through the streets with torches, setting new fires where the old ones hadn't caught, and understood that this was not an accident waiting to be corrected. This was a sentence being carried out.

They were burning the valley because the valley had hidden her.

Or they were burning the valley because the map had led them here and the map had been wrong about where she'd be, and fire was what the crown did with wrong answers. Either way. Either way.

August stood in the bracken with the sacking-wrapped sword on her back and watched her whole life become light and heat and columned black smoke, and she did not cry. She noted that she did not cry, the way you note a strange weather pattern. There was something happening in her chest instead of crying, something vast and pressureless and white, like the moment before lightning when the air itself seems to hold its breath.

* * *

Piece four came later, and it was the one she hated most.

From the ridge, in the last light, she saw figures in the square. Small at this distance, doll-like, arranged around something. She could not see faces. She could see the arrangement of them, though — the way soldiers arrange themselves around a thing they've been ordered to look at but not touch — and at the center of the arrangement, upright, unbending, unmistakable even as a speck against the firelight, stood her grandmother.

Ishikawa Maren. Who had buried a husband and two sons and never once been seen to stoop. Who had taught August the forms with a broom handle in the kitchen and the real blade only on the high pasture, wrapped in wool, unwrapped for minutes at a time. Who had said, *the gift sleeps because we keep it asleep, and we keep it asleep because we promised,* and had never explained the promise until the last night, when it was almost too late to matter.

August could not hear what was said. Distance took the words and gave her nothing back. But she saw her grandmother raise one hand — one hand, palm out, the gesture of the first form, the opening stance of every morning practice since August was six years old — and she saw the soldiers flinch back from that empty palm as if it held a blade.

And then the smoke moved. A house collapsed somewhere, or the wind shifted, or the fire simply breathed, and the square was blotted out, and when the smoke rolled on, the arrangement of soldiers was still there and her grandmother was not standing anymore.

That was all August got. Not the moment itself. Only the before and after of it, with the middle eaten.

For years afterward she would fill in the middle, differently each night. Sometimes her grandmother spoke the sword's name herself, there in the square, and the fire answered her, and the soldiers' flinching was the flinching of men who had just seen what an Ishikawa could do when she stopped pretending. Sometimes she said nothing at all and simply refused, with her whole spine, to kneel. Once — only once, in a fever-dream on the road north — August dreamed the middle truthfully: that her grandmother had looked directly up at the ridge, directly at the bracken where August stood, across half a mile of darkening air, and had found her there, and had smiled.

Not comforted-smiled. *Confirmed*-smiled. The smile of a woman checking that the seed was planted before she let them cut down the tree.

August woke from that dream with her hand locked around the sword's hilt through the sacking, and the blade humming under her palm like a struck bell that wouldn't finish fading, and she did not sleep again that night, or many nights after.

* * *

Piece five: the others.

She almost didn't see them. They were folded into the shadows at the tree line below the ridge — the Widow Corren, whose husband had died in the rockfall eight years past; the Hollis brothers, who mended roofs; little Ada Finch, eleven years old, whom everyone in the valley had always quietly known was *something*, though no one had ever said what, because saying was dangerous and silence was the valley's native tongue.

Seven of them. Eight. Huddled in the trees like animals that had smelled the fire before it started, because some of them had — Ada had come pounding up the hill path an hour before the riders reached the square, white-faced, saying *they're coming, they're coming, I felt them coming* — and the valley had not believed her fast enough, and now the valley was smoke.

They looked up at August on the ridge the way survivors look at anything: with hunger and accusation in equal measure. The Widow Corren's face was streaked black with ash and something else. "Your grandmother," she said, and couldn't finish it.

"I know," August said.

"She sent us up here. Before dawn. She said—" The widow's voice cracked and reassembled itself. "She said, *when it starts, take them to the ridge and keep them off the road.* She knew. Beatrix, she *knew* it would be today."

Of course she had known. She had known since the crier came. Perhaps since before the crier; perhaps the map had been missing from its hiding place for weeks and she had counted the days like a woman counting down to a birth or a death. She had spent her last morning baking bread — August remembered that now, the smell of it, the loaves cooling on the sill as if it were any morning — because a last morning should be used for something, and she had chosen bread.

"Where do we go?" Ada Finch asked. Eleven years old, ash in her hair, asking the question the way children ask it: as if an adult would know, as if the world were still run by competent people.

August looked at the valley. The church had caught now; the bell had stopped mid-swing, or the ringer had fled, or the ringer had not fled. Down the north road, the road her grandmother had ordered her to take, the darkness was ordinary darkness, unburnt, patient.

She did not know where they would go. She knew only where they could not stay, and that the sword on her back had begun, very faintly, to warm — not the warmth of fire-reflecting heat, but a warmth from inside the steel, like a sleeping animal's blood — and that every instinct her grandmother had spent nineteen years training into her said *north, alone, unseen, unnamed,* and every other thing in her body said *down that hill, into that fire, with this blade, and let the gift wake and be damned.*

Both voices were her grandmother's voice. That was the cruelest discovery of the night: that obedience and rebellion had the same accent.

"We go north," August said finally. Her voice came out scraped and strange. "Off the road. Through the high woods. We move tonight, we don't light fires, we don't speak the valley's name again." She looked at their faces, seven and eight of them, hollow-eyed, and understood that she was looking at the oath now. Not the words of it. The weight. This was what her ancestors had carried out of whatever war had made them swear it: not a promise, but *people*, walking, needing to be kept alive.

"And after north?" the widow asked.

"After north," August said, "we find out why."

* * *

The last piece was the smallest, and she kept it longest, and it was this:

Before they left the ridge, before the high woods swallowed them, August knelt in the bracken and unwrapped the sword.

Just for a moment. Just enough to see it catch the firelight from below — the plain crossguard, the worn grip shaped by five generations of Ishikawa hands, the blade that had never been named because naming was the first door and the oath stood in front of every door. The steel reflected the burning valley perfectly, upside down, a whole world inverted in eighteen inches of metal, and August thought: *this is what I am. Everything I love, held reversed in something sharp, and forbidden to speak.*

"I don't know your name," she whispered to it. "But I'm going to find it. And when I say it, it will be for her."

The blade did not answer. Blades didn't answer; her grandmother had been very clear about that, and clear people didn't lie about swords.

But the warmth in the steel deepened, just slightly, the way a banked fire deepens when breathed on. And far to the south, past the burning valley, past the hills, in a direction August could not see and had never been, something old and crowned turned its attention northward — slow as a glacier, patient as rot — and began, for the first time in nineteen years, to listen.

August wrapped the sword, shouldered it, and led the living down into the dark of the high woods, away from everything the fire had taken.

She did not look back. Her grandmother had asked her not to, and she had spent her whole life doing as she was asked.

It was the last time.
