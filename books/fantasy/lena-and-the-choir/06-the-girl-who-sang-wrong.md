## 6. The Girl Who Sang Wrong

The candles always knew before the choirmaster did.

Nell learned to watch them instead of watching him. From her place in the third row of the cathedral choir, wedged between Marta Halloway and a girl whose name she'd never bothered learning because the choirmaster rotated them like furniture, she could see the whole east bank of votive lights. Forty-one candles on a good day, forty-one small flames standing in their red glass cups like patient soldiers. When Nell's voice was behaving, they burned straight and tall. When it wasn't—

The fourth candle from the left leaned.

It was nothing anyone else would notice. A flicker. A breath of wind from a door that hadn't opened. But Nell felt it happen the way you feel a pebble slip inside your shoe: a tiny wrongness, intimate and unmistakable. Her voice had caught on something underneath the note, some thread that ran beneath the hymn like water under ice, and she had tugged it without meaning to.

She swallowed the sound. Closed her mouth around the rest of the phrase and let Marta's voice carry over the gap, and the candle stood back up, ashamed of itself.

Brother Aldous did not notice. Brother Aldous never noticed anything except pitch and posture and whether your collar was straight. He raised both hands, and ninety voices rose with them, and the morning mass at the Cathedral of Saint Verity poured itself upward into stone vaults painted with saints whose gilded eyes had watched six hundred years of children sing.

Nell sang the way she had been taught to survive: beautifully, and slightly less than she could.

That was the trick no one had ever told her aloud but that she had worked out alone, in the dark of the dormitory, counting ceiling cracks. Every voice had edges. Most singers spent their lives pushing against theirs—louder, higher, longer. Nell's edges were not where other voices' edges were. Hers were close. Dangerously close. Inside her own voice there was something else, something that lived in the space between the notes the way mice lived between walls, and if she sang at full truth—if she let the sound out the way it actually wanted to go—the something came with it.

She had done it exactly once on purpose, three years ago, when she was eleven and furious at the choirmaster for striking Marta across the knuckles with his tuning rod. She had sung the Vesper Canticle the way it sounded inside her head, and every candle in the cathedral had gone out at once, all eighty of them, and old Father Ellery had wept into his hands at the altar rail and could not say why, and the dark had felt—for one held breath—full. Attentive. Like the cathedral itself had turned its face toward her.

Brother Aldous had called it a draft. The candles were relit. The incident was filed away in whatever place cathedrals filed impossible things.

But the choirmaster had looked at her afterward. Not with anger. With the expression of a man who has found a coin on the ground and is deciding, slowly, whether to keep it.

He had kept her. That was the thing Nell still could not make peace with, turning it over in her chest like a stone with a sharp side. He had known what she was—or enough of what she was—and he had kept her anyway, moved her to the center of the second row, given her the descants, the solos, the parts that required a voice that could do *more* than a voice should. And the candles had guttered and the widows had wept and the collection plates had grown heavier, year by year, and Nell had sung smaller and smaller inside her own gift like a bird folding its wings in a cage built exactly to their span.

Until tonight. Until the audition.

* * *

The rehearsal room behind the choir stalls smelled of tallow and cold stone and the particular staleness of a space where children have been frightened for generations. There were nine of them left after the winter fevers—nine girls between eight and fourteen, plus Nell, who was fifteen and had been told twice already that this would be her last season, that her voice was a woman's voice now and women's voices did not belong in angel choirs, which was a lie so flimsy that even the youngest girls glanced at each other when it was said. Boys' voices broke and they were sent away too, but the boys went home to families. The choir girls had been given to the cathedral. It was in the ledgers. Nell had seen her own entry once, when she was dusting the scriptorium shelf: *Verity's Foundling Choir, intake 11th of Harvestmonth, one child, female, approx. age 5, no name given, designated "Nell."*

No name given. They had named her the way you name a stray cat. And then they had taught her that her voice belonged to Saint Verity, and Saint Verity belonged to the Crown, and the Crown belonged to God, and somewhere down that long chain of belonging Nell had misplaced herself entirely.

"Osgood." The choirmaster's voice, from the doorway. Not Brother Aldous—Aldous only handled pitch. Master Corvane handled everything else, and he only came to the rehearsal room when something was about to change. "Leave us."

Martas and non-Martas alike filed out, casting Nell looks ranging from sympathy to relief that it wasn't them. Corvane waited until the door clicked shut. He was a tall man gone soft at the middle, with ink-stained fingers and a face like a folded letter—you could see the creases where things had been written on him and smoothed away. He carried a leather folder, and he set it on the music stand, and he did not open it.

"Nell." Her designation, used as gently as he ever used anything. "Sing me the Aurelian verse. As you sang it at the Foundling Feast. Two years past."

Her stomach dropped through the floor stones. "Master, I—"

"You know precisely what I mean. You have been singing at half-voice for two years, and I have permitted it, because half of what you are is worth more than the whole of anyone else I have ever trained. But the Order's assessors come Thursday. They will stand in the nave and they will listen, and they will bring instruments that measure more than pitch." He unfolded his hands. "So I need to know what remains in you. Sing it true. Once. For me."

The candles in the rehearsal room—six of them, in iron sconces—stood very still, as if they too were waiting.

"They'll take me," Nell said. Her voice came out small. "If I show them. The Edict says—"

"The Edict says gifted persons are to be registered in service to the Crown." Corvane's tone didn't change at all, which was how she knew the danger was worst. "You are already in service, child. Registration is a formality. What the Order does with the *unregistered*—" He stopped himself, and the fold in his face deepened. "I am trying to protect my choir. All of it. Do you understand? If they find an unregistered voice of your magnitude loose in the city, they will not ask it politely to sing."

And here was the terrible arithmetic of her life, laid out plain on the music stand next to the folder: sing true for the assessors and become a registered thing, catalogued and owned and fed into whatever the Crown fed voices into these days—because she had heard the whispers too, in the laundry yard, about the cathedral wards and where their power came from, whispers that stopped when the sisters walked past—or stay small, stay half, and hope the instruments measured only pitch.

Hope was not a strategy. She had learned that from watching the fever winter take two girls whose beds were nearest the door.

"I'm afraid," she said, because lying to Corvane had never once worked.

"Yes," he said. "Sing anyway. Fear gives it body."

So Nell closed her eyes, and found the wall inside herself, and—instead of singing against it, as she had for ten years—leaned her forehead against it like a tired child, and asked it to open.

The Aurelian verse came out of her the way floodwater comes out of a breached dam.

She felt it leave her in layers. First the trained voice, the beautiful surface, the thing she had polished since she was five. Then under it the true voice, wider, stranger, resonant in registers her throat shouldn't have owned. And then under *that*—the something. The tenant. It rose through her chest like warmth rising through floorboards and poured into the verse, and the six candles bent sideways as one, flames stretching toward her like flowers toward sun, and the note she was holding split open into harmonies she was not singing, chords hanging in the air above her head, and somewhere in the stone of the walls she heard—a hum. A low answering hum, like the cathedral clearing its throat.

When she finished, the silence afterward had weight. The candles righted themselves slowly, reluctantly, the way sleepers turn back over.

Corvane had gone white. He opened the folder with unsteady hands, and inside it she glimpsed papers bearing a seal she didn't recognize—not the cathedral's, not the Crown's familiar lion-and-sheaf, but a closed eye above crossed keys.

"You are more than I estimated," he said quietly. Almost to himself. "God forgive me, you are considerably more."

"What is that seal?"

He closed the folder. "Thursday," he said. "You will sing the Aurelian verse exactly as you just sang it, and you will let the assessors hear whatever they hear, and you will be registered, and you will be safe, and you will thank me when you understand what I have spared you." He gathered his folder and moved toward the door, and paused there without turning. "The alternative, child, is a cellar under the cathedral you have never seen. Ask yourself why a house of worship needs one."

He left. Nell stood alone in the rehearsal room among the recovering candles, and her whole body shook, and she understood two things with perfect clarity.

The first was that whatever waited below the cathedral was worse than being owned.

The second was that she was not going to be here on Thursday.

* * *

Packing a life takes less time than you think, when your life fits in a satchel.

Nell took nothing that was hers by ledger. The cathedral had provided the habit, the shoes, the hairbrush, the psalter—all of it Verity's property, all of it traceable. Instead she stole, with a thief's trembling competence, three things that weren't: a hooded traveling cloak from the almoner's stores meant for beggars (beggars don't check tags), a heel of bread and hard cheese wrapped in waxcloth from the refectory, and—after a full minute of argument with herself—her own entry page, torn neatly from the intake ledger with a razor borrowed from the barber sister. *One child, female, approx. age 5, no name given.* Proof that she had begun as nobody. She couldn't have said why she needed it. She only knew that a person leaving everything behind ought to carry at least one piece of evidence of what she was leaving.

She went out through the bell tower, because everyone watched the doors and no one watched the bells, and down the outside stair into the alley where the cathedral's shadow ended and the city began.

Caldervane at night was a country she barely knew. Five years since she had walked freely beyond the close—choir girls went to mass, to funerals, to processions, always in file, always supervised—and the streets hit her like weather. Noise first: dogs, cart wheels, a drunk man singing something filthy three lanes over with more conviction than accuracy. Then smell: river mud, coal smoke, frying onions, the sweet rot of the fish market shuttered for night. Then light, everywhere light, lamplight spilling gold across cobbles, and Nell realized she had spent fifteen years living in a building whose windows were stained glass and had somehow never once seen the world in ordinary colors.

She walked. She had no destination; she had a direction, which was *away*, and away pointed south and east along the river road because north was the palace district and west was the sea and both were worse than a road.

By the second mile the terror wore down into something quieter and harder to name. Grief, maybe. Or the strange weightless sickness of a bird the moment after it leaves the ledge and discovers the air does not, in fact, hold it up out of spite.

They will come for you, said the part of her that sounded like Corvane. An unregistered voice of your magnitude. They will hunt you like a deer.

Let them, said the other part, the new part, the one born sometime between the candles bending and the cellar question. Let them chase. I have been caged in the one place in this city where no one would ever think to look for a runaway.

The river road unspooled ahead of her, pale under a half-clouded moon. Fields on either side, hedgerows black against grey, and the great dark shoulder of the hills to the south where the map in the psalter said the land got poor and the roads got honest. She ate a little of the cheese walking. She sang—barely voiced, mostly breath, a wandering fragment of no particular hymn—because silence had started pressing on her eardrums like water, and because for the first time in her life, singing was hers.

The candle-flame feeling came back. Out here, with no glass cups and no choirmaster, she could feel it properly: her voice touching something in the world and the world leaning toward her. The hedgerows seemed to listen. The moonlight thickened. It should have frightened her. It only made her lonelier, in a strange way—to finally feel the shape of the thing inside her, out here where no one existed to be harmed by it or healed by it or hungry for it.

She was mid-phrase when the horse screamed.

There was no other word for it. Ahead, off the road where the hedgerow broke for a cart track, a horse made a sound like tearing metal, and then a man swore with real fear in his voice, and then came a crash of brush and a thud that Nell felt through her boot soles.

She should have hidden. Any sensible fugitive hides. But her feet were already moving, carrying her down the verge toward the cart track, because the something inside her had lurched toward the sound like a dog toward a whistle, and apparently her body answered to it now.

In the moonlight at the bend of the track: a horse backing in circles, reins snapped, saddle empty. A pack spilled across the ruts. And a woman—no, a girl, hardly older than Nell—on the ground with her back against a milestone, one arm cradled wrong against her ribs, and a sword half-drawn beside her and then, seeing Nell, frozen halfway.

For a heartbeat neither of them moved.

The girl had road dust on her to the shoulders and blood at her temple and eyes that went over Nell in one sweep—hood, satchel, city shoes ruined by mud—and Nell watched her arrive at the same conclusion Nell was arriving at: *fugitive*. Two of them. In the middle of nowhere. At midnight.

"You're not with them," the girl said. Not a question. Her voice was rough, low, careful—the voice of someone who had learned to measure every sentence for weapons. "The riders. You're not one of theirs."

"I don't know whose anyone is," Nell said honestly. "I ran from a cathedral."

Something shifted in the girl's face. Not softening, exactly. Recognition—the specific recognition of one locked thing seeing another.

"Your horse threw you?" Nell asked, taking another step closer. "Or—"

"It spooked." The girl's good hand pressed flat against the ground, and Nell saw her wince and disguise it badly. "Out of nowhere. Like something passed through it." Her eyes came up and fixed on Nell with sudden, unnerving directness. "Like something passed through *me*, a second ago, when you were singing. I heard you from up the road. I don't know how I heard you. My sword got warm."

Nell's whole skin prickled. "Your sword got warm."

"Don't." The girl pushed herself up the milestone, teeth gritted. "Don't look at me like I'm mad. I've spent my whole life being told the things I feel aren't happening, and I'm not doing it to anyone else." She paused. Breathed. "August," she said, as if paying out a rope. "My name's August."

The name landed strangely—as though Nell had heard it before, somewhere deeper than memory, in the hum inside stone walls. She dismissed it. Names did that sometimes; the choir taught you that too, how certain syllables rang against the architecture.

"Nell," she said. "Well. No name given, actually. Nell was assigned."

August stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, cracked, disbelieving sound that she immediately seemed embarrassed by, and that pulled a smile up out of Nell before she could stop it.

"No name given," August repeated. "And you tore the page out. Didn't you." Her gaze dropped to the satchel, to the corner of paper showing there. "You took the proof you were nobody, so nobody could use it against you."

Nell's throat went tight. "Is that mad?"

"That's the sanest thing I've heard in weeks." August tried to stand, failed, and sat back down with a hiss. Her wrist, Nell saw now, was swelling fast, and there was a bad bruise blooming along her ribs where the saddle horn had caught her. "I can't ride like this. Can't walk far either. And those riders are real—they're hunting someone, and the someone they're hunting might be me."

The night pressed close. Somewhere far behind them, faint on the wind off the capital, Nell thought she heard a bell tolling—not the cathedral's bronze, something thinner, sharper. An alarm, maybe. Or maybe just the wind. The candles of the world were guttering, all of them at once, everywhere, and she was done pretending not to know why.

"Then we're both running the same direction," Nell said. She crouched, carefully, within reach and not within grab, and held out the waxcloth with the cheese. "South. Toward the hills. I don't know what's there."

"Neither do I." August looked at the offered food, at Nell's face, at the dark road behind them both. Weighing, Nell understood. Deciding whether to keep the coin. "I travel alone," August said—but her hand had already come up and taken the cheese, which undermined the statement considerably.

"Obviously," Nell agreed solemnly, and sat down on the cold grass beside the warm milestone, and began, quietly, to hum.

The horse, grazing nervously at the hedge, drifted closer. August's half-drawn sword slid, very slowly, the last inch out of its sheath on its own, humming faintly against the night like a struck bell—and August looked down at it, and then at Nell, with an expression between wonder and dread.

"Stop that," she said.

"Stop what?" said Nell, who genuinely didn't know—and down the southern road, out of the darkness where the hills began, came the sound of hooves.
