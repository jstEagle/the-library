## 7. An Uneasy Company

The girl slept like the dead.

August had checked three times in the first hour—once out of caution, once out of curiosity, and once because she could not quite believe that anyone could sleep so deeply on bare ground beside a stranger with a sword. But Nell lay curled beneath August's spare cloak with her cheek pressed to her own folded arm, breathing slow and even, and around her the little campfire burned in a way August had never seen a fire burn.

It did not flicker.

That was the thing she kept coming back to. Fires flickered. Everyone knew that. A flame was a nervous creature, always dancing, always leaning toward whatever wind found it. But the flames above these coals stood perfectly still, straight as candlewicks, as if they were listening to something. As if they were holding their breath for the sleeping girl.

August sat across the fire with her unsheathed sword across her knees and watched it happen, and felt the old ache in her palms—the one that lived just under the skin of her hands whenever the gift stirred, which was never, which was not allowed to be ever—and wondered what in all the hells she had let sleep six feet away from her.

She should have left the girl on the road.

She had told herself that at least a dozen times since noon, since the moment Nell had come stumbling out of the hedgerow with her choir robes torn at the hem and her eyes red-rimmed and her voice—gods, her voice—saying *please* in a way that had gone through August like a splinter finding soft wood. August had survived twenty years in Hollowmere by being practical. Practical people did not pick up strays on roads that witch-hunters were already combing. Practical people walked away.

But the girl had sung, back there on the road, without meaning to—a single broken note of grief when August had told her no—and every bird within a quarter mile had gone silent at once, and the wind had stopped, and August's sword had grown warm against her hip like a hand pressing against the inside of a door.

So here they were.

*You're getting soft,* she told herself. *Grandmother would box your ears.*

The thought arrived with its usual weight and then, unexpectedly, with grief behind it, sharp and fresh as a reopened cut. Grandmother was ash now, or close enough to it. The valley was smoke. August turned the thought over carefully, the way you'd turn a hot coal, and set it aside before it could catch.

The fire still didn't flicker.

* * *

Nell woke an hour past dawn, and the first thing she did was apologize.

"I'm sorry," she said, sitting up with her hair full of pine needles, blinking at August like a startled owl. "I'm sorry, I said I'd take first watch and I didn't, I never—" She stopped. She looked at the dead fire, at the neatly stacked kindling August had gathered, at the two fish cleaned and laid on a flat stone, and her face did something complicated. "You did everything."

"You needed the sleep." August handed her a fish without looking up from the whetstone. "Eat. We move in ten minutes."

"Where are we going?"

"Away."

"That's not a direction."

"It's the only one I can afford." August ran the stone along the blade and tried not to listen too closely to the girl's voice, which was difficult, because the girl's voice was the sort of thing you couldn't not listen to. Even speaking plainly, half-asleep, there was something underneath it—a resonance, a second note humming below the first, like a bell struck softly enough that only dogs and swords could hear it. "Where were you headed? Before you fell into my road."

Nell wrapped her arms around her knees. Up close, in daylight, she looked younger than she had sounded—sixteen maybe, seventeen, with a smatter of freckles across her nose and the particular thinness of someone who hadn't eaten properly in weeks. "Nowhere. I just walked. I couldn't stay in Corvale after—" Her jaw tightened. "After what happened at the cathedral."

"The candles."

"You saw?"

"I heard. From everyone between here and the river, apparently. The choirgirl who made the bishop weep in front of the whole congregation." August kept her eyes on the blade. "Stories travel faster than people. Some of them say you're blessed. Most of them say worse."

"What do you say?"

August considered lying, decided it wasn't worth the effort, and said: "I say your fire doesn't flicker."

Nell went very still. "What?"

"Last night. Your fire." August pointed the whetstone at the cold ring of stones. "It burned straight up. No movement. Like it was standing at attention. I've camped alone my whole life and I've never seen anything like it, and I grew up in a valley where nothing magical is supposed to be able to exist at all." She finally looked up. "So either you're doing something, or you're doing something without knowing you're doing it, and both of those mean trouble follows you. Which means trouble follows me now. So I need to know which."

For a long moment Nell just stared at her. Then, quietly: "You really don't know anything about magic, do you?"

"I know how to avoid it."

"That's not the same as knowing about it." Nell unfolded herself and crawled closer to the fire ring, and August noticed—had been noticing since yesterday, had been trying not to notice—that the girl moved like water, like sound itself given a body. When Nell reached out and touched the cold stones, nothing happened. But when she hummed—a single low note, barely more than breath—the ashes stirred. Lifted. Hung in the air in a slow gray spiral, turning in time with the note, and settled again when she stopped.

August's sword went warm against her knee.

Not metaphorically. Not the ordinary warmth of morning sun on steel. It warmed the way it had warmed on the road, the way it had warmed once or twice in her childhood during storms, deep and insistent, like something knocking. She put her palm flat on the flat of the blade and the warmth answered her touch, and for one vertiginous second she felt almost—*almost*—as though the warmth had a shape. A direction. It leaned toward the girl the way a plant leans toward light.

"Stop," August said.

Nell stopped. The ashes fell. "Did I—"

"Don't sing. Don't hum. Don't whistle." August sheathed the sword and stood, needing suddenly to be vertical, to have something solid under her. "Please."

"I'm sorry." Nell's voice cracked on it, and even the crack carried, even the crack had that second note underneath. "I don't—I can't always tell when it's happening. It's like breathing. You don't notice your own breathing until someone tells you to think about it, and then you can't stop thinking about it, and then—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "At the cathedral it was like that too. The choirmaster said if I just practiced enough, if I just learned control, but I practiced for four years and it only got stronger, and last week Father Anselm lit a candle while I was singing and it *bent*, August. The flame bent toward me like it was bowing. And he looked at me like I was something in his food."

August crouched back down, slowly. "Your choirmaster. He trained you?"

"He found me. I was singing in the market when I was small—just children's songs, nothing strange—and he said I had a gift from the Saints and the cathedral would give it a home." Nell laughed, and the laugh was the worst thing yet, bright and hollow. "A home. Yes. A cell in the novices' wing and four hours of practice a day and rules about when I was allowed to speak and when I was only allowed to sing. He said voices like mine were meant for the Saints' ears, not for common air."

"And the wards?" August asked, because Tam's stolen pages had used the word, and because she was beginning to assemble a picture she didn't like. "The cathedral wards. Did he ever say what they were for?"

Something shifted in Nell's face. "How do you know about the wards?"

"Lucky guess. Answer the question."

"They're protection," Nell said slowly. "Everyone says so. The wards keep the city safe. The choirmaster said the choir's singing fed them, that our voices were... woven into them somehow. That's why the choir sang in shifts, day and night, why there was always someone singing in the undercroft. I thought it was devotion." Her hands had gone white where they gripped her knees. "Why are you asking me this? Why are you looking at me like that?"

Because I met a man on the road two days ago, August did not say, a round little disgraced archivist who is currently waiting for us at Greyford with documents proving the king has been wearing a parasite for nineteen years, and the word he used for what feeds such a thing was *voices*. Because the picture I am assembling has you in the middle of it and I hate the frame.

"Because I think your choirmaster lied to you," is what she said instead. "And I think you already know it, or you wouldn't have run."

Nell didn't answer. But she didn't argue either, and after a moment she picked up the fish and ate it in small, careful bites, and that was answer enough.

* * *

They walked.

August set a hard pace—not cruelly hard, but hard enough to discourage conversation, because conversation with Nell turned out to be dangerous in ways August hadn't anticipated. Not because the girl talked too much, though she did, filling silence the way water fills a hollow. Because of what happened to the world around her words.

By midmorning August had catalogued it the way she catalogued everything: patiently, precisely, with growing unease. When Nell spoke of the cathedral, the shadows under the trees lengthened toward her, leaning in. When she laughed at her own joke about a fat deacon, the path ahead seemed briefly, impossibly brighter, the light pooling around her feet like spilled honey. And once—only once, when she went quiet with homesickness and August caught her mouthing the opening line of some hymn—every leaf on every branch within reach of them had trembled, all together, though there was no wind.

And each time, August's sword woke a little more.

That was the part she could not explain and did not dare examine. The warmth came in pulses now, faint but rhythmic, and the rhythm matched Nell's voice the way a heartbeat matches a drum. It was as if the girl's magic and the sword recognized each other across the space between them—as if two strangers in a crowd had caught each other's eye and found something familiar.

*Nothing magical exists in Hollowmere,* Grandmother had said, ten thousand times, in the kitchen, in the dark. *The valley forgets it. That is its mercy and its purpose.* But they weren't in the valley anymore. The valley was burning somewhere behind them, and out here, in the wide green world, August's dormant gift sat inside her like a banked coal, and Nell's stolen magic walked beside her like a bell being carried past a sleeping dog.

At noon they stopped at a stream, and August finally asked the question she'd been circling all morning.

"The magic you have. Is it yours?"

Nell crouched at the water's edge, drinking from cupped hands. "What do you mean?"

"I mean does it come from inside you, or was it put there." August kept her tone level, curious, harmless. "You said the choir sang in shifts. You said your voice was woven into the wards. Wards need power, Nell. Power doesn't come from nowhere. Where did yours come from?"

The girl was quiet for a long time. A leaf drifted down the stream between them.

"There were twelve of us," she said finally, to the water rather than to August. "In the undercroft choir. We rotated. Three hours on, nine off. And when you finished a shift you always felt..." She searched for the word. "*Lighter.* Empty-light, like after crying. And the ones who'd been there longest, the older girls—they got so quiet. Not peaceful-quiet. Hollow-quiet. Sister Maren, she'd been there nineteen years, and near the end she couldn't talk above a whisper, and her eyes..." Nell shook her head. "I asked the choirmaster once where the voices went. The old voices, when a sister died or was sent away. He said they returned to the Saints." She looked up, and her eyes were dry in the terrible way of someone who has cried past the capacity for tears. "I don't think they went to the Saints, August. I think when I sing, sometimes, there are other voices in mine. I can feel them. Little ones. They push."

The stream made its small cold sounds. Somewhere overhead a hawk circled, riding a thermal.

"Twelve girls," August said quietly. "Singing day and night. For years."

"For generations, probably. I wasn't the first odd one. There was a girl before me they called the First Voice, and one before her." Nell hugged her arms around herself. "Is that why you asked? Do you know what they were for? What the singing was *for*?"

August weighed it. Every instinct said hold it close, say nothing, trust no one—you'd known this girl for eighteen hours, and the Edict had made the whole world a place where knowing someone could kill you. But the girl had just handed her something true and ugly, unprompted, with shaking hands. Debts were debts, even new ones. Especially new ones.

"I know someone," August said. "An archivist. He ran from the capital with papers he wasn't supposed to have. Papers about the king." She watched Nell's face as she said it, watching for fear, for the flinch of a loyal subject. She saw neither. She saw hunger. "He thinks the crown isn't just a crown. He thinks something else wears it. Something old. And he thinks things like cathedral wards and purity hunts and royal edicts are all... parts of it. Feeding it. Guarding it."

"The Edict." Nell's whisper made the stream-sounds bend, very slightly, toward her. "That's why the hunters came through my village asking questions. That's why they took Brother Aldous the cooper—he could always find lost things, he'd just touch a hammer and know where the nails were. They took him in a cart with a hood over his head and nobody said a word, and two days later the crier read the Edict and everybody pretended it made sense." Her voice was rising now, and the world rose with it—the water lifting fine mist off the current, the leaves shivering, August's sword pulsing warm against her spine like a second heart. "They took him and he'd never hurt anyone, he fixed my mother's cart wheel for free, and they *took him*—"

"Nell."

"—and everyone just *watched*—"

"*Nell.*"

The name landed like a hand on a struck bell. Everything stilled. The mist sank. The leaves hung motionless, and Nell stood in the center of it breathing hard, and August stood outside the circle of it with her hand on her sword hilt, feeling the blade lean toward the girl like a compass needle finding north.

Neither of them moved for a long moment.

"What is that," Nell said at last, staring at August's hip. "What is—your sword just—"

"I know."

"It *moved*. I saw it move, it tilted toward me, swords don't—"

"I know." August took her hand off the hilt, deliberately, and the warmth faded to an ember, and the world exhaled. She crouched at the stream and drank, buying time, tasting copper and cold stone. When she spoke again her voice was low and even. "Here's what's going to happen. We're going to walk to Greyford, which is half a day north. There's a man there I'm supposed to meet, and you're going to meet him too, because I think he knows what was done to you and I think he might know how to undo it. In exchange, you're going to keep your voice quiet on the road. No singing, no humming, no praying out loud. Can you do that?"

Nell wiped her face with her sleeve. "And after Greyford?"

"After Greyford, I don't know. I'm not promising you anything. I'm not your protector and I'm not your friend. I'm a girl with a sword and a price on her head, walking the same direction you're walking, for now." The words came out harder than she intended, and she saw them land, saw Nell flinch and fold inward like paper in rain, and hated herself a little for the accuracy of the throw. "I'm sorry. I just—if you're expecting someone to save you, I'll disappoint you faster than the cathedral did."

"I'm not expecting anyone to save me." Nell's chin came up. Under the tear-streaks and the pine needles and the terror, something stubborn had surfaced, something with a spine in it. "I saved myself out of that cathedral. You didn't find me; I found *you*. I heard you were heading north from the miller's boy and I followed the road until I caught you, because you walk like someone who knows where she's going, and I have nowhere to go and no one at all, and I figured even a stranger who yelled at me was better than the Silent Order finding me first." She swallowed. "So yell. I don't care. Just don't leave me behind."

August looked at her for a long moment—the ruined choir robes, the freckles, the furious wet eyes, the second note humming underneath everything she said like a soul slightly out of tune with its body—and felt the practical thing in her chest lose another argument it had been losing since yesterday.

"Greyford," August said. "Half a day. Keep your voice down."

"Yes." And then, softer, with that resonance underneath it that made the word feel like a vow: "Thank you, August."

"Don't thank me yet."

* * *

They made Greyford's outskirts by late afternoon, and that was where August saw the banners.

They hung from the tollhouse at the bridge—white and gold, the crowned sun of the royal hunt—and beneath them, checking carts with unhurried efficiency, stood a dozen soldiers in the grey-and-crimson of the witch-hunters. A sweep. A proper one, organized, methodical, the kind that closed both ends of a town and worked inward.

And at the head of it, directing the search with a map in her gloved hands, stood a tall woman with iron-gray hair and a captain's insignia, whose posture August recognized with a sick lurch she couldn't immediately account for—until she could, until she remembered the description Tam had given them on the road two nights ago, laughing nervously into his wine: *Vane, Sera Vane, the hunt-captain, mind her, she's the competent one, she's the one who reads maps.*

The captain looked up from her map and scanned the road, the hedgerows, the slow line of refugees and carters waiting at the bridge.

Her gaze passed over August.

Stopped.

Came back.

Beside August, oblivious, Nell whispered, "Which one is your archivist?"—and the whisper, soft as it was, carried across forty feet of open air like a shout, and Captain Sera Vane's hand dropped to her sword.
