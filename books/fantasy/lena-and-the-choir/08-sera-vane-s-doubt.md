## 8. Sera Vane's Doubt

The ash came down like snow that had forgotten how to be gentle.

Sera Vane stood in the ruin of Hollowmere's mill square and let it settle on her shoulders, on the grey wool of her commission cloak, on the silver badge of the Crown's Hunt that she had worn for eleven years without once asking herself what it weighed. Ash did not weigh anything. That was the cruelty of it. A town could burn down to nothing and still find enough of itself left to fall on you, grain by grain, all day long.

Three days since the fire. The survivors had been gathered into the tithe-barn at the edge of the village, forty-one souls out of perhaps two hundred, and they sat now on benches meant for grain sacks, waiting to be questioned in the order their names appeared on the census rolls Sera's own clerks had compiled two winters past. She knew these names. She had walked this valley four years ago on a routine survey of unregistered households and written *no anomalies* in her report with a steady hand, because Hollowmere had been quiet, and orderly, and poor in the way that made it invisible, and because she had wanted—she could not have said what she had wanted—to leave it alone.

Now it was a black scar in a green valley, and her boots crunched on char when she moved, and somewhere under the ash were the bones of an old woman who had offered her tea and called her *child* and asked nothing about the sword at her hip except whether it was well-oiled.

"Captain." Corporal Drenn hovered at her elbow, young through the nose, holding the ledger too tightly. "The miller's widow is ready. And there's—" he hesitated, lowered his voice, "—the man from the Order is watching again."

Sera did not turn to look. She didn't need to. Brother Quillon of the Silent Order had been standing beneath the eave of the tithe-barn since dawn, hood up against the ash-fall, hands folded into his sleeves, motionless as a post driven into the earth. He had not spoken a word in three days. He did not need to speak. His presence said everything: *We are watching how you question them. We are watching what you conclude.*

"Let him watch," Sera said. "Bring me the widow."

* * *

Maren Ostler was sixty if she was a day, with flour worked so deep into the creases of her knuckles that no amount of washing would ever get it out, and she sat on the bench with her burned shawl clutched around her shoulders and looked at Sera the way one looks at weather—one has endured worse, one will endure this.

"You knew the Ishikawa household," Sera began, because procedure was a rope and she needed something to hold. "How long?"

"All my life, and my mother's before me." The widow's voice was flat as threshing-floor stone. "Old Wren Ishikawa ground our grain. Her granddaughter August ran deliveries. Good girl. Quiet. Polite to old women."

"When did you last see either of them?"

"The night the soldiers came." Maren Ostler's eyes came up then, and they were not flat at all; they held a banked heat, coals under ash, and Sera felt them land on her like a physical weight. "I saw the old woman standing in her doorway with a lamp, telling your men where the road washed out, being helpful, being *civil*, while they put torches to her own—"

"We didn't fire the town." It came out of Sera faster than she intended, sharper, and she heard it crack in the air between them. "The order was seizure and interrogation. No fires. Who gave the order to burn?"

For a moment the widow only looked at her, and something passed over her face—not softening, exactly, but a recalculation, the way a merchant recalculates when a coin rings wrong against the counter.

"You don't know," the widow said slowly. "You genuinely don't know."

"I am asking."

"They came after midnight. Not your regulars—the ones in grey, the ones who don't talk. They went first to the Ishikawa house, not the edges, not the roads. They knew which house. They'd brought oil in cartloads, Captain. You don't cart oil to seize a family." She pulled the shawl tighter. "And the old woman—Wren—I saw her face in the lamplight when she understood what they'd come to do. She wasn't surprised. That's the thing I can't stop seeing. She looked at those grey men like she'd been expecting them for fifty years, and she turned around and went inside and shut her door, and that was the last anyone saw of her."

Sera wrote none of this down. Her hand wouldn't move. Procedure was a rope, but ropes burn too.

"Thank you," she said, uselessly. "Send in the cooper."

As the widow rose, she paused, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, pitched under the hearing of the guards at the door. "You've got a good face, Captain. My mother always said the Crown sends its prettiest faces to do its ugliest work. Don't let them make yours ugly too."

Then she was gone, and the next name was called, and the ash kept falling.

* * *

By late afternoon Sera had interviewed nineteen survivors and heard nineteen variations of the same account, and the variations were what troubled her, because honest witnesses vary and rehearsed ones repeat. These people varied. The fire had come from the grey-cloaked men, not hers. It had started at the Ishikawa house and spread unnaturally fast—unnaturally, several of them said, though they used the word carefully, glancing at the guards, because *unnaturally* was a confession in itself now. One boy, fourteen, kept saying the flames had gone *uphill against the wind*, and every time he said it his mother squeezed his knee until he stopped.

And the Ishikawas themselves had simply vanished. No bodies. Old woman, granddaughter, both gone into a fire hot enough to vitrify the well-stones, and not so much as a tooth left behind. Sera had walked the ruin of the house herself that morning, alone, before the others woke. The cellar door had been open. The cellar floor had been swept clean of debris in one narrow lane, as though something heavy had been dragged—or someone light had run—toward the back wall, where the stones showed fresh mortar in a seam no wider than a hand. Behind that wall, the hillside. Behind the hillside, according to the survey maps, old mine workings, unmapped since the valley's founding.

Someone had known about a tunnel that was not on any map Sera possessed.

She stood in the barn's doorway at dusk and took out the thing she had been avoiding taking out all day: the map. The Ishikawa map. The provenance document that had sent eleven years of her life pivoting toward this valley like a compass needle finding north.

It lay in its oilcloth sleeve, and she unwrapped it with hands that were steadier than she wanted them to be.

She had studied it a hundred times since the Order placed it in her custody six weeks ago. A survey map of Hollowmere and the surrounding vales, drawn in a fine brown ink on good rag paper, annotated in a cramped, precise hand: household boundaries, water sources, the locations of the Ishikawa holdings across four generations, marked with small careful crosses. The court archivists had authenticated the paper, the ink, the hand. The hand belonged to an Ishikawa. An oath-bound family, drawing a map of itself, in secret, and that map surfacing in the royal archive—this was the evidence that had justified the whole operation. *They marked themselves,* the Inquisitor-General had said, sliding the map across the table in a room that smelled of cold iron. *Pride. Arrogance. They thought their valley hid them. Here is their signature, Captain. Bring me the last of the line.*

Sera had believed it. She wanted, even now, to go on believing it, because the alternative was a door opening onto a dark she did not have lanterns enough for.

But she had spent three days among people who had known the Ishikawas all their lives, and not one of them—and she had asked, obliquely, carefully, nine times—not one of them had ever seen an Ishikawa draw so much as a letter. Old Wren had been illiterate. The neighbors said so plainly, without shame, the way one says a thing everyone knows: *she couldn't read her own name, dear, we did her letters for her.* And the granddaughter? Clever with figures, they said, clever with her hands, but schooled at home, taught letters late and grudgingly, and her writing—

Sera had found samples. That was the worst of it. In the ruined schoolhouse, half-burned, she had found the delivery ledgers August Ishikawa had kept for the mill, and the handwriting was round and slanted and hurried, a working hand, a young hand, nothing—*nothing*—like the cramped precise annotation on the map.

Two different hands. Two different people. The map was signed, in effect, by a ghost.

And yet the paper was old. The archivists had sworn to it. Which meant the map had not been forged recently—which meant it had been made long ago, by someone else entirely, someone who knew the valley intimately and wrote like a court clerk, and had then been salted into the archive like meat, waiting for the day someone hungry enough came looking.

Someone at court had wanted the Ishikawas found.

The thought arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts do, fully formed and wearing its own terrible logic, and Sera stood in the doorway with the map trembling very slightly in her hands and watched the ash fall on the graves her operation had dug.

Behind her, inside the barn, Brother Quillon shifted his weight for the first time all day. It was a small sound. Boot leather on floorboards. But in the silence it rang like a bell, and she understood that he had been waiting for her to reach this exact place, standing at this exact door, and that he had been listening for the sound of her understanding.

She wrapped the map away. She did not look at him. She walked back into the lamplight and called the next name off the ledger, because that was what a loyal officer of the Crown did, and because the alternative—turning around, meeting the hooded shape beneath the eave, saying aloud the thing growing in her chest—was a fire of its own, and she was not yet sure she wished to burn.

But when the last survivor had been dismissed and the lamps guttered low, she found the boy, the fourteen-year-old who had spoken of flames running uphill, and crouched to his level, and asked him quietly, off the record, off every record:

"The night they came. Before the fire. Did anyone see which way the girl went?"

The boy looked at his mother. His mother looked at Sera for a long, long moment—a look that was a scale balancing, a ledger closing, a judgment rendered—and then she nodded once, almost imperceptibly, toward the north ridge.

"The mines," she said. "There are old ways through the hills. If she lives, Captain, she'll be making for the river towns by now. Greyford crossing, most like. Anyone running goes to Greyford."

Sera straightened. Somewhere behind her, she heard the Silent Order's man begin, softly, to move.

Greyford. She had a sweep scheduled there in five days' time. She had orders to lead it personally.

She had, she realized, walking back through the falling ash toward her tent with the boy's words folded into her chest like contraband, absolutely no intention of following those orders—and no idea at all what she intended to do instead.
