## 10. Crossing at Greyford

The river came on them the way weather does—first a smell, green and mineral and old, then a sound like the valley had made when it rained, and then all at once it was there: wide, brown-gold, unhurried, sliding past the town of Greyford as if it had somewhere better to be and no intention of hurrying for anyone.

August stopped at the crest of the road and looked at it for a long time.

She had never seen so much water in one place. Hollowmere had its streams, its cold quick creeks that ran over slate and vanished into the pines, but this was another order of thing entirely—a road made of moving earth, broad enough to carry barges, patient enough to carry secrets. Gulls wheeled over the far bank, screaming their small opinions at the sky.

"Big," Nell said, from beside her. She said it the way she said most things lately, half to herself, her voice pitched low out of habit. Even whispering, she was careful with it. August had learned to listen for the way Nell held her words back, like someone carrying water in cupped hands across a long field.

"Big," August agreed.

"You keep looking at it like it might be a trap."

"Everything might be a trap now."

Nell considered this with the grave seriousness she brought to everything, which August was beginning to suspect was not naivety but a kind of armor. "Then we should eat first," Nell said. "Traps are worse hungry."

It was good logic. August had learned, over nine days of road, that most of Nell's logic was good, delivered in that soft cathedral-trained voice, and that arguing with it was a losing proposition. So they went down into Greyford together, two girls in road-dusted cloaks with one sword wrapped in sacking on August's back and a stolen loaf of yesterday's bread between them, and the town took them in the way river towns take everyone: without much caring who they were.

That was the mercy of Greyford, August thought. It smelled of fish guts and tar and wet rope, of frying onions and horse and the particular sourness of a brewery working overtime. The streets were mud and cobbles fighting a losing war. Nobody looked at anybody twice. In Hollowmere, everyone had known everyone since before memory; here, you could be a stranger, and strangers were just Tuesday.

They ate fried fish off a board at a stall near the quay, standing up, grease running down their wrists, and for a few minutes August let herself pretend they were only what they appeared: two girls traveling to find work, nothing more, nobody's edict or quarry or key.

Then the man fell off the barge.

He didn't fall so much as pour himself down the gangplank, a thin, ink-stained figure in a coat too fine for the docks and too ruined for anywhere else, clutching a leather tube to his chest the way a drowning man clutches driftwood. He came ashore sideways, caught his heel, went down hard on the cobbles, and lay there breathing in short, panicked pulls while dockworkers stepped around him with the practiced indifference of men who had seen every kind of arrival.

Nell was moving before August could stop her. That was the other thing about Nell: she ran toward fallen people the way other people ran from fires.

"Sir? Sir, are you hurt?"

The man's eyes opened. They were red-rimmed, sleepless, and they went from Nell's face to August's—to the wrapped shape on August's back—and something in them changed. August saw it happen, the way a lock sees a key: recognition, calculation, and then a terrible, exhausted hope.

"Ishikawa," he whispered.

August's hand found the hilt through the sacking. "You have me mistaken."

"No." He laughed, and it cracked halfway through and became something else, something closer to weeping. "No, I don't. I've spent eleven years in the royal archive, girl, and six months learning your family's name by heart. Ishikawa. Oath-bound line, last of seven, sealed in the valley where the ley-threads run thin." He pushed himself up on one elbow, wincing. "My name is Tam Osgood. I leaked the Edict's hidden clauses to three newspapers, got stripped of my post, and have been running south for nine days with the only proof in the kingdom of what your grandmother died protecting. And I would very much like to not say any of this louder than I already have."

The quay went on around them. Bells rang somewhere. A gull screamed. August stood very still and felt the shape of her life rearranging itself around this moment, the way a river rearranges itself around a dropped stone.

*Your grandmother died protecting.*

She hadn't let herself think those words yet. Not properly. Nine days of road and she had kept them in a room in her chest with the door shut, and now this ruined stranger had walked straight in without knocking.

"Nell," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away. "Help him up."

* * *

They took him to a room above a chandlery, paid for with the last of the coin August had sewn into her cloak hem, because Tam Osgood had been robbed twice on the road and had arrived in Greyford with exactly four coppers and a leather tube he would not let out of arm's reach even to sleep.

He slept first. That was the strange part. He talked for an hour—fast, tumbling, an archivist's flood of names and dates and clause numbers—and then, mid-sentence, sitting upright on the room's single chair, he simply stopped, swayed, and was asleep. Nell eased him onto the bed with the practiced gentleness of someone who had younger siblings. His hand stayed curled around the tube even unconscious.

August sat by the window with the sword across her knees and watched the river turn gold, then copper, then dark.

"He knew Grandmother's name," Nell said quietly. She was braiding her hair back from her face, getting ready for whatever watch she'd claim. "The way you say a prayer you've memorized."

"Yes."

"That means people outside the valley always knew. About the oath. About the line."

"It means someone wrote it down," August said. "And someone read it. And someone decided it mattered enough to hunt us." She turned the thought over and found its underside. "The map, Nell. Captain Vane had a map only an Ishikawa could have drawn. And this man says he's spent six months learning our name. I want to know if those are the same fact."

When Tam woke, near midnight, they fed him bread and the dregs of a cheese wheel Nell had charmed off the chandler's wife—not with magic, just with Nell being Nell, which was its own quiet power—and August put the question to him plainly.

Tam set down his bread. For the first time since the gangplank, he looked genuinely afraid, and not of her.

"The map," he said. "Yes. I know about the map."

"Tell me."

"I drew it." The words came out flat, rehearsed, the way a confession comes when it has been said to oneself many times in the dark. "Four years ago. Under orders. Under—" his jaw worked "—under persuasion I won't describe while the young lady's eating. They brought me a survey of Hollowmere's boundaries and told me to render it as a hunter's chart. Water sources, cellar systems, sight lines. I asked whose authority. I was shown a seal I had no rank to question." He looked at his hands, ink-stained to the second knuckle. "I told myself it was routine cartography. Then the Edict came, and I pulled the file, and I found my own map attached to a warrant for the extermination of an entire bloodline, and I understood what I had drawn."

The room was very quiet. Downstairs, the chandler's dog shifted in its sleep, chains clinking.

"So it was you," August said. "You brought them to my door."

"I did." He did not flinch from it, and August hated him a little for the honesty, and respected him more for the same reason. "I can't unburn your valley, August Ishikawa. But I can tell you why they wanted it burned, and who gave the order, and what the Edict actually is—because it is not what the criers read in the squares. The purity hunt is a search. They're not purging the gifted. They're *sorting* them. Every gifted person taken is questioned about oaths, bindings, seals. There's a list of questions, standard across all interrogations, and every one of them points at one thing." He reached for the leather tube. "May I?"

August nodded.

He worked the cap off with fingers that shook only a little and drew out a scroll—not an official document, but a copy, dense with his own cramped annotations in three colors of ink. He spread it on the floor between them, weighting the corners with the cheese wheel and two boots, and by candlelight August read the hidden architecture of her family's ruin.

"The king has been ill for nineteen years," Tam said softly. "That's the official word. Ill, reclusive, prone to long silences. What the archive shows is different. Nineteen years ago the king's correspondence changes handwriting mid-letter and never changes back. His privy council minutes begin omitting whole sessions. And there is a binding—an old one, pre-dynastic, recorded in fragments across forty documents that no one ever assembled until I did. Something wears the crown, August. Has worn it for nineteen years. And the binding that holds it has seven locks, and six are broken, and the seventh—" he tapped the scroll, tapped the genealogy he'd annotated in red "—is an oath. Sworn by a swordspeaker's line. Your line. The Edict isn't hunting the gifted. It's hunting the *key*, and burning everything else as cover."

August looked at the scroll and felt the valley behind her, the pines, the cold creek, her grandmother's hands steady on the whetstone. *We do not name the blade. We do not leave the valley. We do not ask what we guard.*

"We're not guarding the gift," she said slowly. "We're guarding the door."

"You're the last hinge on the last door," Tam agreed. "And the thing behind it has had nineteen years and an army to look for you."

Nell had gone pale. "Then why hasn't it found her already?"

"Because the valley forgets," August said. It was the oldest saying in Hollowmere, the one children learned before their letters. "Magic cannot follow there. Nothing can follow there. Not even—" she stopped.

"Not even it," Tam finished. "Which is why the map mattered. Which is why the Order needed an Ishikawa-drawn chart to breach a place that erases itself from every other record." He began rolling the scroll with careful hands. "And which is why, August, you cannot stay in any place where magic runs strong for longer than a few days. You are a lit candle in a dark house, and the house belongs to it."

Outside, far off across the river, a bell began to ring. Not the harbor bell with its lazy two-note rhythm. Something faster. Sharper.

Tam's head came up like a deer's. "Sweep bell," he whispered. "They ring it when the Order moves through a town after curfew. Street by street. Door by door." He was already on his feet, stuffing the scroll into his coat. "How do we get out of Greyford?"

"The ferry stops at dusk," August said. Her mind was moving fast and cold, the way it had moved in the cellar tunnels under her burning home. "But there's a ford. The chandler's boy mentioned it—two miles downstream, where the river goes shallow over gravel. Carts use it in low water."

"It's autumn," Nell said. "The water's low."

"It's also freezing," August said. "Get your boots laced."

* * *

They went out the back of the chandlery into an alley that stank of pitch, and the night received them like a mouth.

Greyford after curfew was a different town. Shutters closed. Lamps dead. Their breath hanging in clouds. Somewhere ahead, torchlight moved in a slow procession along the main street, and with it came voices—not shouting, but worse: calm, courteous, methodical. *Open in the crown's name. Open in the crown's name.* Doors opening. Dogs whimpering. Once, a woman's voice rising in protest and then cutting off abruptly, and August's stomach clenched and she made herself keep walking, because stopping was dying and she had promised herself, on the ridge above the ashes, that she would not stop again.

They cut along the backs of warehouses, Nell's hand light on August's shoulder, Tam wheezing but keeping pace with the grim discipline of a man who had been running for nine days and intended to keep running. The river smell grew stronger. Gravel crunched underfoot. Ahead, the town thinned to fences and net-drying racks and then to open dark, and August allowed herself one breath of relief—

Torchlight bloomed at the alley's mouth behind them.

"Halt! In the crown's name!"

They ran. Gravel flew. Behind them boots, many boots, and the flat disciplined calls of hunters who had done this a hundred times: *left side, cut them off, the ford, they'll make for the ford.* Of course they'd make for the ford. Everyone made for the ford. August's lungs burned. The wrapped sword bounced against her spine with every stride, and beneath her fear, beneath even her grief, she felt the faintest hum through the sacking, like a struck string still ringing, like something waking—

The riverbank opened before them, black water sliding silver under a half moon, and the ford was there, a pale ribbon of shallows maybe thirty yards across, and they were twenty feet from it when the riders came out of the trees on the far bank.

Five of them. Torches. And at their center, on a grey mare, straight-backed and still, a woman in a hunt-captain's coat with her helm off and her hair tied back and a face August knew better than her own heartbeat, because she had seen it across her grandmother's kitchen table while the valley burned.

Captain Sera Vane raised one hand, and the riders behind her halted.

August stopped too, chest heaving, one arm thrown out to push Nell behind her. The water sang over the gravel. Ten feet away, Tam stood bent double, gasping, the leather tube hugged to his ribs.

"Surrender the fugitives," said one of Sera's riders, a young man with an eager voice, "and the archivist may yet see trial instead of—"

"I said hold," Sera Vane said, and her voice carried the way a whip cracks, and the rider shut his mouth.

For a long moment she only looked at August. Across the water, across the torchlight, across everything that had happened—the fire, the tunnels, the ridge, the ashes—her face was unreadable as stone and yet August, who had grown up reading faces in a valley where no one lied well, saw something underneath the stone. Something troubled. Something that looked, impossibly, like a woman standing at a crossroads and knowing both roads were wrong.

"You're wounded," Sera said at last. Her voice was quieter. Almost private. "Your left side. From the bridge at Marlow. You've been favoring it since the alley."

August hadn't known she was favoring it. "You've been following us."

"I've been *leading* the sweeps," Sera said. "There's a difference. There has been a difference for some days now, and I find I can't explain it, and I'm tired of trying." She turned her head slightly toward her riders without taking her eyes off August. "The tracks go east along the bank. Toward the marsh road. Two sets, maybe three. Get after them."

"But the ford—"

"*Now.*"

Boots thundered away eastward, torches dwindling, and then it was only the river and the moon and the five of them—or rather the three of them and the two of them—staring across thirty yards of freezing water at each other like figures in a story neither had agreed to be in.

"Why?" August called. The word tore coming out. "Why did you burn my home, and why are you letting us go? Both. Tell me both."

Sera Vane's jaw tightened. For a moment August thought she wouldn't answer at all. Then:

"I followed a map to your door because the crown ordered it, and I have obeyed the crown since I was sixteen years old, and it has never once asked me whether the order was *right*." Her voice roughened. "Your grandmother offered me tea, Ishikawa. While her town burned in her own hearth-fire, she poured me tea and told me I would live to regret this night, and she was polite about it, and I have not slept properly since. As for tonight—" she shook her head slowly, and the torchlight moved on her face like water "—I don't know why. I'll know eventually. When I do, I suspect I'll be on the wrong side of this river to do anything about it. Go. The water's waist-deep at the middle. Keep the child dry if you can."

"She's not a child," August said.

"No," Sera agreed, and something flickered across her face—grief, or its cousin. "None of you are anymore. That's rather the whole crime, isn't it."

She wheeled the grey mare and rode north along the bank, alone, her torch streaming behind her like a comet, and August watched her go until the dark took her.

Then she took Nell's hand, and Tam's sleeve, and waded into the river.

The cold was total, immediate, a living thing that closed around her legs and squeezed the breath from her. Gravel rolled under her boots. Halfway across the current grabbed her hips and leaned, and Nell made a small fierce sound and gripped her arm, and Tam swore in three languages, and they came out the far side dripping and shaking and laughing—actually laughing, Nell first, a bright helpless sound she immediately smothered, then Tam, wheezing, then August herself, a rough bark of a laugh she didn't recognize as her own.

They collapsed on the southern bank among the reeds, steaming in the moonlight, wringing out their cloaks.

"That," Nell said finally, teeth chattering, "was the worst ferry I have ever taken."

"There will be others," August said. "Worse ones. Better ones. We seem to be collecting people, so presumably we'll collect boats eventually."

Tam had gone quiet. He sat with the leather tube across his knees, staring back north across the river, at the distant torches of Greyford, at the country they had just escaped and the captain who had, for reasons she couldn't name, let them.

"She'll come after us," Tam said. "Not with warrants. On her own. Mark me—a woman like that doesn't break her obedience by halves. When it breaks, it breaks all the way, and then she'll ride north looking for the people she failed." He glanced at August. "The question is whether we'll be there when she arrives."

August looked at the sword lying in the reeds, the sacking dark with riverwater, and felt again that faint hum against her spine, stronger now than it had ever been in the valley, as though crossing the water had loosened something—as though the blade, too, had noticed the world beyond Hollowmere and found it interesting.

"Then we go north," she said. "North is where the king is. North is where the answers are. And apparently—" she stood, and offered Nell her hand, and then Tam's, hauling them both up out of the reeds, three soaked strangers on a dark riverbank who were not strangers anymore, not quite, not after tonight "—apparently north is where everyone ends up."

Behind them, far off, the sweep bell in Greyford fell silent, and in the silence that followed, from somewhere deep in the wrapped steel on her back, August heard—clearly, unmistakably, for the first time in her life—her sword sigh.
