## 12. First Song of the Gift

The road north smelled like rain that hadn't fallen yet.

Nell had learned the road's moods in the six days since Greyford: when it was kind, it gave them hedgerows and blackberries and long stretches where August walked ahead humming nothing at all; when it was cruel, it gave her blisters and Tam's endless commentary about the erosion patterns of imperial byways. Today it was holding its breath. The clouds sat low and gray as unwashed wool, and the wind came in gusts that carried the smell of wet iron from somewhere far off.

"You're doing it again," August said, without turning around.

"Doing what?"

"Counting my steps."

Nell flushed. She hadn't realized she'd been doing it aloud — a soft *one-two, one-two* under her breath, the way she used to count rests in the choir stalls when the choirmaster's baton hovered and every girl in the second row held her lungs full and still. "It helps me think."

"Think about what?"

*About how someone is following us,* she didn't say. She'd felt it since morning: a wrongness in the rhythm behind them, a set of footsteps that stopped when theirs stopped, started when theirs started, always half a beat late, like an echo that had learned to walk. Nell had spent nine years in a cathedral learning to hear things other people smoothed over — the way a note could be true and still be a lie, the way silence in a vaulted room was never really silent but full of listening stone. She knew what being listened to felt like.

"About whether we should stop for lunch," she said instead.

August glanced back at her, and for a moment Nell saw the whole wary architecture of the girl's face — the suspicion first, then the arithmetic of trust, then something softer underneath that she kept trying to demolish before anyone noticed it. "There's a farmstead past the bend," August said. "Tam wants to ask about the road conditions north."

"Tam wants to ask about everything," Nell said. "He asked a miller's wife three questions about drainage ditches yesterday. I thought she was going to hit him with her ladle."

"He survived the capital. He can survive a ladle." But August almost smiled when she said it, and Nell counted that as a victory, filed it away in the small treasury of moments she'd been building since the day they'd collided on the road outside the city — literally collided, Nell running with her choir robe still on and her whole life in a satchel, straight into a tall girl with a sword wrapped in cloth who had caught her by both arms and said, in a voice flat as slate, *"You're crying wrong. Who taught you to cry like that?"*

Nobody had ever heard Nell before. That was the truth of her life, and she was only beginning to understand it. In the cathedral she had been an instrument — polished, tuned, played by other hands. On the road she was becoming a person, and persons were allowed to cry however they liked.

They rounded the bend. The farmstead wasn't there.

Where it should have been — Tam had described it twice, red roof, well with a crooked crane, a dog that would bark itself hoarse — there was only a burned foundation, black beams jutting up like ribs, and the well standing alone in a yard gone to weed. The dog was nowhere. The silence where the dog should have been was very loud.

Nell felt the hair rise on her arms before she understood why. Her voice did it first. It always did it first — some deep place under her sternum, the place where the stolen voices lived coiled like eels in a barrel, went cold and began to press upward, wanting out, wanting to *warn* her.

"August," she whispered.

"I know." August had stopped walking. Her hand rested on the wrapped hilt at her hip — not drawing, just touching, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal to steady yourself. "Behind us. Two, maybe three. They've been following since the willow stand."

"How long have you known?"

"Since the willow stand."

"And you didn't *say* anything?"

"I was hoping I was wrong." August turned fully now, and Nell watched her do the thing she did — the terrible calm settling over her like frost forming on a window, breath by breath. "Get behind me. Stay low. If they take me, run back down the road and don't stop until—"

"If they take you, I'm not running anywhere," Nell said, and was surprised by how steady her own voice came out. One of the voices inside her — an old man's, gravelly, one of the first the choirmaster had poured into her — rumbled agreement in her chest like distant thunder.

August looked at her for a long moment. "You're braver than you look."

"I look brave."

"You look like a choirgirl who got lost on the way to evensong."

"That *is* brave," Nell said. "You've never met our choirmaster."

And then the echo-footsteps stopped pretending.

They came out of the ditch-line to the east — three men, moving fast, no longer caring about stealth because stealth had done its work. Bounty hunters, or something worse dressed as bounty hunters: leather jerkins, iron-banded cudgels, and the particular hungry confidence of men who had been paid to bring someone back breathing but hadn't been paid enough to be gentle about it. The leader had a crossbow, already cranked, already leveled.

"Nell Ashgrave," he called out, and her name in his mouth sounded like a hand closing around her throat. "Warrant of the Crown Purity Office. Come quiet and nobody's voice gets cut out today."

*Voice.* They knew. Of course they knew — the Edict had lists, and lists had names, and hers had been on them since the night she fled the cathedral with the choirmaster's screams still ringing in her ears. She had told herself the city was far behind them. She had told herself a lot of things.

"No," Nell said.

The word came out small. She hated how small it was. She was a girl whose voice could gutter candles and crack grown men open like eggs — and here, now, with iron pointed at her, all that power curled uselessly inside her because she had never once been taught to use it for herself. Every lesson she'd ever had had been about obedience. About singing *for*. About giving the sound away.

The crossbowman's finger tightened.

Everything after that happened in pieces, the way lightning happens — a single white instant stretched into a hundred separate eternities.

Nell opened her mouth and sang.

Not a song. Not anything with words or shape or mercy. What came out of her was panic given pitch — every stolen voice in her chest rising at once, the old man and the drowned girl and the twins and the dozens upon dozens of children the cathedral had fed into her year after year, all of them screaming through her throat in a chord that had no business existing in a human body. The sound hit the air like a wall of glass shattering outward.

The crossbow bolt never reached her. It *wavered*, mid-flight, its spin stuttering, and dropped into the mud ten feet short as if it had forgotten what it was for.

But that wasn't the terrifying part.

The terrifying part was the sword.

Nell felt it happen more than saw it — felt it in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the water of the well behind them trembling into rings though no rain had fallen. August's blade came out of its wrappings with a sound Nell had no word for, a sound like a name being pronounced after centuries of silence, and the steel was *singing back.*

It answered her.

Every note she threw, the sword caught and returned, harmonizing, amplifying, weaving her chaos into something structured and vast and *awake*. And then it moved — not in August's hand, not with August's arm, but *on its own*, pulling her forward across the yard like a leaf in floodwater. Nell saw August's face in that moment and would remember it for the rest of her life: not fierce, not triumphant, but *frightened*, genuinely frightened, a girl dragged by something she had spent her whole life refusing to wake.

The first bounty hunter swung his cudgel. The sword took it apart — not blocked it, *unmade* the swing, turning the man's wrist aside with a precision no human arm possessed, and laid him flat in the weeds with the flat of the blade against his chest, and he lay there gasping like the air had been sung out of his lungs. The second man ran. The third — the crossbowman, fumbling for a second bolt — found August suddenly inside his reach, sword-point resting against the hollow of his throat, and the point was trembling, and so was she.

"*Run,*" August said, and her voice cracked down the middle, and it was the most human sound Nell had ever heard her make.

They ran. All three of them, stumbling, leaving their weapons in the mud, crashing away through the hedge-row toward the south and whatever report they'd carry back to whoever had sent them.

Then the yard was empty except for the two of them and the ringing air and the well-water still shivering in circles.

Nell's song died in her throat. The silence that followed was enormous, and inside it she could hear everything: her own heartbeat, ragged; the creak of the well's crooked crane; and August, standing ten feet away with the naked sword hanging from her hand, breathing like a woman who had surfaced from deep water.

Neither of them moved for a long time.

"What," August said finally, and stopped. Started again. "What did you *do?*"

"I don't know." Nell's knees chose that moment to inform her they were done, and she sat down hard in the weeds beside the well. "I didn't — I panicked, and it all just — they all came out at once. All of them. I've never — the choirmaster said if I ever let them out together it would tear my throat out, he said—" Her hands were shaking. She made fists of them. "I'm sorry. Whatever that was, whatever it did to your sword, I'm sorry, I didn't mean—"

"Don't apologize." August was looking at the blade. Slowly, like approaching a strange dog, she turned it in the light. The steel was ordinary steel — gray, unremarkable, the same wrapped mystery it had always been — and yet Nell could still feel it *listening*, the way you can feel a room listen after a door slams. "It's never done that. Nineteen years I've carried this thing. It has never once moved without me."

"It moved *with* me," Nell said, and then heard what she'd said, and felt the cold go through her all over again. "With my voice. It answered me, August. Like — like it knew me. Like it had been waiting for someone to call."

August knelt down across from her, rewrapping the blade with hands that weren't quite steady, and for once she didn't hide it. Up close, in the gray light, Nell could see how pale she was, how wide her eyes stayed even when she blinked.

"When I was little," August said quietly, "my grandmother told me the sword had a name. A true name. And that if I ever spoke it — anywhere outside the valley, anywhere magic could reach — it would be like striking a bell that the whole kingdom could hear. Something would come. Something old." She tied off the wrapping. Her knuckles were white. "I have never spoken it. I have never let myself even *think* it all the way through. And today your voice reached into the steel and said it for me, and I couldn't stop it, and I couldn't feel where my hand ended and the blade began."

Nell hugged her knees. Inside her chest, the stolen voices had gone quiet again, coiled back down into their dark, but she could feel them differently now — not like cargo anymore. Like a congregation. Like they had seen something today too.

"Was it bad?" she asked. "What it felt like. When it answered."

August was silent for a long moment. A crow called somewhere down the valley, and the wind finally broke, and the first fat drops of the withheld rain began to spot the dust between them.

"It felt like coming home to a house I'd been locked out of my whole life," August said. "That's what scared me."

Nell nodded slowly. Because she understood. God help her, she understood exactly — the terror of finding out that the thing inside you that everyone feared was also the only thing that had ever truly fit.

"We should find Tam," she said. "He'll want to write this down. He writes everything down."

"He'll want to write down three pages on harmonic resonance and none of them will help." But August stood, and offered her hand, and pulled Nell up out of the weeds, and her grip lingered half a second longer than it needed to — two frightened people confirming each other solid.

They walked back down the road toward the willow stand where they'd left Tam arguing with a milestone. The rain came on properly then, drumming the leaves, washing the yard clean behind them.

Nell didn't tell August the last part. She didn't know how to say it, and she wasn't sure she wanted to know it herself.

But when the sword had sung, she had felt the voices inside her turn toward it — all of them, every stolen child — and she had felt, beneath the fear and the wonder, something else. Recognition. As if the blade had known their names too.

As if it had been counting them, the way she counted steps.

Waiting for enough of them to sing together.
