## 13. Sera Deserts

The order came at dusk, written in the king's own hand, and Sera Vane read it three times before she understood that her hands had stopped shaking only because they had gone somewhere past shaking entirely.

*By command of His Majesty and the Office of Purity: all persons of gifted blood within the Pale are to be surrendered to the Silent Order for judgment. Those under the age of discernment may be judged summarily. Captain Vane is instructed to carry out the sentence herself, as an example to her company.*

Summarily. The word sat on the page like a fly on meat. She knew what it meant. Every hunt-captain knew what it meant. It meant no trial, no record, no priest, no grave marker unless someone dug one in secret. It meant a child.

The boy's name was Wren Halloway. He was nine years old. He had been found three days ago in the granary loft of his father's farm outside Millbrook, doing what gifted children did before they learned to be afraid: he had made the wheat rise off the floor and hang in the air like golden rain, turning slowly, and he had laughed while it turned.

A farmhand had seen. A farmhand had reported. That was how it went now — that was how it had gone since the Edict, since Hollowmere burned, since the criers with their red-and-black sashes began reading out the Crown's promise that purity would be restored to the kingdom one confession at a time. Neighbors reporting neighbors. Children reporting parents. The whole country learning, street by street and hearth by hearth, that love was now a liability.

Sera stood in the doorway of the guardhouse at Millbrook and looked at the boy through the bars.

He was small for nine. He sat on the straw with his knees drawn up and his arms wrapped around them, and he had stopped crying — she could see the dried tracks on his face, salt-white in the lamplight — because crying hadn't helped, and children learned fast which things didn't help. When he saw her he flinched back against the wall, and something in his flinch was so precisely, so perfectly the flinch of a creature that has already been hurt once and expects it again that Sera had to put her hand flat against the doorframe to keep herself steady.

"Captain Vane." Her lieutenant, Ossler, at her shoulder. He was a good soldier, Ossler. Fifteen years in the hunt, a widower, honest with his ledgers, kind to horses. He held the order out to her again as if she might have misread it. "They want it done tonight. Before the Order's assessors arrive tomorrow. They said—" He paused, and his voice did something complicated. "They said you'd want to do it yourself. On account of your record."

Her record. Eleven years hunting the gifted across three provinces. Forty-one surrenders taken alive, a number her superiors had come to regard not as mercy but as inefficiency. She had always told herself the surrenders were the point — that every gifted soul brought in breathing was a soul the pyres hadn't touched, that the law was the law but she could be gentle inside it, that gentleness inside a cruel thing was a kind of sabotage.

She had told herself that for eleven years. Standing here, with the lamp guttering and the boy watching her with his arms around his knees, she finally heard the sentence for what it was: *I was gentle inside the machine, therefore the machine was fine.*

"Wren," she said through the bars.

He didn't answer. His eyes went to the sword at her hip and stayed there, the way a rabbit's eyes go to the hawk.

"I'm not going to hurt you," she said, and heard how it sounded, and hated it.

"You're the one who took Marta Ellery," the boy said. His voice was hoarse. "My da said you're the one who took her off the mountain. He said you were fair."

Marta Ellery. Two years ago. A hedge-witch of sixty who had kept a fever from taking half a village and had been surrendered — by Sera, gently, with a blanket over the old woman's shoulders against the cold — into the custody of the Silent Order. Sera had never learned what happened after the handoff. She had never asked. She had signed the transfer ledger and ridden south and told herself the same sentence again.

"He said I was fair," Sera repeated quietly.

"Yes."

"And look where fair got you."

The boy said nothing. Outside, the wind moved through the granary district and carried the smell of cut wheat and horse dung and, faintly, from the direction of the assize hall, old smoke — the smell that clung to everything now, the smell the whole kingdom wore like a borrowed coat.

Ossler cleared his throat behind her. "Captain. The men are asking when."

"When what," Sera said, though she knew.

"When it's done. Some of them want it over quick. Some of them—" He stopped again. Ossler was a man who stopped himself often lately; she had watched him develop the habit over the past season, the way a river develops eddies around a new stone. "Some of them are asking why it has to be a child at all. And some of them are asking nothing, which is worse."

"Which ones are asking nothing?"

"The ones who'll make sergeant next month."

* * *

She walked the perimeter of the stockade after full dark, alone, because walking was the only thing that had ever organized her thoughts and there was no one left she could organize them with.

Millbrook was a market town of four hundred souls, and it had changed its face in six weeks. The crier's platform still stood in the square where the Edict had been read, and someone had nailed a list to it — names, dozens of names, in a careful clerk's hand. *Persons of interest.* The baker's daughter who could make bread rise without yeast. The ferryman who had never once lost a boat to the Greyford rapids in thirty years. An entire family named Cotter whose sin, as far as Sera could determine from the annotations, was having a grandmother who had once been seen talking to her garden.

The list fluttered in the night wind like something molting.

She thought about Hollowmere. She had not been there when it burned — she had been two provinces away, chasing a rumor of an Ishikawa girl who had already slipped through the net — but she had seen the reports. *Structure loss: total. Civilian casualties: unrecorded. The matriarch, one Aldith Ishikawa, declined evacuation.* Declined evacuation. Four words standing in a report where a woman's life should have been, and Sera had read them and felt something crack quietly in a load-bearing wall inside herself, and had filed the feeling away under *later*, the way soldiers file everything, the way she had filed Marta Ellery and the fever-witch and the ferryman's face and eleven years of gentle sabotages that had saved no one.

*Later* had arrived. Later was now. Later was a nine-year-old boy named Wren Halloway waiting in a cell for a captain with a fair reputation to come do the crown's arithmetic.

She stopped at the north gate. Beyond it the road ran up through dark fields toward the hills, and beyond the hills, north and west, the road forked and forked again, and somewhere along one of those forks, if the Order's intelligence was worth anything at all — and it was, that was the hell of it, the Order's intelligence was very good — a swordspeaker girl was running with a choirgirl whose voice did wrong things to candles, and possibly with a disgraced archivist, heading nobody-knew-where, carrying nobody-knew-what.

The Ishikawa girl. August. Nineteen years old, per the archive extracts. Trained in the valley discipline, gift bound by oath, dangerous in the way that deep water is dangerous — not because it wants to hurt you, but because it doesn't care what it drowns.

Every instinct of her office said: find her. Every year of her service said: find her, take her, deliver her, sign the ledger, ride on.

And underneath all of it, older than her commission, older than her oaths, older than the uniform itself — some voice she had spent a decade refusing to hear said: *you know what the Order does with the ones you hand over. You have always known. You just never let yourself finish the thought.*

Tonight she finished it.

She stood at the gate until the moon came up over the hills, thin as a paring, and she finished the thought all the way down to its roots, and when she turned back toward the guardhouse her mind was the stillest it had been in eleven years.

* * *

She did not burn the commission in front of the men. That mattered to her, though she couldn't have said why — perhaps because a public burning was theater, and theater was the Order's language, and she was done speaking it. Perhaps because whatever she was about to become, she refused to make a lesson of it for anyone.

She waited until the third watch, when even the insomniacs sleep, and she went to her quarters and took out the commission itself — heavy paper, wax seal, the king's cipher stamped in gold that had dulled to brass over the years of handling — and beneath it the little tin of campaign ink and the folded map of the northern roads, and the letter from her sister in the coast towns that she had reread so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

She laid them all out on the table. Then she took the commission to the brazier.

The paper caught reluctantly. Official paper always did; it was made to survive filing, cataloguing, the long damp years in government chests. It curled and browned and fought her, and then the flame found the wax seal and the seal went up in a hiss of sweet smoke, and the king's cipher softened and ran like a wound, and Sera Vane stood in her own quarters at the third watch and watched eleven years of her life turn to light and ash, and felt — this was the strange part, the part she would remember — not free, exactly. Lighter the way an amputation is lighter.

There would be no going back. Burning a commission wasn't desertion yet; desertion was what happened tomorrow, and the day after, every day she drew breath outside the reach of the hunt. There would be a price on her head by week's end. Former hunt-captains who burned their papers were treated as infected — the assumption being that proximity to the gifted had contaminated them, that treason was a symptom. The Order had a whole doctrine about it. She had taught the doctrine once, to a room of second-lieutenants, in a clear confident voice, and none of them had questioned it, and neither had she.

She took the sword down from its pegs. Hunt-issue steel, plain hilt, the crown's mark stamped at the ricasso. She weighed it in her hand for a long moment, and then — because she was done carrying the crown's mark into whatever came next — she took a whetstone's coarse edge to the stamp and ground it out, slowly, in strokes that filled the room with a sound like a slow heartbeat, until the steel showed bare and anonymous, belonging to no office and no king.

Then she wrote two letters.

The first was to Ossler. It was short. *The boy goes free tonight. I've opened the cell and given him a horse and coin. By the time you read this I'm gone. Tell the men whatever keeps them safe — say I threatened you, say I bewitched you, say anything but the truth, because the truth will get you hanged alongside me. You were the best soldier I ever served with, and the fact that we served this is not your fault or mine, but it is ours to answer for. Go home to your daughter. Don't be a sergeant.*

The second letter she addressed to no one. It was a single line, and she wrote it because her hand demanded it the way a lung demands air:

*I did not know it was a choice until tonight. Now I know, and I choose.*

She left the letters on the table, weighted under the whetstone, and went down into the dark.

* * *

The cell lock was old and she had picked harder ones drunk. Wren Halloway woke at the first rattle of the bars and pressed himself into the corner, and Sera crouched down outside the cell so that her head was below his, below the level of her own sword, and she spoke softly, the way you speak to a spooked animal, the way she wished to God someone had spoken to her at nine years old.

"My name is Sera Vane. I'm the one who was sent to kill you. I'm not going to. I'm getting you out."

The boy stared at her. In the dark his eyes were just wet gleams. "That's a trick," he whispered. "That's what they do. They say they won't and then—"

"I know what they do." She slid the cell open and set the saddlebag on the floor between them, keeping her distance, keeping her hands visible. "Bread. Cheese. A skin of water. Forty crowns in the inner pocket — don't show anyone. There's a horse saddled at the far paddock, a bay mare, she's gentle. Take the north road to the shrine at Cold Harrow — the ruined one, not the working one. There's a woman keeps sheep near it, grey shawl, answers to Mother Cass. Tell her Captain Vane sent you and tell her the word is *thatch*. She'll get you over the border into Tarrowmark, where the Edict doesn't run. Can you ride?"

"I—I think so. Da was going to teach me this summer." The boy's voice cracked on *summer*, and Sera looked away for a moment, at the wall, at nothing, giving him the privacy of his grief the only way she could.

"This summer," she said, when she trusted her voice again, "is going to come. Maybe not this year. But summers come back. Get on the mare and don't stop before dawn and don't take the main road even if it's faster. Do you understand me?"

"Why?" The word burst out of him, sudden and furious, nine years old and shaking. "Why are you doing this? You're one of *them*. You're the one they send."

Sera knelt there in the straw-smell and the dark, and she did not give him the easy answer, because he deserved better than easy, and because she was only beginning to learn the true one herself.

"Because somebody has to be the one who doesn't," she said. "That's all. Somebody has to be the one who doesn't, or the ones who do are all there is."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she had not expected and was not prepared for: he reached out and touched her hand, briefly, the way you touch something hot to see if it burns. And then he shouldered the bag and went, quick and silent down the corridor, a small shadow running toward the paddocks and the north road and whatever summer was going to come for him, and Sera Vane stayed kneeling in the empty cell until his hoofbeats faded, and then she rose, and settled her cloak, and did not look back.

* * *

Dawn found her three miles north of Millbrook, riding hard through country she had patrolled for years and was now seeing for the first time — really seeing, the way you see a room after the furniture's been sold. The hills were turning gold with early autumn. Smoke rose from farm chimneys in straight, innocent columns. Somewhere behind her, by now, Ossler had found the letters, and the alarm bells would be ringing, and her name would be moving up the chain of command with a price attached, growing heavier at each link.

Let it grow. She had carried heavier things for less reason.

She stopped at the fork where the north road split — one branch running toward the coast and her sister and a life she might have had if she had been a different woman, the other climbing northwest into the high country, toward Cold Harrow, toward the passes, toward the places where the Order's writ thinned and the hunted went to ground.

Toward, if the intelligence briefings were right, a swordspeaker girl and whoever had gathered to her like sparrows to a broken eave.

Sera sat her horse at the fork for a long moment, breath steaming, the sun coming up red behind her and throwing her shadow long across the northward road — a shadow with no crown's mark anywhere on it, just a woman-shape and a blade-shape, pointing the way she meant to go.

She thought about the boy's hand touching hers. *You're one of them.* Yes. She had been. And the Order believed contamination ran from the gifted to the clean, but standing here in the cold morning light she understood they had it backwards: it was the hunt that infected everyone it touched, and the only cure was to turn around.

She wheeled the horse onto the northwestern road.

Behind her, far off, the bells of Millbrook began to ring — alarm, alarm, the captain's gone, the captain's gone — and Sera Vane rode toward the sound of her own name becoming a bounty, and did not slow, and did not look back, and smiled for the first time in eleven years, a small fierce thing with no gentleness in it at all.

Three days' hard ride ahead. High passes. And somewhere in the high country, a girl with a sword that had never been named, and a company forming around her like iron filings around a lodestone.

Sera meant to find her before the Order did.

And God help anyone — Order, Inquisitor, or king — who stood between her and the chance to kneel, once, to the last of the Ishikawas, and ask not for surrender, but for a place in the line.
