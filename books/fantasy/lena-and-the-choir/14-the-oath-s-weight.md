## 14. The Oath's Weight

The fire was small because Nell kept making it smaller.

Every time the flames climbed past her knuckles she would glance at them sidelong, the way a person eyes a dog that has bitten before, and hum three low notes under her breath—and the fire would settle, obedient, shrinking down to embers and patience. August had watched her do it four times now without saying anything. The fifth time, she said something.

"You don't have to do that."

Nell's hands froze over the flames. "Do what?"

"Tame it." August turned the spit she'd rigged from a green branch. The hare Tam had snared turned slowly, dripping fat into the coals, hissing like a whisper that wouldn't stop. "Fire's just fire out here. Nothing for it to gutter."

"It's not about the fire." Nell pulled her knees up to her chest. She was still wearing the choir robe, or what was left of it—she'd torn the hem into strips for bandages and bound her boots with another strip, so that she looked like a saint who had been through a war. "It's about being loud. Loud things get found."

Tam looked up from the papers spread across his knees. He had been reading by firelight for an hour, copying fragments onto fresh pages with the grim diligence of a man trying to outrun a memory, and now he took off his spectacles and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "She's not wrong," he said. "The Order hunts by resonance. A gifted voice carries. That's half of why they want you, girl."

"Don't call me girl," Nell said, without heat. She said it every night. It had become a kind of grace between them.

"Very well. Nell." Tam set the spectacles back on. "But the principle stands. Which is why I'd like to know what we're walking toward. Or rather—" he tapped the folded map beside him, the one with the river towns inked in his cramped archivist's hand, "—what's walking toward us. You've been quiet since Greyford, August. Quiet in the way stone is quiet. And I've read enough sealed documents to know when silence is load-bearing."

August fed another branch to the fire and did not answer right away.

They were camped in the hollow beneath a fallen beech, well off the north road, in a fold of hills that smelled of wet leaf-rot and woodsmoke and, faintly, of the hare's browning skin. Three days north of Greyford. Three days since Sera Vane had stood at the edge of the market square with a writ in her hand and let them walk—had looked at August across thirty feet of crowd and done nothing, said nothing, and August still did not know what to make of that mercy, whether it was a crack in the hunt or simply a slower kind of trap.

Three days, too, since the sword had begun to feel heavier.

Not physically. Physically it weighed what it had always weighed, four pounds of valley-forged steel wrapped in oiled leather, slung across her back where it had hung since she was nine years old and Grandmother had first buckled it there. But ever since the sweep at Greyford—ever since she'd put her hand on the grip while soldiers passed within arm's reach, an old reflex, a child's habit of touching the thing she could never use—the sword had felt *aware*. Like a door in a dark house that she'd suddenly realized was standing open.

"Nell," August said. "When you sang at the cathedral. When the candles went wrong. Did anyone ever tell you why?"

Nell blinked. "The choirmaster said my voice was impure. Unclean. He said it like it was a disease." Her mouth twisted. "He said a lot of things like that."

"And no one told you what your voice actually *did*?"

"No one told me anything. That was rather the whole architecture of my life." She laughed, but it came out thin.

August nodded slowly. She looked into the fire, and the fire looked back, patient as embers, and she understood that Tam was right—that the silence had been load-bearing, and that she was the only one who knew how much weight it held, and that a company which did not know the weight would break carrying it.

So she told them.

* * *

"My family didn't come to Hollowmere because we were hiding from magic," she said. "We came because we were holding it shut."

Tam's pen stopped moving.

"The valley isn't dead ground by accident. Everyone thinks it's geology—iron in the soil, some quirk of the river, whatever the miller's cousin who studied at Aldercroft says after his third cup. It isn't. My grandmother called it the Hush. She said our line built it, eleven generations back, when the last of the oath-bound swordspeakers drove a binding into the world and buried its anchor under Hollowmere. The gift can't wake there because the Hush holds everything asleep. Not just us. Everything."

"That's not possible," Tam said—but he said it the way he said all his impossible things, already reaching for paper, already believing.

"I thought so too, my whole life. I thought the oath was simple. Don't name the sword. Don't leave the valley. Keep the gift asleep, keep the family safe. Grandmother made it sound like locking a pantry against mice." August's hand found the hilt behind her shoulder, thumb tracing the wrapped grip, the worn place where four generations of Ishikawa thumbs had worn the leather smooth. "It isn't a pantry. It's a lock. And the oath isn't the key—it's the *hand* on the key. As long as an Ishikawa keeps the oath inside the Hush, the binding stays shut. All of it. Whatever my ancestors bound, eleven generations ago, it stays bound because we stay small and silent and afraid."

"What did they bind?" Nell asked. Her voice had gone very soft, the way it went soft around candles.

"I don't know. Grandmother died before she finished telling me. There were two halves to the history—she always said that. Two halves, and you only get the second half when the first half is paid for." August swallowed. "I got the first half the night before the crier came. The second half is in the sword, if it's anywhere. And I am not going to find it sitting in a valley."

"So you left," Tam said quietly. "Knowing this."

"I left because they burned the valley." The words came out flatter than she intended, and that flatness was its own kind of wound. "I left because Captain Vane had my grandmother's map and witch-hunters had my grandmother's house, and staying would have been dying for nothing. But yes. I knew. Every step past the valley line, I've known."

"Known what, exactly?" Tam leaned forward. Firelight caught his spectacles and turned his eyes into two small coins. "August. Be precise. You're an archivist's nightmare and I need precision."

August took a breath, and it shook more than she wanted it to, and she hated that, and she let it happen anyway, because Nell was watching her with those wide candle-colored eyes and Tam was watching her with an archivist's terrible patience, and both of them deserved the truth more than she deserved her pride.

"The binding on the crown," she said. "The thing Tam told us about. The entity. Nineteen years in the king's body, wearing him like a glove." She turned to face Tam fully. "Your archive. Your hidden clauses. The Edict of Purity isn't a hunt for gifted people. It's a search for the last key—you said that. You said the Order wants an oath-bound swordspeaker. Now hear why they want one alive."

She pulled the sword around off her back and laid it across her knees. Even sheathed, even wrapped, the steel seemed to drink the firelight, a long dark absence in the shape of a blade. Nell drew back an inch without seeming to know she'd done it.

"This sword has a true name. Every oath-bound blade does. Mine has been unnamed for nineteen years—not because no one knew it, but because naming it is the worst thing an Ishikawa can do. Grandmother told me the terms the night before she died. She made me repeat them back until I could say them in my sleep." August's voice dropped, and the fire seemed to lean in. "If I speak my sword's true name outside the Hush—if I speak it here, in the world, where the binding can hear it—the unraveling begins. The lock my ancestors set turns. The binding on the crown starts to come apart. The entity weakens. The king gets a chance to breathe for the first time in nineteen years."

"That's—" Nell started.

"That's what we want," August said. "Yes. And here is the price, so listen, because I should have said this at Greyford and I didn't, and that was cowardice." Her hands tightened on the sheath until the leather creaked. "The binding doesn't unravel cleanly. It's not a knot; it's a cage, and cages have doors, and when the lock turns, every door opens at once. Including the one between the entity and me. The moment I speak the name, the Vessel-of-Crowns will know exactly where I am. What I am. It will feel me the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck. And it will have one purpose from that second forward: reach me first. Because a binding coming apart needs somewhere to go. And the last oath-bound line—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—the last oath-bound line is standing right here, holding the key."

The hare dripped fat into the coals. Somewhere upslope an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"So that's the arithmetic," August said. "I speak the name, the king goes free, and the oldest thing in this kingdom comes hunting me across however many miles are left between us. And I don't know what happens when it arrives. Grandmother never got that far. Second half of the history, remember. Paid for." She looked up. "That's the weight. That's what I've been carrying since the ridge. Not the sword. The sentence."

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. The fire popped. Nell's hands had gone to her own throat, unconsciously, the way they always did when someone talked about voices and power, fingers resting on the column of her throat as though checking that her voice was still hers.

Then Tam began, very carefully, to laugh.

It wasn't cruel laughter. It was the laughter of a man who has spent twenty years in archives and has finally, finally met a document worse than any he's catalogued. "Of course," he said. "Of course. Do you know what this is? This is a countersigned instrument with a self-executing clause and no severability provision. Whoever drafted your ancestor's oath wanted the binding broken eventually—they built in a mechanism—but they wanted it broken *deliberately*, by the right hand, at the right time, and they accepted collateral exposure as the cost of ensuring no casual hand could do it." He pushed his spectacles up. "August. May I see the blade?"

"No one touches the sword."

"I'm not asking to touch it. I'm asking to look. There may be engraving under the wrap. Oath-blades were typically marked—"

"Grandmother said the marks aren't letters. They're older than letters." But August tilted the sheath toward the light, and Tam leaned in, and for a moment the two of them bent over the dark length of steel like scholars over a dying language.

"There," Tam breathed. "Along the fuller, under the leather seam. Feel that irregularity?" He didn't touch; he pointed, his finger hovering a hair's breadth above the wrap. "That's not wear. That's deliberate. Someone stitched the wrapping to conceal a line of marking, and they did it so thoroughly that generations of hands wouldn't feel it unless they were looking." He sat back. "Your grandmother knew. She wrapped it herself, didn't she? Or taught someone to."

"She rewrapped it every spring. I used to think it was just maintenance." August's chest tightened. Every spring. Every year of her childhood, Grandmother kneeling in the smithy with oil and linen and that same closed expression she wore whenever the sword came up, humming something low under her breath while she worked—

Humming.

August went cold. "Nell. When you sing. When the candles gutter and men weep. Does it feel like the song is yours, or like you're carrying something that was already moving?"

Nell stared at her. "What kind of question is—"

"Please."

"...Both," Nell whispered. "Both. It's mine and it isn't. Like the note exists before I sing it and I'm just... letting it through." Her hands were at her throat again. "Why?"

"Because my grandmother hummed over this sword every spring of my childhood," August said slowly, "and I always thought it was a working song, the way millers sing at the wheel. And tonight I'm wondering whether she wasn't maintaining a wrapping. Whether she was talking to it. Whether the second half of the history isn't written anywhere at all, but sung, and stored, the way your choirmaster stored—" she stopped, hearing what she was about to say, and Nell heard it too, and the look that passed between them was awful and clarifying at once.

"Voices keep," Nell said. Very quietly. "Voices keep, if you know how to store them. That's what the crypts under the cathedral are for. That's what I'm *for*." Her chin came up, defiant and frightened in equal measure. "Are you telling me your grandmother hid the truth inside a sword the way the choirmaster hid stolen voices inside wards?"

"I'm telling you I don't know," August said. "And I'm telling you that I've carried this thing for fifteen years and slept beside it every night, and as of three days ago it feels like it's listening back."

* * *

Tam broke the silence that followed, because Tam always broke silences; it was his function in the world, the way Nell's was to carry songs and August's apparently was to carry locks. He gathered his loose pages, squared them, weighted them with a stone, and spoke in the level voice he used for facts that required no courage to state and all the courage in the world to hear.

"Then let's reason from the mechanism. If speaking the name opens every door at once, the question becomes: does it have to be spoken alone?" He looked at Nell. "You said yourself the note exists before you sing it. You carry what was already moving. Could you carry a name?"

Nell went very still.

"A name is a voice," Tam pressed on, gentle and relentless, an archivist opening a box he knows contains something that bites. "A true name most of all. Spoken aloud, it announces. Carried—concealed inside another voice, inside a song, inside a hymn in a crowded square on a feast day—it might arrive without announcing. The binding might begin to turn before the entity understands what it's hearing. Seconds. Perhaps minutes. Perhaps enough."

"That's not how songs work," Nell said. But her voice cracked on *work*, and her hand was at her throat, and August saw her swallow around something.

"It's exactly how your songs work," August said softly. "You told me. In the cathedral, when the wards ran thin, the choirmaster would have you sing the repair-hymns and the wards would hold for a month. You weren't singing *at* them, Nell. You were carrying power into them. Wrapped inside something beautiful enough that no one thought to check what else was in the package."

Nell looked at her for a long time. The firelight moved on her face, and August watched her arrive somewhere she hadn't meant to go, watched her understand that the thing done to her in the cathedral and the thing being proposed now by a disgraced archivist beside a dead hare's fire were made of the same material—and that one had been theft and the other might be choice, and that the difference was the entire moral architecture of the world.

"If I carry it wrong," Nell said at last, "if the name slips out ahead of the song, or the song breaks halfway—"

"Then it's spoken alone," August said. "In the open. With the entity looking straight at us." She reached across the fire and took Nell's hand off her throat and held it, briefly, the way you'd steady a bird. "Which is why nobody is deciding anything tonight. Least of all you. I named the cost because you both deserve to know it, not because I've chosen to pay it. There may be another road. There may be a third half of the history. There may be nothing in that stitching but my grandmother's embroidery." She almost smiled. "Archivists find other roads. That's their whole vocation."

"Occasionally," Tam admitted. He was already writing, fast, the pen scratching like a small animal. "There will be records. Binding-instruments leave counterparts—a lock this old would have been witnessed, registered, duplicated. If the original oath survives anywhere, it survives in a royal depository, and if I can read the original, I can read the exceptions. Every draftsmen leaves exceptions. It's vanity. They cannot resist proving they've thought of everything." His pen paused. "The capital, then. Eventually. Whatever else, the answer is in the capital."

"The capital," Nell repeated, and shivered, and pulled her ruined choir robe tighter around her shoulders. "Where the cathedral is. Where he is."

"Where *it* is," August corrected. "The king is a prisoner. We agreed on that."

"We agreed on that," Nell said, "and then you told us the prisoner's jailer will feel you the moment you pick the lock, and now I'm allowed to be frightened of both of them."

"Frightened is permitted. Frightened is mandatory." August slid the sword back across her shoulders and buckled it, and her hands were steady, and she noted distantly that they were steady the way a soldier's hands are steady, which is to say trained, not calm. "But frightened people who know the weight carry it better than brave people who don't. So. Now you know. Both halves of what I know, which is one and a half halves of what there is to know."

* * *

They banked the fire and took watches, Tam first, then Nell, then August, because August always took the last watch; sleeping before dawn meant dreaming, and dreaming meant the valley, and the valley meant the ridge, and the ridge meant the orange bloom of Hollowmere going up against a black sky, and she had rationed herself to that dream once every third night like a miser rationing coin.

She woke once, deep in the black hours, to a sound.

Not Nell's humming—Nell slept silently, curled like a comma, her stolen cargo of voices finally quiet. Not Tam's snoring, which was prodigious and oddly comforting. Something else. Something at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the hills, at the edge of everything.

A bell.

One toll, far south, so distant it might have been imagination or wind or the blood in her own ears. But bells didn't ring at that hour in country parishes, and they didn't ring once, and August lay with her hand on the sword's grip and felt—distinctly, unmistakably, for the first time since the valley—the steel under her palm grow warm.

Not hot. Warm. The warmth of a hand closing around another hand.

Far to the south, the bell tolled again. Then a third time. Counting something. Calling something. And under the ringing, so faint she would spend the rest of her life unsure whether she'd truly heard it, August thought she caught a thread of melody—low, patient, familiar—rising from the wrapped steel on her back like breath through a keyhole.

Her grandmother's working song.

Coming from inside the sword.
