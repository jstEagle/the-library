## 15. Sanctuary of the Unsworn

Tam Osgood had spent thirty years among shelves, and he had learned to read a room the way he read a folio: by its margins, its water damage, the places where hands had worn it smooth. The hollow beneath the ruined mill at Wren's Crossing read like a book that had been loved hard and hidden often.

They came down into it at dusk, led by a boy of perhaps twelve who had appeared on the road an hour before sunset and said nothing at all—only looked at August's sword, then at Nell, then at Tam, and turned and walked, trusting them to follow. Nell had whispered, *Is this a trap?* and Sera, riding rear guard with her hand never far from her blade, had said, *If it is, it's a strange one. Traps don't send children.*

The mill itself was a lie told in stone: roofless walls, a wheel frozen mid-turn in dry weeds, everything arranged to look abandoned for exactly as long as it needed to look abandoned. But behind the collapsed grain chute, a stair went down into the earth, and the earth here did not feel like earth. It felt like a held breath.

Then the breath released, and they were through, and Tam forgot how to be afraid because he was too busy being astonished.

The cavern opened like the inside of a bell. Lamplight hung from iron hooks along galleries cut into the living rock, and from those galleries came the sounds of ordinary life—the clatter of a pot, a child laughing, someone tuning a stringed instrument badly and patiently. Laundry lines strung between stalactites. A garden of pale mushrooms tended under glass bells. A woman teaching letters to three children with charcoal on slate, her voice carrying the particular weariness of someone who has explained the same thing four hundred times and expects to explain it four hundred more.

*Oh,* Tam thought, with the specific grief of his profession. *A library made of people.*

He had catalogued the Edict's hidden clauses himself—or rather, he had found them, copied them, and failed, catastrophically, to act on them until it was nearly too late. *All persons manifesting unregistered gifts to be surrendered to Crown authority for assessment.* He knew the numbers. He had computed them once, drunk and despairing, in the archive's sub-basement: extrapolating from census records and tithe anomalies, the kingdom's unsworn numbered somewhere between nine and fourteen thousand. Nine to fourteen thousand people, erased from every ledger he had ever kept, living in cellars and fens and border hills, and he—an archivist, whose entire sacred duty was that nothing be lost—had not known they existed.

"You're staring," Sera murmured beside him.

"I'm grieving," Tam said. "There's a difference."

August stood very still at the bottom of the stair, her sword across her back, her face doing something complicated. Tam watched her watch the children with their slates, the mushroom garden, the mended clothes drying in lamplight, and he understood that she was looking at a country she had believed uninhabited. A valley that forgets, she had called Hollowmere, and Tam had thought she meant magic. Now he suspected she meant herself.

The boy who had led them tugged August's sleeve and pointed.

At the far end of the cavern, where the rock fell away into shadow, an old woman sat in a chair built for someone much larger, mending a net. Her hands were brown and knuckled as tree roots, and she did not look up when they approached, but she said, in a voice like gravel poured slow:

"An Ishikawa walks into my house after forty years, and nobody thinks to warn me first. Sit down, girl, before you fall down. You've been walking on that leg wrong since Greyford."

August sat. It was less a choice than a collapse.

* * *

Her name was Maren Ostreva, and she had been born in Hollowmere too—not in the village proper but in the high pasture farms, before the fever winters emptied them—and she had known Edda Ishikawa when they were girls stealing apples from the same orchard and lying about it to the same unforgiving priest.

"Your grandmother," Maren said, working the net, "once convinced me I could fly. Told me our family's gift ran to wings if we only believed hard enough. I jumped off the miller's fence and broke my wrist." She looked up, and her eyes were quick and dark and missed nothing. "She laughed so hard she cried. Then she splinted my arm better than the priest could have, because the other thing our family runs to is knowing how bodies work. That was Edda. Cruel and kind in the same breath, and never sorry for either."

"She never mentioned you," August said carefully.

"No. She wouldn't have." Maren set down the net. "Because mentioning me would mean explaining why I left, and explaining why I left would mean telling you what your family actually is, and Edda decided you'd learn that either never or all at once. Knowing her, she was praying for never." Her gaze moved over the rest of them—Sera with her hunt-captain's bearing she couldn't shed any more than a snake sheds its skeleton; Nell pressed close to August's side with her hands folded like a good choirgirl's; Tam with his satchel of stolen state secrets. "And she sent you out into the world with half a story and a naked blade. Stubborn, faithful, foolish woman. Was she well?"

The silence lasted long enough that Maren's hands stopped moving.

"The valley burned," August said. "Nine days ago. Witch-hunters. She stayed behind so I could get out through the cellar tunnels." Her voice did not break. It did something worse—it flattened, going thin and even as paper, the voice of someone holding a door shut against a flood. "I don't know if she's alive. I know what staying meant. We both did."

Maren closed her eyes. For a moment the whole cavern seemed to lean toward her—the way a household leans toward its eldest grief—and Tam saw a dozen people pause mid-task, sensing without words that something had entered the community that needed absorbing. An old man took the mending from her lap without being asked. A girl brought water. Nobody spoke.

When Maren opened her eyes again, they were wet and furious and absolutely steady.

"Then you'll stay the night," she said, "and eat, and sleep, and tomorrow we talk about what Edda wouldn't tell you. Not tonight. Tonight you're just a girl whose grandmother died buying her time, and I'm just an old woman who lost a friend twice—once when she chose the valley, and once now." She raised her voice slightly, and it carried to every gallery. "They're fed and bedded by the second bell! And somebody find the archivist a table. He has the look of a man who hasn't touched paper in a week and it's killing him."

"It is," Tam admitted.

For the first time since the burning, August laughed—a small, cracked, astonished sound, gone almost before it arrived, but real. Tam filed it away the way he filed everything, and thought: *there. That's the sound of a person discovering she might have had cousins.*

* * *

They gave him a table. Of course they gave him a table—a plank on trestles in an alcove lit by two lamps, with ink made of soot and oak gall, and paper that was really birch bark cured soft, and a young woman named Ilse who had a gift Tam could only describe as *remembering things perfectly* and who had appointed herself his assistant within minutes of seeing him organize his satchel by urgency.

"You're the one who leaked the clauses," she said, grinding more ink. "We got a copy. Six months ago, smuggled out in a hymnal. It's how we knew to move the families north of the ford before the sweeps started." She said it without drama, the way one reports weather. "You saved maybe sixty lives that we know of. Probably more we don't."

Tam stared at his own hands. They were ink-stained and shaking slightly, and he wished she would stop talking, and he wished she would never stop.

"I also wrote the finding aid that let the Inquisition locate the Ishikawa map," he said. "Same year. Same desk. They needed provenance research on pre-Edict gift registries, and I—" His throat closed. He tried again. "I indexed the oath-bound lines for them. I didn't know what it was for. That's the thing no one tells you about archives. Every document is innocent until someone asks it a question, and I answered theirs beautifully."

Ilse was quiet a moment. Then she pushed the ink pot toward him.

"Here," she said, "we keep a ledger too. Names of the dead, names of the saved, names of the ones we couldn't reach. My mother started it. The rule is you write both columns. The saved and the lost. You don't get to only count one." She tapped the page. "So write. Both columns. Every day if you need to. That's the whole religion down here—write both columns and keep going."

Tam wrote. *Hollowmere, burned: unknown, feared many. Saved: August Ishikawa. Nell Carrow. Myself, undeserving.* His hand steadied as it moved. Around him the cavern breathed its ordinary miracles—someone singing a work song off-key, the smell of barley and onions, a baby crying and being hushed with a hum that made the lamp flames stand up straight and tall for a full three seconds before settling.

He looked up sharply. The singer was a middle-aged man stirring a cauldron, entirely unaware, and the hum had come from Nell, sitting nearby, absently soothing a stranger's child the way she must have soothed a hundred cathedral infants, and the flames had *answered* her.

Nell hadn't noticed. Or had noticed, and pretended not to, and pulled her hands into her sleeves.

Tam wrote a third line, in the margin, where marginalia belonged: *Ask the girl about the lamps. Gently.*

* * *

Later, past midnight, when most of the sanctuary slept, Tam climbed to the upper gallery with his notes and found Maren Ostreva awake, sitting at the cavern mouth where a seam in the rock showed a sliver of starry sky. She patted the stone beside her. He sat. His knees complained. Everything about him complained these days; he had come to think of his body as a building scheduled for demolition, still occupied.

"Edda's granddaughter," Maren said, without preamble. "What do you know about what she carries?"

"A sword with no name spoken aloud in forty years. An oath that keeps her gift asleep. And—" Tam hesitated. He was, professionally, a keeper of confidences, but he had learned on this road that hoarded knowledge got people killed, and shared knowledge, sometimes, kept them alive. "And a lock. Her family's oath is the final lock on something bound to the crown. That's what we're riding toward. That's what the king wants her for—or what wears the king wants her for."

Maren nodded slowly, unsurprised, which frightened him more than shock would have.

"My husband was a swordspeaker," she said. "Ostrevan, from the coast clans, before the first purges scattered us. He used to say a swordspeaker's gift isn't a weapon. It's a *hearing*. You hear what the blade knows, and the blade knows what it's cut, and what it's sworn to, and who held it before you. Every true name is a memory that learned to bite." She turned her dark eyes on him. "Edda's line heard something else too. Something older than blades. Do you understand me, archivist? When the Ishikawas sealed themselves in a valley where magic cannot follow, they weren't hiding from the crown. They were hiding *it* from a hearing. If a swordspeaker ever truly listened to the thing on the throne—"

"—she'd hear its name," Tam finished. His mouth had gone dry. "And a name can be spoken. And a name spoken can bind or unbind."

"And whatever hears its name spoken," Maren said, "hears the speaker right back." She looked out at the sliver of sky. "That girl down there is the last hearing in the kingdom that thing fears. You're taking her to it. So I'll ask you plainly, Tam Osgood, keeper of both columns: are you leading her to freedom, or are you leading her to the throne room the way they led the lamb to the altar—gently, and with excellent documentation?"

Tam had no answer. He had, instead, the memory of August's laugh in the lamplight, small and cracked and real, and the ledger waiting below with its two columns, and the terrible arithmetic of his whole life, in which every beautiful answer he'd given had served someone else's question.

"I don't know," he said. It was the most honest entry he'd made in thirty years.

Maren grunted, satisfied in some grim way. "Good. Doubt keeps you checking your work." She rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and paused at the gallery rail. "One more thing, archivist. The choirgirl. When she hummed tonight, my wards tasted it. Old wards, my husband's make—they taste any working within a mile." Her voice dropped low. "That child's voice isn't one voice. It's a *choir*. Dozens of voices braided into one throat, and none of them hers. I've heard that weaving once before, forty years ago, in the cathedral crypts under the capital." Her eyes were very old and very certain. "Children's voices, fed into a vessel to feed the binding. If anyone asks, I never told you. But someone should ask her. Before the road does."

Below them, in the lamplit dark, Nell slept curled against August's side, and the lamp flames near her stood straight and trembling, like singers waiting for a cue.
