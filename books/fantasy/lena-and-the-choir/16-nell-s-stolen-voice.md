## 16. Nell's Stolen Voice

The sanctuary of the unsworn smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and something older underneath—tallow, maybe, or the memory of tallow, the way old churches remembered incense long after the censers went cold. Nell had been there three days before she understood that the smell was people. Dozens of them, living close and careful, cooking over banked fires so the smoke wouldn't rise above the tree line, wearing their magic folded up inside them like letters they were afraid to open.

She sat in the corner of a granary that had been hollowed into a chapel of sorts—a room with no altar, only a circle of stools and a shelf of mismatched candles—and she tried not to listen to the voices.

That was the trouble. She always listened. It was the first thing the choirmaster had taught her to do, back when she was seven and still flattered by the attention: *Listen, Nell. A voice is a door. Find where it opens.*

There were forty-one people in the sanctuary tonight. She had counted their voices without meaning to, the way you count your own heartbeats. The widow Maren, whose speaking voice was gravel and river-stone but whose *singing* voice, when she hummed over the cookfire, was a low gold thread that made the flames stand up straighter. The twin boys who could warm water with their palms and giggled when they did it, as if it were a trick and not a death sentence if the wrong ears heard. A man called Odo who never spoke at all, because his voice killed moths out of the air and he was afraid of what else it might kill.

Forty-one doors. And Nell sat among them with her hands pressed flat against her knees, feeling like a house built from stolen timber.

* * *

It was August who found her there, past midnight, when the granary had gone quiet except for the settling of grain and the small sounds of Sera sharpening her knives outside, which was apparently how that woman prayed.

August didn't sit so much as lower herself, careful as someone approaching a wounded animal, which Nell supposed she was. The sword came with her—August took it everywhere now, even to sleep, even to the privy, its wrapped hilt poking up over her shoulder like a second head. Since the tollbridge, since whatever had half-woken in August on that bloodied span of wood, the sword had stopped being a weapon and started being a presence. Nell could hear it sometimes. Not words. More like a note held under hearing, the way you can feel a bell after it stops ringing.

"You haven't sung," August said. No preamble. August didn't believe in preambles. "Three days. You hum with Maren at the fire, little bits, nothing whole. I've been waiting for you to sing."

"Maybe I'm resting my voice."

"Maybe." August turned the sword across her knees, not drawing it, just resting her hand on the wrap. "Or maybe you're afraid of what comes out."

Nell laughed, and hated how thin it sounded. "You're one to talk about things coming out. Your sword tried to take a man's head off on its own and you've spent three days pretending it didn't frighten you."

"It did frighten me." August said it plainly, the way she said everything, and that plainness was somehow worse than any argument. "But I know what frightened me. Do you?"

The candle nearest them guttered. Not because of Nell—or not only because of her. In the sanctuary, all the fires leaned toward her slightly, like flowers toward sun, like drowning men toward a rope. She had noticed it the first night and moved her bedroll away from the hearth. Nobody had commented. That was the thing about this place: nobody commented. They had all learned to fold themselves small.

"I want to tell you something," Nell said, "and I need you to let me get through all of it before you say anything kind. Because if you're kind in the middle I'll stop, and I have been stopping for eleven years, and I think it's the stopping that's going to kill me before any witch-hunter does."

August nodded once. She pulled a whetstone from her pocket—not to use, Nell realized, just to hold, something for her hands—and waited.

* * *

Nell had been given to the cathedral choir at six years old, which was not unusual. Half the orphaned girls in Carreth ended up in the choir; it was food and a bed and a trade, and the cathedral was beautiful, and nobody asked what happened behind the chorister's screen. What was unusual was that within a month, the choirmaster, a soft-voiced man named Anselm with ink stains on his fingers and a smell of beeswax and something faintly metallic, began keeping her after practice.

Not punishment. Never punishment. Anselm never raised his voice; raising your voice was vulgar, he said, and besides, why would you shout at an instrument?

He would sit her in the warded room beneath the choir loft—the room with the lead-lined walls and the floor tiles worn smooth in a pattern Nell hadn't understood until years later, until Tam, with his archivist's eye, had looked at the geometry and gone pale and said, *that's a binding array, that's a very old one, that's older than the cathedral*—and he would have her sing.

Just scales, at first. Then hymns. Then, as she grew, the hard pieces, the ones written in the old modes that made the other children's noses bleed. And while she sang, Anselm would sit across from her with his hands flat on his knees, exactly the way August's hands were flat on hers right now, and he would *listen*. He'd close his eyes. Sometimes his lips would move, following her, soundless.

And afterward she would be exhausted in a way that sleep didn't fix. Hollowed. Like a jug poured out to the last drop. And Anselm would give her honeyed milk and tell her what a gift she had, what a treasure, how the wards of the cathedral sang stronger than they had in a century, how the Blessed Choir above—the great vaulted ceiling with its painted saints—seemed almost alive lately, didn't she think? How proud the Dean was. How proud *he* was.

"I thought I was tired because I was working hard," Nell said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. She had expected weeping. What came instead was worse: clarity. "I was nine. Ten. You don't ask questions when the grown man with the honeyed milk tells you you're special. You just drink the milk."

August's thumb had gone still on the whetstone.

"The other girls burned out," Nell went on. "You saw them, maybe, if you ever went to services—the little ones with the grey faces who got 'sent home to recover.' There was a girl before me, Brigit. She had my seat. She had my *voice part*. One winter she started singing wrong—cracking, going flat, crying between verses—and by spring she was sent home, and I got her place in the front row, and I remember being *glad*. I was eight years old and glad, because a dead girl's seat felt like a promotion." She heard her own breath shake and kept going, because if she stopped now she would never start again. "I sang at her funeral. Anselm said my voice would comfort the family. I stood over that little coffin and I sang, and halfway through the hymn every candle in the nave went out, and everyone said it was the Holy Spirit moving, and Anselm put his hand on my shoulder and squeezed, once, like a man closing a latch."

"What happened to her?" August asked quietly. "Brigit."

"She died. Officially of fever." Nell looked at her hands. They were a singer's hands, kept soft, nails trimmed blunt so they wouldn't click against the music. "But here's the thing I figured out too late, the thing I've been chewing on since Tam showed me those ward-ledgers. Brigit didn't burn out. She was *emptied*. And then—" and here her voice finally did break, not with grief but with the rage underneath the grief, the rage that had nowhere to go for eleven years, "—and then whatever was left of her voice, whatever Anselm had skimmed off her all those years, it didn't die with her. Because voices don't die, August. Did you know that? Voices *persist*. That's what makes them magic. And Anselm had somewhere to put them."

The candle flame bent toward her, straining.

"The wards," she said. "The cathedral's wards are fed by voices. Everyone knows the choir 'blesses' the stones—that's the doctrine, that's the pretty story they tell the congregation. But it isn't blessing. It's *harvest*. Anselm wasn't teaching me to sing. He was using me as a vessel. A jug. Every lesson, every after-practice hour in that lead-lined room, he poured the other children into me. Their high notes, their breath control, their little cracked attempts at the old modes—all of it funneled into me, stored in me, and then drawn out through me into the binding array under the loft. I was the spout. I was the *pipe*." She was standing now. She didn't remember standing. "Forty years he's been doing it. Forty years of girls. Brigit, and the ones before Brigit, and the ones after, and me, and the little ones coming up behind me right now, tonight, in that building, singing vespers with their eyes shut and their hearts full because the choirmaster told them they're *special*—"

"Nell."

"—and the worst part, the part I can't—" Her voice cracked wide open and what came out of it was not words. It was a note. Low and terrible and full, a note that emptied every candle in the granary at once and made the grain shiver in its bins and made August's sword, wrapped and sheathed and silent, *ring*—one clear harmonic humming up through the scabbard like a struck bell answering a struck bell.

Silence. Darkness. Then, one by one, the candles relit themselves, or were relit—Nell couldn't tell which, and that frightened her more than anything—and August was on her feet too, one hand on the sword's hilt, not drawing it, just holding it, the way you hold a friend's arm.

"That," August said, into the dark. "That's what you've been afraid of."

"No." Nell's laugh came out ragged. "No, that's the least of it. August, when I sing like that—when I *push*—I can feel them. All of them. Brigit's voice is in there. I know her vibrato, I know how she leaned on her high G, I know her better than I know my own mother's face and I never met her alive. There are—I counted once, in the bad nights, in the nights I couldn't sleep—there are *nineteen* voices in me that aren't mine. Nineteen girls. And when I sing, they sing with me, and it's—it's beautiful, that's the obscene part, it's the most beautiful sound I've ever made, and it's built out of stolen children, and I *love* it, I love singing, and I don't know anymore which notes are mine—"

She ran out of air. She stood in the grain-smelling dark with her chest heaving and the candles burning upright again and felt, at last, the thing she had been running from since the night she fled the cathedral: not fear of the hunters. Shame. Cold, institutional, orderly shame, the kind that wears vestments and keeps ledgers and files everything neatly away.

"They didn't even hurt us loudly," she whispered. "That's what I can't get out of my head. There was no cruelty I could point at. Anselm was *kind*. He remembered birthdays. He wept at Brigit's funeral, real tears, I watched them fall. And the whole time he was pouring us out like wine and calling it grace. If cruelty looked like cruelty, I'd know what to do with it. But it looked like *choir practice*."

* * *

August was quiet for a long moment. Outside, Sera's knife had stopped; either she'd finished or she was listening, and Nell suspected listening. The whole sanctuary was probably listening. Voices carried here. Voices always carried around Nell; it was the one law of nature she could rely on.

"My grandmother used to say," August said at last, slowly, as if handling each word before setting it down, "that the most dangerous oaths are the ones sworn gently. That evil mostly doesn't roar. It files paperwork." She turned the whetstone over in her fingers. "I used to think that was about the crown. About witch-hunters and edicts. Big things." She looked up, and in the candlelight her face was very young and very old at once. "But it's about Anselm too. It's about anyone who finds a way to make taking look like giving."

"He gave me everything," Nell said miserably. "Food, training, a place. He gave me the best voice in Carreth."

"He gave you nineteen girls' worth of voice and called it yours." August's jaw tightened. "That's not generosity. That's laundering."

The word landed like a stone in still water. Nell felt it sink through her, down and down, touching bottom. Laundered goods. Stolen silver passed through clean hands until it shone. Every ovation she had ever received. Every time a congregation wept. Every time the Dean had patted her head and said *what a blessing you are, child*—

A blessing. To *them*, yes. She had been a blessing the way a well is a blessing to the town that drinks it dry.

"I want them back," she said, and heard her own voice change as she said it, steadying, deepening, the nineteen harmonics underneath it all leaning in to listen. "Not—gods, not to use. I want them *named*. I want their names said out loud, in a place that keeps records, by someone whose job it is to keep records. Brigit of Carreth. And the others. I'll learn them all. Tam has the choir rolls; he said the archive never throws anything away. I want their names sung where the people who harvested them have to hear it."

August smiled then—small and fierce, the smile Nell had started living for without noticing when it had begun. "Tam's good at naming things," she said. "It's practically his religion."

"And the rest of it—" Nell's hands curled at her sides. "The rest of it, I don't know yet. Whether I can give them back. Whether giving them back kills me. Whether they'd even *want* to leave, after all this time inside me—we're the only company any of us has left." She laughed, wet and disbelieving. "Listen to me. I'm homesick for my own haunted throat."

"You're not haunted," August said. "You're *occupied*. There's a difference. Occupations end."

Before Nell could answer—before she could decide whether that was comfort or promise or threat—a knock came at the granary door, three soft raps, and Maren's voice followed it, low and urgent in a way that made both of them go still.

"Girls. Come quick, and bring the archivist." A pause, and Nell heard the old woman swallow. "One of our watchers just came in off the north road. Says there's a column of riders moving down from the pass. Silent Order livery. And they're not searching the forest the way hunters search." Another pause, longer. "They're searching it the way *shepherds* search. Like they already know exactly which fold the sheep are sleeping in."

Nell's stomach dropped. Beside her, August's hand closed around the sword's hilt, and beneath her palm, faint as breath, the blade began—very softly—to hum.
