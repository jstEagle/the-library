## 18. Sera Finds Them

Sera Vane had followed liars before. She had followed smugglers through the salt marshes of the southern coast and a renegade priest across three weeks of frozen pass in the Greymarch, and she had learned that every fugitive leaves a signature — a habit of mind written into the mud. The clever ones doubled back. The frightened ones walked straight until they dropped. But the Ishikawa girl left something stranger than either: a trail that read like someone who had been taught to vanish by people who had never once needed to run.

She camped high, but on the lee side of ridges, where smoke would drift into stone instead of sky. She bought bread in villages with her hood down and her face turned away, but she always paid with valley coin, old-minted, the wheat-sheaf stamp that hadn't circulated outside Hollowmere in forty years. She walked like a soldier and slept like one too, rising before dawn, breaking camp without fire — except for the nights when she didn't, when a small flame bloomed against the dark like an argument she was having with herself.

And there was the other one. The girl with the voice.

Sera had heard the reports from Greyford, secondhand and thirdhand, garbled by fear: a sweep gone sideways, a song that put six trained men to sleep standing up, a hunt-captain who watched two fugitives walk out of a burning granary and did nothing. That last part had cost her. Commander Aldous Rennick had read the report aloud in the command tent at Greyford, his voice flat as slate, and looked at her the whole time he read it.

*Captain Vane let them go.*

*I was securing the perimeter,* she had said.

*Of course you were.* He had smiled then, and it had been worse than anger. Rennick's smiles were the smiles of a man who kept ledgers, and Sera's name had been entered into one.

So now she rode alone, which was itself a kind of confession. A captain of the Order did not ride alone. A captain of the Order rode with a column behind her and a writ in her saddlebag and the quiet certainty that the kingdom's rot could be cut out with enough steel. Sera rode with none of those things. She rode with a commission she had not yet burned, folded twice in her breast pocket, over her heart, where it sat like a coal she couldn't decide whether to smother or feed.

Three days out of Greyford, in the hill country where the pines grew crooked and the road gave up pretending to be a road, she found the place where they had fought.

She knew it before she dismounted. The air had a texture to it, a wrongness, like a room after a quarrel. Her horse refused to go forward — a big grey gelding, steady under artillery fire, planting all four hooves and trembling — and Sera slid off and walked, because she had learned long ago to trust animals about things men explained away.

The clearing told its story in pieces. Scorch marks fanned out from a dead fire ring, too wide, too symmetrical, not made by any lamp or torch. A bounty hunter's crossbow lay split lengthwise, both halves charred black, as if the wood had been burned from the inside out. There were drag marks. There was blood, old enough to have gone brown, in quantities that said *survived* rather than *died*, because no one buries a man and leaves this much of him behind. And at the center of it all, pressed deep into the soft ground, a single boot print that was not a boot print at all — a shape like a sword's tip driven hilt-deep into the earth and withdrawn, leaving a hole the width of a fist, rimmed with glass.

Glass. Fused earth. In a clearing where no fire had been hot enough to boil a kettle.

Sera crouched beside it and did not touch it. Nineteen years she had served the crown, and in all that time she had seen perhaps four true workings, each one small, each one licensed, each one performed by a registered gift-bearer under supervision for the edification of magistrates. This was not that. This was a sword remembering what it was.

*The last oath-bound line,* the Edict said. *To be surrendered alive if possible.*

Alive. That word had been doing strange work in the Order's orders lately. Alive, and delivered where? To whom? Rennick's instructions on that point had been precise in a way that made her skin crawl: deliver the girl to the Silent Order's escort at the tollbridge, unharmed, unquestioned, and ask nothing further. In nineteen years of hunting, no one had ever told Sera Vane to stop asking questions. Questions were the whole trade. Now they were contraband.

She stood, and the wind moved through the crooked pines, and somewhere north and east of her, faint as a thought not quite finished, she heard singing.

Not with her ears. She understood that even as her ears strained for it. It came in under the hearing, the way cold comes in under a door — a thread of melody with no source, no direction, only a pull, gentle and insistent, like a hand on the wrist of a drowning woman. Sera had grown up in a house full of hymns. Her mother had sung them badly and constantly. This was not that. This was music the way a blade is metal: the same substance, refined past recognition, made for a purpose.

Her horse screamed.

She spun. The grey was rearing, eyes white-rimmed, and beyond him the treeline had changed — not moved, *changed*, the shadows between the trunks suddenly dense and deliberate, the way water changes just before something rises through it. Sera's hand found her sword and her body did everything right, weight low, blade angled, the opening forms of the Order's first stance, and it did not matter at all, because whatever stood in the treeline was not going to be met with stances.

It came out of the shadow like a rumor given weight. Tall, wrong-jointed, its outline refusing to settle — fur, then bark, then something like chainmail made of dark water. It had no face she could fix on. Where a face should have been there was a pressure, a place her eyes slid off of, and looking at it directly felt like pressing her thumb against the point of a needle: possible, briefly, and then punishing.

Ward-beast. The word arrived from some half-remembered briefing, some archivist's lecture she'd slept through twenty years ago. *Unregistered communities sometimes bind guardians into the land itself. Do not engage. Withdraw and report.*

Withdraw. The beast took one step and the ground flinched under it, and Sera understood that withdrawal had been removed from the list of available options. It had let her come this far. It had watched her kneel at the fused-glass print and read their story out of the dirt, and it had decided, with whatever passed for its judgment, that she had read enough.

"Easy," she said, to the horse, to herself, to the thing. "I'm not—"

The beast moved. There was no transition. One moment it stood ten paces off; the next its weight was coming down on her like a collapsing roof, and Sera did the only thing soldiers do when the world ends: she committed. She dropped inside its reach — a mistake, she knew even mid-motion, its reach was a country — and drove her sword up into the mass of it, and felt the blade go in and keep going in, swallowed, and then the thing's attention came down on her like winter.

Cold. Not temperature. *Cold*, the original article, the cold that existed before warmth was invented. It poured through the contact point, through the steel, through her arm, into her chest, and it began, methodically, to take inventory. She felt it find her heartbeat and consider it. She felt it find the folded commission over her heart and pause there, puzzled, the way a dog pauses at a door that smells of both home and strangers. She felt it begin to decide what she was.

Sera Vane had faced death twice before, properly, and both times she had been surprised by how administrative it felt — the narrowing of options, the triage of regrets. This was different. This was being *read*. Somewhere beneath the terror, a scholar's part of her noted that this must be what the hunted felt, all these years, when the Order came through their doors. To be opened like a ledger by something that did not care about your handwriting.

Then the world sang.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, the same thread she'd been following for three days, but now it was a rope, now it was a wall of sound with no sound in it — a chord held by a voice that was also many voices, layered, braided, some of them children's voices, some of them old, one of them so familiar it stopped her heart. The ward-beast recoiled off her sword like a hand pulled from a stove. The cold broke. Sera fell to one knee in the pine needles, gasping, her breath smoking in air that had gone warm again, ordinary again, and above the treeline — impossibly, beautifully — light was moving. Not sunlight. *Chosen* light, gold threading through the branches in patterns too regular for weather, and woven through it, that voice, that impossible choir, saying in a language she did not know a single word of and understood completely:

*Not this one. Not yet. Look again.*

The beast looked again. She felt it — felt the pressure of its attention return, gentler now, sifting through her the way Tam Osgood had once described sifting an archive: page by page, patient, thorough. It found Greyford. It found the granary she had not burned down around two fleeing girls. It found the child in Marrow's Bend whose execution order she had torn in half three nights ago, the act that had ended her life as surely as any blade, though the news hadn't reached her body yet. It found the commission, still folded, still unburned, and it lingered there, and Sera wanted to shout at it, *burn it yourself, then, if you're so wise* —

The presence withdrew. The shadows in the treeline loosened, became merely shadows. The gold light folded itself away like a banner after parade. And the clearing was only a clearing, scarred and silent, and Sera Vane was on her knees in it holding a sword that hummed faintly, like a struck bell forgetting itself.

From the ridge above, a voice said, dry as dust: "Well. You're either the bravest woman I've ever met or the stupidest, and in my experience the categories overlap."

* * *

Tam Osgood came down the slope the way he did everything — sideways, apologetically, as if apologizing to the hillside for using it. He was thinner than he'd been in the capital, grayer, his archivist's coat replaced by a farmer's wool that fit him like a borrowed apology. He stopped six paces away, which Sera noted was exactly the distance of a man who expected to be arrested.

"Captain Vane," he said.

"Osgood." Her voice came out scraped. She got up off her knee, slowly, keeping her sword low and visible, which was itself a message: *I know what I look like to you.* "You're supposed to be in irons."

"I'm supposed to be in a great many places." He glanced at the treeline, at the scorch marks, at her. "You followed us from Greyford."

"You were never hard to follow. Your swordspeaker camps like a textbook and pays with forty-year-old coin." She sheathed the blade. Her hand wasn't steady, and she let him see that too. Honesty was the only currency she had left. "I've been asking myself for three days whether I'm following you to arrest you or to warn you. I haven't answered myself yet."

"That's very honest."

"I'm out of practice with anything else."

Above them, on the ridge, movement: two figures against the sky. The tall one stood utterly still, watching, and even at this distance Sera could feel the assessment in it — the Ishikawa girl, the last oath-bound line, the reason for everything, looking down at her hunter with a face that gave away nothing except that it had decided to give away nothing. Beside her, smaller, wrapped in a patched traveling cloak, the choirgirl leaned out to see better, and even at fifty paces Sera felt the brush of her attention like a change in the weather. So that was the voice. That was the thing that had put six men to sleep and woken a ward-beast's mercy.

"She saved my life just now," Sera said. "Your singer. Or whatever sings through her."

"The ward doesn't kill," Tam said. "It reads. Nell convinced it you were worth reading twice." He paused. "That's rarer than you'd think. Most of the Order's captains, it wouldn't have bothered listening to me about."

"And why would it listen to you?"

"Because I'm the one who told it where to stand." He said it simply, without pride, and Sera remembered — abruptly, completely — why she had liked Tam Osgood in another life, before the archives closed and the questions became contraband. He was a man who confessed things the way other men breathed.

They climbed to the ridge together, and the company received her the way a wound receives a needle: necessary, resented, watched.

The camp was better hidden than anything the Order ran — tucked into a fold of the hills behind a fallen pine, fireless, the horses picketed in a hollow. August Ishikawa stood with her arms crossed and her grandmother's sword at her hip, and up close she was younger than the reports and older than her years, with the specific stillness of someone carrying a weight she has decided not to complain about. The choirgirl — Nell — hovered at her shoulder, studying Sera with open, unnerving curiosity, head tilted like a bird's.

"You're the one from Greyford," Nell said.

"Yes."

"You let us walk out of the granary."

"I did."

"Why?"

Sera opened her mouth and found, to her horror, that she had no polished answer. She had rehearsed answers the whole ride north — duty, doubt, strategy, mercy — and every one of them died on her tongue in front of this girl's plain question. What came out instead was the truth, raw and unarchived:

"Because I looked at you and I couldn't remember what we were protecting anymore. The kingdom, I mean. I stood there with my sword drawn and I tried to picture the thing I'd sworn to defend, and all I could see was the Edict nailed to the mill door in my own hometown, and a boy I grew up with reading it aloud, and everyone deciding, all at once, that some of our neighbors had never really been neighbors." She looked at August. "That's not a defense of what I've done. Nineteen years, I did it. I dragged gifted folk out of their beds for lesser reasons than that Edict. I'm not asking you to forgive any of it. I'm telling you why I'm here, and it isn't orders."

Silence. The wind moved the fallen pine's bare branches. August Ishikawa looked at her for a long moment, and Sera bore it, because she had spent nineteen years making other people stand under her gaze and it was past time she learned what it weighed.

"My grandmother stayed behind for us," August said at last. Her voice was level, and level the way ice is level. "You know that. Everyone in your Order knows what happened at Hollowmere."

"I know."

"Your Order burned it."

"Some of them are mine to answer for, then." Sera held the girl's eyes. "All of it, if that's what you need. I'll carry it."

"That's not—" August stopped. Something moved behind her face, some grief meeting some unexpected thing and not knowing which name to give it. "That's not what I expected you to say."

"What did you expect?"

"'Following orders.' 'The law is the law.' Every captain says it. Rennick says it while he signs things that make me sick." August's jaw tightened. "He's looking for us. Your Rennick. He's not sending columns anymore. He's sending the Silent Order's own, quietly, in twos and threes, and they don't travel with writs."

Sera went cold in a way the ward-beast hadn't managed. "How do you know that?"

"Because we caught one," Nell said softly. "Two nights ago. He didn't have a writ. He didn't have anything. He just had this." She held out a small disc of black metal, unmarked, and Sera's stomach dropped through the hillside, because she knew that disc. Every officer of the Order knew it. It was a token of the Quiet Service, the Order's inner hand, the one that officially did not exist, the one that reported to no council and no crown — only, if the oldest rumors were true, to whatever it was the crown actually served.

"They're not hunting for the Edict anymore," Sera said slowly, working it through, watching it assemble itself into shapes she had been refusing to see since Greyford. "The Edict was never the point. The girl is the point. The *line* is the point. They want the Ishikawa blood found, and they don't care who knows or who asks why, because the ones giving the orders have stopped answering to the kingdom." She looked at Tam. "You knew. In the capital. That's what you leaked. That's why they burned your name."

"I knew the shape of it," Tam said quietly. "Not the whole of it. I'm still assembling the whole of it. As, I suspect, are you, Captain, or you wouldn't be standing here letting us look at you."

August Ishikawa hadn't moved. Her hand rested on the sword at her hip — not gripping it, resting, the way one rests a hand on a sleeping animal — and Sera realized the girl was asking it something, silently, the way you'd ask an elder's portrait for permission. Whatever answer came, it softened her shoulders by a degree.

"There's a bridge," August said. "Tollbridge, two days east. The Order's elite are staging there — your friend Rennick's own picked men. They expect a fugitive company to come through, because they think we're stupid, or because someone drew us a map of our own habits." Her eyes flicked to Tam, and something passed between them, old and unresolved. "We were going to go around. North, through the high passes. Slow. Safe."

"But?" Sera said.

"But safe hasn't worked," August said. "Safe is how Hollowmere burned. And you—" she pointed at Sera, and her finger was steady, and her voice almost was, "—you know how the Order stages a bridge. You know their signals, their rotations, the words that open their checkpoints. If we go through that tollbridge, we go through with you. And if you turn on us at the far side, if this is the longest trap ever baited—"

"It isn't."

"People said that about the Edict, too. People said that about the king."

The words landed harder than she could know, and Sera watched Tam flinch, and filed the flinch away. She stepped forward, into the middle of their wary circle, and did the thing she had decided to do somewhere on the third day of riding, alone, under indifferent stars: she reached into her breast pocket and drew out the commission, folded twice, worn soft at the creases, and held it out flat on her palm.

"Command of the Third Hunt Column," she said. "My name, the crown's seal, authority to kill every person in this camp. I was ordered to burn it the night I tore up that child's execution order. I didn't. I told myself I was keeping it because it might be useful — cover, access, a way to move through their lines." She turned her hand over. "That was a lie I was living in. Here."

She dropped it toward the fire pit — cold, fireless — and before it landed, Nell's hand shot out and caught it.

The girl turned it over, curious, and then frowned, and then went very still. "There's writing on the back," she said. "Under the seal. It's faint — I can barely—" She stopped. Her eyes came up, wide and dark, and Sera saw genuine fear arrive in them, and feared it herself a moment later, because Nell said:

"It's warm. Why is it warm? It's been in your pocket for days and it's *warm*, and it's humming, and I can hear it, and it knows my name."

The wind died. On August's hip, the grandmother's sword began, very softly, to sing.
