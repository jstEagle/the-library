## 19. The King's True Letters

The letters smelled of cedar and old smoke.

Tam Osgood had carried them for eleven days in a oilcloth wrap against his chest, and by now he knew their weight the way a man knows a stone in his boot—by the way it changed every step. He laid them out on the floor of the abandoned tithe-barn with the reverence of a priest arranging relics and the guilt of a thief sorting plunder, which was, he supposed, exactly what he was. Both things at once. He had been both things for most of his life.

Outside, the wind moved through the bare birches with a sound like paper turning. Nell was somewhere near the stream, singing to herself in fragments—not her stolen magic, just a girl's humming, tuneless and content—and August sat on the barn's threshold with that unnamed sword across her knees, sharpening it out of habit or prayer or grief; Tam had learned not to ask which. Sera Vane stood watch at the tree line because she did not yet know how to do anything else with her hands.

And Tam knelt among the private correspondence of Aldric IV, King of Vessarin, and read the words of a dead man who was not yet dead.

* * *

He had stolen them from the royal archive eleven days ago, on the night everything ended—the night the Edict's hidden clauses went into his satchel beside his resignation letter he never delivered, the night he walked out of the Archive of the Crown for the last time and did not look back until the bridges were behind him. Everyone believed he had fled with proof of the hunt's true purpose: that the purity edicts were a search, that the witch-hunters were key-finders, that somewhere in the machinery of the crown a thing wearing the king's face wanted an oath-bound swordspeaker found. That much was true. That much he had told them on the road north, at Greyford, in the firelight, watching August's face go still as a frozen pond.

But there was a second bundle. A smaller one. One he had not mentioned, because he had not yet understood it, and because Tam Osgood had spent thirty years in the archive learning the first law of his profession: never present a document you have not read to the end.

These were the king's letters. Not the state correspondence—those were copied, filed, sealed, safe in their cabinets. These were the drafts. The discarded ones. The ones a young prince wrote in his own hand, late at night, and folded away unsent, and hid in a cipher-box under the false bottom of a drawer that Tam himself had catalogued nineteen years ago as *miscellaneous personal effects, value negligible.*

Nineteen years ago. He had been twenty-six, ambitious, meticulous, and blind. He had catalogued a dead man's last words as *value negligible*, and then he had gone home and slept soundly.

Now he spread them in the dust and read them again, all of them, in order, because order mattered. Because the archive had taught him that truth lived in sequence the way a body lived in skin.

* * *

The earliest letter was dated the spring of the king's nineteenth year—three months before his coronation. The handwriting was beautiful then, confident, the hand of a boy educated by the best tutors in the capital, a hand that had never been afraid.

*To my cousin Maren,*

*They showed me the crown today. Father is very ill and they are already measuring my head for it, which I find obscene, like tailors measuring a corpse. You will laugh at me but I dreamed about it. In the dream it was warm.*

*I asked Lord Chancellor Verel what it is made of and he said gold and old vows, which is not an answer, and when I pressed him he smiled at me the way one smiles at a child who has asked where the meat comes from. I am nineteen years old and I am going to be king and no one will tell me what the crown is.*

*You are the only person who does not lie to me. Tell me if I should be afraid.*

Tam set that page down with fingers that were not quite steady. Outside, August's whetstone whispered against steel, a long slow rhythm like breathing.

There was an answer preserved too—Maren's reply, kept because the prince had kept everything she sent him, tucked between his drafts like flowers in a book. Her handwriting was quick and slanted, a soldier's hand.

*You should be afraid. But not of what Verel thinks. Ask the archivists. Ask the old ones, the ones before Osgood's time. Ask what happened to the crown of Aldric I, and why the histories call the second coronation "the Quiet Year." Ask why there are no portraits of your great-grandfather's regent. Then decide whether to be afraid, and tell no one you asked me.*

*Your M.*

*Ask the archivists.* Tam closed his eyes. Somewhere in the dark behind his eyelids, a young prince had done exactly that—had come down into the archive with those questions, and some archivist, some predecessor of his, some careful old man or woman with dust in their lungs, had shown him the sealed folios. And Tam had never known. Thirty years in that cold cathedral of paper, and the most important reader who ever came down the stairs had come and gone without Tam ever seeing him.

Or—no. He frowned. He reached back through memory the way one reaches into deep water, feeling for something that brushed past years ago. There *had* been a young man, once. Before the coronation. Tall, serious, who had asked strange questions about the regency of Aldric III and the missing year in the temple rolls. Tam had been junior then, fetching folios for the Under-Archivist, and he remembered—he remembered with a lurch—the old Under-Archivist sending *him* away, telling him to go re-shelve the eastern annals, and the prince and the old man sitting alone among the sealed cases for the better part of an afternoon.

He had resented it at the time. He had wanted to be part of whatever important thing was happening.

He had been twenty-six years old and jealous of a conversation that had decided the fate of the kingdom.

* * *

The middle letters changed. Anyone who worked with documents long enough learned to read a life in handwriting the way a physician reads a face, and Tam read this one with his heart climbing into his throat. Over two years, the beautiful confident hand tightened. The letters grew shorter. The ink grew darker, pressed harder, as though the writer feared the words would not otherwise hold.

*To Maren,*

*I wore it. What else was there? You were right about all of it—the Quiet Year, the regent, the thing in the reliquary vault. Verel's faction has the votes and the Order has the blades and if I refuse the crown they will simply put it on someone weaker. My sister is fourteen. Do you understand? They would put it on her, or on some malleable lord's son, and the thing beneath the gold would have a soft mind to wear like a glove.*

*I have read everything the old archivist gave me. I know what it is now. I know what it wants. It is patient the way rot is patient. It does not need to conquer; it only needs to be worn.*

*So I will be worn instead. I am strong. Everyone says so. Let it try a mind with teeth.*

*If I change—if the letters stop sounding like me—you must remember that I knew. Whatever they say of me after, whatever decrees come out under my seal, remember that Aldric chose this. Write it down somewhere. Hide it well. Someone must know that the king was not taken. The king volunteered.*

*Gods keep you. Someone should be kept, even if it cannot be me.*

Tam had to stop. He got up, walked to the barn door, and stood looking out at the grey afternoon, at the birches, at August sharpening her sword on the step with her grandmother's valley in her ruined eyes. She glanced up at him. He managed something that was probably not quite a smile and went back inside, because the letters were not finished, and the first law of his profession held him like a hook.

*The king was not taken. The king volunteered.*

For nineteen years the whole kingdom had watched a slow possession and called it madness, called it illness, called it the weight of the crown—and the court had whispered, and the priests had prayed, and the archivists had filed the decrees under *royal prerogative, irregular periods*. Tam himself had drafted the classification scheme. Tam himself had invented the tidy little heading under which a nation's slow murder was filed away: *His Majesty's Disposition, Ongoing.*

And all the while the man inside had been screaming in the only way left to him—in ink, in cipher, in letters to a cousin who died six years ago and could no longer answer.

* * *

The later letters were the worst. They were also, Tam realized with a cold spreading through his chest, the reason he had been able to flee at all. The reason the archive's seals had opened for him. The reason the cipher-box's lock had known his key.

Because the king—or what remained of the king, buried deep under nineteen years of the Vessel-of-Crowns, holding on the way a drowning man holds a rope—had planned for Tam Osgood specifically. Had left instructions. Had left, in the final letter, dated four months ago, addressed to no one:

*To whoever finds these, if anyone ever does:*

*I no longer know how much of me is writing this. Some mornings I wake and I am myself for a hundred heartbeats, and in those hundred heartbeats I work. I have hidden what matters where only an archivist would think to look, because archivists are the only people in this kingdom who read everything and believe nothing. Whoever you are, you found the box, so you are either an archivist or the thing itself, and if you are the thing itself then none of this matters anyway, so I will write as if you are the former.*

*The binding holds. Barely. It was made to hold—it was made by oath-bound swordspeakers in a valley that forgets, and their oath is the last lock, and as long as their line keeps its silence the crown stays a cage. But the cage is rusting. I can feel it rusting. Every year I lose a little more of the hundred heartbeats. Last spring it was forty.*

*It knows what it needs. It needs the last swordspeaker to break the oath—not killed, killed would only pass the binding elsewhere—it needs them to break it willingly, in its presence, so that when the lock opens the cage opens with it and it can leap. It has been hunting the Ishikawa line through my own hunters for years. I sign the orders. I watch my hands sign the orders. If you are reading this, perhaps my hands have finally signed the one that ends me, and perhaps that is a mercy.*

*But hear me, archivist, whoever you are: do not let them kill the girl. Or the boy, or the woman, whoever carries the gift now. Killing the bearer ends nothing and damns everything. The oath must be broken by choice, freely, by the one who swore it or their heir—that is the terms of it, the old terms, the ones the valley forgot. Broken rightly, the binding comes apart clean and the thing can be caught again. Broken wrongly, or broken by blood, it goes free forever.*

*Find the counter-oath. It exists. The old archivist told me before he died: there is a word-shape, a discipline, a way to re-cage it in a willing keeper rather than a stolen crown. He would not tell me what it was. He said a king's consent could not be trusted, because a king's consent is never only his own. Perhaps he was right. Perhaps that is the bitterest joke of my reign: the one thing that could save me requires a freedom I gave up to save everyone else.*

*I am tired. The heartbeats are shorter. I will spend what remains of them signing pardons in my own name while I still can, small ones, quiet ones, names slipped into the margins where the chancellors do not look. It is not much. It is what I have.*

*Remember: the king was not taken. The king volunteered. And if you ever stand in a room with what wears me—tell it, from me, that it has had nineteen years of my mind and learned nothing worth learning.*

*Aldric*

Tam sat back on his heels in the dust of the tithe-barn, and for a long moment he could not move at all.

* * *

"Tam."

August's voice. He looked up. She was standing over him, sword sheathed now, her shadow falling across the letters. Nell had drifted in behind her, damp-haired from the stream, and even Sera stood at the doorway with her arms crossed, pretending she hadn't been listening to the silence, which of course she had.

"You've been reading since dawn," August said. "You haven't eaten. You're doing the thing where your face goes white in stages." She crouched, picked up the topmost letter—the last one, the one to no one—and her eyes moved over the cramped, deteriorating hand. He watched her read it. Watched her lips shape the phrase *the king volunteered*, watched her go very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"He knew," she said quietly. "All this time. He knew what the crown was, and he—" She stopped. Started again. "He put it on himself."

"To keep it off someone weaker," Tam said. His voice came out rough, scraped raw from disuse. "His sister was fourteen. The chancellor's faction would have crowned a puppet. He read the sealed histories, he understood the thing in the gold, and he chose nineteen years of drowning over letting it wear a softer mind." He laughed, and hated the sound of it, because there was nothing funny anywhere in the world. "We called him mad. The court called him mad. I *filed* him under mad. *His Majesty's Disposition, Ongoing.* I built the shelf he's been buried on for two decades."

Nell had picked up one of the early letters, the beautiful confident hand, and was reading it with tears running silently down her face, the way she seemed to do everything—openly, without shame, as if crying were simply another kind of singing. "He was so young," she said. "In this one he's just—a boy asking his cousin if he should be scared."

"He was," Tam said. "And then he stopped being allowed to be."

Sera spoke from the doorway, and her voice had a strange flat quality Tam had learned to hear as the sound of a soldier's certainties cracking. "The Silent Order serves the crown. I served the crown. We've been hunting the last swordspeaker for a madman's edicts, and the madman—" She stopped. Pressed her thumb hard against her ring, the commission-ring she still wore even now, even burned-in-absentia. "The Order's masters knew. Didn't they. Verel's people knew what the crown was, and they've been using the king's hunters to open the cage for it."

"Or the Order serves the thing directly," Tam said. "I don't know yet which. But it hardly matters, because either way—" he gathered the letters, squared their edges with the automatic tenderness of thirty years' habit, "—either way we are not fleeing a tyrant. We are carrying out the last request of a prisoner. There's a difference, Captain, and I suspect it's going to matter to every one of us before this is finished."

August had not moved. She was still crouched over the final letter, and Tam saw her finger rest, very lightly, on the line about the counter-oath. *A way to re-cage it in a willing keeper rather than a stolen crown.*

"The old archivist," she said. "The one who told the king. Who was he?"

Tam felt the question land in his chest like a coin dropped down a well. He heard it falling for a long time before it struck bottom.

"Under-Archivist Edran Hale," he said. "My teacher. The man who sent me away to shelve the eastern annals the day the prince came asking questions." He swallowed. "He died nine years ago. But he left me his keys, and his seals, and one instruction I never understood until tonight. He said: *when the last Ishikawa comes asking, give her everything, and count yourself lucky if she lets you help.*"

August looked up at him. Outside, the wind turned in the birches, and far to the south—so faint it might have been weather, might have been imagination—came a sound none of them could name yet: a bell, ringing in a city none of them could see, tolling the hour as though counting down.

"We'll need his keys," August said. "And his seals. And everything Edran Hale ever hid." She rose, and the sword at her hip seemed to settle against her side like something waking. "Because if the king volunteered, then breaking this oath isn't betrayal anymore. It's a rescue. And I want to know, Tam—before we take another step north—exactly where your teacher hid the counter-oath."

Tam looked down at the letters in his hands—at the dead-alive king's last hundred heartbeats pressed into ink—and understood that the easy part of the road was over.

"I know where he hid it," he said. "That's the problem. It's in the capital."
