## 20. Ambush at the Tollbridge

The river came out of the north the color of old pewter, and where it met the road there was a bridge that had been a tollbridge once and was now only bones of timber and a memory of a fee. A man sat at its near end anyway, in a little hut with a slate roof gone green with moss, and he held out his hand as we approached as though the kingdom still had laws worth collecting.

"Two coppers a head," he said. "Three if you're carrying steel."

Tam paid him without argument, which told me more about his nerves than anything he'd said all morning. Tam Osgood haggled over bread. Tam Osgood had once, according to himself, argued with a royal treasurer for an hour over the cost of ink. He dropped four coppers into the tollman's palm like a man feeding a fire he hoped would stay small.

Nell was humming. She hummed almost constantly now, low under her breath, fragments of hymns and work songs and things I didn't recognize—snatches she'd swallowed whole from other singers, I understood by then, though she never said so outright. The tune today was one from her cathedral days, something in a minor key that made the river seem to slow when it caught it. I felt it against my sternum like a second pulse. My sword felt it too. It hung at my hip with its old familiar silence, but lately the silence had texture to it, like a held breath.

"You feel that?" Nell asked me quietly, when the tollman had waved us on and the bridge boards began to sound beneath our boots.

"I feel everything lately," I said. "That's the problem."

The mist was what I noticed first, afterward. Not the quiet—the quiet came later, and it was worse—but the mist. It lay along the water in long gray ribbons, thick as wool, though the morning above the banks was clear enough to show us the far hills. Mist pools on rivers; I knew that. I'd grown up beside a stream that fogged every dawn. But this mist did not move. It lay across the span of the bridge and the fifty yards of road beyond it like something poured, like something set there deliberately, and no wind touched it, and the sun could not thin it.

Tam stopped walking. "August."

"I see it."

"There's no toll house past the bridge," Tam said. His voice had gone flat and precise, the archivist's voice, the voice he used when reciting facts he wished weren't facts. "No village within two miles. There's nothing for that mist to be doing there except waiting."

Sera wasn't with us yet. She'd ridden ahead the night before to scout the crossing at Greyford's sister ford, and we were to meet her on the far side—or so the plan went. Plans, I was learning, were things you made so that the world would have something specific to break.

"We go around," I said.

"The bank drops," Tam said. "Clay cut twenty feet down for a quarter mile either way. We'd be rats in a trench with our backs to the water." He pushed his spectacles up his nose, and his hand shook, and he steadied it with the other hand, and looked at me. "I've read the Order's field manuals, August. All of them. They love ground like this. Ground that funnels. Ground that makes you choose."

Nell had gone very still. She stood with her hands folded in front of her like a girl at choir practice, like a girl waiting for a choirmaster's nod, and I realized with a cold turn in my stomach that she was frightened in the oldest way she knew how to be frightened—the way of someone who had learned young that fear was not allowed to have a sound.

"They're here," she said. Not a question.

"Yes," I said.

And then the mist answered her.

* * *

It did not move the way mist moves. It *withdrew*—peeled back from the center of the bridge like a curtain drawn by careful hands, opening a lane down its middle, and standing in the lane were six figures in ash-gray robes with their hoods back and their faces bare, and that was the horror of them, that was the thing my grandmother's stories had never managed to make me believe: they were ordinary. Middle-aged men and women with weathered cheeks and tired eyes, the kind of faces you'd trust at a market stall. No masks. No sigils. The Silent Order wore nothing, marked itself with nothing, because it had never needed to hide. Who hides from sheep?

The one in front was a woman with iron-gray hair cropped short and a scar through her eyebrow, and she looked at me the way a carpenter looks at a joint that has come loose—assessing, unimpressed, already planning the fix.

"August Ishikawa," she said. Her voice carried without effort, level as a mason's line. "You are named in a writ of surrender issued under the Edict of Purity. Your companions are named as accessories. Lay down your arms and submit to questioning, and the Crown guarantees you will not be harmed."

"Liar," Tam said softly, behind me. "There is no such guarantee in the writ. I've read the writ."

"I know you have, Archivist," the woman said, and did not look at him. "That was a mistake you are also answerable for."

My hand found my sword's grip without my deciding it should. The leather was warm. That was new. In nineteen years of carrying it—in all the years before that, watching my grandmother oil it and never draw it—that grip had been exactly the temperature of the air around it, a dead thing, a sleeping thing, a thing bound as surely as I was bound. Now it was warm as a living palm, and beneath my fingers I felt—not movement, nothing so kind as movement—a *readiness*, like a muscle tensed in sleep.

"Grandmother said never," I whispered. To myself. To her, wherever she was. To the sword. *Never outside the valley. Never speak to it. Never let it hear you want.*

"August Ishikawa." The woman took one step forward, and the five behind her moved with her, smooth as water finding its level, no orders given, none needed. "I am Sister-Captain Maren Holt. I have taken eleven gifted persons alive. I would prefer twelve. But the writ permits otherwise, and the Crown prefers otherwise, and I have learned—" here something crossed her face, quick and involuntary, some flicker of a feeling she'd buried so deep even she had forgotten its name— "not to prefer."

"Nell," I said, without turning my head. "Get behind me. Tam, the bank, get down the bank, stay low—"

"August—"

"*Go.*"

They went. I heard their boots on the boards behind me, heard Tam's breath sawing, heard Nell stumble and catch herself—and the six of them flowed apart, three left, three right, unhurried, closing the lane behind my friends the way a hand closes around a moth. Of course. Of course. Not me first. Me last, or me never—they wanted the swordspeaker whole, or they wanted her proof of death delivered properly, documented, witnessed. Everything the Order did was documentation. Tam had told me that once, white-faced, over a dying fire: *They don't burn people in rage, August. They burn them in triplicate.*

"Sister-Captain," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Your writ names me. Let the others walk, and I'll come with you."

Holt tilted her head, almost kindly. "Child, do you imagine you're negotiating?"

"No," I said. "I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"How many steps it takes you to reach me."

Something changed in her face then—the kindness folding away like a knife closing—and she raised one hand, and the six became motion, and the world became noise.

* * *

They were good. That has to be said, because everything after only makes sense if you understand how good they were. No shouting, no war cries, no wasted fury. They came in pairs, blades low, angles interlocking, each pair covering the others, and they did not look at my eyes but at my hips, my shoulders, my weight—reading me the way Sera read fugitives, except Sera read to catch and these read to *end*. Within four heartbeats I was fighting for my life on a bridge over gray water, and I was losing.

Not badly. Not immediately. My grandmother had trained me since I could stand, in a valley where the sword was never meant to sing, and what she'd built in me was foundation: footwork like bedrock, a guard that closed every door, patience hammered into bone. I gave ground board by board and made each inch cost them, and twice I turned a kill into a wound, and once I put a man into the river with a shoulder-check that would have broken a lesser fighter's rhythm entirely.

But there were six of them, and they did not tire, and they did not hurry, and they fought like men who had done this a hundred times and would do it a hundred more, and I fought like a girl who had spent her whole life being taught to hold a blade *back*.

That was the truth of it, and I felt it in every parry: nineteen years of restraint living in my wrists. Every stroke I made was a stroke my grandmother's oath had sanded down, filed blunt, wrapped in wool. I was fencing with a song in my throat and both hands clamped over my own mouth.

Then I heard Nell scream.

* * *

She had gotten ten feet down the bank. Ten feet. One of them—a lean man with a fencer's narrow face, who had peeled off from the press of me with a fluidity I hadn't clocked until too late—had simply walked to the edge of the bridge and stepped off onto the clay slope below, and drawn his blade coming down, and there was nothing between him and her. Nothing. Tam was scrambling, useless, brave, shouting words that meant nothing. Nell had turned and her back was to the water and her face—

Her face was the choirmaster's face. I'd seen it in her once before, the night she'd finally told me about the voices, the stolen ones, the children fed into her throat like coal into a furnace: a look of a person who knows exactly what is about to be taken from her, because it has been taken before.

He raised the blade.

And the warmth in my sword's grip became a *burn*, and the held breath became a voice, and the voice did not speak in words—it spoke in the only language it had ever had, which was mine, which was my grandmother's, which was every Ishikawa who had ever died holding this hilt and keeping this silence—and it said, without language, without mercy:

*Let me.*

"No," I said aloud.

He swung at Nell.

I don't remember crossing the bridge. I remember the boards, afterward—I remember that three of them split behind me, lengthwise, with a sound like ice cracking on a pond, and I did not know until much later whether my speed had broken them or something else had. I remember the fencer turning, surprised, competent, bringing his blade around in a line that should have opened my throat. And I remember my sword meeting it.

I had never heard my sword make a sound. Nineteen years. It had been the quietest object in Hollowmere, quieter than snowfall. When it rang against his blade, the note it struck was so deep and so *full* that the mist itself shuddered, that the river flattened, that every bird for a mile went up out of the reeds at once in a single ragged black sheet—and the fencer's sword did not ring in answer. It *shattered*. Steel burst along its length like frost letting go of a window, and the shards went outward, away from me, away from Nell, curving in the air as though the air itself had opinions, and the man fell backward down the clay bank clutching a hilt and a foot of ruined blade and screaming a name—not mine, not God's, just his own, over and over, like a child calling for himself.

And then the rest of it came up through me.

I have tried, in the years since, to find honest words for what happened next, and the closest I can manage is this: it was like being a cup that had spent its whole life believing it was empty, and discovering the ocean. The gift did not arrive. It *rose*—from somewhere below my ribs, below my navel, below any place I had a name for, a pressure and a heat and a terrible vast attention, and it poured up my arm and into the blade and out into the world, and suddenly I could feel all six of them. Their heartbeats. Their balance. The small betrayals of intention in their wrists a half-second before their blades moved. The world slowed to the speed of syrup and I moved through it like a needle through cloth, and I was not afraid anymore, and that frightened me more than anything else, because the fearlessness was not mine. It was borrowed. It was *old*. It belonged to every ancestor who had ever stood on a battlefield with this same warmth in their hand, and they were all looking out through my eyes, and they were all saying, in their wordless chorus: *this is what you were for.*

I disarmed the second one. I broke the third one's knee and put her down gasping. The fourth and fifth came together and I turned between them like a door swinging and their blades hit each other, and I struck the weapons from their hands—one, two, clean, precise, the strikes of someone who had trained thirty years instead of nineteen—and the sixth, Sister-Captain Maren Holt, backed away three steps and did something none of her manuals had prepared her for.

She stopped.

She stood in the middle of the bridge, in the wreck of her formation, amid the groaning and the splintered steel and the drifting ruin of the mist, and she looked at me with those tired market-stall eyes, and she said, quietly, so that only I could hear it:

"So it's true. The valley kept it asleep." And then, with something that was almost wonder and almost grief: "Do you know what they'll send now? Do you understand what you've just called down? This wasn't the hunt, girl. This was a *collection notice*. And you've just told them the vault is full."

* * *

Afterward, I knelt on the bridge boards with my palms flat against the wood and my stomach heaving over the gray water, and the gift drained out of me the way a fever breaks—leaving me wrung out, freezing, ashamed in a place I couldn't point to. The sword lay beside me on the planks. Quiet again. Warm still. Warm as a body that has recently been alive.

Nell crouched next to me. She didn't touch me. She knew, somehow, not to touch me.

"It saved me," she said. Her voice was raw. "August, it—you saved me. You don't get to look like that. You don't get to look like something shameful happened."

"Three of them are hurt," I said. My mouth tasted of copper and lightning. "I broke a woman's knee, Nell. She was somebody's daughter. She was probably somebody's mother. I felt her heartbeat the whole time. I felt it *speed up* when I came at her, and part of me—" I pressed my fist against my mouth. "Part of me liked knowing it."

"That wasn't you."

"Which part?" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's the question, isn't it? That's the whole cursed question. Grandmother said the oath keeps the gift asleep. She never said what happens when it wakes up halfway. She never said whether the thing that wakes up is *me*."

Tam came up the bank slowly, helping the fencer's abandoned prisoner—no, helping nobody; the wounded Order members had dragged themselves to the far side and were retreating, bearing their broken with them, orderly even in defeat, and I hated them a little for the dignity of it. Tam crouched on my other side and took off his spectacles and cleaned them on his sleeve with hands that would not stop shaking, and put them back on, and looked at the sword.

"It spoke to you," he said. Not a question either. Everyone was not asking me questions today.

"It didn't speak. It—" I searched. "It remembered at me."

"May I?" He reached toward the blade, stopped an inch short, waited for my nod. He didn't touch it. He hovered his fingers over the steel the way a man tests a stove. "Warm," he murmured. "Of course it's warm. August, in the restricted folios—there's a classification I could never verify, a category the archivists called *waking steel*. Blades that were named and then silenced. Bound, not blessed. The records say a waking blade doesn't sleep like a stone sleeps. It sleeps like a sleeper sleeps. It dreams. And it has been dreaming for"—he did the arithmetic silently, lips moving—"for as long as your family has been keeping it."

"Then it's been dreaming of this," I said. "Of a bridge. Of breaking a stranger's sword to keep a choirgirl breathing."

"Is that so terrible?"

I looked at Nell. She was watching me with an expression I couldn't bear—gratitude braided with guilt, because she knew what it had cost, because she had felt the note that shattered steel pass through her own chest and recognized, in it, the same terrible kinship she felt toward her stolen voices. Two instruments played by forces older than their owners.

"It's terrible because it worked," I said. "Because it was easy. Because for about ten seconds I was the strongest thing on this bridge and I have never in my life felt so little like myself, and I want it *again*, Tam. Right now, kneeling here sick over the edge, some part of me wants it again. That's what the oath was for. Not to protect the world from the gift. To protect *me* from wanting it."

Downriver, the mist was gathering again. Not on the bridge—behind us, northward, along the road we would have to take, lying across the land in long patient ribbons. Waiting to be poured again. And far off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of sense, beneath the river-sound and the wind, I felt—or heard, or dreamed—a note so deep it wasn't a sound at all, a vibration in the roots of things, like something enormous turning over in a sleep that was ending.

Nell heard it too. I watched her hear it. Her hand rose to her throat.

"August," she whispered. "What was that?"

I picked up my sword. It was cooling now, degree by degree, settling back toward stone, back toward silence, back toward the long dream. But as I slid it home into the sheath at my hip, one last warmth pulsed against my palm—brief, deliberate, unmistakable—like a hand squeezing a hand.

Like something saying: *soon.*
