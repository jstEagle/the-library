## 22. The Company Divided

The fire had burned down to its bones by the time Sera said it out loud.

She had been turning the thought over for three days, since the tollbridge, since August's gift had woken like a struck bell and thrown four Silent Order blades into the mud without touching them. Three days of watching August sleep sitting up against a birch trunk, her hand never far from the sword, her face in slumber looking younger and more frightened than anything Sera had seen on a battlefield. Three days of Tam scribbling in his stolen archive of a mind, of Nell humming fragments under her breath that made the fire lean toward her like a listener.

Three days was long enough. Sera had been a hunt-captain for eleven years, and she knew what happened to problems you carried too long: they grew teeth.

"Kill him," she said.

The word dropped into the clearing like a stone into a well. Nell stopped humming mid-note. Tam's pen scratched to a halt. August opened her eyes—not with a start, not with surprise, but slowly, the way a person wakes when they have been waiting for something bad to arrive.

"Say the rest of it," August said.

"The king." Sera kept her voice level, the voice she'd used for a decade to deliver orders she didn't always believe in. She'd gotten good at that voice. It was the most useful thing the Inquisition had ever given her, and she hated it more than any scar. "The Vessel-of-Crowns wears him like a coat. You break the oath, you unbind the lock, and then what? You fight it? On its ground, in its palace, with every blade of the Silent Order between you and the throne room?" She shook her head. "You don't fight it. You kill the man it's wearing, and it dies with him. Clean. Quick. One death instead of a kingdom's worth."

"You don't know that it dies with him." Tam's voice came thin from the far side of the fire. He hadn't looked up from his notes, but his pen had stopped moving, which meant he was paying very close attention indeed. "Nineteen years it's been rooted in him. We don't know how deep those roots go. We don't know if it needs the body at all anymore, or if the body is just—furniture."

"Then we find out," Sera said. "But we plan for the clean version. Because the clean version is the only one where everyone at this fire is still breathing at the end of it."

August stood up.

It was not a dramatic motion. That was the thing about August Ishikawa that Sera had learned, slowly, the way you learn the grain of wood: everything in her was quiet until it wasn't. She rose the way water rises, inevitable, unhurried, and she looked down at Sera across the coals with eyes that held the grey of the valley she'd lost.

"His name is Aldric," August said.

"I know his name."

"Do you?" August's voice stayed soft, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "Because I've been listening to you for three days, Sera, and what I hear is *the vessel*. The host. The problem wearing a crown. I don't hear a man who was nineteen years old when someone put that thing on his head."

"He was nineteen when he put it on himself." Tam said it quietly, apologetically, the way he said all true things, as if truth were a debt he was ashamed of owing. "We've been through this. The letters. He knew what the crown was. He wore it anyway to keep it from the Duke of Carreth's hands."

"And that makes it better?" Sera rounded on him. "That makes it *noble*? A boy sacrifices himself to a parasite and now we're all supposed to kneel to the sacrifice? I've spent my whole career watching people do monstrous things for noble reasons. It's the noble reasons that scare me. Nobody burns a village over greed. They burn it over righteousness."

"Is that what you think I am?" August asked. "Righteous?"

Sera looked at her—at the sword across her knees, the sword whose name she still would not speak even here, even now; at the burn-scar on her forearm from the tollbridge, where her own gift had come through her like a flood through a broken door; at the ash of Hollowmere that Sera sometimes thought she could still smell in August's hair, though surely the rain had washed it away weeks ago.

"No," Sera said finally. "I think you're kind. That's what frightens me. Kind people walk into traps because they can't stand to leave anyone behind. I've buried kind people. I've *been* the one who buried them."

* * *

They argued until the moon crossed the sky and began its descent, and the argument did not so much end as exhaust itself, the way storms exhaust themselves, leaving wreckage behind to survey in the morning light.

Sera laid it out the way she'd lay out a battle plan, because it was the only language she trusted herself in. The palace had nine gates. The Silent Order numbered perhaps two hundred, concentrated in the capital since the purge began. The entity—if Tam's research held—was strongest at the seat of power, strongest in the king's body, strongest in the deep hours before dawn. Every variable favored the enemy. Every path to the throne room ran through slaughter.

"Unless there's no throne room to reach," Sera finished. "A knife in the dark. I've done it before. I'm not proud of it, but I've done it, and I'm the only one here who has. Give me a week inside the capital and the whole question ends. No unbinding. No breaking your oath. No marking yourself as its next candidate. You go home, August. You rebuild whatever's left of Hollowmere. You live."

"That's the second time you've offered me my life back," August said. "Like a gift."

"Isn't it?"

"It's a debt," August said. "Every life you offer me is bought with his. And I'd be spending it, Sera. Not you. You'd slide the knife, but I'd be the reason. My grandmother died so that oath would hold. Everyone in that valley lived their whole small lives inside it—the restraint, the forgetting, all of it—so that one thing in this world would stay locked. And you're asking me to trade that for convenience."

"For survival."

"For *convenience*," August repeated, and her voice cracked at last, just once, like ice underfoot. "Do you want to know what I heard in the blade? At the tollbridge, when it half-woke and spoke in her voice?" She didn't wait for an answer. "I heard that the oath was always meant to be broken. Once. By choice. By an Ishikawa who chose it, freely, with open eyes. That's the last lock, Sera—not the words, the *choosing*. If I send you to murder him in his sleep, the binding might unravel anyway, or it might not, but either way I'll have made the choice a murderer makes instead of the choice my family spent two hundred years keeping possible for me."

"And if choosing means dying?" Sera asked. "If the widow's warning holds, and the moment you break that oath, the Vessel-of-Crowns turns and looks at you and sees its next house? You'll be walking into its arms with your gift blazing like a beacon. It will *want* you. Everything about you will be exactly what it wants."

"Then it should be afraid," August said, "of how much it wants me. Because I don't break oaths by accident, Captain. I break them on purpose. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Nell made a small sound. Sera turned. The girl had been silent so long Sera had almost forgotten her, curled in her cloak with her knees drawn up, but her face in the firelight was wet.

"She's right," Nell whispered. Then, louder, as if surprising herself: "She's right. I've heard—I've *been*—" Her hands went to her throat, the gesture Sera had learned meant the stolen voices, the choir children fed into her like coal into a furnace. "I know what it is to be worn by something that isn't yours. Nineteen years. He's been singing someone else's song for nineteen years and nobody, not one person in that whole cathedral of a kingdom, stopped to ask if he wanted to stop." Her chin came up, and for a moment Sera saw the girl who had made grown men weep with a single wrong note. "Killing him doesn't free him. It just silences him. I won't help silence him. I've heard too much silencing."

"Nell—" Sera began.

"Don't." The girl's voice rang, and the fire guttered, and every hair on Sera's arms stood up. "Don't you *dare* tell me I don't understand. I understand better than anyone at this fire."

* * *

Morning came grey and cold, and the company had become two companies.

It happened without ceremony, which was somehow the worst of it. Sera had watched armies fracture before—over pay, over fear, over a bad winter—and there was always a moment, some shouted insult or drawn steel, some clean break you could point to afterward. This was nothing like that. This was Tam folding his maps with unnecessary care and saying, mildly, that he believed the counter-oath research required the king alive as a reference point, which was true, and also a shield, and they both knew it. This was Nell standing and moving her bedroll six feet to sit behind August, six feet that might as well have been six miles. This was the fire burning in the middle of camp like a border drawn in embers.

"I need to know," Sera said, when the sun was fully up and there was no more pretending the night had been weather. "Before we take another step north. Which way does this company march?"

August looked at her for a long moment. Around them the birch trees stood in their pale ranks, dripping last night's rain, indifferent as witnesses.

"We march together," August said. "To Greyford, at least. After that—"

"After that we're enemies," Sera said flatly. "Say it. Don't dress it up. If I go for the knife and you go for the oath, we're working opposite sides of the same war, and I need to know if you'll raise that sword against me."

The silence stretched. Tam had gone very still. Nell's hand had found August's shoulder.

And Sera watched August wrestle with it—watched her actually *wrestle*, jaw tight, the morning wind pulling strands of dark hair loose from her braid—and understood, with a clarity that felt like a wound opening, that August Ishikawa did not know the answer. That the question had never occurred to her, because in August's country—the country of oaths and chosen families and people who burned rather than betray—you did not raise a sword against your own.

"I won't fight you," August said at last. "But I won't let you reach him either. If it comes to that, Sera, I'll stand in your way. And I'm sorry. I'm sorry that loving people means sometimes standing in their way, but I think—" her voice caught, steadied— "I think that's what love *is*, when it's honest. Not clearing the road for someone. Standing in it when they're walking somewhere that will destroy them."

"You sound like her," Sera said. "Your grandmother. The way Tam describes her."

"I hope so."

Sera shouldered her pack. The straps bit into old scars, familiar as prayer. Eleven years in the Inquisition had taught her that every company eventually divided—that loyalty was a rope, and ropes frayed, and the wise soldier counted the strands before trusting her weight to them. She had counted. Two against two, with the truth hanging somewhere between them like the mist off the birches.

But walking at the rear of the column an hour later, watching August and Nell move ahead with their heads bent together, watching Tam trot to keep pace with them, already talking, already forgiven, already *folded in*—watching all of it, Sera felt the rope hold in a way no counting could explain.

They were still one company on the road. Whatever waited for them in the capital would decide the rest.

It was Tam, near midday, who came riding back along the line with a look on his face that Sera had learned to dread—the archivist's look, the look of a man holding paper that weighed more than lead.

"You need to see this," he said. "Both of you. All of us. There's a letter in the king's correspondence I missed—dated three days before the coronation. It's addressed to the Ishikawa family." He swallowed. "August—it's addressed to *your grandmother*. And it says, 'When the time comes, I will ask you to break it. Do not refuse me twice.'"

The column stopped. Ahead, August had already turned in the saddle, her face gone white as the birches.

"How long have you known?" Sera asked quietly.

Tam met her eyes, and for the first time since she'd known him, the disgraced archivist looked less ashamed of his secrets than afraid of them.

"Since last night," he said. "Since the argument. I only just understood what I was reading."
