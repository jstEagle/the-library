## 23. Nell's Choice

The fire had gone down to its red arithmetic by the time Nell slipped away from camp.

She counted her steps as she went—twenty-two to the edge of the light, thirty more to the fallen beech that leaned over the stream like a tired old man—and she counted because counting kept her from listening. Behind her, the company slept in two camps now, though they had not moved their blankets apart. That was the cruelty of it. They still shared the same circle of stones, the same kettle, the same small economies of a traveling family: Sera sharpening both her own blades and Tam's little knife without being asked; Tam reading aloud in the evenings even when no one answered him; August always leaving the first portion of whatever they cooked where Nell could reach it.

They were not speaking of the thing that divided them. They spoke around it the way water speaks around a stone, and every night the stone grew larger, and every night Nell lay awake between them and felt the current pull.

*Kill him,* Sera said. *End it with him.*

*No,* said August, in that low voice like a blade being drawn slowly. *I will not make a murderer of myself for the crown's convenience.*

And Nell, who had spent six years learning that voices could carry more than words, had heard something underneath August's refusal that August herself perhaps had not: not certainty. Fear. The fear of a woman standing at the edge of a well she has dreamed about all her life, knowing that once she leans over it, she cannot unlean.

Nell reached the beech tree and sat down on its root, and the cold of the ground came up through her skirt and settled into her bones like an honest guest.

The stream ran thin and quick over its stones. Above the trees, the moon was three days off full, a coin bitten at one edge. She opened her mouth to test the acoustics—the way she always did, the way the choirmaster had trained into her until it was less habit than breathing—and stopped.

Because here was the truth she had been carrying for four days now, folded small beneath her ribs like a letter she did not dare open:

She knew how to carry August's oath-song.

She had known since the tollbridge, since the night the bounty hunters came out of the dark and August's sword had answered her panic like a hound answering a whistle. She had felt it happen—not heard it, *felt* it, the way you feel a door open somewhere in your chest and wind come through from a room you didn't know you owned. Her voice had gone looking for August's gift the way water goes looking for low ground. It hadn't asked permission. It never asked permission. Every voice the choirmaster had fed into her, all those stolen children's voices sleeping in her throat like coals banked under ash—they had woken all at once, hungry, and found something worth singing to.

And in the days since, lying awake between two camps that would not speak, Nell had done the arithmetic that singers do without wanting to.

An oath is a song. August had said so herself, around the fire, on the night she confessed the terms of her inheritance: *if I speak my sword's name outside the valley, the binding begins to unravel.* But a swordspeaker's voice was made for steel, not for air. August could break the oath alone, yes—but alone, half-heard, half-carried, the breaking would be ragged, and ragged things tear. Tam had said it too, in his careful archivist's way, tapping the old letters he'd decoded: *the rite wants witnesses, and it wants a carrier. A voice to hold the oath while the blade releases it. Otherwise the oath doesn't break clean. It shatters.*

Nell had a voice that could carry anything. That was the whole horror of her life. She had carried wards she never consented to, carried grief out of grown men in a cathedral nave until they wept into their hands, carried candles down to guttering with a single held note. Her throat was a road every kind of magic had traveled, and none of it had ever been hers.

But this—this could be hers. This one thing, chosen.

If she trained for it. If she learned to hold August's oath the way a cup holds wine, steady, to the brim, without spilling. If she built the carrying-shape note by note in secret, in the dark, before anyone could tell her what it would cost.

And she knew what it would cost. She had known since the first night she tried, here at this tree, four nights ago. She had sung a single phrase of the old binding-cadence Tam had transcribed—one phrase, eight notes—and the stolen voices in her throat had risen toward it like moths, and for a moment she had felt them *burn*. Not hurt. Burn. The difference mattered. Hurt was what happened to you. Burning was what you became fuel for. The choir-children's voices were candle-fat, and the oath-song was flame, and there was only so much fat in any lamp.

When she finished the phrase, her breath had come back tasting of copper, and her own voice—her small true voice, the one the choirmaster had buried deepest—had been very quiet for almost a day.

So. There it was. The letter, opened.

Nell looked at the stream for a long time.

Then she stood, planted her feet the way the choirmaster had taught her—*rooted, rooted, the sound comes up from the earth or it comes from nowhere*—and began to practice.

* * *

She practiced every night for nine nights.

She learned the shape of the work the way you learn the shape of a wound: intimately, unwillingly, and all at once. The oath-song was not a melody, exactly. Tam's transcription called it a cadence, and that was truer—it was a structure, a load-bearing frame of intervals, the musical equivalent of an arch. You did not sing it so much as build it, and then you held it up while something heavier than music passed beneath.

Her stolen voices each had their own relationship to it. That was the strangest part, and the saddest. The deep boy's voice—he had been called Pilo, she remembered suddenly, one night, out of nowhere, the memory arriving like a fish surfacing—Pilo's range sat perfectly under the cadence's foundation. He wanted to carry it. She could feel his eagerness in her chest, a warmth like a child pressing close to a fire. The woman's voice with the silver top—she had never learned that one's name; the choirmaster had taken her before Nell joined the choir—that voice feared the song, and trembled against it, and Nell had to spend whole nights simply singing her quiet things, lullabies, until the trembling eased.

*You are not instruments,* Nell told them, one night, her lips barely moving. *I know. I know what he did to us. But this one is ours. This one we choose.* And whether they understood or only felt the intention, the silver voice stopped shaking after that.

The cost was real and it was measurable, and Nell measured it with a singer's terrible precision. After twenty minutes of sustained work, her own voice thinned to a whisper. After forty, she tasted blood. Once, pushing through the third movement of the cadence—the part where the carrier must rise while the oath falls, a crossing like two ships passing in a narrow channel—she pushed too hard, and everything in her throat went white and silent, and she knelt at the roots of the beech tree for the better part of an hour, breathing steam into the cold air, waiting to find out if she would ever speak again.

She could. Barely. But when she finally whispered a scale, three of the stolen voices did not answer.

Three lamps, burned to nothing. Three children whose last ember she had spent in practice, on a riverbank, in the dark, with no one to witness.

Nell sat with that for a long time. The stream talked its small talk. Somewhere an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

*I'm sorry,* she whispered. And then, because she was honest even in the ruins of her own throat: *I'm not sorry enough to stop.*

Because here was the other arithmetic, the one underneath. If August broke the oath alone, raggedly, the binding wouldn't just shatter—it would shatter *toward* August. Tam's letters were clear. The Vessel-of-Crowns, freed of its lock, would leap for the nearest suitable vessel, and the nearest suitable vessel was the last oath-bound line, and the last oath-bound line was a woman who left the first portion of every meal where Nell could reach it. A clean breaking needed a carrier to hold the door open while August walked through it. And a carrier who held the door open stood in the doorway.

The song would burn her out. Maybe not all the way. Maybe. Tam had found one reference—a fragment, a margin note in a dead cantor's hand—to a carrier who survived, and the fragment suggested survival depended on how much of the carrier's voice was truly her own. *What is borrowed burns; what is born endures.*

Almost none of Nell's voice was born. Six years of the choirmaster's feedings had seen to that. She was mostly lamp-fat herself.

But *almost* was not *none*, and Nell had decided, on the fourth night, kneeling in the wet grass with her voice gone white, that she would spend every borrowed voice she had, gladly, if it meant the born one—the small true one, the one that had once, before the cathedral, made her mother laugh in the kitchen—would be the last coal standing when the song ended. She would burn the stolen children home first. All of them. Pilo and the silver woman and the others, spent in service instead of stored in silence, and if that emptied her nearly to nothing, then nothing was a price, and prices were paid, and she had been paying other people's prices her whole life.

This price would be hers. Chosen. Offered in secret, like a gift left on a doorstep.

* * *

On the eleventh night, August followed her.

Nell knew it before she heard the footsteps—knew it the way she knew weather, through her skin, because August's presence had a pressure to it, a gravity, and the night behind Nell grew heavier by degrees. She was mid-cadence, second movement, holding the arch up with her eyes shut and her fists clenched at her sides, and she made the choice in the space of one held note: finish, or falter.

She finished. She brought the phrase down to its resting tone as cleanly as she had ever done anything, and only then did she turn, wiping her mouth with the back of her hand, unsurprised to find August standing at the edge of the trees with the firelight gone from behind her and the moon making her look carved rather than human.

"How long," August said.

It wasn't an accusation. That was somehow worse. It was the voice of a woman asking how long a wound has been festering under a bandage she'd been admiring.

"Eleven nights," Nell said. Her voice was rough from the work, sanded down to its grain. "Twelve, tomorrow."

August came closer. She moved the way she always moved, economical, a woman who had been taught that every motion was a debt. She looked at the beech tree, at the worn place on the root where Nell had sat eleven nights running, at the scuffed earth where her feet had planted themselves over and over. Then she looked at Nell's mouth, and Nell saw her see it—the fine split at the corner of her lip that hadn't healed, the blood she hadn't wiped away thoroughly enough.

"You're training to carry the oath." Not a question either. August Ishikawa did not waste questions on things she already knew. "Tam's cadence. The carrier's part."

"Yes."

"That song will eat you." August's jaw tightened. "You don't know what you're—"

"I know exactly what I'm doing." And there it was, the cathedral rising in her, six years of being handled and tuned and fed upon, and Nell felt her voice go hard and clear, harder than it had any right to be at a whisper. "That's the difference between me and everyone else in my life, August. Everyone else decided what my voice was for. The choirmaster decided. The wards decided. The cathedral decided. I am deciding. One time. For one song. Mine."

The stream filled the silence. August stood very still, and Nell watched her wrestle with it—watched the refusal move through her face like weather, the instinct to protect, to forbid, to stand between smaller people and larger fires. She had seen that same weather on the widow's face, on Sera's. It was the weather of people who loved by shielding.

"You'll die," August said quietly.

"I might live." Nell stepped closer. Close enough to see the exhaustion in August, the nineteen-years-of-it living in a nineteen-year-old woman. "There's a fragment, in Tam's papers. About carriers. What's borrowed burns; what's born endures. I have one voice that's mine. One. If I spend all the others—if I give them this, give them a purpose instead of a prison—maybe the one that's mine survives. And maybe it doesn't. But if I *don't* sing, and you break the oath alone, the binding tears toward you, and the Vessel leaps, and everything—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she pushed through anyway— "everything I just spent becomes nothing. So it isn't a choice between me dying and me living. It's a choice between me possibly dying and you certainly being lost."

August closed her eyes.

For a moment she looked so young, so unbearably young, a girl who had grown up sharpening a sword she wasn't allowed to name, and Nell understood suddenly that August had been doing the same arithmetic she had. That every night Nell had crept away to practice, August had lain awake doing sums of her own, and the sums had all ended in the same terrible column.

"I won't order you not to," August said at last. "I don't get to. None of us got to choose our oaths. I won't take yours away." Her eyes opened, and they were wet, and she did not wipe them. "But you train where I can hear you. From tonight. If you fall, I want to be close enough to catch you."

Nell laughed—a small broken sound, surprised out of her. "That's not secret anymore, then."

"No," August said. "I suppose it isn't." She hesitated, and then, awkwardly, the way a person reaches for something they have no practice reaching for, she put her hand on Nell's shoulder. It was heavy and warm and slightly callused, a sword-hand's weight. "Sing me the second movement," she said. "I want to learn what it asks of you. If you're going to carry my oath, I should know its weight in someone else's arms."

So Nell planted her feet in the scuffed earth, and August stood beside her under the bitten moon, and Nell sang the arch up into the cold air—and this time, with August's gift awake beside it, the stolen voices rose toward the cadence like a congregation, and the sound that came out of her was larger than her body, larger than the night, and far away to the south, in a city none of them could yet see, something wearing a crown turned its attention northward, and listened.
