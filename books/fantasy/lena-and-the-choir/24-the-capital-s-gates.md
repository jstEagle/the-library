## 24. The Capital's Gates

There is a particular smell to a city that has learned to be afraid, and Tam Osgood knew it the way a physician knows fever by the heat of a brow. It was not smoke, though there was smoke in it—chimney-smoke, tallow-smoke, the ordinary exhalations of ten thousand hearths. It was not blood, though the gutters had been scrubbed recently enough that the scrubbing itself told its story. It was something subtler: a hush beneath the noise, a carefulness in the way people walked, as though every street were a room where someone might be sleeping.

He had lived in Corvane for eleven years before his disgrace. He had loved it the way scholars love cities—for its archives, its bridges, its habit of keeping everything, even its cruelties, in writing. Now he came back to it through the western gate road with a forged travel writ in his coat, an archivist's stolen seal-ring on his thumb, and three fugitives arranged around him like notes on a staff, and he found that he had forgotten how heavy the city's air could be. Or perhaps it had never been this heavy before. Perhaps fear had weight now, and the whole capital had been set upon a scale.

"Stop fidgeting," Sera murmured beside him. She rode at the cart's left flank in a plain wool cloak that did nothing whatsoever to disguise her posture; she sat a horse like a drawn blade. "You look like a man rehearsing a confession."

"I am always rehearsing a confession," Tam said. "It's a professional condition."

Ahead of them, walking at the head of their little procession because she refused to ride when she could walk, Nell was singing.

That was the whole of the plan, such as it was—the plan Tam had built out of paper, ink, and desperation. A traveling choir, bound for the capital to sing the autumn votives at the Cathedral of the Ninefold Flame. Choirs traveled constantly; the war against the gifted had emptied cathedral benches of trained voices, and the choirmasters were so hungry for singers that they asked few questions. Tam had forged letters of introduction from a choirmaster in Greyford who had died of winter fever two years past and was therefore admirably discreet. He had invented a patron, a route, a repertoire. He had given them a name—the Vesper Company—and sewn it into their collars where a bored gate-sergeant might find it if he looked.

The only thing he had not been able to forge was the singing itself.

And so Nell sang, walking backward half the time so she could face the cart, her voice rising over the creak of the wheels in a plainsong hymn about the harvest and the mercy of the flame. She had chosen it carefully, Tam knew. Every song in their traveling book had been vetted twice: once for orthodoxy, once for safety. Nothing that made candles gutter. Nothing that made grown men weep. She kept her gift folded down inside her voice like a blade wrapped in linen, and she sang only with the part of herself that was simply a girl who loved music, and even that part was beautiful enough to turn heads along the road.

Which was, of course, the danger. Heads turning was the one thing they could not afford.

"She's doing it again," August said quietly from the cart bed, where she lay among the luggage under a canvas awning with her sword wrapped in sacking across her knees like a sick child. "People are stopping to listen."

"People are *allowed* to listen," Sera said. "That's rather the point. Nobody looks hard at a crowd that's already looking somewhere else."

Tam watched the listeners himself, because watching was his function in this company—he had appointed himself to it without discussion, the way he had once appointed himself keeper of the king's correspondence, and both appointments had been accepted by no one and honored absolutely. There was a miller's boy standing open-mouthed by the milestone. Two women with baskets had stopped mid-stride. An old man had taken off his hat, which was either piety or memory; Tam could not tell which, and either one worried him.

"Nell," he called softly. "The bridge verse. Take it down a third."

She heard him—she always heard him, the first time, which was more than he deserved—and the melody dipped obediently lower, closer to speech, close enough to be ignored. The miller's boy lost interest. The women walked on. The old man put his hat back on and became merely old again.

*Good,* Tam thought, and then, immediately, *God forgive me.* Because what he had just done was teach a girl born with a miracle to make it small, and he recognized the lesson, and he hated that he recognized it. He had spent six years in the royal archive copying out the Edict's hidden clauses in cipher, and every clause came down to that same instruction, delivered by law instead of by archivist: *make it small. Make yourself small. Be nothing, and you may continue to exist.*

The city rose up ahead of them out of the river haze, and Tam felt it the way he always had—a hand closing gently around his heart. The walls had been white once. They were gray now, streaked with rain-rot, crowned with the new watchtowers the Inquisition had raised in the last two years, each one flying the black pennant of the Silent Order. Between the towers, the old banners of the crown still hung, gold stag on green, but they hung oddly, Tam thought, the way clothes hang on a body that has changed shape underneath.

Nineteen years, he reminded himself. Nineteen years the thing in the crown had worn its king like a glove. And the whole city went about its business beneath it, buying bread, arguing prices, hanging laundry, never once asking why the king had not aged kindly, why his edicts had grown teeth, why the word *purity* had begun to appear in laws written by men who had never used it before.

"They'll ask for the writ," Sera said. "Then they'll ask for the roster. Then one of them will want to hear a sample of the singing, because the Order has been training gate-guards to listen for wrongness. I've seen the directive."

"You've seen a great many directives," Tam said.

"That's what being a hunt-captain is. You read directives until you stop sleeping." She said it flatly, without self-pity, and Tam marveled, not for the first time, at the economy of her guilt. His own sprawled across him like a fallen library. Hers fit in a single sentence and weighed more.

August shifted under the canvas. "If they ask me to sing," she said, "I'm going to be a problem."

"You are not going to be asked to sing," Tam said. "You are going to be a woman with a bad chest, traveling under blankets, coughing at intervals. I have written you a cough. It is very convincing. It has three stages."

"I don't want a three-stage cough."

"It's a very good cough," Tam insisted. "I based it on my uncle."

The gates came up to meet them, and Tam's mouth went dry in the old familiar way, the scholar's way, the liar's way. He had crossed these gates a thousand times with nothing to declare but books. Today he carried a dead man's signature, a living lie, and the counter-oath copied out in his own hand, folded twice and sewn into the lining of his coat, and if any of it failed—if one seal was questioned, one name remembered wrongly—then everything ended here, under the gray walls, in front of the miller's boy and the women with baskets and whatever remained of the city he had once loved.

The line moved forward. Nell's voice held steady. Somewhere behind them, hooves rang on the causeway, and Tam did not let himself turn around to see whether they were soldiers.

* * *

The gate-sergeant was younger than Tam expected, and tired in a way that orders cannot fix. He had the writ in his hands and was reading it with the expression of a man reading a letter from someone he owes money to.

"The Vesper Company," he said. "Out of Greyford."

"By way of Marrow Bridge and the fen road," Tam said smoothly. "We took the long way. The direct roads have been—" he gestured delicately at the world— "unsettling."

The sergeant grunted. Behind him, two guards in Order black stood at the inner archway, and they were not tired; they were attentive, in the way of men who have been taught that attentiveness is a virtue and have not yet learned it is also a hunger. One of them was watching Nell. Not her face. Her throat.

"Roster," the sergeant said.

Tam handed it over. Four names, four descriptions, all of them almost true. Almost-true was the archivist's craft: a lie built entirely of truths rearranged. *Nellis Arden, contralto, twenty.* True, except for everything it omitted. *Sera Vane, escort, hired at Greyford.* True, in the sense that Sera had been hired, once, by the crown, to do precisely the opposite of what she was doing now. *Tamsin Osgood, company manager.* He had argued for a woman's name on principle—it threw off description-based searches—but Sera had vetoed it on the grounds that he could not grow a beard convincingly and would therefore spend the entire journey being asked uncomfortable questions.

The sergeant read. The Order men listened. And Nell, bless her, understood without being told that this was the moment, and began to sing again—softly, to the queue behind them, some little round about a sparrow and a thresher's floor, the kind of thing that makes waiting feel less like waiting. The queue relaxed. The sergeant relaxed. Even the attentive guard's eyes drifted, briefly, from her throat to her face, and something in his expression softened, and Tam thought: *there, that's the crack in the wall, that's the whole country in one man's face—they are hunting us and we are still singing to them, and they do not know which impulse to obey.*

"Sample," the sergeant said anyway, because the directive required it. "Just—something short. For the record."

Nell sang four bars of the harvest hymn, unaccompanied, pitched low, her gift folded away, and it was lovely and entirely innocent and Tam's heart broke a little at how well she had learned to be harmless.

The sergeant nodded, stamped the writ with a leaden thunk, and waved them through. "Votive season," he said, almost apologetically. "Cathedral's short of voices. You'll be welcome, I expect."

"We're all short of voices," Tam said, before he could stop himself.

The sergeant looked at him for a moment too long. Then he waved the next cart forward, and the Vesper Company rolled under the arch, between the Order men, into the city, and the shadow of the gate passed over them like a held breath finally released.

Tam did not exhale until they were three streets in.

* * *

Corvane in autumn was worse than Corvane in his memory, and his memory had been cruel enough.

They took the cart down Tanner's Row and across the Weepers' Bridge, following routes Tam had chosen weeks ago from maps he still knew better than his own handwriting—wide streets, good sightlines, no culs-de-sac, no markets where a crowd could close around them. The city performed its ordinary life around them with terrible diligence. Fishmongers shouted. Children ran hoops. A crier was calling the day's notices at the corner of Bellwether Street, and Tam made Sera slow the cart so he could listen, because criers were the archive of the common people and he trusted them more than he trusted most documents.

"—by order of His Majesty, curfew shall ring at the ninth bell in all quarters within the walls. All persons found abroad after curfew without writ shall be detained for questioning. By order of the Office of Purity, householders are reminded of their duty to report unregistered gifts, manifestations, and irregular devotions. Rewards are provided. Reporting is a sacrament." A pause, the rustle of paper. "Grain is up twopence at the King's Market. The Cathedral of the Ninefold Flame announces the autumn votives, all faithful welcome."

*All faithful welcome,* Tam thought. *Even the ones you're burning.*

Beside him, he felt Sera go very still. *Reporting is a sacrament.* He watched her jaw work once, the muscle jumping beneath the skin, and he understood that she had helped write the world that sentence belonged to. He did not say anything. There was nothing to say that she had not already said to herself, at night, in the saddle, in the space between one mile and the next.

On the chapel steps near the Weepers' Bridge, a woman was being questioned by two men in gray—not Order black, but the civil purity officers, the cheaper instrument. She was holding a child's hand. The child was perhaps seven. Tam caught only fragments as they passed: *"...the neighbor says the lamps... the neighbor says when she cries..."* The woman's face was the face of every person Tam had ever read about in a deposition, the face that comes after the door has already been broken once and will be broken again. The child stared at the hoop-runner children in the street as though they lived in another country.

Nobody stopped. Nobody looked. The street flowed around the scene like water around a stone, and Tam understood that this was the hush he had smelled from the road: not silence, but the sound of ten thousand people deciding, every hour, not to see.

Nell had stopped singing.

She started again a moment later, and her voice when it came was smaller, and Tam grieved for it, and made himself do nothing, because doing something was how companies got captured and girls got burned, and the arithmetic of survival had never once cared about his grief.

"There," Sera said quietly, nodding toward a narrow lane off Cordwainer Street. "Is that it?"

It was. The sign above the door showed a candle and a closed book, painted by someone with more devotion than skill. The Guttered Wick, it read. Rooms for quiet travelers. Tam had found it in an old directory, cross-referenced it against three years of archive requisitions, and confirmed what he'd hoped: its proprietor appeared in the records exactly nowhere, which in Corvane meant either irrelevance or excellent discretion, and discretion was the only currency Tam had left to spend.

The stable yard behind the inn received their cart between a hay-wain and a hearse, which Tam chose to find funny rather than ominous. A broad woman with flour to her elbows met them at the door, looked at the four of them—one coughing woman, one contralto, one escort with a soldier's eyes, one soft-bellied manager with ink stains—and said, "Vespers?"

"Company," Tam said. "The Vesper Company. We wrote ahead."

"You wrote ahead," she repeated, in a tone suggesting that no one had ever written ahead to the Guttered Wick in its history, and that she intended to preserve this tradition. But she stepped aside and let them in, and the door closed behind them with the soft finality of a page turned.

* * *

The room she gave them was one room, which meant one room for four people and a sword, and it smelled of lamp oil and dried lavender laid over older smells the lavender had lost the war against. August unwrapped the sword from its sacking the moment the bolt was shot, and Tam noticed—as he had been noticing for days—that she did it first, before her own comfort, before food, before sitting down. The blade came free with a whisper, and the lamplight moved along it strangely, and Tam made himself look away. He was an archivist. He knew what it meant when a primary source made your skin prickle. It meant the source was awake.

"Sera and I take the door watch till midnight," August said. "Nell sleeps. Tam—"

"Tam works," Tam finished. "Yes. I know my liturgy."

He spread his materials on the table by the window: the false ledger of the Vesper Company, genuine-looking because it contained genuine entries; the map of the cathedral close he had drawn from memory and checked against Nell's memories, which were sharper than his, because she had sung there; and beneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth, the pages that had cost him his name, his position, and very nearly his neck—the king's letters, the young king's letters, the ones written before the crown, full of a boy's clear-eyed terror and courage: *I know what it is. I will wear it so that no one worse may.*

Nineteen years ago. And the letters still true, and the boy still wearing it, and somewhere across the city, in the palace above the river, the man who had been that boy was sitting in a throne room with something else looking out through his eyes, and did not know, could not know, that help was coming to him in the form of a forged choir writ and a girl who sang wrong.

"Tam." Nell's voice, low, from the cot where she was supposed to be sleeping. "You're doing the thing where you stare at papers like they've insulted you."

"They frequently have." He glanced over. In the lamplight her face was pale and composed, and only her hands betrayed her, pleating the blanket edge into small sharp folds. "You should sleep. Tomorrow we scout the cathedral close. Day after, the votives begin, and the crypt access opens with them."

"I know the schedule. I helped write the schedule." She sat up. "I keep thinking about the crypt-choir."

Tam's pen stopped.

"The children," she said. "Under the cathedral. Everyone in the choir knows the story—the Founders' Voices, the eternal anthem, the wards that never sleep. They tell it like a fairy tale. Little choristers whisper about it in the dormitory. *If you sing perfectly, maybe you'll be chosen.*" Her voice did not rise, and that was the worst of it; it went flatter, quieter, the way ice goes quiet before it takes the weight. "I sang for that. For nine years I practiced so that maybe I'd be *chosen*, and now I know what chosen means, and tomorrow I'm going to walk into that building and smile at the choirmaster who fed those voices into me, and I need you to tell me, Tam, honestly, as the man with all the papers—" her eyes came up and caught his— "are they still singing? Down there. Are they still alive?"

Every answer he owned was a document, and every document was insufficient. He set down the pen.

"I don't know," he said. "The requisitions for the crypt level continued through last spring. Lamp oil, bread, water. Bread and water, Nell. Not for corpses."

She nodded slowly, absorbing it, and Tam watched her decide—watched the decision move through her like weather—that whatever waited under the cathedral, she was going to go down and face it, and that no one in this room would be able to stop her, and that he would follow her anyway, because that was what he did now. That was the whole of his redemption, if he had one: he followed.

From the doorway, where she had appeared without sound, Sera said, "Lamp on the landing just went dark. Someone pinched it out, didn't blow it. And there's a man across the yard who's been mending the same boot since we arrived."

The room changed. August was on her feet with the sword bare and low before Tam had fully processed the sentence. Nell slid off the cot without a sound. Tam blew out their own lamp with a steady hand he was proud of, and in the sudden dark they all listened to the city: the far bells, the near wind, the creak of the hearse-wain below, and—faint, deliberate, unmistakable—footsteps on the stairs. Unhurried. Unashamed. Counting doors.

Sera drew steel without a whisper. August's blade caught the window-light and gave it back wrong, and Tam pressed himself against the wall with the king's letters flat against his chest, and thought, with the strange clarity of the condemned: *we came in through the gates. Something came in behind us.*

The footsteps stopped outside their door.

Three slow knocks. Then a voice, quiet and courteous and utterly without warmth, speaking through the wood:

"Tamsin Osgood. The Silent Order thanks you for coming home."
