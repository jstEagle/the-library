## 26. Tam's Interrogation

Pain, Tam Osgood had always believed, was a language. He had read enough interrogation transcripts in the royal archive to know its grammar: the escalating questions, the pauses between blows, the way a body eventually began to translate itself into whatever dialect its questioners demanded. He had catalogued confessions the way other men catalogued love letters, and he had told himself, all those years in the cool dark of the stacks, that understanding a thing was the same as being safe from it.

He understood it now. Understanding had not saved him.

The room they kept him in had once been a wine cellar beneath the Tollbridge garrison—stone walls sweating with river damp, a single lamp burning tallow that smelled of old fat and something sweeter underneath, something Tam did not want to identify. The ceiling was low enough that the inquisitor's hooded shadow bent against it when she moved. She had introduced herself as Sister Merren of the Silent Order, though she was anything but silent; her voice was soft and patient and never rose above the register of a woman reading scripture to an invalid.

"Again," she said. "Where is the swordspeaker going?"

Tam hung from his wrists. They had strung him up hours ago—or days; time down here moved like water through silt—and his shoulders had passed through agony into a strange ringing numbness that he knew, from the archive, was worse than pain. It meant the pain would come back with interest.

"I told you," he rasped. "I don't know."

"You are an archivist." Sister Merren turned the lamp's key, and the flame swelled, and the shadows on the wall leaned toward him like an audience. "You are a man who knows where everything goes. You filed the Edict of Purity yourself—I have your hand on the routing slip. A man who files things does not lose them. So: where is August Ishikawa going?"

*The capital,* he thought, and kept his face still by thinking about ledgers. *The cathedral. The crypt-choir under the cathedral. Nell's cover story, the traveling choir, Sera holding the streets until—* He let the thought dissolve before it could reach his eyes. In the archive he had learned that secrets were not kept by silence. Silence was a locked door, and doors invited breaking. Secrets were kept by giving the breaker something else to find.

"Ask me about the map," he said.

Sister Merren went very still. Even her breathing stopped, and in the lamplight Tam saw for the first time how young she was—a broad, plain face, a jaw built for patience, eyes the color of pond ice. "What map?"

"The Ishikawa map." His voice cracked on the second word, and he let it. Let her hear a man coming apart. "The one your hunters used to find Hollowmere. You've been chasing its provenance, haven't you? Captain Vane asked questions about it. I know she did. She was seen asking."

"Who told you that?"

"A dead man," Tam said, which was true, which was the kind of truth that made liars believable. "Before you burned him at Greyford. He talked too much, your people said. So do I. Ask me about the map."

She studied him for a long moment. Then she crossed the cellar, unhurried, and took down the iron rod from its bracket by the door—not the brazier tool, not yet, just the rod, just the promise of it—and held it loosely, the way a teacher holds a pointer.

"Tell me about the map," she said.

*Here it is,* Tam thought. *The only thing I have left to give.* And underneath that, quieter, in the voice of every clerk who had ever balanced a cruel ledger and called it duty: *It should have been given years ago.*

* * *

He had drawn it in the spring of the king's ninth year on the throne—the ninth year of the Vessel-of-Crowns, though Tam had not known that then; he had known only that the king's edicts had begun arriving in a different hand, a hand that pressed too hard, that bore down on the pen as if it were strangling something. Tam had been thirty-one, third-ranked in the Office of Provenance, a thin man with ink-stained cuffs and a talent for reading marginalia the way other men read faces.

They had come for him at night. Not soldiers—clerks. That was the horror he had never managed to put into any report: they had sent clerks. Four of them from the Chancery of Interior Accounts, with a warrant signed by a seal Tam recognized and was not permitted to name, and they had sat him down in his own office among his own beloved shelves and shown him a letter in the king's hand ordering the location of the last oath-bound line of swordspeakers.

"They're traitors," one of the clerks had said, a soft man named Pell who later drowned in the canal and was buried without ceremony. "Heretics. The Edict requires their surrender."

"The valley can't be found," Tam had said. "That's the whole point of it. Hollowmere doesn't appear on any survey. The surveyors' records were burned by the founder's own order. There is no map because there has never been a map."

"There will be," Pell said, "by morning."

And they had shown him the rest of the letter. Not the part about the swordspeakers—that part was real, or real enough—but the clause beneath it, written small, in the same over-pressing hand: *and the archivist will render this service gladly, or his sister's household in Marrowfield will be assessed for sedition.* His sister. Her husband. Their three children, the youngest of whom had once sent Tam drawings of horses so bad they were beautiful.

So he had drawn the map. He had spent six weeks doing it, and he had done it well, because he was incapable of doing anything else badly. He had pulled the fragments of the founder's burned survey from three different collections and stitched them together like a surgeon closing a wound. He had interviewed, under other pretexts, two aging tax collectors who had walked the valley rim in their youth. He had reconstructed Hollowmere the way he reconstructed everything—from paper, from dust, from the residue of other people's lives—and when it was done, the valley sat on his vellum as clean and complete as a specimen in a case.

He had drawn the mill. He had drawn the footbridge. He had drawn, with a steadiness that still shamed him more than any torture ever could, the little cluster of buildings at the valley's heart, and beside them, in his beautiful archival hand, he had written: *Ishikawa homestead.*

Then he had sealed it, and carried it up through the archive into the daylight, and handed it to men whose faces he had already forgotten, and gone back to his desk, and filed something ordinary, and eaten his supper, and slept. He had slept. That was the detail that had finally driven him out of the capital nineteen years later, when the Edict's hidden clauses came across his desk and he understood what the map had been for: not that he had betrayed a family, but that he had betrayed them and *slept*, the way the kingdom itself slept, the way the king slept inside his own stolen body while something ancient wore his crown and called it governance.

Guilt, Tam had learned in the long years since, does not burn. It ferments. It sits in a man in the dark and turns sweeter and more poisonous, year over year, until the man himself becomes a cask of it, and either he bursts or he finds a use for what he has become.

In the wine cellar under the Tollbridge garrison, hanging from his own wrists, Tam Osgood found his use.

* * *

"The map," he said now, and coughed, and tasted copper and tallow-smoke. "You want to know who drew it. Why your provenance inquiry keeps hitting walls. Why Captain Vane's questions went unanswered from the Chancery. I'll tell you. But not for free."

Sister Merren tilted her head. "You are in no position to negotiate."

"No one ever is," Tam said. "We negotiate anyway. It's how we know we're still people." He swallowed, working moisture into a mouth gone to felt. "My freedom. Walk me out of here. Not far. Just past the bridge. Just to where I can see sky."

"That is not within my gift."

"Then we're finished, and you can go back to your superiors and tell them the archivist died knowing something you didn't." He let his head hang, letting exhaustion do the work his acting could not. "They'll ask why. They hate that, your superiors. Questions they can't file."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the garrison floor in slow patrols, and the river spoke its one word against the pilings, over and over, the way rivers do.

Then Sister Merren set the iron rod down on the table with a click that sounded, in the small stone room, like a lock turning.

"Talk," she said. "If what you say proves useful, I will consider the sky."

*Consider,* Tam thought. *Everything in this kingdom is considered and nothing is granted.* But he had expected exactly this, and he had prepared for exactly this, and so he gave her what he had decided, in the lucid hours between beatings, to give.

"I drew it," he said.

Her stillness again—that total, predatory attention. "You drew the Ishikawa map."

"Nineteen years ago. Spring of the king's ninth year. I was in the Office of Provenance." He let the words come slowly, brokenly, a man confessing rather than reporting. "There was a letter. Under the king's seal. Ordering the location of the last oath-bound line. I refused. Then they showed me the second page, and my sister lived in Marrowfield, and—" His voice failed, genuinely; he did not have to manufacture that. Some parts of the story were simply true, and the true parts did the lying's work for him. "I drew it. From the burned survey fragments. Six weeks. Every road, every building. The homestead. I labeled it myself."

"Why?" Soft. Almost gentle. "Why tell me this?"

"Because you're going to find out anyway." He raised his head, and let her see his eyes, wet and ruined and steady. "Your Order has been pulling provenance on that map for months. Vane's inquiries. The Chancery stonewalling you. You think that's accident? The map was never entered in any registry because it was never meant to be traced. It was meant to be used once and vanish. I'm the vanishing part. And I am tired, Sister, of being the vanishing part."

"What else did the letter order?" she asked, and there it was—the hook going in, the question he had been fishing for since they dragged him off the tollbridge with a sack over his head.

"It ordered the valley found," Tam said. "But that wasn't the purpose. The purpose was the *line*. The bloodline. The Edict of Purity is a net, and everyone knows it catches gifted folk, but nets have shapes, Sister, and this net is shaped like one family. Why do you think the hunt has never once deviated? Why do you think gifted children in the provinces get processed quietly while entire companies of the Silent Order ride north after one girl?" He coughed, wetly, convincingly. "Someone at court wants the last Ishikawa found alive. Or found specifically. I drew the map, but I never knew who read it. I only knew it went higher than the Chancery. Higher than the Privy Seal. There's a seal I saw once on the return receipt, and I've spent nineteen years trying to forget it, and I can describe it for you if you want, but you won't thank me."

"Describe it."

He did. Slowly. A seal he had invented three days ago in a fever of composition, assembled from half-remembered heraldry and one genuine anomaly he had noticed years ago in the deep files—an anomaly real enough to make the lie load-bearing. He watched her write it down. He watched her hand hesitate over it, because some part of her, some clerk-deep part of her that predated the hood, recognized that a seal like that should not exist, and that its existence meant the Order's own chain of command had a hole in it shaped like a hunger.

Good. Let her carry that home. Let it ferment in *her* now.

"And the girl," Merren said. "August Ishikawa. Where is she bound?"

"North," Tam said, which was true, and useless, and therefore perfect. "Everyone goes north. The valley's north. The sanctuary stories are north. If I knew more than north, do you think I'd be hanging in your cellar? I'm an archivist. I know where things are *written*, not where they *are*."

"We'll see," she said, and picked up the rod again, and Tam closed his eyes and offered her the body she wanted while keeping, folded small behind his ribs like a letter in a book no one would ever shelve, the thing she actually wanted: *the capital, the cathedral, the crypt-choir, the choir that travels, the captain in the streets, the sword speaking its name.*

* * *

They gave him the sky.

Not freedom—Merren had not lied, exactly, but she had not told the whole truth either, and Tam respected the craftsmanship. At what felt like dawn, two Silent Order brothers hauled him up the cellar stairs and out into the garrison yard, and stood him at the parapet above the river, and let him look east while the light came up grey-gold over the water and the mist burned off the far fields. For perhaps two hundred heartbeats. Then they took him back down.

It was enough. It was more than enough. He stood in the dark afterward with his arms screaming and his mouth full of blood and held the image the way he held all documents: completely, permanently, against all comers. Whatever they did next, they could not take the sky back out of him. It was filed now. It was in the collection.

He laughed, quietly, and the laugh hurt, and he kept it anyway.

Because here was the arithmetic he had worked out in the worst hours, the arithmetic that turned guilt into a weapon at last: every hour they spent on him was an hour they did not spend riding. Every thread he fed them—the seal, the provenance, the shape of the net—was thread they would pull instead of following the road north. He had spent nineteen years being the archive's coward, the man who drew the map and slept. He could not un-draw it. No confession would raise Hollowmere's ashes or bring back the grandmother he had never met and had murdered all the same, in the only way that mattered, with six weeks of beautiful careful work.

But a map, he had realized on the tollbridge with the sack over his head, cuts both ways. That was the whole nature of a map. It was not loyalty to any kingdom; it was knowledge wearing no colors at all. And if the Order wanted to interrogate an archivist, then let them interrogate an archivist—let them pull the truth out of him grain by grain, and let every grain be true, and let the truth be laid out in such a pattern that it led them, patiently, inevitably, in a circle around the place where the people he loved were actually going.

He had spent his life learning how information moved. Now he would move it himself, deliberately, the way a man lays stones across a flood.

* * *

On the second day—they came for him in the mornings; the Order believed, correctly, that pain metabolizes best on an empty stomach—Sister Merren brought a table, a lamp, and paper.

"You said you know where things are written," she said. "Prove it. The counter-oath. The binding texts. The king's correspondence before his illness. What does the archive hold, and where?"

And Tam looked at the paper, and at her pond-ice eyes, and understood that the game had changed: she was no longer hunting the fugitives through him. She was hunting the *binding*. Which meant the Order knew—someone high in the Order knew—what wore the crown. Which meant the hole in their chain of command went all the way down to the thing itself.

Which meant, he thought, with a cold clarity that felt almost like joy, that the enemy had finally shown him its face, and it was a filing problem.

"The king's early correspondence," he said hoarsely, "is in the sub-vault under the west range. Sealed collection. Third shelf, behind the flood inventory." All true. All checkable. And behind it, in the same breath, the lie that mattered: "But you'll need my hand to open it. The seals are keyed to provenance officers. Kill me, and the collection stays shut until someone decodes my filing system, and I promise you, Sister—" he smiled through split lips, "—no one has ever decoded my filing system."

She wrote that down too.

And Tam Osgood, disgraced archivist, liar, mapmaker, keeper of other people's sins, sat bleeding in the lamplight and gave her the archive—gave her its true shape, its real locks, its honest geography—because he had finally understood the last lesson of his profession: that the most dangerous thing a man can do with the truth is not hide it.

It is to index it—for the enemy, in good faith, in his best hand—while the one document that matters sits misfiled on purpose, waiting, a single sheet in the wrong drawer, for the right person to come looking.

Let them search the sub-vault. Let them find the king's letters, the young king's terrible brave handwriting, *I know what it is and I will wear it anyway.* Let them learn their master's origin and choke on it.

And somewhere in that same collection, in a folder marked with a dead clerk's name, in a sequence only Tam Osgood could have devised and had designed, in these last two days of blood and lamplight, precisely for this hour—

—the counter-oath waited, misfiled, patient as paper, one shelf away from the hands that needed it most.
