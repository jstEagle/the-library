## 27. Sera's Old Brothers

The rain had followed Sera out of the capital for three days, thin and gray as dishwater, and by the time she reached the garrison at Thornhollow it had soaked through her oilskin, her coat, and finally her resolve. She stood at the edge of the tree line with water running off her chin and watched the place where she had spent eleven years of her life.

Thornhollow had been a waystation once—a squat stone keep with a stable yard and a bell tower, a place where hunt-captains stopped to change horses on the long road between the city and the border provinces. Now it was something else. The bell tower was draped in black cloth that did not move in the wind, though the wind was blowing hard enough to bend the pines along the road. Lanterns burned in every window, and their light was wrong—not the warm amber of tallow or oil but a pale, steady white, like moonlight that had been told to hold still.

Sera had commanded this garrison for four years. She knew which shutters stuck, which floorboards groaned, which of the sentries chewed licorice root to stay awake on night watch. None of that knowledge helped her now. The building wore its own shape like a mask over something else's face.

*You can still turn around,* said the sensible voice in her head, the one that had kept her alive through two purges and a winter campaign in the Saltmarshes. *Ride north. Find the fugitives. Tell them what you've seen.*

But she couldn't tell them what she'd seen yet, because she didn't know what she'd seen. All she had was a commission burned to ash in a brazier outside Marlow's Ford, an order she had refused to carry out—the child, the little gifted boy with mud on his knees, the order signed not by the king's hand but by the Order's seal—and a question that had been growing in her chest like a splinter working toward the heart: *who does the Silent Order actually serve?*

There was only one person left alive who might answer honestly. And he was down there, in the white-lit windows, wearing her old command.

She went down through the trees.

* * *

The gate guards let her in without challenge, which frightened her more than a fight would have. They were young—she didn't know either of them—but they recognized the coat, or what remained of it, and one of them actually saluted before catching himself and making the gesture into something else, a half-formed thing that died in the air between them. Sera walked past them into the yard and felt the wrongness press closer, like wading into deep water.

The stable yard smelled of horses and beneath the horses something else, something sweetish and rotten, like fruit gone over in a closed room. The black cloth on the bell tower hung directly overhead now, and up close she could see it wasn't cloth at all. It was woven from hair. Hundreds of thousands of strands of human hair, braided into cords and knotted into a covering, and it did not move because there was nothing natural in it left to move.

"Don't look at it too long."

The voice came from the archway to the inner hall, and Sera turned, and there he was.

Corren Vask had been her second-in-command for six years, back when she'd still believed the hunt could be clean. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a beard he'd started growing the day she'd first met him and a laugh that used to shake dust out of rafters. He'd carried her out of the Saltmarshes with an arrow in his shoulder and never once mentioned it afterward. He'd taught her the proper way to break a door, the proper way to write a report, the proper way to bury the ones who didn't come home.

Now he stood in the archway with a lantern of that pale white light hanging from his fist, and his eyes were dry and very still, and Sera understood that the man looking out of his face was standing further back inside himself than any man should have to stand.

"Sera." He said her name carefully, like a word in a language he was trying not to forget. "You shouldn't be here."

"No," she agreed. "Probably not."

"They'll know you came. They always know." He glanced up at the tower of hair, quickly, the way a man glances at a wound. "Come inside. Out of the rain. If you're going to make your mistake, at least don't freeze doing it."

* * *

The great hall of Thornhollow had hosted a hundred musters, a hundred suppers, the winter festival three years running when Sera had ordered the men to drag in a pine and the cooks had argued about plum pudding until midnight. Now the long tables were pushed against the walls and the center of the floor was bare, scrubbed, and marked with a circle of white chalk so fine it looked like salt. The lanterns along the walls all burned that same held-still white. In the hearth, no fire. Just the chalk circle, and above it, hanging from the mantel, a crown.

Not the king's crown. A copy, or a memory of one—black iron, unadorned, older than any smithing Sera knew. It hung on an iron nail as if it weighed nothing, and Sera's skin crawled away from it in a slow wave, from her nape down her spine, because some animal part of her understood that it was watching the room the way a spider watches a web.

"You feel it," Corren said quietly, behind her. "Everyone does, the first time. It fades. That's worse, if you ask me. When you stop feeling it."

"What is it?"

"The original was destroyed. Or they say it was. This is a casting from the casting." He set his lantern down. "Sit, Sera. Eat something, if you want. I'll answer what I can. But I'm telling you now, as the man who pulled you out of the marsh—" his voice caught, just slightly, on the past tense of himself— "as your friend, whatever I say next, you should ride north after and never come back."

"I'm not riding anywhere until I understand." She sat on a bench, deliberately, in view of the iron crown, because she would not let the thing think it frightened her. "The order at Marlow's Ford. The execution order. It bore the king's seal, but the signature block was the Order's. I wrote to the palace asking whose authority. The reply came back in a cipher I've never seen, and the cipher key was old, Corren. Archive-old. Pre-dynastic. What kind of royal edict comes keyed to a code older than the dynasty?"

Corren lowered himself onto the bench across from her. Up close, in the white light, she could see what the stillness in his eyes was made of: exhaustion, bottomless and managed, the exhaustion of a man who has been awake for years against his will.

"Sit there a moment," he said, "and tell me what you think the Order is."

"A religious confraternity. Sworn to the purity of the crown. They advise on gifted detections, they train inquisitors, they—"

"That's the story we were both raised on." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I believed it too, right up until my promotion. You burn your commission, you walk away clean. I took mine. Do you know what the investiture actually is? Not the ceremony they show the ranks. The real one, down here, at midnight, in front of that." He nodded at the crown without looking at it. "They put you in the circle. They ask you one question. Not *do you swear*. They ask: *will you listen?* And if you say yes—"

He stopped. His hands, Sera noticed, were trembling, very slightly, and he pressed them flat against his knees to stop them.

"—if you say yes, then you start hearing it. At first just at night. Then at dusk. Then always. A voice under the voice of everyone who speaks to you. Like a second speaker behind every mouth. It doesn't command. That's the thing nobody understands, Sera—it doesn't *command*. It suggests. It agrees with you. Whatever you already want, it wants louder. And after a while you can't find the seam anymore between what you wanted and what it wanted, and you stop looking for the seam, because finding it hurts."

"The entity," Sera said. The word came out of her flat and strange. She hadn't meant to say it aloud; she'd heard it only in fragments, in Tam Osgood's frantic letters before he fled the archive, in the rumors that moved through the ranks like smoke. "The thing in the crown. The thing wearing the king."

Corren flinched. Actually flinched, his whole body, and his eyes darted to the iron crown on the mantel and back.

"Don't," he whispered. "Don't name it. Not here. It hears its names. That's how it finds people. That's how it found the Ishikawa valley, do you understand? Somebody at court spoke its name and the valley's location, together, in the same breath, and the seal on that place has been fraying ever since. The whole Edict of Purity, the whole hunt—it isn't policy. It isn't the king. The king hasn't spoken an original sentence in nineteen years. Everything that comes out of that throne room is it, thinking through his mouth."

The hearth was cold and the rain hammered the shutters and Sera sat very still, letting the words rearrange the architecture of her life. Eleven years of service. Two purges. The child at Marlow's Ford with mud on his knees, kneeling where she'd been ordered to make him kneel.

"The Order serves it," she said. "Directly. Not the crown. Not the kingdom. The thing itself."

"The Order *is* it," Corren said. "Or its hands are. Every inquisitor who takes the listening, every captain promoted past a certain rank—we're not hunting the gifted because they're impure. We're hunting them because they're *dangerous to it*. A swordspeaker who speaks a true name. A singer whose voice carries what it can't carry. An oath-bound line whose blood is the lock on its cage. It has spent nineteen years using the kingdom's own law to reach for the things that could end it, and calling the reaching righteousness." He laughed, short and terrible, with none of the old rafter-shaking warmth in it. "And I helped. Eleven years, Sera. I drew up the sweep patterns for Greyford myself."

Something moved in Sera's chest at the name—Greyford, the crossing, the moment she had watched three ragged figures slip her net and had, for reasons she still could not explain, looked at the river instead of the road. She thought of August Ishikawa's face in that instant, sharp and wary across the water. She thought of Nell, the choirgirl, small and soaked and singing under her breath to keep herself brave.

"I let them go," Sera said. "At Greyford. I don't know why."

"Yes, you do." Corren leaned forward, and for a moment the man she'd known was fully present in his face, the man who'd carried her out of the marsh. "You do know why. Because some part of you heard the seam. The same part that's sitting here now asking questions instead of reporting me for harboring doubt. That part doesn't belong to it, Sera. It can't get at that part. It can only wear everything around it." He straightened. "Which brings me to why you really came, and why you need to leave."

"The company. The fugitives. The Order's hunting them."

"It's beyond hunting now. Three days ago the Order received new orders, direct from the source. No ciphers, no chains of command. Every listening officer in the province was woken at the same hour, the same minute, in the middle of the same dream." His voice dropped. "We all dreamed the same dream, Sera. A crypt under a cathedral, full of singing. And a girl's voice rising out of it, carrying something. And a sword being drawn in the dark."

Sera's blood went cold. *Nell. August.* The crypt-choir—Tam had written to her of it in his last letter, half-mad with guilt, generations of children singing past death. If the entity was dreaming of it, the entity was close to it. Or afraid of it.

"They're moving on the capital," she said.

"Everyone's moving on the capital. The Order is recalling every field captain for a final muster. The streets will be full of white lanterns within the week." Corren reached into his coat and set something on the table between them: a ring of keys, iron, heavy, each stamped with the garrison's mark. "The armory, the map room, the post archive. Everything I was trusted with, because I stopped being trusted with anything else years ago—they only trust the hollowed ones now, and I learned to look hollowed a long time ago." His jaw worked. "In the post archive there's a dispatch case, sealed with the pre-dynastic cipher. Intercepted correspondence. I'm not supposed to know it exists. I'm certainly not supposed to give you the means to read it."

"Why?" Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Why risk this, Corren? Why not just stay hollowed and safe?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and in the white light his eyes were wet for the first time, though his face stayed perfectly still, trained stillness, the discipline of a man who could not afford to feel anything visibly ever again.

"Because you asked me once," he said, "what I'd do if the hunt ever turned on someone innocent. You asked me at the winter festival, drunk on plum wine, laughing, and I gave you the correct answer, the loyal answer. And you looked at me the way you're looking at me now, and you said, *that's not what I asked.*" He pushed the keys across the table. "It took me eleven years to hear the question. Don't take that long to act on yours. Go north. Find them. Tell the swordspeaker what the enemy looks like when it wears a friend's face—because that's the only warning that will save her. It won't come at her as a monster. It'll come as help. As home. As the voice of someone she loved."

Sera's hand closed over the keys, cold iron biting her palm.

"And you?" she asked. "Come with me."

Corren smiled, and it was the old smile, almost, the one that shook dust from rafters, worn now like a coat two sizes too large.

"Somebody has to stay hollowed," he said, "and stand in the doorway when they come counting. Besides—" he glanced up, once, at the tower of braided hair, and this time he made himself hold the glance— "somebody owes that thing a debt, and I mean to pay it in person, at the worst possible moment for it. Now go. Out the postern, past the kennels. And Sera—" his voice followed her as she rose, low and urgent— "when you see the seam, when you finally see it plain, don't hesitate the way I did. Hesitation is how it gets in."

She went out through the kennels, past sleeping hounds that stirred and whined at her passage without waking, and through the postern gate into the hammering rain. Behind her Thornhollow glowed with its held-still light, and the tower of hair stood motionless against the storm, and she did not look back again until she was under the trees.

Then she looked back once, because he deserved it, and saw him standing in the postern doorway with his lantern raised—a small white star in all that dark, holding steady, the way a man holds a door open for someone he will never follow.

Sera rode north through the rain with the keys against her ribs and the intercepted cipher burning a hole in her thoughts, and by the time dawn grayed the eastern sky she knew two things with total certainty.

The enemy had a face now, and it was worse than a monster's face, because it was every face she had ever trusted.

And somewhere ahead, in a crypt full of stolen voices, her ragged found family was walking straight into the thing's oldest dream—and she was the only one who knew.
