## 28. The Choir in the Catacombs

The way down was behind the reliquary of Saint Vessa, which Tam said should have told us everything we needed to know, because Saint Vessa was the patron of silenced women.

"Nobody builds a shrine to a saint and then hides a door behind her unless they're ashamed," he whispered, easing the reliquary aside on its hidden pivot. The stone groaned. Cold air breathed out of the dark beneath the cathedral, air that smelled of candle smoke and lilies and something older underneath both — dust, yes, but also the faint sweet rot of things kept too long in sealed rooms. "Ashamed, or afraid."

I was afraid. I want to be honest about that, because afterward everyone wanted to make it sound like courage, and it wasn't. Courage is a thing you choose. What I felt going down those stairs was the particular dread of a girl who had spent six years singing in a choir loft and had never once asked where the power in her voice went when the choirmaster collected it.

I had started asking. That was the problem with asking.

August went first, her sword drawn, the blade making no sound at all as it cleared the scabbard, which I had learned was itself a kind of wrongness — good steel sings against leather, and hers never did. Sera came behind me with a lantern, then Tam, his arms full of the decoded letters he would not leave anywhere, not even for a moment, not even to climb a ladder. The stairs wound down farther than the cathedral was tall. I counted them out of nervousness and lost count somewhere past two hundred, and after that there was only the counting of my own heartbeat, which was faster.

The walls changed as we descended. Upstairs the cathedral was all pale granite and gold leaf, light and order, the architecture of a faith that wanted to be seen. Down here the stone went black and slick, veined with something that caught the lantern light and held it, so that our small flame seemed to travel along the walls ahead of us, like the dark was showing us the way it wanted us to go.

"There are carvings," Tam said suddenly, and stopped, and raised his hand for the lantern.

He was right. The walls were carved — rows upon rows of small figures, thousands of them, tens of thousands, each one no bigger than my thumb, each one with its mouth open. Not screaming. Singing. You could tell the difference even in stone, somehow. Screaming figures clutch their throats or throw their heads back; these stood straight, hands folded, mouths open in perfect little ovals, rank upon rank upon rank, receding into the blackness on either side of us like congregations.

"How far does it go?" Sera asked. Her voice came out flat, the hunt-captain's voice, the one she used when feeling would get in the way.

"I don't know," Tam said. "I genuinely don't know."

We walked. The carvings walked with us, mouth after mouth after mouth.

* * *

I heard the singing before I understood what I was hearing, and that is the truth I will carry longest: I heard it, and part of me thought, *how lovely*, before any other part of me knew what it was.

It came up through the floor like water through limestone. A hymn — an old one, pre-Reformation, one they don't teach anymore because its harmonies were considered unstable, liable to induce visions. The choirmaster had made us sing it once, as children, and then never again, and I had gone home that night and dreamed of drowning in warm water and woken up weeping without knowing why.

But this version was wrong. Every voice in it was slightly flat, slightly frayed, the way voices go when they have been singing a very long time. And underneath the hymn, so low you had to press your palm against the stone to feel it rather than hear it, ran a second line — a drone, a sustained note that never ended, that did not breathe, that simply *was*, the way a headache is, the way a debt is.

"That's the binding," August said quietly. She had stopped walking. Her sword-point had dropped, and she was staring at the floor as though she could see through it. "Tam. Is that the binding?"

"The counter-oath needs a sung component," Tam said. His voice had gone strange and careful, the archivist's voice, the one that handles dangerous facts like cracked porcelain. "I knew that. It's in the marginalia of three separate sources. A binding maintained by song requires — " He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "A binding maintained by song requires singers."

"Singers can stop," Sera said.

"Yes," Tam said. "Singers can stop."

Nobody said anything else. We went down the last flight of stairs together, and the lantern light opened into the crypt-choir, and I learned what the crown had been doing with its purity, all these years, all this hunting, all this fire.

* * *

The cavern was natural once, I think — a hollow in the city's bones, ribbed with stone pillars that the builders had dressed and carved until they looked deliberate. Rows of stalls filled it, choir stalls, the kind I had sat in every morning of my childhood, oak and velvet, but ancient, the velvet rotted to lace, the oak worn smooth by generations of small bodies leaning.

And in the stalls, the singers.

They were children. They had been children. Some of them still wore the grey cassocks of cathedral choristers, generations out of fashion — I recognized the cut from portraits in the cloister hall, from two hundred years ago, from four hundred. They sat in their stalls in ranks that receded back into darkness beyond the lantern's reach, and they sang, and they were dead.

Not skeletons. Not quite corpses either. Preserved, the way things are preserved in cold and silence — skin like parchment drawn over small skulls, lips moving around the old hymn, eyes closed, or half-closed, or — some of them — open. Open and fixed on nothing, or on us, it was impossible to say. Their chests rose and fell with the breath of singing, though there was no breath in them, had been no breath for centuries. The drone underneath everything came from the deepest rows, where the smallest figures sat, the ones whose cassocks had gone brown with age, and it did not come from their mouths at all. It came from *them*, from whatever was left in them, poured out and poured out and poured out for generation after generation to hold shut the thing wearing the king's crown.

Nineteen years, Tam had told us, since the entity took the throne. But the binding was older than that. The binding needed voices, and the crown had been feeding it voices since before anyone now living was born.

"They're still singing," I said. My voice sounded very small and very young, which was appropriate, because I felt five years old. "Why are they still singing? They're dead. Dead people can't — "

"The binding doesn't require living voices," Tam said softly. "It requires *given* voices. Voices surrendered freely." He was staring at the nearest stall, at a child whose face had gone to leather, whose mouth moved through the hymn's third verse. "That's why the choirs. That's why the cathedral trains them. A child who loves singing, who offers her voice to God every morning — the offering is real. The magic doesn't distinguish between the god and the use the crown puts it to. It just takes the gift at its word."

"And keeps it," Sera said. Her knuckles were white around the lantern's handle. "Past death. Past everything. It just — keeps it."

I had stopped listening to them. Because I had found her.

* * *

She was in the fourth row, third stall from the center aisle, and I knew her the way you know your own reflection in a window you weren't expecting to catch: with a jolt, with a denial, with a horrible instant recognition.

She was small. Smaller than me. Seven years old, maybe eight, and she had my chin — the chin I'd always been told came from a mother I couldn't remember — and she wore a chorister's cassock that fit her badly, sleeves rolled twice at the wrists, and her mouth was open in the oval of the old hymn, and her throat moved with the melody, and the voice coming out of her was mine.

Not like mine. *Mine.* My timbre, my break on the ascending phrase, the slight huskiness the choirmaster had spent years trying to train out of me and never could. Six years of stolen voice, siphoned Sunday by Sunday into the cathedral wards, and here it was, seated in a dead girl's throat, singing the binding into place, and the dead girl had my chin, and I did not know her name, and I knew her name, because the choirmaster had called the roll every morning and I had heard it a thousand times without ever hearing it:

*Maren.*

Maren Osgood.

I heard Tam make a sound beside me. A small sound, punched out of him, and I turned and saw him looking at the same stall, at the same child, and his face had come apart entirely, the careful archivist mask lying in pieces around his feet.

"My sister's daughter," he said. "My sister's daughter. She was taken for the choir when she was seven. They told us she'd been sent to the provincial school. They told us — " He pressed both hands over his mouth. Above them his eyes were wet and wide and fixed on the small dead singer with his family's chin. "Twenty-two years. I've been in the archive twenty-two years and I never — I signed the transfer orders, Nell. I processed the paperwork. I filed her under *relocation, minor, standard* and I never once asked where the standard relocations went."

"Tam." I reached for him. He stepped back, away from my hand, away from all of us, and stood in the aisle of the dead with his guilt rising off him like heat off a road.

"She has my voice," I said, because someone had to say it, because if no one said it I would scream. "That's my voice. The choirmaster didn't collect it for the wards. He fed it to *her*. To keep her singing. To keep her — " My own voice broke on the word, and the whole cavern heard it, and worse, the cavern *answered*: a hundred small mouths faltered for half a beat, a ripple through the hymn like wind through wheat, and the drone beneath everything shuddered, and somewhere far above us I swear the cathedral bells stirred in their frames.

"Don't," August said sharply, catching my arm. "Don't grieve out loud. Not here. Your voice has weight in this room — you especially. Grief is an offering too. Everything is an offering, down here. That's how it works."

So I bit it back. I stood in the aisle with my hand over my own mouth, holding my grief in like a hymn held on one breath, and I looked down the endless rows of stalls, and I understood, finally, completely, what I was.

I was not a choirgirl with a gift. I was a pipe. I was a spout. I was the living end of a machine whose other end was these children, and every note I had ever sung with love had traveled down here, into these throats, into this work, and the work was holding shut a parasite's cage, and the parasite wore the king, and the king had been begging us, in his true letters, to hurry.

* * *

August walked the rows slowly. She did not touch any of them. I watched her want to — watched her hand rise toward a small figure in the second row, a boy with a cowlick frozen mid-motion above his brow — and then lower again, because she understood what I was learning: that consent was the engine of this place, and touching them without theirs might be one more theft.

"They can stop," she said at last. Her voice carried strangely in the cavern, flat and clear, the swordspeaker's voice, the one that made stone listen. "You said the binding needs given voices. Given can be taken back. That's what an oath is — it holds until it's unheld."

"Theoretically," Tam said. He had composed himself by force, the way he always did, straightening his ruined cuffs like armor. "In practice, releasing a bound voice requires — " he glanced at me, and away, and I saw him decide to say it anyway, because he had decided, somewhere in the last few minutes, that he was done protecting people from the truth, including himself — "it requires the voice's owner, or someone carrying the owner's voice, to sing the release. Here. In the binding's presence. And the release, once begun, cannot be stopped halfway. Either every voice goes free or none do, and the cage opens either way."

"The cage opens," Sera repeated. "Meaning the entity wakes free the moment they stop singing."

"Which is why the counter-oath must be ready first," Tam said. "Unbind and bind in the same breath. Free the children and cage the Vessel in the space between two notes. It's possible. On paper it's possible. It requires a swordspeaker's oath spoken through a voice strong enough to carry it into the binding itself." He was looking at me when he said it. Everyone was looking at me when he said it.

And I looked back down the rows, at Maren, at my voice sitting in her small dead throat, singing and singing and singing a song she had never chosen, and I felt something settle in me that was not fear. Fear had been the whole of me for weeks — fear on the road, fear at Greyford, fear in the catacombs' first dark. This was different. This was the thing on the far side of fear, the quiet after it, the place where terror burns down to a coal and the coal is steady and the coal is *mine*.

"You already know," I said to August. "You've known since the widow told us. Breaking the oath marks you. The Vessel will leap for you. And the only voice that can carry your oath-song into this binding is one the binding already trusts — a given voice. A choir voice." I swallowed. "Mine."

"Nell." August's hand closed on my shoulder, warm and heavy. "No one has decided anything."

"Everyone has decided everything," I said. "We just haven't said it out loud yet. That's all that's left to decide — who says it first."

Above us, faint through fathoms of stone, the cathedral's evening service began. The congregation murmuring. The organ drawing its first breath. And here below, the dead choir sang on, verse after verse, century after century, holding the world's worst secret shut with the only thing they had left, which was the thing that had been taken from them.

Maren's mouth moved through the descending phrase. My voice came out of her, thin and true and tired, and I made myself a promise in the silence behind the hymn, a promise shaped exactly like a note:

*I'm coming back for you. For all of you. And when I sing next, it won't be for them. It will be for you.*

Then Tam, still staring down the rows with his sister's daughter's face among the dead, went very still and said, in a voice I had never heard him use, a voice with no archivist in it at all:

"There's a new stall. At the end of the row. The wood isn't aged. The velvet isn't rotted." He pointed with a hand that shook. "And it's empty. Someone built a new stall down here recently — recently enough that the sawdust hasn't settled — and it's sized for a child who hasn't arrived yet."

Behind us, up the long stairway, faint and far, a bell began to ring the alarm.
