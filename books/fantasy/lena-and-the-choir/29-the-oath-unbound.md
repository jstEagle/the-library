## 29. The Oath Unbound

The crypt-choir was still singing.

That was the thing August could not stop hearing as they descended the last stair into the cathedral's buried heart—the singing that had no throats left to make it. The children of the crypt sat in their stone niches in rows that curved like the inside of a shell, and though their bodies had been dust for generations, their voices remained, wound into the binding like thread through cloth. High voices. Low voices. A voice that cracked on every third note because it had belonged to a boy who never grew old enough to outgrow it.

Nell walked ahead of her with one hand pressed flat against the wall, and August saw her flinch at each niche they passed, the way a person flinches past graves they know by name.

"They're still here," Nell whispered. Not a question. She had already found her own stolen voice among them, three nights ago, and had not slept properly since.

"They're still here," August said, because lying to Nell had become impossible somewhere on the road north, and she could not remember exactly when.

The air below the cathedral was cold in the way of places that have never known sun—cold that lived in the stone itself, older than the masonry, older than the kingdom perhaps. Their torches burned low and blue-edged, drinking something from the atmosphere that ordinary fire should not have wanted. At the center of the chamber stood a dais of black basalt, and upon it, laid across two iron rests, was nothing at all—and everything: the binding itself, visible only when you looked slightly to the side of it, a lattice of pale light like frost on a window, like breath held for nineteen years and counting.

Beneath it, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, August felt the valley.

She had felt it since they crossed into the capital's outskirts—a pressure behind her sternum, familiar as her grandmother's kitchen, the seal on Hollowmere humming its long note of restraint. Nineteen years of her life she had spent inside that note without knowing it was a note. Now she heard it everywhere. It was in the singing of the dead children. It was in the frost-light of the binding. It was in her sword, silent on her back, waiting the way a dog waits at a door it has scratched at for decades.

"August." Tam's voice came soft from the stair. He leaned against the wall, gray-faced, his ribs bound where the Order's work had cracked them, but his eyes were clear—the clearest she'd ever seen them. "The counter-oath is ready. As ready as I can make it. Once you break your family's seal, there will be—" He stopped. Swallowed. "There will be very little time."

"I know."

"And once you speak here, in this place, the Vessel will feel it. Every ward in the palace will turn toward you like an eye."

"I know, Tam."

He nodded and did not say the rest—that he might not live to see what came after. He had said it once, quietly, over Tam's own handwriting, and August had refused to hear it then. She would not insult him by refusing twice.

Sera took up her position at the base of the stairs, sword drawn, facing upward into the dark. "Nothing gets down those steps," she said. It was not bravado. Sera did not do bravado anymore; she had burned that habit along with her commission. It was simply a fact stated in the flat voice she used for facts she intended to make true.

Nell stood beside the dais, hands folded, throat working. Her voice would carry the oath-song. Her stolen magic—twenty-three voices' worth of other people's childhoods—would ignite doing it, and might burn her away with them. She had made that choice in secret and been caught at it, and now she stood in the open with it, chin up, terrified, luminous.

"Last chance," August said to all of them, and meant it as a gift. "Walk away. No one has to witness this. What I'm about to do condemns my whole line in the eyes of half the kingdom."

"The other half," Sera said, not turning around, "is hunting children. Let them condemn us together."

* * *

August knelt at the edge of the dais and drew the sword.

It came out of its scabbard with a sound she had heard ten thousand times and never listened to—a long silver syllable, a word being said slowly. She laid it flat across her palms, blade up, the way her grandmother had laid it across hers on the morning of her twelfth birthday, and had said: *This is yours. You may never name it. That is the whole of your inheritance.*

She had thought, for twenty years, that the ache in her chest was longing. She had built her entire self around that ache the way a tree builds around a fence wire, growing over it, around it, until the wire was invisible and load-bearing both.

Now, in the cold and the singing, she understood that the ache had been something else entirely. Not longing. Recognition. The sword had been speaking to her all along, in a voice too quiet to hear over the seal's long note, and only in the last weeks—in the ambush at the tollbridge, in the burning half-waking of her gift—had the note dropped low enough for her to catch the words beneath.

Her grandmother's words.

"Grandmother," August said aloud, to the dark, to the blade, to whatever of Marisol Ishikawa remained in the steel and in the valley's memory. "You told me the first half of our history in the kitchen, with the bread rising. You told me the oath was a lock. You didn't tell me it was a door."

The torches guttered. In the niches, the dead choir sang on, but the song changed key—shifted, subtly, the way a congregation shifts when the sermon turns toward home. August felt the change against her skin like weather.

"You didn't tell me," she went on, and her voice steadied, "because you hoped I would never have to choose. You stayed in that valley so I could grow up small and safe and unnamed. You let the witch-hunters come rather than leave, because if you left, the lock would weaken, and you were the last hand strong enough to hold it shut. You burned so that I could run." Her throat closed. She let it close, and waited, and opened again. "I ran. And everything you bought with that fire, I am about to spend. I need you to know I know the price. I need you to know I'm paying it with my eyes open."

The sword grew warm against her palms.

Not hot. Warm the way a hand is warm. And in the warmth, faint and unmistakable, she heard it—not in her ears, in the marrow-place where the gift lived, dormant all these years, curled like a seed:

*Little one. Say it well.*

August wept, briefly, silently, the way her grandmother had taught her to weep—without stopping anything else she was doing. Then she wiped her face on her shoulder, because ceremony deserved a steady voice, and began.

* * *

"I am August Ishikawa," she said, "last of the oath-bound line, daughter of Hollowmere, which is ash. I speak before my sword, whose name I was forbidden; before my grandmother, whose voice lives in the steel; before my found family, who are my witnesses freely given; and before the dead of this place, who were never asked and never answered, and to whom I owe an apology my whole life long."

Nell lifted her chin. Began to hum.

It was barely sound at first—a thread of tone beneath the crypt-choir, weaving into it, finding the spaces between the dead children's notes the way water finds the cracks in stone. But as August spoke, Nell's hum deepened, gathered harmonics, became the oath-song Tam had reconstructed from the king's letters and the widow's warnings and nineteen years of hidden archives: the melody the first Ishikawa had sung when the binding was made, recovered note by note from paper and bone.

And the sword woke.

Not fully—not the wild answering leap of the tollbridge, the gift burning through restraint like fire through dry grass. This was slower. Dignified. The blade trembled in August's grip like a throat clearing after years of silence, and light rose along its edge, pale gold, the color of the frost-lattice above the dais, and August felt her ancestors.

All of them. Every swordspeaker back to the first one who had knelt in this same crypt—or one like it, before the cathedral was built atop it—and sworn the family into silence. They were not ghosts. They were weight. The accumulated gravity of generations of restraint, pressing down through the steel into her hands, asking, in a chorus too vast for words: *Are you certain?*

"No," August said honestly, and felt the weight shift, almost amused. "But certainty is just fear wearing armor. I'm done wearing it."

She drew the deepest breath of her life.

"By the authority of the last living bearer of the oath, spoken outside the valley where magic cannot follow, witnessed by the free and the dead alike—I unbind. I release my line from the vow of silence. I release my sword from its namelessness. I release the valley of Hollowmere from its long exile, and I call the seal home from every stone it sleeps in, from every border it guards, from every generation it has kept small."

The crypt-choir swelled. Nell's voice rose through it, carrying August's words the way a river carries a boat—lifting them, bearing them, giving them reach no single throat could manage. August felt her own voice multiplied, echoed, distributed into the dark like seed into furrows.

"And I take the cost onto myself alone. Whatever comes for the breaking of this seal—let it come to me. Whatever wakes because I wake it—let it look for me. My ancestors kept this lock with their lives. I break it with mine. So sworn, so spoken, so—"

She faltered. One heartbeat. Two.

Because the next word was the sword's name.

Twenty years of prohibition ended in a single syllable, and every instinct bred into her bones rose up screaming against it—the way a hand pulls back from a flame, the way a body refuses the cliff-edge. Her grandmother's voice in her memory: *Never, little one. Not even in dreams. The name is the key and the key is the door and the door is the end of everything you love.*

But her grandmother's voice in the steel said otherwise. Said: *Say it well.*

August looked up. Sera at the stairs, planted like a gatepost, watching her with eyes that held no doubt at all—which was somehow more moving than comfort would have been. Tam against the wall, lips moving, feeding the counter-oath's framework into the air like a man laying track ahead of a train. Nell, blazing now, literally blazing, her stolen magic catching alight along her arms and throat in threads of white fire as the song demanded everything she had carried—and still singing, still holding August's voice aloft, tears cutting clean lines through the glow on her cheeks.

Her found family. Her witnesses. Her reason.

August closed her eyes. Found the valley behind them—the green hush of it, the mill-wheel turning, the smell of bread and whetstone oil, her grandmother's hands guiding hers along the blade. Home. Not the prison she had fled. The memory she carried, which no seal and no distance and no fire could touch.

"Home goes with me," she said softly. "It always did."

And she spoke the name.

It was not a word in any language she knew, and yet her mouth shaped it perfectly, the way a bird shapes flight. It was three sounds braided into one—a falling note like a door closing, a rising note like a window opening, and between them a long ringing tone that was neither, that was simply *her*, that had been hers since before her birth, waiting.

The world broke open.

* * *

There was no explosion. There was something far stranger: silence. Total, absolute, the silence of a held breath released—the crypt-choir ending mid-note, the binding's hum ceasing, the eternal cold lifting all at once like a hand withdrawn. For one suspended instant, August knelt in a silence so profound she could hear her own blood, and Nell's ragged breathing, and far above, muffled through sixty feet of stone, the city of the capital going quiet too—as if every bell in every tower had forgotten how to ring.

Then the seal shattered.

She felt it go. Felt it as a wave radiating outward from the sword's named edge, up through the crypt, through the cathedral, through the capital, through the kingdom—through the mountains eastward to a valley of ash and green regrowth where wards older than the kingdom dissolved like frost in morning sun. Magic, locked out of Hollowmere for four hundred years, rushed home into that empty country like water into a dry bed. August felt her birthplace fill with power she had never been allowed to touch, felt trees she had climbed suddenly capable of listening, felt the millpond begin, in some small vegetal way, to dream.

Liberation. It poured through her like dawn.

And it felt like loss.

That was the truth of it, and she held the truth gently, the way you hold a bird. Four hundred years of her family's sacrifice, undone in a breath. The small safe life her grandmother had died defending—gone, irrevocably, and with it the version of August who might have lived and died unnamed in a green valley, sharpening a sword she could not speak to, aching in a way she'd have called normal. That girl was gone. August mourned her for exactly as long as it took to draw one more breath, and then she let her go, because grief for a self you outgrew is just love with nowhere to land, and there was somewhere for it to land now.

"Nell—" She turned.

Nell was on her knees, still glowing, the white fire guttering low. The stolen voices were leaving her—August could see it, hear it, feel it: twenty-three threads of borrowed song unwinding from the girl's chest and streaming toward the niches, toward the dead children who had lent them or lost them. Each niche flickered as its voice came home. The boy who cracked on every third note sang one perfect, whole, unbroken phrase—and fell silent forever, at peace, finished.

One by one, the crypt-choir went quiet. Not silenced. *Released.* The binding that had needed their voices no longer had any claim on them, and they went out like lamps at dawn, gratefully, each niche's faint light dimming to nothing but stone and dust and rest.

Until only one voice remained in the chamber.

Nell's own.

She knelt in the fading glow, hollowed out, emptied of everything the choirmaster had fed into her—and she opened her mouth and sang a single note, small and cracked and utterly, unmistakably hers, and August thought she had never heard anything so beautiful in her life.

"It's mine," Nell whispered, staring at her own hands. "August. It's—it's only mine. It's so *small*." She laughed, wet and wondering. "It's so small and it's mine."

August reached down and pulled her up, and held her, briefly, fiercely, while the last of the light died and the crypt settled around them like an old man into a chair.

* * *

Then the far wall of the crypt moved.

Not crumbled—*moved*, deliberately, stone grinding against stone as seams that had never been seams split wide, and cold air poured through, air that tasted of torch-smoke and livery polish and something else beneath it, something old and hungry and crowned.

Tam made a sound like a man reading his own death warrant. "The passage," he rasped. "The archivists' records mentioned a service tunnel from the crypt to the palace undercroft. I thought—I hoped it was legend."

"It's not legend," Sera said. She had not moved from the stairs, but her sword was up, and her voice had gone flat and hard. "Look."

Through the opened wall, down a corridor lit by torches that had been lit recently—within the hour—came footsteps. Many of them. Unhurried. The Silent Order did not hurry; hurry was for people who could be surprised.

And above them, through sixty feet of stone, through the whole waking city, August felt it turn toward her—the Vessel-of-Crowns, unbound now, unblinded, its long sleep of ignorance ended by the very act that had freed the king. It knew where she was. It knew what she carried. And it had been waiting nineteen years for exactly this door to open.

The sword in her hand—named now, awake, warm as a living thing—pulled gently toward the corridor. Toward the throne room. Toward the dying man who had worn a parasite's crown to keep it from worse hands, and who had begged, in ink, across nineteen years of coded letters: *hurry*.

"Company," August said, and her voice did not shake, "we're going up."

Behind them, the last niche went dark. Ahead, the torches burned, and the footsteps came on.
