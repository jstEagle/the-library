## 33. The Throne Room Door

The palace had gone mad in the space of an hour.

August felt it before she saw it—the wrongness pressing against her skin like a change in weather, like the moment before lightning when the air itself holds its breath. The Vessel-of-Crowns was awake now, truly awake, and the palace knew it the way a body knows fever. Every corridor she ran through carried the stink of it: hot metal and old incense and something sweeter underneath, cloying, like flowers left too long in a closed room.

Behind her, the city burned. She could hear it even through stone—Sera's rebellion holding the streets, bells ringing out of rhythm, screams that were not all terror. Somewhere below, Tam was dying or dead, and Nell's song was still rising through the foundations of the cathedral, thin and fierce as a wire pulled taut. August carried all of them with her, every step, the way her grandmother had taught her to carry a blade: close to the body, never set down.

She came up through the servants' stair behind the east gallery because Tam's maps had said the Silent Order guarded the grand approach, and Tam's maps had not yet lied to her. Her sword was loose in its sheath. It had been loose since the crypt, since the oath broke, since she spoke half of what lived inside her into the dark while dead children sang. The blade hummed against her hip like a held breath.

*They are coming,* it did not say, because swords do not speak in words, not yet, not to her. But she felt it anyway, the way you feel rain coming in your teeth.

The east gallery opened onto the long hall of portraits, and the Silent Order was waiting.

Not many of them. That was almost worse. Six figures in grey, faces bare, which meant they had stopped pretending to be men. Their eyes were the color of candle flames seen through smoked glass, and they stood in a line across the marble with the terrible patience of things that have waited nineteen years for this exact door.

August drew.

The sound of the draw was wrong—or right, depending on how you counted. It rang like a bell struck underwater, deep and rolling, and the nearest portrait—a dead queen with painted pearls—seemed to turn her head. August's gift rose in her like water finding a crack, no longer the trickle the valley had allowed but a flood with nowhere left to go except through steel.

"Last chance to walk away," she said, and heard her own voice doubled, faintly, as if someone older stood just behind her shoulder.

The Order did not answer. They never answered. They moved.

What happened next, August would remember always as fragments stitched with gold thread. The first hunter came apart at the collarbone where her blade met him—not cut so much as *unmade*, the oath-broken gift refusing to let his borrowed strength hold together. The second caught her forearm and she felt his grip burn cold, felt him trying to pour himself into her the way the Vessel poured itself into kings, and her sword sang one clear note and he fell back smoking, screaming in six voices at once. The third reached her blind side—

—and the blade was already there, moving on its own, the way it had moved for Nell on the road, the way it had moved at the tollbridge. Not her arm. Hers only in the way a riverbank belongs to the river.

She killed four of them before the fifth understood what she was. He hesitated—one heartbeat, two—and in that hesitation she saw something human flicker behind the smoked-glass eyes, some remnant of the man who had been recruited or coerced or hollowed, and she could not make herself strike. The sixth used his mercy. A knife slid toward her kidney and she turned it aside by a finger's width and put her pommel into the man's throat instead, hard enough to drop him, and then she was running again because mercy costs time and time was the one thing bleeding fastest out of the world.

The fifth hunter watched her go. She heard him weeping as she took the stairs.

* * *

The doors to the throne room were oak banded with iron, tall as three men, carved with the history of a dynasty that had stopped being real two decades ago. They were sealed. Of course they were sealed. The Vessel was inside, wearing its king like a glove, and it knew exactly what was climbing its stairs.

August pressed her palm flat against the wood.

Through the door she felt it—the presence, vast and patient and *hungry*, coiled around the throne like smoke around a wick. It had been nineteen years in this room, nineteen years eating a kingdom from the inside, and now that its lock was broken it wanted nothing in the world except to finish the meal it had started. And the last course, the final morsel, was currently standing in the hallway with her hand on the door.

It pushed against her mind, gentle as a lover's hand.

*I know what you are,* it said, without words, without sound, directly into the marrow of her. *Ishikawa. Locksmith's daughter. You carry your grandmother's jaw and your grandfather's stubbornness and a sword full of names. Come in. Sit. We could talk, you and I. I have worn crowns older than your valley. I have been worshipped by better kingdoms than this.*

"Get out of my head," August whispered.

*Your head is the smallest room I have ever been invited into,* it said, amused. *And still larger than the one I am leaving.*

Then it laughed—a sound she heard through the door, actual sound, a king's laugh rolling out from under the doors and echoing down the hall, warm and rich and utterly wrong, like honey poured over a wound.

August set her shoulder against the door and thought of Hollowmere burning. Thought of her grandmother turning back toward the house. Thought of Nell's voice tearing itself open in the crypt, of Tam's inkstained hands shaking as he handed her the counter-oath, of Sera choosing the streets over the generalship, of every single person in her ragged found family who had given something irreplaceable to bring her to this exact slab of oak.

"You don't get to laugh," she said aloud. "You don't get to wear anyone anymore."

She raised her sword.

She did not knock. Swordspeakers do not knock.

The blade went through the iron bands like they were ribbon, and she cut the doors open the way you'd gut a fish—one long stroke, downward, the oak sighing apart around her—and stepped through into the throne room of Veymar with her grandmother's name on her lips.

* * *

The room was vast and dim, lit by banks of candles that should have guttered in the draft she'd made and didn't. The windows were shuttered though it was barely past noon; the entity preferred dark, or perhaps the king did, or perhaps after nineteen years there was no longer any difference worth measuring.

And there, at the far end beneath the empty canopy of state, sat the King of Veymar on his throne.

He looked—small.

That was August's first thought, absurdly, and her second thought was grief, arriving unbidden and unwelcome. She had imagined a monster: a towering thing, crowned in shadow, radiating power. What she saw was a man slumped sideways in a chair too large for him, one hand gripping the armrest white-knuckled, the other pressed flat over his heart as if holding something in. His crown sat crooked on his head, tarnished, too big. His robes hung off him like curtains off a window in an abandoned house.

He was old. He was maybe forty-five.

"Nineteen years," the king said, and his voice cracked in the middle of the word like dry kindling. He lifted his head, and his eyes were his own—brown, bloodshot, terrified, human. No smoked glass. No candle-flame patience. Just a man looking at a girl with a sword in a room full of candles that wouldn't go out. "Nineteen years, and I thought—I hoped—if anyone came, they'd come through the doors. Not the windows. Doors mean intent. Windows mean accident." He tried to smile. Failed. "You came through the doors."

"The entity," August said carefully, advancing step by step, blade ready. "Where is it?"

"Fighting me." He coughed, wet and awful. "Losing. It's—it's been pouring out of me since your valley broke. Everything it built, everything it stored—" another cough, worse, "—it's trying to take me with it. Or stay in me. Whichever's easier. I can feel it deciding."

"Then I'm here to end the decision." She stopped ten feet away, sword level, point steady despite everything. "I have the counter-oath. I have the true name. I can unbind you."

"I know." His eyes were bright, feverish, and there was something in them that made her breath catch—gratitude, yes, but underneath it, shame, deep as a well. "I know exactly who you are, August Ishikawa. Your grandmother wrote to my father. Did you know that? Before the binding. Warning him. Warning us all." He laughed, wet and bitter. "We thought she was mad. A valley woman writing letters about crowns and parasites. My father filed her letters in the third drawer with the tax grievances."

"My grandmother," August said, and her voice shook, "died keeping your secret. My home burned for it. Children have sung themselves to death under your cathedral for it."

"I know." He said it simply, without excuse. "I know everything it did, because I've been awake inside it the whole time. Watching. Nineteen years locked in my own skull, watching my hands sign death warrants, watching my voice order hunts, watching—" his face crumpled, "—watching it feed. I screamed until I learned screaming changes nothing. Then I learned to watch. Then I learned to wait." He looked at her sword with something like tenderness. "And then, three days ago, I felt a lock break in a valley I've never seen, and I started fighting again."

August lowered the point an inch. Against every instinct, every lesson, every scar.

"Why?" she asked. "Why fight? Why now?"

"Because you're here." Simple as arithmetic. "Because if I stop fighting, it takes me cleanly and comes for you next, and everyone you love dies slower. Because—" he gripped the armrest, knuckles bloodless, "—because I wore this crown knowing what it was. I read the archives myself, before. Young and stupid and certain I could resist it. I put it on my head to keep it from the man who wanted it, and I told myself I'd take it off in a week." He smiled, and it was the worst thing August had seen all day, worse than the hunters, worse than the burning. "Nineteen years ago today, actually. Happy coronation day to me."

"The man who wanted it—"

"Dead, or serving it, depending on the hour. Doesn't matter now." He waved a trembling hand, then gasped and folded forward, and for one horrible moment August thought the entity had won. But he straightened, shaking, sweat pouring down his face, and his eyes found hers again, fierce. "Listen to me. Listen. It's weak right now but not weak enough, and it's learning fast. When you speak the true name, it will have nowhere left to go, and it will leap for the nearest vessel, and the nearest vessel will be *you*. You're the strongest thing in this room. You're the strongest thing it's found in nine hundred years."

"I know what it wants."

"Do you also know what I want?" He leaned forward, and the movement cost him visibly, a man spending coins he didn't have. "I want you to hurry. Do you understand? Every second you spend being careful, being merciful, being *human*—it spends recovering. My daughter thinks her father abandoned her. My council thinks their king went mad. An entire kingdom learned to hate magic because the thing wearing my face taught them to." Tears cut tracks through the sweat on his cheeks. "I have been a prisoner in my own body for nineteen years, August Ishikawa, and I am begging you—not as your king, I forfeited that the day I put the crown on—*as a dying man*. Hurry."

The candles flickered. All of them, at once, though there was no draft.

Between one heartbeat and the next, the temperature in the room dropped, and August felt the pressure return against her mind—but different now, no longer amused. Desperate. Cornered things bite.

*He lies,* the darkness breathed, seeping up through the floor stones like frost. *He begged me to stay, once. When I offered to leave. He was afraid of what he'd find when the fog cleared—all those orders signed in his name, all those graves. He needs me to remember for him.*

"That's not—" the king began.

*Ask him about the village of Ashford,* it said silkily. *Ask him what his hands did in Ashford, the first winter, when he tried to refuse me and I took the wheel.*

The king's face went white. "That wasn't—that was *you*—"

*My hands or yours, little king? We share so much these days.* The darkness gathered, coalescing along the walls, dripping down the banners like black rain. *Come, locksmith's daughter. Kill him if you doubt me. Put your sword through his heart and end this cleanly. One death, and the parasite has no anchor. Isn't that what your hunt-captain wanted? Isn't that the argument you lost around the fire?*

August stood very still, sword raised, in a room full of guttering candles between a dying man and a starving god.

She thought about Sera's face around that fire. About the argument that had split them. About how easy it would be—how clean—to drive the blade forward and let the crown fall empty.

And she thought about her grandmother's voice, coming from the steel: *the oath was meant to be broken, once, by choice.*

"No," August said quietly.

Both of them—king and parasite—went silent.

"I don't kill sick men," she said, stepping forward, blade singing softly, "and I don't bargain with parasites. I break oaths, and I keep promises, and right now I promise you both: this ends tonight, and neither of you gets to choose how."

The king stared at her. Something in his ruined face cracked open—not fear, not hope, something older than either. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing, for the first time in nineteen years, exactly what he'd been fighting to protect.

"Oh," he whispered. "Oh, you're *her*. You're exactly what she wrote we'd need."

The darkness along the walls surged inward, gathering itself, done talking.

August raised her sword, filled her lungs with candle-smoke and nineteen years of stolen time, and opened her mouth to speak the first syllable of her sword's true name—

—and every candle in the throne room went out.
