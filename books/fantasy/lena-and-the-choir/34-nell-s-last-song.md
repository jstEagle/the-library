## 34. Nell's Last Song

The crypt-choir had gone quiet at last.

Nell felt their silence the way she felt a held breath—not absence but pressure, all those voices gathered in the dark like water behind a dam. The dead children of the cathedral had sung for nineteen years without knowing they sang, and now August stood among them with her sword bare and her grandmother's name on her lips, and every note that remained in this buried place was waiting to see what Nell would do with it.

She was terrified. She wanted to be honest about that, even inside her own skull, even if no one ever heard it. She was terrified down to the marrow, down to the place where her stolen voices lived in their crowded tenement behind her sternum, each one a child she had never met, each one humming in its sleep.

"Nell." August's voice came through the dark like a hand on her shoulder. "You don't have to."

"I know." Nell laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "That's the whole point, isn't it? I get to choose. That's what makes it mine."

* * *

It had not always been hers. That was the wound she carried into this room, the one she had opened herself to look at only days ago, kneeling in the widow's kitchen while the truth came out of her like a splinter worked loose from old wood: the choirmaster's hands warm on her shoulders, his voice gentle as milk. *You have such a gift, Nell. Such capacity. The others' voices would be wasted, scattered. In you they can do good. In you they can serve the Light.*

She had been seven. She had been proud. She had held out her hands like a little cup and let him pour other children into her, and for eleven years she had called it grace.

Now she stood in the crypt beneath the cathedral where it had all been done, surrounded by the small stone beds where the poured-out children lay still singing, and she understood at last what she was. Not a vessel of grace. A lockbox. A purse someone else kept the key to.

Until tonight.

* * *

They had arranged themselves in the pattern Tam's decoded letters described—a ring around the binding-stone, August at the north point with her sword drawn and resting flat across both palms, Sera at the south with her shield planted like a headstone, Tam propped against the wall because he could no longer stand for long, his bandages dark at the edges, his eyes bright as a man who has finally found the page he's been searching for his whole life.

"The song carries the oath," Tam said again, quietly, the way he said things when he wanted them carved somewhere permanent. "Your voice is the road. Her voice is the traveler. The binding was built on song—it was built by singers, children, generations of them—and so it can only be unmade by song. But the words must be an Ishikawa's. An oath freely broken, spoken by the blood that swore it. You are the bridge, Nell. Nothing more and nothing less."

"Nothing more and nothing less," Nell repeated, and felt the stolen voices stir.

They stirred differently now than they used to. For eleven years they had moved when the choirmaster willed it, or when the wards pulled at her, or when fear shook them loose—candles guttering, grown men weeping, doors groaning on their hinges. But since the night she had confessed, since she had named them aloud one by one in front of people who did not flinch away from her, they had begun to feel less like cargo and more like passengers. Like a choir, in fact. Like something that might, if she asked instead of commanded, agree to sing.

She had never asked before. That was the thing no one told you about stolen magic: the theft went both directions. The choirmaster had stolen the children's voices, yes—but he had also stolen hers, the one voice that was truly hers, the one that had made candles gutter before anyone taught it anything. It was buried under all the others like a single grain of wheat under a sack of flour. She wasn't sure she'd know it if she heard it.

Tonight she would find out. Tonight everything burned.

* * *

August turned to her in the dark, and the sword-light caught her face—the set jaw, the eyes wet and furious and steady all at once, the face of a girl who had watched her valley burn and decided that grief was fuel and not an ending.

"When it starts," August said, "you'll feel me. My voice will come up through yours like—" she stopped, searched, failed. "Like water finding a channel. Don't fight it. But don't let go either. If you let go, I lose the words. And if you hold too tight—" her gaze flicked, just once, to the stone beds, to the small still shapes "—you become another room in this place. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"If it hurts too much—"

"It will hurt exactly as much as it needs to," Nell said. "That's what songs are."

August looked at her for a long moment. Then she did something she almost never did: she reached out and took Nell's hand, sword calluses against choir-soft skin, and squeezed once, hard, the way you grip someone when you're passing them something heavy that cannot be set down.

"For my grandmother," August said. "For the valley. For all of them in these stones." Her voice didn't break. Nell admired that and hated it a little. "And for you. Whatever happens after."

"Whatever happens after," Nell agreed, and did not ask what that meant, because she already knew, the way you know the shape of a door in the dark.

* * *

She began the way the choirmaster had taught her to begin—breath low in the belly, spine long, the body a bell hung properly from its beam. Old habits. She smiled around them as she used them. Let the man who stole her give her back this much: technique. The rest she took for herself.

The first note was not a word. It was an opening, the way you open a window in a house shut all winter—just a crack, just enough to feel what the air outside is doing. She felt the crypt answer. Felt the nineteen years of song still soaked into the walls like smoke into plaster. Felt the dead children turn toward her, not rising, not stirring, but *attending*, the way a congregation attends, the way the drowned attend the surface of the water above them.

Then she reached inward, past her own small frightened voice, and knocked.

*Come,* she said, in the language below language, the one the choirmaster had never known because he only ever took and never gave. *All of you. Come sing with me. One last time, and this time it's ours.*

They rose in her like dawn coming up over water.

Not a flood—she had braced for a flood, for the old terrible sensation of being poured full by someone else's will. This was different. They came the way children come to a fire in winter, crowding close, jostling, laughing in that breathless way, each one pressing forward to see. Mira who loved the bells. Osric who hummed while he ate. Little Wren, youngest of them all, whose voice had guttered a hundred candles in the nave above and who had died with the song still in her mouth. They came, and they stood inside her ribs like a choir taking its places on risers, and Nell understood with a clarity that felt like falling: they had always been willing. Even stolen, even spent, even buried—they had only ever needed someone to ask.

Her own voice rose underneath theirs, thin and true, the grain of wheat surfacing through the flour, and she wept without stopping the sound, because grief and joy were using the same throat tonight and there was no time to give them separate rooms.

Then August spoke.

* * *

It came up through Nell like water finding a channel, exactly as promised, and nothing like anything she had ever felt. August's voice was not beautiful. It was not trained. It was a valley voice, plain and flinty, worn smooth by restraint the way river stones are worn by holding still—and when it entered Nell's chest it entered like a blade entering a sheath made for it, precise, inevitable, *home*.

The oath-song had two halves. Tam had found the first half in the king's letters; the second half had lived in August's family for nine generations, passed mouth to ear in a valley that forgot, and August had learned it from a sword that spoke in her grandmother's voice. Now the halves met in Nell's throat and locked together with a sound she felt in her teeth, in the stone under her feet, in the nineteen-year-old binding itself—a vast slow turning, like a mill wheel beginning to move after decades of rust.

She sang the words she didn't understand and was glad she didn't. Some things should be carried, not comprehended. She sang *the lock is broken by the hand that set it*, and the crypt's standing stones began to sweat light. She sang *the debt is paid by the blood that owes it*, and the small stone beds flickered like lamps. She sang *freely given, freely taken back*, and felt August's whole history pour through her—the sharpening of a nameless sword, the crier at the mill, the cellar tunnels, the ridge above a burning valley, a grandmother who stayed behind so the last lock would have something left to unlock.

And the stolen voices burned.

She felt it start in the lowest register, the deepest of them, the ones the choirmaster had taken first, and it was not pain at first—it was *spending*, the way a candle spends itself, gladly, in the act of being light. Each voice as it burned gave off its own small color of sound. Mira's was gold. Osric's was brown and warm as bread. Wren's was white, white, blindingly white, and when it went Nell cried out and kept singing, because the song did not need her to be whole, it only needed her to be open.

The crypt filled with light that was also music that was also names. The dead children sat up in their stone beds—not bodies, never bodies, only the singing part of them, the part that had been kept working past death—and for one impossible moment the crypt-choir sang with Nell, nineteen years of forced service transfigured in a single bar of chosen song, and then they went out, gently, all together, like a room of candles at the end of a vigil.

The binding broke with a sound like a mountain exhaling.

Nell felt it go through her and out of her and away, north and east and west, across rivers and valleys, to a place she had never seen called Hollowmere where a seal older than the kingdom shattered into weather. She felt it leave. And she felt the last of the stolen voices rise in her—not burning now but *leaving*, winging free, every child she had carried going home to whatever waits for sung-out souls—and then there was silence in her chest.

Real silence. Eleven years of crowd-noise, gone.

She had forgotten what it was like, being only herself inside.

* * *

She was on her knees. She didn't remember falling. The light was fading off the stones, settling back into ordinary dark, and August was beside her with the sword still bare and both hands pressed over Nell's, and August was saying her name—*Nell, Nell, stay with me*—in that flinty valley voice that was suddenly, terribly, just a voice again, with no song riding it.

"I'm here." Her own voice came out scraped, small, unfamiliar. She put a hand to her sternum and pressed, the way you press a bruise to learn its borders. Empty. Warm. Empty. "I'm here. It's quiet."

"Don't say it like that—"

"No." Nell looked up, and found she was smiling, and the smile surprised her more than anything else that night. "No, August. Listen." She breathed in, deep, and the air went all the way down to the bottom of her lungs for the first time in eleven years, no crowd in the way, no borrowed weight. "It's quiet, and I'm still here. I'm still *me*. Do you know how long it's been since I was only me?"

August's face did something complicated—grief and relief fighting for the same ground—and then the whole crypt shuddered.

Dust sifted from the vaulting. The binding-stone at the center of the ring, black and seamless for nineteen years, split down the middle with a crack like ice giving way, and from the crack came a sound that was not a sound, a pressure, a *presence*, vast and cold and awake, the way a well is awake at the bottom, the way winter is awake behind a closed door.

Sera was on her feet with her shield up. Tam was dragging himself upright along the wall, clutching the counter-oath scroll to his chest like a man clutching a plank in open water. Above them, muffled through nineteen feet of stone and earth, the cathedral began to toll its great bell—no hand upon it, no rope, the clapper swinging wild—and beneath the bell, rising through the floor like water through a cracked foundation, came a voice that Nell recognized from the widow's warning, from Tam's histories, from the oldest nightmares of the archive:

*Vessel-of-Crowns.*

It knew the lock was broken. And somewhere above them, in the palace on the hill, it was already reaching for the throne room—and for the swordspeaker whose oath had freed it.

August rose, sword in hand, and Nell saw her grandmother's steadiness settle over her like a mantle.

"Hold the song in your memory," August said quietly, to Nell alone. "However this ends—I'll want it sung right."

Then she ran for the stairs, and the dark swallowed her, and the bell above went on tolling like a heart that would not stop.
