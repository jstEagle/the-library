## 37. What Tam Left Behind

Tam Osgood had spent thirty years learning how paper died.

He knew the way ink faded to ghost-grey in damp cellars, the way vellum curled when it remembered being skin, the way a book left too long in sunlight went brittle as autumn leaf and crumbled at a careless touch. He had catalogued ten thousand small deaths of the written word, and he had always believed—had built his whole quiet life on the belief—that a man who understood how things ended could never be surprised by his own.

He was wrong about that. Dying, it turned out, was nothing like a book. A book did not hurt. A book did not grow heavier by the hour, as though some archivist in the sky were pressing him flat between boards. A book did not keep trying to speak.

He lay in the scriptorium of the ruined monastery above the river, on a pallet Nell had made from choir robes and old altar cloth, and he listened to his own breath the way he used to listen to a suspect document: for the forgery in it, the hitch that betrayed the hand beneath the hand. There was a hitch now. It came more often than not.

The counter-oath lay on his chest, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the plain grey cord he had used for forty years to bundle tax ledgers. Beneath it, the true histories—nine volumes, satchel-worn, their spines re-sewn twice by his own needle. He had carried them out of the capital sewn into the false bottom of a grain cart, through three checkpoints, past a boy soldier who had looked Tam full in the face and seen only an old man's fear, which was, thank every patient god, unremarkable enough to pass.

He had thought, in those hours in the cart among the turnips, that if he lived long enough to put them down somewhere safe, the guilt would lift. Thirty years of guilt, since the day a young archivist with beautiful handwriting had copied out an Edict clause he should have burned instead. He had imagined relief like a fever breaking.

It had not come. What had come instead was something quieter and far more frightening: the understanding that guilt was not a weight you set down. It was a thing you handed on, carefully, to someone strong enough to carry it—and that choosing who was the last real act of an archivist's life.

Footsteps on the stair. Small ones, quick ones, with the particular rhythm of someone trying not to hurry because hurrying would mean admitting there was something to hurry toward.

Nell came through the doorway with her sleeves pushed up and candle-smoke still in her hair, and she stopped when she saw him watching her, the way she always did—as though she'd been caught at something.

"You're awake," she said. "Good. Sera said—I mean, she didn't say anything, she just stood in the courtyard looking at the sky, which is what she does instead of saying things." She crossed the room and knelt by the pallet, and her eyes went to the bundle on his chest and then away again, quickly, the way one looks away from an open grave. "How is your—"

"Tell me about the singing," Tam said.

She blinked. "What?"

"Last night. I heard it through the floor. Not the oath-song. Something else. Something older." He smiled, and felt how thin the smile must have looked, because hers wobbled. "I have spent my whole life among dead voices, Nell. Paper voices. I know the sound of one that isn't being forced."

Her hands folded in her lap. For a moment she was silent, and in the silence he could hear the monastery around them—the drip of water in the cloister, wind worrying the broken bell, Sera's boots pacing gravel below, August somewhere on the walls keeping a watch she no longer needed to keep but could not yet stop keeping. The company, holding its shape around him like cupped hands.

"The crypt-choir," Nell said finally. "I've been singing to them. The ones we couldn't free." Her voice did something complicated. "Their own names are mostly worn off the registers. So I sing the parts of their songs they would have known best, and I leave spaces, and sometimes—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "Sometimes something fills the space. Just for a note or two. And then it's done, and it can rest."

"That," Tam said, "is the finest piece of archival work I have ever witnessed."

She laughed—a wet, startled sound—and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "You're impossible."

"I am a professional. There is a difference, though admittedly a narrow one." He shifted, and the pain moved through him like weather, and he let it pass before he spoke again, because what he had to say next deserved a steady voice. "Nell. Bring me the table. And the lamp. And sit where I can see your face. I have a great deal to say and very little time in which to be insufferable about it, and I intend to use both fully."

* * *

They made a desk of his knees, which would have horrified the man he had been a year ago—that fussy, buttoned creature in the royal archive who ironed his maps and apologized to furniture. The counter-oath unwrapped under Nell's careful fingers: a single sheet, four hundred years old, written in the hand of the first council of unsworn, its ink gone the brown of dried blood though it was only oak gall and time.

"This is the part nobody understands," Tam said, "because I made sure of it. When I leaked the Edict's hidden clauses, I gave them the truth in pieces. The binding. The king. The key. But I kept this back—not from cowardice. Or not only." His mouth twisted. "From craft. A secret given away all at once is a weapon that fires once. A secret rationed is a siege engine. I told myself that, anyway, in the dark years, whenever the shame got loud."

"And now?"

"Now the siege is over, and engines are only good for firewood unless someone knows how to aim them." He touched the edge of the page. "Read the third line aloud. Slowly. This text was meant to be spoken; it has been silent too long."

Nell bent over it. She read the way she did everything—with her whole body, her voice dropping into that register that made candles gutter, except that here, in this room, with him, it only made the lamp flame stand up straighter, attentive, like a student.

"*That which is bound by consent may be held by consent,*" she read. "*Let no crown, no chain, no law do what a willing keeper's word can do.*"

"Yes." He closed his eyes and saw it all again, the way he had seen it a hundred times in the archive's lamplight: the young king's letters, the terrible arithmetic of a boy who found a devouring thing in a box of regalia and understood that if he did not wear it, someone worse would. Nineteen years of a man being eaten slowly so that the kingdom would not be eaten quickly. "August caged it with her oath. But oaths outlive their makers' intentions, Nell. That is their nature and their danger. Someday—fifty years, a hundred—someone will need to know why the cage was built this way, and what it costs, and how to open it without breaking it. That knowledge cannot live in one head. One head is how we got Vessel-of-Crowns in the first place."

He opened his eyes and looked at her, really looked, memorizing her the way he memorized documents: the ink-stain on her left thumb, the chip in her front tooth, the way her brows drew together when she was frightened and refused to show it.

"I am naming you keeper of the archive," he said. "Not deputy. Not custodian. Keeper. The full succession, back to Osgood the First, who stole the abbey chronicles out of a burning library in the year the river froze, and was proud of it until the day he died, and rightly so."

Nell went very still. "Tam. You're—you can't just—"

"I can. I have. Watch how easily it's done." He tried for lightness and heard it crack, and abandoned the attempt, because there was no time left for the armor of jokes and he had wasted too much of his life wearing it. "Listen to me. Blood doesn't make keepers. I had no children; the archive was my child, and look how well that turned out—it grew up to be a bureaucracy that fed children into a crypt. Institutions fail, Nell. They fail because people serve the institution instead of the purpose. But you—" his voice caught, steadied— "you sang spaces into the dark last night and let the dead finish their own sentences. That is the entire discipline. Everything else is cataloguing."

"I'm not trained. I can't read half the old scripts. I don't even—"

"You learned the cathedral's repertoire by ear at eight years old. You taught yourself harmony no choirmaster showed you. You read the king's letters with me for six weeks and last Tuesday you corrected my dating of the Greyford tolls." He took her hand. Her fingers were cold; singers' hands always were, he'd noticed, as though the voice drew the warmth up and out. "Training is just love that has been organized. You have the love. We will organize it together for as long as I have—which, if the pain in my chest is any historian, is somewhat less time than we would prefer."

"Don't," she whispered. "Don't talk like that."

"Nell." Gently. "I have spent thirty years pretending I wasn't dying of the thing I did. Let me spend my last days doing the opposite. It's a much better way to go. I recommend it to everyone."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, because grief is bearable in motion and unbearable at rest, and because Tam Osgood had never in his life met a deadline he respected more than this one.

He dictated. She wrote—in her own hand, not his, and he made her promise never to imitate his, because an archive needed many hands the way a choir needed many voices. The catalogue of the nine volumes, volume by volume, with the provenance of each and the lies that had been told about each. The map of the hidden clauses still unrepealed, the laws that would need unpicking thread by thread. The names of the crypt-choir children as far as he had recovered them—one hundred and six, and he recited each one twice so her copy would be sure, and watched her write them, and understood that this was the penance he had actually wanted all along: not forgiveness, but usefulness. To be, at the end, a vessel that carried something true across the water.

Sera came once, stood in the doorway with her arms crossed like a woman guarding a border, and asked gruffly if he needed anything. He asked her to take dictation for a single paragraph—the disposition of the company's evidence against the Silent Order, in case the council needed it—and she sat on a stool too small for her and wrote in a hand like sword-cuts, and when she finished she looked at the page for a long moment and said, "You know, in nineteen years, you're the first person in that palace who ever told me the truth in writing," and then she left quickly, the way soldiers leave rooms where feelings have happened.

August came at dusk and said almost nothing, which was its own kind of eloquence. She stood by the window with the last light on her face and the sword at her hip—named now, resting, its long hunger quieted—and when Tam finished explaining where the duplicate of the counter-oath was hidden (three copies, Nell, always three, one to work from, one to hide, one to lose), August said only, "My grandmother would have liked you, Tam. She trusted people who kept their promises badly and kept making them anyway."

He laughed until it hurt, which was appropriate, and possibly the point.

* * *

Night. The others had withdrawn to the courtyard, giving the room its privacy the way people give a bed to the dying—tenderly, and with visible relief at having somewhere else to stand.

Nell trimmed the lamp and sat beside him, the new ledger open on her knees, her copying finished, her hands finally empty.

"There's one more thing," Tam said. "Not in the ledger. In you."

She waited.

"When I was young—before the archive, before everything—I wanted to be a chronicler. Not an archivist. A chronicler. Someone who writes what happens while it's happening, so the future gets the truth warm instead of cold." He looked at the ceiling beams, black with four centuries of smoke. "I chose the archive because it was safe. Paper doesn't look at you. Dead voices don't ask you to take sides. And then I spent thirty years discovering that safety was just a slower way of taking sides—the wrong one, quietly, with excellent penmanship." He turned his head to look at her. "So here is the last instruction of the Osgood succession, and it supersedes everything in the ledger: don't keep the histories safe. Keep them *alive*. Read them aloud. Sing them, if that's what they need. An archive that no one visits is just a tomb with better lighting, and God knows this kingdom has enough tombs."

Nell's eyes were shining. "I'll read them aloud," she said. "Every year. All of them. Even the boring ones."

"*Especially* the boring ones. The boring ones are where they hide the crimes." He smiled, and then, because there was no one left to perform for and no reason left to perform, he let the smile become what it actually was. "Thank you. For kneeling in a ditch outside Greyford and deciding the trembling old man in the grain dust was worth saving. I have been a debt my whole life, Nell. You're the first one who ever paid me."

"Tam—"

"Shh. Listen." He raised one finger, and his voice dropped into the register he saved for colophons and last pages. "By the authority vested in me by Osgood the First, thief of chronicles, and by every keeper since who chose the truth over the comfortable lie—I name you keeper. The archive passes to you by choice, freely given, freely taken. Say 'I take it.'"

Her voice, when it came, was very small and absolutely steady. "I take it."

"Lamp flame, witness it," he murmured, because the lamp had flared—just slightly, just enough—and he was an archivist to the marrow and believed in recording omens. "There. Entered into the record. No appeal."

Then, because the work was done and the cord was tied and the ledger was dry, Tam Osgood did the thing he had been postponing for thirty years. He closed his eyes. He let his hands rest. He breathed, and the hitch came, and he noted it without alarm, the way one notes a date in a margin: *here ends the account.*

But the account did not end cleanly, because accounts never do.

* * *

He surfaced once, near dawn, in the grey hour when the monastery's broken bell hung silent and the river below ran the color of unpolished silver. Pain had receded to something distant and administrative. Nell was asleep in the chair beside him, the ledger still open on her lap, one hand curled protectively over the page as though even dreaming she guarded the record.

And in the doorway—though he knew, distantly, that dying men see doorways full of things—he saw his archive. Not the building. The idea of it. Shelves going back further than shelves should, and on them every voice he had ever catalogued, waiting, patient as paper, and among them new spaces already prepared, labeled in a hand he did not recognize and somehow knew: *the reign of the council. the songs of the freed. the keeper's own.*

"Well," Tam said, to the doorway, to the dawn, to whatever clerk kept the great ledger beyond it. "I find I have no corrections."

He closed his eyes. The last thing he heard was not his own breath, hitching toward its final page.

It was Nell, waking, beginning softly to hum—and the dead voices of the crypt-choir, miles behind them and forever ahead of her, rising one by one to fill the spaces she left, like light finding the shelves of a room where someone had finally, at long last, opened the door.

Below in the courtyard, hooves on gravel: a rider coming up the valley road at a gallop, carrying news from the capital that would not wait for mourning.

But that, as Tam Osgood would have been the first to insist, belongs to the next entry.
