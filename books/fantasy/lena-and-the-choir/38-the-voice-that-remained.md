## 38. The Voice That Remained

The silence after the last note was the loudest thing Nell had ever heard.

She stood in the crypt with her hands still raised, fingers curved around a voice that was no longer there. The stolen magic had gone out of her like a tide going out—she could feel the shape of its absence, the way an amputee feels a hand. For nineteen years of her life, or so it seemed, she had carried other children's voices in her throat, and now they were gone, sung out into the dark, given back to the small bones arranged in rows before her.

And her own voice remained.

It was such a small thing. Such a thin, unremarkable thing. When she tried to speak, what came out was reedy and cracked and ordinary, a choirgirl's speaking voice with no power in it at all. She said, "Oh," to no one, and it sounded like nothing, and she began to laugh, and then to cry, because it was the most beautiful sound she had ever made.

"Nell." August's hand on her shoulder, steady as a keel. "Nell, sit down before you fall down."

"I'm fine," Nell said, and her voice cracked on *fine*, cracked like an egg, like a shell, like something hatching. "August. I'm fine. Do you hear me? I'm *fine*."

"You're swaying."

"I'm standing in a crypt full of dead children who just got their voices back, and I'm *fine*." She laughed again, wet and wild. "I have never been this fine in my entire life."

* * *

They brought her up out of the dark in stages. Sera carried her partway, when her legs gave out on the crypt stairs; Tam would have helped but he was past helping anyone, propped against August's side with the counter-oath scroll pressed to his chest like a child's toy, his breath whistling thin through him. The palace above them was still shuddering with the aftershocks of the binding's unraveling—the Vessel-of-Crowns caged now in August's willing keeping, the king freed and dying in his ruined throne room—but none of that touched Nell just then. None of it could. She had gone somewhere past fear, past grief, into a country where only one thing was true:

Her voice was hers.

They laid her in a guardroom off the palace's eastern corridor, on a cot that smelled of leather oil and old stone. Someone—Sera, probably—put a blanket over her. Someone else put a cup of water in her hands. She drank it without tasting it and stared at the ceiling and listened to herself breathe.

For years, breathing had been complicated. Breathing was the engine of song, and song was not hers—it was the choirmaster's, the cathedral's, the wards'. Every breath she drew had been drawn for someone else's purpose. Even her panic-singing on the road, even the night her voice had answered August's sword, had felt like being played rather than playing. An instrument. A vessel. A girl-shaped pipe through which other people's music passed.

Now there was just her. Just breath and bone and a small cracked voice that belonged to nobody but Nell.

She slept, eventually, and dreamed of nothing at all, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

When she woke, gray light was coming through a high slit window, and Tam was dying three rooms away, and everyone knew it, and everyone was pretending, in the gentle way of people who have run out of miracles, that pretending might help.

She sat with him in the afternoon. His eyes were bright and feverish, but they found her and sharpened, the way they always did when he was about to say something he'd rehearsed.

"The archive needs a keeper," he said.

"Tam—"

"Hear me. I've spent my whole life guilty, Nell. Guilt is a debt you can't pay forward, only down. I drew the map. I fed the Order its first names. Everything that burned started with my hand on paper." He coughed, wet and long, and she held the cup to his lips without being asked, because some things you learn in a found family: how to hold water to a dying man's mouth. "But archives aren't about guilt. They're about memory. And memory needs someone who knows what it costs to be forgotten." His hand found hers, papery and light. "You were forgotten inside your own body. You know better than any archivist alive what it means when a voice is taken and kept. Keep the voices. All of them. Especially the ones nobody wants."

"I don't know anything about archives."

"You know everything about being archived." He smiled, faint and terrible. "That's the qualification. There isn't another one."

She wanted to refuse. She wanted to say *you're going to live, you're going to see Hollowmere rebuilt, you're going to argue with Sera about road rations for thirty more years.* But his hand was cooling in hers, and lying to the dying is a cruelty dressed as kindness, and Nell had spent enough of her life inside lies.

"Yes," she said. "I'll keep them."

"Good girl." His eyes drifted toward the window. "Sing something sometime. Not for wards. Not for power. Just—" his voice frayed, "—just because it's yours."

* * *

He died at dusk, two days later, with the counter-oath finished and witnessed and the true histories copied in three hands. They buried him where he asked: not in the palace grounds, but outside the city walls, under a plain stone, facing north, toward all the roads he'd never walked clean.

Nell did not sing at the funeral. She couldn't—not yet. Her own voice was still a stranger to her, a room in her own house she'd never entered, and every time she opened the door she found it full of echoes that weren't hers. The choirmaster's cadences. The cathedral's harmonies. Thirty-one children's stolen timbres, layered over her own like paint over wood.

So instead she went back down.

Down into the crypt, alone, with a candle and a stool borrowed from the guardroom, and she sat among the bones of the crypt-choir and did the thing she had promised herself she would do, the thing that had been waiting in her since the moment August spoke the true name and the binding came apart.

She learned her own voice.

It was slow work. Her speaking voice was easy enough—thin, quick, a little sharp at the edges, the voice of a girl who'd grown up watching everything. But her singing voice, her real one, buried under nineteen years of theft—that took finding. She hummed in the dark until she found a note that didn't wobble. Then another. Then a third that fit the first two like stones in an arch.

And underneath her, in the candlelit quiet, the crypt-choir waited.

There were forty-one of them. Tam's records had named them all, in the end—the archivist's final gift, pages of small names in faded ink, dates of arrival, dates of death, and beside each name, in his cramped careful script: *voice recovered, [date], witness: N. [surname withheld by request]*. Forty-one children, some of them decades dead, whose voices had been fed into the living choir generation after generation, powering the binding that kept the crown-entity leashed. Kept singing past death. Kept working past the end of their childhoods.

Nobody had ever sung *for* them. Only with them, unwillingly, unknowingly. Nobody had ever sung them home.

Nell meant to be the first.

* * *

She started on the fourth night.

Not with magic—there wasn't any left, not in her, not anymore. What she had was training, eleven years of cathedral discipline ground into her like grain into flour, and a memory that held every hymn, every lament, every cradle-song the kingdom had ever taught its choir children. Including the ones the cathedral didn't know it still taught her: the old songs, the valley songs, the ones August hummed sometimes at the fire without seeming to notice she was doing it, songs about swords resting and oaths kept and home remembered.

She sang the first one quietly, almost apologetically, her new voice feeling its way along the melody like a bare foot on cold stone.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. The dead don't applaud.

But she stayed anyway. She sang it again, steadier. And on the third time through, something shifted—not in the air, not in the candleflame, but in *her*. A loosening, low in her chest, like a knot she'd been tied in since childhood finally slipping loose. Because here was the truth she'd been circling for days: the stolen voices hadn't vanished when the binding broke. They had gone *home*. Into these bones, these small graves, these forty-one children who had never once been asked.

And a voice, Nell thought, is not a thing you use. It's a thing you answer.

So she answered them.

She sang the naming-song next—the one from the valley, the one August's grandmother must have sung, the one where every verse ends with a name spoken back to the darkness so the darkness remembers you were here. She didn't know their names yet, not most of them. So she sang the verses with the names left open, gaps in the music like empty chairs, and she promised, between one verse and the next, in plain speech, in her own cracked ordinary voice:

"I'll learn them. Every one. I have the list. I'm the keeper now. You're not forgotten—you're just early. Wait for me."

The candle guttered. Or maybe it didn't. Maybe it was just the draft from the stairs, where footsteps were descending, unhurried, familiar.

August came down with a second candle and a loaf of bread and the particular expression of someone who has decided not to ask permission. She set the bread on the step, sat down on the floor beside Nell's stool, and listened to the end of the verse without interrupting, which was its own kind of gift.

"They told me you come down here every night," August said, when the silence settled. "Sera's worried. She thinks you're haunting yourself."

"Someone has to sing them home."

"Why you?"

Nell looked at her. In the candlelight, August's face was still marked with the cost of everything—the oath broken, the entity caged, the king buried that same week beneath a plainer stone than Tam's—but her eyes were steady, and they waited for the answer honestly, the way August always waited, as if the person answering might surprise her.

"Because I was them," Nell said. "A voice kept in a place I didn't choose, doing work I didn't understand, for people who called it holy." She turned back to the bones, the small skulls with their candle-gilding. "Nobody sang me out. I walked out myself, half-broken, and got lucky, and found you. These ones didn't get lucky. So the luck has to go backward, somehow. Down the years. To them."

August was quiet a long moment. Then she said, "My grandmother used to say mourning is just love with nowhere to go."

"It has somewhere to go," Nell said. "It goes into the song. That's the whole trick of it. That's what the choirmaster never understood—he thought a voice was fuel. It's not. It's a door. You sing, and something on the other side gets to walk through."

"And what's on the other side?"

"Rest," Nell said simply. "I think it's rest."

* * *

It took her a season.

She worked through the autumn while the city above them argued itself into a council, while Sera refused a generalship with a straight spine and a hidden smile, while August trained the first unsworn students in a courtyard that had once been a barracks yard. Nell went down into the crypt every evening with Tam's ledger and a candle, and she read a name, and she learned it, and she sang it into the dark until the dark held it.

*Maren, who arrived aged seven and loved the smell of beeswax.*

*Tobin, who arrived aged nine and was afraid of the dark, and was made to sing in it anyway.*

*Sela, who arrived aged six, and whose voice was the first one the choirmaster ever took, and who had been singing in the walls of the cathedral for sixty years without one single soul knowing her name.*

On the last night of autumn, Nell sang Sela's verse, and then she sang the closing verse—the one with no gap in it, the one where the singer's own name goes into the dark last, so the darkness sends it back.

And when she finished, she did not hear applause, because the dead don't applaud. But she heard—she would swear this to August later, over wine, laughing and crying at once—she heard the crypt feel *empty*. Not hollow. Empty the way a bed is empty in the morning, after good sleep. The difference between absence and completion.

Forty-one voices, sung home. Forty-one names, remembered. And underneath them, thin and cracked and entirely her own, one voice remaining: Nell's. Hers. Unstolen, unspent, unmagnified. A choirgirl's voice with no power in it at all except the power every voice has, which is to say *here, I was, listen.*

She climbed the stairs slowly, candle in hand, and found the others waiting at the top—August with her sword slung easy across her back, named at last and resting; Sera with her arms crossed and her doubt worn openly, which was how she wore affection; and behind them, in the doorway, a gaggle of August's new students, come to gawk at the famous singer of the crypt-choir, whispering among themselves.

"Well?" Sera said. "Done?"

"Done," Nell said.

"Then we're leaving at dawn," August said. "All of us. North road, then west. There's a valley that needs remembering, and I'm told the roads are safe now." She smiled, tired and true. "And I'm told there's a singer coming with us who owes the valley a song it's never heard—one with no stolen notes in it anywhere."

Nell looked at her own hands, empty of magic, full of morning.

"Dawn," she said, and her voice held.
