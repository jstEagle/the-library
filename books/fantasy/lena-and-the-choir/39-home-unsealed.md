## 39. Home, Unsealed

The road into the valley had not changed, and that was the first thing that undid her.

August had braced herself for ruin. She had rehearsed it on the long ride north from the capital—through Greyford where the river ran brown with spring melt, through the tollbridge where they had buried two of the Silent Order's dead under cairns Sera built without being asked—rehearsed the sight of Hollowmere as a wound. Charred beams. The mill fallen in on itself. Ash in the shape of a town.

What she found instead was green.

The valley had spent three years doing what valleys do: covering its grief in grass and wildflower and the pale new wood of birch colonizing every burn scar. Fireweed grew in pink drifts along the old cairn-road, so thick the horses waded through it to the fetlock. The river still came down off the high snowfields cold and loud, and the millrace still chattered over its stones as if nothing had ever been asked of it but water.

"August." Nell reined in beside her, one hand pressed flat over her sternum the way she did when something moved through her that wasn't hers—or wasn't only hers. "Do you feel that?"

She felt it. That was the thing. For twenty-two years the valley had been silence to her—a held breath, a room with the windows painted shut. Her gift had slept here like a stone at the bottom of a well, and she had learned to call the stone *peace* because the alternative was calling it a cage.

Now, crossing the invisible line where the seal had stood, she felt magic pour into the valley like light into an opened room.

It came up through her boots first. Through the soles, through the bones of her feet, up the shins her grandmother had once wrapped in linen against the cold of cellar floors. It tasted of iron and rain and something older underneath, mineral and patient, the way the mountain itself must taste to itself. Her sword hummed against her hip—not the urgent, wordless thrum of battle, but a low sustained note, the sound a tuning fork makes when you finally set it down on the right table.

"It knows this place," August said.

"It remembers it," Nell corrected gently. Nell was very precise about remembering now. She had earned the precision. "There's a difference."

Sera said nothing. Sera rarely said anything on the last mile of any approach; she rode with her weight forward and her eyes moving, some habits carved too deep to file smooth. But August saw her hand rest on her thigh near where her sword would hang if she still wore one, and saw her take in the tree line, the ridge, the burned foundations—and then, slowly, deliberately, let all of it go. Let her shoulders come down. In Sera's language, that was weeping.

They came down into Hollowmere proper as the afternoon slanted gold between the peaks.

The town was rebuilt the way a scar is rebuilt: honestly. New timber sat bright beside old stone. Some houses had risen exactly where they fell, same footprints, same crooked doorways, because the people who came back were people who loved their crooked doorways. Others had been raised a street over, turned to face the sun instead of the old defensive lines, because the people who came back were also people who had decided, quietly and forever, that no one was coming down those ridges again. The Edict was ash. The Order was broken. The crown belonged, for the moment, to a council that included a widow who kept unsworn registers and a girl who sang the dead to sleep.

And the people—

The people stopped in the street when the three riders came down the cairn-road.

August had imagined this moment too, on the road, and in every imagining it had gone badly. She had imagined eyes sliding away. She had imagined the particular silence of a town deciding what a person is worth now that the danger of her has passed or the usefulness of her has ended. She had imagined being a story told about the valley rather than a person in it.

What happened was that Old Marta dropped her basket.

Apples went rolling everywhere, down the gutter, into the dust, and Marta didn't care, didn't even look at them, just stood there with her hands open at her sides like a woman catching rain, and said, in the voice she'd used to call August home to supper across twenty years of evenings:

"There she is. There she is, look at her, look at her face—" and her voice broke, and half the street broke with it.

August got down off her horse because her legs made the decision before her pride could vote. Marta caught her by the arms—the same arms that had once pulled burrs out of her hair, that had smacked flour off her shoulder, that had gone stiff and strange the day the crier read the Edict in the square—and held her at arm's length and looked at her, really looked, the way you look at someone returned from a country you'd stopped believing anyone comes back from.

"You got tall," Marta said stupidly, wetly. "You were already tall. You got taller."

"Marta—"

"Don't you 'Marta' me. Three years. Three years and not one letter, not one word, and I find out from a traveling peddler that you put down a king's demon and broke a thousand-year oath and—" She stopped. Her grip tightened. "Your grandmother's house," she said. "We kept it. We argued about it, whole town, nearly came to blows over whether to rebuild it or leave it, and in the end we left it. Come see."

* * *

Her grandmother's house stood at the end of Weaver's Lane, and it had not been rebuilt, and it had not been allowed to fall.

Someone—Marta, probably, though she'd deny it—had kept the roof patched. New shakes over the old scorch. The garden had gone wild and then been half-tamed again: rosemary run riot, the bee balm taller than August remembered, the little stone wall her grandfather built three generations ago still standing its ground against the hillside. The door was closed. Of course it was closed. No one lived there. But it was *kept*, and August understood, standing in the lane with her hand on the latch, that the town had done for this house exactly what she had done for her grandmother's memory: not preserved it in glass, not let it rot, but tended it. Kept it alive enough to visit.

Inside smelled of cedar and dust and, faintly, impossibly, of the dried yarrow bundles her grandmother had hung from the rafters, which should have crumbled years ago and somehow hadn't.

The whetstone was still on the table.

August crossed the room and picked it up before she knew she was moving. It fit her palm the way it always had, worn to a shallow curve by four generations of Ishikawa thumbs, hers the latest groove in a long line. On the table beside it lay the oilcloth, folded. The corner of the hearth where her grandmother used to sit had a chair in it—not the original chair, that had burned, but a chair, placed there, facing the room, as if waiting.

"She'd have hated this," August said. Her voice came out strange. "All this tending. She'd have said a house should be lived in or let go."

"And yet she spent sixty years keeping a sword unnamed," Sera said from the doorway. "People are full of contradictions. It's the best thing about us."

Nell had drifted to the window. She did that in old rooms—stood where the light came in and listened with her whole body, the way other people read. Now she turned, and her expression made August's chest tighten.

"There's a song in here," Nell said. "Not mine. Not anyone's anymore. Just—it soaked into the walls. Someone sang in this kitchen for a very long time. Work songs, I think. And lullabies." A pause. "One song over and over, low, almost talking. Like a woman memorizing something so she'd never lose it."

August's throat closed.

She knew that song. She had heard it her whole childhood without knowing it was anything but a habit, a humming, the sound of her grandmother's hands busy. It was only later—in the crypt, under the cathedral, with the dead children singing and the Vessel-of-Crowns pressing against the inside of the world—that August had recognized the melody for what it was: the first half of the binding-song. The lock-side. Her grandmother had sung the oath to herself for sixty years the way a soldier cleans a weapon. Not because she feared forgetting. Because forgetting was not permitted to be an accident.

"She was keeping it," August said. "Even here. Even in the one place magic couldn't hear her. She kept it anyway, in case."

"In case of what?" Nell asked softly.

"In case the valley ever failed." August looked down at the whetstone in her hand. "In case I ever had to learn it somewhere else."

* * *

They stayed the night in the house. Marta would hear of nothing else, and neither, it turned out, would the town; by dusk there was bread on the table and honey and a joint of mutton and a dozen neighbors who had come to "just drop something off"—a jar of preserves, a bundle of candles, a knitted blanket nobody would admit to having made—and who then stayed, filling the kitchen her grandmother had kept so fiercely empty of everything except duty.

August sat in her grandmother's chair-corner and let it happen around her.

That was the strangest grace of it. All her life, hospitality in Hollowmere had been rationed toward her family—warm, but watchful, the warmth of people who had agreed generations ago not to ask questions and were proud of their discipline. Tonight there was no watching. A farmer named Aldous, whose father had once crossed the street to avoid passing the Ishikawa gate, sat across from her and asked, openly, hungrily, what it had been like in the capital, what the entity looked like, whether the stories about the singing girl were true. When August hesitated, he said, "You don't have to spare us. We're not children. We buried people."

So she told them. All of it, or as much as could be borne at a kitchen table: the crypt-choir, and how the room had gone silent when Nell sang the dead free. Tam, and the archive, and the counter-oath he'd bought with his life, and how Nell carried his true histories now in a satchel she slept with under her head. Sera burning her commission in a brazier while her own soldiers watched. The throne room. The name spoken. The king, freed, dying, choosing his council with his last clear breath.

When she finished, the fire had burned low, and no one spoke for a while, and it was not an uncomfortable silence. It was the silence of people re-weighing things.

Then Aldous said, "My daughter has the gift."

The words landed in the kitchen like a stone in still water.

"She's seven," he went on, staring at the table. "Started last winter, after the seal broke. Little things. The knife sharpens itself when she's frustrated. Candles lean toward her when she laughs. My wife wants to hide it. I keep telling her—" His voice caught. "I keep telling her there's a swordspeaker sitting right in front of me who can tell me what to do. If there's anyone left in this kingdom who'd know."

August looked at him for a long moment. Then at Nell, who was watching her with that steady, unhurried attention she gave to important moments. Then at Sera, who gave the smallest nod—the nod that meant *this is yours, and I'll stand behind whatever you choose.*

"Yes," August said. "I'd know. Bring her tomorrow. Bring anyone. Bring everyone."

* * *

She walked the valley alone before dawn.

She hadn't planned to. She woke in her childhood bed with her heart going hard and the sword-note in her ears, and instead of lying there she dressed and went out into the blue hour, when the mountains were still holding the night and the sky behind the eastern peaks had begun to go the color of a healing bruise.

Magic followed her everywhere now. That was the sentence she kept turning over, walking the cairn-road, climbing the ridge where she had once watched her whole world burn. Magic followed her anywhere. There was no valley left to hide in, no seal, no silence. The gift that had slept in her for twenty-two years walked beside her like a second shadow, and the sword at her hip carried its true name—*Vigil*, her grandmother's voice had called it, in the blade, in the dark, *its name is Vigil, and it was never meant to sleep forever*—and the name was known now, written in Tam's histories, sung in fragments in Nell's songs, spoken in the council halls of the capital.

She climbed to the ridge. The burn scars from the witch-hunters' fires had healed into meadow, soft and silvered with dew. Below, Hollowmere lay in the gray light with smoke rising from a handful of chimneys, ordinary and small and unbearably dear.

And August understood, standing there, what the ache in her chest was.

It wasn't grief. She knew grief's shape; she had carried it three years and it had a specific weight, like a stone sewn into a coat lining. This was different. This was the feeling of setting something down.

She had spent her whole life believing the valley was what kept her safe. The seal, the silence, the oath, the forgetting—all of it a fortress, and she its garrison, born into a war she couldn't see. She had believed that if the walls ever came down, the valley would be lost, or she would be, or both.

But the walls had come down. The entity was caged—caged in *her*, willingly, bound by consent and discipline and the daily practice of a keeper who chose her chains—and the valley still stood. The town had rebuilt itself. Marta still called across fences. The apples still rolled in the gutters in autumn, presumably, and children still stole them, and now one of those children sharpened knives when she was cross and leaned candles toward laughter.

Hollowmere had never been safe because it forgot. It had been safe because it remembered—remembered how to hold each other, how to keep a house for a dead woman, how to argue about a roof patch and mean it as a prayer. The forgetting had only ever been armor. The remembering was the bone underneath.

She didn't need the valley to be sealed. She needed it to be *here*. Needed the graves tended and the names said aloud and the lullaby sung in kitchens until it soaked into the walls for the next child who'd need it. Home wasn't a prison she'd escaped. It was a memory she was part of now—one voice among many in a song that had soaked into stone.

The sun cleared the eastern peak and poured down the valley floor, and the wildflowers lit up all at once, fireweed and gentian and the small white stars she'd never bothered to learn the names of, and the magic in the ground rose to meet the light like breath meeting morning.

Behind her, boots on the path. Sera, of course. Sera always found her at the edges of things.

"Council sent a rider," Sera said, stopping beside her. She handed over a folded paper, heavy stock, the council's new seal—seven spokes, for the seven seats, one of them Nell's. "They're asking formally. Not ordering. Asking." A dry pause. "Progress."

August opened it. Read it. Read it again.

"They want a school," she said.

"They want *you*," Sera said. "A school is the shape it comes in. Gifted children, trained by choice, sworn by choice—or not sworn at all, if that's what they choose. The unsworn seats voted for it unanimously. The old guard abstained, which from them is applause." Sera looked out over the valley, jaw working. "They asked where. Your letter says you get to pick the ground."

August folded the letter slowly.

She thought of the kitchen table, and the whetstone worn to a curve by four generations of careful hands. She thought of Aldous's daughter, seven years old, leaning candles toward her own laughter. She thought of her grandmother singing the lock-song low over her sewing, keeping it alive in a valley built on forgetting, in case it was ever needed somewhere else.

In case it was ever needed *here*.

"The house on Weaver's Lane," August said. "It's big enough. The garden needs work. And my grandmother always said a house should be lived in or let go." She smiled, and it hurt, and it healed, both at once. "I intend to make a liar of her."

Sera huffed—her version of a laugh—and started back down the path. August lingered a moment longer, one hand resting on the pommel where Vigil slept, named and content, a sentinel no longer standing watch over a prison but over a promise.

Below, the first chimney smoke of the day rose straight in the windless morning.

And far down the cairn-road, small in the distance, a rider was coming up the valley—fast, at a gallop, with a white scarf knotted at her throat that August recognized even before the sun caught it, because there was only one person in the world who still wore the colors of the cathedral choir, and she was riding toward Hollowmere like the news couldn't wait.
