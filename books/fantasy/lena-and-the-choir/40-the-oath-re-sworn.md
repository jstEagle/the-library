## 40. The Oath Re-Sworn

The valley had learned to remember.

That was the strangest part, August thought, standing in the dooryard where her grandmother's house had been and where a new house now stood—wider, with windows on the south side, built by hands that had never known the old rules. Hollowmere had always been a place of forgetting. The mist came down off the fells each morning and took the edges off things: names, dates, the reasons for old quarrels. For nineteen years of her life the forgetting had been a kindness, a blanket laid over the family's purpose so that she could sleep at night without dreaming of crowns.

Now the mist still came, but the people stayed awake under it. They kept ledgers again. They spoke the Ishikawa name aloud in the market, not as a curse or a warning but as a fact, the way you might say *the miller* or *the river*. And every spring, when the thaw-water ran loud in the streams, children came up the road from Greyford and beyond with wooden swords wrapped in wool, and their parents stood at the gate looking frightened and hopeful in equal measure, and asked if it was true that Lady Ishikawa would take them.

August had stopped correcting the title. It was easier than explaining that she owned nothing but a sword, a scarred dooryard, and an oath she had written herself.

She heard them before she saw them—the new students, coming up the switchback trail from the village. Six of them this season. She could pick out Tam's boy, Merric, by his uneven gait; he carried the archive's spare ledger against his chest like a shield, though he was here for the sword and not the book. Behind him walked a girl named Sorrel who had burned the fence posts off her father's farm the first time she'd held a blade, and twins from the coast whose gift woke only when they stood back to back, and a quiet boy called Fen who hadn't spoken since the Order burned his town, and last—

Last came Nell, barefoot as always, singing something low and wordless to keep the bees off the path. Her voice did what it had always done, only now there was nothing stolen in it. When she sang, candles leaned toward her instead of guttering. Grown men wept, but they wept the way people weep at weddings.

Nell caught August watching and lifted one hand, and the song changed key into something bright and teasing, and August felt her mouth pull sideways despite herself.

"Late," August said when they reached the gate.

"The ferry was late," Nell said.

"The ferry is never late."

"Nell was late," Merric offered solemnly, and ducked when she swatted at him.

Sera came around the corner of the house with her sleeves rolled and her forearms grey with whetstone dust, and behind her, as always, the shield—a round oak-and-iron thing older than any of them, its face scored with the marks of the Silent Order's blades. She had refused the generalship in front of the whole council chamber, August had heard, in words that were already becoming legend: *I have spent my life holding a line for men who drew the map wrong. I will hold a line for children instead.* Then she had ridden north, alone, and shown up in Hollowmere three days later asking if there was work.

There was always work.

"Form up," Sera said, and the six of them scrambled into the line they'd drilled all spring, wooden swords rising in a ragged chorus. Sera walked the row the way she'd once walked rows of witch-hunters, except that now she touched each shoulder as she passed, and each student straightened under her hand like a plant toward sun.

"What is the first oath?" Sera asked.

"I choose," the students said.

"What is the second?"

"I can unchoose."

"And the third?"

Silence. That was always the third. August stepped forward and let them see her draw the sword—not the wooden practice blades, but the real one, the one that had slept for twenty years in a cellar and woken in a crypt beneath a cathedral. It came out of its scabbard with a sound like a long breath released.

"The third oath," August said, "is that the sword chooses too. And you have to listen well enough to hear what it wants. Because a gift that only goes one direction isn't a gift. It's a leash."

Fen raised his hand—the first time he had volunteered anything since winter. His voice, when it came, was rusty as an unused hinge. "What does yours want?"

August looked down at the blade.

It lay quiet in the morning light, its steel holding the reflection of the fells, the mist, the six small faces. Once it had spoken to her in her grandmother's voice, teaching her the history the valley had buried. Once it had moved through her grip like a living thing, hungry for the Vessel-of-Crowns. But that hunger was gone now, caged and kept, fed daily on consent rather than conquest—and what remained in the steel was something gentler. Something patient.

"It wants," August said slowly, because she had never said it aloud before, "to be used well. That's all. That's the whole of it."

* * *

They drilled until noon. August watched Sera correct Merric's footwork and thought about oaths—the ones sworn to her and the ones she had sworn.

Her grandmother's oath had been a lock: *never speak the name, never leave the valley, never wake.* It had been made in fear, by people who loved their children enough to bury them alive inside a rule. August had broken it in a crypt full of dead children's voices, and the sky over the kingdom had split open like a seam, and everything after—the purge, the burning streets, the throne room, the king dying in Sera's arms while the Vessel-of-Crowns screamed and lunged for her—all of it had come from that breaking.

But the re-swearing had come after. That was the part no chronicle got right, though Tam had tried, in his last months, to set it down properly. The entity had leapt for her, yes. Its cold had closed around her heart like a crown closing around a brow. And she had not resisted. She had opened the door of herself and spoken the only words that could hold a thing like that:

*I take you. On my terms. You will be kept, not keeping. You will serve, not be served. And every day I choose it again, you will feel me choosing.*

A binding by consent. A cage with a willing keeper. The Vessel-of-Crowns had raged, had poured itself against the walls of her like black water against glass—and then, finding no crack, no fear, no stolen crown to wear, it had gone quiet. It lived in her still. Some mornings she felt it stir, cold and sullen, like a prisoner listening to rain. Some nights, in the hour before sleep, she almost pitied it. It had only ever wanted what everyone wanted: to be held somewhere, by someone, forever. It simply had never learned the difference between holding and taking.

Her grandmother could not have taught her that. Only breaking the first oath could.

At midday they ate bread and hard cheese on the wall, and Nell sang the grace-song she'd written for the school—four lines, simple enough for the twins to harmonize, and by the end even Fen's mouth was moving. Afterward August took the students to the stream below the dooryard, where the water ran cold off the high snows, and had them kneel and wash their hands in it.

"Why?" Sorrel asked.

"Because my grandmother made us do it," August said. "And she was wrong about most things, but not about this. You wash your hands before you swear so you remember your oath should leave them clean."

One by one they knelt. One by one they spoke the three lines, freely, in their own words—August insisted on their own words; a borrowed oath was just another leash. Merric swore to protect the record and the people the record forgot. Sorrel swore to burn only what deserved burning, and laughed at herself, and swore it again more seriously. The twins swore as one voice, which unnerved everyone including them. Fen knelt longest, his hands in the water until they went white, and then he said, very quietly, "I choose," and "I can unchoose," and then, after a silence that none of them rushed, "and I will listen." And the stream seemed to run a little louder for a moment, the way it always did when a swordspeaker's gift turned over in its sleep and settled deeper.

When it was done, Nell sang them back up the hill, and the sound went out over the valley, and August stood in the shallows a moment longer than she needed to, letting the cold close over her wrists.

Somewhere behind her sternum, the Vessel-of-Crowns shifted, and she felt it attend—to the water, to the song, to the small clean weight of six new oaths. Not hungry. Attentive. Even a prison learns, if the jailer is kind enough, what freedom looks like from the outside.

*Good,* she told it silently, the way she did every day. *Watch. Learn.*

*We are learning,* it answered, in a voice like coins spilled on stone. It had learned to say *we* two winters ago. She had decided not to be afraid of that.

* * *

Evening found the three of them on the porch, as it found them most evenings—August in the chair her grandfather had built, Sera sharpening knives that didn't need sharpening, Nell sprawled across the steps with her head against August's knee, humming fragments of tomorrow's lessons. The mist was coming down off the fells, gold-edged in the last light, and beyond it the rebuilt village showed its windows one by one, lamps lit by hands that no longer feared the dark.

"Tam would have hated how peaceful this is," Sera said. "He'd have found some clause in the peace accords to worry about."

"He'd have found six," August said. "And footnoted them."

Nell laughed without lifting her head. "He left Merric the ledger. That was his way of worrying in advance. 'Here, child, here are all the things that can go wrong, alphabetized.'"

"Alphabetized," Sera repeated, shaking her head. But her hand had gone still on the whetstone, and August knew they were both doing the same arithmetic they always did in the quiet hours: counting the absent. Grandmother, who had stayed in the burning house so that the seal would break cleanly and the hunt would end with her, not with the valley's children. Tam, who had crawled out of the royal archive with the counter-oath pressed to his chest and a wound no healer could argue with, and who had lived just long enough to put the ledger in Nell's arms and say, *keep it better than I did.* The king, who had lasted nine days free, and spent them signing away his own crown to a council that seated the unsworn beside the titled, and who had died—or finished dying, or however you said it—with Sera holding his hand and telling him, truthfully, that it had been enough.

"I went past the memorial stone today," Nell said softly. "Someone left flowers. And a wooden sword. Small one. Child-sized."

"For the crypt choir," August said.

"For all of them." Nell turned her face up. In the dusk her eyes were the color of the mist. "I sang them to rest, August. All forty-one voices. I sang until my throat bled and my magic—" she flexed her fingers, that habitual gesture, checking for a power that was finally, entirely hers and therefore finite, ordinary, mortal— "until there wasn't anything left to sing with but me. And they went. They actually went. Do you know what that's like? To spend years carrying stolen voices and then get to give them back?"

"No," August said. "Tell me."

Nell considered. "Like setting down a pack you forgot you were wearing."

Sera snorted. "Very poetic."

"You married a poet."

"I married a novelist," Sera said, "who is currently writing a version of events in which I am dignified."

"In which you *are* dignified," Nell said. "Art improves on life. That's its whole job."

August let their voices wash over her, bickering gently, the way they had for five years now, and thought: *this is the inheritance.* Not the sword, not the gift, not even the oath. This. Two people on a porch who knew every terrible thing she had ever done and chosen anyway. A valley that remembered. Children on a hillside swearing oaths they could unswear.

Her grandmother had given her a lock and called it love. August had spent ten years learning to turn it into a door.

* * *

Full dark came, and the students long since bedded down in the dormer Sera had built with her own hands, and the porch lamp burned low. August rose to bank the fire, and paused at the rail, and looked east—toward the pass, toward Greyford, toward the capital where a council of the titled and the unsworn was even now arguing, as councils did, about the shape of the world.

A rider was coming up the valley road.

She saw the lantern first, swinging with the rhythm of a trot, then the horse, then the shape of the rider—small, hunched against the night cold, wearing traveling clothes gone grey with dust. Not a courier; couriers rode fast and left fast. This one rode slow, the way people ride when the journey is the message.

Nell's humming stopped. Sera was already on her feet, and August realized she had seen it too, and that neither of them had reached for a weapon, and that this itself was the measure of how far the world had turned.

The rider came through the gate, dismounted stiffly, and pushed back a hood. A woman of perhaps fifty, with an archivist's ink-stained fingers and eyes red-rimmed from the road. Around her neck, on a cord, hung a sealed tube of the royal archive—the old seal, the pre-possession seal, the one Tam had spent his life decoding.

"Lady Ishikawa." Her voice cracked; she cleared it and began again. "My name is Osgood. Maren Osgood. Tam was my brother." She held out the tube with both hands, the way you offer something heavy. "Before he died, he wrote letters. He said—if the council ever started talking about restoring the monarchy, or about registering the gifted, or about *managing* the unsworn for their own good—I was to bring these to you. He said you'd know what to do with them." Her chin trembled. "They started talking yesterday."

August took the tube. It was lighter than she expected, and heavier.

Behind her, Nell had risen silently to stand at her shoulder, and Sera had come to stand at the other, and the lamp between them threw their three shadows long across the dooryard where a new generation slept, sworn and unsworn and free to choose again.

"Well," August said, and heard her grandmother's voice in her own, and did not mind. "Then we'd best read them before morning."

The mist rolled down off the fells, and the valley remembered, and the sword at her hip rested easy in its scabbard—named at last, and sleeping soundly, and ready, when its keeper chose, to wake.
