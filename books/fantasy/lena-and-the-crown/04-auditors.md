## 4. Auditors

The body was still under the floor of the counting room when the auditors came.

Vesper had spent the night deciding what to do with it, and by dawn had done nothing at all, which was itself a decision—the worst kind, the kind that pretends to be patience. The courier lay beneath the loose flagstone where the guild's oldest ledgers were kept in case of fire, because no one would ever look for a man beneath the records of everything the guild refused to record. Vesper had wrapped him in the oilcloth meant for preserving maps and told themselves, with the flat insistence of a clerk correcting an entry, that this was temporary. That they would think of something. That thinking of something was a task like any other, and could be scheduled.

They had not slept. They had washed the outer ward stones three times, and the third washing was not because there was blood but because scrubbing was a thing hands could do while a mind refused to work. They had re-inked yesterday's ledger entry so that it read *no visitors* in their own unremarkable hand, and then had sat looking at the words *no visitors* and understood that they had committed forgery against themselves.

At first light, the bell above the Hollowgate rang.

Not the courier's knock—that broken, urgent drumming from two nights past. The bell. The guild's bell, hung on the outer post a generation before Vesper's time, rung only by those with the right to demand entry, and it made a sound like a coin dropped into an empty room: bright, brief, and final.

Vesper stood very still in the doorway of the counting room, one hand on the ledger, and felt the old litany rise up through them like water through chalk: *record everything, open nothing, belong to no one.* Eleven years of that litany. Eleven years of nights exactly like this one, except for the body under the floor and the map sewn into the lining of their coat, where it lay against their ribs like a second heartbeat, thin and patient and wrong.

They went to answer the bell.

* * *

There were three of them, which was correct procedure—auditors traveled in threes so that no single judgment could be blamed on a single judge—and they stood in the outer ward in the grey morning light with the snow squeaking under their boots, and they were all, Vesper thought, entirely ordinary, which was the most frightening thing about them.

The woman in front was perhaps fifty, with a face like a well-used ledger: ruled, neat, every line where you expected it. Her coat was guild-grey, her gloves were guild-grey, and pinned at her collar was the small brass pin shaped like an open book that Vesper had seen every day of their working life and never once worn. Behind her stood a young man with ink-stained fingers and the particular stillness of someone who memorized things for a living, and behind him a broad, silent person whose function was not immediately clear and did not need to be.

"Keeper Brightwater." The woman's voice was pleasant. Auditors' voices were always pleasant; Vesper had heard them through the gate a dozen times over eleven years, arriving to check seals, verify stores, confirm that the Hollowgate remained shut and its keeper remained unremarkable. "Senior Auditor Corvane. This is Auditor Pell, and Auditor Marsh." A pause, precisely measured. "You'll forgive the early hour."

"The gate doesn't keep hours," Vesper said. Their own voice came out level, dry, exactly as it always did, and some distant part of them noted this with detached interest, the way one notes weather. "What do you need?"

"A count of your winter stores. Verification of the seal on the inner door. And—" Corvane smiled, and the smile fit her face the way a well-made lid fits a box, "—your assistance in a small matter. A courier passed through this region three nights ago. Carrying guild correspondence. He has failed to arrive."

The wind moved through the ward, lifting fine snow off the stones and setting it down again somewhere else, and Vesper thought: *he is under my floor, and I am standing here with his blood under my fingernails even though I have washed them, because I can feel it there anyway.*

"I haven't seen a courier," Vesper said.

It came out simply. That was the horror of it. No catch in the throat, no heat in the face, no telltale glance toward the counting room floor. Just five words, delivered in the tone of a keeper reporting a quiet week, and Senior Auditor Corvane nodded as if a box had been ticked, and Vesper stood inside their own body watching themselves lie and feeling—this was the part they would hate later, the part they would scrub at the way they'd scrubbed the stones—

*Nothing.*

No. Not nothing. Underneath the nothing, small and hot and shameful as a coal wrapped in wool: *thrill.*

Eleven years. Eleven years of exact entries and sealed doors and the careful, invisible life, and it had taken one dead stranger and one impossible map to teach them that lying was easy. That the ledger's straight lines had been holding back not virtue but only habit, and habit, once broken, broke all at once, like ice.

"May we come in?" Corvane asked. "Standard audit. It shouldn't take long."

"You know the rules," Vesper said. "Auditors enter the outer ward. The counting room is keeper's ground. The inner door is never opened."

"We know the rules," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "We wrote them."

* * *

They counted the stores.

This was the ritual Vesper knew best, and there was a strange comfort in how well they performed it, barrel by barrel and sack by sack, reading figures aloud while Pell copied them into a slim black book in handwriting so small it seemed designed to insult the paper. Marsh stood by the outer door and did not stand by the outer door, in the sense that he drifted, unhurriedly, along the walls, touching nothing, looking at everything: the rake leaning where a rake leans, the woodpile stacked the way a solitary keeper stacks wood, the swept stones, the thrice-washed stones.

Vesper kept their eyes on the grain sacks and thought about the flagstone.

The counting room lay through that archway, twenty feet away, and beneath its floor the courier waited in his oilcloth shroud with the arrow wound in his chest and the last word he'd ever spoken still hanging in the cold air of Vesper's memory: *the thing wearing his crown.* If Marsh lifted that stone, the lie Vesper had just told would become a different kind of lie—not a false entry but a forged page, the kind the guild burned people for. Vesper knew the punishment schedule. They had transcribed it often enough. Concealment of guild property or personnel: forfeiture of post. Concealment with intent: the deep cells below the Hall of Reconciliation, from which, in Vesper's living memory, no one had returned to file a complaint.

And yet.

And yet the map pressed warm against their ribs, and the memory of the map's voice—because it had a voice, it spoke in fragments when Vesper unrolled it alone at night, whispering of hearts and jaws and a throne room that beat like a pulse—was doing something to the arithmetic of fear. The guild had sent auditors after one missing courier within three days. The guild had known, instantly, exactly what he carried. Which meant the guild was not searching for a lost letter.

The guild was searching for its own secret, and wanted it back before anyone read it.

"Your hand," Corvane said suddenly.

Vesper looked up. She was regarding them across the store-room table with mild, professional interest, and Vesper realized their pen had stopped moving, and that a drop of ink had gathered at the nib and fallen onto the tally sheet, a perfect black circle like a period at the end of a sentence they hadn't written yet.

"Tired," Vesper said. "Nights are long here."

"They are." Something shifted behind Corvane's pleasantness—not suspicion exactly, but the attention of a woman who had audited a hundred keepers and knew the precise weight of each one's silences. "You've held this post eleven years. Unbroken. No leave requested. No transfer sought. Do you know how rare that is, Keeper?"

"I imagine the guild prefers it."

"The guild does." She turned the black book toward herself and made a small mark in it, and Vesper could not see whether the mark was good or bad, and understood that this was deliberate. "A keeper who never leaves. Who sees nothing. Who asks nothing." She looked up, and for half a heartbeat the pleasantness went out of her face like a lamp turned down, and what was underneath was older and much more tired. "You must be the most trustworthy woman in the guild, Brightwater. Or the best-hidden."

"I'm the only one here," Vesper said. "Both are easy."

Corvane laughed—a real laugh, short and surprised—and Vesper hated how much they wanted her to laugh again, because Corvane was the enemy, or ought to be, and wanting the enemy's approval was a weakness the ledger had no column for.

* * *

They verified the seal at noon.

This was the part Vesper had dreaded, and it went the way it always went: the three of them walking the length of the passage between the wards, Vesper's keys sounding loud in the silence, until they reached the inner door—the Hollowgate proper, the reason for the name and the post—and Corvane laid her gloved palm flat against the wax seal that spanned its iron seam.

The seal was intact. Of course it was intact. Vesper had checked it that morning out of pure nerves, and the night before, and every night for eleven years, because checking it was the whole of the job.

"Holding true," Pell murmured, copying.

"Eleven years without a hairline," Corvane said. "Remarkable." She left her hand on the wax a moment longer than procedure required, and Vesper watched her fingers and thought, absurdly, of the map's whispered fragments—*the jaw, the jaw, it opens downward*—and wondered what an auditor's glove could feel through a seal that a keeper's bare hands had never once been permitted to feel.

Then Corvane stepped back, and the moment passed, and she was pleasant again, and the three of them walked back to the outer ward through the falling afternoon light.

"It remains only," she said, "to conclude our inquiry into the courier."

"I've told you what I know."

"You've told me what you saw." Corvane smiled. "I'm asking what you know. There's a difference, and the guild employs me to notice it." She let that sit in the cold air between them. Snow had begun again, fat slow flakes that fell without hurry, as if the sky had all the time in the world, which, this winter, it seemed to believe it did. "He would have come up the river road. He would have been hurt—he was carrying something worth hurting him for. And this is the only lighted door for thirty miles. So either he passed you by in the dark, bleeding, in a blizzard—"

"He didn't come here," Vesper said.

"—or he did," Corvane finished softly, "and you're lying to me. And do you know what interests me most, Keeper? It isn't whether you're lying. People lie to auditors constantly; it's practically a folk tradition. What interests me is *how well* you're lying. First offense should look like a first offense. Stumble. Sweat. Overcorrect. Yours looks like eleven years of practice." She tilted her head. "Which is impossible. Isn't it? Because you've never lied to us. Your records are flawless."

The flakes came down between them. Somewhere beyond the wall, a crow said one flat syllable and thought better of it.

"My records are flawless," Vesper agreed, "because nothing happens here. Nothing has ever happened here. If a dying man crawled to my gate, I would have logged it, because logging is all I am. You may search the ward. You may search the stores. The counting room is keeper's ground, but—" and here Vesper did the bravest and stupidest thing of their eleven years, the thing they would turn over at 3 a.m. for the rest of their life, wondering which instinct had possessed them— "if it will close your inquiry, look under whatever you like."

Silence. Pell's pen stopped. Even Marsh, drifting by the woodpile, went still.

Because the flagstone was in the counting room, and Vesper had just invited them to it, and only Vesper knew that the courier was not under the flagstone anymore.

They had moved him at dawn. While the auditors' horses had still been a mile off down the river road, some animal instinct Vesper hadn't known they owned had hauled the oilcloth bundle out through the storm-door and down into the old cistern behind the ward, the dry one, the one the guild's surveys marked as collapsed, and rolled the covering stone back over it, and come inside, and washed their hands, and opened the door to the bell with a face like any other morning. They had done it without deciding to. That was the discovery of the day, worse than the lying: some part of Vesper Brightwater had already left the ledger behind and was improvising, and had not informed the rest of them.

Corvane studied them for a long moment. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she did not brush it off.

"No," she said at last. "We won't search you, Keeper. A search says we suspect you, and the guild doesn't suspect its own—yet." She drew off one glove, finger by finger, unhurried. "Instead I'll leave you with a courtesy. The courier's name was Tam Wickerly. He had a sister in Lowmarsh who will be notified of his death whenever we find a body to notify her about. He was forty-one years old. He owed money to everyone and trusted almost no one, and the one person he did trust was a disgraced royal cartographer named Sable Renn, currently drinking herself blind in a river-town called Marleford, four days south of here." She put the glove back on. "If anything of his surfaces—if a seal, a scrap, a rumor comes through this gate—you'll send word. Immediately. Won't you."

"Yes," Vesper said.

"Wonderful." Corvane smiled her lid-fitting smile, and it was worse than anger, because anger would have meant Vesper had failed to convince her, and this meant she was convinced of something else entirely: that Vesper was worth watching. "Pell will want your mark on the audit. Then we'll trouble you no further."

* * *

They rode out at dusk, south along the river road, toward Marleford, and Vesper stood in the outer ward and watched the three grey shapes shrink into the blue snow-light until the falling dark took them.

Then Vesper went inside, barred the door, took down the ledger, and wrote, in their own unremarkable hand:

*Day 4,016. Audit concluded. Stores verified. Seal intact. No visitors.*

They looked at the words *no visitors* for a long time. Then, beneath them, small enough that only a reader looking for it would find it, they added a second line—the first unauthorized entry in eleven years of flawless service, the first crack in the ice, the first sentence of a story they did not yet know they were telling:

*Three went south. One of them knows. Move the map. Find Renn first.*

Vesper blew out the lamp, sewed the map tighter against their ribs, and began, in the dark, to pack.
