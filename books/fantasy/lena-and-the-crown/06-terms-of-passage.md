## 6. Terms of Passage

**Vesper**

The garret smelled of tallow smoke and river rot, and the woman in it was aiming a crossbow at Vesper's chest before they had finished climbing the last stair.

"You're the one who's been asking after me," Sable Renn said. She was thinner than her guild portrait—Vesper had read the file twice on the road north—and her hair was the color of old rope, tied back with what appeared to be a bootlace. The crossbow did not waver. "The innkeeper said a clerk. Clerks don't pay for information in silver."

"I paid because I wanted good information," Vesper said, keeping their hands visible and empty. Eleven years of gatekeeping taught you how to stand so that nothing about you invited approach. "You're harder to find than your reputation suggested."

"My reputation is a lie I paid three people to spread." She tilted her head. "And you're standing very still for someone with a dead man's seal in their pocket. I can see the outline of it. Left breast pocket. You've been touching it through the cloth since you walked in."

Vesper stopped touching it.

"Sit down," Sable said, and gestured with the crossbow at the room's only other chair, which had three legs and an opinion about balance. "Let's talk about Corvin Hale like civilized people. Slowly. With your hands where I can see them."

Vesper sat. The chair creaked but held. Through the one window, the river town went about its evening below—lamplighters, fishwives, a drunk singing something mournful and off-key—all of them ignorant that the realm's winter had begun to arrive out of season, frost climbing the glass in patterns too deliberate to be natural.

"Corvin Hale carried messages for me once," Sable said. "Before my disgrace. He was the only man at court who didn't pretend I'd invented my own maps. So when a stranger comes asking after him, wearing guild boots and carrying his personal seal—" she let the sentence hang there, letting the crossbow finish it.

"He's dead," Vesper said.

Something moved behind Sable's eyes—not surprise. Confirmation. That frightened Vesper more than the weapon had.

"How?"

"The cold got into him. Or something wearing the cold." Vesper heard their own voice go flat, ledger-flat, the voice they used for recording losses. "He came to the Hollowgate dying. He gave me a map and a name and then he stopped."

"A map." The crossbow lowered an inch. "What kind of map?"

"The kind I'm not going to show you while you're holding that."

Sable laughed—a short, cracked sound, like ice settling on a pond. She set the crossbow on the table between them, within reach of both of them, which Vesper understood was its own kind of statement. Then she pulled a bottle from under the bed, blew dust off it, and poured two cups of something amber and probably illegal.

"Then we're negotiating," she said. "Drink?"

* * *

**Sable**

The clerk drank like someone who'd read about drinking in a book. One careful sip, a pause to assess, a second sip slightly larger. Sable found it almost endearing, in the way you find a locked door endearing: as a challenge.

"So," she said. "Terms. You want something from me, Brightwater. Nobody crosses half the country to find a disgraced cartographer out of sentiment."

"I want the map gone."

"Gone where?"

"Unmade. Burned. Dropped in the deepest well in the Hollowgate ward. I don't care. It should never have reached me, and every hour it exists, things with guild credentials get closer to my door."

"And yet you brought it here." Sable let that sit. "To the one person alive who can read it."

The clerk's face did something complicated. Guilt and resolve fighting over the same ground. "I didn't bring it to be read. I brought it because you knew Corvin, and because—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because I read part of it. On the road. I know what it shows, and I know burning it won't matter if what it shows is true."

Sable set down her cup. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd expected them to shake by now. Ten years of drink had promised her shaking hands and she'd dutifully earned them, and here they were, steady as a draftsman's compass, because somewhere under the ruin of her the royal cartographer was still sitting up straight and paying attention.

"Tell me what it shows," she said.

"No."

"No?"

"Not until we agree on terms. If I tell you, you'll want to go. And if you want to go, you'll go whether I agree or not, and I'd rather have terms than a chase." The clerk folded their hands. It was a bookkeeper's gesture, everything squared and balanced, and Sable suddenly understood why Corvin had trusted this person enough to die at their door. There was no performance in them. Just rules, held like a shield over something soft.

"Corvin saved my life," Sable said. "When the purge came, when they burned my atlas and struck my name from the rolls, he got me out of the capital in a grain cart with a forged pass he'd made himself. He never told me what he'd seen. He just said, *stop drawing the palace, Renn, and never draw it again.* I thought he meant I'd been careless with fortifications." She looked at the window, at the frost writing its patient script. "I've spent ten years being angry at him for ruining me. Now you're telling me he was trying to save me twice."

"I'm telling you he believed the crown isn't a crown."

"I know what he believed. I drew the thing forty times. I measured it. Gold, circlet, seven points, weight of roughly four pounds." She tapped the table. "Maps don't lie, Brightwater. People lie *with* maps. So either Corvin lost his mind, or—"

"Or the map I'm carrying isn't drawn the way palaces are drawn." The clerk's voice dropped. "It's drawn the way bodies are drawn. And the crown is drawn as a mouth."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, the drunk had stopped singing.

Sable reached across the table and took the bottle instead of the cup, and drank, and let the burn give her a moment to arrange her face. When she could speak evenly again, she said: "Terms, then. You'll show me the map. You'll let me copy it—my hand, my memory, my responsibility. And when we reach the capital, I go to whoever is left that Corvin trusted, and I put what happened to him in front of them, and I do not leave until the thing that killed him has a name in an official record. That's justice. That's the term."

"And mine," the clerk said, "is that after that record exists—if it can exist—we burn every copy, including the original, and you never draw the palace again. This time because you choose to."

Sable studied them. Thin, tired, terrified, and absolutely certain. A person who had come all this way hoping to be talked out of something, and had failed to be talked out of it, and was now trying to make sure nobody else got dragged in deeper than necessary.

Too late, clerk, she thought. You knocked on my door.

"One more term," she said. "We travel together. Not because I trust you—because the roads are freezing early and things are hunting that map, and I'd rather die beside a witness than alone in a ditch. We part at the capital. After that, your silence and my ink go our separate ways."

The clerk considered. Sable watched them weigh it—the same way they'd weighed the chair, the cup, the distance to the door. Everything entered into the ledger.

"Together to the capital," they said finally. "Part ways after. Agreed."

She poured two fresh cups and pushed one across. "Seal it properly, then. Guild folk swear oaths on paper. River folk do it with liquor."

The clerk looked at the cup the way they might look at an unlicensed door. Then they picked it up and drank it down without flinching, which cost them visibly, and Sable laughed for the first time in a year and decided that maybe, possibly, against every instinct honed by ten years of betrayal, she trusted them a little.

* * *

**Vesper**

They left at dawn, ahead of the frost, because Sable said the river would lock up within the week and the roads after that would belong to whatever was making the winter.

Vesper had not slept. They had sat by the guttering candle with the map unrolled between them and watched Sable read it, and the reading had been worse than the showing. She traced the palace's corridors with one finger and named what each passage had become in the map's anatomy—artery, nerve, windpipe—and when she reached the throne room she went quiet for a long time, and when she spoke again her voice was rough.

"It's accurate," she said. "That's the horror of it. Whoever drew this didn't imagine it. They *surveyed* it. Someone walked these halls with instruments and recorded what the palace actually is, and the guild buried it, and I—" She had stopped, pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "I drew the pretty version. For eleven years I drew the pretty version and hung it in the council chamber, and the king stood in front of it every morning and thanked me."

Now, on the north road, with the map sealed in oilcloth against Vesper's chest and Sable striding ahead through the grey morning like a woman with a debt to collect, Vesper turned the night's arithmetic over and over and could not make it balance.

Rule one: record everything. Done. The courier, the map, the auditors' visit, the lie—they were all in the hidden ledger now, in cipher, in Vesper's smallest hand.

Rule two: open nothing. Broken, or about to be. Because the map could not simply be burned anymore; Sable was right about that, and Vesper had known it since the third night of reading, since the fragments of voice that rose off the vellum like heat off a stove, saying *north, north, bring it home.* Burning it here would just leave it unaccounted for. Things unaccounted for did not disappear. They waited.

Rule three: belong to no one. And here was the trouble, walking twenty paces ahead, arguing with a farmer about the price of oats and losing gracefully. Here was the trouble sharing a fire at dusk and a flask at midnight and, gods help them, laughing once—actually laughing—at Vesper's description of the third auditor's eyebrows.

"You're doing it wrong," Sable said on the fourth evening, catching Vesper standing watch while she slept. She'd woken without seeming to wake; her eyes were open and level in the firelight. "Guarding. You stand like a man guarding a grave."

"That's essentially the job."

"Mm." She sat up, wrapped her blanket around her shoulders, and held out the flask without looking at it, the way you offer something you already know will be accepted. "Corvin used to say the guild hired two kinds of people. The ones who liked the secrets, and the ones who liked the walls. He said the dangerous ones were the ones who couldn't tell anymore which they liked."

"Which am I?"

"Neither, yet. That's what worries me." She took the flask back and drank. "You keep looking at the map pocket. Every hour, on the hour, like a bell. You're not guarding it, Brightwater. You're deciding."

Vesper said nothing, because the ledger of honest answers had no entry for that question.

On the sixth day the snow began in earnest—fat, silent flakes falling straight down, no wind behind them, snow that fell like it was being *poured*. The villages they passed had shutters closed at midday. In one, a well had frozen solid from the top down, which Sable said was impossible, and which the map, warm against Vesper's chest, seemed to find funny. That night the voice-fragments came stronger, and Vesper sat up holding the oilcloth bundle at arm's length like a snake, listening to it whisper a word it had never whispered before.

Not *north.*

A name. Small, childlike, repeated with terrible tenderness:

*Pip. Pip. Pip.*

"What is it?" Sable was awake, crossbow in hand, following Vesper's stare to the dark mouth of the road south, where the snow was falling thicker than anywhere else—as if the winter were concentrating, funneling, pointing.

"There's someone coming," Vesper said. "Small footsteps. And the map—" They swallowed. "The map knows her name."

Down the white road, out of the pouring snow, came a shape no bigger than a sack of turnips, staggering, barefoot, blue-lipped, and humming a lullaby in a key that did not exist.
