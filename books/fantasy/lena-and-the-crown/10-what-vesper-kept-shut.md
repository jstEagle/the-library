## 10. What Vesper Kept Shut

The fire had burned down to a red eye in the hearth, and Pip was asleep with her back against Sable's pack, one hand curled around the tin cup she refused to let anyone wash. Sable sat sharpening a knife that did not need it. The waystation's single window showed snow falling straight down, without wind, as if the sky were deciding something.

Vesper should have been sleeping. They had the first watch, which meant they were awake and pretending otherwise, hood up, hands folded, the posture of a person who was furniture.

"You don't have to tell me," Sable said, not looking up from the blade. "But you've been carrying that ledger like it's a corpse since the courier died, and I've watched enough bodies to know when the weight isn't the body's."

"I record everything," Vesper said. "Open nothing. Belong to no one."

"Rules," Sable said. "I asked about the wound under them."

The knife whispered against the stone. Outside, the snow kept falling in that patient, deliberate way, and Vesper found—this was the strange part, the part they would remember—that the words came easily. Eleven years of silence, and all it took was a frozen road, a child who heard voices, and a woman who had already seen them lie.

"Her name was Wren," Vesper said.

* * *

They grew up in Marrowfen, which was not on any map worth owning: a guild-kept town of eel-catchers and reed-cutters where the ledgers arrived every quarter in a locked box and left the same way, and no one asked what was written in them because asking was how you stopped being mentioned at all. Their mother kept the toll bridge. Their father had been a soldier until the river took his leg and the guild took his pension, in that order, and after that he sat by the window and taught Vesper two things: how to keep accounts so clean that no auditor ever had a reason to knock, and how to be so quiet that even your own family forgot you were in the room.

Wren never learned the second lesson. Wren was four years younger and loud in the way water is loud—constant, necessary, impossible to dam. She counted everything out loud. Steps, birds, lies. Especially lies. *You lied just then,* she'd say, delighted, pointing at their mother across the supper table. *Your voice went up at the end.* She could hear an untruth the way other people heard a cracked bell, and she thought it was a game, because she was nine, and no one had told her yet that in Marrowfen some arithmetic got people taken away.

The winter Vesper turned fifteen, the quarterly box came early, and it came unlocked.

Later—much later, in the long years of the gate—Vesper would understand that this was not an accident. The Hollowgate's keepers were chosen young, and they were chosen by test, though the tested rarely knew they were being weighed. The box sat open on the toll bridge table like a mouth, and inside lay the ledger, and beside the ledger lay a second book, thinner, unbound, its pages loose as fallen leaves.

Wren read it first. Of course she did. She read everything first; reading was her way of touching things.

"It's names," she said that night, whispering into the dark between their beds, because even Wren knew to whisper this. "It's a list of everyone the guild has ever erased. There are pages and pages. And Ves—" her hand found theirs in the cold, gripping hard, "—there's a page with our name on it. Not us. Someone called Brightwater. From before. It says *keeper*. It says *the gate*."

"What gate?"

"I don't know. That's what I'm going to find out."

That was the sentence that killed her. Vesper had carried it eleven years, turning it over, looking for the seam where a different answer might have been hidden inside it. *Don't find out. Burn it. Forget it.* Any of those. But Vesper was fifteen and tired and their father was snoring in the next room, and so they said the worst possible thing, the thing a careful person says when they want to be loved more than they want to be safe:

"Not tonight. Tomorrow. We'll look together tomorrow."

There was no tomorrow. The auditors came before dawn—three of them, gray as the river fog, polite the way knives are polite. They did not break the door. Their mother opened it, because there was a knock, and in Marrowfen you answered a knock. They stood in the kitchen drinking tea their mother poured with shaking hands while one of them explained, gently, that the girl had committed a violation against the Ledger of Impossible Things, section unreadable, penalty unreadable, and that the family would be compensated.

Compensated. Vester remembered the word landing in the room like a coin dropped in a well. Their father started to rise from his chair and the second auditor put one hand on his shoulder—not hard, just resting—and the old soldier sat back down as if the shoulder had been replaced with stone.

They took Wren through the front door, past the toll box, past the open ledger still lying on the table. She didn't cry. She looked back once, over the first auditor's arm, and found Vesper in the doorway, and her face did something Vesper spent eleven years refusing to describe even to themselves. Not accusation. Worse. Understanding. She had heard the lie in *tomorrow*, Vesper realized. She had heard it, and she had gone into the book anyway, because she trusted that Vesper would come after her.

Vesper did not come after her. Vesper did the thing they had been raised for. Vesper recorded everything—the hour, the number of auditors, the tea gone cold in four cups—and opened nothing, and belonged to no one, and three days later a letter came offering a position at a remote waystation in the north, keeper of a gate, room and board and a lifetime contract, and Vesper signed it before their mother finished reading it aloud.

Because the letter had been written in the same hand as the ledger. Because the gate was the word Wren had died holding. Because if Vesper could not follow her through whatever door they'd taken her through, they could stand at a door instead, forever, and make sure no one else went through one unwarned.

Or so Vesper told themselves for eleven years. The truth was smaller and colder: Vesper signed because standing at a door felt like being close to her. Invisibility wasn't safety. It was a scar worn as a uniform.

* * *

"—and you never went looking." Sable's voice pulled the present back around Vesper like a coat. The knife had stopped moving. "Eleven years. You never went looking for her."

"Where?" Vesper said. It came out flat, almost reasonable. "The guild doesn't have an address. It has a ledger. You don't search a ledger, Sable. You stay out of it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Vesper agreed. "It's the rule."

Sable set the knife down. For a moment she looked older than the garret drunkard Vesper had found in the river-town, older than the royal cartographer whose seal was on the dead courier's wax—older than all her discarded selves stacked together. "My patron," she said quietly. "Corwin Vale. The man who died on your doorstep. He was the one who pulled my name off the purge lists twenty years ago. I owed him a life, and I paid it back with ink—I redrew borders for the crown whenever he asked, and I never once asked what the crown was. So don't tell me about staying out of ledgers. I was in one the whole time. I just never read the page with my name on it."

The fire popped. Pip stirred in her sleep and murmured something—no, not murmured. Answered. Her lips moved around words none of them could hear, and her fingers tightened on the tin cup, and both adults went silent until she settled again.

"She hears it even here," Sable said. "Even now."

"I know."

"And you're going to tell me the rules cover that too?"

Vesper looked at the child. At the small, stubborn shape of her, loud as water, counting things out loud, hearing lies the way other people heard cracked bells. The resemblance was nothing. The resemblance was everything. Vesper had spent eleven years building a life shaped exactly like an absence, and now the absence had walked in out of the snow wearing a workhouse coat and calling north in her sleep.

"When we reach the capital," Vesper said, "there will be records. Guild records. If Wren is anywhere—if there's anything left of her anywhere—it'll be written down. Ledgers are the only prayers the guild answers." They paused, and heard themselves say the rest before they could stop it: "We'll look. Together. That's not a rule. That's a promise."

Sable studied them for a long moment. "First honest thing you've said since I met you."

"Don't get used to it."

* * *

Sleep did not come, so Vesper did what sleeplessness demanded: opened the courier's map by firelight, low, angled away from the window, and read it again the way you reread a letter from someone who is never coming back.

The palace spread across the vellum like a body laid open for teaching. Heart: throne room. Lungs: the great gardens, drawn as branching bronchi. And at the crown of the page—of course at the crown—a jaw of fine black lines, teeth labeled in the courier's cramped hand: *claims. claims. claims.* Every tooth a name the crown had worn. Every name crossed out except one.

Aldric.

And beneath Aldric, in fresher ink, half-formed, the penstrokes trembling as if the writer's hand had been shaking:

*Brightwater.*

Vesper went very still.

It was a common enough name. Rivers were bright somewhere in every province; water named itself a thousand ways. It meant nothing. It was coincidence, the kind the world produced by the barrelful.

But the map was warm under their fingers. Not sun-warm. Body-warm. And as Vesper watched, the half-finished letters seemed to darken, to settle, to become less like ink and more like intent—as if somewhere far to the north, behind palace walls, something had finally remembered the surname of the family that had once held its leash, and was writing it down.

*Hurry home, little door,* Pip had been told. *Your family's waiting.*

Vesper closed the map. Outside, the wind had begun at last—arriving from the north, carrying bells, or the memory of bells, faint and rhythmic as a pulse.

In the morning, Sable said, they would reach the frozen fair at Coldharbour Market. Vesper already knew, the way you know a thing in a ledger before you've read it, that nothing there would be alive.

But something, Vesper was suddenly certain, would be waiting.
