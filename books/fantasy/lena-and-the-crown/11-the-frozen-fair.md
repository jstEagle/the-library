## 11. The Frozen Fair

Sable Renn had drawn maps of a hundred towns she'd never loved, and one town she had, and it was the second kind that broke her.

They came over the ridge at midmorning, if the grey light could be called morning and the cold could be called anything but a verdict. Below them, cupped in the valley like water in a stone hand, lay Millbrook Fair — or what the maps called Millbrook Fair, because Sable had inked that name herself, eleven years ago, with a steady hand and a royal commission and no idea she was signing the place's death warrant by making it worth finding.

"Is it—" Pip started, and stopped. The child stood hip-deep in the drifts they'd waded through since dawn, her breath pluming, her eyes gone wide as coins. "Is it sleeping?"

"No," Vesper said quietly. They had said little all day. They had said little for three days, since the weir, since the blood on the ice, since Sable's arm had started its slow argument with the cold lodged in it. They adjusted the strap of their ledger-satchel and looked down at the fair with the expression of an accountant confronting a sum that would not balance. "No, I don't think anything here is sleeping."

Sable knew what sleeping looked like. She'd slept rough enough winters to know: the twitch of dreams, the shallow rise of a chest, the way a sleeper's face kept working even in the dark. What lay below them was not that. It was stillness of a different order — the stillness of things *kept*.

"Smoke," she said, and pointed.

Chimneys stood along the market row, and from every one hung a thread of smoke, frozen mid-rise. Not drifting, not dispersing. Hanging. Grey ribbons of it, suspended above the rooftops like the strings of some enormous instrument nobody was playing. One ribbon bent where a wind should have caught it, and stayed bent.

"That's not possible," Pip said.

"You'd think so," Sable said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended. The wound in her side had been talking to her all morning in a low, persuasive voice, telling her that warmth was a memory and memories were enough, and she'd been refusing to listen, but looking down at those ribbons of stopped smoke made refusal harder. If smoke could be persuaded to hold still, what couldn't? "I drew this town. Market day is Thursday. There'll be four hundred people on the green, two hundred head of stock, a pie stall that wins prizes, and a man named Odo who sells knives and cheats at his own game of chance."

"How do you know all that?" Pip asked.

"Because I mapped it." Sable started down the slope, each step deliberate on the crusted snow. "You don't map a place from a hilltop, girl. You map it by learning where everything is and why. Where the well is, where the fights start, which lane floods, who owns which grudge. A map is just a town that's been made to hold still." She heard her own words come out and felt them land somewhere under her ribs. "I just didn't know I was practicing."

The descent took the better part of an hour. By the time they reached the first outlying fence, the silence had acquired weight. It wasn't the silence of empty country — Sable knew that silence, the hush of places where nothing lived. This was the silence of a room full of people holding their breath. It pressed against the ears. It had texture.

The first thing they met was a dog.

It stood in the gateway of a paddock, mid-stride, one forepaw raised, ears forward, mouth open around a bark that had never arrived. Frost furred its coat in patterns like fern leaves. Its eyes were open and brown and bright and utterly without anyone behind them, and when Sable reached out — she couldn't say why; some old cartographer's instinct, the need to touch the territory — the fur crumbled against her glove like ash packed with snow.

She pulled her hand back. Under the frost-collapse, the shape of the dog held. It held the way a kiln holds a pot after the clay has gone. Something had taken the warm moving part and left the rest, and the rest was standing guard over an empty field forever.

"Don't touch things," Vesper said. It wasn't a rebuke. It sounded almost gentle, which from Vesper was more alarming than anger. "Rule of the road. Nothing here is finished happening."

"I know," Sable said. "I'm the one who taught you that rule."

"You taught me the rule about maps. I extended it."

Pip had gone very quiet, and Sable saw the child's head tilt — that tilt, the listening tilt, the one that had been getting worse since the weir, since the whispers in the night that Pip answered in a voice not entirely her own. Now the child's lips moved slightly, shaping words with no sound, and then she said, aloud, small: "It's loud here."

"What's loud?" Vesper asked, crouching to her level.

"The crown." Pip's hands found each other and held on. "It's not whispering. It's — humming. Like it's full. Like it ate something big." Her eyes went to the frozen dog, the frozen smoke, the frozen valley. "Like it did all this and it's still swallowing."

Vesper rose slowly. For a moment their face did something Sable had never seen it do in all their careful, ledger-balancing weeks together: it went blank the way a page goes blank, everything written on it deliberately erased. Then it came back, composed, keeper-ish, and they said only, "Then we cross quickly and we don't linger."

But Sable caught the way Vesper's hand pressed flat against the satchel, over the map, over the anatomy of the palace with its throne-room heart and its crowned jaw, and she thought: *you know something you're not saying, gatekeeper, and I am too tired and too cold to make you say it today.* She filed it away. Filing things away was the other half of mapping.

* * *

The fair itself began at an archway of woven hazel, dressed with winter greenery for the season, and passing under it was like walking into the inside of a bell the instant before it rings.

Sable had seen markets in every state a market can be in: roaring, rain-soaked, snowed-out, burned, abandoned, looted. She had never seen one *interrupted*. That was the only word for it. Everything here was mid-act. The whole green was a sentence cut off at the verb.

A woman stood at a stall of dyed wool, holding a skein of madder-red up to the light, her head tilted in appraisal, frost blooming across her eyelashes like white embroidery. Beside her, a boy of perhaps ten reached for an apple from a barrel, fingers an inch from the fruit, and would be reaching forever. Two men clasped forearms over a bargain, mouths open — one smiling, one scowling, and Sable found herself inventing the rest of the negotiation out of pure animal need, because the alternative was to accept that these two strangers were frozen at the exact hinge of a deal, one thinking he'd won and one knowing he hadn't, and neither would ever know how it came out.

"Horses," Pip whispered. In the pens beyond, draft horses stood hipshot and dozing-still, frost sheeting their flanks, breath hanging at their nostrils in little frozen clouds shaped like glass flowers. Alive. Sable would have sworn they were alive — there was a pressure of *presence* about them, the way a sealed letter has weight. Kept. Stored. Like everything else.

"It's the winter," Vesper said. They had stopped in the middle of the green, turning slowly, and their voice had the strained calm of someone reading bad news aloud. "The long winter. It's not weather. Weather moves. This is—" They searched for the word and Sable watched them find it and hate it. "This is *harvest*. Something came through and took everything warm and left the shapes."

"Can we wake them?" Pip asked. "Can we—"

"We don't know how," Vesper said. "And we don't know what waking them costs. And we don't know what's still here doing the keeping." Their eyes moved along the market row, and Sable followed them, and understood: every chimney with its stopped smoke meant a hearth, and every hearth meant a house, and every house meant people who had stepped outside on a Thursday morning to buy wool and apples and had simply been *filed away*.

Four hundred people on the green. She'd written it herself, in the margin of the survey, in her neat cartographer's hand: *pop. ~400, thrives.*

She walked the rows because walking was easier than standing still, and because her legs wanted to keep her heart company. She catalogued without meaning to — it was the trade, it was the sickness of the trade, you saw a place and your mind inked it whether you willed it or not. *Wool stall, east row. Pie stall, corner pitch, prize ribbons nailed to the frame, three of them, blue blue red. Knife-thrower's stand, painted canvas, five wooden targets, chalk score tallied to thirty-one.* Odo. She even recognized the paint scheme; she'd sat on a barrel eating pie while Odo cheated pilgrims at ring-toss and she'd said nothing, because the pie was excellent and the cheating was honest work compared to hers.

Odo was there. Behind his own stand, mid-motion, a knife in his raised hand and three more fanned in the other, frozen in the act of some flourish he'd performed ten thousand times. His face was split in a showman's grin. Frost had gotten into the grin and made it terrible.

Sable stood in front of him for a long moment.

"I owe you a drink," she told him. Her breath fogged and hung there, and she watched it hang, and hated it. "You shortchanged me twice and I let you, and I always figured the debt balanced out against the pie. But I owe you a drink, Odo, and now I can't pay it, and neither can you collect, and that's—" Her voice snagged. She was a drunk and a disgraced royal officer and a liar of long professional standing, and none of those trades had prepared her for talking to a statue that used to laugh. "That's a bad ledger. That's the worst kind. Unpayable."

Behind her she heard Vesper murmur to Pip, low and urgent, and caught only fragments: *"—not weather, I told you, the ledgers call it the tithe—"*, and then Vesper noticing her listening and going silent, and Sable deciding, again, to let it keep.

Her arm throbbed. The freezing-wound from the weir had settled into the muscle above her elbow like a coin of ice someone had sewn under the skin, and it ached worst in places like this — places where the cold had won completely. As if her body recognized its conqueror and saluted.

She turned down the west row, toward the merchants' quarter, toward the good stalls, the ones with locks, and that was where she found the corpse.

* * *

He was behind a merchant's counter in a booth draped with good cloth, and he was the first truly dead thing she'd seen in the fair — dead in the ordinary way, the human way, the way that involved blood and failure rather than preservation. Someone had killed him before the winter took the town. Days before, maybe a week. The blood had frozen black under him and stayed, a dark continent on the floorboards, and the killer had searched the booth — drawers pulled, cloth slashed, a strongbox standing open and empty — and then left, and then the winter had come and filed the whole scene away like evidence.

Sable crouched by the body, careful of her wounded arm, and looked at the man's hands. Merchant's hands, soft, uncallused. Killed kneeling, from the front, by someone he'd let get close. Robbed of everything except—

Except the chain around his neck.

She saw it glinting in the frozen gloom of the booth and her whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the winter, because she knew that chain. She had worn its twin for nine years. Heavy links, plain work, no ornament — and hanging from it, wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth as if the merchant had been trying to hide it even from the winter, a seal.

Royal seal. Second rank — a regional assayer's warrant, the kind carried by men who certified weights and measures and coin in the king's name. Sable had drafted the border design herself, years ago, in the Map Office, one of a hundred small engravings that passed through her hands. She knew the little stars at the crest's edge. She'd complained about them; too fine, she'd said, they'll fill with dirt. And they had. Dirt was packed into the stars right now.

Her hands shook as she unwound the oilcloth. Not from the cold. From recognition.

Because she knew this seal personally. Not the design — the *object*. Three years ago, when the purge came, when the king's shadow swallowed the Map Office and her patron vanished and her name went onto lists, the assayers had been recalled. Every warrant collected, every bearer questioned, every seal melted down in a crucible in the capital's mint-yard while the bearers watched. She'd seen the order. She'd nearly been collected with them; only her disgrace, her drunkenness, her usefulness-as-an-example had saved her to be discarded instead of processed.

Every seal was accounted for. Melted. Except this one, hanging on a dead merchant in a market town three hundred miles from the capital, hidden in oilcloth, in a booth that had been torn apart looking for it.

Which meant the man had been hiding it. Which meant he'd known what it was worth — not its metal, its *meaning*. Which meant either he'd stolen it from the recall, or someone fleeing the purge had passed it to him for safekeeping, the way you'd pass a coal to keep a fire alive. And then someone had come for it. Someone patient enough to search, thorough enough to tear the booth apart, and careless enough — or arrogant enough — to leave the body where it fell.

And then the winter had come and sealed the crime scene shut like wax on a letter.

"Sable." Vesper's voice, close. They had appeared at the booth's entrance without her hearing, which was unnerving; the Hollowgate keeper moved like a correction in a ledger, silently, exactly. Their eyes went to her hands, to the seal, to the dead man. "What is that?"

"You tell me," Sable said. She didn't look up. She turned the seal over. On the reverse, scratched small and hurried with a pin-knife, in a hand she recognized because she'd seen it on a hundred requisition forms and once, memorably, on a note pinned to her cloak warning her to run: a single word.

*North.*

Her patron's hand. Her dead-or-vanished patron, Master Ellery Vance, First Cartographer of the Realm, the man whose seal had opened doors and whose fall had opened the pit under her. He had run north. Everyone said so. Everyone said he'd died at the border, or been taken, or never existed at all, depending on who was saying it and how afraid they were.

And here was his handwriting, scratched into the back of a recalled-and-melted-except-not royal seal, on the neck of a murdered merchant in a town the winter had eaten.

"He was here," Sable said. Her voice came out strange, distant, like someone else reporting. "Or he passed through. Or he sent this ahead of him like a man throwing rope back down a well." She finally looked up at Vesper, and her eyes were burning, and she didn't care. "Your guild's auditors hunt us for a courier and a map. Your ledgers call this the tithe. You know things you don't say. So say this one, gatekeeper: did the guild kill him? Is that what happens to people who carry the truth north?"

Vesper's face did the blank-page thing again. Longer, this time. When they spoke, it was barely above the wind that never moved the frozen smoke.

"I don't know," they said. And then, worse, quieter: "But I think my guild does."

Pip made a sound. Small, choked. They both turned. The child stood at the edge of the booth, staring past them both at the dead merchant, and her lips were moving again, and this time the words had sound, and the sound was not entirely hers — it had a resonance under it, a hum, a depth, like a voice speaking through a pipe.

"*Found it,*" Pip said, in that doubled voice, her small hands opening like something being handed over. "*Found the lost piece. Bring it home. Bring the seal home to the jaw.*"

Then she blinked, and shuddered, and was only a child again, frightened, asking: "Why did I say that? Why do I keep — I didn't say that. Did I say that?"

Sable closed her fist around the seal until the edges bit her palm. Somewhere north, under the stopped smoke and the stored horses and the four hundred people filed away like documents, something vast had just noticed them — had noticed *this*, the small round weight of proof in a disgraced cartographer's bleeding hand.

The winter, she thought, had not come to end the world.

It had come to tidy it. And they were carrying the one loose page.

"We leave," she said, standing, pocketing the seal, meeting Vesper's eyes. "Now. Before whatever keeps this place decides to keep us too."

But even as she said it, even as they turned back toward the hazel arch and the road north, Sable felt the seal against her ribs like a second heartbeat — cold, patient, and addressed, like everything else in this dying country, *north*.
