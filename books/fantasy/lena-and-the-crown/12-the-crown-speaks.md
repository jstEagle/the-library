## 12. The Crown Speaks

The whispers came at dusk, the way they always did, sliding under the door of Pip's sleep like water finding a crack in a wall.

*Little north-child. Little listener.*

Pip had learned not to answer right away. That was the first rule she'd made for herself, back in the workhouse, when the voices started: don't answer, don't let them know you're listening. Matron Catchpole had caught her talking to the dark once and beaten her for it—*no lunatics in my house*—and after that Pip kept her conversations private, conducted them in the space between waking and sleep where no one could see her lips move.

But these whispers were different from the workhouse ones. Those had been shapeless, the mutterings of a house with too many children and too little love, grief and hunger given voice. These had *shape*. These had warmth.

That was the terrible thing about them. They were warm.

*You slept cold again,* the voice said now, gentle as a hand smoothing hair. *I felt it. I feel everything my children feel.*

"I'm not your child," Pip whispered into her blanket. The waystation's common room was empty around her; Vesper was out checking the ward-lines they'd strung up like washing lines between the trees, and Sable was snoring upstairs in a way that shook dust from the rafters. Snow pressed against every window like a crowd of pale faces trying to look in.

*You could be warm,* the voice said. It didn't argue. That was another thing Pip had learned—it never argued, never snapped, never did any of the things grown-ups did when you said no to them. It just... offered. The way a fire offers. The way bread offers. *I have a place for you. A real place. Not a cot by a drafty door. Not a bunk you'll be turned out of when you turn fourteen. A room that has been waiting for you since before you were born.*

"Rooms don't wait."

*This one does.* And then—this was new—the voice showed her.

It came like a memory, except Pip had never been there. A chamber high in a white palace, tall windows full of northern light, a bed big enough to swim in, furs piled deep as snowdrifts. A fire already lit. And on a small table beside the bed, laid out neat as Sunday: a tin cup, a tin plate, and a circlet of tin, bright-polished, sized for a child's head.

*See? I set it out myself. Every night I set it out, in case tonight is the night you come home.*

Pip's throat went tight. She hated that. She hated that her body didn't know the difference between a lie and a kindness, that warmth was warmth to it either way, that some starved animal part of her looked at that imaginary fire and that imaginary plate and wanted to walk into the picture and never come out.

"That's not real," she said. "You're making it up."

*I make many things real.* A pause, and then, softer: *Ask your keeper. Ask what the guild made real, and what it buried. Ask why the snow doesn't stop, little listener. It stops when I am fed. It has always stopped when I am fed.*

Pip sat up in the dark. Downstairs, the banked fire ticked and settled. Somewhere above, Sable rolled over and muttered something about tide-charts.

The winter had been getting worse. Pip had seen the frozen villages from the road—she'd seen the man at the well with his bucket half-raised, the woman at the loom with the shuttle still in her fingers, all of them shining and still as glass animals. Vesper said it was the crown doing it. Vesper said the crown was a parasite, that it fed on the realm and the realm was dying of it, and that was why they were going north, to unbind the king and end it.

But the voice told it differently. The voice said the crown was old, older than the guild, older than the ledgers, and that it had kept the winters short and the harvests fat for a hundred years, and that the gatekeepers had grown greedy, hoarding the gate, keeping the warmth for themselves.

Two stories. Two fires. Pip was ten years old and she had learned early that the story you got told depended on who was telling it, and that the teller usually wanted something.

"What do you want?" she asked the dark. "Really. Nobody gives you a room and a fire for nothing. Matron taught me that much."

The voice was quiet a long moment. When it came back, there was something in it Pip hadn't heard before—something almost like tiredness, or maybe like loneliness, though how a voice could be lonely Pip couldn't say.

*Clever girl. Good. Be clever with me too.* Then: *I want the gates opened. From within. There are doors in the palace that have been shut against me for eleven years—shut from the inside, by hands I cannot move. Open them, and I will ask nothing else of you. Ever.*

"And if I say no?"

*Then you will be cold tomorrow night. And the night after. And one night the cold will win, because it wins everywhere now, everywhere but with me.* The voice gentled further, and this was the worst part, this was the part that made Pip's eyes sting: *I do not want to hurt you, little listener. I want to feed you. There is a difference, whatever your keeper says. Have they fed you? Have they asked your name?*

"They know my name," Pip said. But even as she said it, she thought: *do they?* Vesper called her *girl*, mostly, or *child*, in that careful flat way of theirs, like naming her might cost something. Sable called her *sprat* and *urchin* and once, memorably, *you absolute disaster*, which Pip had privately treasured. But nobody had sat her down and said *your name is Pip and you are ours now* the way matrons did at adoption days, the ones who meant it.

*They took you in because I marked you,* the voice murmured, reading her silence the way it read everything. *You are useful to them. A tool that hears. When the work is done, tools get put away. I have watched keepers for a hundred years, child. I know what happens to the ones who stop being useful.*

"That's not—" Pip started, and stopped, because she didn't actually know it wasn't true. That was the trouble. She didn't know anything for certain except the cold and the whispers and the fact that Vesper had broken their own precious rules to let her come along, which was either proof of care or proof of need, and Pip couldn't tell which.

She lay back down. The blanket smelled of woodsmoke and other people's sleep. Through the floorboards she could hear Vesper moving below, methodical as ever, banking the fire, checking the locks, doing all the small invisible work of keeping people safe that nobody ever thanked anybody for.

*Think of it this way,* the voice said, drowsy-sweet now, working its way down toward her dreams where it did its best planting. *One door. One pair of small hands. No one gets hurt—I give you my word, and my word is iron, it is the oldest law in this realm. You open the doors, and you get the fire, and the room, and the name written in gold over the lintel. And the winter ends, and everyone thaws, and the frozen man at the well finishes drawing his water and goes home to his supper. Everyone lives, little listener. All you have to do is open a door.*

Everyone lives. Pip turned that over in the dark like a coin she suspected was clipped but couldn't prove. Because Vesper said the opposite—that the crown *was* the winter, that feeding it fed the frost—and Sable said worse things, spitting words like *leech* and *usurper*, and both of them were grown-ups who'd been wrong plenty of times, and the voice had never once lied to her. Not exactly. It had shown her a room that wasn't real, but it had never promised her something and then taken it back. Grown-ups did that constantly.

"You'd really end the winter?" she whispered. "If I opened the doors?"

*Child.* The voice wrapped around her like the furs in the imagined bed. *I would end the winter, and name a street after you, and teach the bells your birthday.*

Pip laughed—a small, wet, surprised sound—and then cried a little, quietly, the way she'd learned to in the workhouse, face in the pillow so no one would hear. Because that was the thing nobody understood about temptation. It wasn't a monster with claws. It was a warm room and someone saying your name like it mattered. It was being *wanted*, even if what wanted you was a parasite wearing a kingdom, even if the wanting was a hook. A hook and a hand looked the same when you'd been hanging in the cold long enough.

She must have slept, because when she woke the window was grey with dawn and the whispers had gone, leaving only the usual morning silence and a strange residue, like honey on a knife.

Downstairs, Vesper was already up, re-inking the map by candlelight, precise as a clerk. Sable was arguing with the stove. Neither of them looked up when Pip came in, and the room was cold enough to see her breath, and breakfast was half a heel of bread each because the flour had run out two days ago.

And yet. Vesper glanced up, just once, and said—not unkindly, not warmly either, but in that voice they used when they were choosing their words like steps across ice—"There's a town three miles east. Market day. If we move fast, we eat properly tonight."

Three miles. For food. For her, because she was the one who'd been hungriest, and Vesper had noticed without anyone saying it.

Pip sat down with her heel of bread and chewed and said nothing, and inside her chest two fires burned—one in a palace she'd never seen, promising her everything, and one in a freezing waystation kitchen where a keeper counted out miles like coins so a runaway could eat.

The voice came back that evening, while they walked, soft under the wind:

*You haven't said no yet, little listener.*

And Pip, staring north at a smudge on the horizon that might have been the capital's distant towers, discovered with a lurch of horror that it was right.

She hadn't.
