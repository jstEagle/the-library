## 14. The Anatomy of a Palace

The wound was a handprint.

That was how Sable thought of it, in the lucid moments between the burning: not a cut, not a bite, but a place where the winter had pressed its palm flat against her side and left its fingers printed in her flesh. Cold that didn't bleed but *stayed*, spreading outward from five points below her ribs like ink dropped in water. She had stopped looking at it after the second day. Looking made it real, and she needed it to be a rumor for a while longer, because rumors could be outlived.

"Left here," she said, or meant to say. What came out was "Left," and then nothing, because the corridor had no left anymore.

They stood at a junction that should not have existed. Sable had drawn this palace. Not visited it — *drawn* it, every stair and scullery and hidden passage, over six years in royal service, her pen moving through its bones until she knew them better than her own hands. There were exactly two junctions on this level of the servant warrens, and both had four branches. This one had seven, and one of them went up.

Stairs did not go up from the undercroft passages. She had checked. She had been paid to check.

"That's new," Pip said helpfully.

"It's not new." Sable braced a hand against the wall — cold stone, damp stone, stone that sweated like something alive — and made herself look at all seven branches again. "It's old. It's older than me. The building is remembering what it looked like before I mapped it."

Vesper crouched beside her, and their face did the thing it always did when they were frightened and refusing to admit it: went very still, very careful, like a clerk balancing a column that refused to sum. "Can you get us through?"

"I can get us through if the palace lets us."

"And if it doesn't?"

Sable laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough cost her something she couldn't name. "Then we'll be lost in the walls of the most heavily guarded building in the realm until the winter finds us, and honestly, Vesper, I've had worse weeks."

She hadn't. But lying was a muscle, and hers had grown strong since the river-town garret, since the flask, since the day she'd watched a dead courier's seal melt in her palm and decided her life was worth more than her honesty.

"Left," she said again, and chose the branch that sloped down and smelled of tallow and old bread, because kitchens never moved. Kitchens were the spine of any great house. Feed the spine and the body follows.

The corridor agreed with her for eleven steps.

On the twelfth, the tallow smell became lilies, and the kitchen passage became a chapel, and Sable understood with a cartographer's cold clarity that they were not walking through the palace. The palace was walking through itself around them, rearranging its organs like a man turning in his sleep, and they were inside it, small as thoughts.

* * *

Fever dreams came in colors.

Red first: the courier dying against the Hollowgate stones, saying *the thing wearing his crown*, his blood making a map of its own on the flagstones, and Sable tracing it later with one finger because she couldn't stop being what she was even then, even kneeling in a stranger's blood, even knowing what the map would cost.

Blue next: the frozen fair, the merchant mid-bargain with his hand still open, her own royal seal glinting on his chain like an accusation. *Purge reached everywhere,* she'd said, and Vesper had nodded, and neither of them had said the other true thing, which was that the purge had reached Sable Renn years before the winter ever fell. The crown had eaten her career long before it started eating the country. She'd just been too drunk to feel the teeth.

White last, always white: snow, and under the snow a door, and behind the door a voice saying her name in her patron's cadence, saying *Sable, you owe me, Sable, finish the work* —

"Sable." A hand on her shoulder. Small. Pip's. "You're doing the thing where you talk to people who aren't here."

"Am I winning?"

"You're mostly losing." Pip's face swam into focus above her — sharp-chinned, chapped-lipped, eyes too old for nine. "Vesper says we rest. Vesper says your skin is wrong."

"My skin is fine."

"Your skin is blue where it shouldn't be and red where it shouldn't be and there's frost coming out of the cut when you breathe." Pip delivered this inventory without drama, the way she reported everything, a child who had learned early that facts were the only currency adults couldn't counterfeit. "I can hear it humming. The cut. Like the whispers, but sadder."

Sable lay back against the wall — they were in some kind of linen store now, shelves of folded grey cloth rising into darkness, though ten minutes ago they'd been crossing a courtyard she remembered drawing with a fountain in it, except the fountain was gone and so was the sky — and let herself be honest for exactly four seconds.

"It's in me," she said. "A little bit of the winter. It got in when it touched me and it's mapping me the way I used to map things. That's what it does. That's all it does. It learns where everything is so it can hold everything still."

Pip considered this with terrible seriousness. "So you're a map now."

"A bad one. Incomplete. Unsurveyed territory." Sable smiled, and it hurt. "Don't tell the auditors."

"They're dead."

"One of them is. The guild has more." She closed her eyes. "Rest. Then we move. The king's apartments are above the long gallery, east range, third floor — if the building remembers being built, we'll find them. If it only remembers being *crowned*, God help us."

* * *

They walked for hours, or for minutes, or for no time at all; in the warrens the torches burned at whatever speed the palace preferred, and Sable's internal clock, that instrument she'd trusted for forty years, had gone soft and unreliable as wet vellum.

She navigated by memory and by principle. Memory failed constantly. Principle held.

Principle said: water flows downhill, so follow the drip-lines to find the outer walls. Principle said: servants' stairs are always narrower than grand ones, and always steeper, because architects are cruel and masons are tired. Principle said: a palace is an animal, and animals have habits older than their masters, and if you know where the animal is ticklish you can make it flinch.

The palace flinched beautifully when she found the old coal chute behind a wall that hadn't existed an hour ago. The chute shouldn't have been accessible — she'd sealed it herself, in her final year of service, inking the closure onto three separate plans — but the palace had forgotten her amendment, and its forgetting was the first mercy it had shown them. They dropped through into a service tunnel that smelled of a hundred years of fires, and for a quarter mile the going was honest, and Sable almost believed they'd won.

Then the fever took her properly, and the tunnel became the gallery of her disgrace.

She was walking it at twenty-six, newly appointed, carrying her first commission under her arm — *a full survey of the royal apartments, His Majesty's own request* — and the king met her at the end of it, young Aldric with his kind tired eyes, and he said, "Map everything, Mistress Renn. Especially the parts they tell you not to." And she'd laughed, thinking it a joke, and drawn the rooms exactly as instructed, and left blank the one chamber behind the throne where the draftsman's hand always shook, and it had taken her fifteen years and a dead friend to understand that the blanks in her maps were not oversights.

They were instructions. Someone upstream had wanted those rooms unmapped, and had reached down through the guild, through the court, through her own obedient pen, and made sure.

"Sable." Vesper's voice, close, urgent. "Your eyes are open but you're not here. Come back. We need the map that's in your head, not the one in your head's head."

"The blanks," she heard herself say. "Ask me about the blanks."

"What blanks?"

"In my surveys. Rooms I never drew. The palace is taking me to them." She surfaced far enough to see Vesper's face, pale in the torchlight, watching her with the expression of a person reading a ledger entry they wished they could unwrite. "It knows I'm the one who left them empty. It thinks I'm coming to fill them in."

"Are you?"

"No," Sable said. "I'm coming to cross them out entirely."

* * *

The worst hour was the hall of doors.

It appeared where a wine cellar should have been, and it was wrong in a way that made even Pip go quiet: a corridor lined on both sides with doors, dozens of them, every one slightly different — oak, iron, birch, one of green glass — and every one bearing a brass plate with a name.

Sable read them because she couldn't not. *Keeper Brightwater.* Her stomach dropped. *Keeper Brightwater* again, and again, generations of them, and then — third door on the left, plain pine, cheap hinges — *Lena.*

"Lena Brightwater," she whispered. "Vesper. Look."

But Vesper was already looking, and their face had gone the color of the frost on the walls, and they said, in a voice scraped down to bare wood, "We don't have time," and walked past it fast, and Sable let them, because some debts aren't collected in corridors. Some wait for the vault.

Behind them, softly, one of the doors opened and shut, like a throat clearing.

"Don't touch anything," Vesper said to Pip, unnecessarily. The child had her hands jammed in her armpits and her shoulders up around her ears.

"It's not the doors I don't like," Pip said. "It's that they're all locked from the outside."

* * *

The fever broke its rhythm near dawn — if it was dawn; the warrens kept their own weather — and gave Sable one clean hour, and she spent it the way she'd spent every good hour of her life: drawing.

No paper. She drew in the air, in words, dictating to Vesper while Pip kept watch with a kitchen knife in both fists like a talisman. "Long gallery, east. Windows on the north side, forty-one of them, count them aloud as you pass, the counting keeps the corridor honest. At the thirty-fourth window there's a servant door disguised as paneling — press where the carving shows a stag with a broken antler, left side. Stair beyond, narrow, up two flights. If the stair turns left, you're in my map. If it turns right—" She paused, tasting the old fear. "If it turns right, it's the crown's map, and you run, because the crown's map doesn't have exits."

"Why do you remember all this?" Vesper asked. Not unkindly. Genuinely asking, the way they asked everything, a person to whom knowledge was the only prayer they'd ever learned.

"Because I drew it to be unforgetable," Sable said. "I just didn't know what I was making unforgettable *for*." She coughed, and the frost came again, delicate as lace across her knuckles, and she wiped it away before Pip could see. "My patron — the courier, Wren, the man whose seal started all this — he told me once that a map is a promise. You draw a road, you're promising someone it leads somewhere. I've spent fifteen years breaking promises I didn't know I was making. Let me keep one."

Vesper looked at her for a long moment. Then they said, "Thirty-four windows. Stag with the broken antler. Left is yours, right is theirs."

"Left is mine," Sable said, and slept, and dreamed of roads that kept their word.

* * *

The gallery, when they reached it, had forty-one windows.

Sable counted them aloud herself, hoarse, leaning on Vesper's shoulder, because she did not trust the others' counting and did not trust her own mind and so split the difference between them. One. Two. The windows showed only white — no gardens, no city, no sky, just the long winter pressing its face against the glass like a beggar at a feast. Nine. Ten. Frost ferns grew inward from the panes, branching, precise, and Sable saw with distant professional admiration that they were drawn in perfect cartographic convention: rivers, roads, borders, a whole kingdom rendered in ice on the inside of the windows, as if the winter had already surveyed the realm and filed its map.

At window seventeen, the frost map showed the palace, and marked one room with a small dark star.

At window thirty-four, Sable stopped, and found the paneling, and pressed the stag's broken antler, and the door swung open onto a narrow stair.

It turned left.

"Mine," she breathed.

They climbed. Two flights, as promised, the walls close enough to brush with both elbows, torch brackets empty for decades, dust like grey snow on every tread. And at the top, a landing, and a door banded in black iron, and carved into the door's lintel in letters worn nearly smooth:

*WHAT IS CROWNED IS KEPT.*

Beyond it, faintly, they could hear a voice. One voice, low and level, speaking in the cadence of ritual — the same phrase over and over, patient as tide, exhausted as a man who has been saying it for years:

"—and I do not consent, and I do not consent, and I do not consent—"

Pip's hand found Sable's. Vesper's hand found the door.

"He's awake," Vesper whispered. "After all this time — he's *awake*, and he's been answering it the whole way—"

The frost on Sable's wound flared suddenly hot, then cold, then hot, and through the pain she felt the palace shudder around them like a beast becoming aware of a fly — every corridor at once rethinking itself, every door remembering its lock, the whole vast anatomy clenching toward the sound of their breathing.

The king's voice, behind the iron, faltered. Stopped.

And then, quiet, ragged, addressed — impossibly, directly — to the four of them standing in the dark:

"Whoever you are," King Aldric said, "you'd better be the ones who finally came to believe me."
