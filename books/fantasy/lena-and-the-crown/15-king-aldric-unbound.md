## 15. King Aldric, Unbound

The palace did not want us to find the king.

I understood that by the third hour of walking corridors that should have taken ten minutes. Sable led us with a fever-bright certainty through the servant warrens—narrow passages behind the walls, staircases that smelled of tallow and old bread—and still the building cheated us. Corridors lengthened when we weren't counting our steps. Doors we had marked with chalk appeared on the wrong side of the hall. Once we climbed a staircase for what felt like a quarter hour and emerged in the same corridor we'd left, our chalk marks grinning at us from the wall like a joke told by something with too many teeth.

"It's herding us," Sable said, leaning against the stone. Her wound had gone past red to a bruised violet at its edges, and she spoke through her teeth now, conserving breath. "The crown rearranges the rooms the way a spider reweaves a web. It's not trying to lose us. It's trying to deliver us somewhere."

"Where?" Pip asked.

Sable didn't answer. Neither did I, because I already knew. There was only one place in this palace worth delivering anyone: the throne room. The heart of the map. The jaw.

So we stopped fighting it. That was the trick I should have learned years ago at the Hollowgate—you cannot out-stubborn a thing that is older than stubbornness. Instead I took out the courier's map and held it flat between my hands, and I said, quietly, the way you speak to a gate, "Take us to him, then. We're coming anyway."

The map shivered against my palms. And the next door we opened led down.

* * *

The throne room was cold in a way that had nothing to do with winter.

Frost climbed the pillars in patterns too regular to be natural—spirals, whorls, the branching architecture of lungs seen from inside. The great windows were iced over from within, so that the light coming through them was white and sourceless, like light remembered rather than seen. And at the far end of the hall, beneath a canopy of black cloth gone grey with frost, sat the throne, and on the throne sat King Aldric.

He was performing.

That was the word my mind supplied before any other, and it was the right one. He sat crowned and robed, scepter upright in his right hand, orb balanced in his left, his spine held in the particular posture of a man who has been told a thousand times how a king sits. His eyes were open. His face was composed into mild, distant benevolence—the face stamped on coins, on proclamations, on the guild's own seal of authority. He was doing everything a king does except rule, because there was no one there to rule over, and still he performed, alone, in a frozen hall, to an audience of ice.

"He's been sitting like that for hours," Pip whispered. She had both hands pressed over her ears, though I knew it wouldn't help; the crown's voice came from inside, not outside. "Maybe days. Maybe longer."

"Aldric," Sable called, and her voice cracked in the middle of the name—not from fever, I thought, but from something older. She had drawn maps for this man once. She had signed them *by appointment to His Majesty*. "Aldric Renn-Vale. Your cartographer is here."

Nothing moved. The frost on the pillars creaked, very softly, like a house settling.

Then the king's head turned.

It turned the way a door turns—smoothly, completely, without the small hesitations a living neck makes. His eyes found us across forty feet of frosted floor, and for one long moment they were empty as the coin-face, and my hand went to the ledger in my coat, and Pip made a small sound behind me—

And then Aldric blinked. Twice. Hard. Like a man surfacing.

"Don't," he said. His voice was rust and disuse. "Don't come closer. Not yet." He swallowed, and I watched him fight for the next words the way a drowning man fights for air. "It watches through me when I look directly. Give me a moment. Let me... come back to the edges."

We waited. It was the strangest vigil of my life: standing in a frozen throne room while the King of the Realm closed his eyes and breathed, in and out, deliberately, like a man relearning the shape of his own body. Pip crept forward despite everything and sat down on the steps below the dais, cross-legged, patient, the way she used to sit outside the workhouse matron's door waiting to be noticed. Sable lowered herself onto a fallen banner with a grunt and pressed her palm hard against her wound.

At last Aldric spoke again, eyes still shut.

"You're real. I can hear three heartbeats. It doesn't have a heartbeat. Did you know that? Twenty-two years on this throne and I only understood it last winter, when the physicians stopped coming and the food stopped tasting and I realized no one had spoken to me who wasn't speaking *through* me." A dry, terrible laugh. "I used to think I was going mad. That was almost a comfort. Madness would have been mine."

"Your Majesty," Sable began.

"Don't." The word came out sharp enough to crack frost. Then, gentler: "Please don't kneel. Please don't use the title. If one more person calls me Majesty I will scream, and when I scream, *it* screams, and you do not want to hear it scream." He opened his eyes then—but angled, looking at a point on the floor between us, never quite at us. "My name is Aldric. I was a second son. I was never meant to wear this. My brother was meant to wear it, and he died of a fever at nineteen, and the guild brought me the crown in a box lined with black velvet, and they said the words, and I thought—I was twenty-three, I thought it was an honor."

"What are the words?" I asked.

His gaze flicked to me—my coat, my ink-stained fingers, the shape of the ledger under the wool—and something moved through his ruined face that might have been hope or might have been horror. "You're a keeper. Of course you are. You have the look. They always send a keeper eventually, when the feeding gets heavy and the king starts to spoil." He laughed again, that awful dry sound. "The words. Yes. *What the realm gives, the realm sustains. What the realm sustains, the realm becomes.* That's all. That's the whole coronation. No one ever asks what it means."

"And what does it mean?"

"It means I'm a meal," Aldric said simply. "It means the crown eats, and the realm is the field it grazes, and I am the mouth. Every proclamation I sign feeds it. Every tax, every levy, every winter court where the lords bow and say *long live the king*—it drinks the words. That's why the winter's been coming south for six years. That's why the villages freeze mid-prayer. It isn't weather. It's appetite."

Pip lifted her head. "It talks to me," she said. "In my sleep. It says it can give me a family."

Aldric looked at her then—really looked, forgetting his caution—and the grief on his face was so naked that I had to look away. "I know," he said softly. "It talked to me like that too, at first. Warmth. Belonging. A kingdom that loves you. It learns what you starve for and it offers exactly that, forever, and the price is only everything." He turned his head carefully aside. "Child, whatever it promised you—it lies the way water lies. Smoothly. Completely. It cannot help it. Lying is its shape."

"I know," Pip said. "I almost said yes anyway."

"I know," the king said again. "I say yes every morning. That's the part no one understands about being worn. It isn't a possession. It's a collaboration I can't refuse. Every dawn I put it on because the ceremony requires it, and every ceremony I perform because the realm requires it, and every requirement is a thread, and the threads are woven through me so deep now that I genuinely no longer know which of my thoughts are mine." His hands trembled on the scepter. "Do you understand? I am not asking you to save me. I am asking you to believe me. Because if you don't—if you decide I'm already gone, already the thing on the throne—then it wins without lifting a finger. Its best armor is despair. Everyone who might have helped me decided, years ago, that there was nothing left here to help."

* * *

We came closer then. He let us.

Up close, the king was worse and better than the coin-face suggested. Worse, because the frost had gotten into him too—silvered his temples beyond his years, rimed the collar of his robe, traced pale veins up his throat toward the jaw of the crown. Better, because his eyes were human eyes, exhausted and frightened and furiously, stubbornly present. He was starving. I could see it in the hollows of his cheeks, in the way his gaze kept snagging on Pip's half-frozen apple, on the flask at Sable's belt. The parasite fed on ceremony and left its host the scraps.

Sable unwound her scarf and wrapped it around his neck without asking permission, the way you tend someone who has forgotten they're allowed to be tended. "Your brother's maps hung in my schoolroom," she said roughly. "Prince Tomas's geography prize. Your handwriting was on the citation. You were fourteen. You wrote *may these charts carry him farther than the crown ever will.*"

Aldric stared at her. Something broke open in his face. "You remember that?"

"I resented it for years. I thought it was a rich boy's joke." Her voice frayed. "It wasn't, was it."

"No." He closed his eyes. "No, it wasn't."

I set the ledger on the steps between us, open to a blank page, and I did the thing I had done at every gate I'd ever kept: I recorded. *Found the king alive. Found the king lucid. Found the king begging not for rescue but for witness.* My pen scratched in the frozen silence, and Aldric watched me write with an expression I couldn't parse until he spoke.

"That's the old script," he whispered. "Keeper's hand. My grandmother had books in that hand. She burned them the year I was born—or the guild burned them, and she was made to watch." He leaned forward, chains of ceremony clinking, frost cracking along his sleeves. "Keeper. Listen to me. There is a vault beneath this room. Beneath the throne itself. The guild keeps its original records there—the binding, the terms, the names of every keeper since the first. Everything you need to unmake this thing is under the floor I've been sitting on for twenty-two years, and I could never reach it, because the moment I leave this chair unbidden, the ceremony breaks, and the crown—" his breath hitched— "the crown stops pretending to be an object."

"The final entry," I said. "Is there a final entry?"

"There's a page," he said. "Blank. Waiting. I've felt it waiting, all these years, the way you feel a tooth that hasn't broken yet." He gripped the arms of the throne. "Take me off this chair. Take the crown off my head. I don't care what it costs. But hear me first—all of you—hear me say it plainly, because no one has asked me anything in eleven years and I need one person in this world to know it was said and written down."

He drew himself up. Even starved, even silvered with frost, even worn to a thread, something of the fourteen-year-old mapmaker surfaced in him, and his voice carried down the frozen hall like a bell through snow.

"My name is Aldric Vale. I did not choose this crown. I do not consent to be worn. Whatever it has done in my name, whatever words it has spent through my mouth—I repudiate them. Write it down. Witness it. *Believe me.*"

I wrote it. Every word. My hand shook and I let it shake, and the ink went down into the ledger like roots into soil, and when I finished I read it back aloud to him, slowly, the way you read a debt into the record so it can never be denied.

Pip was crying without noise. Sable had her fist pressed to her mouth.

"Thank you," Aldric whispered, and slumped, and for a moment he was just a tired man in a cold room, wearing a hat that ate kingdoms.

Then the frost on the pillars began to sing.

It was a thin sound at first—a crystalline ringing, high and sweet, almost like a choir. Pip's hands flew to her ears. Sable staggered upright. And the temperature in the hall dropped so fast that our breath froze mid-air and fell as glitter, and every icicle in every window turned at once toward the throne like needles turning toward a vein.

The crown had heard us.

Aldric's eyes went wide with terror—not for himself. For us. "*Go,*" he breathed. "Now—down, beneath the dais, there's a seam in the floor, the vault—the guild auditors will have felt that, the whole palace felt that—go, and whatever you find down there, whatever name is waiting on that blank page—"

The lights in the iced windows flared white.

"—don't write mine," the king finished, in a voice that was suddenly, horribly, not entirely his own. "Whatever you do. Don't write mine in yet. It's listening for names."

The seam in the floor opened with a groan of moving stone, exhaling air that smelled of dust and candle-tallow and very old paper.

And from somewhere above us, deep in the palace's rearranged heart, came the sound of boots. Many boots. Walking in perfect unison.

The auditors had arrived.
