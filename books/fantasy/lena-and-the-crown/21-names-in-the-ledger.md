## 21. Names in the Ledger

The road to the Hollowgate had never been busy. For eleven years it had been a scar of packed mud through the pines, walked by one person and one person only, and even that person had tried to walk it quietly, as though the road itself might report her.

Now there were ruts in the mud from cartwheels. Now there was a fire pit by the outer ward with three flat stones for kettles, and a rack where travelers could hang wet cloaks, and a sign Sable had painted herself on a board salvaged from a drowned barn: **THE HOLLOWGATE — OPEN — COME AS YOU ARE — LEAVE WRITTEN DOWN.**

Pip had watched her paint it, tongue between her teeth, and had said, "That last bit doesn't make sense."

"None of it does," Sable had said, "that's rather the point," and had flicked her brush so a fleck of green paint landed on Pip's nose, and Pip had laughed so hard she'd had to sit down in the mud, because laughing like that was still new enough to knock her over.

That was six weeks ago. Now the thaw had gone properly mad, the way spring does when winter has held a country too long — everything blooming at once, greedy and loud, as if to make up for lost time. The pines were shedding their snow-load in great soft thumps. The ditch beside the road ran loud with meltwater. And every day, sometimes two or three times a day, someone came up the scar of mud toward the gate that no longer kept anything out.

Pip met them at the fire pit, because Vesper had decided — after much visible internal argument — that Pip was the best of them at this part.

"You have a way of being believed," Vesper had said, in the careful voice they used when stating something they'd rather not feel.

"It's 'cause I'm small," Pip had said.

"It is not because you are small."

It was a little because she was small. People looked at a child sitting cross-legged on a stone with a ledger open on her knees and they stopped being afraid, or at least they stopped being afraid *first*, which was enough to get them talking. And talking was the whole thing. That was the rule nobody had expected to be the rule: you could come through the gate freely, anyone could, but before you went on to whatever came next, you wrote yourself into the ledger. Your name. Where you'd been. What you'd lost, if you wanted to say. What you wanted, if you knew.

Vesper had fought that last column for two full days.

"A ledger records debts," they'd said. "Debts and holdings. It has never recorded *wanting*. Wanting isn't accountable."

"Then we're making a new kind of ledger," Sable had said, dipping her pen without looking up, "which is, I believe, the entire point of you now, Brightwater," and Vesper had made the face they made when they'd lost an argument and were secretly pleased about it, and the column had stayed.

Pip loved the wanting column best. She read it at night, sometimes, when the fire was low and Sable was snoring in the keeper's old room and Vesper was up doing whatever Vesper did instead of sleeping (walking the walls, Pip suspected; checking the gate out of habit; standing in the dark being quietly astonished). She read what people wanted. *Warm bread.* *To find my brother.* *A door that stays open.* *To stop running.* One man had written only *eggs*, and Pip had laughed until she cried, and then had thought about it for a long time afterward, how a man who had crossed half a frozen country might want nothing in the world but eggs, and how that was maybe the saddest and most hopeful thing she'd ever read.

* * *

The morning's arrivals came up the road just past dawn: a woman with a miller's forearms and a boy of about ten who was very obviously not her son and very obviously hers anyway. Refugees from the river towns, where the thaw floods had taken the lower streets. They stopped when they saw the gate, the way everyone did — a beat of suspicion, hands drifting toward whatever they carried that could be swung — and Pip gave them her practiced speech.

"Morning! You can come through, no toll, no questions you don't want answered. Only thing we ask is you write your name in the book before you go on. Any name you want. It doesn't have to be the one you were born with." She'd learned to add that part early, after watching a former guild enforcer — big man, scarred knuckles, gentle as a sick horse — go gray at the idea of putting his true name anywhere near ink.

The miller woman squinted at the ledger. "What is this place? We heard there was a gate that opened. We heard—" she lowered her voice, the way people did now, "—we heard the winter broke because somebody refused a crown."

"Somebody did," Pip said. "Four somebodies, actually. But mostly it was a lot of people deciding to stop believing in things that ate them."

The boy tugged the woman's sleeve. "Can I write too?"

"You can all write," Pip said. "That's most of what it's for."

She fetched the good pens — Sable had traded a map-drawing in Marrowford for a dozen goose quills and a pot of iron-gall ink, and guarded them like crown jewels, which Pip found funny for reasons she didn't explain — and set the ledger open on its stand. The miller woman wrote *Marta Cole, Miller's End, lost my mill, want to build one somewhere the water asks permission first.* The boy hovered, chewing his sleeve.

"I don't got a name," he said finally, in the flat way kids said terrible things. "Not one anybody used."

Pip felt that one under her ribs. She remembered the workhouse roll, where she'd been a number next to a chore rotation. She remembered the night the crown had whispered to her across forty miles of frozen dark, offering her warmth and family and home, and how close she'd come to saying yes, and how she'd been saved not by courage but by a keeper breaking every rule she had to sit down beside a child and read her a name out of a book.

"Then pick one," Pip said. "Pick a good one. It's the only name you get to choose your whole life, probably, so make it one you like saying."

He thought about it with his whole body, brow furrowed, lips moving. Then he took the pen in both hands like a sword and wrote, in enormous wobbling letters, *BRAMBLE*, and underneath, in smaller ones, *want eggs.*

Pip laughed until Marta Cole asked if she was all right.

"He'll fit right in," she managed. "There's hens in the yard. Vesper will pretend to grumble about it."

* * *

Midmorning brought a harder kind of visitor, and Pip steeled herself, because these were the ones that mattered most and went worst.

Former guild members had started coming in the third week. After Aldric's abdication and the council of guild-kept towns formed, the Guild of Impossible Things had come apart like wet paper — no more auditors, no more ledgers of debts owed to shadows, no more taking children who read the wrong pages. Some of its people had scattered. Some had defected to the council. And some had come here, to the gate their organization had spent generations pretending didn't exist, carrying the particular hollowness of people whose whole life had been a cover story.

This one was young. Younger than Sable, older than Pip, with the neat, forgettable face the guild trained into its clerks, and he stood at the fire pit wringing his cap like it owed him money.

"I shouldn't be here," he said, before hello. "I filed reports. On towns. I told them which towns were keeping extra grain, and then the requisitions came, and I know what requisitions meant in a hard winter, I *did the arithmetic myself*—"

"Breathe," Pip said.

"—and I'm not asking forgiveness, I'm asking if there's work. Any work. I can keep accounts. I can keep accounts better than anyone alive, that's not boasting, that's just — it's what they made me, and I'd like it to be for something that isn't—" His voice cracked. He stared at the ground.

Pip let the silence sit for a moment. Vesper would have handled this differently — Vesper would have given him a long, level look and said something precise and devastating about complicity and ledgers. But Vesper wasn't here, and Pip had noticed something in six weeks of gate-keeping: people who hated themselves didn't need to be devastated. They needed a task.

"There's a woodpile," she said, "that needs splitting before the next winter, because we've learned our lesson about winters. And there's a henhouse that wants mending. And after that, if you still want accounts, Vesper will put you on the stores ledger, and they will check your figures twice, and if you survive that you'll be the first person ever to do so."

He blinked at her. "You're a child."

"I'm the one holding the book," Pip said, and turned the ledger toward him. "Name?"

His hand shook so badly the first attempt was a blot. He wiped it with his sleeve and tried again, slow, letter by letter: *Oren Wick, clerk, third class. I want to count for people who eat what I count.*

Pip read it twice. Then she closed the ledger gently and said, "Woodpile's around back. Bramble will show you. Try not to let him talk you into giving him your lunch; he's new at having a name and he's testing what it gets him."

* * *

Afternoon, Sable emerged.

This was an event. Sable worked in bursts — three days shut in the map room she'd claimed above the old storeroom, emerging hollow-eyed and ink-stained with something impossible clutched in her fingers, then a day of sleeping and drinking tea and insulting everyone lovingly. Today she came down carrying a rolled sheet of heavy paper and wearing the expression she got when a drawing had gone *right*, which Pip privately thought of as the sun coming out on a mean little cloud.

"Right," Sable announced to the yard at large. Oren Wick dropped his axe. "Gather round, all of you, yes, you too, hen-boy. Behold."

She unrolled it on the trestle table and weighted the corners with stones. It was a map of the realm — but not any map Pip had seen. The old royal maps, the ones Sable had once drawn for a king who wore a parasite, had shown roads and borders and the palace rendered in proud miniature. This one showed something else. Around every town that had signed onto the council, Sable had drawn a small open ring. Around the Hollowgate, a larger one, with radiating lines like a wheel. And along the old courier roads — the routes the dead man had run, the routes the guild's enforcers had hunted — she'd written distances not in miles but in hours, and beside each hour, small annotations in her sharp slanted hand: *well here, unfrozen since thaw. Safe loft, ask for the widow Ansel. Bad bridge, go the ford.*

"What is it?" Marta Cole asked.

"It's a map for people going somewhere," Sable said. "Which sounds obvious until you remember that for the last hundred years, maps in this country were drawn for people deciding where other people were allowed to go." Her jaw tightened; Pip knew she was thinking of the purge, of finding her own royal seal on a merchant's corpse in a market frozen mid-bargain, of mourning a country while it still stood. "So. New maps. Anyone may copy them. Anyone may add to them. If you know a well, a safe barn, a rotten bridge, you come tell me and I'll put it down, and your name goes beside it, because knowledge should have a signature."

Oren Wick had drifted over despite himself, clerk's eyes already tracing the grid. "The scale's wrong," he said, then went pale. "I mean — the scale is *unconventional*—"

"The scale is honest," Sable said. "Miles lie. Hours don't. A mile is the same for a cart and a cripple and a child, is it? No. An hour knows the difference." She glanced at Pip, quick and dry. "Besides, I learned anatomy from a map that drew the palace as a body. Once you've drawn a throne room as a heart, you lose patience with conventional cartography."

Pip looked at the map a long moment. At the little open rings. At the wheel-lines radiating from the Hollowgate, reaching toward towns that hadn't yet sent anyone, toward places that didn't yet know the gate existed.

"They'll come," she said. "More than we've got beds for."

"Then we'll need more beds," Sable said, as if that settled it, and rolled up the map, and went back inside, and Pip heard her start humming, which meant she was happy, which meant everything was fine.

* * *

Evening. Fire low. Vesper came back from the wall-walk and sat on the bench beside Pip without being asked, which was its own small miracle; six weeks ago they would have found a reason to be elsewhere.

"How many today?" they asked.

"Three. A miller, a boy named Bramble, and a guild clerk who wants to do penance via firewood."

"Mm." Vesper turned the ledger toward themselves and read the day's entries. Pip watched their face do the thing it did — the flicker at *want eggs*, the long stillness at Oren Wick's line. When they reached *Bramble* they stopped entirely.

"He chose it himself," Pip said.

"I see that." Vesper's voice was odd. "It's a good name. Thorny. Survives winter." They closed the ledger, then didn't let go of it. Their thumb moved along the cover's edge, back and forth, the way it did when something was working in them.

"You've been strange all week," Pip said. "Stranger."

"I have not been strange. I have been thorough."

"You've been reading the ledger at night. The front of it. Not the new names — the old ones. The first pages." Pip had seen the lamp burning in the map room annex where Vesper kept the book; had heard pages turning at hours when even Sable slept. "You've read the first page, haven't you? You've known for days."

Vesper was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. Out in the dark, a hen muttered. Somewhere beyond the gate, the meltwater ran on and on, the sound of a country unclenching.

"There's a name on the first page," Vesper said at last, "that has been scraped out. Deliberately. With a blade, long ago — the guild's work, I'd wager. But ink goes deep, and parchment remembers. Under the scrape, if you hold it to the light at the right angle..." They exhaled slowly. "There's a name, Pip. The founding keeper's name. The first person who bound the crown, who built the Hollowgate, who began the ledger. Erased from her own book."

Pip's chest went tight. "Whose name?"

Vesper looked at her then, and Pip saw that they were frightened — not of auditors, not of winter, not of anything with teeth. Frightened the way you're frightened on the edge of a gift.

"Brightwater," they said. "Lena Brightwater."

The name landed in Pip like a bell struck. She thought of the ledger's blank final entry, the one that had waited for a name until Vesper wrote theirs. She thought of rules broken, of a sister taken, of invisibility chosen as a wound and then, slowly, painfully, unwound. She thought of a girl picking the name *Bramble* because it survives winter.

"So it was yours all along," Pip said softly. "Not the guild's. Not the crown's. Hers. Yours."

"And tomorrow," Vesper said, and their thumb stilled on the ledger's edge, "I am going to write her back in."
