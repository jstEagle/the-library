## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trick to stealing a memory, Coral had learned by his fourteenth winter, was to be boring.

Not invisible — invisibility drew the eye, made people wonder what they'd missed. Boring. A young man with a crate of apples he didn't particularly care about, arranged with the particular carelessness of someone who had arranged them a hundred times before. A face that promised nothing interesting if you looked at it twice, so you didn't. Hands that moved through the crowd like they belonged to the crowd, because in every way that mattered, they did.

He worked the dusk market at the mouth of the Hollow, where the road came down off the ridge and forgot itself among the stalls. Dusk was the hour for it. Travelers arriving at twilight were already half-dissolved — tired, hungry, grateful for lamplight and the smell of frying onions — and gratitude loosened people. Gratitude was a door left unlatched.

"Apples," Coral said to the woman in the gray traveling cloak, holding one up as though it were the most ordinary apple in the world, which it was. "Last of the Wrenfield stock. Good keeping sort."

She wasn't from anywhere called Wrenfield. Nobody was. He'd invented the orchard years ago because travelers trusted specificity, and because there was something pleasant about tending an orchard that existed only in his lies — pruning trees that would never grow, weathering frosts that never came.

She bought three. And when she counted out her coins, her sleeve brushed his wrist, and Coral did what he had done ten thousand times: he reached into the warm place behind her eyes and took.

It was not violence. People imagined theft as a yank, a wound, but a memory given up gently barely notices it's gone. This one was small — a river crossing, her father's hand on the rope, the water loud and brown and alive. She'd been carrying it forty years like a stone in her shoe. Coral lifted it out, and she blinked, and smiled at him as though he'd done her a kindness, which in a sense he had, and walked on into the valley lighter than she'd come.

The stone went into the jar under his stall. By morning Sable would weigh it, argue about its grade, and pay him in coin that smelled of other people's kitchens.

* * *

The Hollow had one law, and everyone in it could recite it in their sleep, mostly because they'd forgotten everything else worth reciting:

*Magic cannot follow you in. But neither can magic leave.*

That was the whole covenant, carved over the gatehouse in letters worn soft as river stones. The valley sat in a fold of the world where the old gods' attention simply slid off, the way rain slides off oiled wool. Whatever hunted you — a debt, a curse, a god, a grief with teeth — it stopped at the ridge line and paced there, howling, until it gave up. In exchange, anything of the other kind that crossed in stayed in. Prayers died in the air here. Blessings curdled. A charm brought over the pass was just a pretty rock by morning.

And memories, Coral thought, counting his jar by feel in the failing light, were the smallest magic of all. Small enough to slip out in a peddler's pocket. The elders pretended not to know this. The elders were, in Coral's private opinion, magnificent hypocrites with excellent taste in wine, paid for in exactly this currency.

He didn't feel guilty about the trade. He wanted to be clear with himself about that, at least once a day, the way a man checks a fence. The people who came down the ridge came to *forget*. They climbed over the Wrenfield pass — no such place, but the maps agreed to lie about it — carrying things no one should have to carry: wars, betrayals, children buried too small, faces seen at the wrong end of a knife. The Hollow offered them silence, and silence had a price, and Coral was simply the toll-keeper. Somebody had to be. Better him than the elders, who'd have charged triple and wept about it publicly.

Still. There were nights — and tonight was shaping into one — when the jar felt less like a purse and more like a stomach. Full of things that weren't his, digesting slowly, giving nothing back.

* * *

"You've got the hollow-eyed look," Sable said, when Coral brought the evening's take to her shop between the candlemaker's and the shuttered chapel. "The one that means you've been *tasting* again."

"I don't taste them."

"You do. You get this look like you're chewing." Sable was sixty if she was a day, sharp-faced, with rings on every finger except the ones she'd pawned, and she weighed memories the way an apothecary weighs arsenic — respectfully, and with one eyebrow raised. She tipped the jar out onto black velvet and began sorting. River crossings. Cheap. A first kiss, fumbled, behind a granary — mid-grade, sentimental buyers liked those. Then she stopped, held one up to the lamp as though reading a label, and whistled.

"Coral. Where did you get this?"

"The gray cloak woman. Off the ridge road."

"This isn't a memory. It's a *prayer*." She turned it in the light, and Coral saw it then — or rather, he felt it, the way you feel a storm two valleys off. A warmth in the thing, a reaching. Someone had prayed, hard, recently, somewhere past the ridge, and the prayer had *gone somewhere*, and this woman had been standing close enough to catch the shine of it.

"That's impossible," Coral said.

"Obviously," said Sable. "Look at it anyway."

He looked. And looking was a mistake, because memories opened when you looked at them, that was their nature, even secondhand ones, even prayers —

— a hilltop past the ridge, a woman kneeling in wet grass, saying *please, please, please* to a sky that had been silent for three hundred years, and then the sky —

the sky *answered* —

Coral dropped it. The prayer rolled across the velvet and lay still, glowing faintly, like a coal someone had breathed on.

"Pick it up," Sable said, delighted and horrified in equal measure. "Do you know what this is worth? Do you know what this *means*? The gods are answering again. Out there." She gestured vaguely eastward, toward the ridge, toward the entire rest of the world, with the air of a woman indicating a rising flood. "And in here—" she tapped the floorboards, the valley, the whole folded-up quiet of the Hollow, "—nothing. Business as usual. For now."

"For now?"

"Things crack, boy. When pressure builds on one side of a wall." She swept the memories back into the jar, all but the prayer, which she sealed in a lead box, the way you'd house a spider you couldn't bring yourself to kill. "Go home. Sleep. Stop tasting strangers' griefs; it makes you melancholy and melancholy makes you sloppy."

Coral went home. Home was a room above a cooper's shop, one window, one chair, one bed, one shelf of jars he kept for himself — empty ones, waiting, though he'd never once managed to fill them. Because here was the joke at the center of his life, the one he told no one: the thief of memories had almost none of his own. A childhood like fog. A mother's voice, gone to a shape without words. A brand on his wrist he'd had since before he could remember having skin, a small curled mark like a spark frozen mid-fall, which the elders looked at sideways and never explained.

He'd stolen hundreds of memories. He could not steal himself one.

He lay in the dark listening to the valley be silent — truly silent, the silence of a place where no god is listening, which is either peace or exile depending on the night — and told himself, as he did every night, that this was enough. Competence was enough. Coin was enough. An orchard of invented apple trees was enough.

Then, from the direction of the ridge, faint as a struck match: singing.

Not a traveler's tune. Not a work song. It had the cadence of liturgy, of something sung *at* the world rather than to it, and it should not have crossed the pass at all, because nothing holy crossed the pass, that was the covenant, that was the law, that was the whole architecture of his unremarkable life.

The singing stopped at the ridgeline.

And then, distinctly, patiently, it began again — on the near side, coming down.
