## 2. A Prayer Answered

Lena had been told that the Hollow could not be found, only entered, and she had spent eleven days proving the second half of that sentence true while the first half laughed at her from every direction.

The road into the valley did not exist on any map she carried — and she carried three, including one drawn by a dying monk who claimed to have walked out of Vael before it was unmade, which everyone agreed was either a miracle or a lie told for a warm bed. The road simply appeared when her feet were too tired to doubt it: a dirt track winding down between hills the color of old wool, as if the valley had grown bored of hiding and decided, just this once, to let her in.

She understood why, later. The gods had sent her. And things sent by gods were rarely permitted the dignity of getting lost.

But at the mouth of the Hollow, standing in the last of the evening light, Lena paused and did something no priestess of the reawakened church was supposed to do.

She hesitated.

Below her, the valley glowed like a lamp left burning in an empty house. Market lanterns strung along a crooked street. Smoke rising straight up from chimneys, unbothered by wind, because there was no wind here — she could feel it even from the ridge, the stillness, thick as held breath. Somewhere down there, a fiddle played badly, which meant someone was happy enough not to care.

*Magic cannot follow you in,* the doctrine said. *The Hollow is the one place the gods cannot see.*

That was the whole reason it existed. That was the whole reason she had been chosen for this errand, though "chosen" was a generous word for what happened when the high altar burned with white fire and spoke your name in a voice like a landslide, and you said *yes* because the alternative was unthinkable, and then discovered that yes had teeth.

Lena adjusted the strap of her satchel, touched the relic at her throat — a small disc of black glass, warm as a sleeping bird — and began to walk down into the place where her god could not watch her.

It felt, she would admit later, a little like freedom. Which frightened her more than the mission did.

* * *

The market at dusk smelled of roasted chestnuts and wet stone and, beneath both, something faintly sweet that Lena couldn't name until she realized it was the smell of forgetting — of memories being handled, traded, worn soft by many fingers.

She kept her hood up and moved through the crowd the way she'd been taught: unhurried, unremarkable, a woman buying chestnuts. The vendors here sold ordinary things — bread, candles, tin whistles — but she caught the shadow-trade happening at the edges, in doorways and behind carts. A farmer pressing a small clay token into a traveler's palm. A woman weeping quietly over nothing, lighter by one memory than she'd been that morning.

They bought and sold the past here. It should have horrified her. Instead she found herself watching with a kind of hungry fascination, because in the church they taught that memory was sacred, that every recollection was a candle lit before the face of god — and yet these people looked *relieved*. Unburdened. One gray-haired man laughed at his own joke with the delight of someone hearing it for the first time, which, possibly, he was.

"You're staring," said a voice beside her.

Lena turned. The chestnut seller, a round woman with flour on her forearms and eyes sharp as awls, was looking at her with open amusement.

"Forgive me," Lena said. "I've never seen a market like this."

"Mm. Most pilgrims say that." The woman handed her a paper cone of chestnuts, unsolicited, and waved off the coin. "On the house. Consider it a welcome gift." Her gaze flicked, briefly, to the hollow of Lena's throat where the relic hung hidden. "We get all sorts. People running from grief, mostly. Some running from worse." She leaned in, conspiratorial. "If you're looking to sell, the good buyers set up near the well after dark. If you're looking to *buy*—" her voice dropped, "—that's Coral's territory. But he doesn't sell to strangers."

"Coral," Lena repeated, keeping her voice mild, idle, incurious. Inside her chest, her heart had begun to knock. *The ember-bearer lives in the Hollow. He will be unremarkable. He will hide in plain sight.* "Local man?"

"Local as the stones." The woman's expression flickered — fondness and wariness tangled together. "Best thief in the valley, though he'd call himself a trader. Lifts memories so gentle you don't feel it. Half the time folk thank him after." She snorted. "Don't know how he does it. Nobody does. He just touches your hand, asks you a question, and somewhere between the question and the answer, a piece of you goes missing."

Lena's fingers closed around the black glass disc through her shirt.

"And he can't be stolen from?" she asked lightly. "A man in his trade must have enemies."

"Oh, everyone's tried." The chestnut seller laughed. "Man's got nothing to take. Or nothing anyone can reach. Like trying to pickpocket a locked room." She squinted at Lena. "You ask a lot of questions for someone buying chestnuts."

"I'm a curious person," Lena said, and smiled, and the smile was genuine, which was the most dangerous thing about it.

* * *

She found him at the well after dark, exactly as advertised, and understood immediately why no description of him had ever been useful.

Coral Crane was unremarkable the way water is unremarkable. Medium height, dark hair going gray early at the temples, a lean face built for being forgotten. He wore a coat with patched elbows and moved through the crowd without displacing it, the way smoke moves through a room. She watched him work three marks before she approached: a touch on the wrist, a question — *what's the first thing you'll do when you're home?* — a laugh, a nod, done. Each time, the mark wandered away visibly lighter, and each time, Coral's own face did something complicated and quick, there and gone, that Lena might have missed if she hadn't been trained since childhood to read the faces of people talking to gods.

Guilt, she thought. He's full of guilt and calls it competence.

She waited until he was between marks, then stepped into his path.

"Excuse me," she said. "I'm told you're the man to see about forgetting."

Something moved behind his eyes — assessment, fast and professional, cataloguing her the way a locksmith catalogs a strange key. "Depends who's asking."

"A traveler," Lena said. "Passing through."

"Nobody passes through the Hollow." His voice was pleasant, unhurried. "That's rather the point of it."

"Then I'm staying a while." She extended her hand, palm up, an offering. "I have a memory I'd like to be rid of. A large one. I'm told you pay fairly."

He took her hand.

And stopped.

Lena watched it happen — watched the small, practiced focus in his face sharpen into concentration, then confusion, then something she had no name for. His thumb pressed against her pulse point. His brow creased. He tried again, and this time she felt the faintest tug, like a fish testing a line and finding it anchored to the bottom of the sea.

"That's odd," he said quietly.

"Is it?"

"It's never odd." He let go of her hand and studied her with new attention, and Lena felt, absurdly, *seen* — the sensation so foreign to her mission that she nearly flinched. "You're shielded. Warded? No—" he tilted his head, listening to something, "—no ward feels like this. This isn't armor. This is just... nothing to hold. You're like a wall painted to look like a door."

"What a strange compliment."

"It wasn't a compliment." But he was smiling slightly now despite himself, and she noted with interest that he seemed annoyed about it. "Who are you?"

"Lena." True names had power; hers was common enough to be safe. "And you're Coral. The chestnut seller speaks very highly of you."

"The chestnut seller wants a discount on her next forgetting. We have an arrangement." He hadn't moved, but somehow he had shifted — angled himself so the lantern light didn't quite reach his face, so the crowd flowed around them like water around a stone. Invisible again. Practiced. "Look, I don't know what you're carrying or who warded you, but the Hollow has rules—"

"Yes," Lena said. "Magic cannot follow you in."

His smile died. Just like that, gone, and underneath it was something raw and young and terribly awake.

"Nobody says it like that," he said. "Outsiders say it like a rumor. *They say magic can't follow you into the Hollow.* You said it like scripture."

Lena's hand drifted, without her permission, to the relic at her throat.

Coral's eyes followed. And in the space of a single breath, she watched invisibility crack — watched him go from smoke to flame, from forgotten to unforgettable, standing in the middle of the market as if the whole valley had narrowed to the two of them and the small black disc warming against her skin.

"What is that," he said. Not a question. A demand wrapped in a whisper.

"A relic," she said carefully. "From my order."

"Your order being—"

"Would you believe a knitting circle?"

"No." He swallowed. "Because my mother knitted, and she never once made anything glow."

"I never said it glows."

"You didn't have to." He glanced around — quick, instinctive, checking sightlines — and then, before she could stop him, his fingers brushed the back of her hand where it rested near the relic.

The world went white.

Not for her — for him. She saw it happen: his pupils blowing wide, his breath catching, his free hand shooting out to grip the edge of the well as if the ground had tilted. For three heartbeats Coral Crane stood perfectly still in the crowded market, and inside his skull, Lena knew, a stolen memory was playing — one he'd lifted from some traveler weeks ago, some pilgrim's small prayer, filed away and forgotten.

Except it wasn't forgotten anymore. Because beyond the ridge, in a country that officially did not exist, something impossible had happened: a god had answered.

She knew which memory it was. She'd been there. Rain on a scorched hillside. A woman kneeling in the mud, praying to a silence that had lasted three hundred years — and the silence breaking, splitting, pouring light.

When Coral came back to himself, he was shaking.

"That's not possible," he whispered. His eyes, when they found hers, were the eyes of a man whose entire architecture had just developed a fault line. "The gods are gone. They've been gone since before my grandmother's grandmother. The whole reason this valley exists is that they *left*, and things that leave don't come back—"

"They came back," Lena said softly. "Three months ago. All of them. At once."

The market noise went on around them — haggling, laughter, the bad fiddle — but between the two of them there was a silence with weight and edges.

"Why are you telling me this?" Coral asked. And then, slower, watching her face with those thief's eyes that missed nothing: "Why did you really come to the Hollow, Lena-with-no-memory-I-can-steal?"

Every doctrine she had ever learned told her to lie. Every instinct she had ever trusted told her the lie would not hold against this man, not now, not when he had already seen the shape of the truth through her painted door.

So she gave him the smallest true piece she had.

"I'm looking for someone," she said. "Someone the gods lost a long time ago. Someone who doesn't know what he's carrying."

Coral went very still.

And in the lantern light, Lena watched the first crack spread across the careful invisibility of Coral Crane — watched him raise his left hand, slowly, and turn it over, and stare at the faint brand on the inside of his wrist that he had spent his whole life pretending was a birthmark.

"That's funny," he said, and his voice was steady in the way of things about to shatter. "Because I've been having dreams lately. About fire."
