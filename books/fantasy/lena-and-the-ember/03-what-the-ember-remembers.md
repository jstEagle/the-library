## 3. What the Ember Remembers

The relic sat on my worktable like a cat that had decided to stay.

Lena had left it there the night before — deliberately, I suspected, the way she did most things, with the calm of someone who had never once needed to pick a pocket in her life. It was a small disc of black glass on a cord, warm to the touch even though it had been sitting in a cold room all night, and it had no business being in the Hollow at all.

Nothing magical had business being in the Hollow. That was rather the point of us.

I circled it twice, the way you'd circle a sleeping dog of uncertain temperament. "You're not going to do anything dramatic," I told it. "You're a pendant. You hang off necks. You have one job."

It said nothing, which I chose to interpret as agreement.

Here is what you should understand about me: I am very good at knowing what things remember. A stolen memory has a texture, a weight, a smell almost — grief smells like rain on hot stone, joy like bread, shame like copper. I'd spent twenty years reading people the way other men read weather. And this little disc was *loud*. Not with anyone else's memories, either. With something that felt uncomfortably like it had been waiting for me specifically, the way an unpaid debt waits.

Which was ridiculous. I was nobody's debtor. I was the Hollow's best thief and its least interesting resident, and I intended to remain both for as long as the valley would have me.

So naturally, I picked it up.

* * *

My mother's kitchen smelled of burnt honey.

That was the first thing — absurdly, horribly, the first thing after twenty years was the smell of burnt honey, because my mother could never leave the pan alone long enough to caramelize anything properly, and she'd laugh about it, she'd say, *"Coral, love, if I ever make it right, you'll know I've been replaced by something wearing my face,"* and I was laughing too, I was seven years old and laughing —

— and then I was somewhere else, and the somewhere else was worse.

*"Listen to me."* Her hands on my face. Her hands were shaking. My mother's hands never shook; they were steady as a carpenter's rule even when the rest of her came apart. But here they shook, and I was older than seven, I was maybe ten, and there was blood on her sleeve that I understood even then she would have wanted me not to notice. *"When they come — and they will come, sweetheart, someday they'll come — you mustn't tell them what you are. You mustn't tell them what you carry."*

"Carry?" said seven-year-old me, or ten-year-old me, or both of us at once, because memory doesn't keep time the way clocks do.

She took my wrist and turned it over. The inside of it, where the skin is thin enough to see the blue of your own blood. And there — where I had always assumed I'd been born with a birthmark, a small dark whorl like a thumbprint made of shadow — there was a brand. A mark like a door seen from above, or a keyhole, or an eye that had learned to close itself.

*"Vael hid its last fire in a person,"* she whispered, *"because a country can be erased but a child can hide. You are the hiding place, Coral. You are the last door Vael built."*

"I don't want to be a door."

*"No."* She kissed the brand, right there on my wrist, and her tears fell on it and hissed, actually hissed, like water on a stove. *"Nobody does. That's how I know it chose well."*

Then the kitchen went, and the honey-smell went, and I was standing in my own room with the black disc in my hand and my heart going like a trapped bird, and the brand on my wrist was *warm*. Warmer than the pendant. Warm as a coal pulled from a fire, warm as something patient.

I dropped the thing. It landed soft, which felt like mockery.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I want that on the record. Whatever Lena thought she saw when she knocked at dawn — the shadows under my eyes, the untouched supper, the way I flinched when she said good morning like she'd drawn a knife — I want it known that I spent the entire night doing extremely rational things, such as wrapping my wrist in linen, interrogating a piece of jewelry, and considering whether one could emigrate from one's own skin.

"You look terrible," Lena said, setting down two cups of tea she had apparently made herself in my own kitchen, which raised several questions about boundaries I decided not to pursue.

"Thank you. I worked late."

"On what?"

"Inventory."

"At midnight."

"Some inventory is sensitive."

She looked at the black disc lying on my table, then at me, then back at the disc, and I watched her arrive at the truth with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had crossed a mountain range to find me and was not about to be defeated by my poker face. "You touched it."

"I examined it."

"Coral."

"I *examined* it."

"And?"

And. I poured the tea mostly to have something to do with my hands. The brand itched under its wrapping like a secret trying to get out. "It showed me my mother," I said finally, because lying to Lena turned out to be physically difficult, like swallowing stones. "Dying. Telling me things I don't remember being told. Things I'm fairly sure I would remember, if they'd happened."

"Why are you sure they didn't happen?"

"Because one of them involves me being a *door*."

To her credit, she didn't laugh. She wrapped her hands around her cup and looked into it the way priests look into things — like the bottom of the cup might hold an answer, or at least a decent metaphor. "Vael," she said quietly. "Tell me exactly what she said."

So I did. All of it. The honey, the shaking hands, the brand, the words *last door Vael built*, spoken aloud in my own kitchen for the first time, and saying them made them heavier, the way naming a fear always does. Lena listened without interrupting once, which in my experience is rarer than magic.

When I finished, she reached across the table — slowly, giving me time to pull away, which I appreciated and did not take — and turned my wrist over. She unwound the linen with two fingers.

The brand looked up at us both. A door, from above. A keyhole. An eye, closed.

"It's real," she breathed. "The old texts say the ember-bearer would carry the seal visibly by now. They say the gods' return would wake it." Her thumb hovered over the mark without touching it, the way you hover your hand over a flame to learn its heat. "Coral. Do you understand what this means?"

"That I should have stayed a birthmark?"

"That you're why I found you." She looked up, and there was something in her face that wasn't doctrine. Something almost frightened. "Every priestess in my order has prayed for a sign of the ember for thirty years. Candles burned down to stubs. And the gods answered *me*, sent *me*, and I told myself it was because I was faithful, because I was worthy—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "I think the ember called. I think it's been calling since before I was born, and I just happened to be standing where the sound could reach."

"That's a terrible reason to cross a mountain range."

"It's the only kind of reason that works."

I stood up, because sitting still had become unbearable, and went to the window. Below, the Hollow was waking: Sable unlocking her shop with the key she pretended to keep hidden under the third loose brick, old Marta arguing with her goat, the morning fog rolling off the ridge like the valley exhaling. Ordinary. Safe. A place where magic couldn't follow you in and couldn't get out, and I had spent my whole life believing those were the same sentence.

They weren't. Magic couldn't follow you in. But I had walked in carrying it, all along, tucked behind my mother's careful lies and my own convenient forgetting. The valley hadn't been protecting itself from me. It had been holding its breath around me for twenty years.

"My mother died getting me here," I said to the window. "Didn't she. Whoever 'they' are — whatever was hunting Vael's fire — she ran until running killed her, so I could grow up in a valley that swallows memories, and forget everything including the reason she ran."

"Yes," Lena said simply. There was no comfort in it and no pity either, which I suspect is why I could bear it.

"So the ember — the thing inside me — it let me forget? Or she made me forget?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. If it let me forget, then it's patient, and patience in a fire is a horrifying quality. If *she* made me forget, then I've spent twenty years selling other people their pasts while mine sat in my wrist like a—" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Like stolen goods. I'm a fence, Lena. I've been fencing my own inheritance and never knew whose name was on the deed."

Behind me, quiet: "What did she say at the end? Your mother. After the brand."

I tried to remember. The memory had already begun to settle, to blur at the edges the way borrowed things do — except this one wasn't borrowed. This one was mine, dug up out of my own ground, and still it slipped. But the last part held. The last part held like a hook holds.

"She said, *'If a girl comes asking, with light under her skin — run, Coral. Run, and don't look back, and whatever you do, don't love her.'*"

The silence in the room changed shape.

"She described me," Lena said at last, very carefully.

"She described a girl with light under her skin. If the shoe fits."

"Coral—"

"Don't." I turned around. My wrist burned under the linen, steady and patient, a coal that had waited twenty years and could evidently wait twenty more. "Don't say anything kind. Don't say anything true. Because I have just learned that I am a locked door that an entire vanished country built out of my bones, and that the first instruction attached to me — the *dying wish* of the only person who ever kept me safe — was to run from you." I swallowed. "And the truly unforgivable part, the part keeping me up past midnight doing inventory, is that I met you three days ago and I already don't want to."

Lena set down her cup. She rose, unhurried, and for a moment I thought she would touch the brand again, or my face, or some other part of me that wouldn't survive it.

Instead she picked up the black disc, corded it, and held it out to me.

"Then we have a problem," she said, "because I was given instructions too. And mine say to bring you home."

Below us, faint through the floorboards, the market bell rang — and beneath its ordinary note, so faint I might have imagined it, something on the far side of the ridge answered.
