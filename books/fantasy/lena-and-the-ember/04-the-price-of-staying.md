## 4. The Price of Staying

Lena had been in the Hollow for six days, and she had learned three things.

First: the valley smelled like woodsmoke and wet stone, and after a while you stopped noticing, the way you stopped noticing your own heartbeat. Second: nobody here asked where you came from, which was its own kind of courtesy and its own kind of wound. Third: Coral Crane smiled with his whole face when he thought no one was watching, and it was going to be the death of her.

She sat on the low wall outside the market square, ostensibly mending a sock, actually watching him work. He moved through the dusk crowd like water finding its level — a hand brushed here, a shoulder leaned there, a laugh shared just long enough. The travelers never felt it. They walked away lighter and poorer and grateful, the way people always were after Coral took something from them they'd have paid to lose.

*Thief,* she thought, not unkindly. *Heretic. Vessel of the divine fire. Boy who can't keep his elbows off other people's grief.*

The doctrine was clear. The ember-bearer was to be found, claimed, and delivered to the temple at Cindral Hollow's edge — no, that wasn't right, the temple beyond the ridge — so that the gods could take back what their country had hidden in his blood. She had recited the words every morning since she was nine years old. *What was given in secret shall be returned in glory.* Clean words. Simple ones.

Then she'd arrived, and the ember-bearer had turned out to be a young man with ink-stained fingers who cheated at cards and fed stray cats behind the tannery, and the words had stopped being simple somewhere between her first look at him and her second.

A commotion near the well broke her watching. A woman — a pilgrim, one of the winter arrivals, a merchant's widow from the coast — stood clutching the stone rim with both hands, and she was *crying*, and the sound she made was not grief exactly. It was recognition.

"I remember," the widow kept saying. "I remember my daughter's name. Why do I remember my daughter's name?"

Lena set down the sock. Around the square, others were stopping too. An old man by the bakery had gone pale as flour, staring at nothing, mouthing syllables. A boy dropped a basket of apples and didn't stoop for them; he was gripping his own head like a man holding a door shut against wind.

Memories bleeding back. Lena had seen this once before, in a border town where the gods' presence ran strong — stolen things returning to their owners like homing birds. But here? In the Hollow, where magic could not follow?

Here, where magic apparently no longer cared about the rules.

Coral appeared at the widow's elbow before anyone else thought to move. Lena watched him take the woman's hands, watch him murmur something too quiet to catch, watch the widow's breathing slow. He did not steal from her. Lena was almost sure of it. His thumb pressed the inside of her wrist — the way he touched people when he worked — but nothing left her. If anything, something seemed to be *pouring into* him, and his face went carefully blank, the blankness of a man swallowing something large.

He looked up then, across the square, and found Lena watching him. For one unguarded moment he looked terrified. Then he smiled at her — the whole-face smile, the dangerous one — and mouthed: *Not me.*

*Liar,* she mouthed back, and his smile went crooked, and she had to look down at her sock before her face betrayed anything the doctrine would disapprove of.

* * *

The elders convened that night in the meetinghouse, and by Hollow custom any resident could attend, so Lena stood at the back among the farmers and fence-menders and listened to a valley decide a man's fate.

There were eleven elders. Maren Ostwick presided, a broad woman with a voice like a cart axle and a reputation, Lena had gathered, for fairness of the ruthless variety.

"Third bleeding this week," Maren said, without preamble. "Tomas's boy remembered his mother's funeral. The Kettering woman remembered why she came here, which if you recall is precisely what she paid good coin to forget. And today the widow Aldous stood in the square weeping over a daughter half the valley has heard about whether they wanted to or not." She let that sit. "The Hollow holds because the Hollow is silent. No magic in. No magic out. That silence is fraying, and it began fraying when *he* started touching people."

"He's always touched people," someone called out. "That's his trade."

"It's different now and everyone knows it's different now."

Lena found Coral near the front, sitting alone on a bench, hands folded, wearing the expression of a man attending a reading of his own will. He caught her eye and raised his eyebrows in a way that said, *Well, this is cozy.*

"The priestess," Maren said, and heads turned toward Lena. "You've told us what you are. Church of the Reawakened Gods. You've told us there's a fire sleeping in Coral Crane that the old gods want back, and that its waking is why our silence is cracking." She folded her arms. "So I'll put it plainly, girl. Is it true?"

Every head turned. Lena felt the weight of the question settle onto her shoulders like a yoke, familiar as her own name.

Doctrine said: *yes.* Doctrine said: *the vessel must be surrendered, willingly or otherwise, for the good of all mortal things.* Doctrine had carried her across three countries and through the ridge passes in winter. Doctrine had never once asked her how she felt about Coral Crane's crooked smile, and doctrine, she suspected, would be very disappointed to learn.

"It's true," she said. "The ember wakes because the gods wake. It isn't his doing. He didn't ask for it any more than the widow asked to remember her daughter."

"That's not an answer to my question."

"It's the only honest one I have."

Maren studied her a long moment, the way one studies a horse for lameness. Then she turned back to the room. "Here's what I see. We can wait for the fire to finish waking and watch this valley come apart memory by memory until there's nothing left to protect. Or we can give the church what it wants, close the wound, and go back to being forgotten in peace." She looked around the circle. "I call the vote. All in favor of surrendering Coral Crane to the priestess and her god."

Hands rose. One by one by one. Lena counted nine before she made herself stop counting.

Coral raised his own hand.

The room went quiet. Maren stared at him. "You don't get a vote, boy."

"Seems rude not to," Coral said. His voice was light. His knuckles, Lena noticed, were white around the edge of the bench. "For the record. I'm in favor. Anything that keeps the widow Aldous from crying in the square, I'm in favor of." He stood, unhurried, and gave the elders a small bow. "When do you need me?"

"Tomorrow," Maren said, and something in her gruff face flickered — regret, maybe, or its poor cousin. "Dawn. The priestess takes you past the ridge."

"Wonderful." He smiled at the room, the whole-face smile, deployed now like a weapon or a bandage, Lena couldn't tell which. "I've always wanted to see a temple. Do they serve refreshments?"

Nobody laughed. He hadn't expected them to. He walked up the aisle past Lena without looking at her, and she caught, just for a moment, the smell of woodsmoke and something underneath it, faint and warm, like a banked fire — like an ember — and her heart did a thing that doctrine would absolutely not have approved of.

* * *

She found him afterward on the roof of the tannery, because of course he was on the roof of the tannery, feeding the stray cats like a man with nothing whatsoever scheduled at dawn.

"You voted for yourself," she said, climbing up beside him. A cat relocated to her lap with the entitlement of minor royalty.

"Seemed polite." He scratched a gray tom behind the ears. "Also accurate. Nine hands versus ten. Democracy's beautiful when it works."

"You're not afraid."

"Oh, I'm *terrified*." He said it cheerfully. "But fear's a private matter, and the whole valley was watching. Besides—" he glanced sideways at her, and the humor in his face thinned into something older and wearier, "—if giving me up fixes the bleeding, then it's the right trade. I've spent my life taking memories from people, Lena. I know what things cost. This is the first time I've ever been on the invoice."

She should have said something doctrinal. Something about glory, about the gods' mercy, about how the temple would care for the vessel. The words were all there, worn smooth as river stones from years of use. Instead she heard herself say:

"What if I told you there might be another way?"

His head turned slowly. "Another way."

"There are stories. Old ones. Heretical ones." She picked at the cat's fur, not looking at him. "About a door. About a country that hid the fire so thoroughly that even the gods lost the address. If the ember were sealed away — truly sealed, freely given, not taken — the waking would stop. The valley would hold." She finally met his eyes. "It would mean running. It would mean the church hunting us both. It would mean everything I've sworn my life to, traded for a maybe."

The cats had gone still. Below them the Hollow lay dark and quiet, pretending, as it always had, that nothing slept under its stones.

"Lena," Coral said gently, "you're a priestess. It's dawn tomorrow. What are you saying?"

She opened her mouth. Closed it. Somewhere beyond the ridge, faint as a struck match, she felt the gods stir — patient, vast, and hungry — and understood with terrible clarity that whatever she said next would decide not just his fate, but hers.

"I'm saying," she said, "that I don't think we should wait for dawn."

Coral stared at her. Then, slowly, that smile spread across his whole face — and for the first time, Lena saw it for what it truly was: not armor, not performance, but relief.

"Well," he said, standing and offering her his hand, "I've always wanted to be a heretic."
