## 5. Running Toward the Map

Coral had stolen the memory of a man who once outran a forest fire, and he thought of it now, running through the dark with Lena's hand locked around his wrist — right over the brand, as if she could hold him in place by it.

The fire-runner's memory was useless, actually. All he'd kept from it was the smell: pitch and terror and a strange exhilaration, like the world daring you to keep going. Coral had that smell in his nose now, except there was no fire. There was only the Hollow behind them, its lanterns going out one by one along the market road, and ahead of them a darkness that didn't belong to any map he'd ever seen.

"They'll vote at midnight," Lena said, not even breathing hard. She ran like someone who had been running her whole life and found it restful. "The elders. They'll give you to the church."

"I know. I was there for the argument. I stole the memory of it while they were still having it." He ducked under a low branch. "Old Maren voted against. She cried. I took that memory too, so she wouldn't have to carry it home."

"That's either very kind or very grim."

"It's both. Most things in the Hollow are both."

They came out of the trees above the river road, and Coral stopped, because stopping was a habit of his — the thief's instinct, always checking what was behind him. Behind him: nothing yet. Ahead of him: everything he'd spent twenty-six years avoiding.

"Where are we going?" he asked. "You're the one with the mission. I'm just the cargo."

Lena turned to look at him. Even in the dark, even exhausted, she had that quality that had ruined him from the first moment — the quality of being *seen*, which in the Hollow was practically an assault. Everyone else looked through him. She looked *at* him, and it was unbearable, and he would have walked into the sea if she asked.

"The Cartographer of Ash," she said. "Three days north, past the salt flats. If anyone knows where Vael stood, where the Door is — it's him."

"And why does a priestess need a door?"

"Because my gods are hungry," she said simply, "and I would rather feed them a lie than feed them you."

He hadn't expected honesty. Honesty was the one currency he never dealt in; it made his hands itch. But she gave it away freely, the way rich people sometimes did, without understanding what it cost everyone else to hear.

* * *

They walked the river road by day and slept in culverts by night, and somewhere in the second day Coral noticed something alarming, which was that he was enjoying himself.

This was not supposed to happen. Fleeing for your life was supposed to be grim business, all blisters and dread. Instead Lena had begun narrating their journey like a story she was telling herself for courage, assigning names to landmarks — *that's the Hill Where We Argued About Rations, that's the Creek Where Coral Complained About His Boots* — and Coral, who had spent his life unmaking stories out of people's heads, discovered he liked being inside one.

"You complain about your boots a lot," she observed.

"They're stolen boots. They don't fit my conscience."

"Your conscience has feet?"

"My conscience has excellent feet. It walks all over me."

She laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, and then she looked almost embarrassed by it, the way a priestess might be embarrassed to find she still owned a body. Coral filed the sound away carefully, in the place where he kept things he couldn't sell and wouldn't trade: his mother's voice, the shape of the brand under his sleeve, and now this.

By the third morning they could see the salt flats, white and blinding, stretching to a horizon that shimmered like a heat-mirage of itself. And it was there, on the flats' edge, at the last inn before the waste, that Coral saw Sable.

His fence. His employer. The closest thing the Hollow had given him to family, which should have warned him.

Sable sat in the inn's common room eating eggs with terrible elegance, as though the eggs had personally wronged him and he was making them pay. He was a narrow man dressed in expensive gray, with rings on every finger and a smile he rented out by the hour.

"Coral," he said warmly. "And the priestess. Sit. Eat. You look like a rumor of yourselves."

"We can't stay," Coral said.

"Of course not. I only wanted to see you off." Sable dabbed his mouth. "I've been so worried. The elders sent riders, you know. And the church sent others. There's quite a price on you now — did you know? Not a bounty exactly. More of a... tithe. They're calling you the Ember-Bearer in the low country. There are hymns."

"There are *hymns?*"

"Two. One of them rhymes." Sable smiled his rented smile. "I told them nothing, of course. I would never. Now — which way are you going? North, I'd guess. Toward the Cartographer. Everyone always goes toward the Cartographer eventually. It's the maps. People can't bear to be lost."

Coral felt the trap close around the conversation before he understood it was a trap. That was Sable's gift: he made betrayal feel like small talk.

Lena stood. "We should go."

"Go, go," Sable agreed pleasantly. "North. Though mind the flats — the church crossed yesterday, ahead of you. Six of them. Very polite men. They asked me the same question you just answered."

Coral went cold. "I didn't answer anything."

"No," Sable said, and for one flickering moment the rented smile fell away and something older and sadder looked out of his face. "No, you didn't. I'm sorry, boy. Truly. But the valley is cracking, and when the sanctuary goes, I go with it. A fence needs walls. So I sold them your road." He sighed. "Not your name. I kept back your name. Consider it a severance."

* * *

They ran again, out onto the salt flats with the sun hammering down, six polite men somewhere behind them, and no road left to take because every road was now a confession.

"We cut west," Coral gasped. "There's nothing west."

"There's nothing anywhere. That's rather the point of Vael."

"West," he insisted, "there's a dry wash. Old riverbed. It runs north under the flats — the salt crust holds, mostly. My mother used it. Smugglers' route."

Lena stared at him. "Your mother smuggled?"

"My mother hid things for a living. Turns out the biggest thing she ever hid was me." He heard his own voice crack on it and hated that too. "Come on."

The wash was a wound in the white earth, walled with cracked clay, and they dropped into it and moved fast and low, and for a while neither spoke. Then, in the shadow of an overhang, they stopped to breathe, and Lena did something she had not done before: she sat down next to him, shoulder to shoulder, close enough that he could feel her exhaustion as a kind of warmth.

"He was going to sell you," she said quietly. "The man who fed you."

"He fed me because I was profitable. It's fine. I knew what he was. I just—" Coral worked his jaw. "I thought I'd have more warning. When the world started taking things from me, I thought I'd get to steal them back first."

"You can't steal your own memories, Coral."

"I know. Believe me, I've tried. There's a version of me in my head keeping the good ones on a high shelf, and he doesn't negotiate."

That got another laugh out of her, smaller this time, frayed at the edges. Then she reached over and, with great deliberateness, took his hand — the branded one — and held it in both of hers, the way you'd hold something rescued from a fire.

"When we find the Cartographer," she said, "and we learn what the Door costs — whatever it costs — I want you to know that I will be arguing with heaven about it. Loudly. In writing, if necessary."

"Heaven doesn't read."

"Heaven is about to learn."

And Coral, who had built a whole life out of never trusting anyone further than a pickpocket's reach, looked at her dirt-streaked face and her blistered hands and her absolutely unreasonable certainty, and felt the last wall inside himself come down — not dramatically, not all at once, but the way salt dissolves: gradually, invisibly, completely.

"All right," he said. "West and north. Under the flats. And Lena — when this is over, whatever's left of me, remind me to teach you how to steal. Properly. You're clearly a natural."

"I am a priestess."

"You broke your oaths before breakfast."

"I *renegotiated* my oaths before breakfast. There's a difference, and heaven knows it."

They went on through the clay dark, hand in hand, two fugitives inventing a road where no road was drawn. And it was nearly dusk when the wash curved and opened without warning into a cavern beneath the salt, vast and vaulted, its ceiling glittering with crystal seams — and standing in the middle of it, waiting for them beside a lantern that burned ash-gray instead of gold, was a figure with a traveler's pack and eyes like unfinished maps.

"You're late," said the Cartographer of Ash. "I've had your route drawn since Tuesday."
