## 6. The Country That No Longer Exists

The Cartographer of Ash turned out to be a small woman with ink-stained fingers and the manner of someone who had been interrupted mid-thought for roughly three hundred years.

"Sit," she said, before either of them had spoken. "You're dripping salt water on my floor, and the floor is a map of a coastline that no longer exists, so technically you're flooding a country. Again."

Coral looked down. The cavern floor was indeed inlaid with lines of crushed lapis and silver — bays, harbors, a long curling peninsula shaped like a sleeping fox. Water from their clothes pooled in what he now understood to be somebody's ancestral fishing village.

"Sorry," Lena said, and stepped neatly onto a mountain range.

"Don't apologize to me. Apologize to Vael." The Cartographer said it lightly, the way you'd joke about a dead relative, and then didn't laugh, and the joke curdled into something else entirely.

They sat on crates that smelled of cedar and old varnish. The lantern's ash-gray flame threw no shadows, which Coral found deeply unsettling; he had spent his whole life reading people by their shadows, and this woman cast none, which meant either she was lying about everything or she was telling him more truth than he wanted.

"You're the one who keeps Vael's maps," Lena said.

"I'm the one who *is* Vael's maps," the Cartographer corrected. "When they unmade the country, someone had to remember where it had been. Every road, every river, every door. They couldn't erase me — I was already mostly erasure by trade." She spread her hands. Ink crawled along her knuckles like veins. "So I became the margin notes."

Coral cleared his throat. "And the Door of Ash?"

The Cartographer looked at him for a long moment. Not at his face — at his wrist, where the brand sat under his sleeve like a coal that had never quite cooled. Something in her expression softened, the way you look at a house you once lived in.

"The Door opens only where Vael stood," she said. "Not near it. Not where it used to be, approximately, give or take a valley. *Where it stood.* And here is your problem, ember-bearer: Vael doesn't stand anywhere anymore. It wasn't destroyed. Destroyed things leave ruins, and ruins leave memories, and memories can be walked to." She tapped the lantern. "Vael was *unmade*. Taken out of the world so completely that even the ground forgot it had ever been ground. To reach the place where it stood, you have to walk through forgetting itself."

"I've lived in forgetting my whole life," Coral said. "It's not so bad. There's a good market."

"This isn't the Hollow's polite little amnesia," the Cartographer said. "This is the original article. The gods' own recipe. Every step you take toward that door will take something from you. One memory per step. You don't get to choose which. The road chooses."

The silence afterward had weight. Somewhere above them, salt shifted and settled, patient as snowfall.

"How many steps?" Lena asked quietly.

"To the door? Four hundred and eleven," said the Cartographer. "I counted. Counting is most of what I do."

Coral did the arithmetic the way you do arithmetic when you already know you hate the answer. He had perhaps ten thousand memories, if you counted generously — every dusk in the market, every stolen stranger's wedding day, every time Sable had cheated him at cards and he'd pretended not to notice. Four hundred and eleven out of ten thousand sounded survivable. It sounded almost reasonable.

Then he thought about which four hundred and eleven the road might pick.

* * *

They spent the night in the cavern, because the Cartographer refused to discuss logistics after sundown ("Maps are morning work. Everything looks possible at dawn and impossible at noon, and I prefer to lie to myself in the correct order.").

She fed them stew that tasted faintly of pencil shavings and was somehow the best thing Coral had eaten in years. Lena asked questions — careful, priestess-shaped questions about wards and thresholds — and the Cartographer answered them while drawing, always drawing, her pen whispering across paper in a language of contour lines.

Coral watched her hands. Watching hands was an old habit; hands told you what faces were paid to hide.

"You're afraid of something," he said, when Lena went to refill the bowls. "And it isn't the gods."

The pen paused. "Observant. That'll be the thief in you."

"It's the survivor in me. The thief just takes the credit."

The Cartographer set down her pen and studied him with those unfinished-map eyes. "You want to know what frightens me? It's not that the road will take four hundred and eleven memories from you. It's that the road takes them *in order of how much they hold you together*." She turned back to her map. "Forgetting hurts. But being forgotten *by yourself* — walking forward while the person who started the walk comes apart behind you — that's the true toll. Ask me how I know."

"Is that why you cast no shadow?"

"That's why I cast no shadow," she agreed pleasantly. "I gave mine to a bridge in 1213. Long story. The bridge is very happy."

He laughed before he could stop himself, and she smiled, and for a moment the cavern felt less like a tomb for a country and more like a kitchen.

Then she said, gently, "Ask me the other question. The one you've been chewing since I mentioned the toll."

Coral looked at his hands. Thief's hands, memory-thief's hands, hands that had lifted a thousand pasts out of a thousand pockets without ever once finding one worth keeping.

"If each step costs a memory," he said, "and we walk there together — does it take hers too? The ones I have of *her*?"

Because that was the thing he hadn't said aloud, the thing sitting in his chest like a swallowed coal: somewhere between the wash and the betrayal and the running, the memories of Lena had stopped being inventory and started being treasure. Her face when she lied about oaths. Her hand in his in the clay dark. The way she'd said *I renegotiated* as though heaven were a merchant who could be haggled with. He had spent his life selling other people's pasts, and now he was hoarding his own, and the irony was so perfect it felt like punishment.

"They're the deepest ones you have," the Cartographer said. "Roads go for the deep ones last. But they go for them, Coral. Nothing walks that road and arrives whole." She picked up her pen again. "The question you should be asking is not whether you'll lose her. It's whether losing her is a price, or a gift."

"What kind of gift is that?"

"The kind the gods are terrified of," she said. "That's rather the point of the door."

* * *

Dawn came down through the crystal seams in shafts of pale gold, and the Cartographer unrolled the final map on the floor of the cavern — the flooded coastline, the sleeping-fox peninsula, and beyond its tip, a blank space no larger than a coin, drawn with a circle of ash.

"There," she said. "Where Vael stood. As close as ink can get."

Lena crouched over it, tracing the route with one finger. "Four hundred and eleven steps."

"Four hundred and twelve, if you count the threshold, and I do, because I'm thorough and slightly cruel." The Cartographer rolled up the map and pressed it into Coral's hands along with a stoppered vial of gray light. "Ash-light. When the road starts taking things, burn a drop and look at what you still have. It helps. Sometimes."

"And what happens when we open the door?" Lena asked.

"Then that's chapter eight's problem," said the Cartographer, and when they stared at her, she shrugged. "Three hundred years of solitude. You develop narrative habits. Go on. The ridge pass closes by midmorning, and the gods' servants travel faster than regret."

At the mouth of the cavern, Coral stopped. Turned back.

"Why help us?" he asked. "You said it yourself — you're the margin notes. What do you get?"

The Cartographer looked at him for a long time, and her unfinished eyes finished, briefly, into something ancient and tired and kind.

"A country should be visited," she said. "Even a dead one. Especially a dead one. Nobody has walked to Vael since Vael stopped existing, and I have been *so lonely*, ember-bearer." She waved them off with an ink-stained hand. "Now go. Walk carefully. Lose beautifully."

They climbed out of the salt into morning light, and the map in Coral's hands weighed nothing at all, and the road ahead — the road that would eat him one memory at a time — stretched out toward a country that no longer existed, where a door waited that only a man made of fire could open.

Lena took his hand.

"Four hundred and twelve steps," she said.

"Give or take a memory," said Coral, and stepped onto the road.
