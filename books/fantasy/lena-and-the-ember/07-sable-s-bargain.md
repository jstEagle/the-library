## 7. Sable's Bargain

Lena had imagined many versions of her own exile. In the seminary at Vespergate, where novices were encouraged to meditate on martyrdom the way other children practiced skipping stones, she had pictured fire, mostly. Or lions, though the old gods kept no lions. She had never once imagined that her fall from grace would begin in a roadside inn called The Drowned Bell, over a plate of gray stew, while a man with silver teeth complimented her posture.

"You sit like a bishop," Sable said, settling into the chair across from them without being invited. "Spine straight. Hands folded. Very ecclesiastical."

Coral's hand found hers under the table — a reflex now, three days old and already worn smooth as a river stone. Lena did not look up from her stew. Looking up would confirm what she already knew: that the road they'd taken to avoid the church's hunters had delivered them directly into the one set of hands in the Hollow that could not be outrun.

"Sable," Coral said. His voice was careful, the way a man speaks to a dog he isn't sure is friendly. "I thought you were in Marrowdown."

"I was. Then I was paid an extraordinary sum to be elsewhere." Sable smiled, and his teeth caught the lamplight like fish scales. "You've become valuable, little thief. Do you know what the new church pays for a rumor? A *confirmed* rumor? I could buy this inn. I could buy the town the inn is ashamed of."

Lena finally lifted her gaze. Up close, Sable was worse than his reputation suggested — neat where she'd expected squalor, courteous where she'd expected menace. He wore a merchant's gray and a smile that had been oiled daily for years. He looked like a man who had made peace with every ugly thing inside himself and arranged them all comfortably, like furniture.

"Then take your rumor," she said. "We'll be gone before you finish counting."

"Oh, I don't want you gone." He laced his fingers together on the table, a gesture of such terrible patience that Lena's stomach turned. "Gone men don't fetch prices. And besides—" his eyes slid to Coral, warm as a hand on a shoulder, cold as everything else about him— "I have a better offer. For the lady."

* * *

The offer came out of him the way water comes out of a cracked jug: slowly, and all over everything.

The church of the reawakened gods — they were calling it the Church of the Answered Voice now, which Lena thought sounded less like a faith and more like a complaint department — wanted the ember-bearer. They wanted him alive, unspoiled, and *willing*, because willing vessels burned brighter. Sable had been offered enough gold to drown in for Coral's location. But gold, Sable explained, spreading his hands apologetically, was only useful in the world. And Sable had lately begun to suspect the world might not last.

"So here is my bargain," he said. "You, priestess. You give me something worth more than gold. You give me your vows."

Lena blinked. "My vows."

"Renounce them. Publicly. Here, tonight, in front of these good people—" he gestured at the dozen travelers hunched over their suppers, all of whom were pretending very hard not to listen— "and in front of my associate, who will carry word back to the church. A priestess of the Answered Voice, casting off her god in a common room. Do you know what that does to them? Their whole story is that the gods are irresistible. That everyone who hears the Voice comes running. A priestess who says *no thank you*?" He shook his head with genuine admiration. "It's worth ten of your ember-bearer. It's worth a hundred. Doubt spreads faster than fire."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow morning I sell Coral's name, his face, and the road he's traveling, and by noon there will be forty of the faithful between you and the border of Vael." Sable picked up Coral's untouched bread, considered it, and set it down again. "No hurry. Take your time. Stew's getting cold, and I hear it's terrible."

* * *

They took the room upstairs because Coral insisted on arguing somewhere with a door.

"You can't," he said, the moment the latch fell. "Lena. You can't do what he's asking."

"I haven't decided anything."

"You've decided. You get that look." He paced the narrow room, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, a man wearing a groove into his own panic. "Your vows aren't — they're not words, they're *you*. You told me. You told me on the ridge, when I asked why you'd come all this way for someone you didn't know. You said the gods gave you a purpose and you'd rather die crooked than live straight without one."

"I said that?" Lena sat on the bed's edge. The mattress sighed under her like an old woman. "That's very dramatic. I must have been tired."

"Don't joke."

"I'm always joking. It's how I pray." She watched him stop pacing, watch the fear move through him — fear for himself, yes, but underneath it, worse, fear *for her*, and wasn't that the cruelest thing, that he had learned to carry her safety the way she carried his. "Coral. Sit down."

He sat. He looked, in the lamplight, very young and very stubborn, the two qualities she suspected had kept the ember hidden in his bloodline for a thousand years. Hiding places needed to be stubborn.

"If I refuse," she said quietly, "you're taken. And if you're taken unwillingly, the gods burn the Hollow to get to you, and everyone you ever stole a memory for dies knowing exactly why. If I accept, I lose—" she searched for the shape of it and found the shape enormous and formless, like trying to hold water— "everything. My order. My prayers. The certainty that when I speak, someone holy is listening."

"That sounds like everything."

"It is everything." She reached across and took his wrist, the one with the faded brand beneath the skin, the mark of Vael's long patience. "But do you know what I've figured out, these three days on the road with you? Everything was getting heavy anyway. I've been carrying my faith like a full pack since I was six years old, and I kept telling myself the ache was holiness." She laughed, and it came out wet. "Maybe love weighs less. Maybe it's supposed to."

"That's not how bargains work," Coral said hoarsely. "He'll take your renunciation and still sell you both. Men like Sable don't honor terms. They just collect."

"Then I'll make the bargain worth keeping." Lena stood, and something settled in her chest — not peace, nothing so gentle, but a kind of alignment, like a bone going back into its socket. "If I'm going to lose my god, I'll do it standing up. And if Sable breaks his word, then everyone in that common room will see it break. Doubt spreads faster than fire, remember? Let's give him a taste of his own doctrine."

* * *

Downstairs, the common room had not emptied. Word traveled in the Hollow the way water traveled downhill — inevitably, and pooling in the lowest places. Every table was occupied now. Sable had bought them all drinks, which Lena supposed was the closest a fence came to consecration.

She walked to the center of the room and stood in the space between the hearth and the door, and she felt, absurdly, the way she had felt at her ordination: watched, small, and certain.

"My name is Lena of Vespergate," she said, and her voice carried better than she expected, the way voices do when they stop caring whether they shake. "I am — I *was* — a priestess of the Answered Voice. I swore four oaths. To hear. To obey. To deliver. To remember nothing of myself but the god who filled me."

Sable leaned back against the bar, arms folded, wearing the expression of a man watching his investment ripen.

"I renounce them," Lena said. "All four. Not because the gods are false — they're real, and that's worse — but because they asked me to hand them a man I lo—" the word caught; she let it catch; she let the whole room hear it catch— "a man I love. And any god who wants love delivered like a debt collector delivers notices is a god I can refuse. So I refuse. Hear me: I am no one's vessel. I belong to my own road now."

Silence. Then, from a corner table, an old woman raised her cup an inch off the wood — barely, deliberately — and set it down again. One by one, others did the same. Not applause. Something older than applause. Lena felt it land in her chest like a second heartbeat.

Sable pushed off the bar, smiling, and Lena saw the smile for what it was: a ledger closing.

"A lovely performance," he said. "Truly. The church will weep. Now, if we're concluded—"

"We're not," said Coral.

He had come down the stairs without her noticing, and he crossed the room and stood beside her, and when he spoke it was to the whole room, loud, which she would learn later was the first time in his life he had ever been loud on purpose.

"Sable. You wanted to sell me. Here's my counteroffer, free of charge." He held up his wrist, brand showing pale in the lamplight. "Everyone in this room now knows what I am. Forty witnesses. You can't sell a secret the whole valley already owns. Your gold just turned to ash, friend — and ash, as it happens, is the one thing I'm carrying plenty of."

For the first time since Lena had met him, Sable looked genuinely surprised. Then, horribly, delighted.

"Oh," he said softly, rubbing his silver teeth with his tongue. "Oh, you clever little thief. You think that protects you." He straightened his cuffs. "It doesn't matter who knows. The church doesn't need secrecy. It needs *direction*." He tipped an imaginary hat to Lena. "Keep your vows or lose them, priestess, it's all the same to me now. Northwest road. Three days. The Door of Ash opens where Vael stood — and so, conveniently, does everything the gods are waiting behind."

He paused at the door, and glanced back, and for one unguarded moment there was something almost wistful in the silver-toothed face.

"For what it's worth," Sable said, "I hope you make it. I've sold a great many things in my life. It would be nice, just once, to sell nothing at all."

The door swung shut behind him. The common room exhaled.

Lena turned to Coral, whose bravado was already draining out of him like color from a sunset, leaving behind the frightened man who had never been loud before and didn't know how to be loud twice.

"Northwest," he said. "Three days."

"Yes."

"He knows exactly where we're going. He'll tell them. He *did* tell them."

"Probably." Lena picked up her pack from beside the hearth and slung it over her shoulder, and found, testing the weight of it, that it truly did feel lighter than it had that morning. "Then we'd best not be late. I'd hate to keep the gods waiting — after all, I've got a reputation now."

Outside, the night had gone strange and bright, and far to the northwest, past the ridge, past the edge of every map, something was glowing like a banked fire deciding whether to wake.
