## 8. Where the Gods Wait

The border of Vael did not look like a border. It looked like a mistake in the world — a line of gray grass that simply stopped being green, as if someone had painted the valley and run out of color at exactly this point. Beyond it, the land was not empty. That was the worst part. It was full of things that had no names, because names had been unmade along with everything else.

Coral stood at the edge of it with Lena's hand in his and felt the ember in his chest turn over like a sleeper disturbed by light.

"It's watching us," he said.

"Of course it is," said the Cartographer, who had walked ahead of them for three days without once looking tired, which Coral should have found suspicious earlier. "It has been watching you since you were born. Longer, probably. Gods are patient in the way that avalanches are patient."

"I wish you'd stop saying things like that."

"I wish you'd stop asking questions whose answers frighten you."

Lena squeezed his hand. She had not let go since Sable's camp, since she had stood in front of a crowd of smugglers and broken her vows aloud, one by one, like snapping kindling. Her hands were still shaking sometimes. But her grip was steady now, and Coral had learned to read the difference.

"We could still go around," he offered, though he knew there was no around. There was never an around with Vael. The Cartographer's maps showed the erased country as a blank space shaped vaguely like a held breath, and every road they might have taken curved gently, insistently, back toward this gray line of grass.

"There is no around," the Cartographer said mildly, reading his mind or perhaps just his face, which amounted to the same thing with old men who had seen too much. "There is only through."

* * *

They made camp that night on the last green ground, and Coral dreamed of his mother.

He had not dreamed of her in years. The Hollow took dreams first, before memories — everyone knew that. Dreams were the softest thing a person carried, the easiest to lift. He had stolen thousands of them from travelers, folded them into glass vials and sold them to people who wanted to sleep without their pasts pressing on them. He knew exactly what a dream weighed when it left a body.

But this one stayed. In it, his mother sat by a fire he could not feel, holding his wrist where the brand was, tracing its shape with a thumb gone rough from work. *You are the hiding place,* she had told him once, when he was small enough to believe that hiding places were safe rather than hunted. *A country put itself inside you so that it could not be burned out of the world. Be small, my love. Be quiet. Be nothing, and you will be everything.*

He woke with the ember burning behind his ribs like a swallowed coal, and the sky above the gray grass was full of light.

Not dawn. Dawn came gradually, apologetically. This light arrived all at once, standing up out of the horizon like figures rising from chairs, and Coral understood, in the wordless way one understands falling, that the gods had decided to stop being subtle.

"They're here," Lena breathed beside him. She was already on her feet, and though she had renounced her oaths, her body still moved like a priestess's — spine straight, chin lifted, the posture of someone accustomed to addressing the divine and expecting to be answered.

"Yes," said the Cartographer. "Here we are."

Coral turned. "*We?*"

* * *

The Cartographer stood at the edge of the firelight, and the firelight went through him instead of around him, and Coral saw — finally, stupidly late — what he should have seen in the first dusty shop in the foothills, among the maps of countries that had never existed and one map of a country that had stopped.

The old man's outline blurred. His edges softened into something vast, something that wore the shape of an elderly cartographer the way a king might wear a beggar's coat: deliberately, and for reasons of his own.

"You," Coral said. The word came out very small.

"Me." The Cartographer smiled, and it was a kind smile, which somehow made it worse. "You've been traveling with me for six days, boy. You slept eight feet from me while I kept watch. Did you truly think a mortal could walk the border of Vael without going mad?"

"What are you?"

"Older than your valley. Older than your gods' hunger, if we're honest, though they hate hearing that." He stepped forward, and where his feet touched the gray grass, it greened — briefly, reluctantly, like grass remembering an old instruction. "I was one of them, once. Before Vael was unmade. I helped draw the lines that erased it. I helped hide the ember." His eyes, mild and brown and ancient, settled on Coral's chest as though he could see straight through skin and bone to the coal sleeping there. "And then I did something none of them have ever forgiven. I chose to forget how to be worshipped."

"You *left.*"

"I retired," the god said, with the gentle pedantry of a man correcting a map label. "There is a difference. Leaving implies you can be followed. Retiring means you make yourself too small and too dull to bother with. I became an old man who draws maps. Do you know what I discovered?" He crouched by the fire, and for a moment he was only an old man again, warming his knuckles. "Mortality is the only honest religion. You wake up. Your back hurts. Someone makes you tea. Nothing is owed and nothing is demanded. I have been happier in seventy years of aching joints than in ten thousand years of answered prayers."

"And they let you just—"

"They don't *let* anything. That's rather the point of gods." He looked up, and his face went grave. "But they are coming for you now, Coral Crane, and they will offer you mercy, because that is the cruelest thing they carry. So listen to me carefully. Whatever they promise you — and they will promise beautifully — ask them what it costs. Ask twice. They are permitted to lie only by omission, and omission is their native tongue."

* * *

The light arrived at their camp as the stars were fading, and it resolved into shapes.

There were three of them, though calling them three was like calling an ocean wet. They stood at the border of the gray grass and did not cross it — could not, Coral realized, could not enter the last living inch of the world while the ember still hid him — and they were beautiful in the way that fires are beautiful, which is to say: from a distance, and briefly, and always with somewhere else in mind to burn.

The middle one spoke, and its voice was every voice Coral had ever heard saying grace, saying goodbye, saying *please.*

**Bearer.**

"That's not my name," Coral said. His voice shook. He was proud that it was only his voice.

**It is what you are.** The god's tone was gentle, terribly gentle, the gentleness of snowfall on a roof about to collapse. **You have carried our fire faithfully for thirty years without knowing it. We do not blame you. Blame requires that a thing understand its burden, and you did not. But the hiding is over, little vessel. The country that hid itself in you is needed again — or rather, it is needed to stay hidden, permanently, and only you can accomplish this.**

"By giving you the ember."

**Willingly given,** the god said, and the other two inclined their heads, and the whole horizon seemed to lean in. **Given freely, it becomes ours to guard forever, and the Door of Ash opens, and Vael is sealed beyond the world, and the hunger ends. Given unwillingly, we must take it, and taking shatters the vessel, and the vessel's home—"** the god's gaze slid south, toward the Hollow, toward the market stalls and the memory-vials and the ordinary people who had voted, weeks ago, to give him away, **"—burns. We would prefer not to burn it. We are offering you mercy.**"

"There it is," Coral said quietly. "Mercy."

**You doubt it.**

"I'm a thief. Doubting gifts is my profession." He stepped forward, to the very edge of the green, close enough that the god's light warmed his face like an open oven. "Let me ask you something. If I say yes — if I hand you the fire in my chest and open your precious door — what happens to *me?* Not my body. Me. The part that remembers his mother's hands. The part that knows what Lena's laugh sounds like when she's trying not to laugh. Does that survive?"

Silence. The particular silence of a god consulting the fine print.

**You would be remade,** the god said at last. **Elevated. You would sit beside us. You would never hunger, never fear, never lose anyone again, because nothing you loved could die while you watched over it.**

"And what would it cost?"

**Nothing worth naming.**

"Then name it anyway."

The god's light flickered — annoyance, Coral thought, or perhaps respect — and the truth came out the way truths come from gods: sideways, wrapped in glory.

**You would remember everything except what it felt like to need anything. That is all divinity is, bearer. It is the end of wanting. And wanting is the thread that ties a self together. Cut it, and what remains is luminous, and eternal, and perfectly, perfectly empty.**

Coral looked at Lena.

She was crying without any noise at all, the way she did everything important — quietly, completely, without asking to be witnessed. And he thought about the Hollow, about the vials on his shelf, about the hundreds of people who had paid him good coin to feel exactly this: luminous, and calm, and hollowed out. He had spent his whole life selling that emptiness and calling it peace. Now the gods were offering to pay *him*, and the price was the same as it had always been.

Everything.

"No," he said.

**Think—**

"I've thought. That's the problem with me; I do it constantly, it's exhausting." He turned to the Cartographer, who was watching with the expression of a teacher whose worst student has finally said something correct. "You chose this. Being small. Being dull. Aching knees and tea."

"The finest bargain I ever struck," the god said, "and I struck it with myself."

"Then show me the door," Coral said, and the ember in his chest flared so bright that all three gods recoiled a half-step, which was, he would reflect later, the single most satisfying moment of his entire criminal career. "Show me the Door of Ash. Because I'm not giving you the fire. I'm giving it to the door. And you can watch."

The middle god's light guttered. When it spoke again, the gentleness was gone, and what remained was older and colder and far more honest:

**Then come, bearer. And bring your priestess. The door does not open for thieves alone.**

* * *

They walked onto the gray grass as the sun rose behind them, three small figures casting long shadows across a country that did not exist, toward a door that waited at the center of the forgetting like a heart inside a chest.

Lena's hand found his. The Cartographer walked between them, humming something old.

And ahead, faint as breath on glass, the Door of Ash began — very slightly — to glow.
