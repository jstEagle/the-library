## 10. Someone Will Remember

The Hollow in spring smelled like wet stone and bread.

Lena stood at the market's edge with a basket she had no idea how to fill, watching Coral Crane argue with a woman over the price of turnips. He was good at it. He'd always been good at pretending to be exactly what he appeared — a thin, quick-eyed man with ink-stained fingers and a talent for making people believe they'd gotten the better end of a bargain. The valley believed it. The valley, which had spent a generation forgetting everything on purpose, now remembered him as nothing more than the memory-broker who had gone quiet after the winter of the burning sky.

That was the mercy of it, and the cruelty. The gods were sealed beyond the Door of Ash. The stolen memories had flooded home like water finding its level — every pilgrim who had ever sold a grief to Coral woke one morning weeping or laughing or simply *whole*, and the Hollow's elders had declared it a miracle of ordinary weather. Only two people in the world knew what it had actually cost.

One of them was standing in front of her, haggling over turnips, and did not know her at all.

"You're staring," said Sable beside her, arms crossed, silver rings catching the light. "You always stare. It's unbecoming for a woman who threw away heaven."

"I wasn't staring."

"You were doing the thing where your face goes soft and tragic, like a painting of a saint. Buy the man his turnips or marry him, but pick one, because the whole market is starting to gossip."

Lena exhaled through her nose. "He doesn't know me, Sable."

"No," Sable agreed, and for once her voice lost its knife-edge. "But he keeps selling you the good bread at half price and pretending he doesn't know why. Whatever he poured out of himself, some of it pooled somewhere he can't reach. Bodies remember what souls forget." She plucked an apple from Lena's basket, bit into it, and wandered off toward the fence posts, because Sable had never in her life ended a conversation gracefully.

Lena bought the turnips. Coral wrapped them without looking up, then did look up, and frowned the small frown she had learned to love across a hundred miles of ash and road.

"Have we met?" he asked. It was the first time he'd asked it plainly. Before, it had only been the pause before her name, the way his hand hesitated when he passed her change. "You have a face I should know. It's like a word on the tip of my tongue."

"Maybe in another life," Lena said, and smiled, and hated how practiced the smile had become.

"Huh," said Coral Crane, who had once been the last vessel of divine fire and was now a man with turnips and ink stains and a hollow where she used to live. "Well. Careful with those, they're from Marrow's field and Marrow's turnips are mostly spite."

* * *

She had taken the cottage at the edge of the ridge — the one with the crooked chimney that the elders rented cheap to anyone willing to fix it, which meant no one had lived there in eleven years. She fixed it. She learned which floorboards announced visitors and which ones kept secrets, and she learned the paths Coral walked when he couldn't sleep, because he still couldn't sleep; whatever else the ember had taken, it had left him his restlessness, like a coat returned with one pocket torn out.

On the third evening of spring she found him sitting on the low wall above the river, and instead of walking past as she had done forty times, she sat down beside him.

"This is my spot," he said.

"I know. You come here when the market's been too loud. You skip stones badly and blame the wind."

Coral turned to look at her fully. "I've never told anyone that."

"You don't have to tell me things, Coral. I already know most of them." She held up a hand before he could speak. "I know you hate the smell of cloves because your mother wore them. I know you taught yourself to read from a smuggler's ledger and so you still pronounce 'quay' wrong. I know you're left-handed but you write with your right because a fence named Sable told you buyers trusted right-handed ledgers."

The silence stretched long enough that the river filled it. When he finally spoke, his voice was careful, the way a man walks on ice.

"What are you?"

"Lena," she said. "Just Lena. Ex-priestess. Current tenant of the crooked-chimney cottage. Terrible gardener."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I'm allowed to give you yet." She pulled her cloak tighter against the river damp. "But I'll tell you a story, if you want. It's a long one. It has a valley that forgets, and a thief, and a priestess who came to arrest him and failed spectacularly. There's a cartographer who turns out to be much more than a cartographer. There's a betrayal, several betrayals actually, Sable features prominently—"

"Sable? My Sable?"

"Everyone's Sable. That's rather her problem." Lena looked out at the water. "And there's a door made of ash, and a fire hidden in a bloodline, and a choice at the end of it that I am not going to describe while you still have turnips to sell, because you'd drop them, and Marrow's turnips are mostly spite but they're also food."

Coral was quiet. Then, slowly, he laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, and something in Lena's chest cracked open in the old familiar way, grief and joy sharing a single wound.

"All right," he said. "Tell me the story."

* * *

She told it in pieces, the way you feed a fire that must not flare too fast.

She told him about dusk markets and travelers who came to the Hollow to lose their pasts, about a thief whose hands could lift a memory as lightly as a purse. She changed nothing, softened nothing — not the stealing, not the loneliness disguised as competence, not the boy who had learned that being forgotten was safer than being seen. Coral listened with his elbows on his knees and his chin in his hands, wincing at his own worst moments and laughing, unexpectedly, at the best ones.

"I sound insufferable," he said, after the part where he'd tried to steal her memory outside the chapel ruins and found, for the first time in his life, a locked door.

"You were insufferable. You called me 'priestess' like it was an insult."

"It is a little insulting."

"It grew on me." She watched the river carry a leaf past their feet. "Do you want to know the strange part? The part I still can't explain? Everyone in that valley, I could have taken anything from. But you — I never managed to take a single thing from you either. Not one memory. We were both locked doors, you and I. I think that's why the gods feared us together."

"Gods," Coral repeated, testing the word. In the weeks since the winter of the burning sky, the Hollow had decided the light on the ridge had been lightning. Lightning was easier. Lightning didn't ask anything of you. "In the story, am I brave?"

Lena thought about it honestly, because he deserved honesty more than he deserved comfort. "You were terrified almost the entire time. You complained about the walking. You tried to negotiate with a god, which went about as well as negotiating with weather." She smiled. "And then, at the door, when it would have been easiest to run — you gave away everything. Every memory you'd ever stolen, returned to their owners. And every memory of me, given up freely, to seal the door behind the gods forever. The ember needed a willing surrender. You made it the most willing surrender in the history of the world."

Coral stared at her. His hand had drifted, without his noticing, to the faded mark on his wrist — the brand he'd always assumed was a birthmark, shaped like a flame with its tongue cut out.

"And you?" he asked quietly. "What happened to you, in the story?"

"Heaven offered me a place at the gods' right hand. A seat, a crown, the whole architecture of glory. All I had to do was walk through the door and let the world forget you, the way you'd forgotten me." Lena's voice stayed steady; she had practiced this part against the crooked chimney many nights. "I chose exile instead. Because someone had to remember you. Someone had to keep the shape of you alive in the world, or the sacrifice would just be a hole. So I stayed. I'm staying. That's the ending — except it isn't an ending, because endings are just the part of the story where the telling stops, and I don't intend to stop."

The wind moved through the reeds. Coral looked at her for a long time, and she watched him search his own hollow places for her and find only smooth walls, and she watched him decide — the way he had once decided on a road between Vael and the world — to trust anyway.

"The man in your story," he said. "Did he love you?"

"With the whole ridiculous weight of him. It was extremely inconvenient for everyone."

"Then he had good taste," said Coral Crane, "even if he can't remember it." He paused, and the corner of his mouth lifted in the old crooked way. "Tell me the story again tomorrow. From the beginning. I want to hear what I said when we met. I want to get it right this time."

"There are forty-one tellings' worth of details, Coral. This will take years."

"Good," he said. "I was worried you'd run out of reasons to sit on my wall."

* * *

Summer came, and she told him about the Cartographer of Ash, and he argued with her about the geography, delightedly, because arguing was the one language he'd never surrendered. Autumn came, and she told him about Sable's bargain, and he swore loudly at a woman he saw every week at market, and Sable, informed of this later, was heard to say it was the finest review of her career. Winter came again, mild and godless and safe, and Lena sat by the crooked chimney's fire with Coral across from her, sketching in his ledger — he had gone back to keeping accounts, honest ones now, though he still pronounced *quay* wrong — and she began the story again from the first line, the line she had spoken so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone:

*"In a valley where magic could not follow, there lived a thief who could steal anything except the one thing he wanted—"*

"—which was what?" Coral asked, as he asked every time, pen stilled, eyes bright.

And every time, Lena Thackeray, exiled from heaven by her own choosing, keeper of the only inheritance the gods could never touch, smiled and gave the true answer:

"Me."

Outside, the snow fell on the Hollow like a blessing that asked nothing back. Inside, the story went on — and would go on, night after night, year after year, until the telling and the living became the same thing, and the man with the hollow where she used to be learned, one evening at a time, how to fill it again.

It was, she thought, poking the fire, the slowest theft in history.

She intended to get away with all of it.
