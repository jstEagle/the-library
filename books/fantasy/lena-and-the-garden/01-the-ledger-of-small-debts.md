## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The tide came in twice a day in Corvane, and Casper Villalobos balanced the books twice a day too, because the sea could be trusted to keep its schedule and the Guild of Impossible Things could not.

She worked on the third floor of the Counting House at the end of Saltcutter Row, in an office with one window that looked out over the harbor and one window that looked out over nothing at all — a wall of blank brick that had been there longer than the building, longer than the row, longer, some said, than the city's permission to exist. Casper had measured both windows when she took the post nine years ago. The blank one was exactly the same size as the harbor window. She had never found out why, and she had decided early that there were questions worth asking and questions worth leaving alone, and the architecture of the Counting House belonged firmly in the second category.

The morning ledger lay open before her, its columns ruled in the pale green ink the guild used for debts and the deep oxblood it used for payments. Everything else — the trades themselves, the impossible inventory — went into the black book with the brass clasps, and the black book never left her sight except to be locked in the floor safe beneath her chair, where she kept her feet while she worked, as if the weight of her own body pressing down on the guild's secrets was a kind of honesty.

*Three vials of bottled storm,* she wrote, *sold to the Widow Asper of the Lantern District, per contract dated the 14th. Payment received: one year of the widow's life, held in trust, redeemable only by the widow herself.* She blotted the entry, checked it against the delivery manifest the runners had signed, and moved on.

That was the work, really. People thought the guild traded in wonders — storms in glass, years in stoppered jars, luck by the pound, names that could be worn like coats. They did. But wonders were just inventory, and inventory was just arithmetic, and arithmetic was the only honest religion Casper had ever found. Every miracle had a price. Every price had a payer. Every payer had a signature, witnessed, notarized, entered in triplicate. The gods could have the thunder. Casper would take the column totals.

She was good at it. She was, though no one said so above a whisper and no one ever said it to her face, the best the guild had. Not because she could conjure anything — she couldn't, not so much as a cantrip, not a single borrowed hour of her own — but because she could hold a thousand accounts in her head without dropping one, because she remembered every debtor's face and every creditor's grudge, because she had never once lost a ledger page, not to fire, not to flood, not to the small thieving hands that occasionally made it past the Counting House door. Oren Sable called her his anchorite. He meant it as a compliment. Casper took it as a job description: someone who lives in one small cell with one small light and keeps the faith by keeping the count.

Invisibility was not a side effect of the work. It was the work.

She had learned that young, though *young* was a country she didn't visit often. Her mother had taught her letters using map symbols before she taught her the alphabet proper — a mountain for M, a wavy line for water, a little circled dot for *here, this place, this is where we are* — and then her mother had died, and the maps had gone into a trunk, and Casper had discovered that a person who knows how to draw the world can also learn how to erase herself from it. A dotted line here. An unlabeled road there. A woman alone in a counting house, of no family, of no nation, of no story anyone cared to check. The guild hired people like that. The guild was mostly made of people like that.

The harbor bell rang the half hour. Down on the water, the fishing fleet was coming in with the morning catch, gulls screaming over them like creditors. Somewhere below, in the guild's trading hall, the day's first customers were lining up: a merchant who wanted three days of someone else's courage; a bride who wanted the rain to stay away from her wedding, which would cost her, and cost her more than she knew; a man who had heard you could buy back a word you'd said and wanted to unsay something to his brother. Casper would see their contracts by afternoon. Their money, their years, their small terrible hopes — all of it would pass through her columns, and none of it would touch her.

She liked it that way. She told herself she liked it that way, in the firm voice of a woman reading her own terms aloud.

*One borrowed summer,* she wrote next, *returned by the cooper Tomas Bell, per the redemption clause, condition: fair. Re-entered into stock.* The cooper had bought twenty years ago a summer he'd spent as a boy, wanting to feel it again, and had come back last week gray-faced, saying it wasn't his anymore, saying it fit wrong now, like a coat cut for a smaller man. That happened sometimes. Memory was the guild's most volatile commodity. It appreciated and depreciated without warning, and no one — not Oren, not the appraisers, not the hedge-witches down the coast — could say why. Casper recorded the return, noted the condition, and felt the small cold finger she always felt at such entries slide briefly along her spine. There but for the grace of a good forgetting, she thought, which was a prayer-shaped sentence, and she put it away with the others.

Because that was the other thing about Casper Villalobos, the thing under the thing: she did not pray. Ever. On principle, or on scar tissue — she'd stopped distinguishing between the two somewhere around her ninth year. Prayer was a contract with no counterparty, a debt entered in a ledger whose other page you never got to read. Her mother had prayed, at the end. Casper had heard her through the wall, low and fast and bargaining, and then the praying had stopped, and everything else had too, and Casper had been nine years old and learning very quickly what silence cost.

So she kept the guild's books and touched none of its miracles, and if the traders joked that the ledger-keeper was the only person in Corvane who'd never sold or bought a single impossible thing, she let them joke. Let them think her careful. Careful was respectable. Careful was safe. Nobody followed a careful woman home through the fog to see whether she had a life behind her door, because they already knew the answer: she had a desk, a lamp, a locked box, and two windows, one of which looked at the sea and one of which looked at nothing.

It was a good arrangement. It had been a good arrangement for nine years.

Which is why the candle ruined everything.

* * *

She noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a song you know by heart — not at first hearing, but a few beats later, when the shape of the melody has already bent around the error and your mind insists on going back to find it.

Casper worked by lamplight. Always lamplight. The office lamp sat on the corner of her desk, whale oil, brass fittings, a sound like a sleeping animal when the wick burned low. Candles were for the trading hall downstairs, where the clients liked the theater of them, and for the shrine alcove on the ground floor where the guild kept its token offerings to whatever powers still bothered to listen — a bowl of coins, a dish of salt, a rack of thin tallow candles that supplicants could light for a copper, the flame meant to carry a wish up to whoever was minding the sky that week. Casper walked past that rack twice a day and had never once lit one. The coppers went in the bowl. The candles stayed cold. That was the whole of her theology.

But there was a candle burning on the windowsill that faced the blank brick wall.

Small. White. Cheap wax, the kind sold in bundles outside churches and shrines all along the coast. Its flame stood perfectly straight in the draft from the harbor window — that was the first wrongness, the flame refusing to lean — and it had burned down perhaps a third of its length, which meant it had been lit some time ago, which meant it had been lit while she was in the room, and she had not seen it lit, and she had not smelled it, and the office door had been locked since dawn because Casper Villalobos locked her door the way other people said their prayers, constantly and without thinking.

She set down her pen. Very carefully, the way she did everything now, as though the room were a ledger and any sudden movement might smudge the entries.

"Claude?" she said, to the empty air, because the Counting House had a poltergeist named Claude who rearranged the stockroom and was owed, per a contract older than her employment, one saffron bun every Thursday. Today was Tuesday. "Claude, if this is you, the bun isn't due until Thursday, and you know I don't keep candles."

Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered; Claude expressed himself exclusively through misplaced inventory and the occasional rude arrangement of brooms. The candle went on burning its straight, stubborn line of flame.

Casper rose, crossed the room, and stood over it. Up close she could see the wax had pooled unevenly, run down one side in a way that cheap tallow shouldn't, and there — she leaned closer, close enough that the heat pressed against her cheek like a hand — there were marks in the soft wax near the base. Letters. Deliberate ones, scratched with a fingernail or a pin before the wax cooled.

Old Vessani script. She recognized it the way you recognize a face in a crowd forty years after the funeral: instantly, totally, and with no idea how.

She had never studied Old Vessani. No one had. Vessany itself was a word that meant almost nothing anymore — a country struck from the maps four decades before she was born, erased so thoroughly that even the erasure had been forgotten, a blank space on the old charts that cartographers had long since stopped bothering to fill in with *here be monsters* or *uncharted*. When Casper had trained as a mapmaker, her instructors had used the Vessany gap as a teaching example: *see how the trade routes bend around it? See how the rivers simply stop? That is what a dead country looks like on paper.* They'd said it the way you'd talk about a burned-down house. Something that had existed once. Something nobody missed.

And yet her hands, hovering over the candle, knew the letters. Knew them the way her mouth knew songs she couldn't remember learning — her mother humming over the wash basin, over the maps, over the little circled dots that meant *here, this place, we are here.*

The marks in the wax were short. Three characters, maybe four. She should have been able to read them. Some part of her insisted she *could* read them, that the meaning was sitting right there behind her eyes like a word on the tip of the tongue, warm and round and refusing to come out.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with detached professional interest, the way she'd note an irregularity in an account. *Discrepancy observed. Source unknown. Flag for review.*

She blew out the candle.

The smoke rose straight up, ignoring the draft entirely, and hung in the air between her and the blank brick window in a shape that lasted half a second too long to be accidental — a thin gray line, and then a curl, and then, briefly, unmistakably, the little circled dot. *Here. This place.*

Then it was just smoke, and the harbor bell rang the hour, and down on Saltcutter Row a cart driver swore at his mule, and the world resumed being the ordinary world, and Casper Villalobos stood in her office with her heart going like a hammer on a nail and told herself, in her firmest ledger-keeper's voice, that there were explanations. Drafts behaved strangely in old buildings. Wax held impressions from packing crates. Memory played tricks, especially memory that had been sitting unexamined for thirty years in the trunk of a childhood.

She swept the candle into the cold hearth. She scraped the wax out with the poker and washed the soot from her hands, and scrubbed, and scrubbed, until her knuckles were red, because the smell of it — cheap tallow, church-and-shrine smell, prayer smell — had gotten into her skin somehow, under it, and would not come out.

Then she went back to her desk, sat down, pulled the black book toward her, and found that her pen hovered over the green-ruled columns and would not move.

Because here was the thing she could not enter in any ledger, the discrepancy with no column: the candle had been lit for someone. That was what prayer candles *were* — a message with a name on it, a small fire sent to a specific address. Someone had carried that candle up three flights, past the night clerk, past the locked door, into her office, and lit it, and the flame had stood straight and the smoke had drawn her mother's favorite symbol, and every part of it said *you, specifically, Casper Villalobos, this is for you.*

And the guild's rules were clear on the matter, rule eleven, section four, which she herself had copied into the apprentice handbook in her neat, invisible hand: *No uncontracted offering shall be accepted on behalf of any member of the Guild. Any such offering shall be reported to the Guildmaster within one day.*

Reported to the Guildmaster. To Oren Sable, who saw more than he said and said less than he saw, who had hired a friendless nineteen-year-old mapmaker nine years ago with a promptness that had never quite made sense to her, who was even now upstairs in his tower office doing whatever guildmasters did with their mornings.

Casper looked at the blank brick through the empty window. She looked at the harbor, where the light was coming up gold on the water and the ordinary day was assembling itself, gull by gull, bell by bell. She looked at her ledger, at the tidy columns waiting for her pen, at the whole well-ordered architecture of her small, safe, carefully erased life.

Then she opened the floor safe, took out the black book, wrapped it in oilcloth, and put it in her satchel — not because she intended to flee, she told herself, but because a ledger-keeper who feels unease protects the accounts. Standard procedure. Prudence. Nothing more.

Upstairs, Oren Sable was already sending for her. She could feel it the way you feel weather coming — in the old scar-tissue barometer of her bones.

Someone had lit a candle in her locked office. And in the wax, in a dead language from a dead country, in her dead mother's alphabet, someone had written her name.
