## 2. A Country That Isn't There

The client arrived at the ninth bell, smelling of tallow and river water, carrying his dream in a jar the way other men carried lunch.

"Master Casper," he said, setting it on her desk with the reverence of a man laying down an infant. "It came back three nights running. Same dream. I thought—well, they said at the chandlery you do dreams."

Casper Villalobos looked up from the ledger she was balancing—the Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts in Corvane under the name of a shipping concern, and she kept them under the name of nobody at all—and studied him. A lamplighter, by his hands. Burn scars in the shape of wicks. Middle-aged, honest-faced, with the particular exhaustion of a man who had stopped sleeping not because of worry but because of wonder.

"Dreams are four silver," she said. "Six if you want the map inked. Eight if you want to know what it means."

"I want all of it." He fished coins from his coat. "I've had this jar since my wife died. It used to hold pickled plums. Now it holds—" He gestured vaguely at the glass, where something pale moved like smoke that had learned to swim.

"You caught it yourself?"

"Net made of hair. Hers." He said it without embarrassment. In Corvane, on the salt-stained streets below the counting houses, grief was a trade good like any other.

Casper took the jar. The dream inside pressed against the glass the way a cat presses against a window—wanting out, wanting *in*, wanting something it couldn't name. She'd bottled enough dreams in her time to know the difference between a nightmare, which rattled, and a longing, which went still and heavy as deep water. This one was neither. This one moved in patterns.

"Name?" she asked, opening a fresh folio.

"Tomas Bell. Lamplighter, Harrow Street."

"And you'll want the map to scale or to feeling?"

He blinked. "Is there a difference?"

"There is always a difference, Mr. Bell. Scale tells you where things are. Feeling tells you why they're there. Most people only think they want the first."

"The second," he decided. "My wife always said I got lost even in my own head."

*Then we shall see how lost,* Casper thought, and uncapped her ink.

* * *

Mapping a dream was a discipline of patience. You did not simply look into the jar—you listened through it, let the dream pour over your hands like cold stream water while your pen translated what it found. Casper had learned the technique from a one-eyed cartographer in the Spindle Isles who claimed he'd once mapped the inside of a whale's memory, and though she had long since stopped believing half of what old mapmakers told her, she had never stopped using his method: begin at the edges, work inward, trust nothing you recognize.

She began at the edges.

The dream's border was a shoreline.

That alone was unusual. Most dreams were architecture—corridors, rooms, staircases going nowhere, doors painted on walls. Landscapes meant the dreamer had been somewhere real, somewhere the mind kept returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth. Tomas Bell, lamplighter, born and raised in Corvane if his accent was anything to go by, should have dreamed in streets.

Instead: sand. Gray sand, fine as flour, and beyond it water so flat it looked poured rather than fallen. She inked the coast in long, careful strokes, and the pen did that thing pens sometimes did when the hand knew more than the mind—a small shiver, a hesitation between strokes, as though the nib were being *pulled*.

She worked inward. Dunes stitched with a grass she didn't know, pale and wiry. A road of crushed shell. Low hills like sleeping animals. And then, rising from the middle distance, a city: white walls, red roofs, towers crowned not with flags but with windvanes shaped like open hands, dozens of them, all pointing the same direction—out to sea, away from land, as if the whole city were gesturing *go back, go back.*

Casper sat very still.

She knew those towers.

Not from maps. There were no maps—not anymore, not anywhere she had access to, and she had access to everything the guild owned, which was everything worth owning. She knew them from stories. From a voice reading aloud in a kitchen that smelled of cardamom and lamp oil, a voice that sang a counting song about nine towers and eight winds and seven sisters who walked into the sea and became—

"Became the foam-road," Casper whispered. "Seven sisters. The foam-road home."

Her mother's stories. Ilse's stories, told in the years before the soldiers came, before the burning papers, before the night that ended in a different city and a different life and a silence where a country used to be. Vessany. Erased forty years ago—not conquered, not renamed, but *removed*, struck from every chart and atlas and merchant's ledger until even the word sounded made-up, a nonsense syllable children dared each other to say.

And here it was, rising out of a lamplighter's dream, rendered in her own ink.

Casper's hands were shaking. She noticed it the way you notice rain starting—one cool drop, then another—and set the pen down before it could blot the page. Her heartbeat had gone strange, arrhythmic, like a clock wound too tight.

*Tomas Bell.* She looked at him across the desk. He stood by the window with his cap in his hands, watching the harbor gulls, patient as furniture. A Corvane man. Born here, presumably. His wife dead two years, he'd said—or had he? Had he said when? She tried to remember and found the detail missing, a hole in the conversation shaped exactly like a question she hadn't thought to ask.

"Mr. Bell," she said carefully. "This dream. When did it start?"

"Three nights back. Same every time. I walk the beach, I come to the city, and there's someone waiting at the gate." He smiled, embarrassed. "I never get close enough to see who. I wake up with wet feet. Actual wet feet, miss. My boots are dry by the fire and still I wake up with wet feet."

"Has anyone else in your household dreamed this? Your neighbors?"

"Don't know anyone's dreams but mine." He paused. "Why? Is it bad?"

*Bad* wasn't the word. *Impossible* was closer. Dreams didn't leak. That was the first law of her trade: a dream stayed in the skull that grew it unless someone netted it out, and even then it carried only what the dreamer carried. A Corvane lamplighter could not dream a coastline that had been unwritten from the world before he was old enough to walk—unless the coastline had come looking for him.

Unless something was answering.

The prayer candle in her office had burned down to a stub overnight. No one had lit it. She'd told herself she'd forgotten extinguishing it, the way you tell yourself any small wrongness that doesn't fit the shape of your life. But now, looking at the gray sand drying on her pen, another explanation presented itself, uninvited, wearing her mother's voice: *the borders are thin where things were erased, little fox. Erasure doesn't kill a thing. It just makes it hungry.*

"Here's your map," Casper said, sliding the finished sheet across the desk—feeling-scale, the towers rendered with a precision that unsettled her more than any error would have. "The meaning is simpler than you'd think. You're being called somewhere. Or something is calling itself to you. Either way, don't answer prayers near open water for a while, and if the dream offers to tell you its name, decline politely."

Tomas laughed, then saw her face, and stopped laughing. "You're serious."

"I'm always serious. It's cheaper than insurance." She held out her hand for the jar. "I'll keep the dream overnight. Standard practice. If it moves, I'll want to see where it goes."

"It moves?"

"Everything moves, Mr. Bell. That's the whole trouble with the world."

He left his coins and his confusion and went out into the gray morning. Casper waited until his footsteps faded on the stair, then locked the door, drew the curtain, and sat down across from the jar.

* * *

The dream moved.

She watched it for six hours, through the changing light of the curtained room, and it moved the way tide moves—imperceptibly, constantly, with intent. The city in the glass turned slowly, tower by tower, until the open-handed vanes pointed not seaward but *inward*, toward the far shore of the dream, toward a place past the dunes that hadn't existed when she'd inked the map. New country, accreting at the edges like frost climbing a window.

And the sand was creeping up the inside of the glass. Grains of it, fine as flour, settling against the curve like snow against a pane.

Casper opened her ledger of small debts—the private one, the one no auditor would ever see—and wrote, in shorthand only she could read:

*A client's dream contains erased geography. Coastline consistent with Vessany pre-Removal. Source unknown. Dream self-propagating. Sand escaping containment.*

She stared at the last line. Then she added:

*I recognized it. This is a problem. I am the problem.*

Because that was the thing she had built her whole quiet life around not knowing: you could not recognize what you had never known. Somewhere in her, beneath forty years of careful anonymity, beneath the ledgers and the borrowed name and the invisibility she wore like armor, Vessany was still written. Her mother had burned the papers, fled the soldiers, buried the country under new names in a new city—but she had not been able to burn it out of a nine-year-old girl's blood. Stories survive erasure. Songs survive. The smell of cardamom survives.

And now the erased country was bleeding back into the world, and it had found the one mapmaker in Corvane whose hands already knew the shape of it.

The candle stub on her shelf guttered, though there was no draft. For half a heartbeat Casper smelled cardamom and lamp oil, sharp and impossible, and heard—very faintly, from very far away, or from very far inside—a woman's voice reading numbers aloud, the way her mother used to read ledger entries like lullabies.

Then it was gone, and the office was just an office, and the jar on her desk held nothing but a dream going still.

Casper did not sleep that night. She sat with the folio open on her knees, the map of a country that wasn't there, and told herself she would burn it in the morning. She was still telling herself that when the knock came—at dawn, hard and official, from the landing below.

A guild runner, breathless, holding a sealed letter stamped with Oren Sable's personal mark, the one he used perhaps twice a year.

*Come at once. Bring nothing. Tell no one.*

Casper looked at the letter. Then at the map. Then at the jar, where, in the bottom-most corner of the dream, a single grain of gray sand lay against the glass—outside the dream now, inside her office, real as a debt.

She folded the map into her coat after all.

Some instructions, she decided, deserved to be disobeyed on principle.
