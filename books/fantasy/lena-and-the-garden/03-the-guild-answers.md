## 3. The Guild Answers

The summons came the way all of Oren Sable's summons came: a boy in guild gray, breathless from the stairs, pressing a card into Casper's hand as though it might bite him.

*Third bell. My office. Come alone.*

She had been expecting it since the candle.

For two days she had told herself the prayer candle in her office was nothing—a client's forgotten offering, a janitor's superstition, a fluke of the ventilation that carried incense up from the temple district on windy nights. She had even gone so far as to draft a memo to facilities about drafts. But the candle had burned down a full thumb's width overnight with no one to feed it, and its wax had pooled in a shape she did not like: not a puddle but a ring, neat and deliberate, like something poured for a guest expected.

And now the summons. Third bell. *Come alone.*

Casper dressed carefully, which was her way of arming herself. Plain charcoal coat, no jewelry, hair pinned back so tight it made her look severe even to herself. Invisibility was a discipline, and today she would need every hour of practice she had. She tucked the dream-map of the impossible coastline into the false bottom of her ledger case—she could not have said why, exactly, except that leaving it behind felt like leaving a wound unbandaged—and climbed the guild's central stair toward the office at the top of everything.

Corvane's harbor glittered below the windows as she ascended, all rigging and gull-white light, the bottled storms racked in their cellar vaults beneath the tide line. Somewhere down there, someone was trading three borrowed years for half a storm's worth of calm weather. Someone else was selling a memory of their grandmother's kitchen to a man who collected kitchens the way other men collected knives. The Guild of Impossible Things ran on such trades, quiet as water finding cracks, and Casper Villalobos kept its ledgers the way a lighthouse keeper tends his flame: faithfully, invisibly, without ever asking what the light was for.

Oren Sable's office smelled of cedar and old smoke. He stood at the window when she entered, hands clasped behind him, watching the harbor with the fixed attention of a man who suspected the water of lying to him.

"You got my card," he said, without turning.

"I did."

"Sit."

There were two chairs before his desk. Casper took the one farther from the window—an old habit; she had learned years ago never to sit where a man could watch the door over her shoulder. Oren waited until she was settled, then crossed to the desk himself and sat, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. He let the silence do what he always let it do: press down on her, test her, see if she would fill it with something careless.

She didn't. She folded her hands and looked at him the way she looked at an account that wouldn't balance.

"Tell me what you've heard," Oren said finally.

"Heard?"

"Don't be careful with me, Casper. It wastes both our time." He leaned back. "I know you keep your ears open. It's why I hired you. What are people saying?"

Casper considered lying. It would have been easy—he asked for rumors, and rumors were cheap—but there was a particular quality to Oren's questions, a weight, that suggested the answer mattered more than usual. And besides, she had spent two nights staring at a candle that lit itself. She was tired of carrying things alone.

"People are saying," she said slowly, "that prayers are being answered again. Not temple prayers. Real ones. A cooper on Rope Street prayed for rain for his blighted garden and woke to a flood that drowned only his neighbor's rotting compost. A widow prayed for a sign from her husband and found his handwriting on her mirror, in the steam, spelling out a word nobody in this city speaks anymore." She paused. "A fisherwoman off the Salt Quay says her drowned son came home."

Oren's face did not move. That was the thing about him; forty years of guildmastership had sanded him down to something smooth and unreadable, like sea glass. "And the trades?"

"That's the strange part. Impossible trades have tripled in a fortnight. Bottled storms moving at four times the usual rate. Borrowed years changing hands like copper coin. People are desperate to *hold* things, Oren—to own time, own weather, own anything solid. As if they can feel something coming and want collateral against it." She met his eyes. "You summoned me. So tell me what you know that I don't."

He was quiet long enough that the harbor bells rang third bell somewhere below them, the sound drifting up through the glass like a warning delivered politely.

"What I know," Oren said at last, "is that the gods are answering. That is not a rumor. I have confirmation from three separate sources I trust, and one source I don't, which is worse, because the one I don't trust has been right twice already." He opened a drawer—not the locked one, the ordinary one—and drew out a slim folder, which he slid across the desk to her without opening it. "What I don't know is *which* gods. And that distinction matters more than anything else in this building."

"How can it matter which? An answered prayer is an answered prayer."

"Is it?" Oren's voice stayed level, but something underneath it shifted, the way ice shifts on a pond before it breaks. "When the temple gods answer, they answer through their priests. There's a chain. Accountability, if you want to call it that. But these answers—" he tapped the folder once, sharply, "—these are coming direct. No priest, no rite, no offering recorded. A woman prays into her kitchen sink and the sink gives her back her dead. That is not how the old contracts worked, Casper. That is something reaching up out of the dark and taking hold of whatever it finds."

"And you want me to find out what."

"I want you to find out what *quietly*." He said the word as though it were the whole of the assignment. "No guild seal. No official capacity. You will go as a ledger-keeper making routine inquiries about trade anomalies—which, conveniently, is exactly what you are. Interview the cooper. Interview the widow, if her mind hasn't gone. Interview the fisherwoman." His eyes held hers. "Especially the fisherwoman."

"Why especially her?"

"Because hers is the case with a body attached." He rose and went back to the window, and Casper understood that he could not look at her while he said what he said next. "The boy came back wet, Casper. Three days drowned. He came back smiling, and speaking words his mother didn't recognize, and every person who's spoken to him has come away wrong somehow. Shaken. One of them—the temple deacon who examined the family—has stopped sleeping. Says the boy looks at him like he's reading something written on the inside of his skull." Oren's reflection floated ghost-pale against the harbor glass. "I need to know what word the boy is saying. I need to know where he learned it. And I need you to bring me the answer before anyone else in this city understands enough to panic."

"And if the answer is what I think it might be?"

Oren turned then. His face, in the flat harbor light, looked older than it had a moment ago, as though turning had cost him something. "Then you'll bring it to me anyway," he said, "and we'll decide together what the guild does with it. That's the arrangement. That's always been the arrangement."

It was, Casper thought later, the most honest lie he had ever told her.

She took the folder. She rose. At the door she paused, because there was one more thing, and she would not leave without saying it.

"The candle in my office," she said. "The prayer candle. Nobody lit it."

Something flickered across Oren's face—too fast to name, too slow to miss. "I know," he said.

"You *know*?"

"I know candles have been lighting themselves in this building for eleven days. I know they burn down to a perfect ring and go out. I know they started in the archive and have been climbing floor by floor ever since." He returned to his desk and sat, folding his hands, a guildmaster again, seamless. "Yours is the highest one so far. When one lights on my desk, I'll consider us warned properly. Until then—quietly, Casper. Quietly above all."

She left without another word. On the stairs going down, past the racks of bottled storms and the clerks copying out contracts in ink that dried invisible, she pressed the folder to her chest and felt, through its thin leather skin, the shape of the question inside it, sharp as a splinter working toward the bone.

The fisherwoman lived off the Salt Quay. She would go tomorrow.

Tonight she would sit with her self-lighting candle and try not to imagine what it wanted.

* * *

Oren Sable waited until her footsteps faded on the stair, and then he waited longer—until the building's evening sounds had settled into their familiar rhythm, the tick of cooling pipes, the mutter of night clerks, the far-off complaint of the harbor. Only then did he unlock the drawer he had not unlocked in front of her.

Inside lay a ledger older than the guild that employed him.

He did not touch it often. Touching it felt like touching a sleeping animal whose dreams he did not want to interrupt. But tonight he lifted it out and set it on the blotter, and opened it to the marker of braided black thread that had waited there, unmoved, for six years—since the day he had been made guildmaster and sworn, in a room with no witnesses, to keep certain accounts.

The page was headed, in a hand much older than his own: *Debts Outstanding Against the Erasure.*

Below it, entries. Dozens. Each one a name, a sum, a date of collection deferred. Oren read them the way he read everything—twice, slowly, looking for the error—and as always, the same entry snagged him, the way a nail snags cloth.

*One (1) inheritance, royal, Vessany. Holder: Ilse Villalobos, deceased. Transferred by bloodline to: C. Villalobos, Corvane. Status: dormant. Note:* —and here the old hand grew smaller, tighter, urgent— *do not wake. Whatever wakes first will not be us.*

Oren closed the ledger.

He had known Casper Villalobos for nine years. He had hired her precisely because she was careful, invisible, unambitious—a woman who balanced books and wanted nothing else, or seemed to. He had told himself, all these years, that the dormancy would hold. That the girl raised on stories of a country that wasn't there would never find the coastline in her own ink. That the debts would sleep as they had slept for forty years, collectible forever and collected never, which was the entire genius of the arrangement.

But the candles were climbing the floors. The gods were answering prayers with no priests between. And yesterday, a junior cartographer had brought him a client's dream-map for routine authentication—a map of a coastline that matched, stroke for stroke, the borders of a country erased from every atlas in the world—and the authentication stamp had been signed *C. Villalobos.*

Oren had burned that copy within the hour. But you could not burn the original. The original was in Casper's head now, drawn by her own hand, and a thing drawn by the inheritor's hand was not a drawing. It was a summons.

He poured himself a measure of plum brandy he did not want and drank it anyway, standing at the window while the harbor lamps came on one by one below him, little answers of their own, little prayers of light.

*Do not wake,* the ledger said.

But it was too late for that, and Oren Sable knew it, and the knowing sat in him like swallowed stone. The country was bleeding back into the world. The gods were feeding. And the girl he had hired to keep the guild's smallest books was the last living key to a door that should never, under any circumstances, be opened.

He had sent her to the fisherwoman because he needed to know how far along it was. That was true.

He had also sent her because if she walked into that house and heard the drowned boy speak Old Vessani—if the inheritance stirred in her the way he feared it would—he needed to see it happen. Needed to measure it. Because there were factions in this city already, quiet men with quiet ledgers of their own, who wanted that door open, and Oren had spent forty years learning that the only way to control a thing like that was to stand closer to it than anyone else.

Dread dressed as duty. He had been wearing the suit so long he'd forgotten it was a costume.

He set down the glass. He opened the old ledger one more time, to the flyleaf, where in faded ink someone had written the guild's founding charge—the words every guildmaster swore to and none, so far, had understood:

*We keep the debts so the dead may sleep. We keep the door so the living may forget. When the forgetting fails, the keeper goes first.*

Oren read it three times.

Then he blew out his lamp, locked the ledger away, and sat a long while in the dark of his office, listening to the building around him, waiting—for what, he could not have said. For a candle to light itself on his desk. For a voice. For the sound, faint and patient, of pages turning somewhere below in the archive, where no one was working tonight, and where the oldest book in the guild's keeping had begun, very quietly, to open itself.

Tomorrow, Casper Villalobos would knock on a fisherwoman's door and hear a drowned boy speak a word of a dead language.

And Oren Sable, guildmaster, keeper of debts, would learn by nightfall exactly how much time he had left—

because the boy's word, when the report came back, would be a name.

*Casper.*
