## 4. Prayers with Return Addresses

The tide was out at Saltmere Row, and the smell of it—kelp rot and old fish and something mineral underneath, like coins held too long in a fist—followed Casper down the lane like a dog that had decided she belonged to it.

The houses here were built of ship's bones. Not metaphorically; she could see keel-ribs in the walls of the nearer cottages, tarred planking for shingles, a figurehead staring blind from above one doorway as if the house had swallowed its own figurehead and was still deciding whether to spit it out. Nets hung everywhere, drying in nets-of-nets, and in them the morning light caught and broke into small silver arguments.

Number nine had a blue door. The paint was peeling in long curls, and someone—a child, once—had scratched a little boat into the wood at knee height.

Casper stood before it longer than she needed to. She told herself it was professional habit: the moment before an interview when you let the door tell you what kind of grief waited behind it. A well-kept door meant a grief kept tidy. A broken latch meant a grief that wandered.

The latch on number nine was new brass, installed over old damage, which meant the grief had wandered and been caught and locked back in, recently, by force.

She knocked.

* * *

Maren Ostrey was fifty if she was thirty-five, which is what salt water does to faces, and she opened the door on the second knock as though she'd been standing behind it since dawn, waiting for someone to knock so she could stop waiting.

"You're the guild woman." Not a question. "The ledger one."

"Casper Villalobos. Guild of Impossible Things, Corvane office." She showed the seal; Maren looked at it the way people look at weather reports for other countries. "Guildmaster Sable sent me. He received your petition."

"I didn't petition anyone."

Casper kept her face still. "You wrote to the office. Three weeks ago. Regarding your son."

"I wrote to my son." Maren's hands were working at each other, knuckle over knuckle, a fisherman's habit of always being mending something even when there was nothing to mend. "I wrote him a letter and I burned it, the way we do, the way my mother did, and it went where letters go. And then—" She stopped. Her jaw set. "Come in or don't. Standing in my doorway talking about it is worse than either."

Inside, the house smelled of lamp oil and bread and, faintly, underneath everything, of the sea—not the harbor sea, not the working sea, but the deep cold sea, the one that keeps things. Casper catalogued it automatically, the way she catalogued everything, and then hated herself a little for doing it.

There were two chairs at the table. One was worn smooth. The other was new, still sharp-edged, and no one sat in it, and it was set slightly apart from the table, angled toward the window, toward the water.

Casper did not sit in it. She took the stool by the stove instead, and something in Maren Ostrey's shoulders came down half an inch.

"You understand," Casper said, "that whatever you tell me stays in the ledgers under seal. I'm not here to take anything from you. I'm here to take account."

"That's the same thing," Maren said. But she sat, and she began.

* * *

His name was Joss. He was fourteen. The *Kestrel's Mercy* went down in the autumn squall with five men and two boys, and the sea gave back three of them over the following week, and Joss was not among them, and Maren Ostrey did what her mother had done and her grandmother before that: she wrote him a letter, told him all the things mothers say to sons who cannot hear them, and burned it in the hearth, and watched the smoke go up through the chimney into the sky where prayers go.

"And then he came home," she said.

"When?"

"Nine days after. It was night. I heard the door." Her hands had stopped their mending. They lay flat on the table now, pressed down, as if holding the table to the floor. "And I want you to write this down exactly, because everyone in this town thinks I'm mad, and mad women get their children taken away, except he isn't a child anymore, is he, he's something else, and nobody knows what to take."

"I'm writing it exactly."

"He was wet." She said it simply, terribly. "Not damp. Wet. Dripping on my clean floor, seawater, and it was cold, colder than the harbor, cold like the bottom. And he smiled at me. And I knew right away—I knew before I knew anything else—that it wasn't the smile. You know how you know your own child's smile? Like you know their walk, from far off, in the dark? This was his face doing a smile. Someone had taught it the shape."

Casper's pen had stopped moving. She made it move again.

"What did you do?"

"What any mother does. I dried him. I fed him. He ate—that's the worst part, he *ate*, bread and butter and the smoked herring he used to steal when he thought I wasn't looking, and he ate it wrong, too fast, like he was copying eating from memory. And then he slept, and I sat up all night watching him breathe, because drowned boys don't breathe, do they, and this one breathed, but wrong. Too even. Like a bellows. In and out, in and out, the same every time, no sighs, no snoring, nothing extra."

"Has he spoken?"

Maren looked at her then, directly, and Casper saw the thing underneath the exhaustion, the thing that had driven her to write a letter to a dead boy and burn it and then, when the impossible answered, to write again—to the guild, this time, though she'd never admit it.

"One word," Maren said. "He doesn't talk. He nods, he smiles that smile, he does the chores—he does the chores *perfectly*, better than he ever did, hauling nets like he's done it forty years—but he doesn't talk. Except once. The first night. I asked him—" her voice cracked and she forced it flat— "I asked him where he'd been. And he looked at me with those wet eyes and he said one word. I wrote it down phonetic, the way it sounded, because it wasn't Low Speech and it wasn't High and it wasn't the fisher-cant. Here."

She pushed a scrap of paper across the table. On it, in a laboring hand unused to writing, was a single transcribed syllable-set:

*Vess-an-ya.*

The stove ticked. Outside, a gull screamed once and quit.

Casper read the word four times. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were steady, which felt like a betrayal of everything happening inside her chest.

"Say it," she said. "For the record. As he said it."

"*Vessanya.*" Maren pronounced it carefully, a foreigner handling a relic. "Like that. Soft on the middle. What is it? Do you know? Is it a place? Is it where he is?"

"It's a country," Casper said.

"There's no country by that name. I've sailed every coast between here and the Serpent's Teeth."

"No," Casper agreed quietly. "There isn't. There hasn't been for forty years."

* * *

She got the rest in pieces, the way you get truth from frightened people: not all at once but in the gaps they leave when they think the questioning has moved on.

Joss—she could not stop calling him Joss, and Casper noted that she never called him *it* or *the thing* or even *him* with any distance—had come back the night after a storm. Other households on Saltmere Row had lost people too, over the years, and had burned letters, and three of those households now had visitors. Old Aldous Vey had his brother back, drowned thirty years ago, sitting in his kitchen mending nets with hands that never blistered. The Wren sisters had their father, dead before they were born, who stood in the garden at night facing the sea.

None of the returned spoke, except in that one word. None of them slept more than an hour. All of them were wet, always wet, no matter how often they were dried—the damp came back on them like breath on glass.

And all of them, Maren said, lowering her voice as though the walls had learned to listen, all of them *answered*. That was the horror she couldn't make the town understand. When she prayed—when she knelt by her bed and said the old words, *keep him warm, keep him fed, bring him home to me*—the thing wearing her son's body would appear in the doorway within the hour, dripping, smiling its copied smile. Every prayer. Every time. Prompt as a shopkeeper.

"The priest says I should turn it out," she whispered. "Father Ilm says it's a devil wearing a skin and I'm feeding a devil at my table. But Casper Villalobos"—and here she leaned across the table and gripped Casper's wrist with a hand hard as driftwood—"he does the chores. He kissed my forehead last week, the way he did when he was small, exactly the way, and I *know* it's a copy, I know, I've known since the first night. But it's a copy of the truest thing I ever had, and Father Ilm can take his doctrine and drown in it. If the price of having my boy's ghost walk around in a wet skin is that something somewhere is feeding on my grief—" her grip tightened— "then let it feed. Let it eat me hollow. I'd rather be eaten than empty."

Casper covered the woman's hand with her own and said nothing, because there was nothing honest to say, and she had built her whole life on never writing a false entry.

But underneath the pity, underneath the professional calm, something cold was swimming in slow circles.

Because this was not how miracles worked. She kept the ledgers; she knew the economics. Prayer was a letter with no return address. It went up through the chimney-smoke into whatever darkness received such things, and either nothing heard it—which was the usual case—or something heard it and took its time and took its toll. Gods were slow. Gods were expensive. Gods answered in seasons, in generations, in riddles that cost kingdoms.

These answers had come back in hours. These answers had come back *wet*. These answers had come back speaking the name of a country that had been erased from every map and every memory and every ledger forty years ago—and Casper knew it had been erased from the ledgers too, because she had spent three days last week looking, and found only a hole shaped like a nation, a gap in the archives with tidy edges, as if someone had cut Vessany out of the world with a very sharp knife and burned the scraps.

A dead god was a closed account. Everyone knew that. Whatever had once listened on the far side of Vessany's prayers had starved or scattered or been erased along with its country.

Unless it hadn't starved. Unless it had learned, in forty years of dark, to live on something else. Something local. Something abundant.

Grief, for instance. Grief was a kind of memory. Grief was memory with the pain left in.

*Keep him warm.* And the warm dead boy appears. *Bring him home.* And he comes home, dripping, from a country that isn't there. Every answered prayer a withdrawal. Every miracle a debt collected—from whom, Casper wondered, and in what currency?

From Maren Ostrey, she thought, looking at the woman's sanded-down face, her mending hands, her new-brass latch. From her. From all of them. Paid out slowly, in the only coin the poor have ever had: themselves.

*Horror at mercy,* she wrote later in her field notes, and underlined it twice, and could not have said why until much later.

* * *

She asked to see him.

Maren went stiff. "He doesn't like strangers."

"I'll wait in the yard. If he comes to the door, that's enough. I won't speak to him unless he speaks first."

It took two hours. Casper stood in the small salt-killed garden, pretending to study the bean rows, while inside the house a shadow moved past the window and stopped, and moved again, and stopped. Watching her. Being watched. The whole street pretended not to watch them both, which in Saltmere Row was its own form of attention.

Near noon the door opened.

He was Joss Ostrey's height and Joss Ostrey's build—she had the sketch Maren had given her, charcoal, loving, exact—and he wore Joss's spare clothes, and his hair was Joss's hair, brown and sun-streaked at the ends, except that nothing about him was sun-streaked anymore. Everything about him was the color of things kept in deep water. His skin had the grey-green patience of a hull's underside. Water beaded on him freshly, constantly, as though he had just that moment surfaced from somewhere very far below.

He smiled at her.

It was a good smile. It was a perfect smile. It was a smile assembled from observation, fitted to the face like a net over a fish, and Casper felt her stomach drop through the garden, through the salt rock, through the cold floor of the world, because she recognized the craftsmanship. She had seen work like it in the guild's oldest files—in the margins of documents written in a hand she would not see for weeks yet, in the careful copied curves of a script designed to imitate living handwriting so well that only grief would mistake it.

Something had studied this boy. Something patient, and thorough, and *bookish*, had taken him apart and put him back together as a message.

He looked at her for a long time. The gulls wheeled. The tide, far out, ticked against the harbor bars.

Then the boy opened his mouth, and what came out was not the word Maren had transcribed. It was not *Vessanya*, the name of the drowned country, the name of the thing answering prayers along this coast.

It was a different word. Older. Smaller. Spoken in a voice like water finding a crack in stone, in an accent forty years dead, soft on the middle:

"*Casper.*"

Her name. Her name in Old Vessani pronunciation, the vowels rounded the way her mother had rounded them, once, in a life Casper had never been able to source—fragments of accent surfacing in her speech that no teacher in Corvane could account for.

The boy smiled his copied smile, bowed—actually bowed, a small formal dip of the head, courtly, archival, the bow of a clerk acknowledging a superior—and stepped backward into the house, and shut the door.

Casper stood in the dead bean garden for a long time.

Then she took out her notebook, and beneath her notes on Maren Ostrey, on the returned, on the word *Vessanya*, she wrote a single line in cipher, the private shorthand she used for things too dangerous to record plainly:

*The dead country knows my name. It didn't learn it from me.*

She looked up. Far down the row, at the edge of the salt-haze, a figure stood where no figure had been a moment before—tall, still, dressed in travelling grey, with the unmistakable silhouette of a surveyor's tripod slung across its back.

When she blinked, it was gone.

Casper closed the notebook, walked out of Saltmere Row without running, and did not stop walking until she reached the high road—where she turned once, and looked back at the small bone-built town, and the sea beyond it, flat and shining and full of letters.

Somewhere under all that water, something had just filed her name.

And she knew—with the certainty of a woman who has spent her life balancing books that balance themselves back—who would be waiting in her office when she returned to Corvane, and what he would ask, and why she could no longer afford to tell him the truth.

Oren Sable wanted a report.

She would give him one. Every word of it true.

Except the name.
