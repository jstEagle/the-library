## 5. Her Mother's Handwriting

The archive beneath the Guild of Impossible Things had no stairs. It had a door in the floor of the counting room, iron-banded and unremarkable, and below it a spiral of steps that went down farther than the building above it was tall. Casper had been told once, by an archivist who died before she could ask a second question, that the depth was not architectural but contractual: the guild owned its past, and its past had been buried to keep it out of reach of creditors.

She carried a lamp, a slate, and the fisherwoman's testimony copied out in her own careful hand. *The boy said vessa-torren. Wet, wrong, smiling.* She had turned the phrase over for three days like a coin she suspected of being counterfeit. Old Vessani. A dead language from a dead country, spoken by a drowned child who should have stayed drowned.

There were records that might explain it. There were always records.

At the bottom of the stair the air changed—cooler, drier, tasting faintly of salt and old paper, the way a letter tastes when it has been sealed for decades. The lamp found shelves first: ranks of them marching off into dark, taller than a man, each one crowded with ledgers bound in leather gone the color of dried blood. The Archive of Small Debts, the Archive of Borrowed Years, the Archive of Storms Bottled and Uncorked. Casper knew these collections the way other people knew their own kitchens. She had balanced every account in them twice over.

But there was a further corridor, past the last shelf, where the guild's holdings ended and something older began. The archivist who had died had also told her this much: *We keep what we bought. Down there is what we inherited.*

No one had ever given her leave to go down there. No one had ever forbidden it either, which in the Guild of Impossible Things was nearly the same as an invitation, and for nine years she had chosen not to test the difference. Anonymity was a discipline. You did not go poking at foundations when your whole safety rested on no one looking at you at all.

Tonight she tested it.

Her boots sounded wrong on the stone—too loud, too deliberate, as if the corridor wanted her footsteps counted. The shelves down here were different. The ledgers were not uniform; they were hand-sewn, odd-sized, some of them fat as bricks and some thin as hymnals, and their spines bore no catalog numbers. Only titles, inked directly onto the leather in hands that had died centuries ago. *A Reckoning of What Was Owed Before There Were Names For It.* *Debts of the Drowned, First Gathering.* *What We Carried Out.*

Casper stopped at that last one. The leather was pale, almost white, scarred with water damage along its lower edge—as though the book itself had once been carried through a flood.

She did not touch it yet. She walked the corridor slowly, lamp raised, reading titles the way you'd read gravestones in a country you'd never heard of. And then, near the end, on a shelf set slightly apart from the others—the way a single chair is set apart at a table where someone is expected—

A ledger with no title at all.

It was smaller than the rest, bound in gray-green leather gone soft with age, and it lay flat rather than upright, as if whoever shelved it could not bring themselves to stand it on end. There was no dust on it. That was the first wrong thing. Everything else in the corridor wore its years like fur; this book sat clean and waiting, and the lamp flame leaned toward it without any draft to explain the leaning.

Casper set her slate down. She wiped her palms on her coat, which was absurd, because her hands were not dirty, they were shaking.

*You are a keeper of accounts,* she told herself. *Open the account.*

The cover gave under her fingers with the softness of living skin. Inside, the paper was heavy and cream-colored, ruled in faded brown ink, and the entries began in a script so old it took her a moment to parse it—not letters the way Corvane wrote them, but the older hand, the one from before the guild standardized its books. Dates that predated the guild's founding charter by two hundred years. Names she didn't recognize, paired with debts she couldn't classify: *one memory of rain, owed to the river; three generations of lullabies, held in trust; the shape of a door, promised and not yet delivered.*

Vessani script. Vessani accounting. She recognized the structure before she recognized the words—the way obligations were entered not as amounts but as *holdings*, things kept safe on behalf of people who could not yet collect. Her mother had used the same grammar in the margins of Casper's childhood copybooks, doodling little tables while Casper practiced her letters. *This column is what you remember. This column is what remembers you.*

Casper turned pages. The ledger ran backward in time as she moved forward through it, the hand changing every few dozen leaves—generations of keepers, each training the next, the script evolving, simplifying, until near the end it became the modern guild hand, and then—

And then it stopped being the guild hand.

The final entry occupied a single page, and it was written in a hand Casper knew better than her own.

She knew it because she had learned to write underneath it. Because for six years of her childhood, every list, every label, every birthday card in her house had been made by that hand: the tall ascenders that leaned just slightly left, like trees bent by a constant wind; the peculiar crossed sevens; the way the letter *r* ended in a small upward flick, as if each word were about to fly. Ilse Villalobos had written grocery lists the way other people wrote poetry, and she had written poetry the way other people prayed.

Casper's knees found the edge of a reading stool that she did not remember sitting down on.

The entry was dated forty-one years ago. Nine years before her mother's death. Thirty-two years before tonight.

*Received into trust: one country entire,* the entry read. *Vessany, its borders, its debts, its dead, its names. To be held against return. Conditions of release as follows:*

*The country may be re-entered only by one who holds it whole in memory.*

*The holding is hereditary. It passes to the last born of the line who carries it, whether or not the line knows it carries anything at all.*

*Current holder: Ilse Villalobos, who takes this burden up unwillingly and lays it down the same way.*

*Inheritor named: Casper Villalobos, my daughter, aged four months. She will not be told. Telling is how they find you. Let her be a woman of no country, of no story, of no remarkable inheritance. Let her balance other people's books and never once wonder whose book she is written in.*

*I am sorry. I am so sorry. Forget me kindly, little one. Forget me all the way.*

Below that, in the same hand, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*If she is reading this, then they have started answering prayers again, and I failed, and she must not open anything. Whatever asks her to remember — she must refuse. Refuse beautifully. Refuse politely. Refuse the way I taught her to refuse second helpings, smiling, with her hand flat on the table.*

*Refuse, Casper. Refuse.*

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. Somewhere far above her, through forty feet of stone and contract, Casper could hear the counting room's clock striking the hour, and the sound seemed to come from another life.

She read the entry again. Then again. The words did not behave like words; they behaved like weather, moving through her, rearranging things. Her mother had been here. In this corridor, under this city, forty-one years ago—when Casper was an infant in a basket in some rented room upstairs or across town—and Ilse Villalobos had sat exactly where Casper was sitting and written her daughter's name into a book older than the guild, and then climbed back into the daylight and never said one word about it for the rest of her short, burned-papers, whispered-prayers life.

*Telling is how they find you.*

So she hadn't told. She had taught letters with map symbols instead. She had sung songs in a language she claimed she'd picked up from a sailor. She had burned papers the night the soldiers came to Corvane asking after Vessani exiles, and held Casper's hand over the fire and said, *warm your fingers, sweetheart, it's cold out tonight*, while eleven years of somebody's history curled into ash.

And now the prayers were being answered. The boy on the dock had spoken Vessani. The candle in Casper's office had lit itself. The country was bleeding back into the world, and it was bleeding toward its inheritor, and its inheritor was sitting in a basement holding a book that had waited thirty-two years for her hands.

*She will not be told.*

But she had been told. Not by her mother—by the ledger itself, by whatever intelligence lived in the arrangement of these debts. Books in the deep archive did not lose their dust by accident. Something had cleaned this shelf. Something had wanted the finding to happen tonight, and Casper's skin crawled with the arithmetic of it: if the book had been prepared for her, then her arrival had been prepared for her, and if her arrival had been prepared, then the anonymity she had built her whole life inside of was not a wall but a corridor, and corridors lead somewhere, and someone had drawn the map.

She hated maps that lied to her. Especially the ones she was standing inside.

Casper forced herself to stand. Her legs felt borrowed. She took out her notebook—the good one, the one she trusted—and made a faithful copy of the final entry, every word, every crossed seven, every torn place where the pen had pressed through. When she reached *forget me kindly, little one*, her vision blurred and she let it, and she copied the line anyway, because a keeper of accounts recorded what was true even when the truth was salt water.

Then she looked at the ledger itself, and faced the question she had been avoiding since she opened it.

Take it or leave it.

Leave it, and report to Oren Sable that she had found nothing, and spend the rest of her life wondering. Take it, and become, officially and irrevocably, a person with a story—a person the Surveyors and prayer-answerers and guildmasters of the world could find, follow, bargain with, devour.

*Let her be a woman of no country.*

Her mother had spent thirty-two years buying her that invisibility. And the fisherwoman's son had come up out of the harbor wet and wrong and smiling, and the candle had lit itself, and somewhere a country was remembering itself into existence one stolen memory at a time. Invisibility was no longer on offer. The only choice left was whether she met her inheritance on her feet or on her knees.

Casper wrapped the ledger in her coat. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the harbor.

She was halfway up the spiral stair, lamp in one hand and her mother's handwriting against her chest, when she saw the light above the trapdoor. Not lamplight—candlelight, steady and gold, spilling down through the iron-banded door in the counting room floor.

The candle no one had lit. The candle from her office, which she had blown out herself three nights ago and watched die.

It was burning on the top step, waiting for her, and beside it, laid neat as a place setting, was a folded note in Guildmaster Oren Sable's brisk, ugly hand:

*Miss Villalobos. My office. Now. Bring what you found. —O.S.*

He had known. He had known before she did.

Casper stood in the dark between the archive and the daylight, her heart going like a trapped bird, and understood that the vertigo was only beginning—that the past had not merely reached forward to touch her, it had arranged the meeting, sent invitations, and was waiting upstairs with its hat on.

She picked up the candle. She blew it out.

Then she climbed toward Oren Sable's office, carrying everything her mother had tried to bury, and let the door bang shut behind her.

* * *

In the deepest shelf of the archive, in the silence after her footsteps faded, the untitled ledger's empty place on the shelf gathered dust for the first time in thirty-two years.

Down the corridor, on a pale, flood-scarred spine, the words *What We Carried Out* seemed, in the total dark, to darken by a shade.

Something in the archive turned a page.
