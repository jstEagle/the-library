## 6. Oren's Bargain

The summons came the way bad news always came to the Guild of Impossible Things: politely, and too early.

Casper found it folded into her ledger when she opened it at dawn—a slip of guild paper, sealed with Oren Sable's thumbprint in grey wax that smelled faintly of iron and old rain. *The Master requests the Keeper of Small Debts at the ninth bell. Come alone. Come unhurried.* The last instruction was the tell. Nobody told Casper Villalobos to come unhurried unless they wanted her to arrive looking like a woman with nothing on her mind, and nobody wanted that unless they intended to look closely at her.

She dressed anyway as if she had nothing on her mind. The same grey coat, the same ink-stained cuffs, the same plain braid pinned up like an afterthought. Invisibility was not something that happened to Casper; it was something she performed, stitch by stitch, every morning, and she had gotten so good at it that sometimes she forgot there was a woman underneath.

The candle was still burning.

It had burned for three days now—tallow, cheap, the kind sold in dozens outside every temple in Corvane—and no one had lit it. She had checked. She had checked the wick for taint, the wax for fingerprints, the air above the flame for the shimmer of a wish still traveling. Nothing. It simply burned, low and steady, and each morning it was shorter by exactly one day's worth of wax, as if it were keeping time for something. As if somewhere, someone had paid for a prayer and the payment had landed on her desk because there was nowhere else in the world for it to go.

She pinched it out. It relit before her fingers left the wick.

"All right," she said quietly, to the candle, to the room, to whatever was listening. "I'm going."

* * *

The Guildhouse of Corvane had been a counting-house once, then a lighthouse, then a temple to a god whose name had been traded away so long ago that even the god seemed to have forgotten it. Its tower leaned three degrees east, which everyone agreed was impossible, and so the guild had bought it and made impossibility a matter of administration. Ledgers lined the stairwells. Bottled storms slept in the cellar, rattling softly in their glass like dogs dreaming. On the third floor, behind a door with no handle on the outside, Oren Sable kept his office and his secrets in equal disrepair.

The door was open when Casper arrived. That was the second tell.

Oren sat behind a desk built from the keel of some drowned ship, reading glasses pushed up into white hair that he never bothered to tame. He was seventy if he was a day, lean as a ledger-line, with hands that had signed away more miracles than most gods ever granted. He did not look up when she entered. He finished his line first. He always finished his line first; it was a courtesy he extended to everyone, including catastrophe.

"Villalobos," he said. "Sit."

"Guildmaster." She sat. The chair across from him was deliberately uncomfortable, a guild tradition meant to keep negotiations short. She had long ago learned to sit in it as though it were upholstered in cloud. Two could perform.

Oren set down his pen and looked at her over the glasses, and for a moment Casper saw him do the thing she had seen him do to clients a hundred times: weigh her, itemize her, price her. She felt it land differently this time. She felt herself being priced.

"You mapped a dream Tuesday last," he said. "A client named Ambrose Fenn. Night terrors. You charged him four marks and a jar of pickled eels, which I'm told was generous of you."

"The eels were for Teodor at the gatehouse. He likes them."

"Mm." Oren drew a sheet of paper from beneath a paperweight shaped like a sleeping cat—the cat had been real once; that was all anyone would say about it. "Your working notes were filed with the dream, as notes are. Coastline. Harbors. A city with seven bridges and a palace with a broken crown on its gate." He turned the sheet around so she could see her own handwriting, precise and sure, and beneath it, in his own hand, a single word: *Vessany.*

Casper kept her face still. It cost more than it should have.

"I don't know that name," she said.

"No," Oren agreed mildly. "Nobody does. That's rather the point of it." He took off the glasses entirely now, and without them his eyes looked older, rawer, two pale stones worn smooth by a very long river. "Forty years ago, Vessany was erased. Not conquered. Not sunk. Erased. Every map, every treaty, every gravestone, every memory in every head from here to the Salt Reaches—it went out of the world like a snuffed lamp. The people inside it went with it. The people who'd left it before the erasure woke up one morning with holes where their childhoods used to be and griefs they couldn't name, and they were told, by everyone, kindly and firmly, that there had never been anything to grieve."

"And you know this how?"

"Because I was there," Oren said, "when it happened. And because the guild was founded to make sure it stayed happened."

The candle-flame feeling came back—the sense of something burning steadily in a room where no one had lit anything. Casper folded her hands in her lap so they wouldn't do anything unprofessional.

"Tell me about the founding," she said.

* * *

Oren told it plainly, which was worse than telling it dramatically. Dramatic stories could be doubted. Plain ones settled into the bones.

"Fifty years ago there were eleven of us. Exiles, mostly—traders, clerks, a cartographer, two priests who'd lost their god and didn't know it yet. We'd left Vessany for ordinary reasons: debt, love, ambition, a quarrel with a customs officer. Then the erasure came, and we stood in foreign ports holding papers for a country that no longer existed, and we discovered something obscene." He tapped the desk. "Our debts survived. Our mortgages, our contracts, our obligations—they persisted, enforceable, collectible, while everything else about home was gone. Some power, some law older than the erasure itself, had decided that what Vessany owed would outlive Vessany. And things began coming to collect. Not men. *Things.*"

"What kind of things?"

"The kind that accept payment in years. In names. In first memories." His mouth thinned. "We were bookkeepers. So we did what bookkeepers do. We organized. We founded the guild to stand between the creditors and the exiles—to hold the debts, service them, spread them thin enough that no single collector could strip a family bare. We became the middlemen of an impossible foreclosure. For forty years it worked. The country stays erased, the debts stay dormant, everybody pays a little forever, and nobody gets eaten whole." He looked at her. "You've spent six years balancing those accounts, Villalobos. Did you never wonder why the entries were so strange? Why we hold liens on storms and leases on luck? Those aren't novelties. They're *installments.*"

Casper thought of the Ledger of Small Debts, of its patient columns, of the entry she had puzzled over her first week—an annuity paid annually since before her birth, drawn on an account whose holder was listed only as *the estate.* She had assumed a dead merchant. She had assumed wrong.

"And now?" she asked. "Why summon me now?"

"Because the installments have stopped arriving," Oren said, "and the prayers have started getting answered." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the window. Below, Corvane's harbor glittered in the morning light, innocent as spilled coins. "Three weeks ago, a widow in the Lowrows prayed for rain and got a flood that drowned only her neighbor's field. Ten days ago, a moneylender prayed for a son and his wife bore twins nine months early, both healthy, both with eyes the color of no one in either family. Yesterday a fisherwoman's drowned boy walked out of the sea and kissed her cheek." He turned. "The old gods are answering again, Villalobos. And every answer is a withdrawal against an account that should be empty. Something is spending Vessany's remains. And your dream-map is the first new coastline anyone has drawn in forty years."

"You think I caused it."

"I think," Oren said carefully, "that you *recognized* it. Which is a different thing, and a far more dangerous one." He came back to the desk and stood over her, and for the first time in six years he did not look like her employer. He looked like a man standing at the edge of a hole, deciding whether to push her in or pull her back. "There are powers moving that make the collectors look like bailiffs. If they learn that a living person carries true memory of Vessany—not borrowed, not reconstructed, but *carried*, in the blood—they will come for that person. They will be gentle. They will be polite. They will offer trades that seem fair right up until the moment you realize what you've signed away. And when they're done, there will be nothing left of her worth protecting."

"So protect me," Casper said. "That's what the guild is for."

"The guild protects its ledgers," Oren said. "People are storage."

The words hung in the air between them, and Casper watched him hear them, watch him flinch at his own honesty, and understood that he had said them deliberately—that this was the shape of the offer, and he wanted her to see its ugly bones before he wrapped it in kindness.

"Here is my bargain," Oren said. "Give me the ledger you took from Fenn's file. Give me your notes, your maps, everything touching that coastline. In exchange, the guild takes you under formal protection. New name, new posting—I'll send you south, to the Mirroring Office in Sault, where nothing happens and nothing ever will. Full salary. Full silence. You'll live out your life as a small, safe clerk in a small, safe town, and the things that are waking up will spend their hunger on someone else."

"And the person who actually remembers Vessany," Casper said slowly, "stays right here. In Corvane. Holding the bag."

"There is no such person," Oren said. "Officially. Unofficially—" he met her eyes, and did not look away, and she gave him credit for that much, "—unofficially, whoever that person is, they survive this only by becoming no one. I've watched it done before. It can be done."

"By whom?" Casper asked. "Who's been erased before? Who taught you the trick?"

Something moved behind Oren's face—grief, or guilt, or the memory of a woman's handwriting—and was locked away before she could name it.

"That," he said, "is not part of today's negotiation."

* * *

Later—much later, in the small hours, in the office he did not sleep in so much as besiege—Oren Sable sat alone with the guild's oldest key and did not use it.

He had lied to her twice, and he catalogued the lies the way he catalogued everything, in neat columns, because neatness was the only prayer he had left.

The first lie: that the guild protected its ledgers and stored its people. True enough as policy. False as history. The guild had been founded by eleven exiles, yes—but the twelfth founder had never signed the charter, because the twelfth founder had been the one who taught them what needed hiding. Ilse Villalobos. Cartographer. The finest mind the guild had ever sheltered and the only one who had understood, forty years ago, what the erasure really was: not a punishment, not an accident, but a *harvest*. She had seen the shape of the machine before anyone. She had hidden her daughter so well that even the guild's records showed only a clerk hired six years ago out of nowhere, competent and forgettable, with no history worth checking.

He had known. Of course he had known. He had interviewed her himself, six years past, and watched her balance a column of impossible debts with her mother's exact economy of motion, and said nothing, because saying nothing was the last promise he could still keep.

The second lie was smaller and heavier: *there is no such person.*

There was. There had always been. And Oren had just offered to erase her the way Vessany had been erased—kindly, administratively, one small safe life at a time—because the alternative, the thing the Surveyor's faction wanted, the thing the waking gods wanted, was to let her inheritance come due.

He opened his desk drawer. Inside lay a letter he had written and rewritten nineteen times, addressed to a woman who had been dead for twenty-three years, beginning, as all his letters to her began: *Ilse. Your daughter is asking questions.*

He did not send it. There was no one to send it to. But writing it was how he remembered which side of the ledger he stood on, and tonight he needed remembering, because tomorrow the girl would give him her answer, and he already knew what it would be, and he already knew—he had known since the interview, since the pickled eels, since the first time she'd corrected an entry in a hand that was not quite her own—that when she refused him, he would have to decide, finally and forever, whether he was the guild's master or Vessany's debtor.

Outside his window, far out past the harbor lights, the sea glowed faintly along a line no chart had ever shown.

Salt. Humming. Coming closer.

* * *

"Ninth bell was yesterday," Casper said, when she returned. "You said come alone and unhurried. I've been alone and unhurried for a full day. I hope the extra time improved my answer."

Oren looked up from his desk. Whatever he saw in her face made him set down his pen.

"It did not improve," she continued, "but it clarified. I brought the Fenn file." She laid it on the desk between them, and watched his shoulders ease half an inch before she set her hand flat on top of it. "All except this."

Beneath her palm: a single page, torn cleanly from the working notes. The coastline. Seven bridges. The palace with the broken crown.

"Villalobos—"

"You told me the truth about the guild," she said. "Mostly. You told me about debts and collectors and exiles, and I believe you, and I am grateful, and none of it changes my answer. Because here is what I noticed, Guildmaster, in six years of balancing your books: the guild services Vessany's debts, but it has never once tried to recover what Vessany *lost*. Forty years of careful, compassionate administration, and not one entry marked *restitution*. Not one line that reads *we intend to bring them back.*" Her voice stayed level. Her hands did not; she let them, just barely, because some things deserved to be seen shaking. "You don't want me safe. You want the memory contained. You want the account closed quietly, the way it's been closed for forty years, and you want me in Sault where I can't open it."

Oren was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was stripped of everything managerial. "If you stay, they will find you. And they will not hurt you, Casper. That's the horror of it. They'll *trade* with you. They'll be so reasonable. And you'll wake up one morning unable to remember why any of this mattered, and you'll thank them."

"Then that will be my debt," Casper said, "and I'll pay it myself. I'm a keeper of ledgers, Guildmaster. I know the difference between a debt I owe and a debt someone else is collecting on my behalf." She lifted her hand from the page and folded it into her coat, against her ribs, where the paper lay warm as a second heartbeat. "This one's mine."

"Casper—"

"My mother died when I was nine," she said, and watched him go very still, and knew—knew with the certainty of a balanced column—that he had known all along. "I remember almost nothing about her. A song. The smell of ink. Hands over my hands, teaching me letters that weren't letters. And yesterday I dreamed a coastline I've never seen, and my hands shook when I inked it, and I have spent one entire sleepless night telling myself it was coincidence." She stepped back from the desk. "So no. No protection. No Sault. No quiet little life. You can have the rest of the file. Keep it, burn it, file it under *impossible*—it's yours. But the page is mine, and the name on it is mine, and whatever comes to collect—" she reached the door, and paused, and did not turn around, because if she turned around she might soften, and she could not afford to soften, "—they'll find the account already emptied into the one place they can't reach."

"Which is?"

"Me," said Casper Villalobos. "I'm keeping it."

She walked out through corridors of sleeping storms and patient ledgers, past clerks who glanced up and immediately looked away, past the cellar where bottled thunder dreamed of coasts it had once flattened, and out into the gray-gold morning of a city that did not know it was standing next to a country that was bleeding back into the world.

Behind her, in the handleless office, Oren Sable sat motionless for a long time. Then he opened the drawer, took out the nineteenth draft of a letter to a dead woman, and added a twentieth line in a hand that trembled only slightly:

*She said no. Gods help us both—she's yours after all.*

And far to the north, along a line of salt that hummed in the grass, something ancient turned its attention toward Corvane, and began, politely, to walk.
