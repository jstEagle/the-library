## 7. The Road Through Unmapped Land

Casper left Corvane before dawn, when the harbor fog was thick enough to drown a name in.

She had spent three hours preparing, and she was proud of none of it. Pride was a luxury for people whose faces weren't already inked into a ledger older than their griefs. She had dressed as a tallow-merchant's clerk—gray wool, gray hood, a satchel of invoices she'd forged in ten minutes because forgery was simply cartography applied to paper—and she had walked out of the Guildhouse by the coal chute, past the sleeping porter, through the yard where the bottled storms sat shelved like patient jars of wasps. The prayer candle in her office had still been burning when she left. She had not lit it. She had not put it out either. Some part of her, superstitious and unaccounted for, suspected that blowing it out would be an answer of some kind, and she did not want to answer anything yet.

The ledger rode against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, bound to her body with two straps. It weighed less than a hymnal. It weighed more than a coffin. Both things were true at once, and Casper had stopped expecting the world to choose between its truths.

Behind her, Corvane's bells rang the hour wrong. They always rang it wrong on the fourth bell—everyone knew this, everyone laughed about it—but this morning they rang every hour wrong, all at once, seven bells for six o'clock, and then fell silent mid-peal as if someone had set a hand flat against them.

Casper did not look back. Looking back was how countries found you.

* * *

The road north out of Corvane was called the Tallow Road, because it carried fat and candles and the rendered byproducts of comfortable lives up into the hill country. Casper knew it the way she knew most roads: from above, from the vantage of a hundred maps she had drawn or corrected or quietly ruined. She knew that it forked at Miller's Rest, that the western branch drowned every spring and reasserted itself by midsummer, that there was a shrine at the third milestone where travelers tied ribbons for safe feet.

By noon of the first day, the road had stopped agreeing with her.

It began small. A bend she remembered as gentle arrived sharp as a broken bone. A copse of alders she could have sworn stood east of the road now stood west, and not recently moved—their shadows lay long and settled, their roots had made peace with the new ground. She told herself she was tired. She told herself memory was a map drawn by an amateur.

Then she passed the third milestone, and there was no shrine. There was a shrine's *absence*, which is a different thing than empty ground: a circle of pale grass, ribbon-shaped impressions pressed into the dirt as though the ribbons had been pulled underground, and a smell of old wax with nowhere to come from.

Casper stopped. She took out her field book and drew what she saw, because drawing was the only prayer she trusted. Then she walked on, and half a mile later she looked back, and the road behind her was not the road she had walked. It curved differently. Her own footprints were gone—not washed away, not blown over. *Unwritten.* The dust lay smooth as fresh paper.

She understood then, with the cold clarity that had served her all her life in place of comfort: the land wasn't rearranging at random. It was *editing*. Somewhere behind her, something was reading the road faster than she could walk it, and correcting the draft.

Vessany had been erased from every map forty years ago. But erasure, Casper knew from long professional experience, is never clean. Ink soaks. Ghosts of letters rise through the page. And a country being bled back into the world would need roads to bleed along—and roads, like debts, remember their debtors.

She turned off the Tallow Road at the next opportunity, onto a drover's track heading northwest, reasoning—correctly, as it turned out—that whatever was editing the highways would find the sheep-paths beneath its notice. For two days this worked. She slept in hay barns, paid for silence with small honest trades—a mended hinge, a drawn map of a farmer's own fields, which he wept over, saying no one had ever thought his land worth the ink. On the second night she dreamed of her mother's voice reading numbers aloud, entries from a ledger, and woke with salt on her lips and no memory of crying.

On the third day, the drover's track began to rearrange too.

* * *

It happened in front of her this time, which was worse. She crested a low hill and saw, below, a stone bridge over a brown stream—and watched the bridge fold itself away, unhurried, like a fan closing. Not collapsing. *Deciding not to have been built.* The stream widened politely to fill the gap, and the road on the far side continued without interruption, as if bridges had never been necessary there.

Casper stood very still. Downwind, forty yards off, a man was also standing very still, watching the same thing. He was big in the way of men who carry heavy things for a living—shoulders like a doorframe, hands scarred and blunt—and he wore the leather apron of his trade, stained in the way that said *earth, regularly*. Over his shoulder hung a spade with a blade worn thin and bright.

They regarded each other across the distance with the mutual suspicion of two people caught witnessing the same crime.

"You saw it close," the man said at last. His accent was border-country, vowels flattened by wind. "The bridge."

"I saw it."

"Third one this week." He came closer, spade first, the way another man might extend a hand. "Teodor," he said. "Gravedigger. Out of Marrow Fen, or what's left of it."

"Clerk," said Casper, which was true enough. "Traveling north."

"Nobody travels north." He said it without accusation, the way you'd say *nobody swims in winter*. "North is where the roads are sick."

"The roads aren't sick." She heard herself say it before she could stop, and cursed inwardly. "They're being *revised*."

Teodor's eyes—pale, watery, patient as a well—narrowed slightly. "That's a strange thing for a clerk to know."

"It's a strange week for clerks."

He should have pressed. Most people would have pressed. Instead he looked back at the place where the bridge had been, and his big scarred hands tightened on the spade haft, and he said, quietly, "I bury the god-touched dead. Have done for eleven years. You learn to notice when the ground starts having opinions." He shifted the spade. "There's a village ahead called Brasek Hollow. Or there was this morning. I've got work there—or I had work there. An old woman died Tuesday. God-touched. Prayed for rain in a dry year and got it, and then got everything else that comes after." He paused. "If the road's revising itself, I'd take it kindly if we walked it together. Two sets of eyes catch more lies than one."

Casper weighed him the way she weighed all trades: what he offered, what he wanted, what he cost. He offered eyes and a spade. He wanted company on hostile ground. What he cost remained to be seen; everyone cost something eventually. But the alternative was walking alone through a country that was learning to lie, carrying the one object in the world that apparently mattered to it.

"Walk on my left," she said. "And if I tell you to stop walking, stop walking. Don't ask why until later."

"That's fair terms," Teodor said, and fell into step beside her, and neither of them mentioned that they had just agreed to trust each other, because mentioning it would have made it a contract, and contracts, these days, had a way of being collected.

* * *

Brasek Hollow was gone.

Not destroyed—*gone*, in the specific, terrible way that Vessany had been gone. They came over the last ridge at dusk and found the valley below holding nothing but pasture, sweet and green and utterly untroubled, with sheep grazing where a village should have been, and a shepherd among them who waved cheerfully and mouthed something the wind ate. Where the church had stood—Teodor described it, voice going flatter with each detail, the lych-gate, the yew, the leaning headstones he'd trimmed around for years—there was a shallow dip in the land, smooth as a thumbprint in dough.

Teodor stood at the ridge-top for a long time. Casper watched him the way she watched weather systems: respectfully, from a distance, ready for anything.

"She was ninety-one," he said finally. "Marta. Bad hip, good heart. Prayed for rain because the wells were low and her grandson's crops were dying." He turned his head. His face didn't change much—it was a face built for endurance, not expression—but his eyes went wet and furious. "The rain came. Then the boy started speaking Old Vessani in his sleep. Then Marta died smiling, which nobody does naturally, and her body—" He stopped. Started again. "Her body kept *humming*. Low. Like a struck bell that wouldn't finish. That's why they call for me. Regular dead don't hum."

"What do you do with them?" Casper asked. "The humming ones."

"I dig deep. Deeper than law requires. I line the grave with salt and iron filings and I say the words my grandmother taught me, which are Old Vessani, though she claimed they weren't, though she claimed a lot of things weren't." He looked down at the vanished village. "And then I stand there till the humming stops. Sometimes it takes a night. Sometimes three." His jaw worked. "I buried eleven god-touched in the spring. Forty since midsummer. Whatever's answering prayers out there—" he gestured at the whole darkening horizon, the whole revised world— "it's answering *a lot*."

Casper's hand went, without her permission, to the ledger at her ribs.

Teodor saw the gesture. She watched him see it—watched the pale eyes move from her hand to her face and back, watched him decide, deliberately, not to ask. It was the single most trustworthy thing anyone had done in her presence in months.

"There's an inn two miles south," he said. "Or there was yesterday. We'll find out."

* * *

The inn existed, but barely. It stood at a crossroads that Casper was almost certain hadn't been there that morning, and its sign—*The Kindly Hour*—was painted in a style forty years out of fashion, sun-faded in a way that suggested decades of weather that hadn't happened yet. Inside, the fire burned, the keeper poured, and four other travelers sat in the careful silence of people who had each noticed something wrong and agreed silently not to discuss it.

Casper and Teodor took a corner table. The stew was good. The bread was stale in a way that felt like an apology.

"Your grandmother," Casper said, low, when the keeper had moved off. "The words she taught you. For the graves."

Teodor tore bread with those huge hands, delicately, like a man defusing something. "Why does a clerk want to know?"

"Because I think your grandmother was Vessani. Because I think the words are a warding, and wards are just maps of where danger isn't, and maps are my trade." She leaned forward. "And because I'm going to tell you something now, Teodor of Marrow Fen, and I need you to hear all of it before you react, because reacting loudly will get us both unwritten like that bridge."

She told him. Not everything—she kept the ledger's contents, her mother's handwriting, the word *inheritor* locked behind her teeth—but the shape of it. A country erased. Gods answering prayers again. Roads revising themselves. A dead nation bleeding back into the world along lines only certain people could feel, and her, Casper Villalobos, guild accountant, invisible woman, standing at the center of it whether she consented or not.

Teodor listened the way he dug graves: thoroughly, without hurry, letting every word settle into its proper depth. When she finished, he was quiet for a long moment, turning his cup.

"My grandmother used to sing me to sleep," he said at last. "Songs in a language she swore was just gibberish she'd picked up. Lullabies about a garden. About a door in a garden wall." His voice roughened. "When the god-touched started coming, I asked her—I was a grown man, and I asked her what was happening, and she looked at me like I'd asked her to open a grave while she was still breathing in it. She said: *"Some doors were locked from the outside, boy, and some were locked from within, and the ones locked from within are the ones that knock."*" He drank. "She died in March. Humming. I buried her myself, with the words, and the humming didn't stop for four days, and on the fifth day it stopped, and I swear on her spade that the last note of it sounded like my own name."

The fire cracked. At the bar, the keeper polished a glass that was already clean.

"So," Teodor said. "You're carrying something that matters to a dead country, and I'm digging graves for a country that won't stay dead, and the roads between here and wherever-you're-going are being rewritten by something that doesn't want witnesses." He set the cup down. "Where *are* you going?"

Casper hesitated. Then, because he had not asked about the ledger, because he had given her his grandmother's knocking-door riddle unprompted, because his hands were honest hands: "East. Toward the old border. Toward the salt line."

Teodor nodded slowly, unsurprised, and that unsurprise frightened her more than anything the roads had done.

"Then we travel together a while longer," he said. "Because I've got a confession to make, clerk, and you'll want to be sitting down for it, and I'd rather make it on the road tomorrow than in a room full of strangers tonight." He glanced at the other travelers—at the merchant who hadn't blinked in an hour, at the young woman by the fire whose shadow fell the wrong direction—and lowered his voice to almost nothing.

"But tonight, sleep light. And keep your boots on. This inn has been here for forty years, according to its sign, and I dug graves in this crossroads twenty years ago, and there was no inn." He smiled, and it was a terrible, kind smile. "Which means either it's lying, or we're the ones who've been rewritten. And I'd very much like to still be myself in the morning."

Casper slept sitting up, the ledger strapped to her chest, one hand on her field book, listening to the inn breathe around her like something practicing being real.

In her dreams, her mother read numbers aloud, patiently, entry after entry, and somewhere beyond the dream a door knocked, softly, from the inside.

At dawn, the road east was waiting for them—straight, smooth, and freshly remembered—and beside the first milestone stood a wooden stake driven into the earth, new-cut, smelling of sap, with a brass plate screwed to it bearing a single surveyed coordinate and a small, courteous, engraved symbol she had never seen before and somehow recognized anyway:

the mark of something that had measured her in her sleep, and found the measurement promising.
