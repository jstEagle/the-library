## 8. Villages That Remember Wrongly

The village of Hallowmere remembered Vessany as a kingdom of orchards.

"It was all apples," said the innkeeper, a broad woman named Doss who poured Casper's cider with the reverence of a priestess at an altar. "My grandmother came over the border before the erasure. She said you could smell Vessany from a mile off—blossom and rot and cider-press, sweet going sour. Whole country smelled like autumn."

Casper sat in the corner of the taproom with her ledger open under the pretense of accounting, sketching as Doss talked. *Orchards,* she wrote. *Smell: blossom/rot/cider.* The words looked absurd on the page. A country that smelled of one thing, edge to edge? Even Corvane couldn't manage that; the harbor city stank of tar at one end and tallow at the other and something unnameable in between that everyone agreed not to discuss.

"And the king," Doss went on, warming to it, leaning across the bar. "Old King—what was his name. Something with an M. He planted the first orchard himself, they say. Every tree in Vessany grew from cuttings of his hands."

"From his hands," Casper repeated.

"Grafted. That's what grafting is, isn't it?" Doss frowned, suddenly uncertain, as if the story had been solid a moment ago and had gone soft in her grip. "Grandmother said—" She stopped. Her brow creased. For three full seconds the innkeeper of Hallowmere stood with an empty pitcher in her hand and nothing behind her eyes, a woman reaching for a shelf that wasn't there.

Then it passed, the way weather passes. "Well," she said briskly, "cider's a penny, bread's two."

Casper paid for both and carried them to her corner table, where Teodor sat hunched over a bowl of barley soup he hadn't touched. The gravedigger ate like a man who expected every meal to be his last and resented the interruption. He'd been quiet since they crossed out of the rearranging roads three days ago—quieter than usual, which for Teodor was saying something—and he watched the taproom's other patrons the way he watched fresh-turned earth: professionally, looking for what would come up.

"You heard," Casper said.

"I heard." He dragged the bowl closer but didn't eat. "Apples. My mother's people were from near the border. They said Vessany was fishing villages. Salt marsh and herring boats. No orchards anywhere—the ground too wet for trees, they said."

"Both can't be true."

"No," Teodor agreed. "And my mother never lied about herring."

* * *

They spent four days in Hallowmere and its surrounding hamlets, and Casper filled eleven pages of her ledger with testimony that could not be reconciled.

The miller remembered Vessany as flat as a griddle, wheat to the horizon, a country you could cross on foot in a day. The ferryman's widow remembered mountains, white-capped, with monasteries in the high passes where monks kept bees. The schoolteacher—a young man, born long after the erasure, who had never seen the country at all—remembered it as a nation of bridges, famous for bridges, though when Casper asked him to name even one he laughed and admitted he couldn't, and then his laughter died strangely in his throat, and he looked at his own hands as if they'd betrayed him.

None of them noticed the contradictions. That was the worst part. Casper sat them down one after another in kitchens and taprooms and once, memorably, in a pig shed, and read their accounts back to them side by side, and each one blinked at the page and said some variation of the same thing: *well, memory's a slippery thing, isn't it*, and went on believing their version absolutely while holding the contradiction in front of their face without seeing it.

"They're not lying," she told Teodor that night, in the room above the inn, her papers spread across both beds because there was no table big enough. "That's what I keep coming back to. Lying leaves seams. You can find the seam if you press. This is—" She searched for the word. "Smooth. Each account is smooth all the way through. It's as if someone took the truth and broke it into pieces and polished every piece until it shone."

Teodor sat by the window with his coat still on, which he did when he expected to leave in a hurry. "Like breaking a loaf," he said. "You don't get crumbs if you do it clean."

"Exactly. And nobody breaks a loaf cleanly by accident."

Outside, the wind moved through Hallowmere's one street, rattling shutters, carrying the smell of—Casper noted it automatically—apples. Blossom and rot and cider-press. Sweet going sour. There were no orchards within twenty miles of the inn. She had checked.

She pinned the pages up along the wall, testimony by testimony, and stood back, and made herself look at the whole mess at once instead of piece by piece. This was the discipline her mother had taught her, or rather the discipline her mother's marginalia taught her, years later, in a ledger Casper had inherited along with everything else: *When the parts refuse to agree, stop interrogating the parts. Ask what shape holds them.*

Eleven testimonies. Eleven versions of a country that no longer existed. Mountains and marshes, orchards and wheat, bridges and kings named M-something. On their own, nonsense. Contradiction piled on contradiction, forty years of erosion wearing the truth into whatever shape each teller's life left room for.

But contradictions, Casper thought, staring at the wall, are also information. Two witnesses who disagree haven't told you nothing. They've told you where the edges used to be.

She began to map it.

Not the stories themselves—she'd tried that already, and gotten fog. Instead she mapped the disagreements. For each pair of testimonies, she marked the point of sharpest conflict: miller against ferryman's widow, wheat against mountains, and drew a line between them weighted by how violently the accounts diverged. Then the next pair. Then the next. It was the technique she used for mapping dreams, where logic didn't apply but structure always did, and her pen moved faster as the pattern emerged, faster, her breath going shallow—

"That's a door," Teodor said quietly, from the window.

Casper stopped writing.

He was right. She saw it now, the way you see a face in a knot of wood—suddenly, irreversibly. The lines of disagreement weren't scattered. They radiated outward from a single dense knot of contradiction, a place where the accounts disagreed most wildly, where the schoolteacher's bridges collided head-on with the miller's trackless wheat fields. From that knot, the lines of divergence spread in two tall arcs, curving away and down, narrowing toward a base where the last testimonies—Doss's apples, the fisher-stories from the south—all converged into a single threshold of agreement.

Two posts. An arch. A lintel of purest contradiction. And beneath it, a space where every version of Vessany met and canceled and met again.

A door, drawn in disagreement, forty miles wide.

"The border," Casper said. Her voice came out strange. "The knot—that's where the salt line runs. I mapped it against the old survey coordinates this morning. The arch springs from the border itself." She turned to look at him. "Teodor. The country isn't remembering wrong. The country is remembering *around* something. All these versions—they're not failed memories. They're load-bearing. Everyone near the border is holding up a piece of a doorway without knowing it."

"To keep it shut?"

"Or to keep it *defined*." Casper took the pins out of the last page and held it, not looking at it. "A door needs two sides. If Vessany was erased—if it was pulled out of the world—then something has to mark the hole it left. Memory might be the only material that survives erasure. So the erasure left instructions, and the instructions got written into the nearest minds, and now every farmer and ferryman within fifty miles is a brick in an arch they've never seen." She laughed, and it came out wrong, too high. "I've spent my whole career mapping things people don't know they know. I have never once hoped I was wrong before."

* * *

The shrine stood at the crossroads half a mile east of Hallowmere, and Teodor found it the way he found most things—by following the dead.

"There's a woman buried under the yew tree," he said, when Casper asked why they were walking down a rutted cart-track at dusk. "Old burial. Pre-erasure style, arms crossed the other way. Somebody tends it. Somebody who remembers the old ways." He glanced at her sideways. "Gravediggers notice graves the way you notice margins. Can't help it."

The shrine was small and older than anything else in sight: a ring of standing stones no higher than Casper's waist, a yew tree gone silver-barked with age, and in the center, on a plinth of the same grey stone, a statue.

It was a figure perhaps four feet tall, robed, faceless—not worn faceless, Casper saw as she circled it, but *smoothly* faceless, the stone flowing where features should be as if the sculptor had run out of conviction. In its cupped hands it held a shallow basin, and rainwater stood in the basin, and someone had left dried flowers and a stub of candle at its feet. Fresh offerings. The wax was days old at most.

"Vessani border-god," Teodor said. "Boundary keeper. They put these at every crossing into the old country. My mother's people called them—" he said a word in Old Vessani, soft and guttural, and did not translate it.

Casper sketched the statue quickly, working by the last of the light. Faceless robed figure, basin, yew. She noted the offerings, the orientation—it faced west, toward the vanished country—and the wear patterns on the plinth, which suggested the statue had stood here since before the erasure and been tended continuously since. Forty years of candles. Forty years of flowers. People praying to a god of a border that no longer existed, on behalf of a country that officially had never existed at all.

"Who tends it?" she asked.

"Doss, probably. Or the ferryman's widow. Someone." Teodor crouched by the offerings, not touching. "Doesn't matter much who. What matters is that they remember *how*. Rites outlive doctrine. You can forget a god's name and still light his candle right."

They walked back to the inn in the dark, and Casper slept badly, dreaming of doors drawn in handwriting, and woke before dawn with the certainty—unreasonable, total, the kind of certainty she'd learned to trust in thirty years of impossible bookkeeping—that she needed to see the shrine again immediately.

She dressed in the dark so as not to wake Teodor and slipped out into the blue-grey cold.

The statue had a face.

Casper stopped ten paces away and did not move for a long time. The mist stood in the fields around the shrine like an audience. Dew soaked through her boots. Somewhere a lark started up, indifferent, gorgeous.

Last night the statue had been smooth-faced. She had sketched it. The sketch was in her ledger, in her own hand, and she trusted her own hand the way other people trusted scripture. Smoothly faceless, stone flowing where features should be.

Now the stone had flowed into features. A woman's face, middle-aged, strong-jawed, with deep lines around the mouth and eyes that were—Casper stepped closer, telling herself the light was playing tricks, telling herself several things in rapid succession, none of them convincing—eyes that were closed. Not blankly closed. Closed the way a sleeper closes her eyes, or a woman listening to music. The expression was one Casper knew. She had seen it on her mother's face a thousand times before she lost the ability to picture it: the look of someone listening hard to something far away.

The basin's water had frozen overnight, though the season was wrong for frost and nothing else in the shrine had frozen with it. In the ice, Casper saw, there were things suspended. Not bubbles. Letters. Small dark shapes, drifting in the frozen water like insects in amber, and when she tilted her head they resolved, briefly, into characters of Old Vessani, and then refused to resolve again.

Her hands were shaking. She made herself take out the ledger anyway, because her hands shaking was precisely why the ledger existed—*when the world goes soft, write harder*, her mother's margin said, and Casper had built a life on that sentence without knowing whose voice it was in. She opened to yesterday's sketch of the faceless statue and set the two truths side by side: the drawing, the stone. Faceless. Faced. Both real. Both witnessed by the same set of eyes less than twelve hours apart.

The shrine had changed overnight. Which meant the shrine was not a record of memory. It was a *participant* in it.

"Careful," said Teodor's voice behind her, and she nearly drove her elbow into his throat before she recognized him. He'd followed her without her hearing, which should have been impossible; the man moved like a landslide and announced himself like one. But he was beside her now, silent as soil, looking at the statue's new face with an expression Casper had never seen him wear. Not fear. Recognition.

"You know her," Casper said.

"No." But he said it the way people say *no* when the truthful answer is *not anymore*. He crouched at the edge of the stone ring, well outside it, and studied the frozen basin. "Water remembers longer than stone does. That's old gravedigger knowledge—we used to wash the dead in water that had stood a night in the churchyard, so the body would carry the place with it." He nodded at the ice. "Those letters. That's a message, or a menu."

"A menu?"

"Gods don't talk. They offer." Teodor straightened, and his gaze went past Casper, past the shrine, west, toward the invisible border. "Every offering those villagers left for forty years is sitting in that basin's memory. Every prayer. And now the border's thinning, and the thing this statue was built to honor is waking up hungry, and it's showing its face to whoever looks." He looked at her. "It showed your mother's face, girl. Did you notice? Look again. Really look."

Casper looked. And the vertigo hit her like cold water, because he was right—the jaw, the listening tilt of the head, the deep lines carved around a mouth that had sung songs Casper no longer consciously remembered. The statue wore Ilse Villalobos's face the way a glove wears a hand. Not perfectly. Approximately. As if the stone had reached into Casper's own memory to find a face and come back with the best available copy.

"It's using me," she whispered. "The shrine is reading me. I looked at it, and it looked back through what I—"

"Through what you hold," Teodor said. "Which is why we're leaving. Today. Now, preferably, before you look at anything else with a basin in it." He was already moving, gathering her scattered papers with surprising gentleness, folding the map of contradictions with the door-shape facing inward. "Things that read you learn you. And things that learn you can be read *by* others. I've buried enough god-touched dead to know the order of operations. First it borrows your face. Then it borrows your voice. Then somebody prays to it wearing your voice, and answers start coming home."

Casper took one last look at the statue—at her mother's borrowed, listening face—and forced herself to turn away.

At the edge of the yew's shade, her boot struck something small and iron. She knelt.

A surveyor's stake. A foot of dark metal, driven flush into the earth, marked at the top with a stamped sigil she knew intimately: the compass-and-quill of the Guild of Impossible Things. Except the guild hadn't operated this far east in forty years. Except the sigil was subtly wrong, the compass points reversed, the quill replaced by something like a tooth.

Someone—or something—had surveyed this shrine. Recently. The earth around the stake was undisturbed, the grass unbroken, as if the stake had grown up out of the ground rather than been driven into it.

Teodor stared at it for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone very quiet, the voice he used in graveyards.

"That wasn't here last night either," he said. "Something's measuring us, Casper. And it's using your guild's ruler to do it."

Westward, beyond the mist, beyond the salt line neither of them could yet see, the air hummed—faint, patient, and pitched almost exactly like a voice reading numbers aloud.
