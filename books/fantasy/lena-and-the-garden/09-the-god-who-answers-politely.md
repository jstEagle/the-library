## 9. The God Who Answers Politely

They made camp in the hollow of a dead river, where the water had once run and now only remembered running—a bed of pale stones laid out in a long S, like a signature someone had abandoned mid-word. Casper chose it because nothing grew there. Nothing growing meant nothing watching, or so she told herself, and by the third night on the road she had learned to be grateful for even the dishonest comforts.

Teodor built the fire small. He was a man who did everything small—small gestures, small words, a small whetstone he drew along his spade's edge each evening as though the blade might forget its purpose otherwise. The spade was for graves. He had explained this on their second day together, unprompted, in the flat voice of a man reciting his own epitaph: *I bury the god-touched. It's a trade. Somebody has to.*

Casper had not asked what god-touched meant yet. She suspected she would find out soon enough, and that she would prefer not knowing for as long as the not-knowing lasted.

She sat cross-legged on her bedroll with the ledger open across her knees, reading by firelight. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. Not physically—though it was that too, dense as wet clay—but in some other register, some weight that pressed down through her palms and settled behind her sternum like a swallowed stone. Her mother's handwriting filled its final pages, and Casper had begun, without quite deciding to, memorizing them. The way Ilse crossed her sevens. The little hooked flourish she used for the letter *V*, which appeared often, because the letter *V* began *Vessany*, and the ledger returned to Vessany the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

*The debts do not die with the country,* one entry read. *A debt is a shape. Shapes persist. Someone must hold the mold or the thing will pour itself back wrong.*

"Read it aloud," Teodor said. He sat sharpening, not looking at her.

"No."

"You mutter when you read. I hear half of it anyway. Better to have all of it."

"I'm not going to perform my mother's ghost for your entertainment."

He considered this, drawing the stone along the edge with a sound like a slow breath. "Fair," he said, and said nothing else, which was most of why she tolerated him.

The road behind them had been rearranging itself since Corvane. Casper had mapped their route each morning from high ground—the discipline of a lifetime—and each evening the map disagreed with where they'd actually walked. A bridge they'd crossed at noon would be gone by dusk, replaced by a ford, replaced by nothing at all. Once they passed a village that Casper could have sworn they'd already passed, except the well was on the other side of the square and the church steeple leaned the opposite direction, and an old woman selling apples had looked at Casper with recognition and called her by a name that wasn't hers.

It was getting worse the closer they came to the old border. She could feel it in her teeth, a pressure like weather.

She turned a page. The candlelight guttered—

No. There was no candle. Only the fire. But the light had changed, gone still and white and even, the way light behaves indoors, under a roof, in a room designed for reading. The stones of the dead riverbed threw no shadows.

Teodor stopped sharpening.

"I wouldn't get up," said a voice, mild and pleasant and precisely pitched, the way a good clerk pitches his voice to carry across a counting-house without disturbing anyone. "There's no danger. I want to be clear about that from the outset. There has been a great deal of misunderstanding lately, and I find I dislike being misunderstood."

The figure stood at the edge of the firelight, though the firelight did not touch him—or it, Casper corrected herself, because whatever wore that shape was not a man who happened to stand somewhere but a presence that had decided, provisionally, to look like one. Tall. Neat. Dressed in the gray of an old guild surveyor's coat, brass buttons polished, a leather satchel slung across the chest, and in one hand a slender iron rod tipped with a point, like a compass needle grown into a walking staff. Its face was unremarkable in the specific way of faces drawn from averages: you would not be able to describe it afterward, only that it had been reasonable.

"The Surveyor," Casper said. Her voice came out steadier than her hands felt. She closed the ledger over her thumb.

"You've heard of me." It sounded pleased, in a restrained, professional way. "Good. That saves time. Introductions are such a tax on an evening." It inclined its head toward Teodor. "Gravedigger."

"Surveyor," Teodor said, and spat sideways, delicately, into the dark.

"Now, then." The god—it was a god; Casper had interviewed fisherwomen and read ledgers older than guilds and she knew what answered prayers when it came calling—stepped forward until it stood at the exact boundary of the firelight, neither in nor out, a border made flesh. "I'll come directly to business, because I respect yours. You are traveling toward the salt line. You are carrying property that does not belong to you. And you are about to enter a jurisdiction in which I hold certain... interests."

"It's my mother's ledger."

"It is a record of obligations," the Surveyor said gently, "and obligations are my native country. More than yours, in fact. Your country no longer exists." It smiled. The smile was correct. Every tooth accounted for. "Which brings me to my offer."

"I haven't agreed there's an offer."

"There's always an offer. That's how the world works; I merely keep the books." It set the tip of its staff against a stone, and the stone hummed faintly, a tuning-fork note Casper felt in her molars. "You are walking into land that eats travelers. Roads that fold. Villages that remember you wrongly. Gods, madam, hungrier than you can imagine, and far less courteous than I. Alone, with a gravedigger and a book, your chances of reaching the border intact are—" it paused, consulting something behind its eyes the way a man consults a ledger, "—eleven percent. I've surveyed it. My figures are reliable."

"And with your help?"

"Ninety-eight." Another pause, another invisible column summed. "I don't promise perfection. Perfection is expensive."

"What does it cost?"

"One memory." The word dropped into the fire-silence like a coin into a well. They both listened for it to hit bottom. "One, of your choosing. Any one. I am scrupulous about consent—it's the foundation of good commerce. You select a memory, any memory at all, and I will take it cleanly, without damage to the surrounding tissue. Most people describe the loss as painless. Several have described it as a relief." It spread its empty hand, palm up, an accountant's gesture: behold, the simplicity. "In exchange: safe passage to the salt line, my personal guarantee, and my silence regarding your location to parties who are currently paying me to know it."

Casper kept her face still. "Who's paying you?"

"That," said the Surveyor pleasantly, "is between me and my client. Though I will note—for free—that the party in question knows exactly where you are regardless. My offer is not about information. It's about courtesy. I believe in courtesy. It's what separates us from the things out here that simply take."

The fire crackled. Or rather, Casper realized, it didn't—the flames were frozen, leaning like painted flames, and had been since the god arrived. Even the smoke hung in the air, a gray ribbon suspended above the coals. Time around the campfire had been set aside, shelved, like a volume pulled from a shelf and held open at one page.

Teodor hadn't moved. His hand rested on the spade across his knees. Casper caught his eye, a flicker, and he gave the smallest shake of the head: *don't*.

"One memory," she said slowly. "Of my choosing."

"Of your choosing. I would never presume."

"And if I choose one you don't want? One that's worthless to you?"

The god's head tilted, and for just a moment the averaged face seemed to focus, the way a lens focuses, and Casper felt seen—not watched, *surveyed*, measured and entered into columns. "Everything is worth something. That is the first law of my profession. But yes—you may choose anything. A smell. An afternoon. A face." A fractional pause, so brief she might have imagined it. "Even a face."

Her mother's face. It was offering to buy her mother's face, and calling the transaction polite.

Casper looked down at the ledger in her lap. At her thumb holding her place. At the hooked flourishes of the letter *V*. *Forget me kindly,* her mother had whispered, the night the soldiers came, burning papers in the grate while nine-year-old Casper watched the ash rise like gray moths. She hadn't understood then. She was beginning to understand now, and the understanding had teeth.

Here was the thing about Casper Villalobos: she had spent thirty years keeping accounts for impossible things, and the first rule of the trade—the rule carved into the lintel of the Guildhall, the rule every apprentice learned before learning to write—was *never sell what you cannot price.* You could trade a bottled storm because you knew what storms cost. You could trade borrowed years because interest was arithmetic. But a memory of her mother was not inventory. It was the ledger itself. It was the hand that wrote.

And more than that—she thought of the fisherwoman's drowned son, wet and smiling and wrong. Every answered prayer was a withdrawal. Every miracle had a return address. This courteous thing standing at the edge of her fire was not making her an offer. It was making her a customer.

"No," Casper said.

The frozen flames did not move, but somehow the whole hollow seemed to lean in.

"I decline," she said, and found, to her surprise, that the words came easily, the way *no* comes easily when you have practiced it in smaller rooms your whole life. "I decline your offer, I decline your percentages, and I decline your courtesy, which I notice is priced the same as everyone else's greed. You may tell your client where I am. In fact—" she lifted the ledger slightly, "—enter it. *The heir declines.* Put it in your figures. I want it on the record."

For a moment the Surveyor was very still, in the way that deep water is still.

Then it sighed, and the sigh was almost human, almost disappointed, and somehow that was worse than anger. "That is, of course, your right." It straightened its coat. "Consent is the foundation of good commerce. I said so. I meant it. I cannot take what you will not sell." The averaged face bent toward her, and beneath the reasonableness, for one unguarded instant, Casper saw the thing underneath: vast, patient, and hungry as an archive with no shelves left. "But I will leave you with a professional observation, free of charge, as a gesture of goodwill."

It raised the staff and drove it into the earth.

The sound was very small—a soft, precise *thock*, like a seal pressed into wax—but it went through the ground and through the stones of the dead river and up through Casper's spine, and she knew, without knowing how she knew, that the mark it left would outlast the hills.

"A surveyor's stake," the god said. "It marks a claim pending. It means: *this parcel has been assessed, and its value recorded, and its disposition deferred.* Anyone may read it. Everything out here reads it." It stepped backward, out of the firelight, and the darkness accepted it the way a ledger accepts an entry—completely, without comment. Its voice remained a moment longer, mild as ever, drifting:

"When you change your mind—and you will; eleven percent becomes zero percent remarkably fast—simply speak a memory aloud near the stake. Any memory. I will hear it. I am always within earshot of my own claims."

The light unstiffened. The flames lurched back into motion, the smoke-ribbon curled and dissolved, and the night rushed in around the campfire like water filling a footprint.

Casper sat without moving for a long time.

Teodor moved first. He got up, crossed to the stake—it stood perhaps three feet from her bedroll, iron-shod, waist-high, topped with a small blank plate of hammered metal—and stood over it, spade in hand, looking down at it the way he must have looked down at the god-touched dead.

"I can dig it out," he said.

"Can you?"

"No." He said it without shame. "I've tried, on others. The hole fills itself. The dirt won't hold it. Once, I burned one, and the ash wrote numbers on the wind." He crouched, studying the blank plate. "It's not really in the ground, anyway. It's in the *agreement*. The ground's just where it files itself."

"Then we leave it."

"We leave it." He rose, and looked at her, and his eyes in the firelight were the color of turned earth. "You know what it offered to buy. It let you see that. The face. That wasn't carelessness."

"No," Casper said quietly. "It was an appraisal. It wanted to know if I'd flinch."

"Did you?"

She looked at the ledger. At her mother's sevens, crossed like swords. "Ask me again tomorrow," she said, "and hope I lie well."

They did not sleep much. Toward the small hours the pressure in her teeth worsened, and the air took on a taste she had no name for—salt, but underneath the salt something older, like the smell of a house you'd lived in before you could walk. She lay with her back to the stake and did not sleep, and once, on the very edge of dreaming, she heard humming. A tune. Simple, looping, half-familiar, the kind of song a mother makes up to rock a child because no song for the purpose exists yet.

She knew the tune. She could not say from where. That was the worst part—that the melody was *there*, whole, waiting, and everything around it had been carefully, lovingly removed, like a picture cut from its frame.

At dawn the stake was still there, and the frost had formed everywhere except a perfect circle around it, and on the blank plate, faint as breath on glass, new markings had appeared overnight: coordinates, a date, and two words in a clerk's neat hand.

*Parcel assessed.*

Teodor stood looking at them, and then at her, and when he spoke his voice had changed—lower, rougher, the voice of a man deciding something he'd been avoiding for years.

"Eat something," he said. "Then pack. And while you pack, I'll tell you why I really follow the god-touched around the country, and why the gods hunt me, and why you should maybe think twice about trusting a gravedigger whose last grave was dug for himself."

Casper looked up. "Your last grave was dug for yourself?"

"Yes," said Teodor, shouldering his spade. "And the man who buried me isn't finished."
