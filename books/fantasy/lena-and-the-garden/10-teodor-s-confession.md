## 10. Teodor's Confession

The fire was small, the way Teodor liked his fires—small enough to hold in one hand if you were desperate, small enough that its light stopped three paces out and let the dark keep what belonged to it. He had built it in a hollow beneath a leaning stone, and he cooked nothing over it. He only watched it, the way other men watched doors.

Casper sat across from him with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. The roads had been wrong all day. Twice they had walked a mile and arrived where they'd started; once a bridge they crossed at morning was gone by noon, not destroyed but *unwritten*, the river running through empty air as though it had never needed banks. She had learned not to say much about it. Saying things aloud seemed to encourage them.

Teodor fed the fire a stick no thicker than a finger.

"You keep touching it," he said. "The ledger."

"I keep making sure it's there."

"That's not the same thing."

She almost smiled. "No. It isn't." She looked at him across the flames—the broad, grave-dirt hands, the face like something carved for a church door and then left out in weather for forty years. "You've been asking me questions since Corvane. All of them careful. All of them circling. You haven't asked me once why I'm really on this road, and I haven't asked you why a gravedigger leaves his parish. Gravediggers don't leave their parishes, Teodor. The dead are the one congregation that never moves away."

He turned the stick in the fire until it caught.

"No," he said. "Mine did."

* * *

He told it plainly, the way men tell things they have carried so long the weight has worn smooth. Casper had heard confessions before—in taverns, in guild arbitration rooms, from clients who paid her to map their dreams and wept when she showed them what they'd buried. But those confessions always came out crooked, full of self-forgiveness already drying in the cracks. Teodor's came out level as a coffin lid.

His village had been called Sarnholt. It wasn't on any map worth trusting now, which was fitting, because Sarnholt had never quite managed to be anywhere at all—a fold of valley between two hills that couldn't agree which country owned them, thirty houses, a mill, a church with a bell cracked in the casting that rang flat and was loved for it.

"And we had a god," Teodor said. "Not one of the loud ones. Not one that wanted temples or tithes or virgins drowned in wells. A small god. A hedge god. It lived in the boundary stones—the old ones, older than the church, older than the road. We called it the Keeper of the Marches, but mostly we didn't call it anything. You don't name your left hand. It kept the foxes reasonable and the fever out of the calves and the hillside from sliding down onto the lower fields, and in return—" he paused, choosing words like a man choosing stones for a wall, "—in return it ate our remembering. A little. Every year. At harvest, each family brought one memory to the boundary stones and set it down, and the god took it, and the year went well. That was the whole of the religion. One memory, one good year."

"What kind of memories?" Casper asked quietly.

"Small ones, at first. That's how it starts. The color of a dress someone wore once. The taste of a bread nobody bakes anymore. Things you wouldn't miss until you went looking for them and found the shelf empty." His eyes stayed on the fire. "My father gave the memory of his brother's laugh. He didn't know he'd done it until my uncle died, years after, and Father stood at the graveside trying to grieve and found there was nothing in him to grieve *with*. He knew the man had lived. He knew he'd loved him. The knowing was there like a line in a ledger. But the weight of it was gone. He stood there dry-eyed and ashamed, and everyone thought he was hard-hearted, and he wasn't. He was just short."

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, a nightbird called and got no answer.

"The harvests got worse," Teodor went on. "Bad years stacked on bad years, and the god got hungrier—it takes more to do less, when a thing is starving—and the village started giving bigger memories. First marriages. Then children. Old Marta gave the whole of her girlhood in one winter and spent the rest of her life sitting by her window smiling at a view she couldn't remember wanting to look at. And still the fields thinned. Still the fever came. Because here's the thing I understand now, that I didn't understand then: the god wasn't eating our memories to help us. It was eating them to keep itself alive. The good years were bait. The covenant was a hook dressed as a hand."

"How did you see it?" Casper asked. "If everyone else was still paying?"

"Because of my wife."

He said it without ceremony, and somehow that made it worse.

"Her name was Anka. She was going to give the memory of our wedding day. That was the arrangement—we'd agreed, both of us, that if it came to it, that was the one we'd spare everything else to keep, and give it together, so neither would carry the loss alone." His jaw worked. "I dug graves, Casper. Thirty years of them. I know what people look like when grief has finished with them, and I saw it coming into Anka's face a little more every autumn, and I decided—I *decided*—that I would rather curse my village than watch my wife go hollow by inches. So I did what gravediggers know how to do. I dug."

"You buried it," Casper said. "The god."

"It sleeps in the ground. That's the oldest law there is, older than any scripture: everything holy can be put back in the earth it came from, if you dig deep enough and mean it enough. I took the boundary stones down in the dark, all nine of them, and I laid them in a pit below the waterline, under the churchyard where the ground is already full of hallowed weight, and I covered them over, and I tamped the earth flat, and I planted a rowan tree on top because rowans are stubborn and their roots go down angry." He finally looked up at her. "And Sarnholt had the best harvest in living memory the next year. And the year after. Three good years. I let myself believe I'd done a mercy."

"But?"

"But a god doesn't die because you bury it. It starves. And a starving god dreams, and its dream leaks up through the roots of the rowan, and the rowan whispers, and the whisper gets into people who sleep near it." He held out his hands, palms up, and in the firelight Casper saw what she had taken for gravedigger's calluses was something else: a fine lattice of pale scarring, like frost-fern, across both palms and up the wrists. "It started calling me. In dreams first, then waking. Not threats. That's the trick of them—they're polite, the small ones, politer than the great gods ever are. It said: *you buried me alive, Teodor, and I am so hungry, and you know better than anyone where the bodies are.*" 

He closed his hands.

"People started forgetting again. Not at harvest. Just—losing things. Names. Roads home. One man forgot his own son's face and beat him as a stranger at his own gate. And everyone knew, the way villages know things without saying them, that it was because of what I'd done. Because I'd broken the covenant instead of ending it. There's a difference. Breaking a covenant leaves a debt, and debts go looking for someone to sit on." He shrugged, an enormous slow movement, like a hill deciding something. "So I finished it. I dug up the stones a second time and I did it properly—not buried but *killed*, unmade, the way you unmake a thing made of agreement. I took apart the covenant word by word. It took me a night and most of my blood, and when it was done, the god was gone. Truly gone. And Sarnholt was free."

"Then why aren't you there?"

"Because free isn't the same as forgiven." He said it simply. "I'd killed a god, even a small one, even a hungry one, and the other gods noticed. The old ones. The ones waking up now, the ones answering prayers along the whole length of this road. To them I'm not a gravedigger. I'm a *precedent*. Someone proved it could be done, and they want that proof walking around about as much as you'd want a lit lamp in a powder store. They've hunted me for eleven years. Not to kill me—gods don't need to kill, they just need you emptied. Every few years one finds me and offers me a bargain that sounds like rest, and I refuse, and it takes something from me anyway, as a toll, and moves on." He touched the frost-fern scars. "These are receipts. I've paid eleven years of tolls on a debt I already settled. That's what it is to be hunted by a god, Casper. You pay forever for the crime of having once refused."

* * *

The fire had burned down to a red eye. Casper found she was holding the ledger so hard the oilcloth had creased.

"Why tell me this?" she asked. "You've traveled with me four days. You've seen what's chasing me. Telling a hunted woman you're a god-killer—"

"Because you're not a hunted woman. You're an inheritor." He said the word carefully, the way he might handle a bone he suspected was older than it looked. "There's a difference, and the difference matters, and I think you already feel it even if you won't say it yet. Everything hunting you wants your inheritance. The Surveyor's stake outside your bedroll. Whatever cousin or claimant is coming. The land itself, reading you as you walk it. They all want what's in your blood, Casper Villalobos—but blood isn't the inheritance. Blood is just the keyhole." He leaned forward, and the firelight moved in his eyes like water over stones. "The inheritance is *remembrance*. That's what Vessany stored in its archive, that's what the gods are starving for, that's what your mother died protecting in you. And remembrance, done rightly, is the one power the old gods can't digest. They eat memory. They cannot eat meaning."

"I don't follow."

"A covenant is a binding made of remembering. Sarnholt's god existed because thirty families remembered, every year, to feed it. When I killed it, I didn't kill a creature—I dissolved an agreement. And you—" he pointed one scarred finger at the oilcloth bundle, gently, almost reverently, "—you carry a ledger older than your guild, written by a dead woman, naming you heir to a whole erased nation. If the Door of Return is real, then somewhere in that inheritance is the original covenant, the founding agreement, the first entry. And an heir who understands what she holds could do to the great gods what I did to a hedge god. Unmake the bindings. Free the fed-upon. End the economy of sacrifice—not by fighting the gods, but by *closing the accounts*."

Casper was silent a long time. Beyond the leaning stone, the dark rearranged itself in ways she chose not to map.

"You're assuming I want that," she said at last. "Assuming I survive long enough to want anything. Assuming I'm not exactly what they say—an heir who stayed away, a woman who ran from a country that needs her." Her voice roughened despite herself. "My mother wrote my name in a book before I could write it myself. Everyone since has been telling me what my name obligates me to. Why should yours be different, Teodor? Why shouldn't I hear 'unmake the bindings' as just another debt with my name on it?"

For the first time since Corvane, the gravedigger smiled. It transformed his face the way spring transforms a field—briefly, and with visible effort.

"Because I'm not asking you to," he said. "That's the difference. Eleven years of gods asking, taking, demanding—I know the sound of a demand, and I promise you, heir, I am not making one. I'm telling you what I know because you deserve to know it, and because—" he hesitated, and the hesitation cost him visibly, "—because I buried my wife's wedding day in the ground with those stones, in a manner of speaking. I saved her from the giving, but the day itself went into the dark with the god, and she died two winters later never knowing what she'd lost, and I have carried that hole in my chest like a second heart ever since. If your inheritance can truly close accounts—if it can make right what covenants have eaten—then maybe, somewhere in the unwinding, a thing like a wedding day comes back. Maybe not for me. Maybe for someone. That's all. That's the whole of my confession and my hope."

Casper looked at him across the dying fire—at the frost-ferned hands, the patience worn into him like water wears stone, the terrible gentleness of a man who spends his life closing other people's eyes.

"My mother burned papers the night soldiers came," she said quietly. "I don't remember it. I remember being told I remember it. There's a difference, and I'm starting to learn how wide it runs." She loosened her grip on the ledger at last. "If I ever find the founding entry—if I ever stand in front of whatever covenant made all this—I'll read it aloud before I decide anything. That's a promise. Not to unmake. Not to preserve. To *read*. It seems like the least anyone's owed, in a country built on ledgers."

"More than least," Teodor said. "But it will do for a start."

He banked the fire and lay down with his back to a root, and within minutes his breathing slowed into the deep rhythm of a man who had made peace with whatever came in the night. Casper stayed awake longer, the ledger warm against her sternum like a second heartbeat, turning his words over: *they cannot eat meaning.*

She was nearly asleep when she heard it.

A sound from the dark beyond the leaning stone—soft, rhythmic, deliberate. Not footsteps. Something lighter. She opened her eyes and saw, silhouetted against the last grey of the moonless sky, a figure standing at the edge of the firelight's reach. Thin. Patient. Holding something long and pale in both hands, the way a surveyor holds a measuring rod.

It did not move closer. It did not speak. It simply stood there, at the exact boundary where light ended, and waited—with the unmistakable courtesy of a creditor who knows, absolutely, that the debtor cannot run forever.

Beside it, driven silently into the earth while they slept or while they woke, stood a second stake.

Then a third, further out.

Then a fourth, marking a line that pointed, straight as a ruled ink-stroke, toward the salt border of Vessany.
