## 12. Ruins in Good Repair

The first thing Casper noticed about Vessany was that nothing was broken.

She had crossed the salt line at dawn with Teodor a half-step behind her, and she had expected ruin—expected the soft collapse of forty abandoned years, roofs fallen into their own cellars, roads swallowed by root and rot. That was what erasure should look like. A country struck from every map ought to lie where it had been dropped: crumpled, forgotten, going gently back into the earth.

Instead the road under her boots was swept.

Not merely intact—*swept*. The cobbles were clean of moss, the ditches cut and flowing, the milestone they passed an hour in carved so freshly that the chisel-marks still held a pale shine. Someone had painted the distance marker that morning. Someone had painted it every morning for forty years, in a country that did not exist, for travelers who never came.

"Teodor," she said quietly. "Look at the fences."

He looked. The fences along the pastureland ran straight as ruled lines, mended where deer might have leapt them, whitewashed at the posts. Beyond them, fields lay fallow but *kept*—turned once a season, perhaps, or weeded, or simply visited, the way one visits a grave.

"It's like a house where the family died," Teodor said, "but someone still comes to dust."

"Someone does come." Casper stopped walking. Ahead, where the road bent toward a village whose name she did not know and yet somehow did—a name that sat on her tongue like a stone from a river she'd never waded—an old woman stood at the gate of the first house, watching them.

The woman was translucent at the edges. Not faded, exactly. More as if the light passed through her a half-beat late, arriving at her face after it had already touched the wall behind her. She wore a shawl the color of wet slate, and her hands were folded the way hands fold when they have been waiting a long time and have made their peace with it.

When Casper met her eyes, the old woman bowed.

It was not a courtesy bow. It went deep—the whole body folding like a reed in wind—and when she straightened, her expression was the worst thing Casper had seen since the drowned boy's smile. It was joy. Unmistakable, unguarded, forty-years-overdue joy.

"The heir," the woman said. Her voice came the way her light did, a half-beat behind her lips. "The heir who stayed away."

"I'm not—" Casper began.

But the woman had already turned, calling down the lane in Old Vessani, words Casper shouldn't have understood and understood completely: *She's come. She's finally come.* Doors opened. Figures emerged from houses, from barns, from the churchyard at the village's edge where the ground itself seemed to ripple faintly, like water disturbed by something rising. They gathered along both sides of the road—not crowding, never crowding; there was a terrible politeness to them—and they bowed, one after another, a wave of bowing rolling ahead of Casper and Teodor like wind through wheat.

Casper's hands, she realized distantly, had gone cold around the ledger strap.

"They think you're royalty," Teodor murmured.

"They think I'm *salvation*." She kept walking because stopping felt more dangerous than moving. "There's a difference, and it's going to get us killed."

* * *

The village was called Serevan. Casper knew this without being told, the way she knew the road bent east at the well and that the bakery had burned in the winter of some year she couldn't name and been rebuilt by spring. The knowledge arrived without provenance, unbidden, unwelcome—like hearing a song and realizing you know all the words to something you've never heard before.

Her mother's voice had lived in this language. That was the truth underneath it. Ilse Villalobos had sung lullabies in Old Vessani over a daughter too young to remember, and the daughter's bones had kept what the mind was not given.

The dead of Serevan did not touch her. That was the strangest part. They walked beside her at a respectful distance, pointed out the well, the schoolhouse, the shrine, narrating their village in voices pitched between pride and apology—as if the place were a child who had come out badly in a photograph and they hoped no one would look too closely.

But everything rewarded looking closely. The schoolhouse windows were washed. The shrine's lamp held oil. In the bakery—cold, breadless, its ovens forty years cold—a girl-ghost in a flour-dusted apron stood at the counter and had clearly stood there that very morning, because the counter gleamed.

"Why?" Casper asked her. "Why keep it? No one was coming back."

The girl considered the question with the seriousness of the young confronted by an adult's blindness.

"You keep a house ready," she said, "so that whoever returns knows they were expected." A pause, and then, softer, with the terrible gentleness the dead could deploy like a blade: "We knew someone would come eventually. We just didn't know it would take this long. Or that you'd be so young when you left."

"I've never been here."

"No," the girl agreed pleasantly. "You haven't."

* * *

They made camp that night in the courtyard of a manor house outside the village—Teodor insisted on open ground, sightlines, the habits of a man who buried things that sometimes climbed back out—and Casper sat up late with the ledger open across her knees, not reading it, just holding it, the way you hold a railing in an earthquake.

The bowing hadn't stopped at Serevan. All day it had followed them: farmhands in fields going to their knees as she passed, a ferryman at a river crossing who refused payment and called her *the heir who stayed away* in a voice thick with tears he could no longer properly shed. Forty years of expectation, and she was the shape it had finally filled.

"They're wrong about me," she said to Teodor, who was sharpening his spade by firelight. The sound was rhythmic, grounding. "I didn't stay away. I didn't choose anything. My mother carried me out of here before I could walk, and I didn't even know this country existed until three weeks ago."

"Mm."

"That's not an heir. That's an accident with good posture."

Teodor turned the spade, examined the edge. "I dug graves in my village for thirty years," he said. "Do you know what people put in them, besides bodies? Letters. Apologies. Debts written down and sealed, because they couldn't say them out loud to the living." He looked up. "A country is the same. Everything unfinished goes in the ground with it. And everything unfinished waits."

"For what?"

"For someone with the authority to finish it." He nodded at the ledger. "That book makes you the authority. Not your blood. The book. Though I suspect the blood helps."

Casper looked down at the ledger, at her mother's handwriting on the final entry, the letters leaning slightly rightward the way Ilse's always had, hurried and certain. *Inheritor: Casper Villalobos.*

"She wrote this knowing what it would do," Casper said. "Knowing they'd bow to me. Knowing I'd walk in here and be handed forty years of other people's waiting." Her throat tightened. "Why would she do that to me?"

"Maybe," Teodor said, "she was doing the only kind thing available. Maybe the choice was between you carrying it and no one carrying it." He set the spade down. "In my experience those are usually the only two choices anyone gets."

Before Casper could answer, the light changed.

It came from the road—from the direction of the border, far behind them now—a pale gold wash over the rooftops, like sunrise arriving from the wrong quarter of the sky. The dead of Serevan, who had drifted back to their houses and their endless dusting, came out into their doorways and turned toward it. Not afraid. *Attentive.* The way dogs attend to a whistle only they can hear.

"What is that?" Casper whispered.

"Prayer," said a voice behind her, and she nearly drew her knife.

An old man stood at the courtyard gate—or the shape of one, tall and thin, wearing the rotted remnant of a uniform she recognized from nowhere and everywhere: the grey-and-gold of Vessany's last king's guard. He bowed, of course. He was already bowing.

"Every evening," he said, "those of us who remain pray at the hour the border fell. We have prayed for forty years. Lately—" his translucent face did something complicated, grief and hope fighting for the same ground— "lately, the prayers are being answered. You will want to see the cathedral, heir. Before you go on to the capital. You will want to see what our prayers have been building."

"We're not going to any cathedral," Casper said. "We're going to the archive. To the capital. That's all."

The guardsman smiled, and it was the drowned boy's smile again, mercy worn as a mask over machinery.

"Heir," he said kindly, "the cathedral is on the way."

* * *

It was. Of course it was. The road to the capital ran through the river valley, and the river valley held the city of Anselm, and Anselm held the Cathedral of the Returned Light, and Casper learned all of this the way she'd learned Serevan's name—knowledge arriving like weather, from a sky she couldn't see.

They reached Anselm at midday, and Casper stopped dead at the crest of the hill above it.

The city was beautiful. That was the horror of it. Forty years erased, and Anselm stood gleaming in the valley light, its towers whole, its walls repaired, banners hanging fresh from the gatehouses—banners of a design Casper had never seen and knew instantly, the silver compass-rose of Vessany on deep green. Smoke rose from chimneys. Bells rang somewhere below, ringing the hour, ringing it *correctly*, though there was no one alive to hear.

And through the streets moved the dead. Hundreds. Thousands. Not shambling, not haunting—*working*. She watched figures on scaffolds repointing stonework. Watched a crew laying new tile on a roof, passing tiles hand to hand up a ladder of pure habit. Watched, in the great square, a congregation assembling out of doorways and alleys, arranging themselves in rows with the practiced ease of people who had done this every day for four decades.

"They rebuilt it," Teodor said. He had gone very still. "Gods' teeth, they rebuilt the whole city. While it was gone. While it *wasn't anywhere*."

"Not while." Casper's mouth was dry. "Because. Don't you see? The erasure took Vessany off the maps. It didn't take it out of *them*. They were folded up inside the prayer, all this time—and now the gods are answering again, and every answer is a thread pulling the fold open." She thought of the fisherwoman's son, wet and smiling and wrong. "This is what answering looks like at scale. This is a whole country of answered prayers."

They went down into the city because there was no sense pretending they wouldn't. The dead of Anselm bowed as Serevan had bowed, and Casper had stopped flinching at it, which frightened her more than the flinching had. By the third street she was nodding back. By the fifth, an old ghost-woman had pressed a heel of bread into her hands—bread that was somehow warm, somehow real, baked from a memory of wheat—and Casper had eaten it, standing in a vanished city, and it had tasted like Sunday mornings she'd never had.

The cathedral doors stood open.

Inside, the light was gold and dense as honey, pouring through windows Casper's eye kept trying to read as stained glass and failing, because the glass depicted not saints but *streets*—the streets of Anselm, the market, the harbor, the schoolhouse at Serevan—rendered in glass so lovingly exact that the windows were less art than testimony. This was a church built to worship a country. Or to remember one loudly enough that remembering became a form of worship.

At the altar, the congregation of the dead knelt in silence. And above the altar, where any sane cathedral would hang its god, hung instead a single object:

A map.

Framed in silver, vast, drawn on vellum gone amber with age, it showed Vessany entire—every river, every road, every town, rendered in a cartographer's hand so fine it made Casper's own work look like a child's scrawl. And beneath the map, in letters of gold leaf, an inscription in Old Vessani that translated itself in her head without permission:

*What is remembered cannot be fully lost. What is loved cannot be fully taken.*

Casper stood in the aisle with the warm bread still in her stomach and her mother's ledger against her ribs, and she understood, finally, why the ruins were in such good repair. It wasn't maintenance. It wasn't habit. It was argument. Forty years of the dead sweeping roads and washing windows and praying at dusk, all of it one long, patient, collective refusal—the whole country insisting, against every map and every empire, *we are still here, we are still here, we are still here*—

And the gods had heard. The gods were answering. And Casper knew now, with the certainty of a woman who kept ledgers, that every answer had a price column, and no one in this beautiful, immaculate, resurrected city had read the terms.

One of the kneeling dead rose and turned—a child, a small spirit in a nightgown, translucent as smoke, looking up at Casper with eyes gone wide.

"You're crying," the child said, puzzled, not unkindly. "Heirs aren't supposed to cry."

"I know," Casper said. "I'm sorry."

She wiped her face, squared her shoulders, and walked out of the cathedral into the ringing bells of a city that had never gotten the news of its own death—toward the capital, toward the Archive of Names, toward whatever her mother had known she would find there.

Behind her, unnoticed, the framed map above the altar had begun, very slowly, to redraw itself—one coastline at a time—to match the sketches in a ledger carried by a living woman.
