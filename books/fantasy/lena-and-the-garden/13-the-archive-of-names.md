## 13. The Archive of Names

The capital of Vessany had a name once. Casper could feel it sitting at the edge of her mind like a word on the tip of the tongue, round and syllabic and just out of reach. The ghosts who bowed her through its streets did not say it either. They called it *the City*, or *Home*, or nothing at all, and when she asked a woman in a moth-eaten shawl what the place had been named, the woman looked at her with an expression of such gentle pity that Casper did not ask again.

"It's on the tip of everyone's tongue," Teodor murmured, walking beside her with his spade over his shoulder like a pilgrim's staff. "That's how they took it. Not out of the mouths. Out from under them."

The streets were swept clean. That was the worst of it, Casper thought—the tidiness. Ruins she could have borne. But the cobbles were whole, the gutters clear, the shutters on the empty houses latched against weather that never came. Forty years of erasure, and still someone—something—kept the city as if expecting the owners back from a long market day. Window boxes held flowers that bloomed without roots. A child's chalk hopscotch grid remained on one flagstone, unfaded, unsmudged, though no rain had touched this ground since before Casper was born.

The ghosts grew thicker as they climbed toward the hill at the city's heart. They did not speak to her. They simply inclined their heads as she passed, the way servants acknowledge a mistress whose face they know but whose moods they fear, and their deference sat on her shoulders like wet wool.

"They think you've come to fix it," Teodor said.

"I've come to look at shelves."

"You've come wearing a crown you can't see. Same thing, to them."

The archive stood at the summit: a long, low building of grey stone, roofed in slate gone silver with age, its windows tall and many-paned so that light could reach every corner. It was the only building in Vessany that looked *used*. Every other structure wore the polish of preservation; the archive wore the shine of traffic. Its doors stood open.

Inside, the smell hit her first—and Casper, who had spent her life among old paper, knew every smell a book could make. This was not that. This was warm and human and faintly sweet, like the air above a baker's stall, like a coat just off someone's shoulders, like the inside of a house where people had been happy. Memory has a smell, she realized. Of course it does. Everything does.

"Saints," Teodor breathed behind her.

The main hall ran the length of the building, vaulted, lit by clerestory windows that threw long panes of gold across the floor. And the shelves—gods, the shelves. They rose three stories high along both walls, tens of thousands of niches, each niche holding a single vessel: a jar of smoked glass, a bottle of green sea-glass, a clay urn, a lacquered box, a simple corked vial. Each bore a label in a fine, upright hand. Each label bore a name.

Casper walked down the central aisle slowly, reading. *Anka Sorel. Anka Sorel. Anka Sorel.* Dozens of vessels for a single person—one for each year of her life, perhaps, or each season, each remembered room. Beside them: *Davor Ilic. Davor Ilic. Davor Ilic.* The names repeated down the rows like stitches in cloth, whole lives seamed into glass.

"This is where they went," she said quietly. "Before the erasure. Everyone brought their memories here."

"Not all of them." Teodor had stopped at a shelf near the door. His voice had gone strange. "Look at the dust lines."

She looked. Every vessel sat in a niche sized precisely to it—but not every niche was filled. Where a vessel had been removed, a clean circle showed in the dust, sharp-edged, recent. And where a vessel had been removed *long* ago, there was only dust, undisturbed, filling the space like snow.

Half-empty. No—she counted a row, then another—more than half. Two-thirds empty, and the emptiness had a pattern to it. The oldest dust-filled gaps clustered low, near the floor. The fresh circles clustered high, within easy reach of a standing figure. Something had been eating from the top down, taking what was convenient, working its way lower as it starved.

"Something's been feeding here," Teodor said. "Recently. Days, maybe."

"The Surveyor's faction." Casper touched an empty niche. The glass circle in the dust was still crisp. "Or the gods themselves, come home to the pantry."

They walked deeper. The hall branched into reading rooms, and the reading rooms into stacks, and everywhere the same architecture of loss: names in thousands, vessels in hundreds, and between them the negative space of everything taken. In one chamber a ladder still leaned against the shelving where someone—or something—had left it mid-harvest. In another, a vessel lay shattered on the floor, and around the shards the air itself seemed thinner, flatter, a silence shaped exactly like a missing sound.

Casper knelt by the broken vessel. A label clung to one shard: *Mira Kell, winter of the twin floods.* She could not have said why, but she cupped the largest piece in both hands and held it a moment, as if the memory might pool back into the glass if given time. Nothing came. Whatever Mira Kell had known about the twin floods was somewhere inside something else now, digested, converted into divinity or appetite or both.

"We should take stock," she said, standing, because taking stock was what she knew how to do, because ledgers were the only prayer she trusted. "Count what remains. If we can establish what's been taken and when—"

"Casper." Teodor's hand closed gently on her arm. "Turn around."

At the end of the aisle stood a ghost. He was an old man, or had the shape of one, dressed in the style of forty years past—a clerk's grey coat, ink-stained cuffs. Unlike the others in the city he did not bow. He looked at her the way a librarian looks at a stranger found wandering the stacks after hours: wary, proprietary, and desperately hopeful.

"You're the heir," he said. His voice was dry as page-turning. "You've come about the withdrawals."

*Withdrawals.* The word went through her like a splinter. "You keep records of them?"

"I kept records of everything." He gestured with a hand that flickered slightly at the edges, like a candle guttering. "Forty-one years I was under-archivist for the western districts. When the country was folded away, those of us on duty stayed on duty. Someone must mind the collection." His eyes moved to the empty niches, and his face did something complicated and terrible—grief and shame braided together. "I could not stop them. You understand. I am a record myself now. I cannot even bar a door."

"How many?" Casper asked. "How many are gone?"

He led them to a reading room where a great ledger lay open on a table, its columns ruled in red and black. The ghost's own entries marched down the pages in a hand identical to the labels on the vessels—of course it was; he had written them all. Dates. Niche numbers. Names. And in the final column, a notation Casper recognized with a lurch, because it was guild shorthand, guild cipher, the very marks she used daily in Corvane to record trades of bottled storms and borrowed years.

*Withdrawn,* said the column. *Withdrawn. Withdrawn. Consumed on premises.*

"He uses our figures," she whispered.

"Your guild taught him," the ghost said. "Or your guild learned from whoever emptied us. The arithmetic is the same either way." He turned the pages backward, faster and faster, dates retreating through years. "Small withdrawals at first. One vessel a week, then two. Then the prayers started being answered outside the border, and the withdrawals became a flood. Your fisherwoman's son—" he glanced up sharply at her expression, "—yes, we know of him. The dead do not stay dead politely here. He was drawn from a vessel in row nine. What answered that mother's prayer was the memory of her boy, wearing the shape of him. The boy himself—" the clerk's voice caught, a sound like tearing paper, "—the boy himself may still be in his glass. Or may be part of what walks. We cannot tell anymore. That is the cruelty of it. When a memory is eaten, no one can miss it, because missing requires remembering."

Casper gripped the table's edge. The wood was real under her hands, solid, and she anchored herself to it.

"Show me the rate," she said. "Recent days."

The ghost turned to the last pages. The entries crowded together now, several per day, sometimes a dozen, the red ink of the withdrawal column thickening until whole rows were red. The final entry was dated yesterday.

"And the vessels that remain," Casper said. "The full ones. How many?"

"Eleven thousand, four hundred and six." He said it the way a man says the number of his children. "Once there were ninety million."

Ninety million memories. Ninety million mornings and quarrels and songs and recipes and griefs, shelved in glass by a people who had believed—had *trusted*—that putting something in an archive meant keeping it safe. Casper thought of her own guild's vaults in Corvane, the bottled storms racked in their thousands, and felt the floor tilt beneath her certainties. She had spent her life guarding other people's impossible things. She had never once asked what the vaults were *for*.

Teodor had drifted to the window. "Your reverence," he said quietly—he had begun calling the ghost that, some instinct of his profession, one gravedigger honoring another's charge—"who comes to feed? Have you seen it?"

"No one sees it." The clerk smoothed his coat, a gesture so habitually human it hurt. "We find the niches open and the glass empty and the air tasting of... satisfaction. Once, only once, I heard it in the far stacks. It was humming. A child's counting song. It hummed with the voice of whoever it had eaten last." He looked at Casper, and his flickering edges steadied, and for a moment he was not a ghost but a man asking a favor of the last person alive who might grant it. "Heir. You carry the inheritance. While you stand in this building, perhaps it will not dare to feed. Perhaps you are a lock, and it must wait for you to leave."

"Then I shouldn't leave," Casper said.

"You must," the ghost said simply. "Because there is something worse than the feeding, and you need to see it before you decide anything at all."

* * *

He took them down. Stairs behind the reading rooms, descending in tight spirals, the air cooling with every turn until their breath showed faintly. The niches down here were different—larger vessels, black glass, labels not of names but of dates. Single dates. The night of the erasure. The hour of it.

"The national memory," the clerk said, his voice echoing strangely. "When the Council resolved to fold the country away rather than let it be conquered, the law required a master record. One vessel holding the memory of the erasure itself: how it was done, by whom, with what words. So that someday, if the country returned, it would return *knowing itself*." He stopped before a niche larger than any above, curtained in dust-heavy velvet. "It is the keystone. Every other memory in this archive is indexed against it. Lose the index, and the collection becomes ninety million unrelated fragments. Lose the keystone, and no one—not the heir, not the gods, not the door itself—can ever say what Vessany actually *was*."

His hand hovered over the velvet but did not touch it. Ghosts, Casper understood suddenly, could not draw curtains. Only the living could.

"Is it there?" Teodor asked.

"Draw it back," the clerk said. "I cannot. Please."

Casper took hold of the velvet. Her heart was going hard. She thought absurdly of her office in Corvane, the prayer candle no one had lit, the first small wrongness that had started all of this—and then she pulled the curtain aside.

The niche was empty.

Not dusty-empty, not long-ago-empty. Empty the way a mouth is empty after a scream. The black glass vessel's outline was printed in the velvet's nap where it had rested for forty years, and the velvet itself was *warm*, and in the warm air of the vault Casper smelled again that terrible sweetness, closer now, fresher, the smell of something recently and thoroughly enjoyed.

"When?" Her voice came out flat. "When was it taken?"

The clerk's composure broke at last. He folded downward, coat and cuffs and ink-stained hands collapsing into a shape like crumpled paper, and when he spoke his voice was the smallest thing in the vast cold dark.

"Three nights ago. The same night the salt line began to sing. The same night, heir, that you crossed the border." He raised his ruined face to hers. "It waited for you. It took the keystone the moment the country had someone left to inherit it—as if your arrival told it the meal was nearly over, and it had better finish the plate."

Behind them, far above, through floors and floors of shelved and shelving-less lives, a sound drifted down the spiral stairs.

Humming. A child's counting song, in Old Vessani, sung in a voice that had belonged to someone once.

Teodor's hand found his spade. Casper's found the ledger in her satchel—her mother's ledger, older than the guild, heavier than iron. The humming paused, as if listening.

Then it began to descend.
