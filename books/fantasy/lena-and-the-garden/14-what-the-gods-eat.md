## 14. What the Gods Eat

The archive did not want to be read at night.

Casper understood this the way she understood tides and interest rates—not as superstition but as structure. The shelves breathed. She had felt it since she entered: a slow contraction and release, like lungs working under stone, as if the whole building were one enormous throat deciding whether to swallow or speak.

She sat cross-legged on the floor of the reading gallery with six ledgers open around her and a stub of candle that refused to gutter, though its wax was long spent. Ghost-light, probably. Everything here ran on something borrowed.

The ledgers were Vessany's census of memory—*the Tally of Held Things*, the spines read—and they were organized not by name but by sensation. One volume for every grief the country had ever recorded. Another for the smell of rain on particular streets. A third, thinnest, for things people remembered wrong on purpose.

She had been reading for six hours. Her eyes ached. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she would have expected them to shake by now, after what she'd found.

Because the arithmetic was simple, once you saw it. And Casper Villalobos had built her entire life on seeing arithmetic where other people saw mystery.

The erasure had not destroyed Vessany's memories. It had *banked* them.

The final entries in each ledger said so plainly, in the cramped administrative hand of clerks who knew they were writing scripture by accident. *Deposited to safekeeping.* *Held against return.* *Interest to accrue.* Forty years ago, when the border closed and the maps went blank, someone—something—had gathered up an entire nation's remembering and stored it here, on these shelves, in these crystalline drawers she could hear humming faintly behind the iron grilles.

Safekeeping. As if memories were grain against a famine.

And then, in a different ink—a brownish red that made her stomach turn—the withdrawals began.

* * *

The first withdrawal was dated eleven days after the erasure. Small. Almost polite. *One memory of a wedding, returned to the debtor.* Returned to whom? There was no one left to return anything to. The country was gone. The people who remembered it were scattered across four borders, waking with gaps they blamed on grief.

Casper turned pages faster.

The withdrawals came monthly at first, then weekly, then daily. And they were never small again. Whole seasons vanished from the record. A fishing village's hundred years of storms, gone in a single entry. The complete childhood of a girl named Ansa Mirele, age six to fourteen, withdrawn by an authority signed only with a symbol—a circle bisected by a plumb line.

She knew that symbol. She had seen it burned into the surveyor's stake beside her bedroll, three nights and a lifetime ago.

"The Surveyor," she said aloud, and the shelves shivered.

"Not him alone."

The voice came from between the stacks—an old man's voice, dry as ledger paper. Casper was on her feet with her knife out before her mind caught up, and there he stood: a ghost, translucent as breath on glass, dressed in the gray robes of a Vessani archivist. His hands were folded. His face was kind the way old institutions are kind—without any individual left inside it.

"You're the one who's been following me," she said. "Since the salt line."

"I am the Under-Tally," he said. "I keep what the shelves cannot hold. You may call me Piotr. I was junior clerk here, before." He said *before* the way other men said a dead wife's name.

"Before the erasure."

"Before the bargain." He drifted closer, and the candle flame bent toward him like a plant toward sun. "You've found the withdrawals. Good. Most readers stop at the deposits. Deposits are comforting. Withdrawals require arithmetic, and arithmetic requires admitting you were robbed."

"By the gods." She kept the knife up anyway. Habit. "The old gods didn't die with the country. They ate their way through the storage."

Piotr smiled, and it was the saddest thing she'd seen since Teodor described burying his own village's god. "Eat is your word. Theirs was *draw down*. A god, Miss Villalobos, is not a creature. It is a debt that learned to collect itself. When Vessany was erased, our gods lost their worshippers—but a debt survives its debtor. It simply finds new ones."

* * *

He showed her the rest. He did not need to walk; the archive moved around him, shelves sliding, grilles unlocking, until they stood in a rotunda Casper was certain had not existed ten minutes ago. In its center, under glass, lay the master ledger—the one book even ghosts seemed afraid to touch.

Piotr did not touch it either. He merely opened the cover from a distance, the way you might blow open a door.

"Read the covenant," he said.

Casper leaned over the glass. The script inside was Old Vessani, formal and dense, but her mother had taught her letters with map symbols and ledger marks both, and the old tongue lived in her bones somewhere below memory, below theft. She translated aloud, slowly:

*"In exchange for continuance, the Powers of Vessany accept stewardship of the national remembrance. They shall hold all held things. They shall draw upon the store only in famine, only in need, only in measure, and shall render account annually to the Office of the Under-Tally."*

"And then?"

"Then we were erased," Piotr said, "and there was no one left to render account to. No one to say *only in famine*. Only in need. Only in measure." He gestured at the master ledger, and the pages turned themselves, decades of entries scrolling past like floodwater. "Forty years of famine, Miss Villalobos. The gods starved politely for perhaps five of them. Then less politely. Then not at all."

The entries blurred past. Withdrawals in the hundreds. The thousands. Signatures she recognized now—the Surveyor's plumb-line circle, yes, but others too: a coiled rope, a broken bell, a hand holding a key upside-down. Gods she'd have called minor once. Hedge-gods, road-gods, the small hungry powers that lived in thresholds and wells. All of them feeding.

"On what?" she asked, though she knew. She had known since the fisherwoman's drowned son smiled at her with wet wrong lips.

"On the store first. The store was vast—it was a whole country's remembering, forty years of it should have lasted centuries at ration. But gods do not ration." Piotr's voice went flat. "They gorged. And when the store ran thin—and it has run thin, look—" he stopped the pages at the last decade, and the entries changed character entirely, "—they began to eat from the living."

Casper looked. The final pages of the master ledger were no longer records of withdrawals from shelves. They were records of *prayers answered*.

Every one itemized.

*A prayer for safe sea passage, granted. Cost to petitioner: the smell of his mother's kitchen, one unit, non-recoverable.*

*A prayer for a sick child's fever to break, granted. Cost: the petitioner's memory of her own wedding day, partial, the dancing only.*

*A prayer for a drowned son to come back, granted. Cost: —*

That entry was unfinished. Wet ink, though the ledger was older than her grandmother. The archive was still writing itself. Somewhere out in the world, right now, a mother was getting exactly what she prayed for, and the bill was being drafted in real time.

"Every miracle," Casper said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Every answered prayer since the erasure. It's not grace. It's a withdrawal."

"A purchase," Pior corrected gently. "On credit. The gods extended the terms because desperate petitioners make excellent collateral. They take the payment from the part of you that won't be missed immediately—the taste of a summer, the sound of a dead friend laughing, the reason you flinch at certain doors. Small denominations. That is how they've fed so long without being caught. No one misses a single coin."

"But everyone's purse empties eventually."

"Now you are doing arithmetic." He almost sounded proud.

Casper sank onto the reading bench, the master ledger's glass cold through her clothes. She thought about the impossible trades that had spiked in Corvane—the bottled storms, the borrowed years, Oren Sable's nervous summons. She thought about the Guild of Impossible Things, which had brokered strange mercies for two hundred years without ever asking, precisely, where the stock came from.

She thought about herself, balancing accounts in a harbor office, content in her invisibility, while upstairs the guild traded in other people's missing pieces and called it commerce.

"How much does the guild know?" she asked.

Piotr's ghost-form flickered—the closest a dead man comes to flinching. "Your guildmaster's predecessors signed the collection charter. Corvane was founded by Vessani exiles, Miss Villalobos. Your guild is our bank's surviving branch. Every impossible thing it has ever traded was drawn, one way or another, from this room." He paused. "Whether Oren Sable knows the full shape of it, I cannot say. Whether he suspects—I think a man who rises to Guildmaster always suspects."

*Oren,* she thought, *you sent me here to investigate quietly. Quietly for whom?*

But that was a question for later, and later was a country she was learning not to trust.

* * *

"What happens when the living run out?" she asked instead. "When there's nothing left to withdraw? The store is nearly empty—you said so. The prayers are getting more expensive. What's the end of this ledger?"

Piotr was quiet for a long moment. Around them the shelves hummed, that vast slow breathing, and Casper realized with a lurch that the breathing had gotten weaker since she arrived. Or she was finally hearing it honestly.

"There are two endings," he said. "The first is starvation. The gods consume the last reserves, then the last memories of the last exiles, then each other. Vessany dies a second time, permanently, and whatever is left of the Powers scatters into the world as hunger with nowhere to put itself. That ending takes perhaps a generation."

"And the second?"

"The second requires a door." He looked at her then, fully, and his kindness fell away like a robe off a shoulder, and underneath was only an old clerk's terrible precision. "A lawful opening. A way for the country to come back—not as memory but as ground, as soil, as tax rolls and harvests and living worshippers. A restored nation is an inexhaustible congregation. The gods who engineered the erasure did not do it to kill Vessany, Miss Villalobos. They did it to *age* it. To let it ripen into a hoard. And now that the hoard runs low, they want the cellar door opened so the vineyard can be replanted."

"The Surveyor's faction," she said. "Marek."

"If that is the heir's name, then yes. They will make him beloved. They will make restoration sound like mercy. And the moment a rightful inheritor opens the Door of Return from within—" Piotr spread his translucent hands, "—every Power still standing gets its congregation back, plus forty years of accrued interest, payable immediately."

"Payable by whom?"

"By whoever is standing in the doorway."

The candle finally guttered—or rather, chose to—and in the dark the shelves' breathing was very loud, and very faint, like a sleeper near the end of a long illness.

Casper sat in the black and did the only thing she knew how to do. She counted.

One country, erased. Forty years of interest. A few thousand exiles still alive, most of them old, most of them already missing pieces they'd sold in moments of grief without knowing they were selling. A store of memory down to its last shelves—she'd seen them, half-empty, dust where drawers used to sit full. And on the other side of the ledger: gods, starving, patient, and now actively shopping for an heir willing to open the door.

Her mother's handwriting named her inheritor. Marek claimed seniority. The Surveyor wanted a claimant—either claimant—formalized, crowned, marched to the threshold.

It wasn't a contest between heirs at all. It was a bidding war, and the goods were a nation, and the buyers intended to pay with the winner.

"They'll come for the remaining shelves soon," Piotr said into the dark. "Marek's people. The riot is already being organized—you can hear it in the prayers, petitions for courage, for righteous anger. The gods are funding it. Answering dozens a night." He sighed, a sound like a page turning. "They will storm this room within days. Whatever you mean to carry out of here, Miss Villalobos, you should choose it now."

Casper stood. In the dark she couldn't see the master ledger, but she could feel it, humming on its table like the smallest crystal in the hoard beneath the palace—like, she suddenly understood, the small crystal that hummed with her mother's voice.

"One more question," she said. "The withdrawals. The ones taken from the store before it ran low. Where did they go? Are the eaten memories gone forever, or—"

"Hoarded," Piotr said. "Gods do not digest, Miss Villalobos. They *keep*. A memory, once eaten, becomes part of the god's body. Every miracle ever granted is walking around out there wearing someone's childhood as skin." A pause, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to the register men use in churches and counting houses alike. "Which means if you ever wished to rob a god—"

The far doors of the archive boomed. Once. Twice. Not wind. Not ghosts.

Voices, many voices, and above them a charming, hollowed-out timbre she had heard only once before, across a border of salt, promising restoration to a cheering crowd.

Marek Villalobos had come for the shelves tonight, and he had not come alone.
