## 16. The First Claimant

He came up the salt road at dusk, walking as if the road had been laid for him alone.

Casper saw him first from the archive steps, where she had been standing with Oren's warning still lodged in her chest like a swallowed stone. The light was doing strange things in Vessany that evening—the sun setting twice, once in the west and once, faintly, in the north, where the border was being worried open—and against that doubled glow the figure on the road resolved slowly into a man in a good coat gone soft at the elbows, a traveler's satchel slung across his back, his hands raised in greeting before he was near enough for greeting to mean anything.

"Ah," said Teodor beside her, in the flat voice he used when he was being careful. "That's trouble walking."

"You know him?"

"I know the shape of him." Teodor spat, an old habit that seemed almost ceremonial here, where the ground remembered everything it was given. "God-touched. You can see it in how they hold their heads. Like they're listening to a conversation the rest of us aren't invited to."

The man stopped ten paces off and bowed—not deep, not shallow, but exactly the depth a bow should go, calibrated by someone who had practiced calibration all his life. He was perhaps forty, though the hollows under his cheekbones argued for older. His eyes were the problem. They were dark and quick and full of charm, and behind the charm was nothing at all, a room swept clean and the furniture sold.

"Casilda Villalobos," he said warmly. "Or do you prefer Casper? I've been called both, in my time. I'm told names run in families like fevers."

"Who are you?"

"Marek." He spread his hands as though presenting himself were a small gift he'd wrapped. "Marek Villalobos. Your cousin, if the ledgers are honest—and you'd know better than anyone whether they are."

The name went through her like a needle finding a vein. Villalobos. Her mother's name, her own, spoken by a stranger on the road of a country that had been erased before either of them could walk. She kept her face still. She had spent her life keeping her face still; it was the first skill the guild taught and the last one it forgave you for losing.

"There's no record of a Marek Villalobos," she said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "There wouldn't be. My line left early. Before the erasure, even—my grandfather saw the soldiers coming and didn't wait for the burning papers. He took ship from Corvane with a trunk of silver and a wife who never stopped crying, and he raised his sons abroad, and taught them one thing above all others." He smiled. It was a beautiful smile. It reached every part of his face except his eyes. "He taught them that Vessany would come back. Countries always come back, he said. Debts insist on it. And when it did, there would be an inheritance waiting, and inheritors are ranked by blood and seniority, and my branch is the elder branch."

"The ledger says otherwise."

"Does it?" He tilted his head with genuine curiosity, or something shaped so precisely like genuine curiosity that no instrument she owned could have told them apart. "May I see it?"

"No."

"Of course not. Forgive me. Where I've been traveling, people show each other things. It's a habit of the road." He glanced past her, up at the archive's pale facade, at the windows where ghost-light moved like fish under ice. "You've been inside, then. The Archive of Names. Tell me—is the third gallery still standing? The one with the marriage registers? My grandmother's name is in one of those books. I'd very much like to read it in her own hand. I don't remember what her handwriting looked like." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "I don't remember much of anything, these days. That's rather why I'm here."

Teodor shifted his weight. Casper felt the gravedigger's stillness change quality—from watchful to ready—and put a hand back without looking, touching his sleeve: *not yet.*

"What have you traded?" she asked.

Marek laughed, and the laugh was warm and easy and terrible. "Straight to business. Oh, they said you were the guild's cold one, but I confess I hoped the stories exaggerated." He turned his hands over, palms up, examining them as though they belonged to someone more interesting. "What does anyone trade? Pieces. A first kiss. The smell of rain on a particular street—I forget which street, which is rather the point. The sound of my father's voice saying my name. He died when I was nineteen and I couldn't tell you now if I grieved him or only remember having grieved him, which is a different thing entirely, isn't it? Grief with the grief taken out. Very tidy. The gods are tidy creatures, whatever else you say about them."

"You gave away your memories. Voluntarily."

"I *sold* them, which is better than having them eaten, and worse than keeping them, and cheaper than any alternative I could find." His voice stayed light, but something underneath it scraped, like a chair dragged across a floor in another room. "You've been living among gods answering prayers, heir of Vessany, and you haven't yet worked out that everyone in this country is spending themselves? The dead spend their rest. The living spend their pasts. I simply started earlier than most, and spent more freely." He looked at her then, directly, and for one unguarded half-second the charm dropped and she saw the thing beneath: a man standing in an empty house calling out to hear whether anyone would answer. "You'll understand soon. Inheritance is expensive. Ask your mother."

The words landed harder than he could have known—or exactly as hard as he intended. Casper made herself breathe.

"My mother has been dead thirty years."

"Yes," Marek said softly. "And yet."

* * *

They let him camp within sight of the archive walls, because turning him away would have meant explaining on what authority they turned anyone away, and Casper was not yet ready to test her inheritance in front of a witness who claimed a better one. Oren's enforcers watched him from the shadows with the professional boredom of men paid to be suspicious, and Marek, for his part, behaved impeccably. He built a small fire. He shared out dried fruit and honey-cakes from his satchel with a generosity that managed to be both sincere and strategic. He complimented Teodor's spade—"a working tool, none of this ceremonial nonsense, you can bury a man properly with that"—and asked after the roads east, the price of passage, the disposition of the returned dead.

And all the while, Casper noticed, he was counting.

She caught it three times before she let herself believe it. His eyes moving along the archive wall, marking windows. His fingers tapping a rhythm against his knee that matched, when she worked it out, the number of galleries visible from the fire. The way he asked about the eastern road twice, phrased differently, listening not to the answer but to the hesitation before it. He was mapping them. Not the land—them. Her habits, Teodor's loyalties, the enforcers' patrol rhythm, the gaps in their attention. He did it the way she herself did it, with the same patient tradesman's economy, and recognizing her own craft in his hands was like seeing her reflection in water that someone else had already drunk from.

"He's hollow," Teodor said that night, low, at the edge of the firelight. "I've buried god-touched men before. There's a look they get when the bargains start outweighing the man. He's past the halfway mark. Whatever's driving him now isn't greed. It's the shape greed leaves behind."

"Then what's driving him?"

"Fear, likely. Or the memory of fear—he'd have sold that too, eventually, so maybe just the dent where it used to sit." Teodor fed a stick to the flames. "Hollow men fill themselves with whatever's nearest. Right now the nearest thing is Vessany. A throne, a country, a purpose. Something to be instead of all the somethings he sold."

"And if he gets it?"

"Then he'll be a king-shaped hole with a crown on it." The old gravedigger looked at her across the fire, and his eyes, which had seen every kind of burial, were gentle. "I'm telling you this because you'll want to pity him. Pity him if you must—it's free, and gods can't tax it—but don't mistake pity for safety. Hollow men don't want what you have. They want to *be* what you have. And a man who wants to be you will dig out your insides to make room."

Across the fire, Marek was humming.

It took Casper a moment to place why the sound stopped her breath. It wasn't the tune itself—she didn't know the tune, she was almost sure she didn't know the tune—but the *manner* of it, the little rise at the end of each phrase, the way the melody leaned on its fourth note like a child leaning on a doorframe. Ilse had hummed like that. Ilse had hummed exactly like that, over her maps, in the blue hour before sleep, and Casper had not thought of it in thirty years and now could not stop thinking of it, and the horror of the moment was that she could not tell whether the song was truly hers, risen out of the country's soil to meet a returning son—or whether Marek had bought it somewhere, secondhand, from a god with a warehouse full of other people's childhoods.

"That's a Vessani lullaby," she said, when the humming paused.

"Is it?" Marek smiled at her across the flames, and his eyes were warm and empty and bottomless. "I picked it up on the road. A woman taught it to me near the salt line, or maybe I dreamed her. Everything's a bit dreamt, out here." He sang a line aloud, soft and clear, words in Old Vessani that he pronounced perfectly and understood, she would have bet her guild seal, not at all. "Pretty, isn't it? It feels like it should mean something."

"It means *sleep, the river watches for you.*"

His smile flickered—the first true thing she'd seen cross his face, and it was hunger. "You speak it."

"A little. My mother taught me letters with map symbols. Songs came with the territory."

"*Ilse,*" Marek said, tasting the name. "Yes. Aunt Ilse. The one who ran." He shook his head with something that in a whole man might have been sorrow. "My grandfather cursed her name until the day he died. Said she stole the inheritance out from under the family. Said she'd hidden it somewhere and taken the secret into the ground with her." He leaned forward, firelight carving the hollows of his face into something older and less charming. "But secrets don't stay buried in Vessany, cousin. Nothing stays buried here. I've walked through graveyards that apologize to you. So I'm going to ask you plainly, because plainness is a courtesy between heirs: what did she leave you?"

"Nothing," Casper said. "She left me a life outside this country. That was the whole of it."

"Mm." He settled back, and the charm reassembled itself over his face like ice over deep water. "Well. We'll see what the dead think of that. They're the ones who decide, aren't they? Blood counts for something, remembrance for more, and I've been collecting remembrance all the way here. Every village I passed, I sat with the old ones and listened to their stories of Vessany, and I *kept* them, cousin. Every one. I am, by my own rough count, the single largest repository of Vessany's memory currently walking and breathing." He spread his hands again, that gift-wrapping gesture, offering her the conclusion she was meant to reach. "Borrowed, yes. Bought, some of it, truthfully. But held. And the door doesn't care whose hands hold the country whole. Only that they hold it."

"The door," Casper said carefully, "isn't yours to open."

"Neither is it yours. That's rather the beauty of it." He lay back against his satchel, folding his hands on his chest like a man practicing for a finer bed. "We're family, you and I. First family this country's had in forty years. I'd hate for us to be enemies over arithmetic." His eyes closed. "Goodnight, Casper. Sleep well. The river watches for you."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat up with the ledger open on her knees, the old one, the one older than the guild, and read her mother's final entry again by the light of a candle she had not lit and did not extinguish.

*Inheritor: Casilda Villalobos, daughter of Ilse. Let the claim lie quiet. Let no one gather it. Some doors are load-bearing walls, and opening them brings down the house.*

Below it, in fresher ink that had not been there two nights ago, someone had added a single line in a hand she did not recognize—a hand elegant and starved, letters tall as gravestones:

*Seniority contested. Second claimant entered at the salt road, dusk. Witnessed.*

The Surveyor's clerks were everywhere, then. Even here. Even in her mother's book.

Casper closed the ledger and looked across the dying fire at her cousin's sleeping form, at the beautiful ruined architecture of him, at the way even unconscious he seemed to be listening for a conversation he'd sold his ticket to. She felt the pity move through her, slow and unwelcome, exactly as Teodor had predicted. He was what the gods made of people. He was the receipt her whole world was written on. Somewhere in the hoard beneath the palace—if Teodor's rumors held—there were crystals labeled with pieces of Marek Villalobos, his father's voice, his grandmother's handwriting, the smell of rain on a street he could no longer name, shelved and gleaming and waiting to be spent by things that had once been worshipped and had learned, in their long starvation, only to eat.

"Poor bastard," she whispered.

And the country, which heard everything, answered her.

It came up through her boots, through the salt in the ground, through forty years of erasure straining at its seams: a pressure behind her eyes, a warmth at the back of her skull, and then—unbidden, unstoppable—her mother's voice, singing the lullaby, *sleep, the river watches for you*, and with it a flash of blue lamplight, a kitchen table, a map half-inked, her mother's hand moving over it, and Casper gasped aloud because the memory was *moving*, was rearranging itself, was being read by something vast and patient that had just discovered where Ilse Villalobos had hidden the one thing she'd sworn to keep empty—

Her daughter's head.

Marek opened his eyes across the fire. He was smiling.

"There it is," he said softly. "There's the look I was waiting for. Welcome to the inheritance, cousin. It's already starting to collect *you*."
