## 17. Memories of Ilse

The land began reading her on the second night inside the border.

Casper knew it the way a cartographer knows when a map has begun to lie to her—not by any single wrong line but by the accumulation of small insistences, the way a coastline will drift half a degree at a time until the whole chart is a polite fiction. Vessany was not lying to her. It was *reading* her, patiently, page by page, and what it read it returned.

It started with smells.

She woke in the ruined watchtower above the river Sable with the taste of cardamom bread in her mouth—her mouth had never tasted cardamom bread; she had never liked sweet things—and the smell so thick in her nose that she sat up and checked her pack for food she did not have. The tower's broken window framed a sky going grey at the edges, and somewhere below, in the village that repaired itself while no one watched, an oven was breathing warmth into the dark.

*I baked those every name-day,* said a voice that was not a voice, that came from inside her own skull like a thought wearing someone else's slippers. *You always ate the corners first.*

"I didn't," Casper said aloud, to the empty tower, to the country. "I've never been here."

The land did not argue. It simply continued, the way water continues, finding the lowest places in her first.

* * *

The letters came next.

She had stopped to rest in what had once been a schoolhouse—the roof gone, the desks arranged in neat rows by hands that still remembered their duty—and as she traced the faded alphabet board with one finger, the memory arrived whole and uninvited:

A kitchen table scarred with knife-marks. Late afternoon light the color of weak tea. Her mother's hand closed over hers, guiding a stub of charcoal across the back of an old survey map, and Ilse Villalobos—dark-haired, sleeves rolled, ink permanently staining the creases of her knuckles—was not teaching her letters the way other mothers taught letters.

"Not A," her mother said. "This is a *peak*. See? Two slopes meeting. And this—" she drew a shallow curve, "—is a valley, lazy and wide. And this little wiggle is a river that can't make up its mind." She tapped the charcoal against the paper. "Every letter is just a landscape pretending to be small. When you can read, you can walk anywhere without moving your feet."

Small Casper—five years old, maybe six, hair in a braid coming undone—frowned at the page. "What's this one?"

Her mother hesitated. Just a breath. Then she drew two vertical strokes joined by a crossbar, and something flickered behind her eyes like a candle in a draft.

"That's H," she said. "*Halt.* Stop. It means stop." She smiled, and the smile did not reach all the way up. "Remember that one especially, my heart. Some doors you draw a big H in front of, in your mind, and you do not open them."

The memory ended. Casper found herself kneeling on the schoolhouse floor with wet cheeks and no memory of starting to cry.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at herself, at the country, at the dead woman who had taught her to read landscapes before she could read words. "Stop it," she told the room. "I know what you're doing. You're trying to make me remember you *whole* so I'll be useful. Well, I remember you in pieces, and the pieces are mine."

But the schoolhouse only creaked its empty desks, and in the corner, faint as breath, she smelled charcoal dust and cardamom.

* * *

She walked north through the afternoon, Teodor a quiet shape beside her, his spade over his shoulder like a pilgrim's staff. He had learned, in the days since the salt line, when to talk and when to let the silence carry its own weight. Now he broke it carefully.

"It happened to me too, the first week back," he said. "Not memories. Names. Every grave I ever dug started saying its name to me as I passed. Like they were introducing themselves late." He shrugged. "I figured the country was apologizing."

"For what?"

"For forgetting them first." He glanced at her sidelong. "Your mother. You don't talk about her."

"There's nothing to talk about. She died when I was nine. Fever. I have—" Casper counted, the way she counted everything, debts and distances and lies. "I have perhaps a dozen memories of her. Maybe fifteen. I used to think that was grief's doing, the way grief eats the early years. Now I think—" She stopped.

"Now you think the count is exact," Teodor said. "Counted by someone."

"Yes." The word came out smaller than she intended. "Fifteen memories, Teodor. Who keeps exactly fifteen of anything?"

They camped that night in the shell of a roadside inn, and the land read her again, and this time it did not bother with smells or letters. This time it gave her fire.

* * *

She was nine years old and awake past midnight because the house smelled wrong—smoke where there should be no smoke—and she padded down the stairs in her nightgown and saw her mother kneeling at the hearth feeding papers into the flames.

Not burning carelessly. Burning *carefully*, one document at a time, holding each page until the fire reached her fingers, watching the words go black with an attention Casper had only ever seen her give to maps.

"Mama?"

Ilse did not startle. That was the thing Casper would understand only decades later—she did not startle, because she had been listening for small feet on stairs, and she turned with her arms already open.

"Come here, my heart. Sit with me. You're not in trouble."

The firelight moved on her mother's face. There were papers stacked beside her in a leather satchel, and Casper recognized the satchel—the good one, the travel one, the one with the false bottom full of foreign coins.

"Are we going on a trip?"

"Soon." Her mother fed another page to the flames. A seal melted; Casper caught a glimpse of a double-headed bird before it curled and died. "But first we're going to play a game. A remembering game. Do you remember the garden? The one behind the house with the blue door?"

"The one with the wall you can't see over."

"That's the one. Tell me everything in it."

And Casper told her: the pear tree split by lightning, the bee-house, the stone birdbath shaped like a shell, the roses her mother never pruned because she said roses kept their own accounts—

"And the gate," her mother prompted gently. "Tell me about the gate."

"There isn't a gate. The wall doesn't have a gate."

Something crossed her mother's face then—something Casper had no name for at nine and would spend forty years learning to name. Not fear. Grief's older sibling. The look of a person setting down something heavy and knowing they will never be allowed to pick it up again.

"No," Ilse agreed softly. "There isn't a gate. Good girl. Remember that. There has never been a gate."

Outside, far off, dogs were barking in a way dogs bark when wheels are on roads at hours wheels shouldn't be on roads. Her mother stood, kissed the top of her head, and lifted her—she could still lift her, then—carrying her back up the stairs.

At the bedroom door she paused. In the dark, with the firelight from below catching only the edge of her jaw, she whispered it, so quietly Casper was never sure afterward whether she had heard it or dreamed it:

"Forget me kindly, my heart. Forget me kindly, and live long, and be nobody's heir."

Then the soldiers' wheels reached the lane, and the memory tore like wet paper, and Casper was back in the inn's cold hearth-room with Teodor's blanket around her shoulders and his worried face above hers.

"You cried out," he said. "You said *there isn't a gate* three times."

Casper stared into the dead fireplace. "Teodor. What did my mother burn?"

He was silent a moment. "You'd know better than me."

"No. That's just it. I *don't* know. I remember the burning. I remember the game. I remember the garden down to the number of bees in the bee-house. But I don't remember the house. I don't remember the street. I don't remember—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, and pressed on anyway, "—I don't remember her *face*, Teodor. I remember her hands. I remember her voice. But when I try to see her face there's just a blank, like a region no surveyor ever covered. Like a space someone went over with an eraser."

The gravedigger looked at her for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was very gentle, the voice he probably used over open graves.

"Maybe she erased herself," he said. "Some people dig their own memorials small on purpose."

* * *

The fifteenth memory came at dawn, and it was the strangest, because it was not a memory of Ilse at all. It was a memory of being *taught to forget*.

Casper woke standing. She did not remember standing. She was in the middle of a field of tall silver grass outside a village whose name the signposts had given up on, and the grass was moving though there was no wind, parting around her feet in slow spirals, and the sky was doing something at the horizon that skies should not do—a slow folding, like cloth being taken in by careful hands.

And in her head, her mother's voice, calm and instructional, the teaching voice, the letters-as-landscapes voice:

*Again, my heart. What do we keep?*

"The garden," Casper heard herself answer, in a child's voice from thirty-five years away. "The pear tree, the bee-house, the shell birdbath, the roses."

*Good. What do we give away?*

"The rest of the house. The street. The city. The—"

*Say it.*

"The country." The child's voice wavered. "Mama, I don't want to give away the country."

A pause. In the memory-quiet, Casper felt her mother's hands on her shoulders, warm and real and impossibly present, and felt the terrible tenderness in them, the tenderness of someone doing a hard thing gently because the alternative was letting it be done cruelly.

*I know,* Ilse whispered. *Neither do I. But listen to me. A country is a story a lot of people agree to tell. And some stories get so heavy that everyone carrying them gets crushed. So we are going to put ours down, just for a while, just until you're grown and safe and far away. And when you're old enough, if you still want it—*

*If I still want it, I can pick it up again.*

*That's right. And here is how you'll find it, when you're ready. I'm going to leave it in the one place no soldier can search and no god can steal.* A kiss, pressed to the crown of her head. *I'm going to leave it in the ledger.*

The silver grass bowed flat around Casper, all at once, as if the whole field had knelt.

She stood alone in the dawn with tears freezing on her cheeks and the truth assembling itself in her chest with the awful click of a lock accepting its key: her mother had not lost her memories of Vessany. She had *filed* them. Distributed them, hidden them, folded a nation into a child the way you fold a letter into quarters—and then locked most of the child's access to it, leaving only fifteen keys, fifteen memories, scattered like survey stakes across a life.

*I stole your country to save you* — no. That wasn't it yet. That was a sentence she hadn't heard yet, wouldn't hear until the hoard beneath the palace, if Teodor's instincts about the crystallized dark were right. But she felt its shape approaching, the way you feel weather two valleys off.

What she felt now was simpler and worse: *forget me kindly* had never been a request. It had been a spell. Her mother had spent her last living act not saving documents or silver, but editing her daughter—cutting Vessany out of her like a tumor, leaving just enough behind that someday, if the country ever called its heir home, the heir would be able to find the door.

Or be found by it.

"Casper?" Teodor stood at the field's edge, spade in hand, watching the grass rise slowly back around her ankles. "The grass did that for you. Not me. It's never done that for me."

"I know what it was doing." She wiped her face, took a breath, took out her notebook—the new one, the one Oren's enforcer hadn't burned with her maps—and began, with a steady hand, to sketch the field, the folding sky, the place where she had stood. Because that was what Villalobos women did with unbearable things. They charted them.

"Teodor," she said, not looking up. "Last night I dreamed of a gate that doesn't exist. My mother made me swear there was no gate in a garden wall. And this morning the land showed me a field kneeling." She finished the line she was drawing and held the notebook out to him. On the page, unbidden, her pen had drawn it: a wall, seen from within, and set into the stone a shape her hand knew better than her head did.

An archway. Closed. Drawn in the precise, loving strokes of someone who had finally, after forty years, come home to a door.

"That's the garden wall," she said quietly. "From the inside. The side I was never allowed to remember." She met Teodor's eyes. "There's a gate, Teodor. There was always a gate. And I think everything my mother hid in me—all fifteen pieces—is a key to it. Which means somewhere in this country there's an index. An archive. Something that tells me what the key opens, and what opening it costs."

Far off to the north, past the silver grass, past the villages repairing themselves in the dark, the capital waited under its immaculate ruins—and beneath it, though neither of them could know it yet, shelves upon shelves of names, half of them already eaten.

Casper closed the notebook and started walking.
