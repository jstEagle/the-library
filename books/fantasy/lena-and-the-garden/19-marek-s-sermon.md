## 19. Marek's Sermon

The plaza had been a market once. Casper could still read it in the stones—cart-grooves worn into the cobbles, the stubs of iron rings where awnings had been tied down, a fountain basin dry for forty years and now, impossibly, running again with water that tasted of rain that had fallen nowhere. The returned dead had gathered here the way water gathers: finding every low place, filling it without effort, without hurry.

They stood shoulder to shoulder now, hundreds of them, and the living exiles among them looked strange and soft by comparison, like people who had wandered onto a stage mid-performance and hadn't yet been given their parts.

Casper watched from the shadow of an arcade on the plaza's north side, Teodor beside her, his shovel wrapped in burlap and slung across his back like a rifle. Oren had not come. *I'll be more useful where they aren't looking,* he'd said, which was either strategy or cowardice, and Casper had decided she could not afford to know which.

Above the crowd, on the steps of what had been the Hall of Records, Marek Villalobos stood in the open air with no platform, no canopy, nothing between himself and the grey Vessani sky. He wore no crown. He wore his own face, hollow-eyed and handsome, and he let the silence stretch until it hurt.

Then he said, simply: "You remember the smell of bread here."

And the plaza broke.

Not into cheering—not at first—but into a sound Casper had no ledger-category for, a sound like a wound being acknowledged after forty years of being told it wasn't there. Old women wept openly. A man in a fisherman's oilskin dropped to his knees on the wet cobbles. Somewhere near the fountain a child-spirit—the same one Casper had pulled from the archive riot's edge, or one like her; they were hard to tell apart when they were this new—clapped her hands over her ears as if the sound itself might carry her off.

Marek waited it out. He was good at waiting. That was the first thing Casper noted, the cartographer in her taking bearings even as her stomach turned: he did not push the crowd. He let them arrive at him.

"Bread," he said again, when they had quieted. "Rye and caraway, from the ovens on Tallow Street. You remember it. Some of you can almost taste it." He held up one hand, palm out, an offer or a benediction. "You have been told for forty years that you are a rumor. A rounding error. A country that was subtracted." His voice carried without effort; Casper saw why a moment later—a faint shimmer in the air around him, the Surveyor's stake-driven acoustics, the god bending sound the way it bent borders. "You were told your dead went nowhere because there was no somewhere to go to."

He lowered his hand.

"I am here to tell you there is a door."

* * *

Casper had heard demagogues before. Corvane's harbor district bred them the way standing water breeds mosquitoes—men and women with borrowed convictions and excellent lungs. She had catalogued their techniques in the margins of guild ledgers the way other people collected stamps: the flattery of grievance, the enemy named early, the promise always just past the horizon of proof.

Marek used none of them. That was what frightened her.

He told stories instead. Small ones, domestic, unimpressive on their own—and she understood, watching the crowd lean in, that each story was a key cut to fit a specific lock in a specific listener. The woman in the oilskin got a story about herring runs and a son lost to a winter squall. The old men near the steps got a story about the census-takers who came before the erasure, kind and apologetic, writing down names they already knew would be unwritten. Each tale landed in one chest and no other, and each listener turned afterward to their neighbor with the same expression: *he knows. He knows what happened to me.*

"He's not guessing," Teodor murmured. His voice was gravel over deeper gravel. "Look at his eyes when he speaks. He's reading them. Like you read a map."

"Like I read a map," Casper said quietly. "Yes."

That was the horror of it, she thought. Marek was doing what she did—taking scattered fragments and assembling them into something whole and true-seeming. The difference was that she drew coastlines and he drew crowds, and his ink was other people's hunger.

"The Door of Return stands in the deep archive," Marek said, and the plaza went so quiet Casper could hear the fountain. "It has always stood there. It waited through the erasure. It waited through the famine years abroad, the camps, the ships that turned us away. It waited while our names were struck from every atlas in the world." He let that sit. Then, gently, almost tenderly: "It is tired of waiting. And so am I."

A voice from the crowd—an old man's, cracked but carrying: "Open it, then! Open it and take us home!"

"I intend to." Marek smiled, and it was a warm smile, a real smile, and Casper hated how much she wanted to believe it. "But a door is not a wall you break. A door is a covenant. It opens for one who holds Vessany whole—" he touched his own sternum, "—and it closes behind them, forever, sealing this country out of the world's memory so completely that not even the gods will be able to find the seam."

Murmurs. Confusion. Someone asked the question everyone was thinking: *then how do we go through?*

"You don't," Marek said, and the honesty of it was its own kind of theater. "Not through the door. Through the breach it leaves. When the heir opens the door from within, the country pours outward—every street, every field, every grave—back into the world where it belongs. The door closes on the one who opened it. One life, freely given, in exchange for a nation returned." He spread his arms. "One. Against all of us. Count us. Count the dead in this plaza alone. Tell me the arithmetic is not mercy."

* * *

"They'll cheer it," Casper said. Her mouth had gone dry. "They're going to cheer it."

She had known this was coming since the chained book swung shut behind her in the archive. She had said the words herself, hours ago, to Teodor, in the flat voice she used for bad entries in the ledger: *the price will be one man, and they'll call it mercy.* Knowing a thing and watching four hundred people embrace it were different economies entirely.

Below, the crowd was indeed beginning to cheer—but unevenly, in patches, like fire catching in damp wood. Some faces were lit with hope. Others had gone carefully blank, the blankness of people performing agreement while privately calculating whether they might be the one. Casper recognized that look. She had seen it in debtors' eyes across twenty years of impossible trades.

An elderly woman near the front raised her voice, thin and furious: "Who decides? Who chooses the one?"

"The inheritance decides," Marek said smoothly. "There is a lawful claimant. There may be two." His gaze lifted—not searching, precisely, but sweeping the arcades with the unhurried confidence of a man who already knew where everything was. Casper stepped back into the deeper shadow. "Let the dead weigh the claims, as our law provides. Let them choose the heir who will open the door. And let that heir stand before you and say plainly what our ancestors' law already says: that the office of inheritor is an office of sacrifice, and always has been."

"He's making it legal," Teodor breathed. "He's making it a ceremony. A coronation with a funeral built in."

"And the Surveyor will preside." Casper's hands had gone cold inside her gloves. She thought of the stake driven into the earth beside her bedroll, weeks ago, patient as a signature awaiting ink. *Safe passage,* the polite god had offered. *For one memory of your choosing.* It had never withdrawn the offer. Gods that answered prayers did not withdraw offers; they simply waited for the prayer to change shape.

On the steps, Marek was finishing. He had found his cadence now, the rhythm of call and response, and the plaza answered him in waves:

"Will you be erased again?"

**No!**

"Will your children be born nameless?"

**No!**

"Will you let one more year pass in exile?"

**NO—**

And the last roar rolled up against the Hall of Records and broke apart into something else, something Casper had heard only once before, years ago, in a harbor chapel during a storm: the sound of a congregation that has stopped listening to the sermon and started praying through it.

*Charisma laundering hunger into hope.* She had written that phrase once, in a margin, about a Corvane moneylender. She had not known she was drafting a definition for this moment.

* * *

They slipped away from the arcade while the crowd was still singing—some old Vessani harvest song, half-remembered, the verses fraying into humming where memory had been eaten. Even that decay served Marek; Casper could hear him promising, above the music, that *everything sung today will be sung whole again.*

In the side streets the mood was different. Quieter. Here the returned dead went about small errands with terrible diligence: sweeping thresholds of houses that belonged to families scattered across three foreign nations, setting tables, lighting lamps in windows whose glass had long since been carried off by scavengers. Preparing, Casper realized. Not for celebration. For arrival. They believed the door would open because they needed it to, and need, in Vessany, had become a form of law.

A ghost woman paused in her sweeping to watch them pass. Her broom moved through leaves that weren't there. "The heir who stayed away," she said—not hostile, not warm. Weighing. "He says you hold the country whole in your remembering."

"Do I?" Casper asked, before she could stop herself.

The ghost considered her with eyes like weathered paint. "Whole enough," she said at last, and resumed sweeping. "Whole is not the same as complete, child. No one's remembering is complete. That's why the door takes the whole of it. Every scrap. Nothing held back." She glanced up, and something almost gentle crossed her ruined face. "He won't tell them that part. About the scraps. Ask him sometime what happens to the person who gave everything, when there's no person left to thank."

Teodor's hand found Casper's elbow, steadying her more than guiding her. They walked on.

* * *

They took shelter in a tannery on the district's edge, its vats long dried to cracked clay basins, its roof mostly honest. Casper sat on an overturned curing rack and made herself do the work she knew how to do: she unrolled what remained of her maps—she had redrawn three since the betrayal at the salt line, working from memory and ghost testimony—and began annotating.

"Marek's rally points," she said, tapping. "The plaza today. The granary square two days ago. The well-field before that. Look at the pattern."

Teodor leaned over, smelling of loam and lamp-oil as always. "A spiral."

"A spiral tightening inward. Every gathering closer to the deep archive. He's walking them toward the door the way you walk a nervous animal toward a pen—small distances, repeated, each one ending in food and song." She inked the next ring. "By my count, three more gatherings and the outermost crowd reaches the archive doors. Then the Surveyor calls the Contest of Inheritance, the dead vote, and the whole machinery of Vessani law becomes a funnel."

"And if you refuse to stand for the contest?"

"Then Marek wins by default and opens the door himself—or tries to. His memories are borrowed, Teo. Bought piecemeal from starving gods. The door doesn't open for a collage." She set down the pen. "And if I stand and win, I'm crowned in front of four hundred grieving people who have already agreed, publicly, joyfully, that my death is a fair price. Refusing afterward would make me the villain of every story they tell for the next thousand years."

Teodor was quiet a long moment. Outside, the harvest song had ended; in its absence the fountain's water sounded very loud.

"When I buried the god of my village," he said finally, "the hardest part wasn't the digging. It was that the villagers helped me. They carried the chains. They sang while we worked. Because they wanted it done, and they wanted someone else to have done it." He looked at her with those eyes that had been changed by the grave and come back seeing differently. "Crowds don't want you to die, Casper. They want the door to open. Those are different things right up until they're the same thing, and no one ever notices the exact moment."

"That's a terrible comfort."

"I'm a gravedigger. Comfort isn't my trade. Company is."

Despite everything, something in her chest loosened a fraction. She rolled the maps, tied them, and was reaching for her coat when the light in the tannery changed.

Not dimmed—*organized*. The shadows straightened themselves like soldiers. Dust hung motionless in shafts of grey sun. And in the doorway, courteous as ever, stood the Surveyor, its outline precise as a drafted line, its stake still planted somewhere far away and yet somehow also here.

"Lady Villalobos," it said. Its voice was the sound a plumb line makes when it comes to rest. "You attended the sermon. How industrious of you. Field observation is the soul of good surveying."

"I attended a funeral," Casper said, "that hasn't admitted it yet."

"One could say the same of every border ever drawn." The god tilted its head, and though it had no face she felt it smile. "I'll come directly to the point, as one professional to another. The Contest of Inheritance convenes in six days. Your cousin has petitioned. The dead will weigh the claims. I mention this only because"—it produced, from nowhere, a folded document, and laid it on the curing rack between them with two fingers, gently, like a card left on a table—"you should know that under Vessani ghost-law, a claimant who does not appear forfeits. And a forfeited inheritance passes to the remaining claimant. Entirely. Including the part of it currently stored in your keeping."

Casper looked at the paper. She did not touch it.

"You're telling me I have to come to my own coronation-funeral or lose the right to be sacrificed."

"I'm telling you," the Surveyor said, "that the law is impartial. It was written by better people than me, long ago, for kinder purposes. I merely keep it accurate." It straightened. At the doorway, the light resumed its ordinary disorder. "Six days, Lady Villalobos. Do wear something appropriate. The dead notice these things."

And it was gone—not departed, simply *unpresent*, the way a line vanishes when you lift the pen.

Teodor stared at the space where it had been. "It wants you there."

"It wants me there," Casper agreed. She picked up the document at last, unfolded it, and read it twice. Then she laughed—one short, unamused note—and showed him the bottom of the page, where beneath the legal formulas, in handwriting she recognized with a sick lurch as Marek's, someone had added a single line:

*Sister, come argue your case. I promise you a fair hearing. I promise you better than fair. — M.V.*

"He's not afraid of the contest," she said slowly. "He's *inviting* it. Why would a man with borrowed memories invite a contest judged on remembrance?"

Teodor was silent. Then, in the voice of a man reading fresh-turned earth: "Unless the judging isn't what he thinks it is. Or unless he knows something about the door that we don't."

Outside, across the rooftops, the bells of the Hall of Records began to ring—forty years silent, ringing now, calling the city to gather, and gather, and gather, tighter and tighter, toward the door.
