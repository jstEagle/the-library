## 21. Trading with Thieves

The rain found her an hour past the salt line, and by then Casper had stopped pretending she knew where she was.

She had run the way the betrayed run—not forward so much as *away*, crashing through wet grass and hedgerow with the ledger strapped to her chest like a second heart. Her maps were gone. All of them. Forty years of careful ink, every survey she'd ever made of Corvane's harbor fog, every dream-coastline she'd charted for clients who paid in borrowed years—left in her satchel on the ground where she'd dropped it when the rope caught Teodor around the throat and dragged him into the dark. Marek's people had it now. They would read her hand the way she'd once read a client's dream: patiently, greedily, looking for the shape of what she wanted most.

So she walked without maps through country that rearranged itself behind her, and the rain came down, and she let it, because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant seeing Teodor's face over the shoulder of the man who'd sold them—the enforcer with the gray braid whose name she had never bothered to learn. That was its own kind of sin, she supposed. She had catalogued gods and ghosts and impossible debts, but she hadn't learned the name of the man walking three paces behind her for six days, and he had sold her life for whatever Marek paid in these days. Memories, probably. A handful of someone else's summer.

The road did what roads did out here now. Twice it ended in a field that hadn't been there before. Once it forked into three identical branches, all of them smelling faintly of her childhood—which was impossible, because she had almost no childhood to smell of, only fragments her mother had left behind like coins under a pillow. She chose the middle branch because choosing felt better than standing still, and the middle branch took her down into a hollow where the hedges grew wrong.

She knew it before she saw anything. The grass went silver-tipped, then black at the roots. The hedges arched overhead until they met, knitting themselves into a tunnel, and the light inside came from nowhere in particular—the way light comes in dreams, sourceless and flattering and slightly too warm. Somewhere ahead something was humming. Not a song. The hum of a thing counting.

Casper stopped at the mouth of the tunnel and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the salt line.

She had no food. She had no map. She had a ledger older than the guild, a knife, eleven coppers, and one ally, currently in enemy hands. She had walked since dawn and the country was actively hostile to being known—it rearranged itself whenever she tried to fix it, which meant her greatest skill was worse than useless here. It was bait. Every time she mapped a landmark, something *noticed*.

And hedge-gods, whatever else you could say about them, always noticed.

"All right," she said aloud, to the tunnel, to the humming, to herself. "I know you're there."

The humming stopped. The light warmed another degree.

* * *

Hedge-gods were the small change of divinity. That was how Oren had taught it, back when she was new and still believed the guild's ledgers were neutral instruments. Old gods ate prayers; hedge-gods ate *arrangements*. They lived in boundaries—the edge of a field, the seam between two parishes, the moment between sleeping and waking—and they fed on crossings. Every threshold passed was a meal. Farmers left them offerings at gateposts not out of piety but out of bookkeeping sense: pay the toll or the toll pays itself, and hedge-gods collected late fees in strange coin. A milk cow gone dry. A child who forgot his own name for a season. A wedding where everyone wept instead of sang.

Casper had never traded with one. Guild policy forbade it—*no traffic with the minor divine, their rates are ruinous*—and she had spent twenty years believing policy was wisdom rather than monopoly.

The tunnel opened into a clearing no wider than a wagon turned sideways. In the center stood a stile, old oak, worn smooth by generations of legs that had never existed, because this field had not existed either until an hour ago. Sitting on the stile was a figure that took her a long moment to parse, because it kept refusing to be parsed—a woman, then a crow, then a fencepost with knotholes like eyes, then a woman again, settling at last on a shape: an old woman in a hedger's coat, sleeves greened with moss, hands stained black as if she'd been eating berries all afternoon. Her face was pleasant and utterly unmemorable. Casper looked away and could not have described it to save her life.

"You're leaking," the god said. Its voice was dry leaves and a gate hinge.

"I'm what?"

"Leaking." It gestured with a berry-black finger at her, all of her. "Grief. Fear. You've been dripping it across four miles of my hedges. Do you know what grief does to a hedge? Makes it grow *inward*. I've had to prune." It held up a twig, snapped clean. "You owe me for the pruning."

"I don't owe you anything. I haven't crossed your boundary yet."

"Your sadness crossed it miles ago. Sadness doesn't wait at gates, girl. Sadness is a trespasser." The god smiled, and its teeth were thorns, briefly, before they were teeth again. "But I'm generous. Famous for it. Ask anyone who can still remember asking."

Casper put her hand on the ledger at her chest, more reflex than plan. "I need passage. South and east, toward the old capital. Fast, and unseen. Your kind moves things through borders. I want to hire a crossing."

"Hire." The god tasted the word. "You want to *hire* me. With what? Eleven coppers?" It laughed, and the laugh moved through the hedges like wind, every leaf shivering. "I've got coppers growing on my north side. Little copper-colored beetles. Very shiny. Coppers are nothing."

"The guild would offer—"

"The guild!" Now the laughter had edges. "The guild offers paper. Promises. *Borrowed years.* I've eaten a hundred borrowed years and they taste like IOUs, like flour cut with chalk. No. Out here we do honest trade." It leaned forward on the stile, and though its face stayed forgettable, its attention was unforgettable—a pressure, a weight, a lantern swung close. "Flesh prices only. Something real, taken whole, given up forever. That's the old rate. That's the rate your mother knew."

Casper went very still. "You knew my mother?"

"I knew everyone who ever crossed a border near Vessany. Ilse Villalobos crossed more borders than plague." The god's voice softened into something almost like respect, which frightened her more than the thorn-teeth had. "She paid full price, every time. Never haggled. Never wept about it after. That's rare. Most of them weep."

"What did she pay?"

"That's between her and me." The god spread its hands—generous, terrible. "But I'll tell you this for free, since you're leaking all over my property: she always paid in senses. Smell, mostly. Taste, once. She said memories were too dear and names were too dangerous, but a sense—" it tapped its nose— "a sense is just memory with the door left open. You lose it, and everything attached goes quiet. Not gone. Quiet. Like furniture in a house nobody visits."

Casper thought of her mother burning papers while soldiers hammered at the door, whispering *forget me kindly*. She thought: *she was paying tolls even then. Buying our passage out of the world, sense by sense.*

"And if I refuse your rate?"

"Then you walk," the god said pleasantly, "and the country walks with you, backwards, and in three days you'll be back at the salt line wondering why your feet are tired, and Marek's people will find you there, and I'll eat your fear as it happens, which is honestly my favorite vintage." It shrugged. "Or you cross my stile and pay, and be in sight of the capital by tomorrow's dusk, wrapped in a border so thick even gods with factions and armies won't smell you. My borders are good borders. Nobody crosses mine unwelcomed and nobody crosses them followed."

The rain had stopped. In the silence Casper could hear the hedges growing, a soft creaking rustle, patient as interest accruing.

She ran the trade the way she ran every trade, because that was the one discipline the fear couldn't touch. Asset: passage, speed, concealment. Cost: unknown until named. Risk: hedge-gods were famous for creative reading of terms. Mitigation: none available. Alternative: none available.

That last line was the honest one, and she wrote it in her head in her own neat hand: *no alternative.*

"Name your price," she said.

The god looked at her for a long time. Longer than any client had ever looked. It wasn't deciding what to take—she understood that suddenly, with a lurch. It was deciding what she could *spare*, the way a good moneylender never lends past the borrower's ability to bleed.

"You use your eyes for your work," it said. "Eyes are dear. Your hands—dearer still, and half of them already promised elsewhere, I can smell the ink in them. Your hearing—you listen to ghosts, girl, I can hear you listening. No." It shook its head slowly. "You barely use your nose at all. Isn't that funny? Walking through a world absolutely stuffed with information and you keep it shut like a shop on a holy day. You don't trust it. Too animal. Too much like remembering with your body." It smiled its thorn smile. "I'll take your sense of smell. Whole. Forever. Everything it's holding goes quiet with it."

*Everything it's holding.* Casper's stomach dropped before her mind caught up, and then it caught up, and it was worse.

Her mother's kitchen. Yeast and cardamom and woodsmoke—the single most complete memory she had of Ilse, because it wasn't a picture or a word but a *smell*, and smells didn't lie or blur. If the sense went, the kitchen went dark. Not deleted—quiet, the god had said, furniture in a house nobody visits—but she suspected hedge-gods drew the line between *quiet* and *gone* in exactly the place that favored hedge-gods.

"There are other senses," she said, and hated how her voice wavered. "Taste. Touch."

"Taste rides with smell; you'd be getting robbed twice and calling it one theft. And touch—" the god's berry-black fingers flexed— "touch is how you hold the ledger. I'm a thief, not a fool. I take what you can live without and miss the most. That's the honest rate. That's the rate that keeps my hedges green."

It stood, and the stile stood with it, and the whole clearing leaned in like a courtroom waiting on a verdict.

"Terms," Casper said. Her mouth was dry. "Passage south and east, to within sight of the old capital. Concealment—I cannot be tracked, smelled, sensed, or seen by Marek's people, the Surveyor's faction, or any god aligned with them. Safe arrival. Not delivered into a trap. Those are my terms."

"Accepted, accepted, all standard." The god waved a hand. "And mine: your olfactory sense, entire, irrevocable, transferable to me upon crossing the stile. No refunds, no appeals, no taking it back at the far side when you realize what you've done. Sworn on my roots."

"On your roots," Casper repeated. Hedge-gods swore on their roots the way kings swore on their blood; it was the one oath they couldn't wriggle through. She'd read that in a guild text she'd assumed was folklore. "Then I accept. Sworn on my name—Casper Villalobos, heir of Vessany."

The clearing flinched. Every leaf rattled. For a heartbeat the god's forgettable face went memorable—wide-eyed, hungry, awed—and Casper understood she had just paid a bonus she hadn't agreed to. Her name, spoken aloud in a border, was worth something out here. Worth a great deal. The god would dine on the echo of it for a season.

*Clever thief,* she thought bitterly. *And I handed you the purse.*

"Witnessed," the god breathed. "Oh, witnessed and *notarized*. Cross, then."

She put her boot on the stile. Wore smooth oak under her sole. On the other side, the field waited, ordinary-looking, silver grass bending in no wind.

"Last chance for weeping," the god offered kindly. "Most of them weep."

"I'm not most of them."

"No," it agreed, and there was something in its voice that might, in a larger and crueler god, have been pity. "You're hers."

Casper stepped over.

* * *

There was no pain. That was the obscenity of it.

She felt the crossing as a coolness passing through her, like walking through a spiderweb, and then she was standing in the silver field and the god was gone and the hedges had knit shut behind her, and she stood there breathing, waiting for the loss.

It came as an absence shaped like everything.

She breathed in, and there was nothing. Not nothing like a held breath—nothing like a room with the furniture covered in sheets. The air had no content. Rain-wet grass should have smelled like *rain-wet grass*, green and cold and alive; it smelled like the idea of air, like the word *air* printed on a page. She knelt and pressed her palm against the earth and brought it to her face and got dirt-flavored nothing, a rumor of soil, a noun without a body.

And underneath the absence, quieting even as she reached for it: the kitchen. Cardamom. Woodsmoke. Her mother's sleeves rolled to the elbow, flour to the wrists, humming. It was still *there*—she could feel the shape of it, the way you feel a tooth that isn't hurting yet—but it had gone distant, muffled, a house with the door closed, and she stood in the silver grass with her fist pressed to her mouth understanding, finally, from the inside, what every answered prayer in Vessany had cost somebody.

This. Exactly this. Some fisherwoman's son answering from the dead, some storm bottled and sold, some year borrowed against a stranger's future—every miracle was a withdrawal, and somewhere a person was kneeling in a field breathing nothing, reaching for a room that had gone quiet.

*I've been keeping the books,* she thought, *for the thieves.*

She had known it since the archive, since the half-empty shelves. Known it intellectually, the way you know a country is at war. But knowledge was ink. This was flesh. This was her mother's kitchen going dark one room at a time, and she had signed for it, sworn on her name, and the worst part—the part she would carry into whatever came next—was that she would do it again. Because it had worked. Because somewhere south and east of here she was already unfindable, already moving toward the capital inside a border thick as wool, and Teodor was alive because of it, maybe, possibly, if alive still meant anything out here.

Cost made flesh. She had spent her life pricing other people's.

She walked. The field gave onto a lane, the lane onto a ridge, and the country, for once, did not rearrange itself—it *escorted* her, she realized, the god's border wrapping her like a smuggler's tarp. Twice she saw riders on far hills and felt their attention slide off her like rain off oilcloth. Once a crow paced her for a mile, and when it looked at her, its look went through her and kept going, and it flew away bored.

Toward dusk of the next day, the land rose into hills that remembered being walls, and she came over a shoulder of chalk and saw it below: the old capital, Vessany's heart, sitting in its valley under a sky that bruised purple at the edges. Ruins in good repair. Towers with light in windows that shouldn't hold glass. And threaded through the streets, pale as smoke, the movement of the returned dead, thousands of them, going about the business of a country that had been erased and refused, now, to stay that way.

Casper sat down on the chalk and allowed herself, for exactly one minute, to grieve the kitchen. She couldn't smell the chalk dust, or the evening damp, or her own sweat-soaked clothes, or the ghost-smell of cardamom she summoned desperately, twice, three times, reaching into the quiet house where the furniture stood under its sheets. One minute. Then she closed that account, marked it *paid in full*, and opened the next.

Because below her, at the valley's edge, in a fallow field that every instinct she owned said was wrong, the ground had been disturbed. Fresh-turned earth in neat rows. And beside the rows, stakes—surveyor's stakes, a whole constellation of them—and figures moving among the mounds with the unhurried industry of people planting something.

Or burying something.

One of the mounds, she saw as the light failed, was longer than the others. Shaped like a man. And at its head, someone had planted a marker, and even at this distance, even in the dying light, she could read the shape of the letters, because she had spent her life reading marks in the ground:

*T.*

Teodor. Buried alive as an offering—her mind supplied the rite from the archive indexes, *the interred hear the hoard, the interred feed the hoard*—and the gods were coming at dusk to collect.

Casper was moving down the slope before the minute was out, knife already in her hand, learning with every step what her mother had known at every border of her flight: that the honest rate is ruinous, and you pay it anyway, and then you pay again.
