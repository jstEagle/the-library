## 22. The Gravedigger's Grave

The hedge-god's passage had cost her the smell of rain, and she was still learning what that meant.

Casper had assumed, when she made the trade at the crossroads shrine, that losing a sense would be like losing a coin—gone from her pocket, noticed only when reached for. It was not like that. It was like a room in her house she had never known she owned, now bricked over. She kept walking into it. She bent toward flowers and found nothing. She passed a tannery and felt only the memory of revulsion, the shape of disgust without its substance. The world had gone flat in one dimension, and she had not known until it was gone how much of her mapmaking lived in her nose—the river-smell that told her where water ran underground, the iron tang of old battlefields, the sweet rot that marked a god's territory before any other sign appeared.

Now she had to find Teodor by thinking alone. It was slower. It was worse.

She had followed Marek's people south from the salt line for two days, keeping to the drainage ditches and hedgerows, sleeping by day in the hollows where Vessany's unraveling border threw shadows even at noon. Twice she had seen them pass on the road: six riders and a cart, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had sold their route to themselves. They had taken Teodor at dusk outside the village of Hallowmere—or what Hallowmere had become, its church leaning at an angle no architect intended, its bell ringing sometimes when there was no wind and no rope.

The cart had carried a spade, a coil of rope, and a long wicker basket shaped like a coffin but open at both ends, which Casper had not understood until she thought about it for a very long time and wished she hadn't.

*They don't kill him,* she told herself, walking the ditch-line in the grey hour before dawn. *They need him. He knows things about the inheritance they can't get any other way.* But she remembered the look of the god-touched dead she'd interviewed in Corvane, the drowned boy who smiled wrong, and she knew that *need* was a word gods and their servants used very loosely. A man could be needed and used up in the same breath.

On the third morning she found the offering ground.

She smelled nothing—that faculty was spent—but she saw it: a field behind Hallowmere's leaning church where the grass grew in a perfect circle, greener than everything around it, green the way fever is bright. In the center of the circle stood a low mound of fresh-turned earth, and around the mound, driven into the soil at even intervals, were nine stakes of white wood, each hung with something small: a button, a baby's shoe, a wedding ring, a lock of hair tied with red thread. Offerings. Not to the buried man—to whatever was being asked to accept him.

Casper crouched at the circle's edge and did not cross it.

She had grown up on ledgers. She knew an account when she saw one. This was a payment in progress—a life deposited against some debt, left to mature in the dark. And she knew, with the cold certainty of a woman who had spent twenty years pricing impossible things, that if she dug him out before the debt cleared, the creditor would come looking for restitution. Creditors always did. She had been one herself.

*Then let them come,* said a voice in her that sounded like her mother and therefore could not be trusted, and she stood up, and stepped over the white stakes, and knelt in the too-green grass, and began to dig with her hands because she had lost her pack with her maps and her spade both.

The earth came away easily. Too easily. It was warm, as if something beneath it were breathing, and it smelled of nothing at all, which frightened her more than any stench would have. She dug past the first foot of soil, past the second. Her nails split. Her shoulders burned. Somewhere in the leaning church behind her, the bell rang once, softly, like a throat being cleared.

Three feet down, her fingers struck wicker.

* * *

He was alive. That was the first horror—that he was alive, that they had known he would be, that the basket was built with air-holes drilled along its sides in neat decorative rows, little rosettes of breathing holes, craftsmanship in the service of cruelty. Casper tore the wicker away from his face and saw him lying in the dark with his eyes closed and his chest moving, slow and deep, the breathing of a man not asleep but somewhere else entirely.

"Teodor." She got her hands under his arms and hauled. He was heavy with wet earth, heavier than a man should be, as if the ground had already begun to claim its weight of him. "Teodor, wake up. Wake up and help me or I swear I'll leave you here."

His eyes opened.

That was the second horror. They opened all at once, no blinking, no squinting against the dawn, and they were not the eyes she remembered. Teodor's eyes had been the color of pond water, tired and kind and a little amused, the eyes of a man who had dug so many graves he'd stopped counting whose. These eyes were dark all the way through, pupil and iris and white alike, and they moved—not looking at her but *listening*, she realized, tracking sounds she couldn't hear, flicking left and right like a man reading a page written in the air.

"Casper Villalobos," he said. His voice was hoarse but level. "You shouldn't have paid for passage. You should have paid for speed."

"I'm glad to see you too." She dragged him the last arm's length out of the hole and he lay gasping on the grass, mud-black, shaking. She checked him with her hands the way she'd check a damaged ledger—ribs intact, limbs whole, pulse present but strange, beating in long slow swells like a tide rather than the quick stutter of a heart. "Can you stand? We have to be off this field before—"

"They'll know," Teodor said. "Not the riders. The thing they paid. It knows its deposit has been withdrawn." He sat up in one motion, unnervingly smooth, and looked at the nine stakes with their buttons and rings and threads of hair. "Nine offerings. One for each night they meant to leave me. They only needed three, but Marek's people are thorough. Thoroughness is cheap when you're spending someone else."

"What did they pay for? What did they want from you?"

"To ask you questions." He turned those dark listening eyes on her, and she fought the urge to step back. "When you came digging. What you'd say. Whether you'd come at all. I didn't answer them. I want that understood—I didn't answer. But I heard everything they asked, down there, through the ground. Sound travels strangely in a grave. It travels *sideways*." He smiled, and it was almost his old smile, and almost wasn't. "You came anyway. Good. I had money on you coming."

"Teodor." She took his face in her muddy hands and made him look at her. "What did they do to you?"

For a moment he was silent, and the bell in the church rang again, and far off across the fields a dog began to bark and then stopped mid-bark, as if it had forgotten why.

"They gave me back my hearing," he said quietly. "All of it. Every sound I ever failed to hear."

* * *

He walked well enough, once he'd been fed—she had hard bread and a heel of cheese in her coat, and he ate like a man breaking a fast measured in years, though it had been two days. They went north along the ditch-line, away from Hallowmere, away from the salt line, toward the hills where the old capital's ruins were supposed to be, though *supposed to* was doing heavy work in a country that rearranged itself nightly. Teodor set the pace and Casper let him, partly because he seemed to know where he was going and partly because watching him walk made her afraid to interrupt.

He walked like a surveyor. That was the only word for it. His head turned at intervals, precise as a compass needle, marking things. Once he stopped abruptly beside a stone wall gone to ruin and put his palm flat against it, frowning, and said, "This wall remembers being a mill. There was a fire. A girl died in it, and her name was Ansa, and her mother prayed every year on the anniversary for forty years, and then the country was erased and the prayer had nowhere to go, so it just—" he tapped the stone— "*stayed.*"

"You can hear that?"

"I can hear where prayers pooled." He resumed walking. "And I can hear where they were drunk. Eaten. Every miracle since the erasure has been paid for out of somebody's stored remembering, and remembering leaves a residue when it's taken, the way wine leaves a stain in a cup. I can follow the stains." He glanced back at her, and for a moment the old tired kindness surfaced in the dark of his eyes, and it was worse than the darkness. "I'm sorry, Casper. You dug me out of a grave and got a dowsing rod. That's a bad trade. I'd renegotiate if I could."

"Who do you hear right now?" she asked, because she had to know, and because asking was better than imagining.

Teodor stopped. He stood very still in the middle of the abandoned road, head tilted, and the morning was so quiet Casper could hear her own blood in her ears, and then he said, in a voice gone carefully flat:

"There's a hoard under the palace. Under the old capital. I've been able to feel it since I woke up—it's the biggest stain in the country, a lake of it, thousands of memories packed together like salt fish in a barrel. And underneath the lake, at the bottom, there's one voice that isn't like the others. It's been calling since I came out of the ground. It's been calling your name."

The road tilted. Or she did. She put her hand against the ruined wall—the mill wall, Ansa's wall—and waited for the world to steady.

"That's not possible," she said. "My mother died in Corvany. On the flight. I was nine. I held her hand while—"

"I know what you remember," Teodor said gently. "I also know what I hear. And what I hear is a voice under the palace saying *Casper, Casper* in the accent of south Vessany, the accent you'd have had, if you'd stayed." He hesitated, and then, gravedigger to the end, he chose honesty over mercy: "It might not be her. Voices pool down there too. The gods mix what they eat. It might be a thousand mothers blended into one call. But it says your name, and it says it in her cadence, and I think you should decide soon whether we're going to find out."

Casper looked north, toward the hills, toward the ruins she had fled from and been fleeing toward ever since, and felt the ledger of her life open to a page she had never authorized.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a shepherd's cot, walls half-standing, roof open to a sky full of stars that Casper could not quite trust—some of them twinkled in rhythm, like breathing. She built a small fire. Teodor sat close to it but did not seem warmed by it; he sat the way a man sits who has recently been cold in a way weather cannot fix.

"Tell me about down there," she said. "In the grave. What happened to you?"

He was quiet a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark, faintly, a hymn was being sung in Old Vessani by voices that had no throats, and Teodor's head tracked it without his permission, following it across the sky like a man watching a bird.

"When I was young," he said finally, "I told you I buried my village's god. I told you the truth, but not all of it. The truth is that it asked me to dig its grave myself. It said a god buried by its own priest stays dead honest, and a god buried by strangers comes back crooked. So I dug. I laid it down. I filled the earth in over it, and I listened to it singing while I worked, and I did not stop, and when it was done I went home and slept for two days and woke up free." He turned his hands over, studying the palms as if expecting to find the blisters still there. "Free for thirty years. Then your country started bleeding back into the world, and everything buried near it started bleeding too. My god's grave is inside the border, Casper. Has been all along. I've been feeling it wake up for weeks, like a tooth coming back from the dead. That's why I was on that road. That's why they caught me. I was walking toward my own burial site to see if the thing I killed was still dead."

"And now?"

"Now I've been in a grave myself, and graves talk to each other." He smiled, thin and terrible. "I know what's buried in this country. All of it. Every god, every debt, every hoard. It's all one conversation underground, and I've been invited in." He looked at her across the fire, and the flames reflected in those dark eyes without warming them. "Which brings us to the part you're not going to like."

"I haven't liked anything since chapter one of this journey. Go on."

"The thing they buried me to buy. The creditor. It wasn't one of the old gods. It was a *collector*—a servant-thing, a clerk of the hungry ones. And while I was down there, it talked to me, because it thought I was already its property. It talked about inventory." His voice dropped. "It talked about a crystal, Casper. A small one, in the hoard under the palace, kept apart from the rest, labeled in the old script. It said the label reads *ILSE*. It said that crystal is the reason the Surveyor's people wanted you found. Not the throne. Not the door. They want to trade your mother's voice to you for your cooperation, and they wanted to know, before they offered, whether you'd dig for family." He paused. "I told them nothing. But I'm telling you, because you'll make better choices knowing than not knowing, and because I owe you a life, and I pay my debts. Even the ones I hate paying."

The fire burned. The starless patch above the cot breathed in and out. Casper sat with her mother's name in her lap like something hot, and thought: *they will use her against me, and it will work, and they know it will work.*

"We go to the palace," she said at last.

"I hoped you'd say that." Teodor lay back against the wall, and just before sleep took him, he added, almost idly, the way a man mentions weather: "Also, the collector put a mark on me while I was down there. Ownership mark. It'll come to collect eventually—we have time, but not a lot. And Casper?" His eyes were already closing. "When we find that crystal—if we find it—you may have to choose between her voice and the country's freedom. Stains that big don't come out separately."

She fed the fire and did not sleep, and all night the buried things of Vessany sang to her gravedigger, and he hummed along in a language he had never learned, and the stars overhead kept time like patient hearts.
