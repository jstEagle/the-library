## 23. The Hoard Beneath the Palace

They went down before dawn, because Teodor said the dead slept shallowest then and the gods—whatever the collectors were now—kept their deepest hours the way misers kept theirs: alone, counting.

The palace of Vessany had no name anymore. Its name had been eaten with everything else, and so it stood in the capital's drowned heart like a sentence with its verb removed. Casper had mapped its silhouette from three ridges away, working by starlight and memory-logic, and even unfinished the shape was wrong in a way that made her teeth ache. The towers leaned inward. Not fallen—inclined, attentive, like listeners bent over a table.

"It hears us coming," she said.

"Everything here hears us coming." Teodor adjusted the lantern on his shoulder. The light through its horn panes came out the color of old honey, and where it touched the palace's outer wall, the stone seemed to lean into it, grateful. "That's not the same as minding."

The gates stood open. Of course they did. A country that had been erased from every map forty years ago had no use for locked doors; nothing left inside that anyone could steal, except what they'd come to steal.

Inside, the palace was immaculate. That was the horror of Vessany, Casper was learning—the ruins were never ruined. Dust lay in perfect unbroken sheets across the throne room floor, undisturbed for four decades, and yet the banisters gleamed, the windows were whole, the moths that should have eaten the tapestries had died mid-flight and hung there like punctuation. The praying hands of exiles had maintained this place from a thousand miles away, out of guilt, out of love, out of the terrible human need to keep something swept for people who would never come home.

And under all of it, under the marble and the dust-sheeted furniture, Casper felt the hum. It had started at the salt line and grown louder every league since, and here it was no longer a sound at all but a pressure, a fullness behind the ears, as if the whole country were a held breath.

"The larder's beneath the cellars," Teodor said. "The collector showed me before he—" He stopped. Rubbed the back of his neck, where, below the collar, Casper knew, sat the ownership mark: a small gray sigil like a surveyor's benchmark, cold to the touch. He didn't talk about it. She didn't ask. There was a mercy in not asking that she was beginning to understand was also a kind of cowardice. "Beneath the cellars. Follow the cold."

The cold led them down stairs that had been built for processions and now served only descent. Torches had burned in these brackets once; Casper's guild-trained eye read the scorch marks and dated them to the erasure itself, the last night, when someone had carried fire down into the dark and never carried it back up. Her lantern threw their shadows long ahead of them, and the shadows went down the stairs faster than they did, eager, and she chose not to think about that either.

* * *

The cellar was wine once. Now the racks held bottles of dark glass, and when Casper lifted one to the light she saw not wine but weather—a storm bottled whole, lightning coiled in it like a sleeping eel. Guild stock. Corvane trade goods. Bottled storms had been sold in her own harbor city for thirty years, and here was a cellar of them, older than any vintage she'd ever appraised, each bottle stamped with a seal she recognized down to the wax.

*The Surveyor's seal,* she thought. *His mark on goods that passed through my ledgers.*

She set the bottle back with exaggerated care, the way you set down a snake.

Teodor had stopped at the far wall, where a door stood that was not a door—it was a slab of salt, translucent as sea ice, and through it Casper could see light. Not lantern-light. A pale, sourceless glow, the color of the hour before remembering.

"That's it," Teodor said quietly. "I heard them singing through there. Thousands of them. I couldn't count past thousands." His big gravedigger's hands hung at his sides, and Casper noticed they were shaking. The man had buried his own village's god and been hunted for it; he had dug the god-touched dead out of their graves with his bare hands; and his hands were shaking at a door. "Casper. Whatever's in there—it isn't treasure. You understand? Treasure wants to be found. This wants to be *eaten.*"

"I know what a larder is, Teodor."

"No." He turned to her, and the lantern-light made hollows of his face. "You know what an archive is. What a vault is. Those are places where things are kept safe. A larder is where things are kept *to be used up.* We're standing in a kitchen, Casper. We've been standing in the kitchen since the border."

She put her hand against the salt door. It was warm. Everything else in Vessany had been cold or humming or both, but the door was warm as a sleeping animal, and it gave slightly under her palm, like skin.

It opened without being opened.

* * *

The cavern beyond was not large. That was her first thought, absurdly—that after all of it, the eating heart of a nation would fit inside a modest cellar vault, maybe sixty feet across, ribbed with natural stone. Then her eyes adjusted to the glow and she understood that the cavern was not small; it was *deep*, and the depth was filled.

Crystals. They grew from the floor and the walls and the ceiling, and hung in free-floating clusters in the air like grapes on invisible vines, and every one of them glowed faintly with that pre-dawn color, and every one of them, Casper realized with a lurch, was a memory. Whole memories. Crystallized, labeled, shelved—not shelved, *grown*, tended, arranged by some intelligence that understood preservation the way a butcher understands meat.

And the labels. She moved between the clusters as if wading into surf, reading. Each crystal bore its tag in Old Vessani script, inked onto a paper band around its waist like a jar of preserves:

*MARTA VELLIN, AGE 34 — THE WEDDING, THE STORM AFTER.*

*THE LAST HARVEST FESTIVAL OF KOLVARY STREET (COLLECTIVE).*

*ILSE VILLALOBOS — 214 ENTRIES.*

Her breath stopped.

There were thousands. Tens of thousands. The clusters receded into the dark until the glow failed, and still the labels caught her lantern: names, names, names, and beneath the names the contents, itemized with the loving precision of a guild ledger, because of course—they had been itemized by a guild ledger. By *the* guild ledger. By the first guildmaster, the Surveyor, the man deified by his own bookkeeping, who had engineered the erasure of a country so that its memory could be farmed forever, and who had used her guild—her quiet, respectable, impossible guild—as his harvesting hand.

"Casper." Teodor's voice came from somewhere behind her, hushed. "The ones on the ceiling. Look at the ceiling ones."

She looked up.

The ceiling crystals were different. Smaller, newer, brighter—and unlabeled. And inside them, instead of the slow amber drift of stored memory, things *moved*. Figures. Tiny, frantic, silhouetted against the crystal-glow like flies in amber: people, living people, walking through market squares that dissolved as they walked, greeting children who weren't there, weeping without sound. Memories taken fresh from the living. Every answered prayer since the gods woke—every drowned son returned wet and smiling, every borrowed year, every miracle—was hanging here, newly jarred, still warm.

"They're not storing the old stuff anymore," Teodor said. "The old stuff keeps. This—" he gestured at the ceiling, at the tiny struggling figures, "—this spoils. They're hungry, and the pantry's running low, so they're taking it fresh."

One of the ceiling crystals drifted lower as he spoke, curious, and Casper saw inside it a fisherwoman's face—the fisherwoman from the coast, the mother of the drowned boy—laughing at something, and even as she laughed the memory frayed at the edges like cloth wearing through, and Casper understood that somewhere above them, in the waking world, the woman was losing the laugh. Losing the reason for it. Losing, piece by piece, the son she'd prayed back, because mercy had a price and the price was being collected in installments.

Grief weaponized. Horror at mercy. She had felt it before, interviewing that woman; now she saw the machinery of it, and the machinery was beautiful, which was the worst part. The crystals caught the light and threw it back in rainbows. The cavern sang, very softly, a chord of ten thousand voices remembering.

"We burn it," Teodor said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a statement either. He was watching her. "That's why you came. That's why we came. We burn the larder, the gods starve, the prayers stop being answered, the country stops being milked." He said it fast, the way a man talks himself toward a cliff edge. "Right?"

Casper didn't answer. Because she had seen it.

Down among the floor-clusters, half-buried in a drift of smaller crystals like a pearl in grit, sat a single shard no bigger than her thumb. It was older than the others—its glow was deeper, almost bruised—and it was humming. Not the general chord of the cavern. A specific hum. A melody, fragmentary, looping, patient.

She knew the melody. She had known it her whole life without knowing she knew it, the way you know your own heartbeat. A lullaby her mother had sung while burning papers the night the soldiers came, smoke and song together, *forget me kindly*, and Casper had carried the tune for thirty years and lost the words and never once wondered where the words had gone.

Because her mother had stolen them. Ilse Villalobos, previous inheritor, refuser of doors, had scattered her daughter's memories of Vessany to hide her—and the scattered pieces had not vanished. They had been *collected*. Filed. Shelved. Here.

The label on the little crystal was in her mother's handwriting. Casper knew it the way she knew the melody—intimately, painfully, from ledgers and letters and the margins of maps. It said only:

*FOR CASPER — HOLD IN TRUST.*

"Hold in trust," Casper said aloud, and her voice cracked the chord of the cavern, and ten thousand memories shivered in their clusters like a congregation turning to look.

* * *

She knelt beside it. She told herself it was appraisal—guild habit, assess before you act—but her knees had decided before her mind did, and her hands were already cupped around the little crystal, lifting it from its bed of lesser shards, and it was warm, warmer than the salt door, warm as a cheek, and the humming climbed into her palms and up the bones of her arms like water finding its level.

Inside, she could see her. Not clearly—the crystal showed memory the way fog shows a garden—but a figure at a desk, writing, the particular forward hunch of Ilse Villalobos's shoulders, the pen moving in that handwriting, and around the figure the amber light pulsed in time with the lullaby, and Casper understood that this was not a recording. It was a *living* piece of her mother. The gods ate memories, but they preserved them too, perfectly, eternally, the way amber preserves the fly—and somewhere in this shard was the sound of Ilse's voice saying Casper's name, and the smell of the ink she'd used, and whatever she had looked like on the ordinary days, the unburned days, the days Casper's stolen childhood had been robbed of twice: once by her mother's mercy, and once by the gods' appetite.

Take it, said every nerve she had. Take it and run. Let Marek have his throne-memory, let the Surveyor farm his fields, let the door stay shut and the country stay devoured—you are one woman, you never asked for any of this, and here, here is the only inheritance you ever actually wanted, small enough to close your hand around.

Her hand closed around it.

The cavern went silent.

Not quiet—silent, the silence of a held breath, of ten thousand memories listening, and in the silence Casper heard, very distinctly, from everywhere and nowhere, a voice that was not her mother's say:

*Inventory discrepancy.*

Then the lights began to go out.

Not all at once. In sequence, cluster by cluster, from the far dark toward the center, like a house shutting its windows one by one against a storm, and with each extinguished cluster Casper felt the hum drop a note, and the dropping notes formed a descending scale, and the scale was not music. It was a countdown.

"Teodor." She was on her feet, the crystal pressed to her sternum, her other hand finding his sleeve in the failing light. "Teodor, we've tripped something."

He was already moving, hauling her toward the salt door, which was no longer warm. It was cooling with the rest, stiffening, going opaque, going *closed*. Beyond it the cellar waited in darkness, and beyond the cellar the palace leaned its listening towers, and beyond the palace—

The last cluster before the door flickered, and in its light Casper saw, etched into the cavern floor in letters of condensed frost, fresh, still forming, a line of Old Vessani script. She read it in the half-second Teodor's shoulder hit the salt door and the door remembered how to be a wall.

It said: *THE HEIR HAS COME TO COLLECT.*

The door gave. Cold air, cellar air, the smell of bottled storms. Behind them the cavern sealed itself dark, and in the dark the descending scale reached its final note and stopped, and the silence that followed was worse than the humming, because the humming had been storage, and this was attention.

Teodor pulled her up the cellar stairs two at a time, lantern swinging wild, and neither of them spoke until they were out under the leaning towers and the indifferent stars, hearts slamming, the little crystal beating its lullaby against Casper's chest like a second heart.

"He knows," she managed. "The Surveyor knows I'm here."

"No." Teodor bent double, hands on knees, breathing like a bellows. When he looked up, his face in the lantern-light was gray, and his hand had gone unconsciously to the ownership mark on his neck. "Worse. *They* know. All of them. Every starving thing in this country just felt you pick up a piece of the hoard." He swallowed. "Casper—they're not going to send collectors anymore."

Above the palace, very high, very far, something that had been a constellation began to move against the sky.

"They're coming themselves."
