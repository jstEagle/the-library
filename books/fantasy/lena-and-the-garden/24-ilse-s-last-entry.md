## 24. Ilse's Last Entry

The crystal was no larger than a plum.

Casper held it cupped in both palms, the way one holds water over a long thirst, afraid to drink and afraid to spill. Around her, the larder of the gods went on humming its terrible chord—ten thousand crystallized memories racked in the dark like wine bottles in a cellar built by something that had never been thirsty for anything but remembering. Teodor stood a few paces off with his lantern turned low, giving her the privacy of dimness.

"You don't have to," he said. "Not tonight. Not ever."

"She waited forty years to say it," Casper answered. "The least I can do is listen."

She had found it an hour ago, or it had found her—she still wasn't sure which way such things worked. She had walked the aisles of the hoard reading labels by touch and lamplight, VESSANY after VESSANY after VESSANY, whole districts of a dead country shelved like grain against famine, and then, in a crook of stone where someone had tucked it deliberately, carelessly, the way a mother tucks a note into a lunch pail: a small crystal, unlabeled, warm.

Warm. In a cavern where everything else was cold as held breath.

There was no mechanism she could find, no catch, no seam. But when she brought it near her lips, near the breath of her, it brightened—faintly, like a candle recognizing a face—and she understood that this too was a ledger, and ledgers opened for their keepers.

She pressed it to her forehead, the way her mother had once pressed cool hands there when fever came.

* * *

The world folded inward.

She stood in a kitchen she did not remember and knew absolutely: low ceiling, blue-painted beams, a window over the basin looking out on a garden gone wild with something flowering white. Rain on the roof—not falling so much as being remembered, each drop placed with care. And at the table, sorting dried beans into piles with the methodical grace of a woman balancing accounts, sat Ilse Villalobos.

Not the Ilse of Casper's surviving memories—that woman was mostly hands and hemlines, a voice from another room, a smell of ink and bread. This Ilse looked up, and saw her, and smiled the way people smile at someone they have missed across an ocean.

"Cas," she said. "You found it."

"You're not her." Casper heard her own voice come out flat, defensive, the voice she used across trading tables when the other party was about to lie beautifully. "This is a recording. A memory. You're—"

"I'm what your mother chose to leave you, which is nearly the same thing and not at all the same thing." Ilse set down the beans. Her hands were ink-stained at the cuticles. Casper stared at them because if she looked at the face she would come apart. "Sit down, love. It's a long entry."

"I don't sit in other people's memories."

"It isn't other people's. It's yours. It always was—I just kept it for you." A pause, and then, gently, with the terrible patience of the dead: "Sit down, Casper. I only get to say this once, and I've been saying it to an empty kitchen for forty years."

Casper sat. The chair knew her weight. That was the worst part, somehow—the chair knowing her.

* * *

"You'll want to know why," Ilse said. "Everyone wants why first. So: why."

She folded her hands on the table, and Casper saw that they shook, very slightly, the way her own hands had shaken in Oren Sable's office three weeks and one lifetime ago.

"Because I was the inheritor before you. Because I was offered the door, and I refused it, and refusing has a price, and I paid it in the only coin I had left. You."

Outside the remembered window, the white flowers moved in a wind that made no sound. Casper gripped the edge of the table.

"The Door of Return," Ilse went on, "isn't a door the way you're thinking of doors. It's a hinge between what a country was and what a country is allowed to be. When Vessany was erased—when the Surveyor's people salted every map and pulled the name out of every mouth that would let go—it didn't die. Nothing that well-loved dies. It went *inward*. It became a country with no outside, holding itself shut like a fist around forty years of its own remembering. And the fist can only be opened from inside, by someone who remembers it whole."

"You told me that story," Casper said. "When I was small. About the country that hid from the war by hiding from itself. I thought it was a bedtime tale."

"It was. Bedtime tales are how we smuggle the truth past the children's fear." Ilse's smile flickered. "I was born two streets from where you're sitting right now, in a house that no longer exists anywhere but here. I was raised on the ghost-laws, taught the archive indexes before my letters. My mother was the inheritor before me, and hers before her, back and back until the line blurs into the first person who loved this ground enough to be responsible for it. That's all inheritance is, Cas. Not blood. Love with paperwork."

Despite everything—a laugh escaped her, short and wet. "That sounds like the guild."

"Where do you think the guild learned it?" Ilse said quietly. "But we'll come to that. The point is: when the erasure came, I was twenty-six years old, and pregnant, and the door was offered to me. Open it, they said. Hold the country whole in your mind and turn the key, and Vessany steps back into the world, and you are its queen, and your child is born a princess of a restored nation."

"And you said no."

"And I said no." For the first time the recorded woman looked away, out the window at the soundless wind. "Because I'd read the index, love. I'd read the fine print under the fine print. The door doesn't open *for* the one who remembers. It opens *through* them. Everything the opener holds of Vessany pours through the hinge to rebuild the country's face—and the door closes behind, permanently, on whoever stands in it. I would have given it everything. Every street, every song, my mother's voice, my own name's history. And you—" her hand moved, unconsciously, toward the swell of a belly that wasn't there in this memory, wasn't anywhere anymore— "you weren't born yet. I couldn't hand you a crown made out of my corpse. I couldn't hand anyone that. So I ran."

"You fled north," Casper said. "I know that much. Soldiers came. You burned papers."

"I burned papers," Ilse agreed, "and I burned better than paper. Do you know what I actually stole from you, Cas? Not documents. Not heirlooms. I took your memories of this place—all of them, every one, even the ones you hadn't made yet, the ones you would have made if we'd stayed—and I scattered them. Gave some to the wind, gave some to the salt line, traded a few to hedge-gods who owed me favors, buried the rest in gardens. I unmade you as a daughter of Vessany and remade you as nobody's, from nowhere, a girl with a mother who sang songs she claimed not to know the words to."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls of the memory, rain continued its patient, artificial falling.

"I hated you for that," Casper said. Her voice came out small. "I didn't know what I was hating. All those years—you'd go quiet when I asked about before, about family, about why we moved so much. I thought you were ashamed of me. Or hiding something criminal. I built whole countries out of that silence, Mother. Whole maps of blame."

"I know." Ilse reached across the table, and though her hand passed through nothing—though Casper felt only warmth, the warmth of the crystal against her forehead, translated into the shape of forgiveness beginning—she held it there anyway. "I watched you build them. From wherever the dead watch. Every wrong map you drew of me, I read it like scripture and deserved every border. But Cas—listen to me now, because this is the part I need you to carry out of here—"

Her mother leaned forward, and her eyes were fierce, and young, and terrified, and Casper understood suddenly that this memory had been recorded in the last year of Ilse Villalobos's life, when she already knew the sickness was in her, when she had sat in this remembered kitchen night after night speaking to a daughter who might someday, somehow, find her way home.

"They will find you. The gods are starving—they've eaten the shelves half-empty, and a stored memory is thin soup compared to a living one. They will trace the inheritance to you the way water finds the low place. And when they do, they will offer you everything I refused. Restoration. Throne. Your country returned, your mother's sacrifice undone, all of it dressed up as mercy. And it will be *true*, Cas, that's the devilry of it—every word true except the cost, which they will bury in the fine print where the index keeps its teeth."

"So what do I do?" Casper whispered. "Refuse the door like you did?"

Ilse shook her head slowly. "No. That's the trap inside the trap. If you refuse, the inheritance passes to the next claimant—and there will be one; the Surveyor has been breeding claimants like a farmer breeds oxen—and someone weaker will open it, and the door will take them, and the country will return *wrong*, rebuilt out of borrowed memories, a puppet nation on strings of stolen remembering. Forever. I refused because I had a child to protect. You may not have that luxury. You may have to be braver than I was."

"Braver how?"

"I don't know." And her mother laughed—really laughed, helpless and human, wiping her eyes with an ink-stained wrist. "Forty years I've rehearsed this entry and I still don't know. That's the truth of it, love. I stole your country to save you, and I don't know if I was wise or a coward, and I've had four decades of death to decide and I still don't know. All I know is what I believe: that a country is not its borders. It's not the throne or the archive or the door. It's the *remembering*. As long as one living soul remembers Vessany honestly—incompletely, wrongly, imperfectly, but honestly—Vessany isn't erased. It's just small. And small things survive."

She stood. The kitchen began, very gently, to brighten at its edges, the blue beams going pale, the rain slowing drop by drop as the memory spent itself.

"One more thing," Ilse said, hurrying now, a woman running out of time in a room made of time. "The crystal beside mine in that crook of stone—the small cold one, unlabeled. Don't play it. Whatever it promises, whatever voice it wears—don't. I hid it there because I couldn't destroy it and wouldn't leave it where the hungry things could reach it easily. It's bait, Cas. It's the Surveyor's fishing hook, and it's been dangling in that larder for forty years waiting for exactly you."

"What's in it?"

"A voice that sounds like mine." Her mother's image was nearly gone now, just outline and light, but the voice came clear and close, right against Casper's ear, the way it had come across pillows during storms when she was small. "It will tell you the door can be opened safely. It will use my name. It will say everything you most wish were true. And it will be lying with my stolen voice, and you will want it so badly, my darling, my brave, stubborn girl—so promise me. Promise me you'll walk past it."

"I promise," Casper said, and meant it, and knew even as she meant it how heavy promises weigh when the wanting comes.

"Then that's my last entry." The kitchen was almost white now, dissolving. "Ledger closed. Account settled. Oh—Cas—one thing more, the smallest thing, the thing I actually recorded this whole memory to say—"

"Say it."

"I'm proud of you. Of the ledgers you keep. Of the maps you draw honest and blank where blank is true. Of the woman you became with nothing but my silence to raise you. You did that yourself, love. I stole your country, but you built yourself, and it's the finest work I ever saw." The voice was fading, going soft, going the way of all voices. "Forget me kindly. No—no, wait. New instruction. Overwrite the old one. *Remember me however you must.* Just live. Just—"

* * *

Casper came back to herself kneeling on the cavern floor, the crystal dark and ordinary in her hands, tears freezing at her jaw in the larder's cold.

Teodor crouched beside her without touching her, the way he did everything—present, unhurried, leaving her the dignity of her own weather. He had taken off his hat. She noticed that. He had taken off his hat, gravedigger's courtesy for the newly bereaved.

"How long?" she managed.

"An hour. Maybe less." He paused. "Your face. I've dug up men who met their mothers again, down here. It looks like that."

She laughed, or sobbed, or produced some sound that served for both, and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until the world stopped swimming. Then, because she was her mother's daughter and her mother's daughter checks:

"Teodor. The crook of stone where I found this. Is there another crystal there? Small. Cold."

He lifted his lantern and squinted back along the aisle they'd come. "There's a little one, aye. Been sitting there glowing since you started—weeping, I'd have called it, but it's got a rhythm to it. Almost like breathing. Like it knows you're awake now."

Like it knows you're awake now.

Casper rose on legs that felt borrowed. Down the aisle, in the shadowed crook of stone, the second crystal pulsed with a soft, patient light—and beneath the hum of the hoard, beneath the hunger-chord of ten thousand stolen memories, she could just barely hear it.

Singing.

A lullaby. In her mother's voice. The one about the country that hid from the war by hiding from itself—the one Ilse had always claimed not to know the words to.

Every word of it, perfect, and waiting, and warm as an open door.

Casper stood very still, her promise in one hand and her longing in the other, and somewhere far above them both, faint through forty fathoms of stone, came the sound of many feet descending the palace stairs—Marek's people, coming for the larder, coming for the crystals, coming for her.

The singing grew sweeter.

She had perhaps a minute to decide which voice to trust: the one that told the truth, or the one that sounded like love.
