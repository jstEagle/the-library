## 25. Marek Opens Negotiations

The invitation came the way invitations come in a country that has been dead for forty years: folded inside a prayer candle that burned without being lit.

Casper found it at dawn, when she crawled out of the ruin they'd made their camp—a collapsed customs house on the avenue of a capital that remembered being beautiful. The candle stood in the middle of the floor where no candle had been the night before. Its wax was white as bone, its flame perfectly still, and when she tipped it to blow it out, a slip of paper slid from the hollow like a tongue.

*Marek Villalobos requests the pleasure of his cousin's company,* it read, in handwriting so elegant it was almost an insult. *Noon. The Hall of Weights and Measures. He will come unarmed. He will bring tea.*

*He will bring tea.* As if this were Corvane, as if they were two merchants haggling over the price of bottled weather. Casper held the paper over the flame until it caught, and watched her cousin's courtesy curl into ash.

"He knows where we are," Teodor said from the doorway. He had the look of a man who slept badly in every country, including this one—especially this one. His spade leaned against the wall beside him the way another man might lean a rifle. "That should worry you more than the invitation."

"It worries me exactly as much as the invitation."

"No. You're angry, and anger feels like a plan." He came into the room, ducking under the lintel out of habit though there was nothing left above him but sky. "Anger is not a plan. I've buried enough angry people to know."

* * *

The Hall of Weights and Measures had been a long, low building once, all brass standards and stone benches where merchants tested their scales against the state's. Now it was roofless, open to a sky the color of old pewter, and yet—like everything in Vessany—it was clean. No rubble. No rot. The benches gleamed. The great standard weights sat in their niches, polished by forty years of ghostly devotion, each one engraved with its measure: ONE HUNDRED. FIFTY. TEN.

Marek sat at the far bench with a folding table before him, a porcelain service laid out on it, steam rising from three cups. Three. He had counted them coming. That was the first thing Casper noted about her cousin, and she noted everything about him now, because everything about him was a ledger entry waiting to be made.

He looked worse than he had at the contest of claims—or better, depending on how you looked. His charm was still there, worn smooth as river glass, but underneath it his face had gone translucent in places, the way paper goes when it's been handled too much. She could see the architecture of him through the skin of him. Hollow-eyed. Emptied. A man who had paid himself away in installments and kept the receipts framed.

"Cousin," he said warmly, rising. "You came."

"You said you'd be unarmed."

"I am." He spread his hands, turned slowly, a conjurer showing empty sleeves. "Though I'll confess the tea is armed. It's Vessani blackleaf. It remembers being good. Drink it and for a moment you'll remember too. Consider it my opening concession."

Teodor took the third cup himself, sniffed it, and set it down untouched. Oren arrived last, gray-coated, guild-seals quiet at his throat, and stood at the edge of the ruined hall like a man attending a funeral he disapproved of.

Marek poured. "I'll be brief, because I know how you love brevity—you write it beautifully, by the way. Your ledgers. I've read them. The Guild of Impossible Things keeps accounts the way Vessany used to keep graves: carefully, and with names." He smiled. "So let me make you an honest offer, in your own language. Numbers."

"Numbers," Casper said.

"The inheritance is indivisible in law but divisible in fact. The throne-memory—the whole of Vessany held in one head, the key that turns the Door of Return—that's the part the Surveyor wants, the part the dead want, the part worth dying for. But it isn't the only thing I want." He sipped his tea. "Here is my proposal. I take the throne-memory. I open the door. Vessany returns—not as a country, at first, but as a claim, a legal fiction with borders, which is how all countries start. And you—" he pointed the porcelain cup at her, gently, almost affectionately— "you keep your life. Your memories of your mother. Your little office in Corvane with the harbor light through the window. Your anonymity, which I know you treasure more than most people treasure their children. In exchange, you sign a renunciation. Clean, lawful, witnessed. By Vessani law—and I have the ghosts who can swear to it—your line's claim extinguishes, mine consolidates, and no one has to be eaten."

The words hung in the roofless air. Somewhere beyond the hall, the city went on maintaining itself, streets sweeping themselves, fountains remembering water.

"And if I refuse?" Casper asked.

Marek set down his cup. For just a moment the charm slipped and she saw what was underneath, and what was underneath was tired. Terribly, humanly tired. "Then the Surveyor stops negotiating through me," he said. "And starts negotiating through geography."

* * *

They walked back through the afternoon city because Casper wanted to walk, wanted the time, wanted the streets to tell her something while her mind did arithmetic. Teodor matched her stride. Oren drifted behind like weather.

"He's lying," Teodor said.

"Probably."

"Not probably. Certainly. About some of it. The renunciation would extinguish your claim, yes—but claims don't extinguish here, they *transfer*. Everything in this country transfers. Debts, names, sins. If you sign, whatever is yours goes into him, and whatever is his comes into reach of whoever holds his paper. And who holds his paper, Casper? Who emptied him piecewise? Who taught him the word *negotiation*?"

She knew. They both knew. "The Surveyor."

"The Surveyor." Teodor spat, an old habit from living countries. "I've told you what I did to my village's god. I dug its grave and I buried it and I thought that was the end of demanding. But that thing under the palace—" he shook his head. "It doesn't demand. It *invoices*. Your cousin isn't making you an offer. He's reading you a bill."

They crossed a square where a fountain played water upward in shapes that were almost letters. Ghosts gathered at its rim, thin as heat-shimmer, watching Casper pass with that reverence she had never learned to bear. *The heir who stayed away.* One of them pressed something into her hand as she passed—a dried flower, a coin, she didn't look. She couldn't keep accepting gifts from the dead. Every gift was a weight on the other side of her scale.

At the edge of the square, Oren caught up.

"You're going to refuse him," Oren said. Not a question.

"I'm going to think."

"Don't think too long. Thinking is a luxury of people whose opposition hasn't hired the landscape." He fell into step beside her, and lowered his voice, and for once the Guildmaster of Impossible Things sounded less like an institution and more like the tired clerk he had been before he was anything else. "Casper. Listen to me as the man who taught you double-entry, not as the man who lied to you about the founding charter. Take the offer."

She stopped walking.

"Take it," he repeated. "Yes, it's a trap—I've been in the trap business for thirty years, I know the smell. But consider the alternative honestly, the way you'd audit anyone else's books. If you refuse, the Surveyor tears the border open by force. You've seen what it does when it takes a memory wholesale—the salt line, the villages near the border that forgot their own names mid-sentence. Now imagine that at the scale of a nation. Imagine it reaching past Vessany. Into Vessany's neighbors. Into Corvane. Into the ledgers themselves, into the guild, into *you*, taken roughly instead of purchased." His eyes were steady and unhappy. "Marek's door opens under his control, yes. But it opens *slowly*. A controlled return is a wound stitched shut. What the Surveyor wants is the wound torn wide. Between a bad bargain and a catastrophe, a clerk takes the bargain. That's not cowardice. That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Casper said. "You taught me that every column balances. So show me the debit side of your recommendation, Oren. Because there is one, and you're not entering it."

Oren was silent for a moment. Around them the fountain wrote its almost-letters and erased them.

"If you sign," he said finally, "you lose your mother twice. Once to death, once to law. Whatever Ilse hid in you—whatever she stole to save you, whatever you've begun to dig up—renunciation won't erase it, but it will make it worthless. Dead paper. A memory with no claim attached is just grief." He met her eyes. "There's your debit. Entered. Now weigh it against everyone else's memories, everyone else's mothers, everyone else's countries, and tell me the columns balance any other way."

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She sat in the customs house with the ledger older than the guild open on her knees, her mother's handwriting facing the ceiling, and she tried to do what she had always done, which was to reduce a world to entries and trust the entries.

*Offer received: throne-memory surrendered, life retained.*
*Risk, signed route: transfer of all claims to counterparty controlled by hostile deity.*
*Risk, refused route: forcible breach, unbounded consumption.*
*Asset under consideration: one mother. Deceased. Irreplaceable.*

Her pen hovered over the page and would not come down.

It was the word *irreplaceable* that broke her method. Ledgers worked because everything in them could be replaced by something of equal value—one storm bottled for another storm, one borrowed year repaid with interest. That was the whole faith of the guild: that value moved, that nothing was singular, that with enough cleverness every loss could be offset. But her mother was not fungible. There was no second Ilse anywhere in any archive, and Marek's offer—and Oren's counsel, and even Teodor's fury—all of it rested on the assumption that a mother could be entered in a column and weighed against a country.

And the worst of it, the part that made her put down the pen, was that she could see the argument's shape and could not refute it. If she refused and the Surveyor breached the border, it would eat not only her memories of Ilse but everyone's memories of everyone. Her sacrifice would buy nothing. Refusal was only noble if it worked.

But acceptance—acceptance meant letting Marek carry Vessany whole in his head, a man already half-hollow, a man whose paper the Surveyor held. Acceptance meant trusting the fox's accountant to guard the henhouse's ledger.

Teodor found her there past midnight, or what passed for midnight in a country whose sky hadn't decided. He sat down across from her without asking, the way he did, and looked at the ledger upside down for a long time.

"Well?" she said.

"You already know what I'll say."

"Say it anyway. I need to hear it said plainly, because everyone else is saying it sideways."

He nodded slowly. "Refuse. Burn the offer. And then stop negotiating entirely—stop meeting, stop answering candles, stop letting them set the table. Every hour you spend at Marek's bench is an hour he spends learning the shape of your hesitation. These things feed on hesitation. That's what a god is, Casper. A hunger that learned patience." He leaned forward. "You asked me once why I buried my village's god instead of bargaining with it. This is why. Bargaining is how they farm us. The moment you sit down across from a hungry thing with something it wants, you've already agreed to be food—you're only arguing about the plate."

"And if refusing gets everyone eaten faster?"

"Then we fight in the open, where I can bury what dies." He said it simply, terribly, the way he said everything. "I'm a gravedigger, Casper. I'm not afraid of the work. I'm afraid of the paperwork."

Despite everything, despite the hollow night and the impossible columns, she laughed—a short, cracked sound, gone as soon as it came. Teodor almost smiled.

* * *

In the morning she sent her answer by candle.

She wrote it herself, in her best ledger hand, and she spent an hour on it, because every word was a move on a board where the board was also playing. She neither accepted nor refused. She wrote:

*Cousin—Your offer is received and entered. Before I can weigh a division of the inheritance, I require the inheritance itself to be described: itemized, witnessed, and sworn under Vessani ghost-law. I will not trade in bulk what I cannot count. Send your inventory. —C.V.*

Stalling. She knew it was stalling. Oren would call it prudent; Teodor would call it weakness; Marek would call it what it was—an opening, a crack, a door left ajar in a woman who claimed to bar all doors. But it bought time, and time was the only currency in Vessany that hadn't been debased.

The reply came within the hour, which told her the candle had never traveled anywhere at all, that Marek's people—or the Surveyor's—were close enough to watch her write.

It was not from Marek.

The paper was different—stiffer, older, ruled in faint brown lines like dried blood. The handwriting was precise, clerical, beautiful in the way that instruments are beautiful. It read:

*Inventory enclosed upon receipt of one preliminary consideration: a single memory of the undersigned's choosing, tendered in good faith, refundable upon completion of trade.*

*Choose generously. Choose soon. The border is thinner than your caution.*

*—The Surveyor*

Casper read it twice. Then a third time, and on the third reading she saw it—the thing that turned her blood to cold ink.

*Refundable upon completion of trade.*

Gods did not refund. Gods did not use commercial language, did not honor terms, did not *invoice*. She had learned that from the fisherwoman's drowned son, from the hedge-god who took her sense of smell, from every answered prayer that had ever left a bruise. Whatever had written this note understood trade the way she understood trade—intimately, legally, down to the clause.

Only one kind of creature wrote terms like that.

She looked up from the letter, out through the customs house's broken doorway, across the immaculate dead city toward the palace hill where the hoard of crystallized memories glittered in the dark like a larder, and she understood, suddenly and completely, that she had been corresponding not with a god at all—

but with a clerk.
