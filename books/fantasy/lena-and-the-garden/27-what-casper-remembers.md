## 27. What Casper Remembers

They gave her a room in the archive's undercroft because it was the only place in Vessany where the walls did not listen.

That was how the archivist-ghost, a thin woman named Sable-Handed Mirele who had died before Casper was born, explained it: *the shelves remember for everyone else, child, but down here nothing is stored, so nothing can be taken.* The room was stone and lamplight and a table scarred by two centuries of elbows, and when Casper closed its door she heard, for the first time since crossing the salt line, her own breathing and nothing else.

No ledger entries read aloud in her mother's voice. No whispers of the heir who stayed away. Just the small honest sounds of a body in a room.

She set out her tools the way she had set them out ten thousand times in Corvane: ink, three nibs, the good paper she'd been hoarding since the border, a straightedge, a compass whose needle spun uselessly here because Vessany had no north anymore—or rather, Vessany had *only* north, every direction pointing inward toward the palace and the Door of Return beneath it.

Then she sat for a long time without touching any of it.

Because here was the problem, and it was not a problem of cartography. She had been asked—asked by Oren, asked by Teodor, asked by the accumulating weight of the dead who bowed to her in the streets—to hold Vessany whole in memory. That was the price of the door. That was the inheritance. And she had come to this silent room to do the one thing no one else could do: to find out whether she *could*.

Whether there was enough of her left to open anything.

* * *

She began with what could not lie to her: other people's hands.

The guild ledgers came first. Forty years of marginalia, and once she looked for it she found Vessany everywhere in it, the way you find a dead man everywhere in his house. A trade recorded in Year 12 After Erasure: *one bottled storm, sold to a buyer from the coast that isn't there, payment refused, debt carried forward.* In the margin, in some long-dead clerk's cramped hand: *V.?* A note on shipping lanes: *the old charts show a bay here; the new charts show water; both are wrong.* And once, in her own early handwriting, when she was nineteen and knew nothing: *why does every map of this stretch of coast feel like a sentence with a word missing?*

She had been circling her country her whole life without knowing it was hers. The thought should have comforted her. It mostly made her angry—at Ilse, at Oren, at the careful machinery of forgetting that had used her blindness as its lock.

Anger was useful. Anger had ink in it. She uncapped the bottle.

The first line she drew was not a coastline. It was a song.

Her mother had sung it while grinding coffee, while mending hems, while walking Casper home through Corvane's rain with one arm around her shoulders and the other holding an umbrella that never quite covered either of them. Casper did not know its words—not really, not as language. But she knew its shape the way she knew the shape of her own name: four rising phrases, then a fall, then a phrase that repeated itself like someone checking a locked door. She hummed it now, alone in the undercroft, and wrote the melody out in notation she invented on the spot, because there was no existing notation for a thing remembered by a nine-year-old's ear.

Then, below it, she drew what the melody made her see: a road going downhill toward water. Not steeply. A kind road, a road built by people who carried things up it every day and had opinions about stairs. Terraces on the left side. On the right—

On the right, nothing. A blank. Her memory simply ended there, cut clean, and she recognized the cut. It was the same clean edge she'd found all her life at the borders of certain thoughts, the wall she'd always assumed was ordinary forgetfulness.

*I stole your country to save you.*

Ilse's crystal still sat in the inner pocket of her coat, humming faintly, patient as a banked fire. Casper did not take it out. Not yet. If she played it again she would hear her mother explain everything, and explanations were a kind of surrender—the past handed to you whole instead of earned. Tonight she wanted only what she could dig up herself. Tonight she wanted to know exactly how much of her had been taken, so that whatever she offered the door would be measured honestly.

So: a road, terraces, a blank on the right side. She marked the blank with the symbol her mother had taught her for unmapped country—a small open circle—and felt something twist in her chest, because her mother had taught her that symbol with her hands over Casper's small ones, guiding the pen, saying *never fill a blank with a guess, little wren. A blank tells the truth. A guess tells a story.*

Had Ilse known, even then, that she was teaching her daughter the grammar of the thing she'd stolen? Had every mapping lesson been a secret apology?

Casper drew the next line with a steadier hand.

* * *

The ghosts came to her in the morning, because even in a room that didn't listen, word traveled the way smell travels—under doors, through stone.

Mirele admitted them one at a time, and Casper interviewed them the way she'd interview any client bringing a dream to be mapped: gently, precisely, and with her pen moving the whole while. They were eager. That was the hard part. Forty years of erasure had left them starving to be recorded, and they crowded the doorway of the undercroft holding their memories out like beggars holding out chilblained hands.

An old ferryman named Brannoch described the harbor of Vessany's capital in obsessive detail—the number of mooring posts (nineteen), the color the water went in autumn (iron), the exact pitch of the bell on the customs house (he sang it; she notated it). His harbor matched her road-and-water sketch in every particular except one: he placed the terraces on the right side of the descending road, and the blank on the left.

"Memory flips," he said, shrugging his translucent shoulders. "We're mirrors down here, miss. Don't trust a ghost for left and right. Trust us for everything else."

A woman who had been a schoolteacher recited the names of her pupils, forty-one of them, and wept at number thirty-two, whose name she had lost, and Casper wrote *thirty-two: lost* in the margin and thought: *this is what the gods eat. This. One name out of forty-one, gone forever, and the woman will carry the gap like a missing tooth until the world ends or she does.*

A boy who looked twelve and claimed to be sixty described the market square, and the fountain in it, and the game children played around the fountain, which involved hopping the fountain's rim in patterns Casper recognized from nowhere and everywhere—from a clapping rhyme her mother had taught her, the same pattern, the same rhythm, transposed into footsteps.

"It's called *the king's fence*," the boy said.

Casper's pen stopped.

"That's not what my mother called it," she said quietly. "She called it *the queen's garden*."

The boy considered this with the gravity of the very old wearing the very young. "Maybe there were two games," he offered. "Or maybe your mother changed it. Mothers do that." He smiled, gap-toothed, sixty years deep. "They change things so the things can't be used against you."

After he left, Casper sat looking at the two names written side by side in her notes—*king's fence / queen's garden*—and understood that she was not reconstructing a country. She was reconstructing an argument between versions, and the truth was not going to be found on either side of it. The truth was in the fact of the disagreement itself. A country erased doesn't leave facts behind. It leaves *friction*—the places where memories rub against each other and spark.

She began a second sheet. At the top she wrote: *Where the tellings disagree.* Under it, the fountain. Under the fountain, a growing list.

By evening the list had forty-one entries, and eleven of them were blanks.

* * *

Teodor brought her bread and salted fish at dusk and stood in the doorway not entering, the way he did, as if rooms were graves he hadn't been paid to disturb.

"You haven't slept," he said.

"I'm working."

"You've been working for a day and a night." He set the food down beside the lamp. "The dead are asking about you. They say the heir has shut herself underground with paper. Some of them think it's a sign. They're deciding what kind."

"What kinds?"

He counted on thick fingers. "A sign she's preparing something. A sign she's hiding something. A sign she's already failed and can't say so." He looked at her across the lamplit table, at the sheets of paper fanned out like the feathers of a shot bird. "Which is it?"

Casper almost laughed. "Yes," she said.

He grunted—that sound that was half amusement, half assent, the whole of his conversational range some days—and then, instead of leaving, he lowered himself onto the floor with his back against the doorframe, a man settling in for a watch. He did this sometimes. He had done it outside her bedroll on the road, outside the cavern of the larder, outside the room where she'd dug him out of his own grave. She had stopped asking him why. She suspected the answer was that he had buried too many people to leave anyone alone in a small room with a lamp.

"The Surveyor's people are in the city," he said after a while. "Quietly. Buying. There are exiles here who'd sell their grandmother's name for a promise of passage, and the Surveyor knows their prices better than they do." A pause. "Marek's preaching tomorrow. The big square. Your cousin's found his voice."

"He never lost it. He just rented it out."

Teodor's laugh was short and real. Then: "And Oren wants to know if you'll be ready. Whatever ready means. He wouldn't say. He's Oren."

"Tell Oren I'm making a map."

"A map of what? The Surveyor's already got better maps than anyone. It *is* a map, near enough."

"No." She turned the topmost sheet toward him: the road, the terraces, the water, the open circles marking every blank. "Not a map of the land. Teodor, look at it properly. What am I actually drawing?"

He squinted. Gravedigger's eyes, patient eyes, eyes that had learned to read ground before they read faces. "Roads," he said slowly. "Water. Holes." And then his face changed, the way it had changed in the larder when he first heard the crystals singing. "You," he said. "It's you. Every hole is a place she cut you. Every line is a place you grew back."

Casper turned the sheet back around and looked at it herself, properly, for the first time.

He was right, and she had known it without knowing it, the way you know a song's shape without knowing its words. The map was not of Vessany. It was of the space where Vessany and Casper overlapped—the country as it survived inside her, scars and all. The blanks were not failures of memory. They were the record of her mother's knife. And the lines around them, dense and strange and lovingly detailed—the exact iron color of autumn water, the rhythm of a hopping game, the fall of a coffee-grinding song—those were the record of what grows back along the edge of a wound.

"Go away, Teodor," she said, but she said it softly, and he took the fish-smell of the words correctly, and left.

* * *

She worked through the second night, and somewhere in the small hours she ran out of other people's testimony and other people's hands, and there was nothing left on the table but herself.

This was the part she had been avoiding. She knew she had been avoiding it the way she knew weather coming: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper in the back of the throat.

She put down the pen. She folded her hands. She let herself remember on purpose.

Not the archive-version of remembering, not testimony, not notation. Just—the dark behind her eyelids, and her mother in it.

Ilse burning papers the night the soldiers came, feeding documents into the stove with hands that did not shake, which Casper understood now was the most frightening thing she had ever witnessed: fear so total it passed through trembling and came out the other side as calm. Ilse teaching her letters with map symbols because *letters lie, wren, but contour lines don't*. Ilse singing the four rising phrases and the fall and the phrase that checked the locked door. Ilse whispering, once, into her hair, in a voice Casper had spent thirty years pretending she didn't remember: *forget me kindly.*

She had thought that was a blessing. All these years she had thought it was a blessing, the way *sleep well* is a blessing.

It wasn't. It was an instruction. It was the last entry in a ledger of debts, and it read: *when you finally understand what I took from you, hate me gently. Hate me the way you'd hate weather. Because I would do it again, and I need you to survive doing it again too.*

Casper sat in the lamplight and cried the way she did everything—efficiently, privately, with her sleeves pushed back as though for work. Then she washed her face from the jug, dried it on her sleeve, and picked up the pen.

The last sheet. The truest one.

She drew Vessany whole—or as whole as she could make it, which was to say, as whole as a person can be. Coastline assembled from Brannoch's harbor and the ferryman's nineteen posts and her own road downhill. The capital from the schoolteacher's street-names and the boy's fountain, drawn twice, once as the king's fence and once as the queen's garden, both labeled, neither resolved. The salt border. The Door beneath the palace, marked with her mother's open-circle symbol for unmapped country, because she had never seen it and refused to pretend otherwise. And threaded through all of it, the blanks—eleven major ones, dozens minor—each one circled, each one named: *lost. taken. cut. unknown.*

When dawn came gray through the high slit of a window, she held the finished map at arm's length and judged it the way she had judged ten thousand client maps in Corvane: coldly, professionally, as a stranger would.

It was incomplete. Badly incomplete. Any guild examiner would have failed it. Half the interior was conjecture stitched to testimony stitched to a nine-year-old's ear for melody. Whole provinces existed on it only as open circles, honest holes, admissions of theft.

And it was the truest map she had ever drawn.

Because every other map she'd ever made had lied in the oldest way maps lie: by pretending to be complete. By filling coasts with confident ink and interiors with plausible guesses, by smoothing the world into something legible, something owned. This one refused. This one said, in its own drafted hand: *here is what survives of a country in the daughter it was stolen from. Here are the wounds. Here is what grew back. Here is what did not. Read it and know that the reading is partial, and that the partiality is the point.*

Her mother had filled Casper's childhood with blanks to save her. The gods had filled the world with blanks to feed themselves. Marek would fill his crowds with blanks and call it hope. Everyone, everywhere, was filling blanks—with guesses, with stories, with hunger dressed as certainty.

Casper dipped her pen one final time and wrote across the bottom of the map, in letters small and level:

*Incomplete by design. The blanks are load-bearing.*

She looked at it a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, cracked, astonished sound, alone in the listening-less dark—and found that she meant it. Incompleteness was not her failure. It was her authorship. Nobody whole had ever opened that door; only someone who knew exactly where her holes were, and loved the map anyway, could carry a country through one.

She was rolling the sheets when the floor shivered.

Not the undercroft—the whole archive above her, a long groan of shelving, a rain of dust sifting down through the ceiling stones. Then voices, many voices, shouting, and beneath them a sound she had learned on the road and hoped never to hear in a city: the wet crystalline ringing of memory being torn off its shelf.

Mirele's ghost came through the door without knocking, which she had never once done, her thin face lit blue-white with alarm.

"They're in the index hall," she said. "Hundreds of them. Living and dead both, and the living have crowbars." She pressed a hand to her transparent chest as if holding shut a wound. "Child—they've come for the name-shelves. And the ghosts are standing on the stairs to stop them."

Casper took the map, took the ledger, took the coat with her mother's crystal humming against her heart, and ran.
