## 28. Riot at the Archive

The bells of the capital began at dawn, and they were wrong.

Casper had spent three days in the archive's upper galleries, cataloguing what remained of the name-shelves, and she had learned the building's sounds the way she learned any landscape: by heart. The groan of settling stone. The whisper of ghosts moving through the reading rooms like drafts with intentions. The soft tick of the index-clocks that no longer told time but still counted something. Bells were not among them. Vessany's capital had no working bells; it had forty years of silence where bells used to be.

So when the sound rolled across the city—deep, cracked, insistent—she was already at the window before her mind finished the arithmetic of what it meant.

Below, in the great square between the archive and the ruined palace, torches were moving. Not many. Perhaps two hundred, maybe fewer, but in a country of mostly ghosts, two hundred living bodies with fire looked like an army. She could see the banners even at this distance: white cloth torn into strips, tied to staves, the way exiles marked themselves when they came home. Marek's people. His congregation. His harvest hands.

Teodor appeared in the doorway behind her, breathing hard, grave-dirt still on his cuffs from the night's work among the returned dead. "They're coming for the shelves," he said. "Word went round the camps last night. Marek preached at the salt line—he told them the archive holds enough names to crown him without contest. Told them every shelf they carry out is a vote."

"Names aren't votes." Casper was already moving, pulling her satchel closed, checking the ledger against her ribs the way other people checked for knives. "A name is everything a person was. You don't carry those out of a burning library. You stand in the door."

"There are two hundred of them."

"Then I'd better be quick."

She took the stairs three at a time, past the galleries where the half-empty shelves stood in their long ranks like teeth after a bad winter, down to the great doors of the national archive—the ones her mother must have walked through, once, carrying a child who would not remember. The doors were bronze and green with age, and they were already shuddering under the first blows from outside.

The ghosts had gotten there first.

They lined the entrance hall in their hundreds—not the polished revenants of the palace district, but the ordinary dead of Vessany: clerks in rotted livery, a fishwife still holding a basket that had been empty for four decades, soldiers whose uniforms had gone grey as ash. They had no weapons. They had, instead, their records. Each of them held whatever scrap of themselves they'd managed to reclaim from the shelves—a ledger page, a marriage certificate, a child's copybook—and they held these things out before them like lanterns, like shields, like proof.

*We were here,* the pages said. *We are still here.*

"They can't hold," Teodor said, coming up beside her. He said it gently, the way he said most true things. "Living hands move through them like smoke through smoke. One shove and—"

The doors burst.

It happened all at once, the way disasters do: the bronze gave with a sound like a struck bell finally finding its note, and the torchlight poured in, and the crowd came through shouting words Casper half-knew—Old Vessani prayer-phrases, twisted into slogans, the language of her childhood weaponized into a mob's grammar. *Take back what's ours. The country belongs to the living. Carry the names to the new king.*

The first ghost they touched simply came apart.

Not violently—that was the horror of it. A clerk near the doors reached toward a young man with a torch, perhaps to plead, perhaps only to be seen, and the young man's hand passed through the page the clerk held, and then through the clerk, and where it passed, the clerk grew thin. Thinner. Translucent. He looked down at his own fading hands with an expression not of fear but of terrible recognition, as if he had seen this happen to others and always wondered how it would feel, and now he knew: it felt like being read aloud by someone who skipped the ending.

He was gone before he finished fading. The page drifted to the floor.

"No!" Casper was moving before she decided to move, shoulder into the crowd, using the low, brutal economy of motion that harbor brawls in Corvane had taught her and her guild training had refined. She didn't fight the rioters. She fought *past* them, toward the fallen page, and snatched it up, and thrust it back toward the space where the clerk had been. Nothing took it. There was nothing left to take it.

She put it inside her coat anyway. Someone should carry it. That was the whole of her philosophy some days, and today it would have to be enough.

"They're not stealing paper!" she shouted, wheeling on the nearest group—three men with a shelf between them, actually lifting one of the name-racks onto their shoulders like a coffin. "You're not carrying names, you're carrying *people*. Every shelf is a parish. Every drawer is a family. Put it down!"

One of them spat at her feet. "Marek says—"

"Marek says whatever the Surveyor breathes into his ear." She grabbed the shelf's edge. It was heavier than wood had any right to be, dense with stored lives, and when she pulled, the men pulled back, and for a moment the three of them and she stood locked together over the remains of a nation like dogs over a bone. "He doesn't want you restored. He wants you *spent*. Ask him what he traded to hollow his own eyes!"

That landed. She saw it land—one of the men flinched, and his grip loosened, because everyone in the camps had seen Marek up close, had noticed the strange emptiness behind the charm, and had chosen not to ask. Fear is a poor foundation but it holds weight.

Then the far end of the hall screamed.

Casper turned, and the scene rearranged itself around the sound the way a map rearranges around a new border. Near the children's wing—the low shelves, the small drawers, the section she had been avoiding for three days because she could not yet bear to know which of those names had survived—half a dozen rioters had broken through the ghost-line, and beyond them, in the corner where the smallest records were kept, something was happening that made even the rioters step back.

A god was feeding.

It had come up through the floor—or perhaps it had always been there, coiled in the foundations, one of the starved old things the erasure had left buried. It had no shape worth naming: a darkness like spilled ink given appetite, a hunger wearing the suggestion of limbs. It crouched over the low shelves, and its attention moved along the rows, and wherever its attention rested, the names dimmed. Faded. Went out like candles pinched between wet fingers.

And huddled beneath the lowest shelf, in the corner, was a child-spirit.

She couldn't have been more than six or seven when she died—forty years ago or more, though death had kept her exactly as she was. She clutched a single page to her chest with both arms, the way children hold things they've been told matter more than anything: a school exercise, letters practiced in a careful hand. *My name is Sela. My name is Sela. My name is—*

The god's attention slid toward her like oil finding the low place in a table.

Casper did not think. Thinking was for afterward. She was already running, vaulting a toppled reading desk, and she heard Teodor roar behind her—heard him do something with his voice, the gravedigger's voice, the one that carried authority over the dead and the buried—and she didn't look back to see what obedience it bought.

The god saw her coming. Of course it did. It turned its attention on her fully for the first time, and she felt it the way you feel a hand pressed flat against your sternum: a weighing, an appraisal, a price being calculated. It had eaten ten thousand memories to rebuild that much attention. It knew what memory was worth. It could see hers, probably—all of them, laid out like coins.

*Rich*, the pressure seemed to say. *This one is rich.*

"You can't have her." Casper dropped to her knees beside the child-spirit, put her body between the small ghost and the dark, and held out the one thing she had carried since Corvane without ever quite admitting why: the old ledger, her mother's ledger, the one that predated the guild. She didn't open it. She just held it, and spoke the way her mother must have spoken, once, in a language she was relearning syllable by syllable:

"*Vessa-ni doru. Sel-eth anna.*" She didn't know if the grammar was right. She hardly knew what the words meant—something about home, something about keeping. But the child-spirit's eyes came up, wet and enormous, and the god went very still.

Because the ledger was older than the god's hunger. Because it was written in the hand of the last inheritor. Because some debts outrank appetite.

The dark recoiled—not far, not forever, but a hand's width, a breath's width—and in that width Casper scooped the child-spirit up. Ghosts weighed nothing. Ghosts weighed everything. The little one folded against her chest like cold smoke with a heartbeat in it, still clutching her page, still whispering her own name like a spell against the dark.

"Casper!" Teodor reached her, blood on his brow from somewhere, a shovel in his hands—he had come armed with a shovel into a riot, because he was Teodor—and together they backed away as the god gathered itself for another consideration of the problem. It was patient. Hunger is patient. But the ledger's shadow lay across the floor between them, and patience, tonight, met its match in paperwork.

They fought their way out through the west cloister, the child pressed against her heart, the ledger under her arm, while behind them the great hall filled with smoke and shouting and the terrible sound of shelves going over—forty years of careful preservation ending in an afternoon, the way nations do.

* * *

They ended up, of all places, in the bell tower.

It was Teodor's idea—"high ground, one stair, easy to hold"—and he was right, and also she suspected he chose it because the tower was where the dead of the capital went to think, and the dead there owed him favors. They sat on the worn steps below the silent bells, and Casper held the child-spirit in her lap, and slowly, slowly, the little ghost stopped shaking.

Outside, the city burned in small, stubborn patches. Below, the archive's roofline glowed. Somewhere in that glow were the shelves she hadn't saved, the names she hadn't carried, the clerk who had faded mid-recognition. She kept seeing his face. She kept seeing the exact moment he understood.

"I couldn't hold the line," she said. It wasn't an apology, exactly. It was an accounting, and accounting was her native tongue.

"You held one line," Teodor said. "The one that mattered." He nodded at the child.

Sela—she had offered the name shyly, twice, as if unsure it still worked—was examining Casper's satchel buckles with the solemn fascination of the very young. When she spoke, her voice was small and clear as a bell in a quiet room. "You said the keeping-words wrong."

Casper blinked. "I did?"

"Grandmother says them right. At bedtime." The child frowned with the effort of precision. "*Vessa-ni doru, sel-eth anna, mira-veth.*" And the last word she sang, a falling minor third, and the note went through Casper like a needle finding an old scar.

*Miraveth.* Her mother had hummed that phrase. Had hummed it over the stove, over the maps, over nine-year-old Casper's head at night, never translating it, and Casper had grown up thinking it was just a tune, just nonsense-syllables, just a mother's private music. It was a benediction. It was the closing line of a keeping-prayer. Her mother had sung the archive's oldest words to her as a lullaby, and Casper had carried them for thirty years without knowing she was carrying the country's whole heart in three notes.

"The song," Casper said. Her voice came out strange. "The one grandmothers sing. Do you know all of it?"

Sela did. Of course she did. Children keep what adults lose; that was practically the definition of them. And so in the bell tower, above a burning archive, in a country that was being unmade a second time, a dead girl taught a living woman a lullaby, verse by verse, while the bells that would never ring hung overhead like patient witnesses.

When it was done, Casper wrote it down. She wrote it in the margin of her mother's ledger, in the space beside the final entry, because some things belong next to each other whether the archivists approve or not. Then she looked at what she'd done—at the lullaby nested against the inheritance, at the child asleep against her shoulder—and understood, with the calm of someone watching a map complete itself, that this was why she had come. Not for the throne. Not for the door. For this: to be the place where a song got written down before the singer was lost.

Teodor was watching the stairwell. He'd been watching it for several minutes, she realized, with the particular stillness he adopted when listening to things she couldn't hear.

"What is it?" she asked.

"Footsteps," he said. "A lot of them. Coming up slow." He glanced back at her, and in the torchlight from the window his face was grim and steady. "But they're not Marek's. These ones walk like people trying not to wake anyone." A pause. "There's a woman at the front. Old cloak. She's asking for you by name—your *old* name. And Casper—" he hesitated, and Casper felt the tower tilt around the next words before he said them, "—she says she has the founding seals. And that Oren sent her. And that we have until moonset to decide what kind of country we're going to be."

The child stirred in her sleep. Down in the dark, the footsteps climbed.
