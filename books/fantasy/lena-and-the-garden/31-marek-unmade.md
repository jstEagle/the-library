## 31. Marek Unmade

The silence after the contest was worse than any cheering would have been.

The assembled dead stood in the great hall of the archive—thousands of them, ranked in the aisles and on the balconies and pressed against the high windows like frost on glass—and they did not move. They had weighed the two claims the way Vessany's ghost-laws demanded: not by blood, which both claimants had in equal measure, but by remembrance. And remembrance had tilted toward Casper the way a scale tilts when one side holds iron and the other holds ash.

Marek stood where he had stood through all of it, at the center of the marble floor, beneath the shattered dome through which the Surveyor's distant hunger could be heard as a low grinding, like a millstone turning somewhere under the world.

He was still smiling.

That was the first thing Casper noticed, and it was the thing that frightened her most. The smile had not left his face since the dead began their weighing. It sat there the way paint sits on old wood—adhered, cracked, no longer connected to anything underneath.

"Congratulations," Marek said. His voice was pleasant. It had always been pleasant. That was how he'd been raised, she supposed—somewhere abroad, in some exile household that taught its children charm the way other households taught prayers. "You won a country."

"It isn't a prize," Casper said.

"No." He looked down at his hands. He turned them over slowly, as though checking whether they were still attached. "No, I don't suppose it is."

* * *

She saw it happen from ten feet away, and even so she could not say afterward exactly when it began.

It started with his hands. They stopped turning over. They went still at his sides, palms up, fingers slightly curled—the posture of a man asleep, or of a puppet whose strings have been cut one at a time. Then the smile went. It did not fade; it *left*, the way a tide leaves, dragging things with it. The muscles around his eyes unclenched. The set of his jaw loosened. And what was revealed underneath—not gradually but all at once, like a sheet pulled off furniture in an abandoned house—was a face Casper had never seen before, though it had been standing in front of her for days.

It was young. That was the horror of it. Under all the borrowed polish and the hollow-eyed confidence, Marek Villalobos was young—younger than her, maybe twenty-five, maybe less—and he looked like a man who had been awake for years without once being allowed to sleep.

He made a sound. Not a word. A small sound, wet and involuntary, the sound a person makes when they surface from deep water and discover their lungs still work.

Then his knees went.

Casper moved before she decided to move. She crossed the marble floor and caught him under the arms as he folded, and he was lighter than he should have been—that was the second horror, the lightness, the way his body gave against hers like a bundle of dry reeds—and together they went down, not gracefully, her knees cracking on stone, his head lolling against her shoulder.

"Marek." She said it twice before he focused on her. His eyes were enormous. They were the eyes of someone looking out from inside a collapsing building, watching the rooms he used to live in come down around him one by one.

"What did you take from me," he whispered. "What did you *take*—"

"I didn't take anything. The dead did. The law did. You made bargains, Marek. The bargains were called due."

"They said—" His breath hitched. "They said it was a loan. They said I could pay it back when—" He stopped. Something moved behind his face, some memory surfacing and finding nothing to attach to, sliding back under. "When what? When *what*? I can't remember what I was going to be when."

Casper held him. Around them the dead watched with expressions she could not read—or could read too well. Pity, mostly. The pity of people who had been consumed themselves and recognized the symptoms.

* * *

They got him to a bench near the archive's eastern wall, where the name-shelves stood in their long ranks, half-empty, humming faintly with everything that remained. Oren brought water. Teodor brought a blanket from his cart—he had carried it up from the salt line that morning, saying you never knew when someone would need burying or unburying, and a blanket served for both—and they wrapped Marek in it, and he sat shivering in the warm air of the archive as though it were winter.

Oren crouched in front of him, the way you'd crouch for a child or a wounded animal. "Tell me what you traded," he said gently. "All of it. In order. It helps to say it in order."

Marek's mouth worked. "I don't know the order anymore," he said. "That's the thing. That's the—when you sell a year, you think you'll remember which year it was. You don't. There's just a hole shaped like a year and you walk around it and everyone pretends they can't see it." He looked up at Casper, and his eyes were wet and furious and terribly clear. "Do you know what I sold first?"

"No," Casper said.

"My mother's voice." He laughed—a single broken note with no humor in it. "I was nineteen. A hedge-god in a market in Sarnholt, wearing a coat made of moths. It offered me luck. Just luck, small luck, dice-and-cards luck. And the price was one memory, my choice, and I thought—I actually thought—*I have hundreds of memories of her, what does one cost?*" His hands shook inside the blanket. "I can't remember her voice now. I can't remember if she sang. I know she must have sung, everyone's mother sings, but there's nothing. There's a hole shaped like singing."

The archive hummed around them. Somewhere far off, the grinding of the Surveyor's work continued, patient as erosion.

"And then?" Oren asked, quiet.

"And then it was easier." Marek stared at the floor. "That's what nobody tells you. The first one is hard and every one after is easier, because each sale takes the part of you that would have refused. By the end I wasn't selling pieces of myself. I was selling pieces of the man who used to object." He looked up again. "The Surveyor found me three years ago. It didn't threaten me. It *congratulated* me. It said I had a talent for transaction. It said blood like mine shouldn't waste itself on ordinary life. It showed me Vessany on a map that shouldn't exist and told me I could have it back. All of it. A whole country, returned to me, in exchange for—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the hollow where a man used to be, "—the rest."

"You wanted a throne," Casper said. It wasn't an accusation. She needed to hear him say it or deny it.

"I wanted to be *someone*," Marek said, and his voice cracked clean through. "I grew up in a house where my father kept a portrait of a country on the wall and told me we were royalty of nowhere. Royalty of *nowhere*, Casper. Do you know what that does to a boy? Every day he looks at you like you're a king in exile and every night you go to bed knowing there's no kingdom, no crown, no court—just a story he tells to make his own failure feel like fate. When the Surveyor showed me that map, I didn't see a country. I saw the look on my father's face when I finally came home with something real."

The words fell into the silence and lay there.

Casper thought of her own childhood: a mother burning papers by candlelight, whispering *forget me kindly*, teaching letters with map symbols because maps were safer than names. Two children of Vessany, scattered in opposite directions—one given a country-shaped hole and told to fill it with glory, one given nothing at all and told it was mercy.

Both holes, she understood now, had been dug by love. Only one had been dug honestly.

* * *

It was near dusk—though dusk meant little under the shattered dome, where the light came down gray and sourceless—when Marek tried to give himself away.

Casper had turned to go, to check on the evacuation routes along the salt line, when she felt his hand close around her wrist. His grip was astonishingly strong for such a light man. Desperation is heavy; it anchors.

"The inheritance," he said. "Take the rest of it."

She stopped. "What?"

"You won. The dead chose you. But the law needs a claimant whole enough to hold it, doesn't it? That's what the Surveyor wants—it wants the door opened under its control, it wants a claimant it owns." His eyes burned into hers, fever-bright. "So take me. Take what's left. Fold it into your claim. Whatever I still am—my name, my blood, the scraps the gods haven't chewed—take it and use it. Make the inheritance big enough to matter. Make me *useful*."

"Marek—"

"I'm not asking for mercy." His voice rose, and heads turned among the dead along the shelves. "I'm asking for arithmetic. I'm worth more as part of you than as myself. Look at me! I'm a ledger with the entries torn out. I'm a— I'm a *coat* with nothing in the pockets. But a coat can still be worn. Take me the way they took everything else. At least then I'd be *spent on something*—"

"*No.*"

The word came out of her harder than she intended, and it rang off the marble, off the shelves, off the dome, and the archive went very quiet, the way a room goes quiet when a prayer has been answered too loudly.

Marek flinched as if struck.

Casper knelt in front of the bench so their eyes were level. Up close she could see what the bargains had left: fine cracks across his irises like glazing on old porcelain, the faint translucence of his fingertips where flesh had been paid away and imperfectly replaced. A man assembled from remainder.

"Listen to me," she said. "I've spent my whole life keeping ledgers. Do you know what I learned? Every entry teaches you the same lesson, if you let it: that a thing's value is what someone will pay for it. That people are sums. That anything can be entered on either side of the page." She took his hand—both his hands, cold as river stones—and held them. "I refuse. Do you hear me? I refuse the whole grammar of it. I will not consume you. Not your name, not your blood, not the scraps. You are not currency. You are not a coat. You're a frightened man who was lied to by something that eats, and eaten by the things it introduced you to, and none of that makes you spendable."

"But I'm *empty*—"

"Empty is not gone." Her voice gentled. "I've seen this archive, Marek. Half these shelves are empty too. And the ghosts defend them anyway. Do you know why? Because an empty shelf still says: *something was here. Something mattered enough to keep.*" She squeezed his hands. "You're not a hole. You're a shelf. And shelves can be filled again—slowly, badly, honestly. New memories. Small ones. The taste of bread. The weight of rain. Nobody can sell those back to you, and nobody can buy them cheap. That's the only wealth I believe in anymore."

Marek stared at her. Then, horribly, he began to cry—not the elegant grief she might have expected from a man trained in charm, but the ugly, gasping crying of someone much younger than his face, someone perhaps finally allowed to be the age he actually was.

Teodor appeared at her shoulder with the unhurried solidity of a man who had spent decades sitting with the dying. He put one broad hand on the back of Marek's neck, the way you'd steady a spooked horse.

"First thing tomorrow," Teodor rumbled, "you'll help me dig. Nothing teaches a man he's still real like dirt under his nails."

Marek laughed through the tears—a real laugh this time, startled out of him, cracked and rusty. "Digging graves?"

"Planting, mostly," Teodor said. "But the tools are the same."

* * *

Later—much later, when the archive lamps had been trimmed low and the dead had drifted to their posts along the walls—Oren found Casper standing at the eastern window, looking out over the dark city toward the salt line, where the horizon pulsed faintly with the Surveyor's labor.

"He offered you himself," Oren said. "And you refused."

"Yes."

"That was the hardest trade you'll ever make," Oren said quietly. "Refusing something free. Most people never learn how."

Casper didn't answer. She was watching the horizon, where the pulsing had changed. She had been watching it for an hour, telling herself she was imagining it, and she was done imagining.

The pulse was faster.

And along the southern dark, where the salt line ran, a new light had appeared—not the gray grind of the border being eaten, but a thin, vertical brightness, like a door standing open in the middle of a field, seen from very far away.

"Oren," she said. "How long has that been there?"

He followed her gaze. She felt him go still beside her.

"That's not one of ours," he said.

Down in the archive behind them, on the half-empty shelves, thousands of remaining names began, very softly, to hum.
