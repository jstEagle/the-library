## 32. The Surveyor Moves

The salt line screamed first.

Casper heard it from the archive steps, half a mile from the border, and the sound went through her teeth like a struck tuning fork—a high, thin keening, the note a wire makes just before it parts. She had crossed that line twice now. She had felt it hum under her boots like a living thing, patient and old, a fence of memory holding a country's shape the way a corset holds a body. Now it was being unmade, and it knew, and it was crying out in the only language it had.

"Teodor." She was already moving. "The border. *Now.*"

He came down the archive steps three at a time, grave-dirt still on his sleeves from the morning's burials, his face already doing the thing it did before bad news—going smooth and still, a pond before a stone. Behind them, in the archive's great hall, the ghosts paused in their work of shelving names and turned their translucent heads toward the sound, the way a herd turns toward a wolf's first cry.

They ran through streets that were already forgetting themselves.

That was the thing Casper could not make anyone who hadn't seen it understand: a country did not unravel all at once, like a sock with a pulled thread. It unraveled in *details*. The cobblestones under her feet went smooth as river stones, then soft as loam, then were simply a suggestion of ground, a rumor of a road. A bakery she had passed a dozen times—she had counted, a dozen, she knew its crooked chimney, its smell of caraway—stood with its windows dark and its door a blank rectangle of nothing, as if someone had described a bakery to a stranger who had never seen one. A woman stepped out of an alley carrying bread that was not bread, that was the *idea* of bread, and by the time she reached the square she was carrying nothing at all and had stopped to wonder why her arms were raised.

"The Surveyor," Teodor said, not a question. He had buried god-touched dead for thirty years. He knew the sound of a god feeding.

"It was denied," Casper said. Her breath burned. The ledger was strapped against her ribs under her coat, and she could feel it there like a second heartbeat, warm, almost alive. "The contest went against it. The dead chose me. So now it stops pretending there are laws."

They came over the last hill above the salt line together, and Casper stopped so abruptly that Teodor nearly went past her.

The border was dying in a way she had no symbols for.

The line of salt that ran from horizon to horizon—white, humming, patient—was being *eaten from above*. There was no army. There was no shape, not even the courteous gray figure of the Surveyor itself. There was only a wrongness in the air, a column of absence descending on the border the way light descends, and where it touched, the salt did not vanish so much as become *unremembered*: it stopped being salt, then stopped being a line, then stopped being a place where anything had ever been drawn. And Casper could see—she pressed her knuckles to her mouth to keep from making a sound—that the wrongness was moving along the border in both directions at once, devouring the line the way a candle flame devours a wick, and that it was *fast*, and that it was *deliberate*, and that somewhere inside it, patient as an auditor, the first guildmaster of her guild was doing sums.

"It's not just taking the border," she said. Her voice came out very flat, which was what her voice did instead of breaking. "It's taking the *memory* of the border. Look at the land on either side."

Teodor looked. On the Vessani side, the hills were still there, but they were wrong—too smooth, too general, hills as a child would draw them, because the specific hills, the ones with names and scars and the particular way the morning fog pooled in the valley of the Ald, were being drunk down like broth. On the far side, the ordinary world of grass and fenceposts flickered, and for a heartbeat Casper saw through it to something else: not void, but *potential*, the unmarked page a country gets erased onto.

"It's going to eat the whole country," Teodor said. "Not open the door. Not claim the inheritance. Just—" he made a slow gesture with both hands, the gesture of a man smoothing a bedsheet over a face, "—close the book."

"It can't." Casper said it and heard how thin it was. "The dead are crossing. The evacuation's barely begun. If the border goes before—"

"Then we make the border go *slower*," Teodor said.

She turned to look at him. He was watching the devouring column with the expression she had learned to read on the road, the one he wore over graves: grief and practicality fused into a single working tool.

"How," she said, "does one slow down a god."

"You told me yourself," he said. "Everything it eats, it has to *digest*. Memory isn't meat, Casper. It's got to be carried, catalogued, stored. It's a *ledger-keeper's* god. It can't help itself." He looked at her, and there was something almost like a smile at the corner of his mouth, grim as a spade. "So we give it indigestion."

* * *

The Surveyor had once been a man who believed the world could be balanced.

That was the mercy no one had ever extended to it: to be remembered as what it was, rather than what it became. It had been a guildmaster in an age when the guild was six people in a rented room above a fishmonger's, keeping honest accounts of impossible things—storms in bottles, years lent against years, a widow's grief traded fair and square for a winter's warmth. It had believed in the ledger the way other men believed in gods, and when the prayers stopped being answered and the old gods began to starve, it had done the arithmetic that no honest accountant should ever be permitted to finish: that memory was the oldest currency, that a nation was a hoard of it, and that a nation erased from every map was a nation whose hoard no one else would ever come to claim.

Vessany had not been destroyed. Vessany had been *deposited*.

For forty years it had sat in the dark of its own erasure, and the Surveyor had skimmed the interest—small withdrawals, a memory here, a memory there, routed through a guild that never knew whose ledger it was keeping. It had been a patient investment. It had planned to draw down the principal over centuries.

And then a woman with her dead mother's handwriting in her name had walked in and begun contesting the account.

The Surveyor did not feel anger. Anger was a debt emotion, and it had long ago paid off all its debts to itself. What it felt, standing at the border with the salt line dissolving between its fingers like sugar in rain, was the clean displeasure of a balance sheet that would not close. The lawful door was denied it. Very well. A door was only a formality. A country was only a container. And a container could be broken.

It knelt—though kneeling was only a courtesy it still paid to its old human shape—and pressed its palms flat against the ground, and began to read the land the way it had once read ledgers: line by line, entry by entry, taking everything.

* * *

"They're falling," Casper said.

She stood in the bell tower of a church that was forgetting it was a church—the bell had already gone, the pews were going soft at the edges like wet paper, but the tower still stood because enough people remembered climbing it—and she looked out over the borderlands and counted what the land was losing.

The evacuation had been going for two days. Ghosts by the hundred, exiles by the dozen, each one carrying what they personally held of Vessany: a grandmother's lullaby, the exact blue of a kitchen door, the name of a dog dead eighty years. Oren's enforcers had turned from hunters to ferrymen without ever quite admitting it, hauling the oldest spirits across the salt in carts that creaked with the weight of nothing. Teodor walked the lines of them like a shepherd, and the ghosts, who feared almost everything, did not fear him.

But the Surveyor was eating the country out from under the evacuation, and it had learned.

"It's not eating evenly," Casper said. She had the ledger open on her knee, her mapping pens out, and she was doing what she did, the only thing she had ever been good at: she was making a map of a catastrophe. "Look. It's skipping whole districts. It took the Ald valley in an hour, but it hasn't touched the southern marshes at all. It's not random, Oren. Nothing that thing does is random."

Oren Sable stood at the tower's edge with his hands clasped behind his back, and for once he did not look like a guildmaster. He looked like a tired man on a high place, watching a fire he had helped to bank for forty years finally catch.

"It's eating in order of value," he said.

"Yes."

"It's eating the memories that would be needed to *rebuild* Vessany. The founding records. The royal line. The shape of the law. The songs that hold the language together." He said it without inflection, the way he announced exchange rates. "It's not trying to stop the evacuation. It's trying to make sure that whatever escapes isn't *enough*. That whatever survives out there is a handful of refugees with a handful of lullabies, and no country to ever hang them on again. It can't have the inheritance." He turned to her. "So it's destroying the estate."

Casper looked down at her ledger, at the map taking shape under her pen, and felt the cold arrive in her chest, the specific cold of a figure not adding up.

"Oren. The door."

"What of it."

"The door opens for someone who holds Vessany whole in memory. *Whole.* That was always impossible—that's why my mother ran, that's why the door never opened, because no one person could hold a whole country." Her pen stopped. "But the Surveyor is doing the opposite of what I thought. I thought it was erasing the country so no one could ever claim it." She looked up. "It's *consolidating*. It's eating everything into itself. It's not destroying the estate, Oren. It's *amassing* it. It's making itself the only one who holds Vessany whole—so that when the last memory is swallowed, the door will have no choice but to open for *it*."

The wind off the border carried the keening of the salt line, thinner now, further away, a wire wearing through.

Oren was silent for a long moment. Below them, in the churchyard, a ghost woman was teaching three living children a skipping rhyme, and the rhyme kept losing words, and she kept patiently putting them back.

"Then we have two clocks," Oren said finally. "Theirs and ours. They need to finish eating before your people finish leaving—or rather, they need to finish eating the *right* memories. And we need the door open before the country is thin enough to break." He looked at her, and his eyes had the flat shine she had learned, on the long road, to distrust and rely on in equal measure. "How much of Vessany do you hold, Casper?"

"Enough," she said. "Not whole. Not yet. I've been—" she touched the ledger against her ribs— "I've been gathering. Every ghost's testimony. Every song. The map I'm building in the archive, the one the dead are helping me draw. It's honest. It's incomplete. But the door doesn't ask for a perfect country. It asks for a *remembered* one."

"And the Surveyor knows that too," Oren said. "Which is why it's eating the way it's eating. Every district it takes is a district you can never map. It's not starving you, Casper. It's *editing* you."

* * *

They went down to the marshes because the marshes were next.

Casper knew it the way she knew weather: the Surveyor's appetite had a rhythm, and she had spent two days charting it, and the southern fens—Vessany's oldest district, the one the exiles' songs called *the mother of reeds*—would be taken within the day. In the fen villages, the evacuation was going badly. The dead of the marshes were old and slow and stubborn, and many of them refused to go.

"We were born here," an old spirit told her, standing in a doorway that was becoming less of a doorway by the hour. He had been a reed-cutter in life; he had been dead for a century and a half; he had the patient insolence of both. "We were born here and we'll be forgotten here. Let the thing come. Let it try to swallow me. I'm mostly mud and grudge. It'll choke."

"It won't choke," Casper said. She was tired down to the marrow of the day. "It will take you apart and file you. That's worse than swallowing. Swallowing, at least, is honest."

The old reed-cutter looked at her for a long moment, and then at the fen behind her, where the reeds were going gray at the tips—not dying, *de-specifying*, becoming reeds in general rather than these reeds, with their rust heads and their particular whisper that every Vessani child had been sung to sleep inside of.

"You're the heir," he said. "The one the dead chose."

"I'm the one the dead are stuck with," Casper said.

That, unexpectedly, made him laugh—a dry sound, like reeds in wind. "Good," he said. "The ones who claim to be chosen are always lying. All right, ledger-keeper. Tell me what you want."

"Your memories," she said. "All of them. Everything you hold of this place. Not for me—for the map. For the door. Every reed, every grudge, every song. Give them to me and then cross the salt, and when you get to the other side, you'll be a man with no country and a long life of being annoyed about it, which I'm told is the normal condition of exiles."

He looked at her a long time. Behind him, his doorway finished forgetting itself, and he stood in open air and did not fall, because ghosts stand on memory and his was still his own.

"You'll keep them honest?" he said. "You won't prettify the mud?"

"I keep honest books," Casper said. "It's the only thing I've ever been any good at."

* * *

They took the fen's memories in a single night, and it nearly killed her.

Not the Surveyor—her. Casper sat in the fen village's last standing house with the ledger open on her knees and the dead of the marshes filing past her, one by one, each one laying their hands on the page and *pouring*, and she took it all in, because someone had to be the vessel and she was the inheritor and that, she understood now, was what inheritance had always meant: not a throne. A *container*. A country's worth of other people's remembering, held in one mortal skull, and the skull complaining about the fit.

By the gray hour before dawn she was weeping without noticing, and the tears were not hers—she was crying a reed-cutter's grief for a wife dead two hundred years, crying a child's terror of the fen-lights, crying a whole district's small and enormous loves, and Teodor sat beside her with his hand flat on her back between the shoulder blades, saying nothing, being a wall she could lean her whole self against.

"You don't have to hold it all," he said quietly, at the worst of it.

"Yes, I do," she said, and took the next memory, and the next.

Toward dawn, the last of the fen's dead crossed the salt, and the Surveyor arrived an hour later and found the district already hollowed—found mud and reeds with no one left to be mud and reeds *for*—and Casper, watching from the ridge with her ledger fat against her ribs, saw the column of absence pause over the empty marsh.

And she would have sworn, then and ever after, that it turned.

That across the ruined distance, the courteous gray shape at the heart of the devouring light looked directly at her—the heir, the thief, the woman who had learned its own trick of consolidation and was using it faster—and inclined its head, very slightly, in the manner of a professional acknowledging good work by an amateur.

Then it turned south, toward the capital, toward the archive, toward the last great shelves of names, and began to eat in earnest.

* * *

"It'll reach the archive in a day," Oren said. "Maybe less. It's feeding faster now. Starving things always gorge at the end."

"Then we move the name-shelves tonight," Casper said. "Everything. Every name that's left. The ghosts can carry them; they're their own names, half of them. And the door—Oren, the door can't wait for the Surveyor to finish. If I go to it *now*—"

"You're not whole," Oren said. "You said it yourself. There are districts you never gathered. The north. The coast. Half the capital itself. The door will open for you, but it will only be as open as your memory is full, and a half-open door is a wound, not an exit."

"Then I'll fill it," Casper said. "Tonight. I'll take the archive's own index, every name on every shelf, the whole national record—"

"The Surveyor will be there before you finish the first shelf."

"Then I'll be *fast*."

They stood in the fen village's ruined square, the three of them—Casper, Oren, Teodor—while behind them the marsh went on forgetting its reeds, and before them the road ran south toward the capital, toward the archive, toward the thing that had once been the first guildmaster and was now only an appetite with excellent handwriting.

Teodor spoke first. He had been quiet a long time, the quiet of a man arranging weights.

"There's a way," he said. "You're not going to like it."

"I never like any of your ways," Casper said. "Tell me."

"The larder," Teodor said. "Beneath the palace. The hoard. Everything the Surveyor's eaten in forty years—every crystal, every stored memory, all of it labeled and shelved, because it's a ledger-keeper's god and it *can't help itself*." His eyes were steady on hers. "You don't need to out-eat it, Casper. You need to *rob* it."

The keening of the salt line rose behind them, higher, thinner, a wire one breath from parting.

And far to the south, over the capital, the sky began, very quietly, to forget its blue.

*End of Chapter 32*
