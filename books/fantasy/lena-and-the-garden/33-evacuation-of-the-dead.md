## 33. Evacuation of the Dead

The breach opened at dawn, and it did not open like a door. It opened like a wound.

Casper stood at the salt line with the ledger under her arm and watched the border tear itself wider—a seam of white light running north to south through the grass, humming now not a keening but a chorus, hundreds of voices singing the same note in different keys. Through the seam she could see the other side: the wet green meadows of Vessany's interior, the road to Corvane, a cart track she had walked in a dream once and never in her life.

It would not hold. She could see that the way she could see a badly drawn coastline—something in the proportions wrong, something straining. The Surveyor was pulling at the border from within, tearing memory out of the ground itself, and every hour the seam widened and thinned, widened and thinned, like skin over a swelling.

Behind her, Vessany. Ahead of her, everything else.

"We have to move them," she said. "All of them. Before it closes."

Teodor stood beside her with his spade over his shoulder, looking at the light the way he looked at graves—with respect and no illusions. "How many?"

"However many there are."

"There are more than you think." He nodded back toward the ruined capital, toward the villages with their wrongly remembered shrines, toward the hills where the returned dead had been gathering for weeks like birds before a storm. "They've been coming since the prayers started again. They felt the country breathing. They came home." His voice caught on the word. "And now we tell them home is closing."

* * *

She organized it the only way she knew how: as an accounting.

She set up at the old customs house on the salt line, a roofless stone building whose walls still bore the faded stamps of a vanished revenue service, and she made it into a station. A table from a farmhouse. The ledger open on it. Ink that she mixed herself from ash and rainwater because her own bottles had been lost at the betrayal, and this ink wrote strangely—it wrote what needed writing rather than what she meant to say—and she decided she could live with that.

The ghosts came all day.

They came in ones and twos at first, then in families, then in processions. They were not the way the living imagined ghosts—not gray and moaning—but mostly they were just people, worn thin at the edges, translucent in the way that paper is translucent when held too long against a lamp. Some carried things: a loom shuttle, a child's shoe, a jar of honey that glowed faintly because honey remembers summer better than almost anything. Each of them carried memories, and the memories were the cargo.

"Understand what I'm offering," Casper told them, again and again, in her ledger-keeper's voice, the voice she used for explaining interest rates to people who didn't want to hear them. "The door isn't open yet. What I can give you is the breach, and the breach is temporary, and it will close whether or not you're through it. If you cross carrying your own memories—in your own hands, in your own keeping—the Surveyor cannot take them on the other side. They'll be yours. Truly yours. Outside its jurisdiction."

"And if we stay?" asked an old woman whose shawl was woven from forty years of fog.

"If you stay, you are eaten when it falls. Or worse—you are shelved." Casper touched the ledger. "I've seen the shelves."

The old woman looked at the breach for a long time. Then she took off her shawl and folded it and held it against her chest like a bundle she was carrying across a river. "My mother wove this," she said. "I remember her doing it. Her hands were quick. That's mine. No one shelved it yet."

"No one will," Casper said, and wrote her name in the ledger, and the name stayed, and the woman stepped through the light and did not scream, and came out the other side standing in wet grass under a foreign sky, and began, very slowly, to weep.

After that, they came faster.

* * *

By noon the customs house was a river of the dead, and Casper discovered something she had not expected: they did not want to leave empty-handed, and they did not want to leave alone, and they would not, most of them, go before everyone weaker had gone first.

An old soldier who had died at thirty-one and spent sixty years being thirty-one refused his turn so that a child-spirit could pass ahead of him. Two sisters who had quarreled in life and haunted each other for decades crossed holding hands, having apparently resolved nothing except the decision to keep arguing on the far side. A ferryman who had drowned with his boat insisted on going last, after his passengers, all eleven of them, though his passengers had been dead longer than he had and kept telling him so.

"You're organizing them," Oren said, appearing at her elbow with the particular expression of a man watching a system work that he had told her was impossible. He had come through the breach two days ago with his enforcers and had not left, and had not explained why, and Casper had stopped asking.

"I'm keeping accounts," she said. "It's different."

"It's the same thing. It was always the same thing. That's what I should have taught you instead of teaching you to hide." He watched a grandmother shepherd three ghost-children through the light, counting them aloud the way she must have counted them alive. "The guild spent forty years treating memory as inventory. Look at them. It never was. It's *cargo*. It's what people carry. We were thieves who convinced ourselves we were warehousemen."

"Don't philosophize at me, Oren. Count heads."

He counted heads. To her lasting surprise, he was good at it—he moved down the lines with the founding seals in one hand and a tally stick in the other, calling names, settling disputes about precedence, and somewhere in the third hour a dead woman kissed his cheek in gratitude and he went pink to the ears, and Casper decided not to mention it ever.

* * *

Teodor ferried the oldest spirits.

That was the phrase that grew up around him by afternoon—*the gravedigger's ferry*—though there was no water and no boat, only Teodor walking the line of the breach with his spade across his shoulders and the oldest of the dead clinging to him like burrs. The ancient ones were the problem. The recently dead could walk; the ones who had been dead for centuries had forgotten how, had gone soft and strange, had become less like people and more like weather. One of them was only a voice. One of them was a smell—woodsmoke and bread—that drifted along the ground and had to be coaxed. One of them was a man so old that his name had worn off him entirely, and he answered to whatever anyone called him.

Teodor carried them. Not metaphorically. He put the voice inside his coat, close to his heart, where it hummed like a second pulse. He led the smell by walking slowly and talking to it about ovens. The nameless man he carried on his back, arms hooked under the old knees, and the old man weighed nothing and everything, and Teodor sang to him as he walked—an ugly, tuneless song, half hymn and half work-chant—and Casper heard other voices join it, ghosts picking up words they hadn't spoken in lifetimes, until the whole line of the breach was singing and the seam of light pulsed with the rhythm of it.

"What happens to them on the other side?" she asked him, during one of his brief rests. He sat on a fallen wall, sweating, the voice in his coat murmuring like a drowsy child.

"They remember," he said. "Out there, away from the hoard, away from the hunger—they start remembering. The old ones especially. The country was holding them here, and the country is dying, and I thought that meant they'd scatter." He looked at his hands. "But they don't scatter. They *settle*. Like silt. Like seeds." He smiled, small and crooked. "I buried my village's god to stop it feeding on us. I've spent thirty years thinking I was a man who ended things." He stood, and rolled his shoulders, and went back to the line. "Turns out I'm a ferryman."

* * *

Toward evening, the Surveyor noticed.

It did not come in person—it never bothered with persons anymore—but its attention came, a pressure like the moment before hail, and along the southern horizon the sky finished forgetting its blue and turned the color of unwritten paper. The wind changed direction and began blowing *into* Vessany instead of out, pulling at shawls, at jars of honey, at the edges of ghosts, trying to drag the cargo backward into the hoard.

"Hold what's yours!" Casper shouted, and her voice carried farther than a voice should, because the land was listening and the land wanted them to make it. "Hold what's yours and keep walking! It can't take what you carry in your own keeping—it has never been able to—that's the whole law, that's the first entry, *nothing taken may be kept, nothing given may be lost*—"

Where had she read that? She couldn't remember. It didn't matter. The ghosts heard it and believed it, or chose to believe it, which in Casper's long professional experience was the same mechanism under different names, and they pressed their bundles to their chests and leaned into the wind and walked.

A boy-spirit—nine years old, drowned in a well in the year of the erasure, carrying nothing but the memory of a dog—was plucked backward ten feet by a gust and shrieked, and Casper dropped the ledger and ran and caught him by both wrists, and the wind pulled, and she pulled, and for one horrible moment she was playing tug-of-war with a god over a child, and then Teodor was there, and Oren, and three enforcers, and the boy came free and fell through the breach and landed in the wet grass of the world, gasping, alive-ish, clutching his dog.

Casper knelt in the trampled grass, shaking. The ledger lay open beside her. The wind had turned its pages to a page she had not written, in handwriting that was almost hers:

*Withdrawals suspended. All debts recalled to bearer.*

"That's not me," she said.

"No," said the boy, wiping his face. "That's the country. It's helping." He looked up at her with eyes like well water. "It knows it's dying. It wants to give everything away first. That's what countries do, at the end. My grandmother did it. She gave away all her furniture before they came."

* * *

They worked through the night.

Bonfires along the breach, because firelight steadied the dead. A queue miles long, winding back through the darkened villages, and Casper at the table with ink-stained fingers entering names until her hand cramped and Oren took the pen and entered names in his precise guildmaster's script, and then an exile woman took it, and then a ghost who had been a schoolteacher took it, and the ledger passed down the line from hand to hand, each soul writing the next, until the accounting had become a communal act and Casper sat back and watched her life's discipline dissolve into something larger and stranger and better.

Marek did not come. She found herself watching the southern dark for him anyway—for the Surveyor's faction, for some final intervention—and the dark gave her nothing but the sound of the hoard-gates grinding beneath the palace, miles away, the sound of a god eating faster because it knew dinner was ending.

At midnight, the smell-that-was-a-grandmother crossed, and the woodsmoke-bread smell settled over the breach like a blessing. At the second hour, the nameless old man went through on Teodor's back, and as he passed the threshold he suddenly, unmistakably, remembered his name—and shouted it, one syllable, triumphant, into the other country—and was gone.

At the fourth hour, the breach began to narrow.

Not quickly. But visibly. The seam of light drew in at the edges like a cooling puddle, and the hum climbed in pitch, and everyone in the queue saw it and everyone in the queue did the same thing: they turned around and helped the person behind them move faster.

Casper had seen crowds do many things in her years of impossible trade. She had seen them panic, stampede, betray, bargain, kneel. She had never seen a crowd of the dying and the dead organize itself into tenderness because time was short. She stood at her table with her aching hand and let it happen around her, and understood that this, whatever the ledgers said, was the actual wealth of Vessany, and it was leaving the country honestly, carried in its own keeping, item by item, person by person.

* * *

Near dawn, the queue ran dry.

The last through was the old woman with the fog-woven shawl's sister, who had waited, she explained, to be certain the shawl got safely across. After her, an empty road. After the road, a country holding its breath.

Casper walked the line of the breach one final time with Teodor, checking, the way he checked a grave. The light was thinner now—paper-thin, breath-thin. Through it she could see the meadows, the cart track, the distant smoke of Corvane, and figures standing in the wet grass on the far side: the dead of Vessany, thousands of them, looking back at the country that had made them, not leaving so much as *arriving elsewhere*.

"How many didn't make it?" she asked quietly.

Teodor was silent a moment. "Some wouldn't go. Keepers, mostly. Ones who made vows to the shrines." He shrugged, heavy. "You can't ferry a man who won't board. And some..." He looked south, toward the capital, toward the grinding beneath the palace. "Some are already shelved. In the hoard. Forty years of harvest, Casper. Thousands of crystals. Every one of them someone."

"I know." Her hand found the ledger. "That's the next part."

"The larder," Teodor said.

"The larder." She looked at the narrowing light, at the country dissolving behind her, at the sky the color of unwritten paper pressing down from the south. "If the breach closes before I get back—"

"It won't," Teodor said. "Because I'll stand in it and hold it open if I have to."

"You'd be unmade."

"I'm a gravedigger, Casper. I know exactly what I'm worth buried." He smiled his crooked smile and picked up his spade. "Go rob the god. I'll mind the door."

Far beneath them, the grinding stopped.

And in the silence that followed, every ghost remaining in Vessany—every keeper at every shrine, every holdout in every village—turned their face toward the palace at once, and whispered, in Old Vessani, a single word that Casper did not know and somehow understood:

*Run.*
