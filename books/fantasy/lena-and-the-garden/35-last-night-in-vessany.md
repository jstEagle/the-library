## 35. Last Night in Vessany

The door had named its price at dusk, and Casper had asked for one night, and the door—being older than greed, older than the guild, older even than the Surveyor's first careful ledger line—had said yes.

Now the light was going out of Vessany the way light goes out of a room when someone, somewhere far away, is closing a shutter. Not darkening. *Dimming*, deliberately, like a candle being conserved for one last purpose. The streets of the capital held their color stubbornly: the blue-washed doorways, the copper weathervanes shaped like herons, the cobbles worn into two shallow rivers by four hundred years of feet. All of it immaculate. All of it maintained by hands that no longer existed, for a country that had already ended and simply hadn't been told.

Casper walked out of the archive alone.

Teodor had wanted to come with her. She had seen it in his face—the gravedigger's instinct to keep vigil over the dying—and she had refused him gently, the way you refuse someone who has offered to dig your grave. *This part I do alone,* she said. He had pressed her hand once, hard, his palm rough as bark, and gone to help Oren's people move the last of the name-shelves toward the breach. The dead were still evacuating. Somewhere beyond the salt line, the Surveyor was tearing at the border with teeth made of subtraction, and every hour it grew louder, a sound like a page being slowly ripped in half across the whole width of the sky.

But not here. Here, tonight, the country had drawn itself inward, around her, like a hand cupping a flame.

She turned left out of the archive square because her feet knew to, though she did not know why until she reached the street and saw the sign: *Lanternmakers' Row*, the letters flaking gold on a board that hung from an iron bracket shaped like a fish. Her breath caught. She had never walked this street awake. But Ilse's crystal had played through her all day since the archive—the mother-voice threading through the ruins—and some of what Ilse remembered had settled into Casper like rain into dry ground. Lanternmakers' Row. Where her mother had bought the little brass lamp with the cracked glass, the one that sat on Casper's desk in Corvane for twenty years, and Casper had never known why the crack mattered, or why Ilse touched it every morning before lighting it.

The lamp shop was still there. Of course it was. Everything here was still there; that was Vessany's whole tragedy and its whole tenderness. A ghost stood behind the counter, translucent as breath on glass, trimming a wick with small precise scissors. When he saw Casper he set down the scissors and bowed, low, the way they all bowed now—the heir who stayed away—and she shook her head before he could speak.

"Don't," she said. "Please. Tonight I'm just a customer."

He considered this. Ghosts were literal creatures; they weighed words the way merchants weighed salt. Then he nodded and lifted a lantern from behind him, a small thing of green glass, and lit it, and the light that came out was warm and slightly wrong, the color of a memory of fire rather than fire itself.

"For the road?" he asked.

"For the road," she said, and had nothing to pay him with, and he waved this away as if payment were a foreign custom he'd heard of but never practiced.

She carried the lantern out into the dimming streets, and the country began, in its slow patient way, to teach her its last song.

* * *

It started with the bells.

There were seven bell towers in the capital—or there had been, once; the maps in her head disagreed with each other, and tonight she let them disagree, because incompleteness was honest, she had decided that on a hillside with ink-stained fingers and a heart full of fragments. Six of the towers were ruined shells. The seventh, the one called Old Meridian by everyone who had ever lived under its voice, stood whole, and at some hour that felt like midnight but might have been any hour at all, it began to ring.

Not the alarm-peal she'd heard during the riot. Not the funeral tolls Teodor had described from the villages. This was something else—a pattern, nine strokes, then five, then eleven, then a pause long enough to walk a street's length, then nine again. Casper stopped in the middle of a crossroads and listened, and understood, with a lurch, that it wasn't random. It was a song written in numbers. It was a *counting song*. Children had learned arithmetic to it. She knew this the way she knew the shape of her own hands—not from memory but from the country pouring gently into every hollow the gods had left in her.

An old woman sat on a bench beneath the tower, knitting something without wool, her needles clicking soundlessly. She didn't bow. That was the gift the country gave her tonight, Casper realized: everywhere else, reverence; here, just company.

"It changes every time," the old woman said, nodding at the bells. "Forty years we've been ringing it, and it never rings the same way twice. The tower remembers all the versions." She smiled with half her face. "You'll carry one of them out with you. Whichever one you hear."

"Will I know it's Vessany's?" Casper asked. "After?"

The old woman's needles paused. "No," she said, kindly. "That's rather the point, dear. You'll hum it in a market in Corvane someday and some child will ask what it's called, and you'll say you don't know, and you'll be telling the truth. And the song will be alive again anyway. Songs don't need names. They need mouths."

The bells rang nine, then five, then eleven. Casper stood in the cold and memorized it with everything she had left—not writing it down, refusing to write it down, because a thing written could be taken from a shelf and eaten, and a thing only carried in the body belonged to the body alone.

* * *

She walked the market next, because the market had been Ilse's favorite place, and the crystal-voice had said so three times today in three different entries, each time with a different detail: the cheese seller who gave extra to red-haired children; the spice stall where her father proposed; the winter morning the whole square smelled of oranges because a ship from the south came in against all odds.

The market stalls stood open and empty, goods arranged by ghostly vendors who had nothing left to sell but the arranging. One spirit, a man with flour to his elbows, was folding pastries that would never be baked, creasing the dough with the tenderness of someone saying a rosary. He looked up as Casper passed.

"You're the one buying the lantern," he said. Not a question. News traveled fast among the dead; news was nearly all they had.

"Yes."

"Good. Green glass. My wife sold those. Best glass in the city—she put a grain of salt in every pane, so the light would taste of home." He laughed, a sound like wind through a doorway. "I told her that was nonsense. Light doesn't taste. Forty years dead and I still owe her an apology."

"I'll carry it," Casper said. "The lantern. If it helps."

"It helps," he said simply, and went back to his pastries, and Casper walked on with the green light swaying at her wrist and her throat tight as a knot.

This was the shape of the night, and she surrendered to it completely: the country giving itself to her piece by piece, street by street, not asking her to keep any of it—knowing she couldn't—but needing, the way the dying need a witness, to be *seen* one last time by eyes that would remember having seen, even if they never remembered what.

* * *

Near the river she found the school.

It was small, a single room with tall windows, and inside, rows of desks held ghosts of children—actual children, spirits who had died young across the centuries and come back with the rest, sitting patiently at their lessons as if erasure were merely a long holiday. At the blackboard, a teacher-spirit wrote letters in chalk that left no mark: the Vessani alphabet, the one Ilse had taught Casper disguised as map symbols, *this squiggle means river, this one means mountain, and this one, my love, means stay.*

A girl at the front desk looked up. Seven years old, maybe, with braids coming loose, and Casper recognized her with a jolt—the child-spirit from the riot, the one she'd pulled back from the Surveyor's hunger with both arms and no plan.

"You saved me," the girl said.

"You saved me first," Casper said. "In the archive. You showed me which shelf."

The girl shrugged, pleased and embarrassed. "Are you really going to close it? The door?"

"Yes."

"Then you need to learn the last verse." The girl said it matter-of-factly, the way children announce important things. "Everyone knows the door takes memories. But it gives one back. Nobody talks about that because nobody's ever opened it from inside. But the index says so. Page nine hundred and twelve." She pointed at the blackboard. "It gives back one memory. Any one you want. You choose it while you're walking, and when the door shuts, that's the one you keep."

Casper stared at her. "That's not in any version of the index I read."

"The index has more pages than people read," the girl said, with the mild contempt of childhood for adult carelessness. "Page nine hundred and twelve. It's in the appendix. Nobody reads appendices except us, because we have forever." She hopped off her chair. "So pick good. Pick the best one. And don't tell the tower lady I told you, she thinks I talk too much."

Then she was gone between the desks, and the other children bent over their invisible lessons, and Casper stood in the doorway of the school with her heart hammering and the green lantern trembling in her grip.

One memory. Kept.

She thought of her mother's face and found, probing carefully, that it was still there—still hers to lose. Every memory of Ilse, the door had said. Every one. And after, nothing: no face, no voice, no reason for the tears that would come without object. But one. One she could smuggle through the closing like a coin sewn into a coat lining.

She began choosing immediately, and could not stop choosing, and understood that this was the cruelest kindness the country had yet done her: now she would spend her last hours not only saying goodbye but *ranking* her love, holding each memory up to the light of the lantern and asking, *you? Is it you I keep?*

The morning Ilse woke her before dawn to show her a comet, and her mother's breath smoked in the cold and her hand was shaking on Casper's shoulder, not from the cold.

The night soldiers came and Ilse burned papers in the sink, calm as washing dishes, and afterward made cocoa and told jokes until Casper slept, and only the smell of smoke in her mother's hair betrayed anything.

The word *stay*, in the alphabet of map symbols, traced in steam on a window.

Every single one. She wanted to keep every single one, and the door would take them all but one, and the girl was right: she had better choose well.

* * *

Toward what should have been dawn, she climbed the hill above the capital where the palace ruins stood in their good repair, and sat on the steps, and took out the ledger.

Her mother's ledger—the one older than the guild, the one with the final entry in Ilse's hand naming Casper inheritor. She had carried it through everything: the rearranging roads, the salt border, the betrayal, the hedge-god's bargain that had cost her the smell of rain and bread and her own hair. She set it on her knees and did not open it. She knew every word. Instead she laid her palm flat on the cover and spoke aloud, to the hill, to the city below, to the woman thirty years dead whose handwriting she loved more than any face she could no longer picture clearly enough.

"I'm not angry," she said. "I want you to know that, wherever you are, whatever's left of you. You stole my country and I spent my whole life not knowing I'd lost one, and then I got it back for six weeks and it's the most beautiful thing I've ever mapped, and I'm going to give it away tomorrow, and I am *not angry.*" Her voice cracked and she let it. "You did the math, Mama. You always did the math. Someone had to be the one who stayed away so someone could be the one who closed it. You couldn't do it—you tried, I understand now why you couldn't—and so you made me able to. That's not a theft. That's a terrible, enormous gift, and I forgive you, and thank you, and I'm sorry, all three, all at once, forever."

The wind moved through the palace ruins, through forty empty rooms kept clean by praying hands, and it sounded almost like an answer, and she didn't pretend it was one. That was the rule she'd set herself tonight: no false comforts. Elegy without bitterness, but also without lies.

Below, the city glowed faintly, lantern by lantern, as the ghosts lit every lamp in the capital for the last night. From up here it looked like a constellation that had decided to become a country instead.

* * *

She went down into the streets one final time as the false dawn grayed, and the country sang to her the whole way.

It came through the bells, and through the river slapping its stone banks, and through the shutters of the houses clacking in the wind, and through the ghosts humming at their doors as she passed—they all hummed now, the same melody, each in their own key, so that the whole city was a chord slowly resolving. The last song of Vessany. Not a dirge. She noticed that and held onto it. It was in a major key, or whatever the ghost-equivalent of major was; it sounded like harvest, like a table being set, like *come home, come home, everything's cooked, everyone's accounted for.* A song for ending well.

She hummed along badly. The country corrected her gently, adjusting her pitch the way Ilse used to adjust her grip on a pen. *Here. Like this.*

And somewhere in the learning of it—in the third verse, maybe, the one about the river keeping count of everyone who ever bathed in it—she chose.

Not the comet. Not the burning papers. Not the word *stay* on the window, though God, she wanted that one, she wanted the word *stay* more than she wanted her own heartbeat.

She chose the smallest thing. The thing she'd almost forgotten until the lanternmaker's green light caught it: Ilse touching the cracked brass lamp every morning before lighting it, that small habitual gesture, thumb finding the crack the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. Twenty years Casper had owned that lamp without knowing why the gesture mattered. After tomorrow she wouldn't know either. But she would have it—the thumb on the crack, the morning light, the tiny sacrament of a woman beginning another day in exile. Something with no story attached. Something the gods couldn't contextualize, the door couldn't weigh, the Surveyor could never have farmed. Just a hand, and a lamp, and love with no explanation.

"That one," she said aloud to the empty street. "I keep that one."

The bells rang nine, then five, then eleven, and paused, and rang again, and she walked toward the salt line through a city singing itself to sleep, carrying a lantern of green glass with a grain of salt in the pane, so the light would taste of home.

At the edge of town, where the last house gave way to the white grass and the breach shimmered in the distance like a wound in the air, Teodor was waiting with the cart, and Oren beside it, and behind them a crowd of the dead stretching back into the singing streets, all of them come to see her to the door.

The girl with the loose braids was at the front, holding page nine hundred and twelve, folded, like a talisman.

Casper handed the lantern to the girl.

"Hold onto this," she said. "Someone should remember how it tastes."

And the country, behind her, began to sing the last verse.
