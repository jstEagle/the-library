## 37. Waking Without a Country

She woke to the smell of rain on grass and did not know her own name.

That was the first thing. Not the ache in her bones, not the rawness of her throat, not the strange new silence where a whole country had been — but the blank space at the top of herself, the place where a name should sit like a keystone. She lay on her back in wet grass under a gray morning sky and reached for the word *Casper* and found nothing there. Not an emptiness exactly. An emptiness would have edges. This was a room with the furniture gone, and no memory of what the furniture had looked like.

She sat up slowly. Her hands were filthy. There was salt dried white in the creases of her knuckles, and she stared at it for a long time without knowing why it made her chest hurt.

"You're awake."

The voice came from her left, low and rough as a shovel through gravel. A man sat a few feet away, wrapped in a coat that had been mended more times than it had been whole, his big hands resting on his knees as if he had been holding very still for hours so as not to wake her. His face was seamed and kind and terribly tired, and when she looked at him something in her said *safe* — but nothing said his name.

"I don't know you," she said. Her voice came out cracked. "I'm sorry. I don't know you."

Something moved across his face — grief, she thought, or its cousin — and then he nodded, slow, like a man accepting a verdict he'd been dreading for days.

"I know," he said. "Teodor. My name's Teodor. I dig graves." He paused. "Dug. I dug graves."

"Casper," she said, testing it, because it seemed important to offer something back even if it wasn't hers anymore. The name sat strangely in her mouth, like a coat that fit but belonged to someone else. "I think I'm Casper."

"You are," Teodor said quietly. "You're Casper Villalobos. And you walked out of a door that doesn't exist anymore, carrying a country on your back, and then you slept for two days." He looked away, out over the grass. "We weren't sure you'd wake up with anything left at all."

*Two days.* She looked down at herself as though her body might confirm it. Mud on her boots. Salt in her hair. In her pocket, against her ribs, something small and hard pressed when she shifted, and her hand went to it before her mind could object — a reflex older than her memory, some habit of keeping that had survived everything else.

She drew it out. A seed. One seed, brown and wrinkled, unremarkable as any seed in any hedge in any country. She turned it over twice in her fingers.

"Why do I have this?"

Teodor's jaw worked. "Those are mine," he said. "Seeds I saved. From places that—" He stopped. Started again. "From gardens that won't exist soon. I gave you one. Before. You held onto it the whole way." His voice went rough on the last word. "You wouldn't let go of it. Even sleeping. Even after."

She closed her fist around the seed. It felt less like an object than like an answer to a question she could no longer hear.

"Where are we?" she asked.

"Outside," Teodor said. "Outside is the only word for it now. Where Vessany used to border the world." He gestured behind him, and she turned, and saw it: a line of green grass running to the horizon, unbroken, unremarkable, growing lush and indifferent over ground that had hummed with salt for forty years. No scar. No seam. Just grass, bending in the wind, as if it had always been only grass.

She looked at it and felt nothing, and the nothing was worse than grief. Grief has a shape; it points at what it mourns. This was a hole in the shape of a hole.

"There was something there," she said. It wasn't a question, but Teodor answered it anyway.

"There was a whole country there," he said. "And you closed it. And it's gone now, all of it, forever, and everyone who was going to get out got out because of you." He swallowed. "Most of them. The ones who wanted to go."

"And I paid for it with—"

"With your remembering," Teodor said. "Yeah. That was the price. You knew it going in." He hesitated, and his big hands curled into fists on his knees. "You chose it. I want you to know that, in case anyone ever tells you different. Nobody made you. You stood at that door and you opened it anyway, knowing what it would take, because the alternative was letting that thing wear the country like a skin." He shook his head. "I've buried a lot of people, Casper. I never once watched somebody give themselves away on purpose to save others. I'll remember it. Since you can't."

She looked at this stranger who spoke about her with such fierce certainty, and she tried to feel like the person he was describing, and failed. Somewhere inside her was a woman who had done those things. That woman had walked streets she could not picture, loved a mother whose face she could not summon, carried ledgers whose contents she could not recall. All that remained of her was this body, this seed, this salt under her fingernails, and a stranger's testimony.

"Do I have family?" she asked. "Someone waiting?"

Teodor was quiet for a long moment.

"You had a mother," he said finally. "Ilse. She died when you were nine. You told me once — before — that she taught you letters with map symbols. That she burned papers the night soldiers came. That she told you to forget her kindly." His voice frayed. "I'm telling you this because someone should. Because you shouldn't have to find out from a document."

And that was when the weeping started.

It came up out of her with no warning and no object — no face attached to it, no voice, no single remembered kindness to hang it on. She wept the way you weep at a funeral for someone you never met, except worse, because this funeral was her own, and the coffin was empty, and she was both mourner and mourned. She wept for a mother who was a name and a handful of secondhand facts. She wept for a country that was a word other people used around her carefully, like walking on eggshells. She wept because her body remembered loss even when her mind could not, because somewhere below the level of memory the shape of Ilse Villalobos was still pressed into her, the way a pressed flower keeps the flower's outline long after the petals have crumbled — outline without substance, grief without a face.

Teodor didn't touch her. He just stayed, solid as a headstone, and let her cry, and when it finally ebbed he handed her a waterskin and said, "There's a man waiting for you. Down the road. Been waiting two days. Says he has papers."

"Papers," she repeated. The word landed strangely. Some part of her flinched at it — a flinch with no story behind it, a scar aching in weather she couldn't feel.

"He says you'll understand them," Teodor said. "He says you used to be very good with documents." A pause. "He also looks like a man who lost an argument with himself and hasn't decided which side won. So. Fair warning."

* * *

The man was waiting at a crossroads half a mile on, beside a cart and a horse that stamped impatiently at the wet grass. He was tall and gray-templed and dressed in the kind of clothes that had been fine once and had been traveled in hard since, and when he saw her coming his face did several things at once — relief, guilt, fear, and underneath them all something older and more worn, like a floorboard polished by years of pacing.

"Casper," he said.

"That's my name," she said. "So I'm told."

He stopped. Whatever speech he had prepared — and he had clearly prepared one; she could see its scaffolding in the set of his shoulders — collapsed on the spot. "You don't—" He passed a hand over his mouth. "Of course. Of course you don't. Oren Sable. Guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things. Your—" He faltered on the word. "Your employer. Once. Your friend, I hope, though you'd have every right to dispute the second part."

She studied him. Nothing. No recognition, no warmth, no wariness either — just a stranger's assessment of a tired man standing in the rain-damp grass with a cart full of boxes.

"You look like a man delivering bad news," she said.

"I'm delivering documents." He climbed onto the cart's bench and began unlacing a leather satchel with hands that were not quite steady. "Your effects. What survived. What I could recover." He hesitated. "And some things that aren't yours. Were never yours. Things I need to show you because—" He exhaled. "Because you're the only one left who can decide what happens to them, and because you deserve to know what was done in your name while you couldn't stop it."

He laid the documents out on the bench between them, one by one, like a man dealing cards in a game whose rules he no longer trusted.

A guild ledger, its cover scorched at one corner. She opened it and read the first entry, and understood every word individually and none of them together:

*Bottled storm, minor, sold to the widow Anselm of Corvane, payment: three borrowed years, interest waived out of respect.*

"Borrowed years," she read aloud. "What does that mean? Whose years?"

"People's," Oren said simply. "Ours. The guild traded in impossible things, Casper. Storms in bottles. Years out of lives. Names. Memories, near the end — though most of us didn't know that's what the old trades really were." He tapped the ledger with one finger. "You kept these accounts. For eleven years. You were the best accountant the guild ever had, because you believed the debts were real. You believed that everything taken should be written down, so that someday it could be given back."

She looked at the page. The handwriting was hers — she recognized the hand the way you recognize your own reflection, instantly and without warmth. *So that someday it could be given back.* Something stirred in her, faint and far away, like a light burning in a house she was locked out of.

"This next one isn't a guild document." Oren's voice changed, dropped, became careful. "This is the founding charter. Of the guild itself. Written by the first guildmaster." He unfolded a heavy sheet, sealed with wax stamps she didn't recognize, and laid it flat. "Read the third clause."

She read it. *The Guild shall serve as steward of the collectible debts of the erased nation of Vessany, in perpetuity, until such time as the nation's inheritance is claimed.*

"In perpetuity," she said. "What does that mean, practically?"

"It means," Oren said, and here his composure finally cracked, showing the ruin beneath, "that the guild was never a guild. It was a harvesting tool. The first guildmaster engineered the erasure of an entire country so that its memories could be farmed, slowly, forever, by gods he had made of himself. Every trade we ever made, every bottled storm, every borrowed year — the machinery was built to keep a dead nation's debts collectible. To keep the larder stocked." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "You worked for it your whole life. So did I. We were the brooms in a god's kitchen."

Teodor, standing behind her, made a low sound in his throat. "The Surveyor."

"The Surveyor," Oren agreed. "Who is sealed now. Who is *gone*, thanks to—" He stopped himself, glanced at her, and visibly rephrased. "Thanks to what was done at the door."

She looked down at the charter, at the elegant, murderous clauses, and felt the same terrible absence as before. She should be furious. She could see, intellectually, where fury would go — a lifetime of labor in service of theft, her own hands keeping the books of her own plundering. But fury needs a self to burn in, and hers had been spent buying a door.

"What do you want from me?" she asked.

"To dissolve it." Oren said it fast, like a man diving into cold water. "The charter, the seals, the whole rotten structure. Legally — by ghost-law, by guild-law, by whatever law still binds these things. With the Surveyor gone and the inheritance spent, the charter has no claimant, which means it can be dissolved by its senior surviving officer and its final inheritor jointly. That's you and me. Two signatures." He produced a pen. "I've been sitting at this crossroads for two days rehearsing how to ask you. I had speeches. About duty, and restitution, and the future." He looked at her — really looked, at the salt in her hair and the hollowness behind her eyes — and set the speeches aside. "Here's the truth instead. I helped build the cage. You broke it. And I am asking you to help me burn the blueprints, because I don't trust myself to do it alone, and because you deserve to watch it die."

She looked at the pen. At the charter. At the line where her signature would go — a signature she would have to learn all over again, like a child, like a forgery of herself.

"I don't understand any of this," she said quietly. "Not the guild. Not the laws. Not what I did or why. You're asking me to sign away an institution I can't remember joining, using knowledge I don't have, based on the word of a man I met ten minutes ago."

"Yes," Oren said.

"And if I say no?"

"Then the charter stands. Empty, dormant, harmless — probably. Until someone finds it. Someone always finds things like this." He held her gaze. "I won't force you. I've done enough forcing for one life. But I'm asking. As the man who owes you more than he can ever repay, and as the man who, I think — I hope — you once called a friend."

Behind her, Teodor shifted his weight. "Casper," he rumbled. "I've known you three weeks and you've forgotten all of it, and I still know this much: you don't leave debts unwritten. Whoever you were, that was the spine of her. Write it down. All of it. Then burn it."

She looked at the seed in her fist. She had been holding it this whole time, she realized — through the waking, the weeping, the walk, the whole gray morning. She hadn't put it down once.

"All right," she said. "Show me where to sign."

* * *

They burned the charter that evening, in a fire pit Teodor dug with the efficiency of a man who had dug many holes and took comfort in the digging. The seals cracked in the heat and spat green sparks, and the clauses curled and blackened one by one — *in perpetuity* going last, stubborn as a nail, until even it surrendered to ash.

Oren fed the fire the guild ledgers next, page by page, reading each entry aloud as it burned — every bottled storm, every borrowed year, every debt — speaking them into the air one final time so that they existed, briefly, as sound, as witness, before becoming smoke. It was, she thought, watching him, a kind of funeral rite. He was burying his life's work with full honors, and the honors were the point.

When the last page was ash, Oren sat back on his heels and looked ten years lighter and twenty years older, both at once.

"There's one thing left," he said. "One document I didn't burn. Couldn't." He drew it from his coat with the care of a man handling a reliquary. "The new ledger. The one you'd started — before. First entry in your hand. I found it glowing on your desk the night everything ended, and I've kept it since, because—" He stopped. Held it out to her. "Because I think it was meant to outlast all the rest. Read it."

She took the book. It was small, plain-bound, the paper good and heavy. On the first page, in handwriting that was hers and yet somehow calmer than hers, more certain, the ink still faintly luminous as if lit from within, was written:

*Let it be entered, first and last, in the Ledger of the Guild of Impossible Things, henceforth dissolved: nothing traded here shall be taken from anyone's remembering.*

She read it twice. Three times. The words went into her like water into dry ground, finding channels she hadn't known were there. *Nothing taken from anyone's remembering.* She didn't remember writing it. But she recognized it — recognized it the way you recognize a song you can't name, humming in your chest before your mind catches up. Whoever she had been, this was what she had believed at the end. This was the sum. Everything else — the guild, the country, the door, the price — had been the working-out, and this was the answer she had arrived at, and the answer had survived when the mathematician had not.

"It's a good first entry," Teodor said softly, over her shoulder.

"It's the only entry," she said. "The book's empty otherwise."

"Then it's not a record of what was," Teodor said. "It's a rule for what comes next." He nodded at the blank pages. "Somebody's going to have to keep it honest."

She looked up at him. Then at Oren, who was watching her with an expression she couldn't read and didn't need to — some things survive translation between strangers.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Tomorrow we'll talk about what comes next. Tonight—" She looked east, toward Corvane, toward a harbor city she had apparently lived in for eleven years and could not picture at all. Toward an office with a prayer candle that no one had lit, waiting for her like a patient ghost. "Tonight I'd like to sleep without salt in my hair."

But sleep, when it came, brought dreams — and the dreams were not empty.

She dreamed of a garden. Not any garden she had ever seen, and yet her dreaming feet knew every path of it: beds of flowers that bloomed in colors with no names, hedges trimmed into shapes like map symbols, a gate that stood open. And in the middle of the garden, with her back turned, kneeling in the dark soil with her sleeves rolled up, a woman was planting. The woman was singing — a song in a language the dream didn't translate, low and sweet, a work-song, a planting-song — and Casper walked toward her through the flowerbeds with her heart hammering, knowing, the way you know things in dreams, that if the woman turned around, everything would come back. The face. The name. The country. All of it, returned at a glance.

She was three steps away. Two. The song rose, and Casper's lips moved with it, shaping sounds she shouldn't have known—

The woman began to turn.

Casper woke in the gray before dawn with tears on her face and the seed clutched so hard in her fist it had left its imprint in her palm, and outside the little tent the birds were singing, and somewhere far off — impossibly, absurdly, in a harbor city she could not remember living in — she would have sworn she heard a ledger's pages turning, as if the book were already waiting for her hand.

She lay still, heart pounding, imprint of the seed pressed into her skin like a brand, and whispered into the dark the only word the dream had left her:

"Lena."

She didn't know why. She didn't know where it came from, or what it meant, or why it felt less like a name than like a door — slightly ajar, with light behind it, and the smell of turned earth, and someone singing.

But she wrote it down.
