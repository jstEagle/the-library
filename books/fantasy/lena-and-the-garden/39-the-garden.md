## 39. The Garden

The salt line ran through the field behind Casper's cottage like a scar that had decided to become a road. Forty paces of pale ground where nothing had grown for a year after Vessany dissolved—not because the soil was poisoned, but because the soil had forgotten how. She had tested it once, early on, out of professional habit: knelt down, pressed her palm flat against the earth, and waited for the country underneath to tell her its name. Nothing came. Just dirt. Just the honest, dumb silence of dirt, which was more than she had ever gotten from a map.

She planted the first seeds in March, on a morning so cold her breath hung in the air like small ghosts refusing to move on.

Teodor had brought them in a leather pouch that had once, he admitted, held a god's teeth. He poured them into her cupped hands with the reverence of a man returning borrowed money, and she had looked down at them and felt her chest tighten around something she could not identify—a sensation like a word on the tip of the tongue, like a door remembered but never opened.

"Where did these come from?" she had asked.

"Everywhere," Teodor said. He was thinner now, grayer, his hands still carrying the permanent half-moon crescents of grave-dirt in the creases of his knuckles. "A jar from a shrine in the hill country. A pocketful from a dead woman's coat. Seeds the old exiles sewed into their hems when they fled, in case they ever wanted to plant a piece of home somewhere else." He shrugged, a gesture that contained forty years of exile in a single lift of the shoulders. "People carry strange things when they run. Seeds. Songs. Names. The small things that fit in a hem."

"And these grew in Vessany?"

"Some of them. Some of them grew *because* of Vessany, which is a different thing." He had looked at the salt line then, at the pale scar in the grass, and his voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet when he spoke of graves. "Ground that's been erased doesn't stay empty, Casper. It stays *hungry*. You don't fill a wound like that with nothing. You fill it with something living, and you let the living do the remembering."

She had not understood him then. She was beginning to understand him now, in the middle of June, kneeling in the dirt with her sleeves pushed up and her hands black to the wrist.

* * *

The garden did not grow the way gardens were supposed to grow.

Casper had been a mapper her whole life, and a mapper knows that things occupy space according to rules: the oak is taller than the fern, the river runs downhill, the row you planted in April comes up in May in the order you planted it. The garden behind her cottage obeyed none of this. The seeds came up in a single week, all together, as if they had been waiting in their leather pouch for permission. They grew in spirals instead of rows. A vine she had never seen before—blue-green, with leaves shaped like the delta at the end of a river's course—climbed the fence post on the east side and refused, absolutely refused, to climb the one on the west.

And the flowers. When they opened, in the second week of June, they opened in colors Casper had no names for.

She tried, at first, to name them. It was reflex. Her whole career had been built on the conviction that an unnamed thing was an unguarded thing, that the map was a kind of armor, that if you wrote a coastline down honestly enough the coastline could not lie to you. She sat in the dirt with her ledger open on her knee—she still kept ledgers; she supposed she would keep ledgers until she died, it was the one part of herself the country had not taken—and she wrote descriptions. *Petals the color of the moment before a storm breaks. A yellow like the memory of yellow. Blue, but blue as heard rather than seen.*

The descriptions were true and they were useless. The flowers were not colors she had lost. They were colors that had never existed anywhere but in the soil of an erased country, and the soil had kept them the way a miser keeps coins: not to spend, but because they were *his*.

She stopped writing on the fourth day. She closed the ledger, sat back on her heels, and looked at the garden for a long time.

It was not grief, exactly, that she felt. Grief required an object, and her grief had been amputated at the door of Vessany along with everything else. What she felt was more like standing in a house she had lived in once and could not remember living in, and finding her own handwriting on the walls.

The flowers turned toward her when she moved. Not toward the sun—toward *her*. All of them, all at once, a slow and silent congregation.

"All right," she said to them. "I don't know what you are. But you're welcome here."

The wind came up from the south, and the whole garden moved, and for one impossible half-second Casper heard—not remembered, *heard*, the way you hear a bell in the next room—four bars of a song she did not know. It was gone before she could hold it. She knelt in the dirt and wept without knowing why, and the blue-green vine put out one new leaf, gently, like a hand offered.

* * *

The neighbor was called Marta Ostrow, and she was the kind of woman who considered a fence a personal insult and an unexplained phenomenon a moral failing. She appeared at the garden's edge on a Tuesday evening, arms folded, in the posture of a woman who had been composing her opening remarks since breakfast.

"That's not wheat," she said.

"No," Casper agreed.

"It's not barley, or rye, or flax, or anything else that grows in this district, because I've grown them all and my mother before me, and my mother's mother, and I'll thank you not to tell me it's some Corvane fancy seed you bought off a peddler, because I know every peddler on this road and none of them sell *that*." She pointed one thick finger at the blue-green vine, which had, in the past week, produced flowers like small brass bells. "What is it?"

Casper looked at the garden. The garden looked back at her, in its slow, vegetable way. The flowers had opened again that morning, all of them, in colors the world had no words for, colors that had lived for forty years in a pouch made from a dead woman's coat, colors that had survived the erasure of an entire country by being small enough to sew into a hem.

She thought about lying. It would have been easy. She could have invented a merchant, a seed catalog, a botanical Latin name—she had spent her life making maps, and a map was only a lie told with sufficient care. She could have given Marta Ostrow a name for the flowers and the neighbor would have gone home satisfied, and the flowers would have gone on being nameless underneath the name, the way a person goes on being themselves underneath a nickname.

But she had rewritten the ledgers. She had burned the seals. The first entry in the new book of the Guild of Impossible Things, in her own hand, in ink she had ground herself: *Nothing traded here shall be taken from anyone's remembering.* And she had meant it. She had meant it with the whole hollowed-out weight of everything she no longer knew she had lost.

"I don't know," she said.

Marta Ostrow's eyebrows climbed toward her kerchief. "You don't *know*."

"I don't know what they're called. I don't know where they come from, not really. A friend saved the seeds from places that don't exist anymore." Casper brushed the dirt from her hands and stood, her knees cracking in a way that reminded her she was no longer young. "They grow toward me instead of the sun. They bloom in colors I can't describe. And I don't know what they are."

The two women regarded each other across the garden's spiral rows. Somewhere down the road a cart rattled past, and the ordinary sounds of the ordinary world went on being ordinary, and the flowers turned their nameless faces toward the fading light.

Then Marta Ostrow did something Casper had not expected. She stepped over the low stone border—fences, Casper was learning, meant nothing to her—and knelt beside the vine with the brass-bell flowers, and she looked at it the way Teodor looked at graves: with the professional attention of a woman who had spent her life attending to things that were finished.

"My grandmother had a word," she said slowly. "For a color. She used to say it when she was mending, when the dye came out wrong, some shade of between-red-and-brown that she loved and couldn't match. She said it was a Vessani word. She'd been a girl there, before." She touched one of the bell-flowers, very lightly, with one work-roughened fingertip. "That's the color. That one. That's the word she meant, and I never knew what the word was, and I thought I'd never see the color again."

Casper's throat tightened around the wordless thing that lived in her chest now, the thing that had been her mother and her country and her name, all of it gone through a door she had opened herself.

"Would you like to help with the watering?" she asked.

Marta Ostrow stood up, folded her arms again, and became once more the imperious neighbor of the Tuesday morning. "I'll come Thursdays," she said. "And I'll bring my granddaughter, because a child ought to see something that doesn't have a name. It's good for them. Teaches them the world is bigger than their schoolbooks." She was already walking away, then paused at the gate without turning around. "The yellow ones by the fence," she said. "My grandmother had a word for those, too."

She left. Casper stood alone in the garden as the light went amber and then violet, and she laughed—a short, surprised, unguarded laugh, the first in longer than she could measure, which was not a long time, because measuring time had gotten strange since the door.

She didn't know. She had said it out loud, to a neighbor, about her own garden, and the sky had not fallen and the ground had not opened and the gods had not come to collect. *I don't know.* Two words, and they had cost her everything she used to be to learn them, and now they were the truest thing in all her ledgers.

* * *

Teodor came on the solstice, as he had promised, and found her sitting in the middle of the garden with her ledger closed beside her and her hands full of the blue-green vine's seed pods, which had ripened overnight and were warm to the touch, like eggs from a bird that had just left the nest.

"You're harvesting," he said.

"I'm *saving*." She held up a pod. It was the color of dusk over water. "Marta Ostrow wants some for her granddaughter. The widow Hessel from the crossroads wants some of the yellow. A man came from two towns over because he'd heard about the flowers, and he stood at the fence for an hour, and then he cried, and then he asked if he could have a cutting, and I said yes, and I didn't ask him why he cried." She looked up at Teodor, and the evening light caught the gray in his beard and the crescents of old grave-dirt in his knuckles. "They're spreading. Not the plants. The *wanting* them. People come from miles. They stand at my fence and they look at something they have no name for, and they go home quieter than they came."

Teodor lowered himself to the ground beside her, with the care of a man whose body had carried too many coffins. He took a seed pod from her hand and turned it over.

"Ground that's been erased doesn't stay empty," he said. "I told you."

"You told me."

"I've been burying things for fifty years, Casper. Dead people, dead gods, dead villages. And here's what the ground taught me, if you listen: nothing that was loved goes all the way down. It comes back up. Different. Smaller, sometimes. Stranger. It comes up as a flower where a house was, or a spring where a battlefield was, or a word in a child's mouth that nobody taught her." He pressed the pod back into her palm and closed her fingers around it. "Your country's gone. I know. I carried the last of it out myself, in a leather pouch, in my teeth, some days. But it's coming up. Look around you. It's coming up all over this field, and it doesn't remember being a country, and it doesn't need to. It just needs to *grow*."

The first star came out. The garden's flowers did not close with the light, as ordinary flowers do; they opened wider, all of them, and their colors deepened into shades that belonged to no daylight anywhere, and the wind moved through them, and the wind carried, very faintly, four bars of a song.

Casper went still. Teodor watched her.

"Do you hear that?" she whispered.

"No," said Teodor, who had heard it every evening for a month and knew better than to say so.

She hummed it. Four notes, rising, then a fifth that fell away like a step down into a garden. She hummed it twice, three times, her eyes closed, her dirt-blackened hands resting open on her knees, and the flowers turned toward the sound of her voice the way they turned toward everything about her, which was to say: completely.

"I can't place it," she said, opening her eyes. "I've been humming it for weeks. I don't know where it came from. I don't know what it's called. I don't know if it's a lullaby or a work song or a hymn or the first four notes of something enormous that I've forgotten the rest of." She laughed again, that short surprised laugh. "I don't know. Isn't that something? I've made a whole life out of knowing. Maps and ledgers and names for everything. And the truest thing I own now is four notes I can't place."

Teodor smiled, slow and creased. "What will you do with it?"

"What I do with everything." She picked up her ledger, opened it to the first clean page, and uncapped her pen. "I write it down. Honestly. Incomplete. With the blank spaces left blank." She began to mark the staff, the four notes, and then—deliberately, with a care she had never brought to any coastline—she left the rest of the page empty. "Someone will finish it. Not me. Someone who comes after. That's how it works, I think. That's the only trade left that's honest: you pass down what you have, even the broken parts. *Especially* the broken parts."

The garden breathed around them in the dark. Down the road, in a farmhouse, a granddaughter was being told about a word for a color that her great-great-grandmother had loved. In a pouch on Casper's shelf, seeds waited for spring, and for other springs after that, in hems and pockets and hands not yet born. And the salt line under the soil, the scar where a country had been, was no longer pale. The roots had found it. The roots had gone down into it like fingers into a wound, and the wound was closing, and what grew over it grew in colors no map would ever carry, and that was all right. That had always been all right.

Casper set down her pen and looked at the flowers, and the flowers looked back, and neither of them knew the other's name, and it was enough.

* * *

She was watering at dawn three days later—Thursdays were Marta's days, and the rest belonged to her—when she saw the child.

A girl, perhaps ten, standing at the fence in the gray early light, barefoot, in a traveling coat too big for her. She had walked a long way; her feet were dusty and her eyes had the flat, watchful look of a child who has learned that adults are weather. She held something in her hands, wrapped in cloth, and she held it the way Teodor held seeds: like something entrusted.

"You're the one with the nameless garden," the girl said.

"I'm the one with the garden," Casper said carefully. "The namelessness is mostly the flowers' own business."

The girl considered this, found it acceptable, and unwrapped the cloth. Inside was a book—small, water-stained, its cover cracked with age. A ledger. Casper's heart, which had learned to be quiet, went loud.

"Where did you get that?"

"It was my mother's. She said—" The girl's voice caught, steadied. "She said when the flowers started blooming out here, I was to bring it to you. She said you'd know what to do with it. She said—" the girl frowned, reciting, holding the words exactly, the way children hold words that matter, "—she said, *tell the mapmaker the last page is hers, and tell her she wrote it in a hand she doesn't remember, and tell her to look at the ink.*"

She held the book up through the fence slats. Casper took it. The leather was cold. The paper inside was the heavy, cream-colored stock the old guild archives used, the stock that had been in her hands ten thousand times, the stock of the ledger older than the guild itself.

She turned to the last page.

The handwriting was her mother's. She did not know how she knew that—she could not have described the hand, could not have said what made it Ilse's and no one else's—but she knew it the way the flowers knew the sun, the way the soil knew the seed, the way she knew the four notes of the song. Some things had gone through the door and some things had gone under it, and this had gone under.

The ink was not black. It was not brown. It was a color she had no name for, and she had seen it every morning for three months, turning its nameless face up out of her garden.

The entry was short. She read it once, and the world went very still around her, and the child at the fence watched her with those flat, patient, weather-watching eyes.

Then Casper looked up, and past the girl, at the road—where, far off in the dawn mist, a figure was walking toward the cottage. Tall. Unhurried. Carrying a surveyor's stake the way a man carries a walking stick, and smiling, from a hundred paces away, a smile she could see perfectly.

"Child," Casper said quietly, closing the ledger, "how fast can you run?"
