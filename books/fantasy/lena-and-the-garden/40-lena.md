## 40. Lena

The morning fog came off the harbor the way it always did in Corvane, slow and gray and smelling of salt and tar, and Lena Villalobos stood at the window of the old guild office with a cup of tea going cold in her hands and watched it eat the ships.

She had been Lena for three years now. The name had arrived one night without ceremony, the way her tears had arrived in the weeks after the salt line—without object, without story, just arriving. She had woken on Teodor's bedroll with the grass wet against her cheek and Oren's face hovering above her, and when he had said her name—*Casper*—the word had landed like a coin dropped into a well she could not hear the bottom of. It had taken her a long moment to understand he was calling *her*. Longer to answer.

Lena had come later, chosen from nowhere she could name. It felt like a key that fit a lock she no longer owned. But it fit. That was enough.

Behind her, in the workroom, someone was being very quiet, which meant someone was up to something.

"Petra," Lena said, not turning around. "If you're drawing on the practice vellum again, remember what happened to the last sheet."

A pause. A guilty silence with a specific shape. "I'm not drawing," Petra said, which was true only in the sense that she had already finished.

Lena smiled into her tea and let her have another minute. The girl was eleven, sharp-elbowed and sharp-eyed, the daughter of a chandler two streets over who had brought her in last spring because she wouldn't stop mapping things—the alley behind the house, the arrangement of fish in the market stalls, the exact pattern of cracks in their kitchen floor, annotated in a child's careful hand: *this one goes all the way through.* Her mother had thought it a sickness. Lena had recognized it immediately as a vocation.

She set down the cup and went in.

Petra sat at the big worktable, knees folded under her on the chair because sitting properly was for other people, bent over a fresh sheet with the good ink—the expensive ink, the ink Lena kept locked—and before her lay a map of the harbor district so precise it made Lena's chest ache. Every warehouse. Every mooring post. Every gull, presumably, if you looked closely enough.

Except for one place.

In the middle of the map, where the old counting-house stood on Tallow Street—a building everyone in Corvane knew, a building Petra walked past every day on her way here—there was a blank. A clean white rectangle, edged carefully in fine hatching, with a small notation beside it in Petra's tidy letters:

*Unknown. I have not been inside.*

Lena stood over her shoulder for a long time.

"You drew the whole street," she said finally. "Every window. You drew the number of barrels outside the cooper's, and I'd wager you counted them twice."

"Three times," Petra said. "Two were wrong."

"And then you left a hole in the middle of your map."

Petra's chin came up, defensive, ready to be scolded. "You said—" She stopped. Started again, slower, testing the words. "You said a map is a promise. That every line you draw is you telling whoever holds it: *I saw this. I swear I saw this.* And I haven't seen inside the counting-house. So if I drew it, I'd be lying."

Lena pulled out the second chair and sat down across from her.

Three years ago—no. Not three years. Some other amount of time, measured in some other currency, because the woman she had been before the salt line had traded in borrowed years and bottled storms and knew better than anyone that time did not flow, it *accumulated*, it pooled in the low places of a life—three years ago, or whatever it was, there had been a guild in this building that traded in impossible things. There had been ledgers full of debts owed by people to gods and gods to people. There had been a founding charter, burned in the yard out back on a windy afternoon while Oren Sable wept openly and unashamed, the seals cracking in the fire like knuckles.

There had been a country. She knew this the way you know a room you've slept in: not as an image but as a certainty, a weight, a set of proportions. Vessany. She could say the word. She could write it, and did, in the new ledger, in the entry that would never be erased: *Vessany existed. Forty years it was gone from every map, and it was real the whole time. This page exists so that no one can ever say otherwise.*

But she could not see it. When she closed her eyes and reached for it, she found the same clean-edged blankness that Petra had drawn on Tallow Street, hatched neatly at the borders, annotated in a hand that was almost her own: *Unknown. I have not been—*

And then nothing. Even the annotation dissolved when she looked at it directly.

She had learned to stop looking directly. Most days.

"The counting-house," Lena said. "What will you do about your hole?"

"Knock on the door?" Petra offered. "Ask to see inside?"

"That's one way." Lena turned the girl's map toward herself, studied the blank space, the honest hatching around it. "Or you leave it. You give the map to someone else with the hole still in it, and you tell them: *here is everything I know, and here is the edge of it.* And maybe they knock on the door. Or maybe they draw what they imagine is inside, and then their apprentice corrects them, and their apprentice's apprentice corrects them, and in a hundred years the counting-house has been drawn a hundred ways, and every single one of them is a little bit true."

Petra frowned. "That sounds like a bad map."

"It sounds like a *living* one." Lena tapped the blank rectangle once, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "The worst maps I've ever seen were the ones with no holes in them. Perfect maps. Maps where every line was certain." She heard her own voice go somewhere distant and reeled it back. "Certainty is what gets eaten first, little one. Remember that. Whatever else you learn from me, remember that."

Petra wrote it down. Of course she did; she wrote everything down. *Certainty is what gets eaten first.* Lena watched the letters form and felt something turn over quietly in her chest, some small animal shifting in its sleep.

* * *

Oren came at midday, which meant something, because Oren never came at midday if he could come at dusk, and never at dusk if he could put it off until morning. He had grown old in the last three years the way a tree grows old—all at once, in a single storm, and then standing afterward as if nothing happened. His hands shook now unless he was holding a ledger, and then they were steady as compass needles.

He nodded to Petra, who was pretending very hard to be absorbed in her hatching, and lowered himself into the chair across from Lena's desk with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions.

"The last of the charter cases cleared yesterday," he said. "The Marrowfield debt. You remember it?"

"I remember the file." She remembered the file the way she remembered most things now—by its edges. A village that had sold its winter to a hedge-god in a bad year, forty years back, and the debt had come due and come due and come due, generation after generation, until the guild had stepped in and bought it out and torn up the paper. "It's done?"

"Burned the contract myself. The god screamed. Small scream. More of a whine, really." He almost smiled. "They're learning that we don't honor those papers anymore. Word travels slowly among gods—they're used to debtors running. They're not used to creditors burning the ledger."

"They'll adapt," Lena said. "Everything adapts. That's the trouble with everything."

Oren turned his hat in his shaking hands. Under the desk, out of Petra's sightline, he passed her a folded paper, and she opened it and read it and felt the familiar cold walk up her arms and sit down in her shoulders.

A surveyor's stake. Drawn in ink, from a report: found driven into the earth at the edge of the salt flats east of the borderlands, plain wood, unremarkable, except that grass refused to grow within a hand's breadth of it, and a shepherd who touched it forgot the name of his firstborn for six hours.

"They're staking again," Oren said quietly. "Not many. Not yet. Scouting lines. Whatever sealed itself under the border three years ago—it wasn't destroyed, Lena. We both know that. It was *closed*. And a closed thing waits."

She looked at the drawing for a long moment. Then she folded the paper, and held a corner of it to the candle on her desk, and watched it catch, and dropped it into the brass dish she kept there for exactly this purpose. The flame took the stake, took the shepherd, took the six lost hours, and gave back light.

"Then we keep mapping," she said. "We keep teaching them to knock on doors instead of imagining what's behind them. We keep the holes in our maps honest." She met Oren's eyes. "You didn't come at midday to tell me about one stake."

"No." He rubbed his face. "No, I came because the archive sent word. The copyists finished the third volume. Everything recovered. Everything the dead carried out, everything the exiles remembered, everything pieced together from forty years of smuggled songs and burnt papers and—" He stopped, and his voice did something complicated. "Everything your predecessor scattered. Ilse's fragments. They're all transcribed. Cross-referenced. It's done, Casper."

*Lena,* she almost corrected him, and didn't. He was the only one left who used the old name, and only in rooms without children in them, and she had decided a long time ago that letting him was a kind of mercy—for him, and maybe for whatever was left of her.

"It's done," she repeated. "Good. Put it in the deep shelf. Unlocked. Anyone may read it."

"You won't look at it yourself?"

She was quiet for a moment. Outside the window, the fog was lifting off the harbor, and a ship was coming in that hadn't been visible ten minutes ago, and the world went on doing its ordinary miraculous work of appearing.

"I read the first volume," she said. "You remember. I read about the harvest festivals and the salt law and the names of the rivers, and it was like reading about a stranger's beautiful dream. It hurt in a secondhand way. Grief for someone else's loss." She turned her teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "But sometimes, reading it, my hand would move on its own. Marginal notes. In a handwriting I don't recognize and know perfectly. Once I caught myself humming along with a song printed on the page before my eyes reached it." She looked up. "The country isn't gone from me, Oren. It's just somewhere I can't follow. And I've made my peace with knocking on that door and having no one answer. What I won't do is stand outside it forever, rattling the handle and crying. I did that for a year. It fed nothing and grew nothing."

Oren nodded slowly. He was crying a little now, the way he did, easily and without shame, and he wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand and pretended he hadn't.

"Your mother," he started, and stopped. Tried again. "Ilse. In the fragments, she comes across as—"

"As a thief," Lena said gently. "As a woman who stole her daughter's country to save her daughter's life. I know. I've read the testimony." She smiled, and the smile was real even if the memory underneath it belonged to someone else. "It's the best crime I've ever heard of. If I ever meet her, I'm going to tell her so."

"She'd have liked you," Oren said. "The you that you are now."

"I think," Lena said, "that the me I am now is the only me she ever got to meet. So I hope so."

* * *

Afternoon lessons were cartography proper: scale, projection, the ethics of distortion. Petra worked with her tongue between her teeth, inking coastlines, and Lena moved around the room straightening lamps and sharpening pencils and being, deliberately, boringly present, because apprentices learned more from watching a master be ordinary than from any lecture.

It was near the end of the hour that Petra asked it. She asked things sideways, always, approaching questions the way she approached cats.

"Lena. Why are you called that? Lena. Everyone else has family stories. Marta's named after her grandmother. Joss is named after a sailor who saved his father. You're just—" She gestured with the pencil, apologetic. "Lena."

Lena considered several answers. *It chose me. It fell on me like weather. I woke up one morning on the far side of a door I opened from the inside, having paid for the opening with everything I loved, and this name was what remained in my pockets.*

"I don't know," she said, and found that it was easy, and true, and cost almost nothing anymore. "I woke up with it. Like waking with a song in your head that you can't place. Some names aren't given. They're found."

"Found where?"

"In the last place you'd think to look." She ruffled the girl's hair, which Petra endured with the martyred patience of the eleven-year-old. "Finish your coastline. The eastern shore is drifting."

But the question stayed with her after Petra had gone home, after the lamps were trimmed, after the harbor had gone dark and gold-flecked with reflected lights. She sat at her desk with the new ledger open—the guild's ledger, the rebuilt one, the one whose first entry she had written in her own hand on the first night back in Corvane while her eyes were still swollen and her hands still smelled of salt:

*Nothing traded here shall be taken from anyone's remembering.*

Below it, three years of entries. Debts forgiven. Contracts burned. Storms bottled and sold honestly, years lent and returned with interest, prayers answered by *people* now, flesh-and-blood guild members who drove stakes in the ground and said *someone was here, someone saw this, you are not alone with your impossible thing.* The guild of impossible things, remade into something she could bear to be part of.

She turned to the back pages, the ones she kept for no practical purpose, and began, as she did most nights, to sketch. Coastlines from dreams. Buildings she had never entered. A door, once, that she had drawn and then burned, because it had too many hinges.

Tonight her hand made hills. Low green hills, terraced, with stone walls between them, and a road that switchbacked up through orchards toward a ridge where—her pencil hesitated, hovered, and then drew it anyway—a small white shrine stood with its door open.

She didn't know the hills. She wanted to be clear with herself about that: she did not *know* them. And yet the walls were the right thickness. The orchard trees were pruned the right way, open-crowned, generous. The shrine's door faced east, toward morning, which was apparently how shrines ought to face, according to some authority she could not cite.

*Unknown,* she wrote beneath it, in the manner she taught. *I have not been here. I may have been here. Both things are true.*

She signed it, as she signed everything now: *Lena Villalobos.* The surname had come with the given name, attached like a boat to its anchor. Villalobos. Wolves. Somewhere, sometime, there had been wolves, or people bold enough to claim them.

* * *

It was raining lightly when she walked Petra home that evening—the girl insisted on evening lessons now, greedy for them—and the streets of Corvane shone and smelled of wet stone and frying fish and someone's woodsmoke. Petra talked the whole way, about coastlines and gulls and whether maps could lie by telling the truth at the wrong scale (they could; Lena promised to devote a whole lesson to it), and Lena listened with half her mind and let the other half drift over the rooftops toward the water.

At the chandler's door, Petra stopped. Frowned. Reached into her satchel and pulled out her practice book—the cheap one, the one full of abandoned attempts and ink blots and the constant marginalia of a working mind.

"I keep forgetting to show you this," she said. "It's probably nothing."

She opened the book to the last written page and held it out.

On it, in Petra's careful hand, was a line of musical notation. Rough, amateurish, a child's best attempt at catching something airborne—but notation, unmistakably, with a time signature and everything. Beneath it she had written: *You were humming this. Tuesday. And Thursday. I don't know it, so I wrote it down so it wouldn't get lost.*

Lena looked at the notes for a long time.

The rain came down softly on the awning above them. Somewhere down the street a dog barked twice and thought better of it. And Lena Villalobos, who could not remember her mother's face, who could not remember a country, who had paid the whole of her remembering for a door to close behind her—stood in a wet street in a free city holding a child's transcription of a song she could not place, and hummed it.

It rose in her without permission. Three notes climbing, a fourth that fell away like a step down into a garden, a phrase that turned at the end the way a path turns at the end, toward home. Her voice cracked in the middle—not with grief, or not only with grief, but with the sheer strangeness of carrying something she could not open, a gift wrapped in paper she was no longer allowed to tear.

"There," Petra said, satisfied, and wrote beneath the notation: *confirmed.*

"Petra." Lena's voice came out rougher than she intended. She crouched to the girl's height, rain dripping off the awning edge between them. "Do you know what you did? Do you know what that is?"

"A song?"

"A song that survived." She tucked a strand of hair behind the girl's ear, gentle as handling a moth. "Listen to me. One day you'll map places no one has mapped. You'll find holes in the world that other people filled in with lies because the lies were comfortable. And you'll be tempted, sometimes, to fill your own holes—to make your maps perfect, to make yourself certain. Don't. Leave the blanks. Write *unknown* and mean it. Because the blanks are where the true things wait. Songs. Names. Countries." She smiled, and meant it. "People."

Petra was studying her face with that unnerving directness, cataloguing it, mapping it. "Whose song is it?" she asked. "Yours?"

Lena stood. The rain gentled. Down at the harbor, a ship's bell rang the hour, and the sound rolled across the water and came back changed.

"I don't know," she said. "But it knows me. That's better than nothing. That's nearly everything."

She walked home through the shining streets with the song still rising in her, unplaced, unnamed, unhoused—and singing anyway. And behind her, in a child's cheap practice book, in careful ink that would outlast them both, the melody waited on the page: written down, confirmed, passed on.

Whatever we lose, it seemed to say, in notes instead of words—we pass on anyway.
