## 1. The Ledger of Small Miracles

The trouble with bottling a thunderstorm, Edmund Yarrow had learned over eleven years of keeping the Guild of Impossible Things' ledgers, was never the catching. Anyone with a strong jar, a long ladder, and a healthy disrespect for their own mortality could catch one. The trouble was the paperwork.

She sat at her desk in the counting room of the guildhall — a room that smelled of ink, beeswax, and the faint ozone ghost of trades past — and turned the bottle in the lamplight. It was no larger than a wine flask, stoppered with wax and iron, and inside it the storm turned restlessly: a fistful of cloud the color of a bruise, rain falling upward, lightning flickering against the glass like something knocking to be let out.

*Item the first,* she wrote, in the small, exact hand that three generations of guildmasters had praised and none had bothered to thank. *One (1) bottled thunderstorm, summer vintage, caught off the western hills by Master Ferrow on the fourteenth of Harvestmonth. Contents agitated. Provenance clean. No liens, claims, or curses attached.*

She paused. She uncapped the bottle's wax seal just enough to listen.

The storm grumbled at her, low and petulant, like a cat put out into the rain it had been born from.

"No liens," she confirmed aloud, and wrote it down, because the ledger did not care what she said unless she said it in ink.

That was the whole of the guild's business, when you stripped away the romance of it. People thought the Guild of Impossible Things dealt in wonders — and it did, in its way — but wonders were only ever the raw material. The true trade was in *keeping*. Memories kept safe from grief. Names kept out of cruel mouths. Small miracles kept from being spent all at once by people who didn't know their worth. Every shelf in the vaults below held something somebody had trusted the guild to hold, and every shelf answered, in the end, to a line in Edmund's ledgers.

She was very good at lines.

She was less good at everything else, which was why the counting room was hers alone after sundown, and why the tea she drank had gone cold an hour ago, and why the chair across from her desk — the one that had belonged to old Master Quill before he died, the one nobody had ever formally reassigned — remained empty, as it always did, as it always would.

Edmund flexed her right hand. The oath-mark sat along the back of it, a fine silver script that curled from wrist to knuckle like frost grown deliberate: *I will speak, and my word will be done.* The mark of a swordspeaker, though there had been no swords worth speaking for in twenty years, and precious few before that. The last of a lineage that had once been an army. The last, full stop.

The mark trembled.

It had been trembling for three weeks now — a fine, fast shiver, as if the script were cold, or frightened, or trying to tell her something in a language she'd been taught to read but not to hear. She had told no one. She had, in fact, gone to some lengths to tell no one: wearing her cuff buttoned, holding her quill in a grip so controlled the nib sometimes tore the page, arranging her life around the small dishonesty the way you might arrange furniture around a crack in the floor until the whole room faced the wrong way.

She pressed her left thumb hard against the mark, and the trembling stilled, the way it always did, the way it was beginning to do more slowly each time.

"Right," she said to the empty chair, because talking to furniture was a habit she'd stopped apologizing for somewhere around year six. "Where were we."

The ledger were we. She dipped her pen.

*Condition of contents:* she wrote. *Restless but sound. Storm has been informed of its storage terms and has declined to accept them.*

A knock at the counting room door — two raps, then a third delivered apologetically late, which meant Ferrow.

"It's open," Edmund said, not looking up. "And if you've come to argue about the appraisal fee again, the answer is still that catching a storm is worth forty marks and complaining about it is worth nothing."

Ferrow came in anyway. He was a broad, rumpled man built like a piece of well-used furniture himself, with sawdust permanently in his eyebrows from his workshop upstairs, and he carried the particular expression of a man who has done something impressive and wants it noticed without having to ask.

"You listened to it," he said.

"I always listen."

"And?"

"And it's a good storm," Edmund allowed. "Clean-tempered underneath the bluster. Whoever raised it raised it honestly." She looked up. "You're fishing for compliments, Ferrow, and I keep accounts, not aquariums."

He grinned and dropped into Master Quill's old chair without asking, which was either a kindness or an intrusion depending on how lonely she admitted to being, and she had decided years ago to file it under kindness. "Forty marks," he said. "For a storm I stood on a hilltop in the teeth of, mind, with hail the size of—"

"Hail the size of your regrets, yes, you've told me." She finished the entry, blotted it, and turned the ledger toward him. "Sign here. And here. Initial the condition note."

"'Storm has been informed of its storage terms,'" Ferrow read aloud, and snorted. "One day it'll get out, you know. They always do, the big ones. You'll come down to the vaults and find a wet patch and a very smug hole in the ceiling."

"The vaults are warded nine ways," Edmund said. "If it gets out, it will have signed a release form first. Everything in this building obeys procedure eventually."

But her hand, resting flat on the ledger, gave its small treacherous shiver as she said it, and Ferrow — who was not a clever man in most directions but was a perceptive one in exactly one — glanced at her cuff.

"Cold in here?" he asked.

"Warm as toast," Edmund said, and moved her hand beneath the desk, and hated herself a little for how smoothly the lie came. Swordspeakers weren't supposed to lie. It wasn't forbidden; it was simply that words came out of them already shaped, already weighted, and a lie from a swordspeaker's mouth landed heavier than the truth ever could. She had gotten very good at lies that weighed almost nothing. It was, she suspected, the only kind of swordsmanship she had left.

Ferrow signed, complained about the fee once more for form's sake, and went up to bed. The door clicked shut behind him. The counting room settled back into its familiar silence — the tick of the mantel clock, the restless mutter of the storm in its bottle, the creak of the guildhall's old bones shifting through the night watch.

Edmund worked another hour. There was always another hour. A widow in the lower town had deposited her late husband's laughter — six minutes of it, in a locket — and needed the interest terms explained for the fourth time. A hedge-witch had traded a memory of her mother's face for a cure she couldn't otherwise afford, and Edmund had entered it carefully, neutrally, *one memory, visual, familial, surrendered freely*, and had not written down the way the woman's hands shook, because the ledger did not care and Edmund was not sure she could survive writing it down.

That was the loneliness, if she was honest, and she tried to be honest at least once a week, usually on Sundays with the shutters closed. Not the empty chair. Not the cold tea. The loneliness was this: that she spent her days recording what people gave away to survive, and she knew — precisely, to the decimal, in her own exact hand — what everything cost except herself. Her name was in the front of every ledger. *Keeper: E. Yarrow.* That was all anyone had ever asked of her. That was all, she sometimes feared, there was.

The oath-mark shivered again. Harder, this time. She set down the pen and watched it — watched the silver script flicker like the lightning in the bottle, like something knocking to be let out.

"That's enough of that," she told it quietly.

The mark did not answer. It never had. But tonight — she would remember this later, in the way one remembers the last ordinary thing — tonight it felt less like a tremor and more like a pull. Faint. Directional. Somewhere north and east of the counting room, toward the border, toward the wards that had stood unexamined for longer than she'd been alive, something was tugging on the far end of her word.

She pressed it flat. She finished the widow's entry. She closed the ledger, blew out the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark with the storm muttering on the shelf beside her, two restless things in glass and skin respectively, both waiting for weather.

Outside the window, the sky over the city was clear and thick with stars, the same stars that had hung over every ledger she'd ever kept, patient as punctuation.

She did not know yet that she was looking at them for the last time.

* * *

The dream, when it finally took her, was an old one. She stood in a field of tall grass with a sword too heavy for a child's hands, and a voice like her grandmother's said, *say it, little bird, say it and mean it,* and she opened her mouth, and —

She woke to the sound of the storm bottle rattling on its shelf.

Not muttering. Rattling. She was out of bed and down the stairs with a candle before her mind fully joined her body, and in the counting room the bottle was jumping against the wood like a trapped heart, lightning hammering the glass from inside, and every other vessel in the room — the lockets, the jars, the little lead boxes of kept names — had begun, softly, to hum.

Edmund stood in the doorway with her candle shaking in her hand and felt the pull again, unmistakable now, reeling in from the northeast like a fisherman's hook finding flesh.

Her oath-mark was burning.

"Everything obeys procedure eventually," she whispered, to no one, to the dark, to whatever was coming — and far above the guildhall, above the city, above the border wards that had guarded two realms since before her grandmother's grandmother, the first star went out.
