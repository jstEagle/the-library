## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with cataloging a bottled thunderstorm, Edmund Yarrow had learned by her third hour at the desk, was that thunderstorms did not believe in ledgers.

The specimen sat on her blotter in a jar of smoked glass no bigger than a loaf of bread, and it sulked. Every few minutes it threw itself against the glass with a sound like a fistful of gravel thrown at a window, and every few minutes she wrote down the attempt, because that was what the ledger was *for*. The Guild of Impossible Things kept records of everything. Especially the things that objected to being recorded.

*Item 4471,* she wrote, in her small, exact hand. *One (1) thunderstorm, bottled. Provenance: the shepherd Tam Wicke of the Greyfell slopes, who caught it in his coat after it wandered off from its parent front and could not find its way home. Temperament: sulky. Humidity: considerable.*

She paused to dip her pen and the storm took the opportunity to produce a single, indignant raindrop against the inside of the glass.

"Noted," Edmund told it, and noted it.

This was the work, and she was good at it, and she had long ago stopped pretending that being good at it was enough. The guild's counting room smelled of ink and candlewax and the faint metallic tang of miracles in storage — like the air after lightning, or before it, depending on the miracle. Around her rose the shelves: jars of last words, spools of borrowed courage, a crate marked *DO NOT OPEN UNTIL SOMEONE DESERVES IT* that nobody, in forty years, had deserved. Somewhere below, the night clerk was arguing with a customer about the exchange rate on names. Above, through the counting room's one high window, the stars were doing what stars did, which was burn, patiently, in their appointed places.

Edmund liked the stars best for exactly that quality. Patience. Appointedness. A star never renegotiated its position in the sky; a star never sold its neighbor down the river to cover its own debts. She found this restful in a way she could not have explained without admitting things she preferred not to admit.

Her right hand rested on the ledger as she worked, and the oath-mark there — a fine silver scar, old as her memory of holding a sword — lay quiet tonight. That was worth noting too. Some nights it ached like a tooth. Tonight it was only a scar, and she was only a woman doing sums, and the world was exactly as ordered as the ledger said it was.

*Value assessment:* she wrote. *Sentimental: low. Practical: moderate — useful against droughts, sieges, and dull dinner parties. Hazard rating:* 

The storm flared. Not its usual sulky grumble but a real flare, white light boiling up inside the smoked glass, and Edmund set down her pen with the particular calm of a woman who has handled many dangerous objects and intends to go on handling them.

"None of that," she said. "You're an item now. Items behave."

The light guttered. And then, in the space of one heartbeat, every impossible thing in the guild went silent at once.

Edmund had worked here eleven years and had never heard silence like it. The shelves full of murmuring memories went quiet. The spools of courage stopped humming. Down in the vaults, something that had been weeping softly since the reign of the last guildmaster stopped weeping. It was the silence of a room full of people listening.

Then the wards screamed.

There was no better word for it. The border wards ran along the edge of the world like a fence runs along the edge of a field, and everyone knew they were there the way everyone knows the sea is there — you didn't think about them, you just built your house a respectful distance inland. But when they screamed, the whole city heard it, a sound like a violin bow drawn across the sky itself, high and thin and wrong, rising past the range of hearing and then somehow continuing anyway, felt in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the fillings of one's teeth if one had any.

Edmund stood up so fast her chair went over backward. She did not hear it hit the floor.

She looked out the high window, and the stars were gone.

Not clouded. Not dimmed. *Gone.* The whole black bowl of the sky, wiped clean, as though some enormous hand had come along and licked the icing off the top of the world. Where the constellations should have been — the Shepherd, the Weeping Sisters, the great slow wheel of the Oathkeeper's Chain — there was nothing. Not darkness, even. Darkness would have been honest. This was absence, a hole shaped like everything that used to be there.

The oath-mark on her hand woke up.

It did not ache this time. It *pulled.* A hook behind her sternum, a fisherman's line tied somewhere under her ribs, hauling gently, insistently, toward the north wall of the guild, toward the hills beyond it, toward whatever breach had opened in the fabric of the night. Her swordspeech — silent for years, folded away like a wedding dress in an attic — stirred in its sleep and turned toward the sound.

"No," Edmund said, out loud, to her own chest, which was not a thing sane people generally did. "Absolutely not. You're retired."

Her chest disagreed. Politely, but firmly, the way a well-trained sword disagrees.

* * *

The stairs down to the guildmaster's study were narrow and worn in the middle, dished by two hundred years of worried feet, and Edmund took them three at a time because the pulling in her ribs made standing still feel like drowning slowly. Halfway down she met the night clerk coming up, carrying a lantern he was gripping in both hands like a life rope.

"The wards—" he began.

"I know," Edmund said.

"—and the stars—"

"I know."

"—and Master Corvane wants everyone—"

"In the study. Yes." She moved past him. He caught her sleeve, and she saw his face in the lantern light, young and gray around the mouth. "Edmund. The ledger room. The items are all facing north."

That stopped her for exactly one second. "Facing?"

"Lying facing. The bottled winds, the caged songs, the — the grief, the grief in the blue jar, it's turned itself to face the north wall. All of them. Like iron filings." His voice dropped. "Like they know where home is."

Edmund looked back up the stairwell toward the counting room, at the silence pouring down it like cold air, and thought of her thunderstorm pressed against the north side of its glass, and did not say what she was thinking, which was: *so do I.*

Corvane's study was the only room in the guild that never changed. Other rooms got new shelving, new locks, new coats of paint whenever the guild could afford them, which was rarely. Corvane's study had the same green lamp, the same worn map of the borderlands, the same smell of pipe smoke and old decisions. Guildmaster Aldous Corvane sat behind his desk with his hands folded, and he was — this was the thing Edmund noticed first, and it frightened her more than the empty sky — he was *calm.*

Around the walls stood the senior members of the guild, maybe twenty of them, in various states of undress and terror. Old Mistress Halloway still had curling papers in her hair. The twins Fen and Ferro had clearly come straight from bed and were wearing matching nightshirts, which they would be mortified about later, if there was a later in which mortification mattered.

"Sit," Corvane said, and everyone sat, because Corvane's voice had the property of furniture: solid, familiar, impossible to argue with.

"The wards have fallen," he said. "All of them, along the entire border, within the space of four minutes. The stars are out. I am aware. Before anyone asks — no, I do not yet know why, and yes, I am taking every measure." He unfolded his hands and laid them flat. "Here is what happens next. Nothing."

A pause, in which twenty people tried to work out whether they'd misheard.

"Nothing?" Mistress Halloway said faintly, papers still in her hair.

"The city will panic by morning regardless. Our task is to make sure the guild does not panic with it. No one speaks of the wards. No one speaks of the stars. No one opens the vaults, no one answers questions from the watch, no one sells anything, buys anything, or trades anything until I say otherwise. As of this moment the guild is closed. We are a counting house, and the count is suspended." He looked around the room, and his gaze touched each face in turn, and Edmund watched him do it and thought, distantly, that he counted people the way she counted miracles. "Am I understood?"

Ferro raised a hand. "Master, with respect — the sky is *empty.* You can't ledger an empty sky."

"You can," Corvane said, "if you write small enough. Which is precisely what we are going to do. Fear is a market like any other, gentlemen, madam, and the first rule of any market is that whoever panics first pays the highest price. We will not pay it." Something moved behind his eyes then, quick and private, gone before Edmund could name it. "The breach will be handled. By me. Alone."

And there it was again — the pull. The hook behind her sternum hauled once, hard, like a fish testing the line, and Edmund's hand curled into a fist over the oath-mark, and she realized with a strange floating clarity that Corvane had just told a room full of experts to stay home while he walked alone toward the largest catastrophe in living memory, and that every instinct she owned was screaming in two directions at once.

Duty said: obey the guildmaster. Duty had been fed and watered and trained since she was seven years old. Duty was the oldest voice in her head, older than her own name.

But the other voice — the swordspeech, the oath, the hooked line — said nothing at all. It simply pulled. North. Toward the breach. Like a compass needle that had finally found its pole after years of spinning, and the terrible thing, the thing she would lie awake examining later, was how much it felt like relief.

"Yarrow." Corvane's eyes had found her. Of course they had. "Your mark. Is it active?"

Every head turned. Twenty faces, lamplit, waiting.

"It's quiet," Edmund said.

"Liar," said the mark, in the wordless way marks have, and she pressed her palm flat against her knee and kept her face the smooth blank page the guild had spent thirty years teaching her to be.

"Good," Corvane said. "Keep it that way. Swordspeech near a breach is like ringing a dinner bell in a famine. Whatever came through the wards will feel it from a hundred miles." He picked up his pipe, considered it, put it down unlit — the first time Edmund had ever seen him decline tobacco, and somehow that small refusal frightened her more than the screaming wards had. "Go home. Sleep if you can. Be here at dawn. Discreetly."

They filed out. In the corridor, Mistress Halloway squeezed Edmund's arm and whispered, "North wall, third shelf, the blue jar — it's *warm*, Edmund. Grief isn't supposed to be warm." And shuffled off toward her bed, papers still in her hair, a woman carrying the end of the world home under her arm like leftover bread.

* * *

Edmund did not go home.

She told herself she was going home. She got as far as the guild's front hall, keys in hand, cloak over her arm, a woman performing departure for an audience of shadows. Then she stood in the doorway a while, looking north, where the hills were, where the breach was, where the hook wanted her, and she had an argument with herself that lasted approximately four seconds and was conducted entirely in the language of the body: her feet said *north*, her duty said *bed*, and her feet won, which told her something about how much of her duty had ever really lived below the neck.

She let herself back into the counting room instead. Just for a moment. Just to check the ledgers — that was the story, and it was even true, the way a locked door is true while the thief picks it.

The silence in there had weight now. She lit one candle and carried it down the aisle between the shelves, and everywhere she looked, the impossible things had turned. The bottled winds strained northward in their stoppered bottles, fogging the glass with their breath. The caged song had wound itself tight against the north bars of its cage. On the third shelf, the blue jar — grief, Mistress Halloway's grief, donated decades ago by a woman who could no longer afford to carry it — sat warm as a sleeping bird, and when Edmund laid her palm against it, gently, the way you'd touch a door you suspected someone was crying behind, the warmth pulsed. Once. Twice. North.

Everything in the guild was pointing at the wound in the world like a roomful of weathervanes.

Except, she noticed, one thing pointed at her.

It was a small item, low on the eastern shelf, in a plain clay pot with no label she could read in the candlelight — and as she passed, it turned. Not north. Toward her. She stopped. She knelt. She lifted the pot, and inside it, coiled in its own dark like a snake made of shadow and patience, lay a length of something that was not rope and not hair and not smoke, and it rose, slowly, toward the warmth of her hand, and the oath-mark on her wrist answered it, silver flaring faintly in the dark like a struck match.

Edmund set the pot down very carefully and stepped back.

"Well," she said to the empty counting room, to the pointing shelves, to the hole in the sky above the roof she couldn't see but could feel like a missing tooth. "That's settled, then."

Because here was the truth she had been circling all night, the truth the ledgers had no column for: the wards hadn't fallen on the guild. They had fallen on *her.* Or rather — and her mind shied from the thought and then returned to it, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth — the wards had fallen, and something in the deep inventory of the world had checked its books, found a debt outstanding, and known exactly whose account to debit. The pull behind her sternum wasn't fear. It was recognition. Somewhere out there in the starless dark, something had said her name — her true name, the one she'd been told she outgrew, the one the guild held in trust — and her blood had answered *present* before her mind had finished asking the question.

She blew out the candle. In the dark, the shelves breathed.

At dawn, Corvane would tell them all to stay discreet, stay small, stay silent. At dawn, Edmund Yarrow would sit at her desk and pick up her pen and open the ledger to a fresh page, because that was what she did, that was who she was, that was the shape cut for her thirty years ago by hands she trusted.

But tonight, alone in the dark among the pointing miracles, she allowed herself one entry, written mentally, in the language of oaths rather than ink:

*Item 4472. One (1) night, starless. Provenance unknown. Value: incalculable. Hazard rating:*

The mark on her hand pulled north, gentle and irresistible as the tide.

*Hazard rating: it already knows my name.*
