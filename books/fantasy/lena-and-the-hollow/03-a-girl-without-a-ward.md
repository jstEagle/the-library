## 3. A Girl Without a Ward

The wards didn't see Lena.

That was the first thing she'd learned about them, back when she was small enough to believe everyone knew this and simply hadn't mentioned it. The wards of the city were old things, older than the walls they ringed, older than the guild that maintained them — great slow rivers of light that ran beneath the cobbles and hummed along the gateposts, tasting every soul that passed for the flavor of permission. Everyone else walked through them like wading through honey. Lena walked through them like walking through morning fog. They parted around her without a ripple. They did not taste her at all.

She had spent years trying not to take it personally.

Tonight, with the sky gone black as a closed ledger and the whole city holding its breath, the wards should have been screaming. She could hear them doing it from three streets away — a high, thin whine like a kettle left too long on the fire, rising and falling, rising and falling. People stood in their doorways looking up at where the stars used to be. An old woman was burning something in a copper dish, some herb that smelled like regret and onions. A child asked his mother when the lights would come back on, and the mother said *soon* in the voice people use when they mean *I don't know and I'm frightened*, and Lena ducked her head and kept walking, because there was nothing she could do about the sky, but there was something she could do about her stomach, and it had been three days since either of those things had been properly attended to.

The Guild of Impossible Things sat on Wrenhill like a fat black cat that had decided the hill belonged to it. Everyone knew what the guild traded in — memories, mostly, and names, and the occasional small miracle if you had the coin or the collateral. Everyone also knew you didn't go near it after dark, and *especially* not tonight, when the wards around its grounds were supposed to be the strongest in the city, humming like a hive of very serious bees.

Lena put her hand against the guild's outer ward and felt nothing.

Not resistance. Not the numb tingle other people complained of. Nothing, the way you feel nothing pressing your hand into empty air. The ward flowed around her palm like water around a stone, uninterested, incurious. Somewhere in its ancient architecture there was presumably a clause about who might pass, and somewhere in the making of Lena Thornbury — no, that wasn't right, that was a name she'd borrowed off a gravestone when she was eleven and needed one — somewhere in the making of *her*, there was a gap. A blank line. A place where a name should have been written and wasn't.

She slipped through like smoke through a keyhole.

The guild kitchens occupied the ground floor of the east wing, and they were magnificent, because impossible things apparently required excellent bread. Lena had scouted them twice before, from the outside, through windows fogged with steam. Tonight the kitchen was empty — the cooks all summoned upstairs, she guessed, to stand in halls and look worried on command — but the ovens still held their warmth, and the racks still held their wealth: loaves with crusts like lacquered wood, dark and shining; a plaited thing studded with currants that Lena would have committed several minor crimes for under normal circumstances; and, on a marble slab by the window, an entire round cheese wearing a cloth cover like a hat, as though it were modest about being adored.

"Oh," Lena said softly, to the bread, "you beautiful idiots."

She worked fast. She'd been hungry enough, often enough, to be good at this — quick hands, quiet feet, the particular patience of someone who has learned that desperation is loud and survival is silent. She took two dark loaves and the plaited currant thing, hesitated over the cheese, and took the cheese too, because the sky had gone out and she felt the world owed her. She wrapped everything in the spare apron hanging by the flour bin, which was theft upon theft but she'd decided tonight was a night for compounding interest.

She was halfway to the window, one leg already over the sill, when the door opened behind her.

*

Edmund Yarrow had come down to the kitchens for the least romantic reason imaginable: the tremor in her hand made inkwork difficult after midnight, and she'd thought a walk might settle it, and the walk had turned, as walks do, into a circuit of the lower halls, checking bolts and latches out of thirty years of habit while the wards screamed overhead like gulls in a storm. She had not expected the kitchen to be occupied. She had especially not expected it to be occupied by a girl of perhaps nineteen, perched on the windowsill with one leg inside and one leg out, clutching an apron bundle that was very obviously full of bread, wearing the expression of a cat caught mid-burglary who has decided to commit to the burglary.

They looked at each other.

Up close, Edmund saw things the dark had hidden: a girl thin in the way that comes from long habit rather than short misfortune, hair cut unevenly as if by her own knife, eyes the grey-green of the sea before weather. And no ward-mark. That was the thing that stopped Edmund's breath for half a second. Every citizen carried the ward-mark somewhere — wrist, throat, collarbone — the small sigil the border wards wrote into you at birth, the signature that said *this one is accounted for*. This girl's skin, wherever Edmund could see it, was smooth and unmarked as fresh paper.

"Good evening," Edmund said, because thirty years of guild manners will out even at midnight during an apocalypse.

"Evening," said the girl. "Lovely kitchen. Terrible security."

"You're stealing our bread."

"I'm *liberating* your bread. There's a difference. Liberation implies moral justification, and I'd argue a starless sky qualifies." She shifted her weight, ready to drop out the window, and Edmund noticed her hand had drifted, casual as anything, to the knife at her belt. Not threatening. Just honest. "You can shout for the guards if you want. But they'll take ten minutes to get here and I'll take four seconds to leave, and then we'll both have wasted a perfectly good evening."

"I'm not going to shout."

The girl blinked. It seemed genuinely to surprise her, which told Edmund something sad and familiar about how the world usually treated her.

"No?"

"No. It's been a strange night. Bread seems beside the point." Edmund crossed to the marble slab, lifted the cloth from the cheese, considered it, and cut a wedge with the knife kept there for exactly that purpose. She held it out. "But if you're going to steal from the Guild of Impossible Things, you should at least do it properly. That's a hard aged goat's cheese and it deserves better than to be eaten running."

The girl stared at the cheese. Then at Edmund. Then at the cheese again, with the wariness of someone who had learned that gifts are usually hooks with the barb filed down.

"What's wrong with you?" she asked.

"An enormous number of things, most of them documented. Why?"

"Nobody offers thieves cheese. There's a rule. It's practically a law of nature." But she came off the windowsill — dropping lightly back inside, which Edmund noted with some distant professional admiration was the opposite direction a sensible thief would have gone — and took the wedge, and ate a piece standing there, watching Edmund the whole time like a bird deciding whether the hand holding the seed was safe.

"It's good," she admitted, betrayed.

"It's very good. We pay our cooks scandalously well. It's one of the few things the guild gets right."

*

Later — much later, though neither of them would ever quite agree on how later — Lena would try to explain to herself why she stayed. Hunger explained the first five minutes. Curiosity explained the next ten. What explained the rest was harder to name, and had something to do with the fact that the woman across the kitchen island moved like a person built entirely out of straight lines and rules, and yet had looked at a starving thief and reached for the cheese knife instead of the alarm bell.

"So," the girl said, around a mouthful of stolen loaf, perched now on a stool like she owned it. "You're the swordspeaker."

Edmund's pen-hand — the marked one — twitched against her thigh. "I'm the guild's record-keeper."

"Everyone says *swordspeaker*. Old Ferro down at the docks, he used to talk about you. Said you could wake steel with a word. Said there used to be hundreds of you and now there's just the one, and the one works in an office." She said it without mockery, which somehow made it worse. "Is it true? That you're the last?"

"The last registered," Edmund said carefully. "Which is a different thing."

"Everything's a different thing with you, isn't it? I bet you alphabetize your regrets."

"I file them chronologically, actually."

And the girl laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, bright and rough at the edges, the laugh of someone who hadn't had much practice lately — and something in Edmund's chest shifted half an inch, like a book sliding slightly out of true on a shelf.

"Lena," the girl said, and stuck out her hand, and then paused, and looked at it, and something flickered across her face — a hesitation Edmund couldn't read yet, wouldn't understand for chapters and years. "Just Lena. No family name. Don't ask, it's boring."

"Edmund Yarrow."

"I know. You signed the notice on the guild gate. Your handwriting has *opinions*." Lena shook her hand, and Edmund braced out of habit for the ward-mark's usual greeting-pulse, the little acknowledgment the wards sent between marked citizens —

— and felt nothing.

No pulse. No acknowledgment. As if the mark itself didn't know what to make of the hand it touched. Edmund kept her face still, but inside, the record-keeper woke up and started taking notes. *Item: one (1) handshake, anomalous. The wards decline to comment.*

"And you came in through the outer ward," Edmund said. "Tonight. While it's screaming."

"Was it screaming? I couldn't say. It never makes noise at me." Lena shrugged, tearing another piece of bread. "It doesn't really do anything at me. Never has. I'm not complaining — it's the single most useful thing about me, and the list isn't long."

"That's not possible. The wards register everyone. It's their entire function. They taste your name and your blood and your—" Edmund stopped.

Because Lena had gone very still, the way animals go still when they've stepped somewhere they shouldn't. Not afraid, exactly. Braced. The look of someone waiting to find out whether the truth is going to cost them.

"I don't have a name," Lena said. "Not a real one. I had one once, maybe, before — before whatever happened happened. I've been borrowing names since I was eleven. The current one's off a gravestone in Marsh End, if you must know, and I apologize to the deceased, she seemed like she'd have approved." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "So when your fancy wards reach out to taste me, there's nothing there to taste. I'm a blank page, guildswoman. They skip me like a word they don't know."

Edmund Yarrow had spent thirty years among impossible things, cataloguing them, pricing them, filing them. Bottled thunderstorms. Caged echoes. Love potions that worked only on people already in love. None of it had ever made the hair rise on her arms the way a hungry girl saying *I'm a blank page* in a warm kitchen did.

Because Edmund knew — in the way you know a thing at three in the morning, before you can stop yourself knowing it — that ledgers had blank pages too. That somewhere in the guild's oldest books there were entries missing, lines struck through, names sold and struck and sold again until nothing remained but the transaction. That the wards tasted names, and this girl had none, and the coincidence of that on the night the stars went out was not a coincidence at all.

"Stay here tonight," Edmund heard herself say.

Lena raised an eyebrow. "Bold. We met four minutes ago and I robbed you."

"You robbed the guild. I'm merely employed by it. There's a distinction, and yes, I'm aware I alphabetize distinctions." Edmund pulled the cloth back over the cheese, absurdly domestic. "There's a store room off the pantry with a cot and a lock on the inside. Tomorrow the guildmaster will order silence and discretion and business as usual, because that's what he always orders, and everyone will obey him, and the sky will stay dark, and we will all pretend that's survivable." She paused. "You strike me as someone who doesn't pretend well."

"It's my worst subject," Lena agreed. "Right after 'trusting people.'"

"Then stay anyway. One night. The bread's already paid for — well. It's already stolen. Same accounting category, roughly."

Lena looked at her for a long moment — the sea-colored eyes narrowing, weighing, the survivor's arithmetic running visibly behind them. Edmund recognized the calculation because she performed a version of it herself every day: *what will this cost, what will it buy, is the trade clean.* And then, to Edmund's lasting surprise, the girl nodded once, sharp, decision made, done.

"One night," Lena said. "And tomorrow you tell me why a record-keeper looks at a blank page like she's seen a ghost."

"Tomorrow," Edmund said, "I may tell you worse than that."

She walked the girl to the store room herself, past the pointing miracles sleeping in their cases, past the shelf where a bottled thunderstorm muttered in its glass like a grumpy god. At the door, Lena paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, not quite looking at Edmund, "the cheese was a decent trade. Most people offer me nothing, or a cell. You offered dairy and a question. Best offer I've had in years."

"Low bar."

"Astonishingly low. Goodnight, Edmund Yarrow."

"Goodnight, Lena-of-the-gravestone."

The door clicked shut. Edmund stood a moment in the dark corridor, her marked hand tingling faintly where the handshake should have pulsed and hadn't, thinking about blank pages and missing entries and the way the pull toward the northern breach had felt, last night, like a hook set gently in her flesh.

Then she went up to her desk, opened the ledger to a fresh page, and wrote, in her precise, opinionated hand:

*Item 4473. One (1) girl, wardless, unnamed, bread-thieving. Provenance: unknown; possibly a gravestone in Marsh End. Value:* 

Her pen hovered. Below the window, the wards went on screaming at everyone except the one person who might matter most.

*Value: pending. Hazard rating: she looked at me like I was a person, and I found I wanted to keep being one.*

Outside, far to the north, something in the dark between the dead stars turned over — slowly, patiently, the way a reader turns toward a page that has finally, after a very long time, begun to fill with words.
