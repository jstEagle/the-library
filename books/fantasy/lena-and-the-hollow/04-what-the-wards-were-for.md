## 4. What the Wards Were For

The Archive of the Guild of Impossible Things occupied the three floors beneath the counting house, and it was organized, Edmund had long maintained, the way grief organizes a house: nothing thrown away, everything misplaced.

She descended the spiral stair at dawn with a lamp in her good hand and a ledger under her arm, and she did not tell herself she was being foolish. She was an oath-bound swordspeaker and a guildswoman of nineteen years' standing. She did not do foolish. She did *thorough*. And thoroughness, after the night the stars went out, looked exactly like this: descending into the oldest strata of the guild's records to find out what, precisely, the border wards had been *for*.

Corvane had ordered silence. Of course he had. Silence was Corvane's first instrument, the way other men reached for a hammer. *No speculation. No panic. The wards are being assessed.* He had said it to the assembled factors with that smile of his, the one that made you feel included in a joke whose punchline you'd be charged for later.

Edmund had nodded along like everyone else. Then she had gone home, slept four hours in her clothes, woken with her oath-marked hand aching like a tooth, and come here.

Because here was the thing about silence: Edmund Yarrow kept ledgers for a living. She knew what silence in a book meant. It meant a page torn out. It meant ink scraped away. It meant a gap in the columns where a number should be, and in nineteen years of trade — memories, names, small miracles, one bottled thunderstorm currently sulking on a shelf upstairs — she had learned that gaps were never empty. Gaps were where people hid things.

The lower archive smelled of dust and tallow and, faintly, of old magic gone stale, like a hearth cold for a century. The oldest ledgers lived behind a gate of black iron that required two keys: hers, and the archivist's. The archivist, Master Fenwick, was a small round man who had been old when Edmund was apprenticed and gave every sign of intending to be old when the sun itself was apprenticed.

"You want the Founding Ledgers," he said, not looking up from his cataloging. "At this hour."

"I want the Founding Ledgers."

"No one wants the Founding Ledgers. People *are assigned* the Founding Ledgers, as a punishment." He peered at her over his spectacles. "You look like a woman punishing herself."

"Fenwick."

"Third shelf, alcove nine. Mind the damp on the left side; the binding on the second volume weeps if you tilt it." He returned to his catalog, then added, without any change of tone whatsoever: "Your hand's worse, then."

Edmund stopped. "What?"

"The tremor. You've been holding your right hand against your ribs when you think no one's watching. Three days now." He turned a page. "I keep a ledger too, girl. Mine is just smaller."

She didn't answer that. She took the keys, went through the gate, and let the dark of the founding shelves close around her like water.

* * *

Alcove nine held eleven volumes, each the size of a paving stone, bound in hide that no tanner would admit to having supplied. The first volume opened with the guild's charter, written in a hand so beautiful it was almost aggressive:

*Let there be a house between the realms, where what cannot be bought elsewhere may be fairly traded, and let the price always be known to both parties.*

Fairly traded. Price known to both parties. Edmund read those words twice and felt something in her chest go tight and strange, because those were the words she had built her whole life inside. She had recited them at her swearing-in. She had enforced them across a hundred counters. They were the walls of her house, and she had never once thought to ask who built them, or what they stood upon.

She read forward through decades of founding entries. The first trades: a winter sold by a village that couldn't afford another one. A name purchased back from a debt-collector of the Unseelie court. A lullaby, bottled, that could put a siege to sleep. Small miracles, large ones, all of it flowing through the guild's hands with the tidy gravity of a river that has been given excellent banks.

And threading through all of it, from the very first pages: the wards.

*The border wards are raised,* wrote the first guildmaster, a woman named Ashgrave whose handwriting slanted like someone walking into wind. *They are not a wall. Let no successor mistake them for a wall. A wall keeps things out. The wards are a seam — they hold two worlds together where the making tore them apart, and they must be paid for, forever, as all seams are paid for.*

Paid for. With what?

Edmund turned pages until her lamp guttered, refilled it, turned more. The entries grew sparser as the founding generation died off, the way entries do, each guildmaster writing less than the last, the great questions shrinking into routine. By the fourth volume the wards appeared only in annual maintenance notes. By the seventh, only in the prices column — a standing cost, deducted quarterly, itemized simply as *the Keeping.*

But in the third volume, in Ashgrave's own slanting hand, there was a passage that made Edmund set down her tea and forget about it entirely until it went cold:

*The Hollow presses at the seam again. We have fed it silver, memory, years, and it eats all and remains hungry, for it is not a creature but an absence, and absences are not fed — they are filled. The covenant requires a keystone. Not gold. Not blood. A name, freely given, of one born under both skies, to stand in the seam as a promise stands in a marriage: not a chain, but a choosing, renewed in the heart even when the mouth is silent.*

*A name freely given.* Edmund read it five times. Something cold was beginning at the base of her skull and working its way down her spine with the patience of frost climbing a window.

Born under both skies. That was a legal term; she knew it from the trade codes. It meant a child of a mixed crossing — one parent of the near realm, one of the far. Rare. Precious to the covenant. And there had been exactly one such child enrolled in the guild's apprentice rolls in the year the Keeping's price changed from quarterly to —

She checked. Her hands were steady. She made them be steady, the way she made a scale balance, by force and habit.

— from quarterly to *paid in full, in perpetuity, one transaction, sealed.*

The date was the ninth of Thawmonth. Forty-one years ago.

Edmund Yarrow had been six years old.

* * *

She did not remember much of being six. This had never struck her as remarkable. Children remembered little; that was the common wisdom, and Edmund had built her life on the common wisdom of ledgers, which said that anything not recorded might as well not have happened.

But now she sat in the cold of alcove nine with the third Founding Ledger open before her and went looking, carefully, methodically, the way she would audit a factor suspected of skimming — and found the shape of the absence immediately, because absences have a shape once you know to feel for them.

She remembered her mother's hands. Flour, mostly; her mother had baked. She remembered a door, blue paint, worn to grey at the height where a child's shoulder would brush it. She remembered a voice singing something in a language she did not know and could not now pronounce, though her mouth tried, helplessly, around shapes it almost understood.

She did not remember her father's face.

There should have been a face. There was a fact — *she had had a father* — the way there is a fact of weather, but beneath the fact, nothing. No voice. No hands. No single image, not blurred, not half-gone, not faded: *nothing*, clean-edged nothing, the nothing of a page cut cleanly out of a bound book.

And she did not remember how she had come to the guild. Every apprentice remembered their arrival; it was guild tradition, told and retold at suppers, the story of the day your old life ended. Edmund had always said, when asked, that she'd been young, that it was foggy, that she'd been brought by a relative who died soon after. People had accepted it because Edmund Yarrow was precise, and precise people were believed.

She sat very still in the cold and listened to forty-one years of assumption crack, hairline by hairline, like ice on a pond in spring.

"Freely given," she said aloud, to the dark shelves. Her voice came out flat. "A choosing."

Had it been? Had anyone asked a six-year-old? Or had the word *freely* been doing the work that the word *fairly* did in the charter — standing in the ledger like a coin of good weight while underneath it, in the vaults, something else entirely changed hands?

She found the entry itself an hour later, or perhaps it found her; she had stopped steering by then. Volume three, final quarter, in Ashgrave's hand but shakier, older, pressed hard enough to tear:

*The Keeping is paid. The keystone-name is entered and sealed. I record here, against the day someone reads this who should not, that the price was agreed by the guardian of the child, per the forms, and that the child was told — *

The sentence stopped. There was no period. There was a scrape, as if the pen had been dragged sideways, and then nothing for two pages, and then a different entry about grain futures in a clerk's neat hand, as if the greatest secret of the age were a spilled pot one simply mopped up.

*The child was told —*

Told what? Told yes? Told nothing? Told a lie shaped like a bedtime story?

Edmund closed the ledger. She sat with her palms flat on its cover, feeling the hide's cold animal patience, and she thought about her oath-marked hand, which had trembled for three days — since the night the stars went out, since the wards fell, since whatever had been sealed with her name had come loose and started calling her home through the ache in her bones.

Her name. Sold. Before she was old enough to know it was hers to sell.

And the worst of it — the part that made her press her lips together in the dark like a woman holding a door shut against wind — was not the selling. Guilds sold things; that was the trade; she had sold bottled thunderstorms and bottled griefs and once, terribly, a man's courage, and she had recorded every sale in a fair hand because the alternative was chaos. Selling was not betrayal. Selling was weather.

The betrayal was the *silence after*. Forty-one years of it. Every master who had trained her, every factor who had praised her precision, every supper-table story she had never been able to join because hers began in fog — they had all known. They had watched her grow up inside the thing that had been bought with her, and they had handed her the keys to the archive and taught her to read and never once, not in nineteen years, let slip a word.

Or — and this thought arrived uninvited, cold and reasonable, wearing Corvane's smile — they hadn't known either. Only he had. Only the guildmaster kept the Keeping's true account, and everyone below him kept the tidy fiction, and wasn't that the most efficient fraud of all: a lie maintained not by conspirators but by structure?

She wanted it to be that. She noticed how badly she wanted it to be that, and distrusted the wanting, and sat with both.

* * *

It was nearly noon when she climbed back up into the world, blinking, dusty, with the third volume's relevant pages copied out in her own fair hand — because whatever happened next, Edmund Yarrow did not walk into a room without documentation.

Fenwick looked up as she passed his desk. His little eyes went from her face to the copies to her right hand, which was trembling openly now and which she had stopped bothering to hide.

"The Keeping," he said quietly. Not a question.

Edmund stopped. "You know the word."

"I've shelved these alcoves for fifty years, girl. I can't read the old script, but I can read a gap." He folded his spectacles. "Volume three has a page missing. Has had, all my tenure. Cut close to the spine, professional work. I always wondered what weighed enough to take out."

"A name," Edmund said. "Mine."

Fenwick was quiet a moment. Then he nodded, slowly, the way men nod at a tide coming in — not approval, just acknowledgment that the sea does what the sea does.

"There's a copy," he said. "If it matters. Ashgrave didn't trust seals. Kept a private folio, said a record that exists in one place doesn't exist. It'll be in her effects, wherever the guild stored those when she died." He looked at Edmund over the spectacles one last time. "Alcove nine is cold. Take the stairs slow. And Edmund —"

"Yes?"

"When you go to ask him about it — and you will, because you're that kind of fool — don't show him your hand first. Show him the paper. He can argue with paper. He can't argue with a shaking hand; he'll just be kind, and kindness from that man is a purchase order."

Edmund almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth the cellar had offered all day.

"Ashgrave's effects," she said. "Where?"

"Sealed storeroom, top floor, west wing. Under Corvane's key." Fenwick reopened his catalog, conversation over. "Which is why I mentioned the fool part."

Edmund climbed the stairs with the copies against her chest like a breastplate, and above her, through the counting house windows, the sky hung starless even at noon — a pale bruised blank where the sun struggled like a candle in fog — and her oath-marked hand pulled toward the north breach, gently, insistently, like a compass needle that had finally been told its true north.

She had spent her whole life believing she was the guild's sword.

Standing in the stairwell light, she understood, with the first full cold taste of it settling on her tongue, that she had also been its payment.
