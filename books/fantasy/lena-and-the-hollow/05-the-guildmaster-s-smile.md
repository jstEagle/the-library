## 5. The Guildmaster's Smile

Edmund did not sleep.

She tried. She lay in her narrow room above the counting-house with the shutters closed and the candle pinched out, and she listened to the guild settling around her like an old dog turning circles before it lies down — the creak of beams, the tick of the great clock in the atrium, the distant mutter of apprentices who should have been abed an hour since. None of it helped. Every time she closed her eyes she saw the ledger line again, written in her own careful hand forty years ago by someone else's instruction:

*One (1) name, whole and unencumbered. Received: continuation of covenant. Seller's mark affixed.*

Her name. Her small, ordinary, hard-won name, sold before she was old enough to know what a name was for.

At some point past the third bell she gave up on sleep entirely, dressed, and went down into the dark of the guildhall with a lamp held steady in her oath-marked hand. The tremor was worse tonight. She pretended it wasn't.

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its oldest ledgers in the Undercroft, behind a door that only opened for someone carrying a genuine grievance. Edmund had always assumed this was a formality. Tonight she pressed her marked palm against the iron and felt the lock consider her — really *consider* her, the way a moneylender considers a debtor — and then swing inward with something that was almost sympathy.

"Well," she said to the darkness beyond. "That's ominous."

* * *

She read until her lamp burned low and then until dawn came gray through the ventilation grates, and what she found was this:

The covenant between realms was not a treaty. Treaties were made of ink and mutual distrust. The covenant was made of *weight* — a promise so heavy that both worlds could hang their borders from it, the way you hang a bridge from a chain. And a promise needed a promiser. Someone had to stand at the center of it and mean it, forever, or the whole structure came down.

Someone had. A child, apprenticed young to the guild, sworn before she understood what swearing was. Her name had been entered into the covenant as surety — the collateral against which two realms had borrowed their continued existence.

And when the child grew up and became a swordspeaker and a ledger-keeper and a woman who catalogued bottled thunderstorms with a steady hand, nobody told her. Because telling her would have been, according to a marginal note in Corvane's own slanted script, *"an unnecessary risk to operational stability."*

Operational stability. Edmund read the phrase four times. On the fourth reading she laughed — one short, ugly sound that echoed off the Undercroft shelves — and put the ledger down before she did something to it that the guild would have to enter as a loss.

There was more. Of course there was more. The covenant had three pieces: the name, the fire, and the promise. The name was hers. The fire belonged to something old and sleeping that the ledgers referred to only as *the ember beneath the ash*. And the promise —

The promise was the part that made her hands go cold. Because the promise had been *kept*, once, by a warden whose name had been struck from every record with a thoroughness that looked less like history and more like guilt. And thirty years ago, on a night the ledgers described only as *the adjustment*, the promise had been broken, and the wards had begun to leak, and something on the other side of them had begun to drink.

The Hollow. The devouring absence between worlds. Feeding on a broken bargain.

Feeding on *her* bargain.

Edmund sat in the gray light with dust settling on her shoulders and thought about the stars going out, and the wards screaming down, and the hook she'd felt in her chest pulling toward the breach — and understood, finally, what the hook was. It was her own name, calling its owner home.

* * *

Corvane received her at the ninth bell, in his office at the top of the guildhall, because of course he did. He always knew. That was the thing about Corvane that had unsettled her even as a young apprentice: he never seemed surprised by anything, which meant either that he knew everything or that he had simply stopped bothering to be surprised, and after forty years Edmund still hadn't decided which was worse.

"Edmund." He didn't look up from his desk. He was writing, in that same slanted hand, with a pen that left faint silver traces on the page — guildmaster's ink, made from things Edmund had never been cleared to know about. "You were in the Undercroft all night. You've tracked ash from the old shelves onto my stairs."

"You can see my stairs from here?"

"I can see everything from here. It's the entire job." He finished his line, blotted it, and looked up. And smiled.

That was the other thing about Corvane. He was not a handsome man — he was all elbows and ledger-lines himself, a man built out of arithmetic — but when he smiled, he smiled with his whole face, warmly, generously, the smile of a favorite uncle at midwinter dinner. Edmund had watched him use it to close trades that ruined people. She had watched him use it to comfort widows whose husbands' names he had bought. She had felt it herself, over the years, a hundred times, and had never once managed to hate it while it was happening. That, she suspected, was precisely what it was for.

"You've been reading," he said.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped you would, actually. I'd have been disappointed if you'd spent another decade not noticing." He gestured to the chair across from him. "Sit down, Edmund. Have some tea. You look terrible."

"I don't want tea."

"It's good tea."

"I don't *want* tea, Corvane." She stayed standing. Her marked hand had curled into a fist without her permission; she made herself open it, finger by finger, the way she'd make herself do anything difficult. "My name is in the covenant."

"Yes."

"*My name.* Sold. When I was six years old."

"Seven," said Corvane mildly. "You'd completed your first year of letters. The covenant required a name that could be *written*. Yours was the cleanest hand we'd ever seen in a child that age. There was rather a lot of excitement about it, if I'm honest. Old Master Pell swears he wept."

"Wept," Edmund repeated.

"With pride. It was considered a great honor, being chosen."

"By whom? Did anyone ask *me*?"

"You were seven."

"That's rather my point!"

Corvane set down his pen. The smile faded — not into anger, which she could have fought, but into something gentler and far more dangerous: patience. The look of a man preparing to explain arithmetic to someone he genuinely believed was capable of understanding it.

"Do you know what the border wards cost, Edmund?" he asked. "Not in coin. In *weight*. Two realms, hanging from one promise. Everything either world has ever built — every harvest, every hearth, every child asleep under a roof instead of under an open sky full of things that eat children — all of it suspended from a single act of meaning. And acts of meaning require a meaner. A person. Flesh and blood and a name that can be written down and held."

"And you chose me."

"The covenant chose you. I was twenty-six years old and I carried the paperwork." He spread his hands. "I have regretted it every day since. I'll say that plainly, because you deserve plain speech: I regretted it within the hour, and I have regretted it every day for forty years. But I would do it again. This morning, knowing everything I know, having buried everyone I've buried — I would sign the same line. Because the alternative was the Hollow, Edmund. Do you understand what the alternative *was*?"

"No," said Edmund. "Tell me. Since apparently I'm the last person in this building to be told anything."

For a moment — just a moment — the warmth went out of Corvane's face entirely, and what was underneath was very old and very tired.

"Before the covenant, the space between the realms wasn't empty," he said. "It was *hungry*. Not evil — evil has intentions, evil can be negotiated with. Hunger doesn't negotiate. It ate the borders. It ate the years. Whole villages went to bed in one world and woke up in neither. We sealed it with a promise, and the promise held for thirty years, and then—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Then the promise broke. And the Hollow has been feeding through the crack ever since. Slowly. Carefully. The way water finds its way through a dam. And now the stars are going out, which means the crack is widening, which means the dam is failing, which means—"

"Which means you need me," Edmund said quietly. "Again. Without asking. Again."

"I need the covenant renewed. Yes."

"And you weren't going to tell me."

"I was going to tell you when I had a solution that didn't require telling you."

The honesty of it was somehow worse than a lie would have been. Edmund stood there in the silver morning light and looked at the man who had run the guild for as long as she could remember — the man who had taught her double-entry bookkeeping, who had given her her first pair of gloves to cover the oath-mark, who had remembered her name-day every single year with a small cake and no explanation of why he bothered — and felt the strange, sick vertigo of discovering that a person you have known your whole life has been standing in front of a wall the entire time.

"What happened to the warden?" she asked. "The one who kept the promise. Their name is struck out of everything. Even the Undercroft. Even *your* private ledgers, Corvane, and I know how much you love your private ledgers."

Something flickered across his face. Fast. Gone before she could name it.

"The warden is not relevant."

"Everything is relevant. That's the first thing you ever taught me."

"The warden," Corvane said, each word placed like a coin on a scale, "is not relevant to your duties. Which brings us to those." He rose, walked to the window, and stood with his back to her, looking out over the city where the sun was rising on a sky with no stars in it anywhere, day or night, and everyone pretending not to notice. "Here are your instructions, Edmund, and I want them heard clearly. You will not investigate the Hollow. You will not pursue the covenant. You will not leave the guildhall without escort. You will resume your ledger work — the thunderstorm trade needs closing out, by the way, the buyer has been patient and patience costs us interest — and you will let the guild handle the crisis, as the guild has handled every crisis for two hundred years."

"And if I refuse?"

He turned. And smiled.

It was the worst one yet — the full, warm, uncle-at-midwinter smile, deployed now with nothing behind it but necessity, and Edmund realized with a lurch that she was watching him spend it the way he spent everything: deliberately, at cost, because the books required it.

"If you refuse," he said gently, "then I will invoke the clause. The one in your apprenticeship contract. The one you signed at seven years old, in the cleanest hand old Master Pell had ever seen." He let that sit for a moment. "You are oath-bound, Edmund. To the guild, to the ledgers, to me. It's the shape of your whole life. You've never once broken an oath. I'm asking you not to start with this one."

* * *

She walked back down through the guildhall in a daze, past the apprentices sweeping the atrium, past the great clock ticking its indifferent tick, past the notice board where someone had pinned a new placard in the guildmaster's neat hand:

*RE: THE NIGHT SKY. Members are reminded that celestial irregularities do not constitute guild business and should not be discussed with clients.*

Celestial irregularities. Edmund almost laughed again. Somewhere in this building there was a ledger entry for a bottled thunderstorm, and somewhere else there was an entry for the end of the world, and both had been filed under *operational matters* by the same man with the same warm smile.

Duty and suspicion, walking down the stairs together, holding hands like the worst sort of married couple.

Because that was the trap of it, and Corvane knew it was the trap, and he had built his whole argument inside it: she *was* oath-bound. Obedience ran in her marks the way blood ran in her veins. Forty years of keeping the ledgers, of honoring trades, of being the woman whose word was worth more than most people's houses — and now the same fidelity that made her who she was demanded she sit down, shut up, and file the apocalypse under pending.

And yet.

Yet the Undercroft door had opened for her *grievance*, not her obedience. Yet the warden's struck-out name itched at her like a splinter under the skin. Yet Corvane had flinched — she'd seen it, small as a miscounted penny — when she asked what happened to the keeper of the promise. Guilty men did not flinch at questions unless the answers were load-bearing.

And yet, most of all: a girl with no ward had stolen bread from the guild kitchens two nights ago, slipping through collapsed defenses as though the magic itself couldn't see her. Edmund had reported the incident, because reporting incidents was what she did. Corvane had thanked her, smiled his smile, and done absolutely nothing about it. No search. No inquiry. No entry in any ledger she could find.

As if a wardless girl walking untouched through a dying border was not an impossibility worth recording.

As if he already knew.

Edmund reached her office, closed the door, and stood in the quiet with her marked hand trembling against her heart. On her desk sat the day's work: the thunderstorm ledger, the interest schedules, the small orderly machinery of a life she had believed in until last night.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she took out a fresh sheet of paper, wrote at the top of it — in the cleanest hand in the guild — the words *Private Ledger, Unregistered*, and beneath that, the first entry of her new account:

*Item the first: one wardless girl, whereabouts unknown. Value: possibly everything.*

Outside the window, the starless morning went on pretending to be ordinary. And somewhere in the city below, Edmund was certain — with the certainty of a woman who had spent her life learning to read what people didn't write down — a girl who owed the guild a loaf of bread was about to learn what it cost.
