## 6. Running the Hollow Road

### Edmund

There were three ways out of the Guild of Impossible Things, and Edmund Yarrow had spent thirty years pretending she didn't know any of them.

The front door was for members in good standing, of which she was no longer one — Corvane had made that clear with a smile that could have been filed under *cursed objects, minor*. The cellar door led to the wine stores, which were guarded by a man named Brackle who had once traded away his ability to be surprised and now found even armed intruders tiresome. And then there was the third way: the old coal chute behind the ledger hall, which every guildmaster since the founding had solemnly declared sealed, and which every apprentice since the founding had used within a week.

Edmund went down the coal chute with her satchel of stolen ledgers pressed to her chest and soot rising around her like a black bloom.

She landed badly. Her oath-marked hand took her weight, and the mark flared white-hot up her wrist, and she bit down on the sound she wanted to make because sound carried, and tonight everything hunted by sound.

*Get out,* she told herself. *Grieve later. Panic later. Later is a country you can visit when the borders are open.*

The ledgers against her ribs were the oldest ones, pulled from the vault beneath the guildmaster's study while he slept — or pretended to sleep; she suspected Corvane pretended at a great many things, including being merely human. Three books bound in grey leather, their spines stamped with a sigil she'd never been permitted to read. The covenant. Her name, sold like a bolt of cloth. She hadn't finished reading the entry. She wasn't sure she wanted to know what else had been thrown into the bargain.

Behind her, above her, through the floorboards, the guild began to wake.

Not with bells. Corvane didn't believe in bells; bells announced things, and announcement was a form of honesty he found distasteful. Instead there was a low, rising hum, like a struck tuning fork the size of a cathedral, and Edmund's blood went cold because she knew that sound.

Oath-hounds.

They weren't dogs, whatever the name suggested. They were what happened when a broken promise was given legs — constructs of debt and hunger, all nose and no heart, stitched together from the residue of vows people had failed to keep. They couldn't be bribed. They couldn't be outrun for long. And they tracked one thing only: the scent of an oath in motion.

Which meant they would come for her first. She was, after all, the most broken promise in the building.

"Charming," Edmund whispered to the dark, and ran.

* * *

### Lena

Lena had been in the coal chute's shadow when Edmund came down it, mostly because the coal chute's shadow was the last place in the guild nobody ever looked, and Lena had a talent for those places. Not magic. Wards slid off her like rain off oiled canvas. Doors forgot to lock themselves behind her. Guards' eyes found her and then, politely, found something else. It wasn't a gift anyone had ever paid her for, but it kept her fed, and lately it had kept her alive, which was better.

So when the woman crashed out of the chute in a cloud of soot, swearing in three languages and clutching books like a drowning woman clutches driftwood, Lena did the sensible thing.

She grabbed her arm.

Edmund nearly took her head off. There was a heartbeat where a blade was somewhere near Lena's throat — she never saw it drawn, which was its own kind of frightening — and then the woman saw her face and stopped.

"You," Edmund said. "The bread thief."

"The ledger keeper." Lena dusted soot from the woman's shoulder, mostly to prove she wasn't afraid. "We really must stop meeting like this. People will talk."

"People are talking." Edmund's eyes flicked upward, toward the hum. "Hounds."

"I know. I heard them wake up." Lena tilted her head. "You're planning to go out the postern gate, aren't you."

Something crossed Edmund's face — surprise, quickly filed away under *later*. "How do you—"

"Because everyone plans to go out the postern gate, and there are already two hounds sitting outside it like cats at a mousehole. I checked." She jerked her chin toward the far wall, where a drainage grate sat behind a stack of retired filing cabinets. "Storm culvert. It comes up on the Hollow Road past the tanner's. Nobody guards it because nobody remembers it exists."

"And you remember it because—"

"Because nothing remembers me," Lena said, before she could make it sound light. "I'm very easy to forget. It's my best quality."

Edmund looked at her for a long moment. In the dark, with soot on her face and terror underneath her composure like a stone under moss, she looked less like the terrifying ledger keeper of guild rumor and more like someone very tired holding herself together with procedure.

"They'll track me," Edmund said finally. "Not you. You're not—" She stopped, visibly recalibrating. "What *are* you?"

"Lena," said Lena. "Come on. We can have this conversation while running."

* * *

### Edmund

The storm culvert smelled the way Edmund imagined the inside of a regret might smell, if regrets were physical and had been left out in the rain. She followed the girl's narrow silhouette through water ankle-deep and tried not to think about how completely she had just placed her life in the hands of someone whose primary qualifications were stealing bread and being forgettable.

Then again. Look at her own credentials: keeper of accounts, speaker of swords, sold as a child by the man she'd served for thirty years. Perhaps forgettable was exactly the right companion for the road ahead.

The culvert bent twice, climbed once, and ended in a rusted grate above the tannery sluice. Lena shouldered it aside as though it weighed nothing, which it did not, and which Edmund noted with professional interest — the girl was stronger than she looked, or the world simply moved out of her way in small mechanical ways too.

Outside, the night was wrong.

Edmund had known it would be. She'd felt it from the moment the stars went out — a pressure behind the sky, like a held breath. But knowing and standing in it were different things. Above the rooftops of the lower city, where stars should have been scattered thick as spilled salt, there was nothing. Not clouds. Not haze. An absence, smooth and total, and her oath-mark throbbed in time with it, a second pulse beneath her skin.

"That's the Hollow," Lena said quietly. Beside her. Too close, and yet somehow the only comfortable distance left in the world. "Isn't it? Everyone keeps saying the wards broke, but nobody says what got in."

"Nothing got in," Edmund said. "That's rather the point of it. It's the space where things stop being." She flexed her marked hand. "It's been feeding on the covenant for decades. On the gap where the bargain broke. Tonight it simply... finished eating through."

"And your name is in that bargain."

"Yes."

"So it's been eating *you*. Slowly. For years." Lena said it flatly, the way she seemed to say everything, but her hand found Edmund's sleeve and stayed there. "That tremor in your hand. That's not age."

"It's depreciation," Edmund said, and heard how thin the joke came out. "Keep moving. The road bends north past the tannery, and if we're on the Hollow Road proper by moonrise—"

"There's no moonrise," Lena said. "No moon either. I checked."

"—by *then*, then."

The Hollow Road was called that long before the Hollow had a name, which Edmund found either prophetic or deeply embarrassing for whoever named it. It ran along the old border line between the realms, a rutted track lined with wardstones worn featureless by centuries of weather and neglect. Once, those stones had burned with covenant-light. Now they were just rocks, and the dark between them had texture.

They had gone perhaps half a mile when the humming started again.

Closer now. Behind them, back toward the city — no. Edmund turned. *Two directions.* The hum split, echoed, multiplied. They hadn't sent hounds after her.

They'd sent hounds ahead of her.

"Off the road," she breathed. "Now."

* * *

### Lena

Lena had run from a lot of things in her life: a miller with a cudgel, a tax collector with worse than a cudgel, a winter, a fire, a man in Vell's Crossing who'd smiled too much. She knew the geography of fear the way other people knew the streets of their hometown.

This was different. This was fear with a *sound* to it.

She heard the hounds before she saw them — that tuning-fork hum climbing into something like baying, except the bay had words buried in it, half-heard fragments of every promise anyone had ever broken on this road. *I'll come back. I'll pay you Tuesday. I'll love you always.* The hounds were made of the wreckage, and they sang it as they ran.

They burst over the ridge to the east — three of them, maybe four, shapes like dogs assembled from smoke and unpaid debts, their eyes the dull silver of coins left on a dead man's eyes. They came down the slope toward the road in long, loping strides, and Lena's whole body said *run*, and she ran, dragging Edmund by the wrist—

—and the lead hound reached the place where the road met the drainage ditch, and stopped.

Not slowed. *Stopped*, like a cart hitting a wall that wasn't there. It threw back its smoke-and-debt head and bayed in frustration, pacing along some invisible line, and its fellows piled up behind it the way water piles behind a dam.

Lena stood ten feet inside the line, chest heaving, and understood.

"They can't cross," she said. "Where I'm standing. They can't—"

"Wardless," Edmund said. She'd caught up, and she was staring at Lena with an expression Lena couldn't read — wonder, maybe, or the particular look of an accountant discovering a sum that shouldn't balance and does. "The hounds are built from oaths. Oaths are a kind of ward-magic. And you're..." She laughed, short and disbelieving. "You're a hole in the weave. They can't perceive you well enough to touch you."

"Story of my life," Lena said, "finally paying off."

But the hounds didn't leave. They paced the invisible boundary, humming, patient as interest accruing, and Edmund's face went grim.

"They'll hold the line and signal the rest of the pack. We need to move along it until it ends, and it ends nowhere good, because boundaries like this end at *her*." She nodded at Lena. "At wherever you stop being a hole and start being a person they can smell."

"Then let's find out where that is," Lena said. "Before they do."

* * *

### Edmund

They walked the edge of the girl's absence for two hours, and Edmund used the time to do what she always did with unbearable things: catalog them.

Item: the hounds numbered seven visible, likely twelve total. Item: they could not cross within roughly a spear-length of Lena, an effect that held even when Lena herself was unaware of it, sleeping-walking through the dark with her hand still hooked in Edmund's sleeve. Item: Edmund's own oath-mark flared whenever the hounds bayed, answering them, calling to them, and she had taken to wrapping the hand in a strip of linen torn from her shirt, which helped not at all but felt administrative, and administration was her native tongue.

Item: she trusted this girl. Completely. Unreasonably. After four days.

That last entry refused to file itself properly.

"You keep doing that," Lena said, without opening her eyes. They'd paused in the lee of a fallen wardstone, sharing the heel of bread Lena had produced from somewhere — Edmund had decided not to ask where. "The counting thing. Your lips move."

"Habit."

"You count when you're frightened."

"I inventory when I'm *concerned*," Edmund said with dignity. "Counting is for cowards."

"What are you concerned about, then? Besides the obvious smoke-dogs and the end of the world."

Edmund chewed. Considered lying. Found, to her mild alarm, that she didn't want to.

"That you'll realize what I am," she said. "A walking breach in the covenant. A debt with legs, same as the hounds, just better dressed. That the sensible thing — the *guild* thing — would be to put me down before the Hollow finishes collecting." She turned her wrapped hand over. "I sold myself a story, these past days. That we're running *toward* something. But everything that hunts us is hunting *me*, and every mile I walk, I make your road shorter."

Lena was quiet for a moment. Then she tore the bread and handed Edmund the larger piece, which Edmund noticed, because she noticed everything, and which undid her more than any speech could have.

"When I was nine," Lena said, "a ward-mage in Corve's Ford told me I was a flaw. A mistake in the great design. He said it kindly, which was worse." She brushed crumbs from her knees. "Took me twenty years to figure out that every wall built to exclude someone is also a door, if you're the one it excludes. The hounds can't touch me. The wards don't see me. The Hollow—" she glanced up at the starless dark, and didn't flinch, which Edmund could not have done, "—the Hollow hasn't found me yet either. Maybe I'm not a flaw. Maybe I'm the part of the pattern nobody planned for. The margin note."

"A margin note," Edmund repeated.

"Every important thing I've ever learned was written in a margin." Lena stood, and offered her hand — the unmarked one, the ordinary one, and Edmund took it and was surprised by how much it steadied her. "Come on, ledger keeper. The line ends ahead. I can feel it. Like stepping out of shade."

* * *

### Edmund

The line ended at the ruins of the old tollhouse, exactly as Lena had felt it, and the moment they stepped beyond the reach of her absence, the nearest hound lifted its head and *sang*.

Edmund ran.

They ran together, down the Hollow Road with the pack converging from both sides, seven shapes of smoke and broken word flowing over the rutted ground like ink poured uphill. Edmund's lungs burned. Her mark blazed through the linen wrapping. Ahead, the road forked at a leaning signpost whose letters had been eaten away — by weather, or by the thing in the sky — and both paths ran straight into dark.

"Left or right?" Lena shouted.

"Left goes to the ferry at Greywater. Right goes into the Ashlands." Edmund's mind raced through ledgers, distances, prices. "Ferry means people, people mean witnesses, witnesses mean Corvane finds us by dawn."

"And the Ashlands?"

"Mean dragons used to."

Lena grinned — actually grinned, teeth flashing in the starless dark, alive, absurd, magnificent. "I've never seen a dragon."

"That's because they're extinct."

"Then we'll be the first to see one anyway." And she pulled Edmund right, into the Ashlands, and Edmund — thirty years a keeper of careful accounts, a woman who had weighed every risk against every reward since childhood — went without balancing the column at all.

Behind them the hounds hit the fork and split, and their song rose into the ruined sky, full of every promise ever broken on this road.

Ahead, faint as a dying coal, something in the Ashlands answered.

* * *

### Lena

They lost the hounds at dawn — or what passed for dawn now, a grey bruise of light with no sun behind it — by wading a mile up a riverbed and letting the water carry their scent nowhere at all. Lena's absence confused them further; twice a hound passed within arm's reach of her crouched form and veered away, whining, as though the space she occupied offended it.

The second time, Edmund watched it happen with her mouth slightly open, and afterward said, "Twice. It's saved us twice. I'm going to have to start a ledger entry just for you."

"What would it say?"

"*One wardless girl, acquired in a kitchen. Value: incalculable. Provenance unknown.*" Edmund paused, and something gentler moved through her tired face. "Though I suppose I could amend that. Provenance: chosen."

Lena looked at her for a beat too long, then busied herself wringing out her hem. "Careful, ledger keeper. Talk like that and I'll think you're keeping me."

"I don't keep anything anymore," Edmund said quietly. "I'm done with keeping. But I might—" she flexed her wrapped, burning hand, and did not finish, and did not need to, because Lena heard it anyway:

*I might choose to stay.*

They walked on into the Ashlands, two small figures on a road that had forgotten its purpose, and far ahead, at the edge of sight, the ground glowed a deep and patient red — like an ember that had been waiting a very long time for someone to breathe on it.

And in the glow's reflection, Lena thought she saw wings.
