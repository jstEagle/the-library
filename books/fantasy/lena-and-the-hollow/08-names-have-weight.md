## 8. Names Have Weight

The dragon's words hung in the caldera air like smoke that refused to rise.

*Ask her what she traded away to survive.*

Lena felt the question land on her before Edmund even turned, the way you feel a change in weather before the wind arrives. She had spent her whole life being unseen — by wards, by guards, by the tidy machinery of a world that ran on names and magic and had no slot for a girl who was neither. She had made an art of it. She had made a *living* of it, mostly, in the way that stealing bread and sleeping in haylofts counted as living.

But Vessith saw her. That was the trouble with dragons. They were too old to be fooled by absence.

Edmund turned slowly. The firelight from the dragon's guttering breastbone caught the swordswoman's face and did something complicated to it — softened the ledger-keeper's precision, cracked open something younger and more frightened underneath. Her oath-marked hand, Lena noticed, was trembling again. It had been trembling since the stars went out, though Edmund hid it the way she hid everything: behind straight posture and precise speech and that voice like a well-kept account book.

"Lena," Edmund said carefully. "You don't have to—"

"She's right, though." Lena heard herself say it, and was surprised. "The dragon. I did trade something away."

The caldera was quiet except for the slow tick of cooling stone and the wet rasp of Vessith's breathing — a sound like a bellows with a hole in it, like a fire deciding.

"I don't remember what," Lena said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

She hadn't meant to tell it here. She hadn't meant to tell it anywhere, actually; it was the one possession she owned outright, this hole in herself, and she guarded it the way other people guarded gold. But there was something about a dying dragon's caldera that loosened the tongue. Perhaps it was the heat. Perhaps it was that everything else had already gone wrong — the wards fallen, the stars snuffed out, the Hollow chewing at the edges of the world — so one girl's small private ruin hardly mattered anymore.

They sat on a shelf of black stone near the caldera's rim, close enough to Vessith's warmth that the night's cold couldn't reach them. The dragon had curled herself into a smaller shape than seemed possible for something the size of a chapel, folding her wings like a tired innkeeper closing up for the season. Her ember-glow lit the underside of the starless sky, and against that darkness Lena could see Edmund clearly for the first time in days: the shadows under her eyes, the ink stains on her fingers even now, the way she held her marked hand curled against her ribs as if it were a wound she'd decided not to mention.

"Start wherever you like," Edmund said. "Or nowhere. I'm told silence is also an answer."

"You're told that because you're a guildswoman and guildspeople think everything is a transaction." Lena pulled her knees up. "Fine. Here's my transaction."

She looked at the empty sky where stars should have been. It was easier to talk to nothing.

"I don't know how old I was. Young. Young enough that the memory of forgetting is the oldest thing I have, which is its own kind of joke. There was a man — or something wearing a man — at a crossroads shrine. One of those little roadside altars, the kind travelers leave buttons at. He said he was a collector. He said times were hard, the roads were dangerous, and he could offer passage. Safe passage. Through anything." She shrugged. "I was alone. I'd been alone long enough to know that alone gets you killed eventually. So I asked him what it cost."

"And he said?"

"He said—" Lena laughed, and hated the sound of it. "He said, *only your heaviest thing.* And I thought about it, truly thought about it, the way children do, very seriously and entirely wrongly. And I understood that the heaviest thing I carried was home. Not a house. I don't think I had a house. But *home* — the whole weight of it. Whoever I'd been, whoever had loved me, whatever I'd lost or been taken from. All of it, bundled up in my chest like a stone I'd been carrying so long I'd forgotten it was a stone. So I gave him that. And he gave me passage. And the wards never stopped me again."

Edmund had gone very still. Stillness, Lena was learning, was what Edmund did instead of weeping.

"The wards don't see you," Edmund said quietly. "Because they're looking for names. For threads of belonging. And you sold yours."

"I sold the *memory* of mine. Apparently the thread came with it." Lena picked at a loose thread on her sleeve — actual thread, which was satisfyingly ironic. "Do you know what it's like? Everyone has a somewhere. Even people who hate their somewhere have it. You can ask anyone in any town, *where are you from,* and they'll say a name, and the name will have a taste to it, a weight. Ask me, and there's just—" she gestured at the starless dark above them— "*that*. A smooth place. A scar with no story. I used to make things up. Told people I was from Millbrook once, because it sounded soft. Another time I said the Salt Coast. Lies are lighter than holes. You can put them down whenever you want."

Vessith rumbled. It might have been sympathy. With dragons it was hard to tell sympathy from indigestion.

"*Names have weight,*" the dragon said, her voice like coals settling. "*That is not a saying among my kind, little thief. It is physics. A name is a hook set into the world. Pull on it, and the world pulls back. Your collector took your hook and left you free-floating — which is why the wards slide off you, why the Hollow cannot get its teeth into you, why a dying dragon can see you when the covenant itself cannot find you.*" The great eye blinked, molten and slow. "*And why, I suspect, you can walk where magic cannot. You are not unmagicked, child. You are unclaimed.*"

"Unclaimed," Lena repeated. It sounded less like freedom than she'd always pretended. "That's a polite word for it."

"It is an accurate one." Vessith's gaze shifted to Edmund. "*As accurate as 'oath-bound' is for her. Two women, one bought and one sold. My caldera fills with bargains tonight.*"

* * *

Later — much later, when the dragon's breathing had slowed into something like sleep and the cold had crept down to claim the rim of the world — Lena found Edmund awake, of course, sitting with her back against warm stone, turning her marked hand over and over in the firelight like a page she couldn't stop rereading.

"Can't sleep either?" Lena dropped down beside her. Close beside her. She told herself the stone was narrow. The stone was not narrow.

"I keep calculating," Edmund admitted. "It's a bad habit. Three pieces of the covenant. One dragon with perhaps a season left in her. One swordspeaker whose hand shakes every time she reaches for her blade. And one wardless girl whose only useful quality is that she's nobody." She exhaled. "I don't love the arithmetic."

"Your arithmetic is wrong, then."

"Oh?"

"You said my only useful quality is that I'm nobody." Lena bumped her shoulder against Edmund's, deliberately, testing. "It's not a quality. It's a quantity. Everything I'm not. Every name I don't have. Do you know how much nothing weighs? Neither do I. Nobody does. That's the whole problem."

Edmund was quiet for a moment. Then, in the tone she probably used for reading difficult clauses aloud, she said: "When we find the second piece — when this is over, if there is an over — I'll help you look for it. What you traded. Who you were."

Lena stared at her. "You don't even know if there's anything to find."

"A man at a crossroads shrine bought the heaviest thing a child carried. Collectors keep what they buy. Guild law, article one: *nothing sold is ever truly gone; it has merely changed hands.*" Something flickered across Edmund's face — the ghost of a smile, quickly banked. "If your home exists anywhere, it exists in someone's ledger. And no one alive reads ledgers better than I do."

"That's the saddest boast I've ever heard."

"It's also true, which makes it worse." Edmund looked down at her shaking hand, and Lena watched her decide to be honest — watched it happen, like watching someone step off a ledge on purpose. "I know what it is to have a piece of yourself missing. Mine isn't gone. Mine was *sold*, by someone I trusted, before I was old enough to understand what a name was worth. I've spent thirty years being precisely, perfectly Edmund Yarrow because it was the only self I had left, and I kept it in ledgers because paper doesn't betray you." Her jaw tightened. "So when I say I'll help you find yours, understand that it isn't charity. It's the only vow I'm currently capable of making. And unlike my oaths, this one is freely given."

Lena's throat did something inconvenient. She covered it with a grin. "Careful, swordspeaker. Keep making vows like that and I'll start thinking you mean them."

"I always mean them. It's my most exhausting quality."

Above them the sky hung blank and enormous, a page with nothing written on it. Lena looked up at all that emptiness and, for the first time since the stars went out, didn't feel like the only blank thing in the world.

"All right," she said softly. "Then here's mine. Freely given. When we find my name — when *you* find it, ledger-keeper — I want to hear it from you first. Before anyone. Before the collector, before whatever's left of my mother or father or whoever they were. You read it, and you say it to me, and for one moment I'll be somebody because *you* said so." She swallowed. "That's my price. That's my trade."

Edmund turned to look at her, and the firelight moved in her eyes, and for a heartbeat she wasn't a guildswoman or a swordspeaker or the last of anything. She was just a woman making a promise she intended to keep.

"Witnessed," Edmund said quietly. "And entered into the record."

Somewhere below them, Vessith's ember pulsed — once, twice — and the old dragon's voice rose drowsily through the dark, amused and terrible:

"*How touching. Now sleep, both of you. Tomorrow we ride for the Market of Unmade Things, and I promise you—*" the ember flared, casting their shadows huge against the starless sky— "*there is a stall there that sells memories. And its keeper owes me a debt older than your guild. Which means, little unclaimed one, that tomorrow someone is finally going to ask about a man at a crossroads shrine. And I think you will want to be holding Edmund's hand when they answer.*"
