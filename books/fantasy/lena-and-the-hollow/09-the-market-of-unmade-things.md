## 9. The Market of Unmade Things

*Edmund*

The city of Vespergate did not appear on any map, which was the first sensible thing about it. Maps, in Edmund's experience, were how governments found things they wanted to tax or burn. A city that refused to be mapped was a city that had learned to mind its own business.

It clung to the underside of a cliff like a barnacle with ambitions, all rope bridges and lantern-light and stairways that went sideways when you weren't looking. The air smelled of frying onions, hot metal, and — underneath it, faint as a whisper through a wall — wrongness. The Hollow's scent. It got into everything now, like smoke after a fire.

"Keep your hood up," Edmund murmured, "and your hands visible."

"Why visible?" Lena asked, falling into step beside her on the swaying bridge. Below them, three stories down, a fishmonger argued with a customer about whether an eel could legally be sold as a memory. "I'd have thought invisible hands were safer."

"Invisible hands get cut off here. Visible ones only get watched." Edmund adjusted her sword — plain scabbard, plain belt, nothing that said *oath-bound swordspeaker of the Guild of Impossible Things*, because that guild had tried to have her arrested twice since they'd fled, and she was trying very hard not to be findable. "Also, if someone grabs at you, I want to see whose fingers those are before I break them."

"That's almost sweet," Lena said. "In a threatening way."

"I've been told it's my best quality."

The market proper opened below them like a wound in the cliff, a great hollowed cavern strung with ten thousand lanterns and stalls selling everything the world had decided to stop wanting. Bottled last words. Secondhand courage, slightly worn. A woman selling shadows by the yard, measured out on a wooden spool. Somewhere a fiddler played a tune that made everyone who heard it briefly remember being eight years old and happy about it, which was either magic or extremely good musicianship.

Edmund felt the pull immediately — the ledger-keeper's instinct, the itch to catalog. *One market of unmade things, containing approximately everything.* She'd spent thirty years trading in impossibilities, and even she had to admit this place made the Guild look like a village fair.

But they weren't here to browse. They were here for the covenant-piece: the fragment of the original bargain that a broker named Half-Mad Sable was rumored to hold. Vessith had called it *the first clause*. Edmund didn't know what clause it was, exactly, but she knew what it looked like — a shard of black glass that hummed when held near an honest promise. She also knew that roughly four other people were looking for it, and that at least two of them worked for Corvane.

"Sable trades out of the Drowned Row," she said. "Past the luck-sellers, past the—"

"The stall with the screaming teapot," Lena finished. "You mentioned. Twice."

"It screamed at me. I'm allowed to mention it."

They descended into the noise. And it was there, between a stall selling unspent wishes and one selling perfectly ordinary apples (which, in this market, was somehow the most suspicious thing present), that Edmund felt the hair rise on the back of her neck.

Not the wards. Not the Hollow. Something older. Something that lived in her hand.

She turned, casually, the way she'd turned ten thousand times in crowded rooms, and saw them: three figures in gray traveling cloaks, spread across the market like punctuation marks. Not looking at her. Looking at everything *except* her, which was what trained eyes did.

Corvane's agents. Oath-hound handlers, by the way they stood — weight forward, hands loose, the posture of people whose weapons answered to words.

"Lena," Edmund said quietly. "Don't run."

"I wasn't going to run," Lena whispered back. "I was going to walk quickly in a non-running manner."

"That's running with extra steps."

"It's *dignified* running."

* * *

*Lena*

Lena had been wardless her whole life, and she had long ago made peace with what that meant: magic slid past her like rain off oiled cloth. Wards didn't see her. Spells forgot her mid-cast. Once, as a child, she'd walked through a hedge of thorn-magic that had been specifically grown to keep children out, and it hadn't so much as snagged her sleeve.

It meant, among other things, that the oath-hounds couldn't smell her.

That was the plan, anyway, the one she and Edmund had built on the road: if trouble came, Lena would be the ghost, the one who slipped through, while Edmund — loud, bright, oath-marked Edmund, who blazed through every ward in the world like a bonfire seen for miles — drew the eyes.

What the plan had not accounted for was the third agent. The one already standing behind Sable's stall when they reached the Drowned Row, holding the black glass shard up to his lantern like a man appraising jewelry.

Sable herself — a small, sharp-faced woman with too many rings and no eyebrows — was saying, in a voice pitched to carry, "Now, sir, I'm sure we can discuss price—"

"There is no price," the agent said pleasantly. "There is only custody."

Lena looked at Edmund. Edmund looked at the shard. And Lena watched something happen on Edmund's face that she had never seen there before: not fear, not calculation, but grief. Quick and terrible, like weather.

Because Edmund wanted that shard. Needed it. And Edmund Yarrow did not *want* things; she traded for them, precisely, fairly, keeping her hands steady and her heart in a locked drawer. Wanting things openly was a vulnerability, and here was her whole face wearing one in public.

"We can't fight three of them," Lena breathed.

"No," Edmund agreed. "We can't."

"So what do we do?"

Edmund's hand drifted to her sword hilt. And stopped. Hovered. Like a bird remembering it had once known how to fly.

"I haven't spoken it awake in six years," Edmund said, so quietly Lena nearly lost it under the market noise. "Swordspeech. You speak the true name of your blade, and it... answers. But every word costs. Corvane told me it was a finite account. I believed him, because believing him was easier than asking what he'd spent it on."

"Edmund." Lena reached out and covered that hovering hand with her own. "What does it cost?"

Edmund looked at her then, and lied. Lena saw it happen — saw the truth get set aside neatly, like a ledger entry deferred. "Years, maybe. Nothing tonight."

Later, Lena would remember that moment. Later, she would take it out and examine it and be angry at both of them — at Edmund for lying, and at herself for choosing, just then, to let the lie stand. Because the hounds were coming, and the shard was leaving, and there was no time to audit anything.

The agent with the shard turned. His eyes found Edmund's face, and widened with recognition, and he did the single worst thing possible in a crowded market.

He shouted her name.

"Yarrow! The Guild names Edmund Yarrow — seize her!"

And the market, which had minded its own business for two hundred years, decided all at once to stop.

* * *

*Edmund*

Chaos is a currency too. Edmund had learned that young. Panic spreads faster than orders, and in the space of one overturned apple-stall she had counted exits, marked the hounds' handlers, and put Lena behind her — all before the first oath-hound cleared its leash.

They were ugly things, oath-hounds: dogs the size of ponies, assembled from shadow and old promises, their eyes lit with borrowed oath-fire. They tracked the shape of a sworn word the way normal dogs tracked blood. Edmund had helped raise two of them, once, in the Guild kennels. She'd fed them by hand.

"Hold," she said to the nearest handler, in her ledger-voice, the one that closed deals. "Your hounds are bound to Guild authority. I carry Guild authority. Call them off and walk away, and this never enters anyone's ledger."

The handler hesitated. For one shining second, it almost worked.

Then the man with the shard — a lean, scarred fellow named Vetch, if Edmund remembered her files correctly, and she remembered everyone's files — smiled and said, "Corvane's orders supersede. Take her."

The hounds came.

Edmund drew her sword.

It was an ordinary sword. That had always been the joke of it, the private irony she'd carried for years: the last swordspeaker in the realm, armed with good honest steel from a village smithy, because her true blade — the oath-blade, *Vigilant*, forged the day she took her first vow at age nine — hung in Corvane's vault, where he kept it like a trophy. Or a hostage.

An ordinary sword, then. But swordspeech had never really been about the blade. The blade was just the ear.

Edmund filled her lungs with lantern-smoke and onion-fry and the cold breath of the Hollow seeping down from the broken border, and she spoke.

"*Rise,*" she said, in the old tongue, the tongue of binding, the first language of promises. "*Steel that remembers being ore. Steel that remembers the mountain's patience. Wake, and be my witness.*"

The world went quiet.

Not silent — quiet. The difference mattered. Every sound in the market leaned toward her, the way crowds lean toward a falling glass. The sword in her hand woke. She felt it the way you feel a limb come back after sleeping: pins and needles, then heat, then a low singing joy, steel remembering what it was for. Light crawled along the edge like dawn along a mountaintop.

"Oh," Edmund heard herself say, half-laughing, half-grieving. "Oh, I missed you."

The first hound hit her guard and came apart like smoke in sunlight. The second circled, wiser, and she met it with a stroke that wrote light across the dark, and it, too, unraveled — not killed, for hounds were only promises given teeth, but *released*, sent home to whatever quiet place promises went when they were done. The handlers stumbled back. One dropped his leash entirely and ran.

Vetch stood his ground, shard in fist, and Edmund crossed the distance between them in five strides with her blade burning, and put its point against the man's collarbone, gentle as a librarian's finger marking a page.

"You're going to give me that," she said, "and then you're going to sit down, and drink some water, and think very hard about your employer."

Vetch, to his credit, considered it. Edmund watched him weigh it — the burning sword, the fleeing colleagues, the small sharp-eyed girl at Edmund's shoulder whom his wards had apparently failed to notice at all.

Then he threw the shard. Not to her — past her, high, over the crowd, an arc of black glass spinning up toward the lantern-strung dark, toward the cliff wall, toward—

Lena moved.

Lena, who had never in her life caught anything more athletic than a dropped spoon, stepped off the Drowned Row's edge onto empty air — onto a rope bridge that the wards of the market had, in their blind spot where Lena was concerned, simply forgotten to render — and caught the shard out of the sky like it had been arranged in advance.

The whole market went still again. Even the fiddler lost the tune.

"Well," Lena said, pocketing it, walking back onto solid ground as though she hadn't just performed a miracle by accident. "That went fine."

* * *

*Lena*

They ran, because winning a skirmish in a black-market city mostly means deciding when the bill arrives, and Edmund preferred to leave before the invoice.

Up the sideways stairs, across two bridges, into the upper warrens where the lanterns thinned and the onion-smell gave way to cold stone. Edmund led until she couldn't, and then Lena led, because she noticed what Edmund was trying very hard not to show: the way her right arm had begun to tremble. The way her breathing had gone shallow and careful, like someone counting coins.

They stopped in an abandoned shrine niche — some dead god of travelers, its statue worn faceless — and Edmund sat down on the steps with the controlled precision of a person lowering something fragile.

"Edmund." Lena crouched in front of her. "Look at me."

"I'm fine."

"You're lying."

"I'm *resting*."

"You're both." Lena took Edmund's right hand. It was shaking badly now, the oath-mark on the back of it — the old sigil burned into her skin at nine years old — gone grey and dull, like a coal after the fire passes. "Tell me what it costs. The real number."

Edmund was quiet a long time. In the dimness, with the market's distant music floating up, she looked suddenly very tired and very young, a woman of forty-odd wearing the face of the child who'd signed her first contract without reading the margins.

"When I speak it awake," she said finally, "it burns years. Not metaphorically. Corvane wasn't lying about that part — he just never told me the exchange rate. Tonight cost me..." Her jaw tightened. "A winter. Maybe two. I felt them go. Like coins dropping out of a torn pocket."

Lena's chest hurt. "Two winters. For three hounds and a rude man."

"For the shard." Edmund closed her hand around Lena's. "For the piece that might save the sky. I'd pay it again in a heartbeat, and that's the problem, isn't it? Because next time I'll pay it faster, and tell myself it's nothing, and you'll let me, because I'll lie well enough." She laughed, short and bleak. "Thirty years a ledger-keeper. I know exactly how debts work. That's what makes me dangerous to myself."

"Then we make a rule," Lena said. "Right now. No swordspeech unless we both agree. Both of us, Edmund. You don't get to spend yourself alone."

Edmund looked at their joined hands. Something in her face softened and broke open a little, the way ice does in spring — not dramatically, just enough to let water through.

"All right," she said. "Both of us."

"And you tell me the truth about costs. Even ugly ones. *Especially* ugly ones."

"...All right."

"You're a terrible liar, by the way. Your left eyebrow does a thing."

"My eyebrow does *not*—"

"It absolutely does. It has a whole vocabulary."

Despite everything — the trembling hand, the grey oath-mark, the hounds somewhere below regrouping, the entire Guild presumably en route — Edmund laughed. Actually laughed, warm and startled out of her, and Lena thought: *oh*. Thought it with a lurch, the way you notice a stair that isn't where you expected. She filed the feeling away in whatever drawer Lena kept for feelings she wasn't ready to inventory.

Edmund drew out the shard. Up close, in the shrine's dimness, it was beautiful and awful — black glass shot through with veins of faint gold, and when Edmund held it near her own mouth and said, quietly, "I promise we'll finish this," the thing *hummed*, resonating like a struck bell, gold flaring along its veins.

"The first clause," Edmund murmured. "Vessith was right. It responds to kept promises." She turned it in the light. "Which means the others will too. Which means—" She stopped.

"What?"

"Which means I know where the second piece is." Edmund's voice had gone strange. Reverent and horrified at once. "I've known for years. I catalogued it myself, back when I still trusted the Guild's archives. A drowned shrine, off the Starless coast. The warden's memorial." She looked up at Lena, and her expression did the complicated thing again, grief and guilt braided together. "Lena. The warden's memorial. The warden who built the border wards."

Lena went cold. "I don't know who the warden was. I told you. I traded that memory—"

"I know. I know you did." Edmund reached out and tucked a strand of hair behind Lena's ear, gently, an absent gesture that seemed to surprise them both. "But the shard knows. Look."

Lena took the black glass. Held it near her own lips. And before she could decide whether to speak at all, the shard flared — brighter than it had for Edmund, gold blazing up her wrist, humming a note that went through bone and memory alike, and for half a heartbeat Lena smelled woodsmoke and lavender and heard a woman's voice saying her name. Not *Lena*. Something longer. Something that started with L and ended in a door opening, home, home—

The vision snapped shut. Lena was on her knees on the shrine steps, gasping, the shard dark and quiet in her fist, Edmund's arms around her.

"It knows me," Lena whispered. "Edmund, it *knows* me."

Below them, far off in the market, a hound began to bay — a new note in it, triumphant, having found a scent at last. And beneath the bay, thin and cold, came another sound that made Edmund's blood turn to winter water:

Bells. Guild bells. Corvane's men entering the city, and not quietly this time.

Edmund pulled her up. "Can you run?"

"I can outrun dignity, yes."

"Good. We're leaving through the eel market." At Lena's look: "Trust me. Nobody follows anyone into the eel market."

They ran — out of the faceless god's shrine, down into the lantern-dark, one shard richer and one question heavier, while above the unmapped city the starless sky pressed close as a lid, and somewhere in Edmund's chest, two winters lighter than yesterday, the waking sword sang softly to itself, hungry for the next word.
