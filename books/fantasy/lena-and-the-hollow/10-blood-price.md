## 10. Blood Price

The morning after the Market of Unmade Things, Edmund woke to find her left hand had gone the color of old parchment.

She lay very still in the narrow bed above the Drowned Lantern inn and studied it against the grey light seeping through shutters that didn't quite close. The oath-marks — the fine silver script that wound from her wrist to her elbow like a sentence someone had started writing on her skin and never finished — had faded. Not dimmed. *Faded*, the way ink fades when a ledger is left too long in the sun, until you can no longer read what was paid or owed.

That was, she understood with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, exactly what was happening.

Swordspeech had never been free. Every word spoken awake cost something; every word cost *her*. The guild's oldest training manuals called it "the tithe" and were maddeningly vague about what was tithed, which Edmund now suspected was deliberate. You could not refuse a price you did not understand. She had spent three words in the market — *rise, remember, burn* — and each one had gone through her like a hot wire pulled slowly out through the marrow.

Three words. Perhaps a year apiece. Perhaps more. The arithmetic was not kind, and she had stopped doing it somewhere around dawn, because arithmetic done at dawn tends toward despair, and despair makes for sloppy bookkeeping.

She flexed her fingers. They obeyed. That was something.

Below, Lena was already up. Edmund could hear her through the floorboards — the particular rhythm of a girl who had grown up hungry moving through a strange kitchen: quiet, quick, cataloguing exits and edibles in the same glance. A moment later came the unmistakable sound of someone being talked into giving away more bread than they'd intended. Lena had a gift for it. She couldn't charm wards or speak to swords, but she could make a baker's wife feel like the most interesting person in the city, and somehow there was always an extra loaf afterward.

Edmund sat up, pulled her sleeve down over her wrist, and went downstairs to be lied to by someone she loved.

* * *

"You look terrible," Lena said cheerfully, sliding a plate across the table. "In a heroic way."

"I'm always heroic."

"No, you're usually *tidy*. There's a difference. Tidy people don't have ash in their hair." Lena reached over without asking and picked a fleck of burnt silk out of Edmund's braid, turning it between her fingers. It still smelled faintly of the market — ozone and scorched velvet and something underneath both, like a cellar where nothing had ever lived. "The agent who did this. Did he—"

"He's resting."

"Resting."

"Unconscious," Edmund admitted. "Possibly for some time. Possibly in a way that requires other people to feed him."

Lena snorted into her tea. It was, Edmund reflected, a good sound. Worth any amount of ash in her hair. She ate her bread and let Lena talk — about the innkeeper's daughter who collected river-glass, about whether griffins could swim, about the exact number of stairs in the Drowned Lantern (seventeen, but only if you counted them honestly; sixteen if you skipped the broken one, which everyone did). Lena talked the way water ran, finding every low place, filling them. Edmund mostly listened. Listening was easy. Lying was the work.

Because here was the shape of the lie, and it was a small one, and small lies are the heaviest things in the world to carry:

Lena knew swordspeech had a cost. Everyone knew that much; it was guild common-knowledge, the sort of thing told to apprentices to keep them humble. But the common version said it cost *strength*. A day abed after a hard speaking. A fever. A shaking in the hands. And so this morning, when Edmund had come down the seventeen honest stairs looking pale, Lena had read the pallor as exhaustion and offered sympathy accordingly, and Edmund had accepted the sympathy with a smile and said nothing about parchment-colored skin or fading script or the specific, unshareable knowledge that she was spending herself like a miser's last coin.

It wasn't even a hard lie. That was the terrible part. It fit so neatly into the space between them that Lena stepped right over it without ever seeing it was there.

"We should move today," Lena said, businesslike now, pulling the covenant-piece from her coat. It sat on the table between them like a bruise: a shard of something that wasn't quite glass and wasn't quite bone, dark at the center and lit at the edges, warm to the touch in a way that made your teeth ache. The first piece they'd won. "Before Corvane's agents regroup. I asked around — the ferryman at the Weir will take us as far as Thornmouth, and from Thornmouth there's the old pilgrim road to the shrine country."

"The Weir." Edmund turned her cup slowly. "The ferryman at the Weir is a man named Osk. He owes the guild eleven years of dues and hates me personally."

"Hates you *personally*?"

"I repossessed his luck once. Professionally. It felt personal to him."

"So we can't use the Weir."

"We can't use the Weir," Edmund agreed, "which means we take the long road north along the cut, past the toll-bridge at Hallowford—" She stopped. Something in Lena's face had gone carefully blank, the way faces go when someone is doing arithmetic of their own. "What?"

"Nothing."

"Lena."

"It's just—" Lena turned her cup too, mirroring her, and Edmund realized with a lurch that she'd taught her that gesture without noticing, two weeks on the road and already they were becoming each other's habits. "Hallowford bridge has a ward-gate. I can't pass ward-gates. I mean — I *can*, obviously, they don't see me, but anyone standing next to me gets seen twice, if you follow. If I walk through holding your hand, the gate reads you and finds extra. Guards notice extras."

Edmund had not followed, actually, and was privately delighted by the image — Lena as a second shadow, an uncounted passenger riding along inside a ward's blind spot. But she kept her face serious. "So we avoid Hallowford."

"So we avoid Hallowford, which means the long way, which means four days instead of two, which means four days of Corvane's agents knowing roughly where we are." Lena said it lightly, but her thumb was pressing hard enough on the table to whiten at the nail. "I'm sorry. I know I'm the reason everything takes the long way."

"You are not the reason anything takes the long way." Edmund heard her own voice come out harder than intended and softened it. "Corvane is the reason. The Hollow is the reason. You are the reason we've gotten this far at all — do you know how many times those hounds would have had us on the Hollow Road? Twice. Twice, minimum. Your strangeness is the single most useful thing either of us owns."

"My strangeness," Lena repeated, and smiled in a crooked way that suggested she was filing the phrase away somewhere permanent. "All right. Long way north. Four days." She stood, gathering the piece and wrapping it in wool. "I'll get supplies. Don't follow me into the kitchen, the innkeeper's wife likes me better when I'm alone, it's some kind of—"

"—hunger-based charisma," Edmund said. "Yes. Go."

And then Edmund sat alone at the table with her fading hand and did the arithmetic she'd sworn off at dawn, because apparently she was the kind of person who did arithmetic she'd sworn off, and the numbers came out the same as before, and she put her sleeve down and thought: *four days. I can hide anything for four days.*

It was at this point, she would later note in the margin of her memory, that she began keeping a second ledger — the dishonest kind, the kind with entries missing — and that all the worst trades of her life started exactly there.

* * *

The long road north followed the cut, an old canal half-drained and gone to reeds, running straight as a ruled line through country that had clearly given up on being impressive. Hills, but modest ones. Woods, but polite ones. A sky that couldn't decide between rain and not-rain and committed fully to neither.

They walked. Lena invented a game on the first morning where every ruined building they passed had to be assigned a fictional history, delivered with total confidence. ("This was a monastery of the Order of the Perpetually Damp. They worshipped a god of mildew. Very gentle faith, lots of picnics.") Edmund, whose entire life had been precision, found this game scandalous and could not stop playing it.

By the second evening, though, the pretending got harder.

It started as a stiffness in her back that wasn't stiffness. Then her vision would blur at the edges, just briefly, like breath fogging a window and clearing. Once, crossing a stile, her legs simply declined for a full three seconds to participate, and she stood gripping the fence post while Lena chattered ahead about a chapel dedicated to Saint Whoever-Lost-The-Keys, and by the time Edmund caught up she had composed her face into mild interest and nothing had happened, nothing at all, see how well I am.

The third night they sheltered in a shepherd's hut with a roof that worked in spirit more than fact. Lena built a fire with the competence of someone who'd slept rough for years, and while it caught, Edmund unwrapped the covenant-piece to check it — habit, ritual, the guild reflex of counting stock — and found it brighter than yesterday. Warmer. It hummed faintly against her palm, and the hum traveled up her arm and settled somewhere behind her sternum like a second heartbeat, impatient, counting down to something.

"It likes you," Lena said, watching.

"It's a fragment of a broken interdimensional covenant. It doesn't like anyone."

"It's humming louder when you hold it than when I do. I checked." Lena sat back on her heels, firelight on her face, and gave Edmund the look — the direct, unguarded one that made Edmund feel like a window someone had washed clean. "You'd tell me. If something was wrong."

The question hung there, simple and enormous.

Here is what Edmund Yarrow, keeper of ledgers, last of the swordspeakers, had learned about lying in her forty-one years: it is easiest with strangers, harder with colleagues, and nearly impossible with someone who looks at you like that. So she did what liars have always done in the final extremity. She told the truth about something else.

"When I was nine," Edmund said, "the guild tested me. They put a sword in my hands and a dead man's name in my ear and asked me to wake it. And I did — first try, no training, and the sword spoke back, and the examiner cried, and I didn't understand why until years later. He wasn't crying because I'd succeeded. He was crying because he'd watched a child pick up a debt she couldn't see." She turned her hand over, sleeve slipping, and looked at the silver script rather than at Lena. "Everyone thinks the gift is the speaking. It isn't. The gift is the *carrying*. The speaking is just spending what you carry."

Lena was quiet. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, an owl conducted its tedious business.

"What does it cost?" Lena asked finally. Softly. "Really."

*A year a word,* said the honest ledger. *Three words spent. More coming. A shrinking margin, entered in red.*

"A good night's sleep," Edmund said, and smiled, and hated herself with a thoroughness that would have impressed the examiners. "Truly. I'm tired, Lena. That's all. Old woman tired. Let me have my dignity."

Something moved behind Lena's eyes — a flicker, a doubt, a door opening onto a room Edmund couldn't see into — and then closed again. "Fine," Lena said. "But if 'old woman tired' starts falling off stiles, I'm carrying you."

"You could not carry me."

"I absolutely could. I'm sturdier than I look. Wardless girls develop excellent shoulders; we're always climbing through windows."

Despite everything — despite the fading script and the humming shard and the second ledger growing fat with omissions — Edmund laughed. Really laughed, until her ribs ached in the ordinary way, and Lena looked pleased with herself in the manner of someone who has just won a duel, and for a little while the hut was warm and the night was only a night.

Later, when Lena slept, Edmund took out the piece again and held it and listened to it hum, and made herself a promise in the old formal cadence, the kind that used to bind: *I will finish this. Whatever it costs, I will pay it quietly, and she will never carry the bill.* It was, she understood even as she swore it, both the noblest and stupidest thing she had ever done, and she was a person with a documented history of both.

* * *

On the fourth day, the road ended.

Not gradually. The cut simply stopped — a wall of fresh-cut timber and torn earth where men had been busy, and beyond it, where the map promised a ford and a ferry landing, there was a garrison. Guild colors. Guild tents. And drawn up along the near bank, patient as arithmetic, a line of soldiers who were not attacking anything, because they didn't need to. They were guarding the crossing. All of them.

Lena crouched beside Edmund in the reeds, binoculars borrowed from a farmer who would miss them. "That's the whole route," she said. "Thornmouth was the only way across without going back through Hallowford, and Hallowford's worse — Corvane moved a full company there two days ago. I heard it from a drover." Her voice stayed level, but Edmund heard the strain under it, the sound of a plan dying. "We're boxed. Everything south of the river belongs to him now."

Edmund lowered the borrowed lenses and looked at the garrison, and looked at the water — wide, brown, slow, deceptively calm — and felt the covenant-piece pulse against her chest like a second heart insisting on its own schedule.

"There's another way across," she said.

Lena looked at her. "There's a garrison, a company, and a river. What's the other way?"

"The river."

"That's not a way, that's a *place*—"

"People have swum rivers before. Historically. Repeatedly."

"People have also drowned in them, historically, repeatedly, and at *scale*—" Lena stopped. Recalibrated. Looked at the water, then at Edmund, and Edmund watched her arrive at the same conclusion from the other direction, the way she always did, meeting Edmund in the middle of a bad idea like it was a bridge they'd built together. "You can't swim that. Not in armor, not with that shoulder, not—"

"Then we'll find a boat."

"Every boat on this stretch is on *their* side of the river, Edmund."

"Then," Edmund said, with the bright false cheer of a woman reading numbers she doesn't like, "we'll steal one from their side. Tonight. Quietly. You're very good at quietly."

Lena stared at her for a long moment. The wind moved the reeds. Far off, a garrison bugle called the change of watch, thin and clear over the water.

"You're planning something reckless," Lena said at last, "and you're hiding how scared you are, and I can't prove either one, and I hate it."

"I'm planning something reckless," Edmund agreed. "The rest is slander."

But that night, checking the piece one last time before they moved, Edmund saw something that stopped her breath. The shard had changed. The dark center had thinned — just slightly, just at one edge — and through the thinning, for half a heartbeat, she had seen light. Not firelight. Not starlight. There were no stars to give it. A light from somewhere else, patient and vast, like a lamp burning in a house at the end of a long road.

And beneath the light, faint as breath on glass, something that might have been a voice, or a memory of a voice, saying a word she almost knew.

"Edmund?" Lena whispered from the reeds. "Ready?"

Edmund wrapped the shard, buried it against her heart, and rose.

"As I'll ever be," she said — and did not add the rest, the true rest, which was: *and less than I was yesterday, and tomorrow less again, and the river is cold, and the ledger is short, and God forgive me, I would spend every page of it to keep walking beside you.*

They went down to the water in the dark, toward the fires of the garrison, to steal a boat from an army — and behind Edmund's ribs, the covenant-piece began, very softly, to sing.
