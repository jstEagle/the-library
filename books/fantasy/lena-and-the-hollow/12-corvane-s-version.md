## 12. Corvane's Version

They took me at the ford of the Weeping River, which I will note — because pettiness is the last comfort of the captured — was a terrible tactical choice on my part. I had been so pleased with ourselves for evading the oath-hounds through the marsh country that I had stopped looking over my shoulder. Lena had not. She had said, not an hour before, "Edmund, you keep walking like someone who's already won," and I had laughed and said something clever about momentum.

Momentum, it turns out, is what carries you into a net of woven silence-threads strung across a river shallows at dusk.

I heard Lena shout my name as they pulled me under the threads — the world going woolly and mute, my swordspeech smothered like a candle under a cup — and then hands, many hands, and the particular smell of guild-issue travel cloaks that Corvane had never once thought to update in forty years. Lavender and old paper. The smell of every ledger room I'd ever loved.

That was the cruelest part, really. They smelled like home.

* * *

I woke in a tent that was far too comfortable to be a prison, which is a very specific kind of insult. There was a writing desk. There was tea, still steaming. There was a chair positioned across from mine with the careful geometry of a man who has rehearsed this conversation in front of a mirror.

Corvane sat behind the desk, reading one of my ledgers.

My ledger. The one from the Hollow Road, the one I'd abandoned in our flight from the guild, and he held it the way a priest holds scripture — thumb resting in the gutter of the page, head bowed slightly, as though the columns of traded thunderstorms and returned childhoods were holy writ.

"You catalogued the rain," he said, without looking up. "In Vell's Crossing. Three days of it. You wrote down what each farmer paid, and what the rain was worth, and you noted that Widow Marsh overpaid by two coppers and you refunded her personally." He closed the book gently. "Do you know how long I've waited for someone in this guild to refund a widow?"

"Guildmaster." My voice came out steadier than I felt. My hands were unbound — another insult, or perhaps a message: *you are no threat to me* — and I made myself fold them in my lap where the tremor couldn't be seen. "You've gone to a great deal of trouble for a conversation."

"Trouble is the currency of everything worth having." He poured tea. Two cups. He pushed one toward me and I did not touch it, and he smiled at that, a small genuine smile, which was somehow worse than a villainous one. "Drink or don't. I'm not going to poison you, Edmund. Poisoning implies I consider you a problem. You're not a problem. You're a *misunderstanding* with legs."

"That's the kindest thing anyone's said about me all year."

He laughed. That was the worst of it — he laughed easily, warmly, the way he used to laugh in the archive when a junior clerk mislabeled a bottled echo as a bottled scream. Forty years I had known this man. He had taught me the difference between a name and its weight. He had signed my apprenticeship papers with a flourish and told me that precision was a form of love.

I had believed him. Some traitorous corner of me still wanted to.

"The girl," he said. "Lena. Where is she?"

"Gone."

"Edmund." He set down the teapot with a click of gentle reproach. "We both know she doubled back the moment they took you. We both know she's currently crouched in the reeds trying to decide whether to cut my tent open with a kitchen knife." He glanced, almost fondly, toward the north wall of the tent. "Tell her the knife won't cut silence-thread and she'll only hurt herself."

My stomach dropped, and I hated that he saw it.

"She's wardless," I said. "She walked through your collapsed wards like they weren't there. Your hounds can't hold her scent. Your agents can't see her coming. And you're sitting here drinking tea instead of hunting her, which tells me you need her alive more than you need me quiet." I leaned forward. "So let's skip to the part where you tell me why."

For a moment — just a moment — the warmth went out of him like a draft through a door. What remained underneath was tired. Profoundly, geologically tired, the tiredness of a man who had been holding up a ceiling for forty years and had forgotten there was any position other than braced.

"Yes," he said quietly. "Let's do that."

* * *

He stood, and went to the tent flap, and looked out at the night — at the sky with its terrible absence, the starless dark pressing down like a lid.

"Do you know what I was doing the night the stars went out?" he asked. "The first time. Forty years ago. Before you were born."

"You've never spoken of it."

"No one has. That was rather the point." His back stayed turned. "I was twenty-six years old. Junior warden's assistant. I stood on the border ridge with the old Warden Ashvale — Lena's mother, though I didn't know yet what that would come to mean — and we watched the covenant renew itself. It does it every hundred years, Edmund. Did. The realms shake hands. The wards drink deep. The stars stay lit. A simple ceremony, beautifully engineered, and it required exactly one thing."

"A sacrifice," I said. "A name, freely given."

His shoulders stiffened. "You've been reading the deep ledgers."

"I found my own name in them. Sold. Sealed into the covenant when I was six years old." I kept my voice flat, which cost me everything I had. "So yes, Guildmaster, I know what the ceremony requires. I know that *someone* chose me. I'd very much like to hear you say it."

He turned around then, and I expected deflection. I expected the guildmaster's smile, the polished one, the one that had closed a thousand dangerous conversations. Instead he looked at me with an expression I had never seen on him, and it took me a moment to recognize it, because I had only ever seen it in mirrors on very bad nights.

Shame.

"The covenant needed a name freely given," he said. "Given by the person themselves. Not sold, not stolen, not inherited. *Given.* A child's willing promise, renewed at the century's turning by the same voice, grown. That was the engineering. The old wardens built it that way deliberately — a covenant of consent, so no realm could ever bind another against its will." He laughed, short and bitter. "Beautiful. Principled. And it meant that every hundred years, some family had to raise a child knowing, and that child had to stand on the ridge at the appointed hour and say *yes* with their whole heart, or the wards would fail."

"And you were the one who had to ask."

"My teacher was. Warden Ashvale. She had chosen a child — a foundling from the guild orphanage, marked at birth with the oath-sign, raised for it, told stories about honor until the honor grew into the bone." His eyes came to mine. "She could not do it, Edmund. She stood in front of that six-year-old with the paper in her hand and she could not ask a child to spend her life holding up the sky. So she refused. She tore up the appointment. She said she'd find another way."

"And there wasn't one."

"There was *always* another way," Corvane snapped, and for one flash I saw the man the guild whispered about, the man who had out-traded archdevils and talked a river backward. "There is always another way if you're clever enough and ruthless enough and willing to pay. I was all three. I bought the child's name — legally, cleanly, through intermediaries she never met — and I sealed it into the covenant as a *sale* rather than a gift. The wards accepted it. Names have weight; coin has weight; the mathematics balanced." He spread his hands. "The stars stayed lit for forty years. No child was asked to die for the sky. I saved everyone."

"The wards fell anyway."

"The wards fell," he agreed softly, "because a sale is not a promise. Because the covenant drank a name given under false pretenses and it has been *starving* ever since, feeding on the gap between what it was owed and what it got. Yes. I know. I have known since the night the stars went out, and I have spent every hour since trying to buy, steal, or forge a replacement before the Hollow finishes eating through the wound I made." He sat down heavily. "So. Now you have my version. Am I the monster you came here thinking I was?"

And here is the thing I will never put in any ledger, the thing I am setting down now only because a chapter must be honest even when its author cannot be:

For a moment — a real moment, three heartbeats long — I believed him. Not the justification. The grief. I believed that he had stood where Warden Ashvale stood and made the choice she couldn't, and that he had carried it alone for forty years, and that some ruined, stubborn scrap of him genuinely believed he had been kind.

It would have been easier if he were simply wicked. Wickedness you can fight. But Corvane was what happens when a good man decides he is the only competent person in the world: he stops asking permission, then stops asking questions, then stops seeing people at all — sees only variables, payments, weights. He hadn't sold my name despite his conscience. He had sold it *instead* of consulting it, and called the silence mercy.

"You never asked me," I said.

"You were six."

"You never asked me at seventeen, or twenty-five, or last year, when I stood in your office and handed you the ledgers and trusted you with my whole life." My voice cracked and I let it. "You had forty years, Corvane. One sentence. *Your name holds up the sky — do you consent?* One sentence, and I'd have said yes. Do you understand that? I'd have said yes at six years old, because you'd have made it sound like an honor, because I worshipped you. You stole a gift you could have had for free."

He flinched. Actually flinched, the great Corvane, the trader of impossible things, and for a heartbeat he looked like nothing so much as an old clerk who had gotten the arithmetic wrong and couldn't bear to correct the ledger.

"I know," he said.

And that nearly undid me. Not the crime — the *knowing*. I looked at this man who had taught me that precision was love, and I understood that he had known for forty years that the covenant was starving on a lie, and he had kept trading anyway, kept smiling, kept pouring tea, because the alternative was to open the books and admit the founding entry was fraud.

"You're not going to fix it," I said. "Whatever you're planning with Lena — whatever the vessel is, whatever you think her wardlessness makes her — you're going to do it *to* her, not *with* her. Same entry, different ink."

"Lena is the daughter of the woman who built the wards," Corvane said, and there it was again, that terrible gentleness. "Her blood is in the covenant's foundation. If she stands at the Hollow's heart willingly—"

"*Willingly.*" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "You don't get to use that word. You spent it."

"Then what would you have me do, Edmund?" He rose, and his voice rose with it, and forty years of braced exhaustion finally showed through the polish. "Let the Hollow eat both realms? Hold a committee? I have made *every* trade available to me. I have paid in names and years and other people's children, and I would pay in my own if it were worth anything — but it isn't, that's the joke, my name weighs nothing to the covenant because I *chose* wrong at the start and everything since has been interest." He leaned across the desk, close enough that I could see the tremor in his hands too, and oh, that landed somewhere soft. "Tell me what you'd do differently. Truly. I'll listen. I have always listened to you."

And I opened my mouth to say something magnificent, something ledger-worthy —

— and the tent wall tore open, and Lena fell through it in a shower of canvas and silence-thread, holding a kitchen knife that had, as predicted, done absolutely nothing to the threads and a great deal to the canvas, and behind her the night filled with shouting and lantern-light and the belling cry of oath-hounds.

Corvane sighed the sigh of a man whose evening has been ruined by exactly the people he expected.

"Lena," I said, standing, heart hammering, "the knife doesn't cut—"

"I *noticed*," she gasped, grabbing my wrist, "so I brought fire instead," and hurled a lantern through the tea service.

What happened next happened quickly and mostly sideways. Fire caught the canvas. Hounds bayed. Corvane did not reach for a weapon — I want that recorded — he reached for the burning ledger on his desk, my ledger, and pulled it clear of the flames with bare hands, cradling it, and in that instant of distraction Lena hauled me through the torn wall into the dark.

We ran. Reeds, mud, the river's cold slap, her hand locked around mine, both of us laughing in the unhinged way of people who have recently escaped a villain via arson.

But as the camp fell behind, I looked back once. Just once.

Corvane stood in the burning doorway of his tent, silhouetted against his own miscalculation, holding a ledger to his chest like a child holds a blanket, watching us go. And he did not send the hounds after us.

He let us run.

"He knows where we're going," Lena panted as we climbed the far bank. "Doesn't he. He's not chasing us because he doesn't have to. He's letting us gather the pieces for him."

"Yes," I said.

"Edmund." She stopped, breathless, starlight-less, her face turned up to the empty sky. "If he's right — if the covenant needs someone willing at the Hollow's heart —"

"It needs a promise," I said. "Freely given. Freely *renewed*. And there is exactly one person alive whose name is already written in it." I squeezed her hand, and did not say the rest, and she did not ask, because she already knew, and we stood together in the dark a moment longer, two liars keeping each other's secrets under a sky with no stars.

Then the ground shuddered — far off, eastward, a deep tectonic groan like the world clearing its throat — and a faint red glow bloomed on the horizon, pulsing slow as a heartbeat.

Vessith. Calling us home.

Or calling us to the fire. With dragons, I was learning, the distinction was academic.
