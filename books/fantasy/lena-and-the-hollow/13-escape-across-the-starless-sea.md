## 13. Escape Across the Starless Sea

The boat was called the *Second Chance*, which Edmund considered either a very good omen or a very bad one, depending entirely on how the next three days went.

She'd bought it from a fisherman in Greyharbor with most of what remained of their coin, and the transaction had taken longer than it should have because the fisherman kept asking questions. Was she planning to cross? Nobody crossed anymore. Not since the stars went out. The sea past the shallows belonged to the Hollow now, and everyone knew what happened to boats that sailed into an absence.

"Everyone knows," Edmund had said, counting out the coins, "and yet here I am, buying your boat."

"That's my point exactly," the fisherman had said.

Lena had ended the negotiation by simply picking up the mooring rope and starting to walk toward the water, and the fisherman had watched her go with the expression of a man watching his common sense leave on someone else's deck.

Now, two hours out from harbor, the last lights of the coast had sunk behind them, and there was nothing anywhere at all.

Edmund stood at the tiller and tried not to look up.

It was harder than it sounded. For thirty years, the sky had been furniture — the fixed and reliable backdrop against which every ledger entry, every trade, every small miracle had been recorded. She had dated documents by starlight. *Recorded under the Lantern, third quarter.* And now the sky above the sea was a single unbroken sheet of black, so complete that it didn't feel like night. It felt like a ceiling. It felt like something leaning down to see what they were doing.

"You're doing the thing," Lena said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at nothing and pretend you're cataloguing it." Lena sat cross-legged amidships, mending a sail patch by the light of their one lantern, needle flashing. "You get a line between your eyebrows. Right there. Like you're trying to invoice the dark."

"I am *assessing* the dark."

"It's the same thing."

"It is not the same thing," Edmund said, with the dignity of a woman who had once traded a bottled thunderstorm to a man who paid in memories, and who felt this entitled her to certain distinctions. "Assessment is a professional activity. Cataloguing is a compulsion. I'll have you know I've been cured of neither."

Lena grinned and bent back over her mending, and Edmund felt some knot behind her ribs loosen by a degree. That was the trouble with Lena, she was beginning to understand. The girl made danger feel survivable, which was far more dangerous than danger itself.

The sea beneath them was wrong, too. Not black — black would have been fine, black would have been honest. It was *unlit*, the way a room is unlit when the lamp has been carried out of it. Their lantern threw its little circle across the deck and the water simply declined to participate. Where the light touched the waves, they moved without gleaming, like silk in a dream, like something remembering water rather than being it.

Edmund checked their heading by dead reckoning and the compass both. The compass pointed somewhere with great confidence; whether that somewhere was north was another matter. Under a starless sky, instruments got philosophical.

"How long?" Lena asked.

"To the drowned shrine? Two days, if the wind holds. Three if it doesn't." She paused. "Four if the sea decides we're interesting."

"And if the Hollow finds us first?"

Edmund adjusted the tiller a half-degree that did not need adjusting. "Then we'll have a conversation about it."

"With the Hollow."

"People talk to me all day," Edmund said. "It's my profession. I don't see why absence should be exempt."

But her oath-marked hand had begun, very faintly, to ache — the old scar across her palm warming like a banked coal — and she did not mention that part.

* * *

They took turns sleeping, or attempting to. Edmund took first watch, then second, then a third watch she did not report, because sleep had become a country whose borders she couldn't find. Around what she judged to be midnight — though midnight, too, had gotten philosophical — Lena appeared beside her with two cups of tea brewed from the dregs of their supplies, which tasted mostly of hot water and optimism.

"You're supposed to wake me," Lena said.

"I was about to."

"You were *not* about to."

"I was structuring my thoughts toward waking you."

Lena pressed a cup into her hands and sat down close enough that their shoulders touched, which Edmund suspected was deliberate. "Drink. And tell me something true, since we're apparently going to die on this boat. It's traditional."

"We're not going to die on this boat."

"Something true," Lena repeated, patient as tide.

Edmund drank her terrible tea and looked at the black horizon, and heard herself say: "I've never been on open water before. Thirty years keeping ledgers for impossible things, and I've never once left sight of land. Every miracle I ever brokered happened within walking distance of my desk." A pause. "I used to think that made me safe. Now I think it just meant everything I loved fit in one room."

The words came out quieter than she intended. Lena didn't laugh at them, which Edmund appreciated, and didn't pity them either, which she appreciated more.

"My turn," Lena said. "I don't know how to swim."

Edmund turned to look at her. They were in the middle of a haunted sea. "You're joking."

"Wardless girls don't get swimming lessons. Wardless girls get *lessons* in staying unnoticed, which mostly involve learning to run." Lena shrugged, but Edmund saw the tension in her shoulders, the joke worn thin over something real. "So if the boat goes down, one of us has a plan and one of us has you."

"I'd hold onto you," Edmund said, before she could decide whether to say it.

Lena looked at her then, in the lantern light, and something passed between them that neither of them filed away in any ledger. "Well," Lena said finally. "Then I suppose I have a plan after all."

That was when the sea stopped moving.

Not calmed. *Stopped*. The swell that had rocked them for hours simply ceased, mid-motion, the way a sentence stops when the speaker forgets what it was about to say. The boat settled into water gone flat as poured glass, and the silence that followed had texture to it — a listening quality, vast and attentive.

Edmund's hand closed around the hilt of her sword before thought caught up.

"Lena."

"I know." Lena had gone still, eyes fixed on the water off the port side. "I see it."

There was nothing to see. That was the whole horror of it. No shape, no glow, no ripple — just a region of the sea where even the lantern light seemed to fall more reluctantly, a patch of darkness darker than the night, sliding toward them with the patience of something that had never once needed to hurry.

"The Hollow," Edmund breathed.

"It found us," Lena whispered. "Or we found it. I can't tell which."

The edge of it reached the hull.

Edmund braced for impact, for cold, for teeth — for anything a body could fight. What came instead was *remembering*. The Hollow brushed the boat the way a blind man reads, and it read *her*, and she felt herself being opened like a book.

* * *

The emptiness it showed Edmund was not dramatic. That was the cruelty of it.

She was standing in the guildhall again, at her desk, and the desk was exactly as it had always been, and the ledgers were stacked in their perfect rows, and the light came through the high windows at the angle it always came, and none of it was real and all of it was hers. In the vision she was old. Her hand didn't tremble. Her sword hung on the wall behind her, unworn, its edge bright with thirty years of disuse, and she understood with total clarity that this was the life she had actually lived — that the road, the dragon, the girl were the dream, and this desk was the truth, and she had traded away every wild thing for the comfort of being precise.

And the room was silent. No footsteps but her own. No voice calling her name — because she had sold her name, hadn't she, and what remained was a function, a clerkship, a hand that signed things. She would die at that desk and the guild would enter it in a ledger and someone else would inherit the chair, and the sum total of Edmund Yarrow would be a neat column of figures ending in zero.

*You were never brave,* the emptiness said, in no voice at all. *You were only careful.*

"No," Edmund said aloud, on the deck, in the rainless dark, her knuckles white on the rail.

Beside her, Lena had gone rigid, her face slack, her eyes fixed on nothing — reading her own book. Edmund grabbed her shoulder and the contact grounded them both, two anchors thrown to each other in the same storm.

Whatever Lena saw, it made her breath come in short, sharp pulls. "No," she was whispering. "No, that's not — I don't want it back, I don't want—"

*Lena,* Edmund said, and then, because names have weight and hers was spent, she said it again, louder, into the dark, into the thing reading them: "*Lena.* You are here. You are with me. Whatever it's showing you — it's showing you an empty room. This isn't empty. *I'm* not empty. Come back."

Lena shuddered, gasped, surfaced. Her hand found Edmund's and gripped it hard enough to hurt, and Edmund held on, and the pain was the most honest thing aboard.

"It showed me—" Lena's voice cracked. "It showed me the memory I sold. Except filled in. It offered to give it back — my home, my mother's kitchen, my name — if I'd just stop fighting it. If I'd just let go and sink." She laughed, wet and shaky. "It tried to bribe me with my own childhood."

"Of course it did," Edmund said. "Everything in this world runs on trades. Why should the end of it be different?" She lifted their joined hands. "What did you tell it?"

"I told it I'd rather build new memories than live in a museum." Lena wiped her eyes with her free hand. "What did it show you?"

Edmund hesitated. Then, because terror shared was the only currency that had never once devalued: "A desk. An old age alone. It told me I was only ever careful, never brave."

Lena studied her — really studied her, the way she'd once studied the wards that couldn't see her. "It's lying," she said simply. "I've seen you speak a sword awake. I've seen you buy a boat from a man who thought you were mad. Careful people don't do any of that." A pause, and gentler: "And you're not alone. Look around. Terrible boat, worse tea, no stars. And me."

"Yes," Edmund said. Her throat was tight. "I had noticed the me."

The Hollow withdrew.

There was no drama to it. The flat water remembered how to move; the swell returned like a servant resuming duties; the pressure against the hull eased and slid away astern, unhurried, unoffended. Somewhere in the black sky above, Edmund would have sworn she felt — not saw, *felt* — a single point of faint gray light kindle and gutter, like a coal deciding whether to become fire.

Lena saw it too. "Did you—"

"I saw it."

"A star?"

"An ember," Edmund said slowly, her oath-marked palm burning now in earnest, warm as a held promise. "Vessith's ember. Or the covenant answering. Something, somewhere, saying *not yet*." She turned the boat's head back toward the drowned shrine, and found that her hands, for the first time since the stars went out, were steady. "Hold course, Miss Lena. We've been read, weighed, and found worth sinking — and we're still here."

Ahead, through the starless dark, faint and green and impossible, a light rose from beneath the sea — the drowned shrine, waking as they approached, as if it had been waiting.

As if it knew whose daughter was coming home.
