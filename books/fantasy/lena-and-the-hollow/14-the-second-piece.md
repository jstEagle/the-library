## 14. The Second Piece

The drowned shrine did not look like much from above.

It looked like a ring of black stones breaking the surface of a lake that had no business existing this far inland — a lake the maps called the Weeping Mere and the locals called, with the flat certainty of people who had learned not to ask questions, *the Bad Water*. The water was the color of strong tea left too long, and it did not ripple when the wind touched it, which Edmund found professionally offensive. Water rippled. That was one of the few reliable contracts left in the world.

"Cheerful place," Lena said, standing at the stony shore with her boots off and her trousers rolled to the knee, testing the shallows with one toe as though the mere might be persuaded to be reasonable. "I've seen friendlier graveyards."

"You've seen very few graveyards."

"I've seen *enough* graveyards." She wiggled her toes in the water and frowned. "It's warm."

Edmund crouched beside her and dipped two fingers in. Lena was right. The water was blood-warm, faintly sweet-smelling, like a bath someone had forgotten to drain. Somewhere beneath that tea-dark surface lay the second piece of the covenant, and somewhere above it, if Vessith's directions could be trusted — and the dragon had delivered them in the tone of a woman reading out a debt collection notice, so Edmund trusted them the way one trusts an invoice — waited the ghost of the warden who had built the border wards.

Lena's mother.

Edmund had not told her yet. She had turned the problem over for three days of travel, on the road and then on the slow barge down the Mere's feeder river, and every version of the telling sounded wrong. *By the way, the shrine is haunted by the woman whose sabotage your father died for.* Or: *Good news, we've found a piece of the covenant; bad news, it comes with a ghost who may ask you things you cannot answer.* There was no gentle arithmetic for it. Some sums simply came out cruel.

"Lena," she began.

"If you're about to tell me something heavy," Lena said, not looking up from the water, "do it after I've decided whether I'm swimming or not. I can only dread one thing at a time, and right now the thing is fish."

"There are no fish in the Bad Water," said a voice behind them, dry as old paper. "Nothing lives in it. Nothing dies in it either. It only remembers."

Edmund turned, hand going to the hilt of her sword out of habit rather than hope. Their guide stood among the reeds where no one had been standing a moment before: a small, stooped woman in a fisherman's oilskin gone green with age, her face seamed like a walnut shell, her eyes the same tea-dark color as the lake. She had appeared at the edge of their camp two nights ago, announced herself as Mother Sallow, and refused to leave. She also refused, when pressed, to explain how she had known they were coming, which Edmund considered a significant omission in any introduction.

"And what does it remember?" Lena asked, wriggling her toes again, because she was constitutionally incapable of leaving a mystery alone once it had been identified.

"Everything that was put into it," Mother Sallow said. "That's what drowning is, girl. It's being kept." Her gaze slid to Edmund, and there was something in it — recognition, or its opposite, the look of someone confirming a face against a description decades old. "You'll want the sunken door. Third stone past the broken one. And you'll want to go down before dark, because the shrine keeps honest hours and dishonest guests."

"How do you know all this?" Edmund asked.

Mother Sallow smiled with about four teeth. "I've been waiting a long time for someone to ask me that question properly. Come back up alive and I'll answer it."

* * *

The sunken door was exactly where the old woman said it would be, which Edmund found more alarming than if it hadn't been. Diving in a lake that remembered things was bad enough. Diving toward a door that a stranger had predicted was the sort of precision that usually preceded a bill.

They went down together, because Edmund had learned by now that separating from Lena was less a strategy than a superstition, and because Lena had looked at the dark water and said, "Well, it can't drown me if it doesn't know I'm there," which was either courage or theology, and Edmund had stopped trying to tell the difference.

The wards didn't see Lena. That was the whole strange truth of her — wards parted around her like crowds around a blind beggar, spells slid off her skin, magic in general treated her as a gap in the ledger, an entry that wouldn't balance and so was quietly ignored. It had saved them from oath-hounds on the Hollow Road. It had gotten them through three checkpoints in the Market of Unmade Things. And now it meant that as Edmund swam down through the tea-dark water, feeling the shrine's old protections wake and press against her like hands checking her pockets, Lena swam beside her untouched, a hole in the world shaped like a girl, and Edmund found it steadying rather than eerie. Whatever else the deep held, at least one of them would arrive unexamined.

The door was bronze, green with age, set into the side of a stone platform that must have been dry land once, before the mere gathered here like a memory that refused to disperse. Words were chased into the metal in the old ward-script, and even through the water Edmund felt them hum against her teeth — the signature of the original warden, the hand that had raised the border wards, the craft that Corvane had ordered undone.

She pressed her oath-marked palm to the bronze.

The door knew her. That was the horror of it. It read the mark on her hand the way a merchant reads a seal, and it opened — slowly, grinding, admitting her the way a bank admits a debtor — because whatever else she was, she was still, in the covenant's cold accounting, collateral.

They surfaced inside an air pocket, a dome of hollow stone where the water fell away below them and lamplight — actual lamplight, golden and patient, burning without fuel — filled a shrine that time had forgotten to finish ruining. Pillars rose from black water. A floor of white tile stretched between them, cracked but clean. And at the far end, on a low altar of the same white stone, something small glowed with a light like a coal wrapped in silk.

The second piece of the covenant.

"There you are," Lena breathed, treading water at Edmund's side, hair plastered to her face, grinning like a thief in a temple, which was precisely what she was. "One tiny glowing thing. All that swimming for one tiny glowing thing."

"It's never just the thing," Edmund said. "In my experience the thing is always the receipt. What matters is who's holding the account."

As if summoned by the observation — and Edmund suspected, grimly, that it had been — the water at the center of the shrine began to rise.

It did not splash. It climbed. A column of the mere lifted itself out of the black pool and stood, and stood, and became a woman: tall, broad-shouldered, wearing the grey traveling coat of a border warden with the insignia worn smooth as river stones. Her face was made of water and memory both — Edmund could see the tiles of the floor through her cheek, and also, unmistakably, the line of a jaw that Lena had inherited, the set of eyes that Lena made when she was deciding whether to trust someone.

Lena went absolutely still.

Edmund had seen her afraid. She had seen her furious, exhausted, grief-struck on the Hollow Road when the hounds came close enough to smell. She had never seen her *still*. Stillness, on Lena, was like frost on a summer window: wrong season, wrong everything.

"Mama?" Lena said. The word came out very small, in a voice Edmund had never heard her use, a child's voice, dredged up from wherever children keep the words they don't get to say.

The ghost's expression did something complicated. Grief moved across it like weather. When she spoke, her voice was the sound water makes in a cave — echoing, patient, sad.

"Not mama," the ghost said gently. "The shape of her. The shrine keeps what was given to it, and she gave the wards her likeness so they would know their maker. I am the keeping, not the kept." She drifted closer, and the lamplight bent around her. "But I remember being her. That is the cruelty of shrines. They remember better than the living do."

"What's your name?" Edmund asked, because names had weight, because she had learned that lesson in blood and ledgers both, and because if this thing was going to stand in front of Lena wearing her mother's face, Edmund wanted at minimum its true name on record.

"Warden Serevan Hale," the ghost said. "Builder of the border wards. Keeper of the second seal. And—" her water-made eyes settled on Lena with a tenderness that hurt to watch— "mother of the girl who is standing in my shrine uninvited, unwetted, and unrecognized by every ward I ever raised, which should be impossible, and which means the covenant has been busy while I slept."

"She's my daughter," Lena whispered. "You're really—"

"I am what remains when a person gives everything away," the ghost said. "Which is a lesson, child, and I'd have you learn it secondhand." Then she turned to Edmund, and the tenderness banked itself like a covered fire, and what remained was the flat gray authority of a warden reviewing a breach report. "Edmund Yarrow. Swordspeaker. Last of the oath-bound. Your hand has been shaking since the stars went out. Don't insult us both by hiding it behind your back."

Edmund, who had indeed been hiding it behind her back, brought it forward with as much dignity as she could salvage, which was not much. "You know what I am."

"I helped write the terms that bound you." The ghost's voice didn't soften. "When you were six years old and your name was worth more than your life, some of us argued against the sale. We lost the argument. I built the wards anyway, on the strength of a bargain I hated, because the alternative was the Hollow walking through the border unopposed." She paused, and the water of her face trembled. "And then a man named Corvane came to me and told me the wards needed one final adjustment, and I was tired, and I trusted him, and I signed the order that let him hollow them out. So when I ask you for forgiveness, swordspeaker, understand that I am asking the one person in the world with the least of it to give."

The silence rang like a struck bell.

Edmund looked at the ghost of Serevan Hale and felt the strange vertigo of hearing her own betrayal described from the other end of the telescope. *He sold my name,* she had thought, in the guild archives, cold to the bone. *He spent me like coin.* And here was the mirror of it: *he used my craft, he spent my life's work, he made my hands the instrument of the ruin.* Corvane had a talent, Edmund reflected, for making everyone his counterparty and no one his partner. Even now, even dead, even dissolved into remembering water, the warden stood in the wreckage of his signature.

"Why are you telling us this?" Lena asked. Her voice had recovered some of its edge, though her eyes hadn't. "Why now?"

"Because you've come for the second piece," the ghost said, "and the second piece cannot be taken. It can only be *released*. And release requires forgiveness — freely given, honestly meant, from someone with standing to give it." She spread her water hands. "I guarded this seal for forty years on the chance that someone would come whom I could ask. Not command. Ask. Do you understand the difference? Corvane never did."

"And if we refuse?" Edmund asked.

"Then the piece stays, and you swim home, and the Hollow finishes eating the sky, and I will still be here, still asking, until the water forgets even me." The ghost shrugged, a small human gesture absurdly at odds with her vastness. "I am not threatening you. Threats are Corvane's currency. I'm making a request. It is the first one I have been able to make in forty years, and I find that my hands—" she looked at them, streaming— "are shaking. So. Warden to whoever comes next. Girl whose face I wore into the wards. Swordspeaker whose name I failed to protect. Will you forgive me? Not for everything. I know what I cost you both, and 'everything' is not forgivable in an afternoon. For the signing. For the tiredness. For trusting the wrong man with the only thing I had."

Lena made a sound that wasn't quite a word.

Edmund watched her struggle with it — watched the war move across her face, the wanting and the anger sharing one body, the daughter in her reaching for the mother-shaped thing and the survivor in her holding the reach back by the wrist. This was the trap of ghosts, Edmund thought. They asked for absolution precisely because they could not be punched.

"I can't," Lena said finally, and her voice broke on it, and she said it again, straighter. "I can't forgive you. I don't — I don't know enough yet. You died before I could know you. Everything I feel about you is secondhand, and forgiving someone secondhand isn't forgiveness, it's theater." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, furiously, as though tears were a personal failing. "But I can *pity* you. And I can believe you're sorry. And I can — I can carry it. The not-forgiving. Until I know more. Is that — is that enough? For the seal?"

The ghost was quiet a long moment. The lamplight flickered.

"No one has ever answered me honestly before," Serevan Hale said at last, and there was wonder in the cave-water voice. "The others wanted the piece. They said yes, yes, of course, forgiven, forgiven, and the seal stayed shut, because the shrine weighs words and theirs were hollow." She turned to Edmund. "And you?"

Edmund thought about her name, sold at six years old. About the ledgers, and the trembling hand, and the guildmaster's smile. About how easy it would be to say *yes*, to spend a cheap forgiveness and take the prize, and how the shrine would weigh it and find it wanting, and how she would deserve that finding.

"I forgive you for being tired," she said slowly. "I forgive you for trusting him, because I trusted him too, for twenty years, and I know exactly how good he is at being trusted. I don't forgive you for the wards yet, because I don't yet know everything they were for, and I've learned not to sign documents I haven't read." She met the ghost's eyes. "Partial payment. On account. Will the shrine take it?"

For a moment nothing happened. Then the ghost laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, brief and bright and terribly human — and the whole shrine shivered with it, lamplight dancing on black water.

"Oh, *well done*," Serevan Hale said. "On account. Forty years I've waited, and the honest answer is partial payment on account." She raised one streaming hand, and the small glowing thing on the altar rose with it, drifting through the air toward them, shedding light like a lantern trailing embers. "Yes, swordspeaker. The shrine takes it. Honest debts are the only kind it recognizes." The piece came to rest in Edmund's cupped palms — warm, impossibly light, humming with a frequency she felt in her back teeth. Through its glow she could see, faintly, a pattern: half of a design, a circuit, a promise drawn in fire with a missing middle. "Take it to the third. And take my message to my daughter, when she finds herself — tell her the wards weren't broken from outside. Tell her the flaw was mine, and the mending is hers by right, if she chooses it. Tell her—"

The ghost flickered. Stabilized. Flickered again, and Edmund saw with a lurch that she was coming apart, spending herself, forty years of careful keeping poured out in a single conversation.

"—tell her I kept the lamp lit," the warden finished, softer now, thinning at the edges like breath on glass. "All these years. However dark it got. I kept the lamp lit."

"Mama—" Lena reached out, and her hand passed through the water-shape, and closed on nothing, and Edmund watched her learn in one heartbeat the exact weight of the word *ghost*.

The last of Serevan Hale smiled — Lena's smile, worn briefly by the water — and said, in a voice already mostly echo:

"She'll need to carry fire where fire won't go. Remember that. Remember—"

And then the shrine was empty except for the two of them, the lamplight, and the piece of the covenant warming Edmund's hands like a sleeping bird.

* * *

They floated in the black pool for a while afterward, neither willing to be the first to speak. Above them, through the shaft of the entry, the daylight was going amber. Below them, the white tiles gleamed, swept clean by forty years of faithful water.

"She said *my daughter*," Lena said at last, to the ceiling. "She looked at me and she knew. Ghosts aren't supposed to know things. That's the whole point of ghosts."

"This one kept books," Edmund said. "Of a fashion. Wardens keep records the way guilds do. Maybe the shrine let her read hers."

"A flaw that was hers, and a mending that's mine by right." Lena turned the words over like coins she suspected of being clipped. "What mending? What flaw? Edmund, what did she mean?"

"I don't know," Edmund said, which was true, and then, because she was trying — genuinely, newly trying — to stop managing the people she loved by omission: "But I think we're going to find out sooner than either of us would like. Vessith called you something, back in the caldera. Before she named me. Did you hear it?"

Lena frowned. "She didn't name me. She barely looked at me."

"She looked at you exactly long enough." Edmund turned the covenant-piece over in her fingers, watching its half-pattern spin, the missing middle turning toward her and away like a door deciding. "*The vessel,*" she quoted quietly. "She said it under her breath, like a prayer or a warning. *At last, the vessel comes.*"

The water around them had gone very still — stiller than still, the stillness of a held breath — and somewhere far above, faint as a memory, the first star in weeks pricked through the amber dusk, guttered, and went out again.

Lena stared up at the place where it had been.

"Well," she said, in the voice she used for stealing bread and walking into the Hollow's shadow and every other impossible thing she had ever decided to do, "then we'd better go find out what I'm a vessel *for*."
