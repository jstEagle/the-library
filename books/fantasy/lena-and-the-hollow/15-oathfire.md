## 15. Oathfire

The caldera had a smell to it, once you got past the ash and the sulfur and the general atmosphere of *the world is ending*. It smelled, faintly and absurdly, of bread.

"Tell me I'm not imagining that," I said.

Edmund didn't look up from the ledger she'd balanced on her knee. She'd been keeping notes since we arrived — of course she had; the woman catalogued her own sneezes — but her pen had slowed over the last hour, and now it stopped entirely. "You're not imagining it."

"Bread." I crouched at the rim of the ember-pit and peered down into the glow where Vessith lay coiled like a dying hearth-fire given a very long body and a great deal of regret. "The last dragon in the world smells like a bakery."

"She's burning her memories," Edmund said quietly. "To keep the covenant's flame alive. Memories burn differently than wood or coal. They have a scent. Hers must be... domestic."

I thought about that. A dragon, alone in a hole in the world for decades, feeding herself piece by piece into a fire so that two realms wouldn't come apart at the seams — and what she chose to feed it first was the memory of someone baking bread for her. Maybe a child. Maybe a village that had long ago stopped believing in her.

"Well," I said, because someone had to say something and Edmund had gone into one of her silences, the kind that meant her feelings were too large for her precision, "at least we know what to get her for a retirement gift."

Edmund made a sound that was almost a laugh. Almost. It caught somewhere in the middle and came out as an exhale. She'd been doing that a lot since the drowned shrine. Since her mother — since *Lena's* mother, which was still a sentence my brain kept tripping over like a loose floorboard — had asked us for forgiveness neither of us knew how to give.

We'd been walking for three days after that. Not talking about it. Talking about everything else instead: rations, routes, whether oath-hounds could swim (they couldn't, thank every merciful god), and whether Edmund's new habit of wincing when she flexed her right hand was anything worth mentioning.

It was worth mentioning. I'd decided not to mention it yet. We all carry our own ledgers.

* * *

Vessith spoke around dusk.

Not with sound, exactly. Dragons don't bother with sound when they can put words directly into the space behind your eyes, which is a deeply unpleasant experience the first time and only slightly less unpleasant the fourth. The words arrived like frost forming on glass: slow, deliberate, beautiful, and cold enough to make you aware of your own warmth.

***The wardless girl.***

I looked down into the pit. One vast eye had opened, amber shot through with veins of grey, like a candle guttering behind stained glass.

"That's me," I said. "Though I prefer 'Lena.' Or 'hey, you.' I'm flexible."

***You are the vessel.***

"I'm sorry, the what?"

Edmund stood up so fast her ledger slid off her knee and into the ash. She didn't pick it up. That's how I knew this was serious. Edmund Yarrow did not abandon paperwork.

"Vessith," she said carefully, "what do you mean by—"

***The covenant was never sealed with objects alone.*** The dragon's voice moved through me like water through a reed. ***Objects hold the terms. But fire needs a carrier. Someone who can walk where wards fail, where magic cannot go, and bring the flame through untouched. Every realm has such a soul born once in an age. The warden who built your border wards knew. She built them around her own daughter's absence, a shape left deliberately empty, so that when the time came—***

"Stop," I said.

***—the vessel would be ready.***

"I said stop."

The eye blinked. Slowly. The frost-voice softened, if frost can soften.

***You did not know.***

"No, I didn't know." My voice came out smaller than I wanted it to. "Because I sold my memories to survive, remember? Because whoever was supposed to tell me what I was — my mother, maybe, or the whole guild, or anyone — decided it was easier to let a child trade away her own past than explain it to her. So no. I didn't know. Imagine my surprise."

The silence that followed had weight. Even Edmund didn't fill it, and Edmund could fill any silence with footnotes.

It was Vessith who broke it, gently, the way you'd set down something fragile.

***I am sorry, little vessel. I forget how short your lives are, and how much of them is spent learning things too late.***

"Yeah, well." I wiped my face with my sleeve and pretended it was just ash. "Welcome to being human. It's mostly that."

* * *

That night we camped at the caldera's edge, under a sky that still refused to hold stars. Edmund built a fire the way she did everything: precisely, economically, with the air of someone who had once been graded on it. Then she sat across from me and looked at me with that expression I'd learned to dread, the one that meant she was composing sentences before saying them, editing as she went, and would arrive at something careful and correct and completely wrong.

"Don't," I said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're winding up. I can see the wind-up."

She exhaled through her nose. "Lena. What Vessith described — carrying the covenant's fire through the Hollow itself — the Hollow eats magic. It eats names, oaths, wards, everything woven. You're the only thing it doesn't see. Which means you're the only thing that can deliver the flame. And it also means—"

"It means I walk into the thing that unmade the border wards holding the only fire left in the world, and if I stumble, everything ends." I poked the campfire with a stick. "Yes, Edmund. I can do arithmetic."

"There's more." Her jaw tightened. "Carrying isn't passive. Vessith's fire will live inside you. Burn inside you. For however long the journey takes. And you're human. You're—" She stopped. Started again. "There may not be a *you* at the end of it. The vessel might survive as a vessel and nothing else. An empty shape that walked where it needed to walk."

And there it was. The careful, correct, completely wrong speech, delivered exactly on schedule.

I let the silence sit for a moment, because I wanted her to feel it. Then I said, "Say the rest."

"Lena—"

"The rest. The part where you offer to do it yourself. The part where you've already spent the last three days drafting a plan where you somehow carry the fire despite being, and I quote Vessith here, 'woven through with oaths like a hedge through a field,' and the Hollow would eat you before you took your second step. Say that part out loud so I can watch you hear how it sounds."

Her mouth opened. Closed. She was, I think, genuinely surprised that I'd seen it. Edmund always assumed her plans were watertight. Edmund forgot that I had spent my whole life reading people, because when you have no name and no history, other people's faces are the only book in the house.

"I've survived worse," she said finally.

"You've survived worse *because people loved you enough to lie to you about the cost.*" I threw the stick into the fire. "Your guildmaster sold your name and called it salvation. Your mother — our mothers, apparently, this family tree is a disaster — hid the truth and called it protection. Everyone who ever tried to keep you safe did it by deciding you didn't get a vote. And now you're sitting there doing the exact same thing to me, and you can't even see it, because to you it's not betrayal, it's *duty.*"

"That's not fair."

"No," I agreed. "It's not. Nothing about any of this is fair. The stars went out. The world is coming apart. A dragon smells like bread. Fairness left the building months ago." I leaned forward. "But here's what *is* true, Edmund Yarrow, last oath-bound swordspeaker, keeper of impossible ledgers: I am the vessel. Me. Not you. Not Corvane. Not some guild acolyte with better handwriting. The warden built the wards around my absence. The Hollow walks past me like I'm furniture. Whatever the covenant needs, it needs *me* to do it, and every day you spend planning around that fact is a day closer to the realms tearing open while you alphabetize your regrets."

She stared at me. In the firelight her eyes were very bright, and I realized with a small, unwelcome jolt that she wasn't angry.

She was frightened.

Edmund Yarrow, who had spoken her sword awake against Corvane's agents, who had faced down a ghost in a drowned shrine, who had fled oath-hounds along the Hollow Road without once letting me see her hands shake — she was frightened, and it was for me, and she had absolutely no idea what to do with that.

"Oh," I said softly. "Oh, you absolute idiot."

"That's uncalled for."

"It's entirely called for. You love me." I said it plainly, no ceremony, the way you'd note the weather. "And you've never once been loved badly enough to learn that protecting someone and erasing them look identical from the outside. So you're going to have to take my word for it."

* * *

We sat with that for a long time. The fire crackled. Somewhere below us, Vessith breathed, a sound like the tide deciding whether to come in.

It was Edmund who finally spoke, and to her credit, she didn't argue. She negotiated. That was the guild in her, and I found, to my surprise, that I didn't mind it. Negotiation was a kind of respect. It meant she'd accepted I had standing.

"Terms," she said hoarsely. "If you carry the fire, then I set terms. You train with Vessith before we march. Every day. You learn to hold the flame, not just endure it. And if — *if* — the burning starts to take you, if you feel yourself going empty, you tell me. Immediately. No heroics. No quiet suffering. You tell me, and we find another way, even if there isn't one. Those are my conditions."

"And if I refuse your conditions?"

"Then I'll follow you into the Hollow anyway and be eaten beside you, and you'll have to live with that."

"That's blackmail."

"That's *guild practice.*" The ghost of a smile crossed her face. "I told you. I know how trades work."

I laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the shrine, since my mother's ghost, since the sky went dark — and the laugh cracked something open in my chest that had been sealed a long time. Grief and purpose and terror, all of it sloshing around in there, and underneath it all, steady as a banked coal: *yes.*

"Yes," I said.

Edmund blinked. "Yes to the terms?"

"Yes to all of it. Yes to carrying the fire. Yes to your fussy little conditions. Yes." I stood up, because some things you shouldn't say sitting down. "Before you can object. Before you can draft a counteroffer. Before you can open a ledger and start itemizing the risks. The answer is yes, Edmund, and it was yes the moment Vessith said 'vessel,' and the only reason I didn't say it then is that I wanted to watch you try to plan your way out of it first."

"That was cruel."

"It was *educational.*"

Below us, the great eye opened again, and the frost-voice rolled up out of the caldera, and I swear on whatever name I'll eventually recover that the old dragon sounded amused.

***The vessel consents.***

"The vessel prefers 'Lena,'" I called down. "And the vessel wants it noted for the record that she consented *first*, before any swordspeakers could interfere."

***Noted,*** Vessith said, and there was warmth in it now, real warmth, the kind that comes off a forge rather than off ice. ***Then come down at dawn, little Lena, and we will begin. Fire-keeping is not learned in a day. It is learned the way grief is learned: slowly, unwillingly, and forever.***

"Wonderful," Edmund muttered beside me. "Even the dragon does foreshadowing."

* * *

I couldn't sleep. Big surprise.

I climbed out of my bedroll and walked the caldera rim under the starless sky, and I let myself feel it, all of it, properly, for the first time. My mother had built the wards. My mother had built them around me. My mother's ghost had asked forgiveness and I had turned away, and now I was going to walk into the wound her murderer's bargain had torn in the world, carrying fire in my chest, and there was a decent chance I wouldn't come back as anyone at all.

And do you know what I felt, underneath the fear?

*Ready.*

Not brave. Brave is a word for ballads. Ready is a word for people who have nothing left to lose except each other, and I had Edmund, and Edmund had me, and that was a ledger entry no guildmaster could touch.

I was halfway back to camp when I saw the light.

Not firelight. This was silver, cold, moving low along the far ridge — moving in a line, spaced evenly, like lanterns carried by marching men. A lot of marching men. Coming up the Hollow Road behind us, following the trail we'd spent three days scrubbing from the mud.

And ahead of them, fainter, higher, on the ridge beyond the caldera — a second light. Red-gold. Wrong. Moving with the particular patience of something that had been hunting us since the market city and had finally, finally caught the scent.

Oath-hounds didn't travel in packs that size.

Corvane hadn't sent hounds.

He'd sent an army.

I ran back to camp, and Edmund was already awake, already armed, already watching the ridgeline with the expression of a woman recalculating everything.

"They found us," I said.

"No," Edmund said quietly, drawing her sword, and the blade woke with a whisper of syllables. "They found *her.*"
