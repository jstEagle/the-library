## 16. The Third Piece Is a Person

The drowned shrine had left its mark on all of us.

Vessith smelled of brine and old smoke for two days, and complained about it the way only an ancient dragon can complain — in long, rolling sentences that circled back on themselves like weather. Lena wrung out her boots by the fire and said nothing about what her mother's ghost had asked of her, which was its own kind of loudness. And I sat apart with the second covenant-piece cupped in my palms like a bird with a broken wing, and I did not sleep.

The piece was small. That was the first insult of it. After a drowned shrine and a ghost and a crossing of the Starless Sea that I would pay for in nightmares for a decade, the thing itself was no bigger than a coin — a disc of pale metal, warm as a held hand, with a groove worn into it as if someone had worried at it with a thumb for years. Which, I supposed, someone had. The warden's ghost had worn it smooth before she'd given it up, and before that she'd worn it smooth keeping it, and before that —

Before that, it had been part of a whole.

I set it beside the first piece on the flat stone between us, where the firelight could reach them both. They leaned toward each other. Not metaphorically — actually leaned, the way iron filings lean toward a lodestone, humming faintly against the rock until I nudged them apart again.

"Stop that," I told them. "You'll wake the dragon."

"Too late," said Vessith, from the dark beyond the fire. "Everything wakes me. I am very old and mostly regret."

Lena laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered, the way she still laughed sometimes, as if joy were something she'd stolen from a kitchen and might be made to give it back. She'd been doing that less lately. I kept count anyway. It was the sort of ledger I kept without meaning to.

"Read it again," she said.

"I've read it four times."

"Read it a fifth. Maybe it'll be different."

It would not be different. But I took out the scrap of vellum I'd copied from the shrine wall — my hand steady enough now, though the oath-marks along my wrist ached in damp weather like an old soldier's scars — and I read it aloud into the night, because she asked, and because some part of me hoped she was right.

*Three are the pieces of the bond: the word that was spoken, the fire that was given, and the promise that is kept. The first may be found. The second may be carried. The third must be renewed, freely, by the one who swore it, or the bond remains broken and the Hollow feeds.*

"There," Lena said, when I finished. "That's not so bad."

"It's terrible," I said. "It's the worst sentence ever chiseled into wet stone by a woman standing in rising seawater."

"You're being dramatic."

"I am a swordspeaker. Drama is load-bearing."

But we both knew which piece was which by then. The word that was spoken — the first piece, pried out of the Market of Unmade Things at a price I hadn't told Lena about. The fire that was given — Vessith, guttering but alive, the last ember of the covenant's forge. And the promise that is kept.

Freely renewed. By the one who swore it.

There was exactly one person alive who had sworn the covenant-oath. There was exactly one oath she had ever sworn that mattered. And there was exactly one problem with renewing it, and the problem was written in the same stone, three lines down, in letters the ghost-warden had cut deep enough to bleed shadow:

*A promise renewed is a promise remade. What is remade is unmade first. The swearer gives what the oath was built from.*

And my oath — the original one, the one Corvane had sold my name to seal, the one I'd been carrying in my sword-hand since I was nine years old — was built from my swordspeech. From every word I would ever speak awake to steel. Possibly, if the old formulas held true, from everything else besides.

I folded the vellum away and smiled at Lena across the fire, because I was a coward in precisely one direction, and it was toward her.

* * *

We spent the next day doing nothing, which is harder work than it sounds.

The caldera where Vessith lay was sheltered, almost gentle — a bowl of black glass and grey ash where hardy white flowers grew out of cracks that should have killed them. Lena picked a handful and put them in a tin cup on the stone between the covenant-pieces, "so they have something nice to look at," and I did not tell her that the pieces didn't have eyes, because it was a kind thing and the world was short of those.

Vessith slept in fits. When she woke, she woke all at once, like a house falling over, and talked.

"You've worked it out," she said, on the second evening, while Lena was down at the spring. Her voice came out slow, each word banked like coals. "You have the look of a person who has done arithmetic they hate."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Swordspeaker. Ledger-keeper. You have always known what things cost. It is your whole religion." Her eye, vast and amber and half-lidded, fixed on me with the patience of geology. "So cost it out. Say it in trade-tongue, if that helps. What does renewal run?"

I looked at my hands. The oath-marks ran from knuckle to elbow on the right, fine silver script that had been fading for years — every use of swordspeech burning a little more of me, a little faster, a tax I'd been hiding from Lena since the Market. The marks were thinner now. Paler. Like writing on paper held too close to a flame.

"The original oath," I said, "was sworn with my name and my speech together. Two halves of one binding. Renewal requires both, freely given. The name comes back to me when the covenant reforges — that part's clean. But the speech—" I stopped. Started again, in ledger-language, because she was right, it helped. "The speech doesn't come back. It goes into the bond. Permanently. It becomes the mortar."

"And the rest of you?"

"The rest of me is collateral." I heard how it sounded and hated it. "Possibly. The texts disagree. Some say the swearer survives diminished. Some say—"

"Some say ash," Vessith said. "Yes. I remember the ones who wrote those texts. I lit their fires." She shifted, and the ground shivered, and somewhere below us something in the caldera groaned like a ship in a storm. "I will tell you what none of the texts say, little keeper, because I was there at the swearing and you were not. The warden who wrote that stone — the girl's mother — she offered herself first. Did you know that?"

My head came up. "She said—"

"She said *forgive me*, and you heard it wrong, both of you. She wasn't asking forgiveness for building the wards. She was asking forgiveness for what she was about to do, which was this: she went to the Hollow's edge meaning to renew the promise herself, and the promise refused her. Because it wasn't hers anymore. She'd already given her part. A promise can only be renewed by the one who owes it." The great eye closed, opened. "You owe it, Edmund Yarrow. No one else on any world can pay. That is not duty. That is simply arithmetic."

"Then the arithmetic is wrong."

"Arithmetic is never wrong. That's what makes it cruel." Vessith lowered her head until her breath stirred my hair, warm and smelling of deep places. "But hear an old fire before it gutters: there is a difference between a debt and a gift, even when the sum is identical. The covenant does not want your death. It wants your *yes*. What grows from a yes is never only ash. Remember that, when the hour comes. And do not—" the eye narrowed, ancient and unexpectedly sly, "—do not lie to the wardless girl. She counts better than you think."

* * *

I meant to tell her. I want that written down somewhere, in a ledger, in my own hand: *she meant to tell her.*

Instead, that night, Lena climbed up onto the ledge where I kept watch — she always found me on watch, no matter how I arranged the rotation; I suspected bribery of the rotation, possibly of me — and sat down close enough that our shoulders touched, and said, "Show me your arm."

The fire crackled. Somewhere below, Vessith snored like a landslide rehearsing.

"My arm," I said.

"Your arm, Edmund."

"It's cold out. Arms should stay covered. Guild regulation."

"We're not in the guild."

"All the more reason for discipline." I was aware, distantly, that I was babbling, and that babbling was a confession in itself, and I babbled on regardless, because the alternative was her face when she understood. "There's a whole chapter in the ledgers about sleeve integrity. Very moving. There's a footnote—"

"Lena," I said instead, because she had taken my wrist gently, the way you take a hurt bird, and turned it to the firelight, and there was nothing left to hide behind. The marks ran thin as spider silk now. In two places they had gone out entirely, leaving plain skin, ordinary skin, the skin of a woman who could not speak a sword awake to save anything.

Lena was quiet for a long time. She traced one fading line with her fingertip, not reading it so much as listening to it.

"How long?" she said.

"Since the Market."

"Edmund."

"It bought us the first piece. It was a fair trade. I've made worse."

"That's not—" She stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice was low and very controlled, which anyone who knew her would recognize as the most dangerous weather she owned. "Every time you use it, it burns you. And you let me walk next to you and plan and hope, and you didn't say."

"I'm saying now."

"You're saying now because I caught you."

"In fairness, you catch everyone. It's your defining trait. Wardless, unstoppable, catches liars—"

"Don't." Just the one word, quiet, and I stopped. She still held my wrist. Her thumb rested over the place where the oldest mark, the first word of my first oath, had burned almost to nothing. "How much is left? Truly. Ledger-true."

I could have lied. Gods know I tried to find the shape of a lie that would fit, and there wasn't one, because she was looking at me and she had walked through wards that couldn't see her and through a sea that showed her her worst emptiness, and there was no version of me she hadn't already stood inside of.

"After tonight's accounting," I said, "enough for one speaking. Perhaps two, if I'm careful, and I'm never careful."

"And the renewal. The third piece." Her eyes came up to mine. Of course she'd done the reading. Of course she'd done the arithmetic; she'd grown up trading memories to survive, she knew the price of everything worth having. "It takes the speech. And maybe more."

"Yes."

"No."

"Lena—"

"*No.*" She said it the way she'd once said yes to carrying dragon-fire — instantly, totally, before I could object, which was apparently a family resemblance between her courage and her stubbornness. "You don't get to spend yourself alone. That's not how this works. We found the pieces *together*. We crossed the sea *together*. Whatever the third piece costs, we—"

"It isn't yours to share." I said it as gently as I could, which was not gently enough, and watched it land anyway. "The promise is mine. Only mine. The stone says so, Vessith says so, the whole rotten architecture of the covenant says so. If I could split it with you I would carve it in half with my teeth. But it's mine, and I choose to pay it, and choosing is the entire point. Freely, Lena. It has to be free."

"Free," she repeated. Her hand had gone still on my wrist. "Like I was free when I traded my memory of home? Like my mother was free when Corvane sabotaged her wards? Everyone in this story keeps being *free* to lose things, and I am so tired—" Her voice cracked, and she hated that it cracked, and pressed on through it, which is the bravest thing I have ever watched anyone do, and I have watched dragons die. "I just got you. Do you understand? I traded away everyone I ever belonged to and I don't even remember their faces, and then there was you, dropping bread in the kitchen, glaring at me like I was a ledger error, and I thought — *there*. That's the thing I couldn't remember. That's what home felt like. And now you're planning to die politely and leave me a garden."

"I hadn't gotten as far as the garden."

"Well, plan further! Plan *staying!*"

The fire popped. Below us, Vessith's snoring hitched — awake, then, and pretending otherwise, giving us what privacy a mountain-sized creature can pretend to give.

I turned my hand under hers until we weren't inspector-and-evidence but palm to palm. Her fingers were cold. Mine were shaking, and the oath-marks ached, and none of it mattered even slightly.

"I don't want to leave you," I said. "I want you to know that first, before anything else, ahead of duty and prophecy and every line of wet stone. I don't want to go. I have wanted approximately nothing my whole life except to keep my ledgers balanced and my oaths intact, and then you stole bread in front of me and ruined everything, and now what I want is enormous and specific and completely impossible to enter in a book." My throat tightened. "But the stars are still out, Lena. The Hollow is still eating the space between worlds, and it eats outward, and eventually it reaches whatever garden we plant. The promise is mine. The yes has to be mine. All I can ask—"

"Don't ask me to be okay with it."

"—all I can ask is that you let me say it properly. Once. At the edge, where it counts. Let me keep the last thing I have the way I've kept everything: on purpose."

She looked at our hands for a long moment. When she raised her face, her eyes were wet and furious and more beautiful than anything the stars had ever managed, back when they bothered to show up.

"Fine," she said. "Say it properly. At the edge. On purpose." Her grip tightened, sudden and fierce. "But hear me, Edmund Yarrow, last of the swordspeakers, worst liar in two realms: I am coming to the edge. I am the vessel, I carry the fire, the covenant needs me too — your own precious stone says so. So whatever you're planning, plan around me. Because I have lost a mother to this and a childhood to this and I will not lose you to it *quietly*, and if you try to slip away alone in the night like some tragic hero in a cheap novel, I will find you. I always find you. It's my defining trait."

Despite everything — despite the fading marks and the arithmetic and the ash waiting at the end of the road — I laughed. It came out ragged, half sob, entirely true.

"Noted," I said. "Ledgered. Witnessed by dragon."

From below, without opening her eye, Vessith rumbled: "*Witnessed.*"

Lena stayed on the ledge until my watch ended, shoulder against shoulder, neither of us sleeping, both of us pretending. And it was near dawn, when the sky did its starless grey trick of almost looking normal, that I saw it — far off across the ash flats, at the caldera's rim, a line of lights. Torches. Many torches, moving in formation, disciplined as a ledger column.

Guild torches. Marching not away from the Hollow, but toward it.

And at their head, riding a grey horse that had no business surviving the ash road, a figure I knew by his silhouette alone, the way you know a scar.

Corvane had found us. Or perhaps — and this thought arrived cold and unwelcome, wearing the guildmaster's patient smile — he had simply followed the same arithmetic we had, all the way to its final entry.

Lena saw them a heartbeat after I did. Her hand found mine in the dark and gripped it hard.

"He's early," she said.

"No," I said, watching the torches descend toward us through the ash-fall, and feeling my last fading oath-mark begin, very softly, to sing. "He's right on time. He always is. That's what makes him dangerous."
