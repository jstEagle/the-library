## 17. The Argument at the Edge

### Edmund

Edmund had been planning to leave alone since the moment she'd understood what the third piece was.

She'd done the arithmetic the way she did all her arithmetic — carefully, twice, in a ledger no one else would ever read. The covenant wanted a kept promise, freely renewed. Her promise. Her name, sold when she was six years old and too small to know what was being signed away in her stead. To renew it meant standing before the Hollow's wound and speaking herself whole again, and everything she had learned about swordspeech said that speaking whole meant being spent. Every use of the gift had burned years off her life like candle-wax; this last one would burn the candle entire.

So: she would go alone. It was simple bookkeeping. One debt, one debtor, one payment. Lena didn't appear anywhere on the ledger.

The problem with ledgers, Edmund thought grimly, watching Lena across the fire they'd built in the lee of Vessith's caldera, was that other people kept refusing to stay out of them.

Lena sat cross-legged on a flat stone, turning the second covenant-piece over in her fingers — a shard of pale glass that hummed faintly, like a struck bell deciding whether to ring. She'd been quiet for an hour, which from Lena was never a good sign. Quiet Lena was calculating Lena. Quiet Lena was about to be trouble.

"You're packing," Lena said, without looking up.

"I'm organizing."

"You're packing *at night*. You've sharpened both blades. You gave me your heavier cloak." She looked up then, and her eyes were the gray of a sky deciding whether to rain. "You're leaving without me."

Edmund reached for the honest answer and found, annoyingly, that she'd left it somewhere behind several layers of protective lies. "I'm going ahead," she tried. "To scout."

"To scout the wound in the world. Alone. With two swords and a death wish you haven't told me about."

"Lena—"

"Don't." Lena stood, and the shard caught the firelight and threw it back in little stars. "Don't do the voice. Don't do the thing where you explain it so reasonably that I feel foolish for objecting. I've watched you do it to guild clerks for months. I'm not a guild clerk."

"No," Edmund agreed. "You're worse. Guild clerks give up."

It was the wrong thing to say, and she knew it the moment it left her mouth, which was unfortunately always the moment such things left her mouth.

* * *

### Lena

Lena had been stolen from once already — her memory of home, traded away by a child who hadn't known better, which was to say by herself. She knew precisely what it felt like to have someone else decide what you could keep.

She was not going to stand in the ashes of a caldera and let Edmund Yarrow decide it for her too.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever the arithmetic is. Say it out loud where I can hear it."

"There's nothing to say."

"Then say *that*, and we'll both know you're lying." She stepped closer, close enough to see the oath-marks on Edmund's hand, the fine silver script that had been dimming all month, each use of the swordspeech burning it paler. Lena had been counting those too. She counted everything now. It was what you did when you had no memories of your own — you memorized the present instead, hoarded it, refused to lose a single hour of it.

And Edmund planned to make herself into an hour Lena couldn't keep.

"The third piece is a person," Lena said quietly. "Vessith told us. A kept promise, freely renewed. And there's only one promise old enough, isn't there. Only one name in the covenant." She watched Edmund's jaw tighten. "You're going to renew it. You're going to walk into the Hollow and speak yourself whole, and you're going to be spent, and you're going to call it scouting."

"It might not kill me."

"*Might.*" Lena laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "That's your offer? *Might?* You hid the blood price from me for three chapters of our lives, Edmund. Every time you spoke the sword awake, you paid in years, and you smiled at me after so I wouldn't count them. I counted anyway. I always count."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, in the dark throat of the caldera, Vessith's ember-glow pulsed slow as a sleeping heart.

"You can't come," Edmund said at last, and her voice had gone low and careful, the guildmaster's voice, the negotiating voice. "The covenant needs a vessel. It needs someone who can carry dragon-fire where wards fail. That's you, Lena. That has always been you. If you're beside me when I renew the oath, and something goes wrong—"

"Then I'll be beside you when something goes wrong! That's what beside means!"

"—and the fire dies with you, and the stars stay dead, and the Hollow eats everything including every person who ever trusted us—" Edmund stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again it was barely above the wind. "I am not losing you to my own mistake. I've made enough of those. I sold nothing, I signed nothing, and still — everyone who stands near me ends up in the ledger. Corvane saw to that. I won't add you."

"That's not your choice."

"It's entirely my choice. It's the only thing in this whole wretched affair that is."

"No." Lena's hands were shaking, and she let them shake. "It's mine. My feet. My road. My mother built those wards, Edmund — she built them and Corvane broke them, and I have spent my whole life walking through places magic can't see because some piece of her work is still written into me. The Hollow took my home from my memory. It does not get to take my ending from my story too."

* * *

### Edmund

There it was. The thing Edmund had been dreading, laid out plain between them like a blade on a merchant's table.

Because Lena was right. That was the unbearable part. Edmund had spent thirty years believing that duty meant carrying things alone — that the whole point of an oath-bound swordspeaker was to be the one who stepped forward so others didn't have to. It was a beautiful belief. It had kept her warm through decades of cold ledgers and colder nights.

It was also, she was beginning to suspect, just loneliness wearing armor.

"If you come," Edmund said slowly, "and I fall—"

"You won't fall."

"If I fall, you'll be standing in the Hollow with a piece of glass and a dead woman's fire, and no one to carry you out. Have you thought of that? Have you done *that* arithmetic?"

"Yes," Lena said simply. "I did it while you were pretending to organize your pack. And here's mine: if you go alone and you fall, I spend the rest of my life knowing I let you. Knowing I was *protected* out of it. I've already lost everything once, Edmund. I got it back in pieces, from strangers, in a market of unmade things. Do you understand what that cost? I will not be made into a memory again. Not even a beloved one. Especially not a beloved one."

The word landed like a dropped coin ringing on stone.

*Beloved.*

Neither of them moved. The fire crackled through the silence, shameless.

"That's the first time you've said it," Edmund managed.

"You've been thinking it loudly enough for both of us for weeks. Someone had to break the tie." But Lena's voice had gone unsteady, and her eyes were bright, and furious, and wet. "Don't you dare use it against me. Don't you dare take my love and file it under *reasons to leave me behind*. If you love me, then love me out loud, in front of me, where I can argue with it."

* * *

### Edmund

Edmund Yarrow had faced oath-hounds without flinching. She had spoken her sword awake against Corvane's agents with years of her life streaming off her like smoke, and she had not screamed. She had stood in a drowned shrine while a ghost asked forgiveness neither of them could give, and she had held her ground.

She could not hold it now.

"Every plan I have," she said, and her voice betrayed her, cracking straight down its center like river ice, "every single one ends with me alone at the edge. It's the only shape I know how to draw. I've been drawing it since I was six years old. They took my name and I learned to make do without one, and making do became a habit, and the habit became a life, and now there's you, standing in the middle of it, refusing to fit the shape, and I don't—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I don't know how to do this. I know how to die properly. I don't know how to live with someone watching."

"Then learn," Lena said. "We'll be terrible at it together. I have no memories and you have no practice. We'll be the worst-kept couple in two realms. It'll be legendary."

A laugh escaped Edmund — ragged, unwilling, real. "That's not how oaths work."

"I'm wardless, Edmund. Nothing works on me the way it works on anyone else. Why should love?"

And that — that absurd, impossible, perfectly Lena sentence — undid whatever last knot Edmund had been holding the whole structure together with. She crossed the space between them in three steps, took Lena's face in her oath-marked hands, and kissed her.

It was not graceful. Edmund's nose bumped Lena's cheek; Lena made a small startled sound that turned into a laugh halfway through; the covenant-piece nearly got dropped and had to be rescued by reflex more than dignity. It was clumsy and desperate and honest, which was more than either of them could say for anything else in their lives.

When they broke apart, Lena rested her forehead against Edmund's collarbone and said, slightly breathless, "For the record, that changes nothing about my argument."

"For the record," Edmund said into her hair, "I know."

"So we're agreed. We go together. You renew the promise, I carry the fire, and nobody gets protected out of anything."

"We're agreed on the going-together part." Edmund tipped Lena's chin up. "The rest we argue about tomorrow. Tonight I'd like to stop fighting the person I just kissed."

Lena grinned — that crooked, wardless, unbowed grin — and Edmund thought, with the clarity of a woman who has finally balanced a ledger she'd been carrying wrong for thirty years: *oh. So this is what the entries were for. All those trades. All those small miracles. This is the one they were all leading to.*

Above them, the starless sky shuddered.

Not clouds. Not weather. Far to the north, past the caldera's rim, the darkness itself seemed to flex — a slow contraction, like something vast turning over in its sleep, or waking. The air pressure changed. Vessith's ember flared bright orange, then guttered, and the dragon's voice rolled down from the heights, thin and urgent as a dying bell:

*"It knows you're coming. The Hollow has begun to move. Whatever you mean to do, children — do it soon."*

Lena's hand found Edmund's, oath-marks and all, and gripped hard.

"Together?" Lena asked.

"Together," Edmund said, and reached for her sword.
