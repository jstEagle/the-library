## 18. March on the Hollow

The wound in the world looked, from three miles off, like a hole cut into a painting.

That was how I described it to Vessith, anyway, because she had asked — asked me, a swordspeaker with no sword and a ledger-keeper's vocabulary — what it looked like. She was too large to see anything whole anymore. Her eyes were built for horizons, not for details, and so I had become, over the past two days of marching, something like her reading glasses.

"A hole," I said. "In the sky. In the ground. In between them, where the sky and ground are supposed to agree on things."

"Ah," said Vessith. "A disagreement."

"You could put it that way."

"I could put it many ways. I am old enough that most of my ways have been used up." She exhaled a thread of smoke that smelled of banked hearths and very old promises. "I prefer *disagreement*. It suggests the world might yet be talked around."

I did not tell her that I had spent my whole life believing exactly that, and that the belief had never once been tested against anything like this. There is a time for honesty and a time for morale, and a march is mostly the second thing.

Behind us — behind *me*, which still startled me every time I turned around — came perhaps four hundred people. I will not call them an army. Armies have banners and supply trains and officers who shout. We had Lena's cousin Marta, who had arrived from the Market of Unmade Things with eleven smugglers, a one-eyed cartographer named Pell who kept redrawing our route out of spite, a contingent of wardless children too young to fight but old enough to carry water and ask devastating questions, and a woman called Sister Anselm who had once been a guild archivist and now carried the third covenant-piece wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a fussy infant.

And, walking at the rear because he refused to walk anywhere else, Brannick — the oath-hound handler who had chased us down the Hollow Road a lifetime ago, back when the stars worked. He had switched sides somewhere around the Starless Sea and had never quite explained why, except once, quietly, when he thought I was asleep: *"You fed your horse before yourself. I've followed worse men for less."* I had been feeding the horse because the horse was frightened and I am a soft touch, but I let him keep his version. People need their reasons to be dignified.

Four hundred people, give or take, marching toward the place where the world had been unpicked. Grim hope, Lena called it. I had asked her what made it grim, and she'd said, "The hope part. Hope is always grim if you're paying attention." Then she'd stolen my last apple, which I felt rather undercut the philosophy.

* * *

We made camp that final night within sight of the wound, close enough that its absence pressed against us like cold glass. The stars were still gone — all of them, everywhere, a sky like the inside of a closed book — but near the Hollow there was a subtler wrongness: the dark there wasn't empty. It was *attentive*. You could feel it noticing you, the way you feel a stranger's gaze across a market square.

I sat by the fire doing what I do best, which is making lists. The Guild of Impossible Things had taught me that terror and inventory are natural companions; you cannot fear a thing properly until you have counted it.

*One dragon,* I wrote. *Guttering. Perhaps two more days of flame in her, three if we're gentle.*

*One vessel,* I wrote, and here my pen hesitated, because the vessel was asleep twenty feet away with her head on Marta's pack and her boots still on, and writing *vessel* about Lena felt like writing *inventory* about a sunrise. But the covenant needed precision, and precision was mine to give. *One vessel, willing. Carries fire where wards fail.*

*One broken promise,* I wrote. *Mine. Freely renewable. Cost: everything I am that speaks.*

I looked at that line for a long while. Around me the camp murmured — Pell arguing with a smuggler about whether the Hollow counted as navigable water (it did not, Pell insisted, because "you can't drown in a disagreement," which I thought showed real optimism), the children daring each other to look at the wound without flinching, Sister Anselm humming to the covenant-piece as though it were indeed an infant and might wake cranky.

Vessith's shadow fell across my page.

"You write small," she observed. "For someone carrying such large handwriting."

"It's a ledger habit. Small entries fit more sins per page."

"Mm." She lowered her great head, and one eye — amber, slit-pupilled, older than several nations — regarded me with terrible gentleness. "You have decided, then. About the cost."

"I decided weeks ago."

"And told her?"

"She knows what renewal costs. She read the same ledgers I did."

"That is not what I asked." Vessith settled onto her haunches with a sound like a mountain sitting down. "Edmund Yarrow. I have eaten promises for nine hundred years. Do you know how I tell a true one from a false one?"

"How?"

"The false ones are tidy. A promise made to be kept cleanly, without mess, without anyone being hurt or inconvenienced — that promise was never alive. The true ones bleed at the edges. They drag other people in. They make the promiser lie awake doing inventory." Her eye crinkled, which in a creature that size is roughly a landslide of affection. "Yours bleeds. I only wonder whether you've noticed that the bleeding isn't only yours."

I closed the ledger. "She'll hate me if I go alone."

"Yes."

"She'll hate herself if I don't come back."

"Yes."

"So either way—"

"Either way," Vessith agreed comfortably, "you have chosen the interesting option. Good. Boring dragons die of boredom, and I refuse to die of anything so pedestrian." She rose, shaking ash from her wings like a woman brushing crumbs from a shawl. "Sleep, swordspeaker. Tomorrow we go and argue with the world."

* * *

Lena found me at first light, which is to say she found me having not slept, sitting on a rock with my sword across my knees. Not that it was much of a sword anymore. It had spoken to me twice since the Market of Unmade Things — twice, and each time the words had cost me something I couldn't name and didn't dare count, because counting would make it real, and I was already lying to Lena by omission, and the lies were getting heavy.

"You look terrible," she said, dropping beside me and handing me tea. It was terrible too. We matched.

"You say the most romantic things."

"I'm saving up. When this is over I'm going to say something devastatingly romantic and you'll be completely unprepared." She blew on her tea. "Marta says the scouts report movement from the south. Lots of movement."

"Guild?"

"Guild." She said it carefully, watching my face. "Banners and everything. Corvane's brought the whole weight of it. They'll reach us by midday."

I had known this was coming — you cannot steal a guildmaster's covenant pieces, burn his authority in front of four hundred witnesses, and expect him to send a strongly worded letter — but knowing a thing and feeling it arrive are different animals. My hand, the oath-marked one, trembled on the teacup. Lena saw. She always saw. She put her hand over mine, warm and steady, and said nothing about the trembling, which was its own kind of mercy.

"He'll want to negotiate," I said. "Or buy us. Or arrest us. Or all three, in ascending order of politeness."

"He'll want to win," Lena said. "That's the thing about Corvane nobody else sees. Everyone thinks he's a trader. He's not. Traders want fair exchanges. He wants to *win*, and he's spent forty years learning that other people's names are cheaper than his own sacrifices." She took a sip of terrible tea and grimaced. "My mother's ledgers said the same thing, you know. Before he sabotaged the wards. She wrote that Corvane was 'a man who mistakes accounting for courage.'"

"Your mother sounds like she'd have liked me."

"She'd have adored you. Two people who hide feelings in paperwork." Lena bumped her shoulder against mine. "It's disgusting. I'm surrounded by it."

Despite everything — the wound on the horizon, the army coming, the sword in my lap that whispered at night — I laughed. That was Lena's particular miracle, the one no covenant had ever accounted for. Not walking through wards. Making me laugh on the morning I expected to die.

* * *

They crested the southern ridge at midday, just as the scouts promised: the Guild of Impossible Things in full martial array, four thousand strong, banners snapping in a wind that shouldn't have existed under a starless sky. Pikes. Ward-casters. The oath-hounds in their iron muzzles, straining toward us like compass needles finding north.

Our four hundred went quiet in the way crowds do when they realize the arithmetic has changed.

I walked out front to meet them, because I was still, whatever else had happened, a guildswoman with rank enough to demand parley, and because if Corvane wanted to talk, better he talk to me than start shooting at children. Lena flanked me, because of course she did; we had fought about it, properly fought, at the edge of the world, and the treaty we'd written afterward was simple: no one gets protected out of their own story.

Corvane rode forward alone. That was theatrical, and Corvane knew it was theatrical, and I suspect he considered theater a form of honesty.

"Edmund Yarrow," he called down. His voice was exactly as I remembered it from my childhood in the guild halls: warm, reasonable, the voice of a man explaining why the porridge has run out again. "You've made rather a mess of things."

"I learned from the best."

Something flickered behind his eyes — amusement, maybe, or its taxidermied cousin. "Give me the pieces. Give me the girl. Take whatever pardon you like; I'll even restore your name to the rolls, un-sold, spotless. You can be the last swordspeaker again, properly honored. A statue, if you want one. I know how you feel about permanence."

It was a good offer. That was the horror of it. Some cold, tidy part of me — the part that kept ledgers, the part that had spent thirty years believing order was worth any price — leaned toward it like a plant toward light. Be restored. Be *kept*. Never again be the unkept promise, the sold child, the woman whose own name had been currency before she was tall enough to see over a counter.

"No," I said.

"Edmund—"

"You sold my name when I was seven years old." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "You sealed a bargain with it and then you spent forty years letting the Hollow eat the difference, and when the bill came due you sent hounds after a girl who'd done nothing but be hungry. I don't want the pardon. I don't want the statue." I glanced up at him, and let him see, for once, everything. "I want the stars back. And I want you to watch."

For a moment — just a moment — Corvane's mask slipped, and underneath it I saw what chapter twelve of my life had shown me in his study: a tired man who had once stood where I stood, asked to sacrifice what he loved, and chosen instead to spend everyone else. He wasn't evil. That was the worst discovery of my year. He was simply a man who had mistaken accounting for courage, right up until the accounts came due.

"Then you leave me no choice," he said, and it sounded almost like grief. He raised his hand.

And the guild army — four thousand pikes, forty ward-casters, a hundred muzzled hounds — did nothing.

Because behind them, at the rear of the column, soldiers began setting down their weapons. First dozens, then hundreds. A captain I recognized from the border garrisons unbuckled her sword-belt and laid it in the dirt. An oath-hound handler pulled his muzzle off his beast and simply sat down on the road, and the hound, unmuzzled, looked at the wound in the world, whined, and lay down facing *away* from us.

"They're not here to stop you," Lena breathed beside me. "Edmund. Look. They're not here to stop you — they're here to *follow*."

Corvane turned in his saddle and beheld his army dissolving into pilgrims, and I watched forty years of careful accounting collapse on his face like wet paper. He had brought them to crush a rebellion. He had brought them, it turned out, to a procession.

"You can't—" he started, and then stopped, because there was nothing left to finish the sentence with. You cannot command people who have remembered what they actually serve. He had spent his whole career trading in names, and the army had just remembered theirs.

Vessith chose that moment to crest the ridge behind us, wings spread against the starless sky, and the sound four thousand soldiers made was not a war cry or a scream but something older — the sound people make when a story they'd stopped believing in stands up and stretches.

"To the wound!" I shouted, and my voice cracked on it, and I didn't care. "To the wound, and we argue with the world!"

* * *

We marched.

Not charged — marched, four hundred and then four thousand and then more, a river of the frightened and the stubborn flowing toward the hole in everything. Vessith lumbered at our center, conserving her last flame. Sister Anselm clutched the covenant-piece. The children carried water. Pell navigated by spite. Brannick walked beside his unmuzzled hound like a man reunited with an old argument.

And Lena walked beside me, her hand in mine, fire waiting under her skin like a held breath.

At the edge of the wound the world simply stopped agreeing with itself — grass becoming sky becoming nothing — and I felt my sword wake against my hip, whispering the first word of a sentence it had been trying to finish for thirty years.

Beyond the edge, in the attentive dark, something vast turned toward us with the slow interest of a creditor.

"Well," said Lena. "Ready?"

I checked my ledger one last time. One dragon. One vessel. One broken promise, freely renewed.

"No," I said honestly, and stepped through.
