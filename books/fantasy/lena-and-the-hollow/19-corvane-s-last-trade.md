## 19. Corvane's Last Trade

### Edmund

The Hollow's heart was not a place so much as an argument the world had lost.

Edmund felt it in her teeth first — that grinding absence, like biting into bread and finding nothing there. Then in her oath-marks, which had gone cold as river stones. The wound in the world opened before them: a tear in the air the size of a cathedral door, its edges fraying into grey threads that unraveled even as she watched. Beyond it, nothing. Not darkness — darkness was something. This was the space where something had been subtracted.

Vessith's last flame rode in the lantern at Lena's hip, a coal no bigger than a thumbnail, and yet it burned steady here at the edge of unmaking, stubborn as a debt collector.

"You're doing the ledger face," Lena said.

"I don't have a ledger face."

"You absolutely do. Jaw set, eyes narrowed, like the world has presented you with an invoice you intend to dispute line by line." Lena bumped her shoulder against Edmund's arm. "For the record, I find it very handsome."

"Lena. We are about to walk into the mouth of the devouring absence between worlds."

"And I'm telling you it's handsome *now*, while we can still appreciate it."

Edmund almost smiled. Almost. Behind them, the sound she'd been dreading all morning finally arrived: boots. Hundreds of them. The guild's armies had come over the ridge of ash in good order, banners snapping in a wind that shouldn't exist this far into the Hollow's reach — and at their head, riding a grey horse that seemed to have forgotten how to be any color, came Guildmaster Corvane.

He dismounted with the unhurried grace of a man arriving at his own dinner party. He looked older than he had in his study, weeks ago. Or perhaps just more honestly worn, the way a coin looks when you've finally cleaned off the gilt.

"Edmund Yarrow," he said. "And the warden's daughter. You've made remarkable time. I confess I expected you to die on the Starless Sea."

"The sea had other plans," Lena said pleasantly. "Mostly involving not eating us."

Corvane's gaze went to the lantern, to the ember, and something flickered across his face that Edmund recognized from forty years of guild work: hunger, wearing the mask of appraisal. "You have the pieces. Both of them. And Vessith's fire." He spread his hands. "Then we needn't be enemies. Walk with me, Edmund. Let us conclude this transaction properly. You of all people know the value of a well-drafted agreement."

"I know the value of reading one," Edmund said. "Yours had my name in it when I was seven years old. I wasn't consulted."

"No," Corvane agreed. "You weren't. And for that, I will apologize — once, sincerely, and never again, because we haven't time for more." He turned and gestured at the wound in the world, and at the armies behind him, and at the starless sky above. "But hear my offer first. All of you. Hear it, and then tell me I'm wrong."

* * *

### Lena

Lena had met a great many liars in her life. She'd been one herself, briefly and badly, back when lying still seemed worth the effort. So she knew the species, and Guildmaster Corvane was not it. That was the worst part. He believed every word he said, and belief is the most contagious thing in the world.

He stood before his assembled soldiers and the two of them and the dying dragon-fire, and he laid out his trade the way Edmund laid out a ledger entry: clean, precise, itemized.

"The covenant demands a kept promise, freely renewed," he said. "A sacrifice, willingly given. That is what broke it the first time — not the sale of a child's name, though I do not pretend that was kind. What broke it was that the sacrifice was *taken* rather than offered. The Hollow has fed on that theft ever since. Every collapsed ward, every devoured village, every star snuffed out — interest accrued on a stolen principal." His voice carried effortlessly; Lena suspected magic in it, or just fifty years of being obeyed. "So. The renewal requires a willing soul who owes the covenant a promise. There is exactly one such person standing here."

He did not look at Edmund. That was the trick of it, Lena realized. He looked at the armies. At the thousands of faces behind him.

"Edmund Yarrow intends to offer herself," Corvane said. "I have read her ledgers. I taught her to keep them. She will stand at the wound's edge and speak her old oath, and the covenant will take her — her swordspeech, her years, quite possibly everything she is — and the stars will come back, and we will all applaud her heroism, and in ten years no one will remember why." He paused. "I am here to propose a better price."

"Better for whom?" Edmund asked quietly.

"Everyone. That is rather the point." Corvane reached into his coat and drew out a book. Not a ledger — something older, bound in grey leather that hurt to look at directly. "This is the Register of Names. Every name the guild has ever held in trust. Forty thousand names, Edmund. Farmers and smiths and children and kings. Names we took as collateral over three centuries, most of them never reclaimed, most of them *forgotten by their owners*." He held it up. "I offer them. All of them. Returned, freely given, a mountain of willing sacrifice to feed the covenant — enough to seal it a thousand times over, without costing a single life. Without costing *her*."

Murmuring ran through the ranks behind him. Hope, Lena thought sourly, spreads faster than plague.

"Think of it," Corvane pressed, and now he did turn to Edmund, and his voice gentled into the register of a man selling salvation. "No one dies. No one burns. The girl keeps her swordspeaker. The swordspeaker keeps her girl. I keep my guild, reformed under new rules — I'll sign whatever charter your scruples demand. And the Hollow closes on a feast of returned names instead of one woman's grief. It is, by any honest accounting, the superior trade."

And the horrible thing — the thing Lena would chew on for years afterward — was that it *was* a good trade. It was generous. It might even work.

She looked at Edmund and saw her lover doing the thing Edmund always did: running the numbers, weighing the columns, hunting for the error in the arithmetic. And finding none.

"Say no," Lena whispered.

Edmund didn't answer. Her hand drifted toward the lantern.

* * *

### Edmund

Here is what no one tells you about a lifetime of keeping accounts: eventually, you become one.

Edmund stood at the edge of the wound and looked at Corvane's book of forty thousand names, and she thought: *that would work.* She thought: *no one has to burn.* She thought of Lena's face, and of a garden they'd once joked about planting, someday, if the world allowed it, and she thought about how the world was offering, right now, to allow it.

And then she thought about what a name actually was.

She had spent decades trading them. She knew their weight better than anyone alive. A name was not currency. A name was a *self*, folded small enough to carry. And Corvane — brilliant, terrible Corvane — was proposing to spend forty thousand selves to save one, because spending others was the only trade he had ever learned. He could not give himself. He had proven that thirty years ago, when the covenant first demanded a sacrifice and he had gone looking through the guild rolls for someone cheaper.

"Your offer is generous," Edmund said. "Genuinely. I want that recorded."

"Edmund—"

"Let me finish. I've been rehearsing this since the Sea." She stepped forward, past Lena, past the armies, until she stood between Corvane and the wound, and the cold of the Hollow pressed against her back like a second skin. "Here is the flaw in your accounting. The covenant doesn't want *names*. It wants a promise. Freely renewed. Kept." She raised her voice so the whole army could hear. "Forty thousand names given back isn't a promise. It's a payment. It's buying your way out with other people's collateral — which is the exact crime that broke the covenant in the first place. You'd be reforging the bargain with the same theft that shattered it. The Hollow wouldn't close. It would *eat faster*."

Corvane's composure cracked, just at the edges. "You cannot know that."

"I keep ledgers," Edmund said. "I know what happens when you balance books with money that was never yours. The debt doesn't vanish. It compounds." She turned to face the armies — the frightened, hopeful, tired faces of people who had marched into hell because their guildmaster told them it would be quick. "He's offering you a miracle where nobody pays. There is no such miracle. There never was. That's rather the definition."

"So what then?" someone shouted from the ranks. "Who pays?"

Edmund looked down at her own hands. The oath-marks had faded nearly white now, spent almost entirely, thin as old scars. Whatever remained of her swordspeech sat in them like the last coins in a purse.

"Me," she said. "Willingly. Which is the entire point, and the only thing he—" she nodded at Corvane, and her voice did not shake, though it wanted to, "—could never offer. Because he has never once, in fifty years, put himself on the wrong side of a trade."

She turned back to the wound. Behind her, she heard Lena make a small, furious sound — but Lena had promised, back on the boat, back after the worst fight of their lives: *you don't get to protect me out of my own story, and I don't get to protect you out of yours.* So Lena did not grab her. Lena stood witness. That, too, was a kind of love, and Edmund loved her the more for it.

"Guildmaster," Edmund said formally, falling into the cadence of contract-speech, the old words rising in her like water finding its level. "I decline your counter-offer. And I enter mine."

"Edmund Yarrow—" Corvane began.

"*Item the first,*" she said, louder, and the words came out ringing, swordspeech waking in her throat for the last time, bright and terrible and hers. "*I, Edmund Yarrow, last oath-bound swordspeaker of the Guild of Impossible Things, do hereby renew the promise made in my name at seven years of age — freely, knowingly, and of my own will. Item the second: I tender myself in payment. My speech, my years, my self entire. Item the third:*"

Her voice caught. Just for a moment. Because item the third was the hard one, and she had written it on the boat, in the dark, with Lena asleep against her shoulder.

"*Item the third: let it be enough.*"

The wound in the world shuddered. The grey threads at its edges stilled. Somewhere inside the Hollow, something vast turned its attention toward her like an eye opening.

And Corvane — Corvane moved.

Not toward her. Toward the lantern. Toward Vessith's ember, his hand darting out with the speed of a man who had spent fifty years reaching for other people's things, and his fingers closed on empty air because Lena was already there, already holding the lantern high, already saying, in a voice that carried the authority of every ward her mother had ever built:

"That's not yours to trade. *Nothing* is. That was always your problem."

* * *

### Lena

Later — much later, in the garden, in the ordinary golden light of restored stars — Lena would try to explain to Edmund what she saw in that moment, and fail, and try again, and settle finally on: *it was like watching someone lose an argument with the sky.*

Because the Hollow answered Edmund.

It rose up around her — not attacking, *receiving*, the way the sea receives a diver — and every thread of grey absence bent toward the small, plain, oath-marked woman standing at its edge with her chin up and her terms stated. Forty thousand names in a grey book meant nothing to it. One woman, offering freely, meant everything. The mathematics of it were obscene and beautiful: the entire difference between Corvane's empire and Edmund's single sentence was the word *willingly*, and the Hollow had been starving for that word for thirty years.

"She'll die," Corvane said. He sounded, for the first time, simply like an old man. "You understand that. The original terms—"

"The original terms are being renegotiated," Lena said. "By the only person with standing to renegotiate them." She lifted the lantern higher, and Vessith's ember flared in answer, sensing its purpose approaching. "You built a world where everything had a price and nobody paid. She's ending it. Stand there and watch how it's done."

Behind them, the armies had gone silent. Ten thousand soldiers watching a five-foot-four bookkeeper argue with the end of the world — and win, term by term, clause by clause, the way she'd won every argument Lena had ever seen her enter: by knowing the contract better than anyone else in the room.

Edmund turned, once, and found Lena's eyes across the distance.

"Hey," Edmund called, and her voice was already going strange, resonant, half-borrowed by the covenant speaking through her. "If this works — the garden. By the breach. I want roses. Impractical ones."

"You'll plant them yourself," Lena called back, and her voice broke on the last word, and she let it. "Plain-handed. I'm holding you to it."

"Enter it in the ledger," Edmund said, and smiled — that rare, unguarded smile, the one Lena would spend the rest of her life trying to earn — and turned, and stepped into the wound, and the Hollow closed around her like a book accepting its final entry.

The ember in Lena's lantern blazed white.

Somewhere beneath the ash, something ancient began to wake.

And the last thing Lena heard, echoing up out of the closing dark, was Edmund's voice — no longer afraid, no longer alone, speaking the oldest word in any language of trade:

"*Accepted.*"
