## 21. After the Stars

The first thing I learned about peace was that it was loud.

Nobody warns you about that. You spend years thinking quiet is the thing you want — and then the stars come back, one by one, like lamps being lit in a house you'd given up for empty, and suddenly there are birds again. Actual birds. They had been waiting somewhere, apparently, all this time, folded up in whatever pocket of the world birds go to when the sky stops answering. And they had *opinions*. At dawn they held what I can only describe as a committee meeting outside my window, and every member had a grievance.

I lay in bed and listened to them argue and thought: this is what my mother built the wards to protect. Not the borders. Not the covenant. This. The noise of an ordinary morning that nobody has to fight for.

Then I got up, because the guild doesn't reform itself.

* * *

Reforming the Guild of Impossible Things turned out to be less a matter of sweeping reforms and more a matter of sweeping. Corvane's fall had left the great ledger hall full of ash — Vessith's last flight had taken the roof off the east wing, and dragon-ember does not discriminate between evil architecture and good. For three weeks I went through the archives with a broom in one hand and a lantern in the other, reading ledger after ledger by candlelight, learning what my mother's guild — because it *was* hers, once, before it curdled — had actually been.

The answer, mostly, was kinder than I expected.

The old entries weren't trades at all, not really. They were records. *Kept:* one memory of a wedding, returned to a widow who'd sold it in a famine year, no charge. *Kept:* a name restored to a foundling so she could be claimed by her grandmother across the sea. *Kept:* rain, sent where rain was needed, on the understanding that someone downriver would send bread back when they could. The impossible things the guild traded in were never supposed to be commodities. They were supposed to be promises with paperwork.

Corvane hadn't invented the corruption. He'd just stopped pretending it wasn't one.

"You're doing the face," Edmund said, appearing beside me with two cups of tea and setting one on the ledger I was reading, directly over the entry about the widow. "The face where you're angry at a dead man and a book at the same time."

"I'm allowed one face."

"You have six. I've catalogued them." She sat down on the crate across from me — she still sat like a swordspeaker, spine straight, weight balanced, even though her hands were plain now and would stay plain. "Which one is this?"

"The one where I'm going to fix everything."

"Ah." She blew on her tea. "That one takes years. Ask me how I know."

I did ask, and she told me, and we lost the rest of the morning to it, which I have decided counts as work.

* * *

My memories came back in the second month, and not the way I'd imagined.

I'd pictured it like a door opening — one dramatic moment, all my stolen childhood flooding home. Instead it came back the way frost melts, edge by edge, in patches, some of it so small I almost missed it. The smell of tallow candles. A song with words in a language I suddenly understood again mid-hum. The specific weight of a wooden practice sword, and my mother's voice saying *elbow in, little wren, the ward flows from the elbow.*

Little wren. That was mine. That was *hers*, and it had been sitting inside me the whole time behind a wall made of a trade I didn't remember making, and when it came back I had to put down the ledger I was holding and go stand in the courtyard until my eyes agreed to behave.

Edmund found me there. She didn't say anything clever. She just stood next to me in the cold and let me lean on her shoulder, which is, I have come to believe, the entire secret of love, or at least ninety percent of it. The other ten percent is tea.

"It hurts," I said finally, "in a good way. Is that allowed?"

"Nothing about memory is allowed," Edmund said. "It does what it likes. Believe me." She flexed her right hand — the oath-marked one, the mark gone pale as old scar now, the swordspeech burned out of it forever. Sometimes she did that. Flexed it, looked at it, put it away again. Like checking a room where something used to live. "Does it feel like yours? When it comes back?"

"Yes." I thought about it. "That's the strange part. It never stopped feeling like mine. It just felt like mine from very far away."

She nodded slowly. "That's what losing the speech felt like too," she said, and then she smiled, small and real, and said, "But quieter. And with better company."

* * *

By spring, the recovered pieces had assembled themselves into a picture, and the picture had a name on it: Warden Sera Vane. My mother. Keeper of the border wards, architect of the covenant's anchor-points, the woman whose ghost had guarded a drowned shrine and asked forgiveness neither Edmund nor I had been able to give yet.

I say *yet.* I've been working on it.

Her legacy turned out to be more than blood. In the deepest vault — the one Corvane had sealed with three oaths and a very smug lock, which yielded to Edmund in an afternoon once she pointed out that oaths sworn falsely don't bind, and Corvane had sworn his falsely to *everyone* — we found her plans. The original design for the wards. Notes in her hand, margins crowded with sketches: not a wall between realms, but a *handshake*. A covenant meant to be renewed every generation, freely, by volunteers. A living bargain, tended like a garden.

Corvane had read those same notes, decades ago, and seen only the cost line. *One name, willingly given, per renewal.* He'd decided willingness was too expensive, bought a child's name instead, and called it statesmanship.

"He wasn't wrong that it needed a price," Edmund said quietly, the night we finished reading. We were in the vault, surrounded by my mother's handwriting, and Edmund had her plain hand flat on the table like she was steadying it. "He was wrong about everything else."

"Everything else," I agreed.

"But he read the same page we did." She looked up at me. "Lena. The covenant wants renewing. Every generation. Freely."

"I know what you're going to say."

"Someone has to—"

"*No.*" I said it fast, before the idea could get its boots on. "Nobody has to give anything. Not this time. Read her notes again — the *margins*, Edmund. She spent twenty years trying to design a renewal that didn't cost a life. The last sketch isn't a sacrifice. It's a *tending*. Someone keeps the fire fed. Small offerings. Ongoing. A garden, not a pyre."

Edmund stared at me. Then she pulled the final folio toward her and read the margin, really read it, lips moving slightly, and I watched something unknot in her shoulders that had been knotted since before I met her.

"A garden," she repeated.

"She was a warden, not a butcher. It took me this long to see it, but—" I tapped the sketch. "She always intended mercy. Corvane just couldn't imagine mercy with a budget."

Edmund laughed — actually laughed, that rusty sound she still hadn't gotten much practice at — and reached across the table and took my hand, plain palm against scarred one, and we sat in the vault among my mother's mercies and planned.

* * *

The breach itself was a wound in the world for exactly as long as it took Edmund to decide otherwise.

We went out in early summer, the two of us plus a cart, plus Vessith's ashes in an iron urn that weighed almost nothing and felt like it weighed everything, plus forty sacks of soil hauled from the river valley because the ground where the Hollow had been was gray and sour and refused, with great dignity, to grow anything.

The place where the sky had torn open was just a field now. A wide, scarred circle of dead earth under a sky so full of stars it looked show-offy. Grass crept in at the edges, tentative, like guests arriving early to a party.

Edmund stood in the middle of it for a long time.

"This is where I was supposed to die," she said conversationally, which is how she talks about the worst things. "Right about here. I had a whole speech prepared."

"What was the speech?"

"'Tell Lena I'm sorry about the boat.'"

"That's a terrible speech."

"It was a stressful day." She crouched, took a handful of the gray soil, crumbled it. "Sour. But soil's just soil. It forgets." She glanced up at me. "Help me make it forget?"

So we worked. We hauled and spread and turned and mixed, and I learned that Edmund Yarrow, last of the oath-bound swordspeakers, terror of the Market of Unmade Things, could not tell a weed from a desirable plant and had strong opinions anyway. We argued cheerfully about rows versus beds. We buried a pinch of Vessith's ashes at the center, at Edmund's insistence — "dragons are *fire*, and fire is the oldest fertilizer there is" — and she said a few words over it in a language I didn't know, and the wind moved through the grass at the edges like it was listening.

Then we planted. Seeds my mother's notes had recommended: ash-root, star-thistle, wardbloom. Things that grow deep and hold soil together. Things that keep.

"There," Edmund said at last, filthy to the elbows, sitting back on her heels in the middle of the circle where the world had nearly ended. She looked around at the neat dark rows. "Ledger entry: kept."

"Kept what, exactly?"

She considered. "A promise. To a dragon, a girl, a warden, and myself. All four at once. Very efficient."

* * *

The guild's new master was elected in autumn, and to everyone's surprise except mine, it was Edmund.

To be precise: everyone nominated her, she refused with tremendous eloquence, and then the guild voted for her anyway on the grounds that refusing the position demonstrated precisely the judgment the position required. She appealed. The appeal was voted down. She threatened to spend the rest of her life gardening. The assembly noted approvingly that this showed excellent priorities for a guildmaster.

"They can't do this," she told me that night, pacing our rooms above the ledger hall. "I'm unqualified. I'm retired. I'm — Lena, I burned my *oaths*. I'm the least bound person alive."

"Mm," I said, from the comfortable chair, mending a sock. "And who reformed the archives? Who rewrote the trade codes? Who personally audited every outstanding debt and forgave the ones that were cruelty wearing a contract's clothes?"

"That was *work*, not leadership."

"Same thing here."

She stopped pacing. She gave me the look — the sixth face, the one I'd catalogued privately as *caught* — and then she sat down heavily on the arm of my chair and rested her chin on top of my head, which is her way of surrendering.

"If I do this," she said into my hair, "you're doing it with me. Co-master. Non-negotiable. I refuse to be responsible alone for a building full of people who think bottled thunderstorms are currency."

"Terrible terms," I said. "Accepted."

* * *

Winter came gently, that first year. The wards hummed — my mother's handshake, reforged, tended now by rotation, a dozen volunteers a season feeding the anchor-fires small things: a memory gladly shared, a song, a name spoken with love. Nobody sacrificed anything. The stars stayed lit. Some nights I stood on the guild roof and checked them anyway, all of them, twice, and Edmund would come find me and stand with me without asking why, because she knew why, and because she checked them too when she thought I wasn't looking.

On the longest night of the year, we wrote the ledger entry together. But that's another chapter, and this one belongs to the morning after — the first morning of the new year, when I woke to the bird-committee outside the window, louder than ever, insufferable, alive.

Edmund was already up. I found her in the kitchen, badly singing the song in the language I'd relearned, burning porridge, wearing flour she had no use for, and when she saw me she stopped mid-line and grinned at me with the whole of her face.

"Happy first ordinary morning of the rest of everything," she said.

And I opened my mouth to answer — when the knock came at the door. Three knocks, formal, guild-pattern. Urgent.

From the garden side of the house. Where there was no door.

Edmund and I looked at each other, and the porridge burned, and neither of us moved to stop it.
