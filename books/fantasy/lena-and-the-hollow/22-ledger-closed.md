## 22. Ledger, Closed

### Edmund

The garden where the breach had been was coming up wild.

I hadn't planned it that way. I'd planted in rows, because I am Edmund Yarrow and I have never in my life planted anything any other way, and the rows had lasted precisely until the first spring rain, at which point the beans decided they knew better than the ledger of my intentions and went wherever they pleased. Lena said the beans were showing initiative. Lena said a great many things about the beans.

I stood at the garden's edge with the morning fog still on it and let myself do the thing I had been practicing for a year now, which was nothing. Just standing. No ledger under my arm, no oath humming in my wrist, no guild seal to answer to. My right hand hung quiet at my side, plain as any other hand, and if I pressed my thumb into the old scar where the mark used to sit, there was no pull, no hook, no voice waiting behind my teeth for permission to speak the world awake.

It had taken me most of the winter to stop reaching for it. Longer to stop grieving it. Somewhere around the thaw I had noticed I was sleeping through the night, and I had lain awake until dawn deciding whether that counted as betrayal or healing. I still wasn't sure. But the beans didn't care either way, and there was a certain freedom in being accountable to beans.

"Edmund." Lena's voice from the kitchen window, pitched to carry across the yard the way only she could pitch things — not loud, just *arriving*, the way she arrived through wards that couldn't see her, through doors that ought to have been locked, through every defense I had ever built. "You're standing in the fog again."

"It's called thinking."

"It's called avoiding breakfast. The kettle's on and Vessith's egg is doing the thing."

I crossed the yard at a speed I would deny under oath. Not that anyone could administer one anymore. That was rather the point of me now: unsworn, unbound, the last swordspeaker turned first non-swordspeaker, which is a title I invented myself and defended fiercely whenever anyone suggested it wasn't a real thing.

The cottage we'd built on the guild's old eastern land was small and crooked and mine — ours — in a way no guild posting had ever been. We'd raised it ourselves, mostly, with help from Sera Venn and half the reformed Guild of Impossible Things, who had discovered in the year since Corvane's fall that impossible things included load-bearing walls. The roof leaked over the pantry and nowhere else, which Lena insisted was the house having opinions about my organizational habits.

Inside, the egg sat in its cradle of banked ash by the hearth, and it was, indeed, doing the thing: rocking. Gently, rhythmically, like something inside was testing whether the world outside was worth waking up for.

Vessith had given her last ember to the reforging. Everyone knew that; it was in the histories now, three separate accounts, two of them accurate. What the histories did not know — what only Lena and I and the ash-cradle knew — was that a guttering flame, given wholly and freely, does not always go out. Sometimes it goes *inward*. Sometimes, a year later, there is an egg in your hearth the size of a loaf of bread, warm as a sleeping child, and neither you nor the foremost dragon-scholars of two realms can explain it, and you have stopped trying.

Lena stood over it with her sleeves pushed up, flour on her forearms, watching it rock with the expression she wore for all living things: fierce, unsentimental tenderness.

"If it hatches during breakfast," she said, "I'm naming it."

"You are not naming a dragon after a bread."

"I named the goat after a bread and look how well that worked out."

The goat, whose name was Cobble, looked up from her corner with the smug serenity of a creature who had won an argument years ago and knew it.

I hung my coat on its peg and did not think about how many coats had hung on guild pegs before this one, in halls where I had catalogued bottled thunderstorms and traded names like coin. Some mornings those memories came gentle. Some mornings they came like weather. This morning they came gentle, and I let them, because Lena had taught me that too — that grief kept honestly is just love with nowhere to put itself yet.

On the table, between the bread board and the honey pot, lay the ledger.

Not a guild ledger. Those were Sera Venn's problem now, and she handled them with a competence that made me feel, variously, proud and obsolete. This one was ours: a plain bound book, good paper, no seal. We had started it the week we moved in, mostly as a joke — *Ledger of the Household of Yarrow-and-No-Name-Yet*, Lena had written on the first page, and then refused to elaborate on the name situation, and I had refused to ask, and we had both been extremely casual about it for eleven months in a way that fooled absolutely no one, including the goat.

We wrote down what we kept. That was the rule we'd set ourselves. Not trades — the guild could keep its economy of exchange; we'd both paid enough into it. Just a record. A jar of the first honey. The day the stars came back fully bright, all of them, even the ones the old charts had never bothered to name. The evening Lena remembered her mother's face.

That entry was short. Some entries should be.

I picked up the pen while the kettle sang, and turned to a fresh page, and found that Lena had already written at the top of it, in her quick slanted hand:

*Entry the final? Ask Edmund. She'll say it shouldn't be final. She'll be wrong.*

I laughed alone in my kitchen, which is the best kind of laughing there is.

### Lena

He was smiling at the ledger when I came back with the teapot, which meant he'd read my note, which meant the campaign was going exactly as planned.

Let the record show: I had been dropping hints about the name situation since midwinter. Subtle ones. Ones like writing *Yarrow-and-What-She'll-Say* on a jam jar, and leaving the marriage customs of the northern vale open on his side of the bookshelf, and once, memorably, asking him directly, "Edmund, hypothetically, if someone wanted to stop being a girl without a ward and start being a girl with a household, what would the paperwork look like?" — to which he had replied, with the gravity of a woman defusing a curse, "There's a form," and then gone pink to the ears and burned the toast.

I loved her desperately. I want that stated plainly somewhere permanent, because I spent too long being a person nobody had records of, and I have opinions about permanence now.

You should understand what it was like, growing up wardless. The wards don't see you. Doors don't quite believe in you. You learn to move through the world like a rumor of yourself. And then the Hollow fell, and the wards fell with it, and everyone else felt naked and screaming while I walked through the wreckage untouched, and for the first time in my life the thing that made me nothing made me everything, and a woman with a trembling oath-marked hand caught me stealing bread and looked at me like I was a person.

She still looks at me like that. Even now. Especially now. It's the single most reliable phenomenon in two realms, more reliable than sunrise, and I have data.

"The egg's rocking harder," Edmund said, pouring tea without looking, because she always knew where everything in her kitchen was, down to the spoon. "Should we—"

"We're not missing breakfast for a hatching. Dragons take days. Vessith told us. 'Days,' she said, 'and do not hover.'"

"She also told us not to bury her heart in the caldera and we did that anyway."

"We buried it *with* the covenant-fire, which is different, and she approved retroactively." I sat across from him — from her, forgive me, I do this when I'm happy, the pronouns get loose in my hands like unruly goats — and pulled the ledger toward me. "So. Entry the final?"

Edmund set down the teapot with great care, the way she handles anything that matters, which has always included me.

"I don't think anything in this house should be final," she said. "Final implies closed. Closed implies finished. I've spent my whole life finishing things, Lena. Oaths, ledgers, covenants. I'd like one thing that just... continues."

My heart did the thing it does. I let it.

"So we don't close it," I said. "We just write today's entry. And tomorrow's. And the one after that, until the paper runs out, and then we buy more paper, because we're wealthy in paper now, thanks to the guild settlement, thank you, Sera Venn, ruthless angel."

"That's not how ledgers work."

"It's exactly how ledgers work. I've read yours. Half the entries are 'see previous page.' You've been continuing things your whole career; you just call it accounting."

She opened her mouth to argue, and the egg cracked.

Not dramatically — no burst of ember-light, no trumpet of destiny. Just a small, dry sound, like a knuckle cracking, and then a hairline seam down the shell, glowing faintly gold, and both of us on our feet so fast our chairs went over, and Cobble bleating in outrage from her corner.

We hovered. Vessith would have been insufferable about it.

* * *

### Edmund

The hatchling was small and damp and furious, which is to say, perfect.

She came out of the shell in stages, over hours, while we sat on the hearthstones in our ruined breakfast clothes and took turns holding the warmth of the fire toward her, though she needed little of it — she carried her own ember, dim but stubborn, a coal-red gleam under new scales the color of banked ash. When she finally got her head free, she fixed one eye on each of us in turn, and made a sound like a bellows sighing, and I felt something in my chest answer it.

Not swordspeech. I checked. I checked hard, the way you press a tongue against the gap where a tooth was. Nothing answered. There was no voice behind my teeth, no power, no pull.

But there was *recognition*. Plain, wordless, human-shaped recognition, the kind that needs no magic at all, and I understood that this was the trade Vessith had actually made, the one beneath all the histories: she had traded her voice for ours. Her fire for our fire. The last dragon, reborn into a house with a leaky roof and a goat, to be raised not by oaths but by people.

"Well," Lena said, tears running freely into the flour on her face. "Hello."

The hatchling sneezed a spark the size of a candleflame and set the edge of the ash-cradle smoldering, and Lena patted it out barehanded without flinching, because of course she did, because fire had never been able to decide what to make of her and had long ago settled on *fondness*.

"What do we call her?" I asked.

Lena looked at the hatchling for a long moment. Then at me. Then at the ledger, still lying open on the table, at the page headed in her handwriting: *Ask Edmund.*

"I have an idea," she said, "but it requires paperwork."

* * *

### Lena

Here is what I knew about the old ways, from my mother's recovered notes, which we keep in a box that Edmund polished so many times the corners went soft: in the warden's tradition, households were sealed not with contracts but with names, spoken together, witnessed by fire. Not sold. Not traded. *Given.* Freely, and to each other, and the giving was the whole of the magic, and it cost nothing, and it held forever, because it was renewed every time it was said.

My mother built walls that kept worlds apart. I liked to think she'd have approved of a wall with a door in it.

So that evening, with the hatchling asleep in a blanket-lined basket (she preferred the basket to the cradle; dragons, it turns out, are born with opinions), and Cobble supervising from the doorway, and the first stars of the evening coming up over the garden where the breach had been — real stars, all of them, burning steady — Edmund and I sat at our table with the ledger open between us.

"There's a form," she said solemnly.

"There is not a form."

"There could be a form. I could draft one. Clause one, mutual—"

"Edmund Yarrow." I took her hand — the plain one, the scarred one, the one that had held a sword awake and then chosen, at the end of everything, to hold mine instead. "Say it with me. Just say it with me."

And she did. Because underneath all the precision and the ledgers and the armor of competence, Edmund Yarrow had always been the person who gave things away freely — her years, her voice, her name to a covenant that never deserved it — and all I had ever done was teach her that some givings come back.

"*I give you my name,*" we said together, to each other, to the fire, to the listening dark that was no longer hungry, "*not to keep, but to share.*"

The hearth-flame leaned toward us, just slightly, the way a cat leans into a hand. Whether that was covenant-magic or draftiness, we agreed never to investigate.

Then Lena Thornbury — my name, my own, the one I'd pieced together from a dead woman's letters and a living woman's stubbornness — wrote the entry. The real one. The one we'd been circling for a year.

*Household of Yarrow & Thornbury, established this day, witnessed by fire.*
*Kept: one name each, shared. One hatchling, unnamed pending committee. One goat, insufferable. One garden, unruly. One another.*
*No trades. Nothing owed. Ledger open.*

Edmund read it twice. Then she added, beneath, in her small exact hand:

*Correction to prior ledgers, all of them: belonging was never a thing to be bought, sworn, or sealed. It is a thing you come home to. Entry continues.*

Outside, the stars burned on. Inside, the eggshell in the basket glittered like a fallen constellation, and the hatchling slept, and the kettle sang, and somewhere past the garden rows the road ran on toward the guild, toward the sea, toward whatever impossible thing would come knocking next — because something always does, and we had a door, and these days, we opened it together.

*Entry continues.*
