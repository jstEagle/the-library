## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley woke the way it always did: slowly, and pretending it had never been asleep.

Petra Vasquez climbed the switchback path to the Hollow Gate before the sun cleared the eastern ridge, boots crunching on frost that would be gone by midmorning, breath steaming in the grey light. The path was worn smooth in its center from generations of feet—keepers' feet, all of them Vasquez feet as far back as anyone could say, which in the valley was not very far back at all. That was the first thing a stranger noticed about Hollowvale, if a stranger ever came: nobody remembered much. Not the founding. Not the old names of the hills. Not why the gate was called hollow when it was plainly a door.

Petra had asked once, at seven years old, standing where they stood now, tugging Maren's sleeve. *Why hollow?*

Maren had looked down at them with those pale eyes that seemed to hold more weather than warmth, and said, "Because nothing passes through it whole." Then she'd crouched, pressed Petra's small hand flat against the cold stone of the door, and added, "And because you don't need to know more than that."

Twenty years later, Petra still didn't know more than that. But they knew everything else.

The Hollow Gate stood alone in a saddle of granite between two shoulders of the mountain, a slab of black stone twice the height of a man and half again as wide, set into no wall, framed by no arch—just a door standing upright in open grass, as though the world had begun to build something there and then thought better of it. Its surface was unmarked by chisel or hand except for seven seal-points, each one a shallow cup of stone the size of a coin, arranged in the pattern of the constellation the valley's oldest map called the Loom. The cups were filled with wax that Maren mixed herself—beeswax, ash, salt from the flats, and one ingredient she never named, carried in a stoppered jar that Petra was forbidden to touch.

Every dawn, the seals were checked. Every dusk, they were checked again. And every seventh day, they were renewed.

Petra knelt now in the frost-wet grass and began.

It was work done with the fingers more than the eyes: pressing each wax seal gently with the thumb, feeling for give, for crumble, for the faint wrongness of a seal going soft. A healthy seal felt like river ice in late winter—solid, but with a hum of tension underneath, as though it were holding something back by deciding to. A failing seal felt like bread gone stale. In eleven years of tending the gate, Petra had found exactly three soft seals, and each time Maren had renewed the wax within the hour, her face closed and silent, and each time the valley had gone on forgetting whatever it forgot, and nothing had come through the door.

Nothing ever came through the door.

That was the point. That was the oath.

Petra sat back on their heels and let their eyes travel up the black stone, as they did every morning, as they had since childhood—the way other people looked at horizons. The stone drank the early light without returning any of it. Up close, in certain moods of weather, the surface seemed less like rock than like water that had decided, very firmly, to be solid. Once, years ago, Petra had held a lantern close enough that the flame's reflection should have shown their face.

It hadn't. It had shown a road.

Just for a heartbeat—a smear of white light on distant stone, a line of hills that belonged to no map of the valley—and then Maren's voice behind them, sharp as a whipcrack: *Away from the glass, child,* and the lantern taken from their hands, and a silence afterward that lasted three days.

There was no glass in the gate. Petra knew that now, adult and sensible. Stone didn't reflect roads. It had been a trick of the flame, of tired eyes, of a child's wanting.

They told themselves that most mornings.

* * *

By the time Petra came down from the saddle, the caravan was already visible on the south road: six wagons and outriders, dust hanging over them like a banner even at four miles' distance. Trade day came twice a month in the warm seasons, once a month in the cold, and it followed a ritual as fixed as the seals.

Petra took their place at the trading post by the boundary stones—a low-roofed shelter of timber and fieldstone with a counter worn glossy by decades of goods sliding across it—and waited while the wagons crawled the last stretch. Maren emerged from the keeper's house as the lead wagon halted, wearing her grey shawl and her grey expression, and stood slightly behind and to the left of Petra, which was her way of being present without being seen to govern.

"Vasquez," called the caravan master, a broad woman with a sunburnt neck and a smile she spent carefully. "Two months. We thought maybe the valley finally swallowed you."

"The valley doesn't swallow," Maren said mildly. "It keeps."

The caravan master's smile flickered—just barely—and recovered. "Salt pork, iron nails, lamp oil, and a bolt of blue dye for the weaver's daughters. And news, if you're selling."

"We buy news. We don't sell it."

That was the rule, and everyone knew the shape of it even if no one said it aloud: trade happened fast, at the stones, and no one lingered. Caravans arrived by midmorning and were rolling again before the shadows turned. No trader had slept a night inside Hollowvale in living memory—not because anyone forbade it, exactly, but because staying past dusk made people uneasy in a way they couldn't name, and by afternoon they were all finding reasons to be elsewhere. Petra had watched it happen dozens of times: strangers arriving full of jokes and road-dirt, growing quieter through the day, checking the sky, touching charms at their throats they hadn't touched on arrival. By the time they left, they rode like people leaving a funeral early.

"It's the null," Maren had explained once, when Petra was old enough to hear the word. "Magic can't live here. Can't enter, can't root, can't breathe. Whatever a person carries of it—charms, blessings, hexes, the little warm gifts most folk don't even notice having—it goes quiet at our boundary. Like a man stepping out of sunlight into a cellar. They feel the dark. So they go."

"And us?"

Maren had been quiet a long moment. "We were born in the cellar. We don't miss the sun we've never seen."

Trading took two hours. Petra haggled the dye down, argued the nail count, counted coins twice because the caravan master's apprentice had quick fingers and quicker eyes. News came in pieces, traded for smoked trout and honey: a border war flaring and guttering somewhere east; a king deposed in a city whose name meant nothing in the valley; prices up on anything touched by magic, which was everything, these days, according to the traders, who said it the way farmers talk about rain—too much or too little, never right.

"They say the courts are buying again," the caravan master offered, leaning on the counter, lowering her voice as if the valley itself might eavesdrop. "Souls, futures, firstborn luck. Undercourt's been hungry since the last war. You'd know about that, surely—valley folk, keeping clear of all that."

"We keep clear of all that," Maren said, in a voice that ended conversations.

But Petra caught it—the half-second pause before the caravan master spoke, the glance that flicked toward the mountains, toward the gate's saddle, and away again. Traders always did that. Looked at the mountain, then looked away, quickly, like people not staring at a scar.

After the wagons rolled, trailing their banner of dust southward, Maren stood a long time watching the road empty.

"You stared at the caravan master today," she said, without turning.

"I haggle better when I can see their face."

"Mm." She pulled the shawl tighter, though the day was warming. "See less, Petra. Want less. It's a keeper's best tool." She started up the path toward the house, then paused. "Seals were sound this morning?"

"Sound."

"Good." And she went inside, and did not come out again until dusk, when they walked up together to check the seals a second time, as they had done every dusk of Petra's life.

* * *

The rulebook lived in the keeper's house, in a chest under Maren's bed, and it was less a book than a litany. Petra could recite it asleep, and sometimes suspected they had learned it that way, hearing it through the wall of their childhood room:

*Check the seals at dawn and dusk. Renew them on the seventh day. Let no one linger past dark. Let no one touch the gate but a keeper. Let no keeper cross the boundary stones. Let no question be asked twice.*

That last one was Maren's own addition, Petra was fairly sure. It wasn't in the oldest copy of the litany, the one written in a hand so cramped it might have been written running. But Maren enforced it like scripture. Ask her something once, and she'd answer, or refuse, but either way it was finished. Ask twice, and something shut behind her eyes, a door within the door, and the subject was dead forever.

Petra had a list of questions killed that way. *What's beyond the pass? Who wrote the litany? Why does the wax smell of iron? What did grandmother's grandmother swear, and to whom?*

All dead questions. All buried in the same unmarked ground.

So Petra had learned to ask differently—to ask with charcoal and paper, in the hour after dusk-sealing when Maren retired early and the house went quiet. They kept the papers loose, unbound, hidden beneath a floorboard under their bed, because a bound book was a confession and loose pages were only scribbles. On them, over eleven years, Petra had drawn the valley entire: every stream, every fold of hill, every stand of pine, the flats, the boundary stones, the gate. It was a good map. It was also, Petra knew with a certainty that sat in their chest like a swallowed stone, a map of a cage drawn by the thing inside it.

Because the edges were what interested them now. Had always interested them, if they were honest—the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth.

Beyond the southern boundary, the traders' road ran down out of the valley and forked, and no one in Hollowvale could say where the forks led, because no one had walked them. Beyond the western ridge, the traders spoke of a lake country. East, over the pass below the bare grey shoulder everyone called the Old Man, the road climbed toward somewhere the caravans came from and returned to, and never described. And north—north the mountain rose, Mount Ceren, higher than the Old Man, darker, holding its snows into summer, and the traders wouldn't look at it at all. When Petra had once mentioned Ceren to the caravan master, the woman had laughed, but the laugh had come out wrong, and she'd changed the subject to nails.

On the newest page, by lamplight, Petra drew the roads they'd never walked. Not from sight—from assembly. Trader gossip, the angles of ridgelines, the direction birds flew at dusk, the lie of streams. They drew the south fork splitting into three. They drew the lake country as a guess, shaded with question marks. They drew Mount Ceren last, and longest, its slopes rendered in strokes heavier than any other line on any page, and at its base they drew a small square, and hesitated, and wrote beside it: *something is there. everyone knows. no one says.*

Then they heard the floorboard in the kitchen—the particular creak of Maren's weight—and blew out the lamp faster than sense required, heart hammering like a boy's, twenty years old and hiding drawings like contraband.

Footsteps paused outside their door. A long silence. Then, evenly: "Dawn comes early. Sleep."

The steps moved away. Petra lay in the dark, listening to the house settle, listening to the wind come down off Ceren and slide around the walls like something looking for a way in.

They weren't afraid of Maren. That was the strange truth underneath everything—they loved her, wholly, the way you love the only fixed star in your sky. Maren who had raised them alone, who had taught them letters and seals and how to gut a fish and how to stand in a storm without flinching. Maren whose iron rules had kept the valley safe for fifty years, safe from whatever the gate kept out, safe from whatever the traders wouldn't name.

It was just that safety, Petra was beginning to understand, tasted different when you were the one inside it.

* * *

Three days later, on the evening of the sixth day of the week, Petra climbed to the gate alone for the dusk check, because Maren's knees had been bad since the cold snap and she'd waved them off with the seal-jar and a grunt that meant *go.*

The evening was windless and gold, the kind of evening that made the valley look like a promise rather than a sentence. Petra checked the seven seals in order, north to south, and found six of them sound, humming with that held-back tension, and found the seventh—

Soft.

Not crumbling. Not broken. Soft, the way wax goes when something warm has breathed on it from the other side.

Petra knelt there a long moment, thumb resting in the seal-cup, and felt their own pulse knock against the stone. Eleven years. Three soft seals in eleven years, and Maren had renewed each within the hour, silent as a grave. This one was different. This one wasn't failing.

This one was *warm.*

Below in the valley, smoke rose straight from the keeper's chimney. Somewhere a dog barked, unhurried. The world was exactly as it had been that morning, and every morning before it.

Petra looked up at the black face of the gate—at the place where, as a child, a lantern had shown them a road instead of their face—and the light was doing something it had never done at dusk. The stone wasn't drinking the sunset anymore.

It was holding it. Keeping it, the way the valley kept everything. And deep in the stone, faint as a vein of ore, something was glowing.

From the treeline below, a long way off, a bird startled up—all at once, a whole flock of them, bursting from the pines as if the ground had shouted.

Petra stood, seal-jar forgotten in their hand, and watched the trees at the edge of the valley, where nothing ever was, and waited to see what would come out of them.
