## 2. A Stranger at the Treeline

The storm came down the valley like something being chased.

Petra felt it before they saw it—a pressure behind the ears, a wrongness in the way the pines along the eastern ridge stopped swaying and simply stood, braced. The goats felt it too. They had crowded against the far wall of their pen an hour past noon, silent in a way goats were never meant to be, staring up-valley toward the Hollow Gate as if they could see through the mountain to whatever was coming.

"Barometric," Petra said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud made them ordinary. Grandmother Maren had taught them that. *Name a thing small enough and it stays small.* But the word sat poorly in their mouth. Barometric storms did not make livestock pray.

By late afternoon the light had gone the color of old pewter, and the first wind arrived—not gusting but steady, a long exhaled breath from beyond the treeline that smelled of wet stone and, faintly, of something else. Something sweet and rotten, like fruit left too long in a cellar. Petra had smelled it only once before, seven years ago, the day a caravan's lead mule had bolted for the gate and had to be put down. Maren had burned the harness and told Petra never to speak of it.

Petra was checking the western seals when the sky finally broke.

The Hollow Gate was not a gate, not really—that was the first thing every child in the valley learned and the last thing anyone understood. It was a gap. A place where the mountains should have met and didn't, a corridor of grey stone perhaps forty paces wide, floored with old road-stones worn featureless by feet that had stopped coming generations ago. On either side rose the boundary stones: nine of them on each flank, hip-high, uncarved, unremarkable, except that nothing grew within arm's reach of them and rain, Petra had noticed once as a child watching from the porch, seemed to fall *around* them rather than upon them.

The valley itself lay below like a cupped hand—three villages, terraced fields going gold with the barley harvest, the mill-pond, the bell-tower of Lowmere with its one cracked bell that rang only for fires and funerals. Two hundred years, Maren said, since anything from beyond the gate had come in and stayed. The caravans came to the trading yard twice a season, traded fast, and left before dark. They never slept in the valley. Petra had asked why, once, at eight years old, and Maren had looked at them for so long that Petra had learned to stop asking.

The seals were simple work, if you knew what you were looking at. Each boundary stone bore a shallow depression on its inner face, and each depression held a plug of packed clay mixed with ash and salt—the same mixture, the same proportions, renewed every equinox without fail. Petra knelt at the fourth stone, rain already needling their shoulders, and pressed a thumb into the seal. Firm. Dry beneath its skin of moss. They moved to the fifth.

That was when they saw the light.

It came flickering through the trees beyond the gate—or rather, it came *staggering*, lurching between the trunks like a drunk man made of lantern-glass. For three heartbeats Petra stood frozen in the rain, thumb still resting in the fifth seal, telling themselves it was sheet lightning, will-o'-wisp, some caravan lantern blown loose. Then the light stumbled out of the treeline onto the old road-stones, and it resolved into a man, and the man was burning.

Not burning the way wood burns. Burning the way paper burns when it is very near a flame it has not yet touched—edges glowing, outlines shimmering, his whole body rimmed in a thin corona of pale gold fire that gave off no heat that Petra could feel across thirty paces of hammering rain. Where the light touched him, his skin was *wrong*: reddened, cracked like sun-baked mud, weeping thin lines of smoke even in the deluge. He wore a courier's oilskin, split at every seam, and he was dragging one leg, and he was coming *into* the valley.

Every rule Petra had ever been given stood up inside them at once. *If anything crosses the stones, do not touch it. Do not speak to it. Ring the bell at Lowmere and run home by the low road.* Maren's voice, iron-calm, drilled into them across twenty years of evenings. And underneath the rules, quieter and older, another voice entirely—the one that had spent whole childhood afternoons crouched at the treeline wondering what was out there, sketching roads on scraps of birchbark, roads that went over the mountains to places Petra could name but would never see.

The burning man fell six paces short of the nearest boundary stone.

He did not fall forward, the way tired men fall. He fell *backward*, arched, as though something were pulling him by the spine, and where his shoulders struck the ground the golden light flared and guttered like a candle pinched between wet fingers. He screamed. It was not a loud scream—it was worse than that, a thin, exhausted sound, the scream of someone who had been screaming for a long time and had almost used it all up.

Petra ran.

Later—much later—they would try to reconstruct the decision and find nothing there to reconstruct. No weighing of rules against curiosity, no moment of choice. There was a man dying in the rain, and there were their own boots splashing through puddles on the old road, and between those two facts there was simply nothing at all.

Up close, the burning was worse. The gold light clung to him in patches now, sinking *into* him, and wherever it sank the flesh beneath turned grey and stiff, like meat left out in frost. His eyes were open. They found Petra instantly, and what was in them was not fear. It was relief so violent it looked like terror.

"You're—" His voice came out in pieces. "You're *inside*. You're standing inside and you're not—" A cough wracked him, and the cough was bloody, and where the blood struck the road-stones it hissed and steamed. "How are you *standing* there?"

"I live here," Petra said. Stupidly. The most obvious sentence in the world, and the man stared at them as though they'd spoken in a language dead a thousand years.

"Here." He laughed, or tried to; it came out as a rattle. "In the null. You live in the null." His hand shot out and caught Petra's wrist, and his grip was astonishingly strong for a dying man, and cold, cold as the rain. "Listen to me. Listen. I don't have—I crossed too far. I know what I did. I thought I could carry it just to the stones, just to the stones, and then turn back, but the storm—" Another cough. The gold light pulsed in the hollow of his throat, dimming, brightening, keeping time with his failing heart. "There's no time. Do you understand me? There is no *time*."

"The bell," Petra heard themselves say. "I can ring the bell. There's a surgeon in Lowmere—"

"There is nothing in Lowmere for this." He said it flatly, without bitterness, the way a man states the weather. "What's killing me isn't in your surgeon's books. It's in mine." His free hand fumbled at his chest, at the oilskin, at something bound beneath it—and Petra saw then that the front of his shirt was soaked not with rain but with blood, black in the storm-light, spreading from a wound low in his abdomen that the golden fire seemed to be deliberately avoiding, the way water avoids grease. Wounded first, Petra realized. Burned second. Someone had done this to him *before* he ever reached the valley.

"What's your name?" Petra asked, because their hands were shaking and asking questions kept them from noticing.

"Ivo." He got the binding loose—a leather wallet, waxed, swollen with damp—and dragged it out into the rain between them. "My name is Ivo. And yours—" His eyes fixed on theirs, and something moved through his face that Petra had no name for, grief and awe and a horrible kind of vindication all at once. "Yours doesn't matter. That's the whole point. Yours doesn't matter, and that's how I found you."

"That makes no sense."

"It makes perfect sense." He was smiling now, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was gentle. "Null-born. No scent. No thread. No name written anywhere in the ledgers—do you know what that means, girl? Boy? Whatever you are? Do you know what that means to the people who bought you?"

The rain hammered down. Somewhere above them thunder rolled along the ridge like a barrel tumbling downstairs.

"Bought," Petra repeated.

"Open it." He pushed the wallet against their chest. "Open it or I'll die holding it and you'll never forgive yourself."

Petra opened it.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with age, was a map. Not a surveyor's map—Petra knew those, had copied a dozen from the trading-yard merchants' samples, all clean lines and confident borders. This was drawn in ink that had once been red and was now brown, and the brown was layered, worked over, *renewed*—and even in the storm-dark, even soaked, Petra's fingers knew what they were touching before their mind caught up. Blood. Old blood, refreshed many times, laid down in careful lines along roads that spiraled inward, past named places—*Ashmarket, the Weeping Woods, the Drowned Stair*—toward a mountain rendered in heavy strokes at the map's heart, and beneath the mountain, in script unlike any alphabet Petra had ever seen, a single word repeated over and over in margins and borders and hidden corners until it became less a word than a texture:

*Undercourt. Undercourt. Undercourt.*

And in the center, where the mountain's roots should have been, a symbol Petra knew intimately. Knew from the seals. From the clay-and-ash-and-salt plugs they had renewed every equinox of their life. The mark of the Hollow Gate—but inverted, hollowed, a door drawn from the wrong side.

"This is blood," Petra said.

"It's mine. Partly." Ivo's grip on their wrist tightened. "The rest belonged to the heir before me. And her before her. It passes down. It's how the court finds its property." He pulled the map free of the oilcloth and pressed it into Petra's hands, folding their fingers closed over it with both of his ruined ones, and the contact seared—literally seared, the gold light leaping from his skin to theirs for one instant, and Petra gasped and felt *nothing*, felt the light slide off them like rain off slate, and watched Ivo's face break apart at the sight of it.

"So it's true," he whispered. "It slides off you. All these years I thought the stories were—look at it. Look at it slide *off* you." He was crying now, or the rain on his face was indistinguishable from it. "You don't even feel it. You've never felt anything, have you? Not once. Not a whisper. Not a dream. Nothing has ever touched you from the other side."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," he agreed softly. "You don't. That's the cruelest part. She kept you blind and called it keeping you safe."

"She?" Petra's voice came out strange. Distant. "Who is *she*?"

But Ivo wasn't listening anymore. The light in his throat was guttering in longer intervals now, darkness stretching between each pulse, and he seemed to be spending what remained of him on something more urgent than answers. His hands found Petra's face and held it, and his eyes—grey eyes, ordinary eyes, a courier's tired eyes—bored into theirs from a handspan away.

"Solstice," he said. "Winter solstice. The debt comes due at solstice, and they will send collectors, and collectors pass the stones like rain passes a roof. You cannot hide in the null. You were never hiding *in* the null. You *are* the null's anchor, do you understand, you are the price that keeps this whole valley quiet, and when they collect you the silence goes with you—" A spasm took him; the light flared white and sank. When he could speak again his voice was nearly gone, a thread of air shaped into words. "Take the map. Go over. Find the ones who'll help you—there are always three, there are always *three*, the map knows them even if you don't. Get under the mountain before the moon dies. Take back what's yours."

"Mine," Petra said. "What's mine."

"Your soul, child." He said it with unbearable kindness, the way one tells a patient the name of their disease. "She sold it before you drew breath. Before you had a name to sell it under. That's why you have no scent, no thread, no shadow in their ledgers—you've been *paid for* since before you existed. Every keeper before her did the same. Bought the valley's peace with an unborn grandchild, generation after generation, and told themselves the oath was mercy." His breath hitched. "Maybe it was. Maybe it was. I ran instead. I was chosen before you, and I ran, and I lived twenty years out there with my name in their ledger, and let me tell you what living on borrowed soul is worth—"

He lifted his hand from Petra's face and touched, with two fingers, the wound in his abdomen. The gesture needed no translation.

"—it's worth exactly this much."

The storm roared. Down in the valley, distantly, the cracked bell of Lowmere began to ring—not for fire, not for funeral, but because someone down there had seen the burning light on the mountain road and panicked. Petra barely heard it.

"I can't be—" Their voice cracked. "I'm the keeper's granddaughter. I check the seals. I count the caravans. I *sketch maps*, that's all, that's all it ever was—"

"I know." Ivo smiled again, and this time the gentleness in it was aimed at himself. "I sketched maps too."

Then his gaze went past Petra's shoulder, up the road, toward the gate, and everything in his face collapsed at once—hope, fear, guilt, all of it falling inward like a roof giving way.

"She's coming," he breathed. "I can't—she can't see me like this, she'll know what it means, she'll—" His strength returned in one final, terrible surge, the last coin of a bankrupt man. He seized Petra by both shoulders and dragged his mouth close to their ear, and the words came out in a rush of blood-warm breath:

"*The heir comes home.* Say it back."

"The heir comes home," Petra whispered.

"Not you. Not yet. But soon, whether you walk or get carried." His hands slid away. He sagged back against the boundary stone—the nearest one, the first of the nine, and where his burning body touched it the stone began, very slowly, to steam. "Tell her Ivo sent you. Tell her Ivo finally stopped running. She'll understand. She's the only one left who'll understand."

"Ivo—wait—what do I *do*—"

But the light in his throat pulsed once, twice, and did not return. The gold fire went out of him all at the same moment, everywhere, like a lamp snuffed under a hood, and what remained slumped against the boundary stone was just a man—grey-skinned, rain-soaked, ordinary, and utterly still. The rain kept falling on him. It had stopped falling around him. Whatever he had carried, he had finished carrying.

Petra knelt in the flooding road with a blood-marked map crushed against their chest and did not move for a long time.

* * *

They heard the footsteps before they saw her, and by then it was too late to decide what to hide.

Maren came up the low road through the rain with a storm-lantern in one hand and her walking staff in the other, moving faster than a woman of sixty-three had any right to move, her grey braid plastered to her back. She had left the house the moment the bell rang; Petra knew her grandmother's stride the way they knew their own heartbeat, and this was the stride she used only for emergencies, for escaped livestock, for the year the millrace flooded.

She stopped ten paces away.

She was looking not at Petra but at the body against the boundary stone, and Petra watched—would remember forever, in the terrible clarity that disasters grant—watched their grandmother's face go through recognition, denial, and grief in the space of a single breath, and settle at last into something older and colder than any of them. Something that looked like it had been waiting fifty years for this exact moment and had hoped, every one of those fifty years, to be wrong.

"Maren," the dead man had said. Not *the keeper*. Not *old woman*. Maren.

"Grandmother," Petra said, and their voice sounded like a stranger's. "He came out of the trees. He was burning. He gave me this—" They held up the map, and the rain immediately began to spot the blood-brown lines, blurring the word *Undercourt* into a bruise. "Grandmother, he said—"

"I know what he said." Maren's voice was level. Terribly level. The voice she used for naming things small enough to keep them small, deployed now at a scale it had never been built for. She came closer, leaned down, and looked into Ivo's slack face for a long moment. Her free hand rose—hesitated—and then closed his eyes with two fingers, gently, the way she closed the coop at dusk.

"He was the boy," she said quietly. To herself, or to the rain. "Forty years, and he was still the boy."

"Grandmother." Petra stood. Their legs were shaking. The map was dissolving in their hands and they found they couldn't make themselves care. "He said my soul was sold. He said *you* sold it. He said every keeper—"

"I know what he said." She straightened, and turned, and looked at Petra full-on for the first time, and Petra saw that behind the iron of her expression there was something else, something that had been held behind that iron for twenty years and was now pressing against it hard enough to bend it. Rain ran off her chin. She did not wipe it away.

"Come inside," Maren said. "Both of us are going to catch our deaths standing here, and you—" her voice caught, actually caught, a hairline fracture in the iron, "—you deserve to hear it dry, and sitting down, and from the beginning."

She turned and started down the low road, lantern swinging, staff striking the stones in her old unhurried rhythm, as though the world had not just ended against her boundary wall.

Petra looked down at the map in their hands—at the drowned ink, the spiraling roads, the mountain with the inverted seal at its heart—and then at the boundary stone where a stranger who had run for twenty years had finally stopped running, close enough to touch the valley that had been bought with a child not yet born.

The rain fell on both of them alike.

Petra followed their grandmother home to hear the beginning.
