## 3. What Grandmother Swore

The night before the collectors came, Maren Vasquez dreamed of her own mother's hands.

They had been beautiful hands once, in the way that old tools are beautiful—scarred, sure, worn to the shape of their work. In the dream they were young again, unblemished, and they were pressing a seal into wet clay, and the clay was warm, and it was breathing.

Maren woke with her heart hammering and lay in the dark of the keeper's house listening to the rain come down off the ridge. Down the hall, Petra's door was shut. She could hear nothing through it, but she knew her grandchild was awake too. Petra had been awake for three nights now. Ever since the storm. Ever since the stranger.

*Ivo*, she thought, and the name tasted like copper.

She had known this day would come for forty years. She had rehearsed the confession a thousand times in the privacy of her own skull, standing at the gate at dusk, walking the boundary stones, kneading bread that no one ate while it was still warm because warmth was a luxury keepers learned early to do without. She had rehearsed it so many times that the words had gone smooth as river stones, and she had told herself, all those years, that when the moment came she would speak them plainly. That Petra deserved plainness.

Now the moment sat in the kitchen below her like a guest who would not leave, and every smooth stone in her throat had turned back to gravel.

*Forty years ago,* she thought. *The winter I was seventeen.*

---

*Her mother had woken her the same way—by not waking her at all. By simply being there when Maren opened her eyes, sitting in the chair by the window with a candle burned down to a puddle, watching the snow come down over the Hollow Gate.*

*"It's time you knew what we bought," her mother said.*

*Maren had been a keeper's daughter her whole life. She had learned the seals at six, the boundary-walk at nine, the caravan law—the never-linger, the never-ask, the never-follow—at twelve. She had thought, at seventeen, that there was nothing left to learn about the valley except how to die in it, which her mother assured her was the easiest lesson of all.*

*"Bought," Maren repeated. "You mean protected."*

*"I mean bought." Her mother's voice had been very level, the voice she used for correcting a misdrawn seal. "There is a difference, and you are old enough now to be ashamed of confusing them."*

*She took Maren out into the snow, past the boundary stones, close enough to the gate that the hair on Maren's arms lifted—not from cold but from the particular wrongness of the Hollow Gate seen at night, the way the air above it refused to hold starlight, the way sound died a half-step too soon on either side of it. Magic could not enter the valley. That was the first truth every child in the valley learned, sung in lullabies: *the hollow keeps us, the hollow keeps us whole.* No witch's curse, no warlock's fire, no fae bargain could cross those stones. The valley slept safe inside its silence like a coin inside a fist.*

*"Who made the silence?" her mother asked.*

*"The first keeper. Grandmother Lena."*

*"Lena made the *gate*. The silence was purchased." And then her mother told her.*

---

Maren came down the stairs at dawn with her spine straight and her hands steady, because if she let them shake now they would never stop.

Petra was already at the table. Of course they were. They had inherited her inability to sleep on bad news, along with her crooked left ring finger and, though neither of them knew it yet, a debt older than both their lives. Petra sat with Ivo's map spread flat before them, weighted at the corners with the salt dish, the candlestick, a spoon, a river-smoothed stone from the boundary line. Their eyes were red-rimmed. Their jaw was set in the exact shape hers went when she was frightened and refusing to admit it.

"You're going to tell me," Petra said. Not a question.

"I'm going to tell you."

"Then sit down, Grandmother. Because I've spent three nights looking at this map, and the markings aren't any script I know, but the *margins*—" Petra's voice caught, steadied. "The margins are in your handwriting."

Ah, Maren thought. So the boy had written more than he'd had time to say.

She sat. She poured tea for both of them because her hands needed work to do, and because the ritual of it—pot, cups, the good honey kept for grief—was the last ordinary thing she would be able to offer. Outside, the rain had stopped and the valley lay washed and green and utterly silent, the way it always did, the way it had for four generations, the way it would for exactly one more season unless she found words equal to the truth.

"Ask me," she said. "Don't guess. Guessing is how people forgive themselves."

Petra's hands flattened on the map. "What did you swear?"

And there it was. The first stone, turned over.

"I didn't swear," Maren said. "That's the cruelty of it. None of us swore for ourselves. We swore for each other, and we swore for children who couldn't refuse." She looked at the window, at the valley beyond it, at the fields where the barley stood in rows straight enough to draw with a rule. "You've grown up believing the valley's silence is a blessing. A protection. Something Lena built with her own two hands to keep the magic out."

"It isn't?"

"The gate is real. The null is real. But the null isn't a wall, child—it's a *debt collection notice*. It's the court beneath Mount Ceren holding up its end of a bargain, and doing it loudly enough that everything magical in the world can read the sign. Nothing crosses into this valley because nothing dares. It isn't holy ground. It's claimed ground."

Petra had gone very still. Stillness was Petra's weapon and their armor both; Maren had watched them use it against angry caravan masters and injured horses and, once, a wolf on the ridge road. She watched them use it now.

"Bought," Petra said quietly. "With what?"

"With a soul."

The word dropped between them and neither moved to pick it up. The fire ticked in the stove. Somewhere outside, a cart bird called its flat, stupid call, indifferent to the ending of worlds.

"Every keeper," Maren said, "for four generations, has renewed the bargain by pledging a soul to the Undercourt. Not their own—that was the mercy my mother clung to, and I have hated her for that mercy every day of my life since. The pledge passes down. You promise the court a soul not yet born, a soul that cannot argue, cannot weep, cannot run. And in exchange, the valley sleeps safe. Crops grow. Children live. The things beyond the stones stay beyond the stones." Her voice cracked on the last of it and she let it. "It has worked for ninety years."

"How long," Petra said, "has it been *me*?"

"Since before you drew breath." She made herself meet their eyes. "Your mother carried you eleven weeks when I signed. I held her hand while the broker marked the contract. She cried the whole time and signed anyway, because the alternative was letting the bargain lapse, and a lapsed bargain means the null fails, and a failed null means everything out there comes in here. She chose you, Petra. God help us both, she chose everyone *but* you."

"My mother." Petra's voice was almost gentle, which was worse than anger. "She knew? All those years, she knew, and she never—"

"She died knowing. Yes." Maren wrapped her hands around her cup because they had begun to shake after all. "And I have known every day since. Every morning I woke and checked the seals and walked the stones and looked at your face across the breakfast table and said *pass the salt* as though you were a girl who would grow old in this valley. As though you were anything other than collateral."

* * *

*In her seventeenth winter, after her mother finished telling her, Maren had done the thing she was proudest of and most ashamed of for the rest of her life: she had run.*

*Not far. There was nowhere far to run from a valley ringed with claims. She got as far as the treeline, to the boundary stone with the chipped corner, and she stood there with snow soaking through her boots and screamed at the mountain until her voice gave out. Come and take mine instead, she had shouted. Take mine. Take mine.*

*Nothing answered. Nothing ever answers in the valley; even screams die politely at the stones.*

*But something heard. Three days later a courier came through with the caravans—a lean, grey-eyed man who lingered one hour too long at the gate, which should have been impossible, and handed Maren a letter sealed in grey wax, which should have been doubly impossible. The wax smelled like cold iron and pennies.*

*The letter was courteous. That was the horror of it. It thanked her for her offer. It declined it—the debt was structured, it explained, in terms a merchant would understand; substituting collateral weakened the whole instrument. But it noted her willingness, and it filed it away, and it closed with a sentence she would carry in her chest for forty years like a swallowed coal:*

***The court finds your devotion noted, Keeper. Devotion compounds.***

*She hadn't understood that last part until the year Petra was born.*

---

"They'll come for me," Petra said. It wasn't a question either. They had Ivo's blood on their sleeve still, Maren saw—a brown ghost of it, scrubbed twice and stubborn. "The collectors. When's the debt due?"

"At solstice." Maren set down her cup. Her hands obeyed her again, mostly. "Midnight of the longest night. The court honors its instruments to the hour; it's the only courtesy it knows. Until then you're safe inside the null—they can't touch what's pledged, only collect it. After midnight..." She stopped. Started again, plainer, because she had promised herself plainness. "After midnight, they take you down the Drowned Stair, and you don't come back, and the valley gets another ninety years of quiet paid for with whatever's left of you in a jar."

"And you knew." Petra rose from the table, slow, like something unfolding. "Every harvest festival. Every birthday. Every time you taught me a seal and said *someday this will be yours*—you knew someday meant five months."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you tell me?" And now the gentleness was gone, and underneath it was the fury Maren had been dreading, the fury she deserved. "I could have—I don't know—run, fought, found some way—"

"To do what?" Maren stood too, and her voice rose despite her, forty years of swallowed coal catching fire all at once. "Run where? Beyond the stones the whole world smells like magic, and you'd be a debt walking around loose with your name on every collector's ledger! Fight whom? They crossed our boundary without bending a blade of grass, child—the null doesn't stop them because the null is *theirs*. Tell you when? At eight, so you could be afraid your whole childhood? At twelve, so you'd hate your mother's grave? At sixteen, so you'd spend your last free year saying goodbye to everything?" She slammed her palm on the table and the spoon jumped and the map shivered. "I kept it so you could have a life instead of a countdown. That was my sin. I confess it. I loved you wrong, and I'd do it again, and I will burn for both of those things."

The kitchen rang with silence after. The valley kind of silence, Petra might have said once, bitterly. The bought kind.

When Petra finally spoke, their voice was low and terrible and calm. "Ivo knew. He came to warn me. He crossed the stones knowing it would burn him alive, and his last act was handing me a map, and the margins are in *your* handwriting, Grandmother, so you're going to tell me who he was, and you're going to tell me now."

Maren closed her eyes.

*Ivo*, who had been nineteen and brilliant and the previous heir, the soul pledged before Petra's mother ever married. Who had found out at seventeen the way Maren had, and who had not screamed at the treeline but *acted*—who had slipped through the gate one autumn night and vanished into the magic lands, and whose escape had cost the family dearly, because the court does not forgive flight, it *compounds*. His running had added years to the debt. His running had, in the court's cold arithmetic, made a second pledge necessary.

His running had made Petra necessary.

"He was the heir before you," Maren said. "My mother's pledge. He fled rather than be collected—and the penalty for flight fell on the next unborn child of this line." She opened her eyes and made herself look at what she had made. "I wrote those margins thirty years ago, trying to chart a road to the Undercourt that a living person could survive. I never found one. Ivo must have taken the map when he ran. And he came back, at the end, to put it in your hands, because he couldn't live with what his cowardice cost you."

Petra stared at her. And Maren watched—helpless, heartbroken, proud past all bearing—as the fury in her grandchild's face did not collapse into tears or despair but hardened into something else entirely. Something with edges.

"Then he died for nothing," Petra said, "unless I finish it."

"That's suicide."

"That's an heir." Petra folded the map with terrible care, creasing each fold sharp, and tucked it inside their shirt. "Solstice, you said. Midnight. Then I have until midnight."

Outside, far up the valley road, where the morning mist still hung between the boundary stones, Maren saw movement. Grey shapes. Unhurried. Walking through the null as if it were a doorway built for them alone.

They had come early. The court, she understood with a lurch, had never intended to wait for solstice at all.

"Petra," she whispered. "Get behind me. *Now.*"
