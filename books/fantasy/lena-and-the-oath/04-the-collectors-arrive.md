## 4. The Collectors Arrive

They came on the ninth morning after the storm, when the frost had finally melted off the boundary stones and Petra had almost convinced themselves that Ivo's death was a fever dream.

Almost. The map was still hidden beneath the loose floorboard in their room, wrapped in oilcloth, its blood-marked lines seeming to shift when they weren't looking directly at it. And Maren hadn't spoken more than ten words to them since the night of the confession—*the heir comes home* still hanging between them like smoke that wouldn't clear.

Petra was at the gate when the grey figures appeared on the far side of the valley road.

There were three of them, walking in perfect step, and Petra's first thought was that they were pilgrims of some kind—robed travelers come to gawk at the famous Hollow Gate, the pass where magic went to die. Caravans came through twice a season; once a year, some zealot or scholar made the trip to stand before the ancient archway and declare it a myth, a trick of geology, a story keepers told to seem important.

But pilgrims didn't walk like that. Pilgrims didn't move as though the space between their footsteps had been measured by something patient and precise.

Petra stood up from the seal-stones they'd been inspecting, brush still in hand, and watched the three figures approach down the road. Grey robes, hooded, trailing slightly as if the fabric were reluctant to touch the ground. They didn't carry packs. They didn't carry anything at all.

The valley's null should have stopped them.

That was the first wrong thing. Everything beyond the gate carried magic the way skin carried sweat—a faint sheen, invisible but present, and the moment anything touched the Hollow Gate's reach, that sheen simply *ceased*. Petra had watched mule-drivers cross the boundary stones and stumble, disoriented, like men stepping off a boat after years at sea. Magic couldn't enter the valley. Nothing with magic in it could.

The three figures crossed the boundary stones without so much as a hitch in their stride.

Petra's mouth went dry. They looked down at the seal-stone in their hand—the small carved marker they'd been cleaning, one of twelve set in a ring around the gate itself—and felt suddenly, absurdly, like a child holding a stick against a flood.

"Grandmother," they said aloud, though Maren was half a mile away at the house. The word fell flat in the cold air.

The figures reached the gate.

Up close, they were worse. Their robes were grey the way old ash is grey, the way a sky is grey before hail. Their hoods were deep enough that Petra could see nothing inside them—not shadow, not face, just a darkness that seemed to have texture, like looking into well water. When the lead figure spoke, its voice came from everywhere at once, quiet and courteous, the voice of someone reading terms aloud.

"Keeper of the Hollow Gate." A pause, as if consulting a document only it could see. "We are here regarding an outstanding obligation."

Petra's hand found the gate's stone frame behind them without their meaning to. The arch was older than the valley's name, older than Maren's grandmother's grandmother, its surface worn smooth by centuries of keeper-hands. It hummed under their palm—not magic, never magic, but something like the memory of a held breath.

"There's no debt," Petra said. "The valley is free. That's the whole point of it."

"The valley is free," the figure agreed, and there was something almost gentle in its tone, the way a moneylender is gentle when explaining interest to a widow. "The valley was purchased. Purchases require payment. We have come to collect what is owed."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"We know you do not." The figure tilted its hood, and for a moment Petra had the horrible sense of being *examined*, turned over like a coin whose weight was being judged. "You were not told until recently. This is customary. Debtors who know too early attempt to flee, and flight damages the merchandise."

*Merchandise.* The word landed in Petra's stomach like a swallowed stone.

"You're talking about me."

"We are talking about an account," said the second figure, its voice identical to the first. "Opened before your birth. Signed by the sitting keeper. Payable at the winter solstice, at the place of signing, in full."

"The solstice is six weeks away," Petra said, and hated how their voice shook. "Why are you here now?"

"Notice," said the third figure, and all three inclined their hoods together, a movement like wheat bending in wind. "We are required to give notice. Payment will be collected at the winter solstice. Resistance will be noted. Flight will be pursued. These are the terms."

"And if we refuse?"

The lead figure was silent for a long moment. When it spoke again, its voice had not changed at all—that was the worst part, the terrible evenness of it—but the air around the gate grew colder, and the frost that had melted from the boundary stones crept back across the grass in a widening circle, killing it brown.

"The court does not refuse debtors the privilege of refusal," it said. "Refusal merely alters the method of collection."

Then they turned—all three at once, in unison—and walked back across the valley, through the boundary stones, out along the road, and were gone. No horse, no cart, no trail. Just three grey shapes diminishing into the treeline like ink fading into water.

Petra stood at the gate until the sun climbed past noon, one hand still pressed against the humming stone, trying to make their lungs work properly.

*Payment will be collected.*

*Merchandise.*

They thought about Ivo, bleeding out against the boundary stones, magic burning him alive from the inside. *The heir comes home.* He'd known. He'd run, and running had killed him slower than staying would have.

Six weeks.

* * *

Maren took the news standing in the kitchen, and she did not sit down again for the rest of the day.

Petra told her everything—the robes, the voices, the words *merchandise*, the frost, the notice. Maren listened with her arms folded tight across her chest, her jaw working like she was chewing on something that wouldn't break down, and when Petra finished, she said only: "Show me exactly where they crossed."

They walked the boundary line together in silence. Maren crouched at the stones where the collectors had passed and pressed her palm flat against the ground, and Petra saw her grandmother's face do something it had never done in Petra's entire life.

It went blank.

Not angry, not afraid—*blank*, the way a page goes blank when someone realizes the ink has run. Whatever Maren had expected, whatever plan had been forming behind her eyes for sixty years, it died right there in the frost-killed grass.

"They shouldn't be able to cross," Maren said quietly. "Nothing crosses. That was the whole—" She stopped. Started again. "The bargain was specific. The valley nulls all magic. Anything of the court that entered would be snuffed out like a candle in a cellar. That was the protection. That was the *point*."

"Except them."

"Except them." Maren rose, knees cracking, and looked east toward the mountains with an expression Petra had never seen on her before. It took Petra a long moment to recognize it, because they'd only ever seen it aimed outward, at strangers, at caravans, at the world beyond the treeline.

It was fear.

"Because they're not *in* the magic, girl. They're what's left when the magic is taken *out*." Maren spat into the dead grass, an old habit, defiance in the shape of contempt. "You can't drown something that's already dry."

* * *

For six days, Maren worked.

Petra woke each morning to find her grandmother already gone from the house, and each evening she returned smelling of chalk and cold iron, her fingers stained with substances Petra had no names for. She re-inspected every seal-stone around the gate. She climbed to the high cairn above the valley and did something there that made the sheep refuse to graze on the near slope for two days. She pulled a book from the false bottom of her cedar chest—a book Petra had never seen, bound in leather gone dark with age—and sat up nights copying lines from it onto strips of parchment, muttering the old formal speech of oaths, the language of binding.

She was preparing for war. Petra understood that much. What they couldn't understand was what weapon she thought she had.

On the sixth night, Petra found her in the kitchen, the oath-book open before her, a candle burned down to a puddle. Her copying hand had stopped. She was just staring at the page.

"Grandmother."

"They'll honor the letter of it," Maren said, without looking up. "That's the one mercy of the court. They are creatures of contract, utterly, perfectly. They cannot take a hair from your head outside the terms." She tapped the page with one stained finger. "So we change the terms."

"How?"

"A contestation. An old right, older than their court, written into the foundation of all their lending. Any contract may be contested by trial, if the debtor invokes it before collection." She finally looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and ferocious. "I've been reading this book since before your mother was born, waiting for the day I'd need it. I know the invocation. I know the forms. If we demand trial, they must grant it, and trials take time, and time is—"

"Grandmother." Petra sat down across from her. "What happens if we lose the trial?"

Maren's jaw tightened. "Then the debt stands."

"And what happens to you? If you invoke it? If you stand in front of those things and argue?" Petra reached across the table and took her grandmother's hand—the hand that had taught them to hold a seal-brush, to read weather, to stand straight at the gate no matter who was watching. The hand was cold. "What does the court do to people who resist?"

Maren withdrew her hand. "People who resist get collected early," she said. "That's why we won't resist. We'll *contest*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

But she wouldn't meet Petra's eyes when she said it, and Petra lay awake most of the night listening to the wind come down off the mountain, wondering what their grandmother wasn't saying, and knowing, with the sick certainty of someone watching a cart start to roll toward a cliff, that whatever it was, it was already in motion.

* * *

The collectors returned on the eve of the solstice moon's first quarter, and this time they did not stop at the gate.

Petra saw them coming from the high field, three grey shapes on the evening road, and this time they walked straight past the boundary stones, past the gate itself, up the lane toward the house, and Petra ran the whole way, lungs burning, arriving at the yard at the same moment the lead collector reached the door.

Maren was already outside. Of course she was. She stood on the porch with the oath-book in one hand and a strip of fresh-inked parchment in the other, and she had put on her good dress, the dark blue one she wore for funerals and the winter market, and Petra understood with a lurch that she had dressed for this the way you dress for a funeral because it *was* one, hers, planned and accepted.

"Maren Vasquez, keeper of the Hollow Gate," said the lead collector, in that courteous everywhere-voice. "You have invoked contestation."

"I have." Maren's voice was iron. "By the old right, written in the foundation of your court before your court had a name. Any contract may be contested by trial, if invoked before collection. I invoke it. Here are the forms." She held out the parchment.

The collector did not take it. All three hoods tilted, in unison, toward the paper—and then, slowly, back toward Maren.

"The right is acknowledged," the lead collector said. "The trial is granted. It will be held at the place of signing, at the hour of collection, at the winter solstice." A pause. "However."

The word dropped into the yard like a stone into a well.

"Contestation requires a debtor of sound mind and full knowledge to stand at trial. The debtor named in this account is Petra Vasquez." The hood turned, and Petra felt that examining gaze settle over them again, weighing, pricing. "The debtor is present, and may invoke or decline. But the *signatory*—" the voice turned back to Maren, and something in its evenness shifted, deepened, became almost regretful, which was infinitely worse, "—has acted outside her standing. The signatory has attempted to alter terms she no longer holds. The signatory has interfered with collection."

"I'm her grandmother," Maren said. "I'm the keeper. I signed the—"

"You signed *away*," the collector said. "What you gave, you gave wholly. You hold nothing now, keeper. Not the debt. Not the terms. Not the standing." The three hoods inclined together, a bow of terrible courtesy. "Interference carries its own schedule of penalties. We are required to give notice."

"Don't," Petra said. The word tore out of them before they knew they were speaking. They stepped forward, between Maren and the collectors, arms spread like a child guarding a door. "Whatever you're going to do to her, it's me you want. The debt is me. Take the trial, I'll be there, I'll invoke it myself, just leave her alone."

"Petra." Maren's voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Get inside."

"The debtor's offer is noted," the collector said. "The debtor's courage is noted. Neither alters the schedule." And it raised one grey sleeve—it had hands then, pale long-fingered hands that Petra had somehow never seen before—and touched two fingertips to Maren's forehead.

"No!" Petra lunged, and the other two collectors moved for the first time—one simply *was* in front of them, impossibly fast, blocking the way with a body that smelled of nothing at all, of empty cellars and dry wells. Petra beat their fists against it and might as well have struck a wall of cold water.

Maren didn't scream. That was the thing Petra would carry forever, the thing that would wake them at night for years afterward: their grandmother did not scream. She stood on the porch in her funeral-blue dress with the oath-book falling from her open hand, and her eyes—her fierce, iron, unbreakable eyes—went wide, and then soft, and then *empty*, like windows in a house where the fire has gone out.

The siphoning took less than a minute. It looked, horribly, like remembering. Maren's face moved through expressions in quick succession—surprise, grief, laughter, tenderness, pride, pride, pride—as if someone were flipping through the pages of her life and tearing them out gently, one by one, admiring each before discarding it.

When the collector withdrew its fingers, Maren swayed. Petra caught her before she hit the boards of the porch, and her grandmother looked up at them with polite, mild confusion, the look of a woman approached by a friendly stranger.

"Oh," Maren said pleasantly. "Hello. Are you the one who's come about the gate? My granddaughter keeps the gate. Lovely valley, isn't it? I've always thought so. Have we met?"

Petra could not speak. Something in their chest had torn loose and was thrashing.

"She doesn't remember," the lead collector said, and it was not gloating—it was worse than gloating, it was *bookkeeping*, flat and final. "Memories siphoned against penalties accrued. Sixty years of standing, surrendered. She retains her life and her love. She has lost the reason for both." The hoods inclined. "The trial stands granted, debtor. Solstice. Place of signing. Come willingly, and no further penalties accrue."

They walked away down the lane, unhurried, three grey shapes in the last light, and the frost followed them out of the yard like a dog following its master home.

Petra knelt on the porch holding their grandmother, who patted their arm kindly, the way you comfort a stranger's crying child, and asked them again if they'd come about the gate, and said it was such a lovely valley, and wasn't the sunset beautiful, she'd always loved the sunsets here, though for the life of her she couldn't say why.

Petra looked up over Maren's silvered head, at the darkening road, at the mountains going black against a bruised sky, and the grief in their chest closed around something hot and hard and shaped like a coal.

Not despair. Fury. Fury with a direction now, fury with an address, fury that knew the way beneath Mount Ceren.

Six weeks. The solstice. The place of signing.

Petra eased their grandmother down onto the bench, stood, and went inside to pack.

They would need the map. They would need the horse. And they would need Maren's seal-ring, which sat in its dish by the door—because whatever else had been torn out of her, the ring was still a keeper's ring, and Petra was going to need every scrap of keeping that was left in this family.

Outside, the first snow of winter began to fall, settling softly over the frost-killed grass, over the boundary stones, over the valley that had forgotten how to protect the people who protected it.

Petra picked up the ring, and the packing, and the rage, and began.
