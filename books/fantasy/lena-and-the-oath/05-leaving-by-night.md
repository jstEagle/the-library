## 5. Leaving by Night

The house was quiet in the way it always was after grief—too quiet, the kind of quiet that had weight and edges. Petra moved through it barefoot, one hand trailing along the wall the way they'd done since childhood, past the kitchen where Maren's kettle sat cold on its hook, past the shelf of seal-stones that no longer needed tending because Maren could not remember what they were for.

Maren slept in her chair by the hearth, wrapped in the grey shawl she'd worn every day of Petra's life. Her breathing was slow and even. Her hands lay open in her lap, palms up, as if waiting to receive something that would never come.

Three days since the collectors left her like that. Three days since Petra had knelt in the dirt beside her grandmother and shouted her name until their voice broke, while the grey-robed figures walked back through the Hollow Gate without so much as a ripple, taking with them sixty years of memories as casually as merchants carrying off a bolt of cloth.

When Maren woke, she'd looked at Petra with polite puzzlement and asked if they were the new keeper. *You have your grandmother's chin,* she'd said. *She was a good woman. Stubborn. You'll want to check the seals at dusk.*

She remembered the work. She remembered the duty. She did not remember Petra at all.

Petra stood over her now, a long time, watching the firelight move across her face. Somewhere under all that blankness was the woman who had taught them to read the gate's moods, who had made honey-cakes on the first frost, who had lied to them every single day of their life in order to keep them safe—or to keep them owned; Petra still couldn't decide which—and the fury and the love were tangled together so tightly that pulling at either thread threatened to unravel everything.

*I'm going to fix this,* Petra thought, and didn't say it aloud, because some part of them had learned in the last three days that promises spoken into empty rooms were heard by more than empty rooms.

They took three things.

The map went inside their shirt first, folded against their sternum where they could feel it with every heartbeat—Ivo's blood-marked map, its lines drawn in a hand that shook, its margins dense with cramped annotations Petra hadn't yet dared read all the way through. The blood had gone brown and flaking, but the marks beneath it seemed to shift when Petra wasn't looking directly at them, which was either imagination or magic, and Petra was done pretending the two were different things.

Second, Maren's seal-ring, lifted from the little iron box beneath the floorboard in the keeper's room. It was heavy silver, worn smooth on the inner band by four generations of fingers, set with a stone so dark it swallowed light. The ring had touched every seal on the Hollow Gate for longer than anyone alive could remember. If anything in the valley carried authority, it was this.

Third—the hardest thing—a knife from the kitchen, and bread and dried apples wrapped in cloth, because whatever waited beyond the gate, Petra refused to meet it hungry.

They wrote no note. There was no one to write to. The valley had perhaps two hundred souls, scattered across three villages, and every one of them had watched the collectors come and go and had already begun, with the gentle self-protective amnesia the valley specialized in, to forget it had happened. By spring, Ivo would be a story about a stranger lost in a storm. By summer, maybe less.

At the door, Petra stopped. Turned back. Crossed the room and pulled the blanket up over Maren's shoulders, tucking it the way Maren had once tucked it for them, and pressed their lips briefly to her forehead.

"Watch the seals at dusk," Maren murmured without waking, and Petra laughed—one cracked, wet sound that was mostly not laughter at all—and went out into the night.

* * *

The stable behind the keeper's house held three animals: old Bramble, who plowed nothing anymore but was too beloved to eat; a pair of goats with opinions; and Sorrel, the bay mare the valley used for the long trade runs to the treeline. Sorrel was fast, sure-footed, and would be missed within a day—but a day was exactly how long Petra intended to be beyond anyone's reach.

"I'm sorry," Petra whispered, fitting the bridle. "I'll bring you back. Or I won't bring me back. One of those."

Sorrel flicked an ear and let herself be saddled, which was more cooperation than Petra deserved and less than they needed.

The moon was a thin nail-paring overhead, giving just enough light to ride by and not enough to be seen by. Petra led the mare out the back way, past the well, past the boundary cairn where children were taught to leave a pebble for luck even though no one remembered why, and took the low path along the creek—the path that wound up toward the pass, toward the standing stones, toward the Hollow Gate.

They had walked this path ten thousand times. They knew every root that grabbed ankles, every stone that rolled, every bend where the wind came down off the heights smelling of snow. But tonight each familiar thing felt like it was being shown to them twice: once as it was, and once as something they might never see again. The crooked pine on the ridge. The flat rock where they'd eaten lunch at fourteen and sworn to Maren they'd never wonder about the other side. The place where, at nine years old, they'd asked *what happens if you walk through?* and Maren's face had gone so carefully, so terribly blank, and she'd said *nothing good, little wren* —and Petra had never asked again, because love is obedient, right up until it isn't.

The gate came into view under the stars.

It didn't look like much. That was the joke of it, the valley's oldest joke: the thing that guarded everyone's whole world was a gap between two standing stones, maybe eight feet tall, weathered smooth, unremarkable as any of the hundred other stone formations littering the high country. No arch, no door, no glow. Just a space between stones where the air hung very slightly wrong, like the pause before someone answers a question they've been dreading.

Petra had checked its seals every dusk for eleven years. They knew the ritual by heart: touch the north stone with the ring-hand, speak the keeper's words—words whose meaning had been lost even before Maren's memory was taken, syllables passed down like an heirloom nobody could translate—then walk the circuit, then home. Every dusk. Never cross. Never even lean close enough to feel whether the air on the far side smelled different.

Tonight there were no seals to check. Tonight the words would be spoken once, walking forward instead of circling.

Petra dismounted twenty paces out and stood with Sorrel's reins in a sweating grip.

Here was the truth they hadn't said aloud to anyone, couldn't say even to themselves in daylight: they were terrified. Not of the collectors, though the collectors were real and patient and had hollowed out the only person Petra loved. Not of the Undercourt, though a court that bought souls before their owners were born had earned fear honestly. What terrified Petra was smaller and stranger than all of it.

They were terrified of the other side of the stones.

Because Ivo had crossed too far in and burned. Because magic, according to everything the valley believed, was poison to people like them—except apparently they weren't people like them, except apparently they were the opposite, the heir, the debt, the purchased thing coming home. And because for eleven years Petra had stood at this line and wondered, and the wondering had been the truest part of them, and once they stepped through, the wondering would be over. Whatever they found, they would never again be the person who only imagined.

Some doors don't open. Some doors just remove the wall.

"Right," Petra said aloud, and their voice sounded absurdly small against the mountains. "Right."

They spoke the keeper's words—not the dusk-check version but the full cadence, the one Maren had drilled into them for emergencies, the one that ended on a falling note like a key turning. The dark stone in the ring grew warm against their finger. Between the standing stones, the air shivered, the way water shivers when a fish passes beneath the surface.

Then, before courage could fail them, Petra led Sorrel forward and stepped through the Hollow Gate.

* * *

Nothing happened.

That was the first second. A heartbeat of pure anticlimax—cold air, starlight, the same mountain smell of pine and stone and distance. Petra actually laughed, one shaky breath, half-turned to look back at the valley—

And then the world turned itself inside out.

It began as color. The darkness between the stars wasn't dark anymore; it was layered, deep, *inhabited*, violet shading into gold shading into a green that had no name in any language Petra knew. Then sound: the night was full of voices, or almost-voices, a murmur like a crowd heard through a wall, rising and falling in patterns that wanted desperately to be language. Then smell—rain that hadn't fallen yet, crushed herbs, woodsmoke from fires lit centuries ago somewhere far away. Then touch, worst of all: the air itself pressing against their skin with intent, curious, *interested*, like a thousand invisible fingertips cataloguing them.

Magic. Not the word for it—the thing itself, undiluted, everywhere, pouring into them through eyes and ears and lungs and the naked skin of their face and hands.

Petra had been blind for nineteen years and had never known it. Now the bandages were off and the light was unbearable and magnificent and they could not, could *not*, stop looking.

They heard themselves make a sound. Sorrel screamed and reared, and Petra's body—trained by eleven years of hard country—did the right things: kept the reins short, turned with her, spoke low and steady while their mind was elsewhere entirely, drowning in glory. The mare came down stamping, wild-eyed, and Petra got an arm around her neck and held on and breathed, or tried to.

Because underneath the wonder was the other thing. The dangerous thing.

It felt *good*. That was the horror of it. The magic flooding into them felt like cold water to a woman burning with fever, like the first bite of food after days of hunger, like every relief the body knows compressed into a single endless moment—and some deep animal part of Petra wanted more of it, wanted to run deeper into that colored dark and open their mouth and drink. They understood, suddenly and completely, why Ivo had crossed the line knowing it would kill him. Not desperation. Appetite. The null of the valley wasn't protection. It was fasting, lifelong and total, and now the fast was broken and the feast was infinite and Petra's own soul—the soul somebody else technically owned—was leaning toward it like a plant toward sun.

*Dangerous,* said the small cold voice that sounded like Maren. *This is why we stay behind the stones. This is exactly why.*

Petra clenched their jaw, counted breaths—ten, twenty, thirty—until the flood settled from torrent to river, from river to current, until they could stand upright again with Sorrel trembling under their hand and the world still blazing with impossible color but bearable, bearable, the way a shout becomes a hum.

Their hands were glowing.

Faintly—barely—a pale luminescence clung to their fingers like foxfire, pulsing gently in time with their pulse. Their skin drank the light in rather than casting it, and where it sank in, the ache of three sleepless nights eased, and the raw scrape on their palm from the stable latch faded to pink, then vanished.

"Oh," Petra said softly. "Oh, that's not fair."

The heir. The debt. Of course the magic wouldn't burn them. It recognized them. It had been expecting them for nineteen years.

They mounted, mostly to stop their legs from shaking, and turned Sorrel away from the gate. Behind them, the valley lay in ordinary darkness—no color, no murmur, just the honest black of a land with nothing hidden in it. From here it looked peaceful. It looked like a painting of peace, hung slightly crooked, with the price tag still attached somewhere out of frame.

Petra looked at it for a long moment. Home. The only home there had ever been.

Then they looked forward, down the road Ivo's map described, into lands where luck was sold in coins and weather came from peddlers' carts and a person with no magic-scent drew every eye like a wound draws flies. Where collectors were already hunting. Where a court beneath a mountain held a jar with Petra Vasquez's true name written on it, patient as winter, waiting for solstice.

Terror and exhilaration rode together in Petra's chest, inseparable, indistinguishable, two names for the same wild heartbeat. They were the same feeling, Petra realized. They had always been the same feeling. Wonder was just terror that hadn't decided what it wanted yet.

"Come on, Sorrel," they said, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "Let's go steal my soul back."

The mare stepped forward, and the Hollow Gate fell silent behind them, and the road ran down into a darkness full of color, and somewhere ahead—though Petra could not have known it yet, could not have known for another hundred miles—a thief named Sable was about to have a very bad night that would end, eventually, in the best decision of her life.

But that was later. For now there was only the road, and the ringing silence of a girl-shaped debt riding out from under the mountain's shadow, and the first stars of a sky that had finally, finally decided to show her what it really looked like.
