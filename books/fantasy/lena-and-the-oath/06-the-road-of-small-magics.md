## 6. The Road of Small Magics

The first thing the outer world taught Petra was that it never stopped talking.

Not people—though there were plenty of those—but the world itself. The valley had been silent the way a held breath is silent, and Petra had mistaken that silence for emptiness all their life. It was not empty. It was *muffled*. And now, three days past the Hollow Gate, every step they took landed in a world that hummed.

They saw it first at a roadside well outside a village whose name sign had been painted over so many times it was just a grey rectangle of wood. A woman drew water with a bucket that glowed faintly blue at the seams, and when she set it down, the water stayed perfectly still, not a ripple on its surface, as though the bucket had promised the water it would be safe. An old man sat by the well turning a copper coin over his knuckles; each time the coin passed his ring finger, it flickered like a fish scale catching sun. Luck-coin, a carter told Petra later, when they asked too directly and he looked at them too long. Cost you a week's wages, lasted a year, and if you dropped it in a well you'd have the worst luck of your life, so don't.

Petra learned to stop asking directly.

They learned instead to watch. By the second day they had catalogued a whole litany of small magics that no one in the valley had ever thought to mention, because no one in the valley had ever seen them. Truth-knots sold in bundles of ten: tie one around your wrist before a negotiation and you could feel a lie coming the way you felt weather in an old scar. Weather-sellers worked the crossroads with wagons stacked with lidded clay jars—a jar of rain for farmers, a jar of stillness for brides who didn't want wind ruining their veils, a jar of fog, according to the chalkboard sign, *for those with something to leave behind*. A boy ran alongside their stolen horse for half a mile trying to sell Petra a spelled ribbon that would keep saddle-sores away, and when Petra finally bought one out of sheer exhaustion, the ribbon did work, warm against the horse's back like a living thing, and Petra had to fight down a laugh and a sob in the same breath.

This was what lay beyond the gate. Not sorcery. Not the towering, terrible magic of Maren's warnings. *Chores.* Magic doing dishes and curing chilblains and keeping flies off meat. The Undercourt's shadow fell across all of it—Petra could feel that now, a cold undertow beneath the warmth, the sense that every petty wonder was a coin spent from some vast account—but on the surface, the world was bright and busy and utterly indifferent to them.

Which should have made them invisible.

It did the opposite.

* * *

It happened first at the well village. Petra led the horse—it had a name, Bristle, which the previous owner had burned into its stall door and which Petra felt guilty about but not guilty enough to return it—to the trough, and a woman selling plums stopped mid-haggle and stared. Not at the horse. At Petra. Her eyes went wide, then narrow, then she turned and said something quietly to the old man with the luck-coin, and he looked up, and then *he* stared, and then a child staring because adults were staring, and within the space of thirty heartbeats Petra stood at the center of a ring of attention so complete they could hear their own pulse.

"Where you from, stranger?" the plum woman asked. Not unkindly. That was the worst part. She asked the way you'd ask someone with a wound you couldn't look away from.

"The hill country," Petra said, which was true enough, and moved Bristle along.

But the stares followed them down the street, and at the far end a man stepped out of a doorway to watch them pass, and he didn't blink once.

That night, camped in a stand of birches well off the road, Petra tried to understand it. They built no fire. They ate hard bread and dried apples and thought.

The valley was null. No magic entered it—that was the whole point, that was the oath, that was the cage dressed as a sanctuary. Which meant Petra had been raised in a place where magic simply *did not exist*, and whatever it was that let people here carry luck-coins and sell jars of fog, Petra had none of it. Not none the way a poor man has no gold. None the way a hole has no wall. To everyone in this humming, whispering world, Petra must read as a gap. A silence shaped like a person. Every charm within twenty paces would go quiet around them, the way candles lean toward or away from a draft. The luck-coin man's coin had stopped flickering when Petra walked past—he'd looked down at it with genuine alarm.

They were not invisible here. They were a *hole* in the visible world, and holes draw the eye worse than anything.

"You're going to get us killed," Petra told Bristle. The horse flicked an ear and went back to sleep, having no opinion on the matter.

* * *

The town was called Ferrow's Crossing, and it was the first place large enough to have walls, and walls meant gates, and gates meant someone looking closely at who came through them.

Petra considered going around. The map—Ivo's map, blood-marked, its margins full of cramped handwriting Petra hadn't yet dared read properly—showed a road through Ferrow's Crossing and a road around it, and the road around it added four days through marshland marked with a symbol Petra chose to believe meant reeds rather than teeth. Four days mattered. The solstice was coming down on them like a closing fist. But four days also meant four more days of being a walking absence on open road, and Petra's paranoia had begun to whisper that the collectors wouldn't need to chase them at all. They'd only need to ask the road. Ask the luck-sellers and the toll-keepers and the children who noticed strange things. *Have you seen a person who isn't there?*

So: through the gate.

The gatekeeper was bored, which helped. He barely glanced at Bristle's papers—stolen along with the horse, and Petra had practiced the owner's signature until it almost looked confident—and waved them into Ferrow's Crossing with the enthusiasm of a man waving away flies.

The town swallowed them, and for an hour Petra thought they might actually manage it. Market streets, awnings, the smell of frying onions and hot metal. Magic everywhere in miniature: a knife-sharpener whose wheel sang a note that made dull edges weep themselves keen; a moneylender behind a lattice of knotted red cord, each knot a contract, tugging one absently while he counted; a fountain where the water rose in slow spirals because the mason had loved the sound of rain and asked a friend to make the water remember it. Petra kept their hood up, kept their eyes down, kept their hands in their pockets where no one could see how badly they shook.

Then they saw the notice board.

Every town had one, apparently—Petra had passed two already without stopping, because notices meant law and law meant attention. But this board stood at the mouth of the market square where four roads met, and it was impossible not to see, because the top third of it had been papered over in sheets of good white vellum, dozens deep, layered like feathers on a bird.

Each sheet bore a face.

Petra stopped walking. Stopped breathing. The market roared on around them—haggling, laughing, a fiddle somewhere—and Petra stood fixed before the board as the noise bent and warped around the ringing in their ears.

The face was theirs. Their own face, rendered in fine black ink by someone better at likenesses than any artist in the valley had ever been: the sharp jaw, the crooked brow, the particular way their hair fell over one ear. Below the portrait, in a script so elegant it looked like it was apologizing for its content:

*BY ORDER OF THE UNDERCOURT OF CEREN*

*THE BEARER OWES A DEBT OF THE FIRST RANK.*

*TO BE RETURNED WHOLE AND UNSPOKEN TO THE COURT BENEATH THE MOUNTAIN BEFORE WINTER'S TURN.*

*REWARD: ONE BOON, FREELY GRANTED, NO QUESTIONS ASKED.*

*DO NOT HARM THE GOODS. DO NOT SPEAK ITS NAME ALOUD. IT HAS NONE THAT BINDS.*

*It has none that binds.* Petra read the line four times. Their name—the name Maren had given them, the name carved nowhere, written nowhere, spoken only in the valley—was worthless here. The court knew it. Of course it knew it. You didn't put a debt on paper unless you'd already measured exactly what you held.

And the reward. *One boon, freely granted.* Not coin. Not land. A boon from the Undercourt itself, no questions asked—which meant every desperate soul in this half of the world had reason to be looking. The farmer whose crops failed. The mother with a sick child. The thief in debt to worse things. Anyone could hand Petra over and ask the court for anything, and the court, patient and courteous and terrible, would grant it.

There were other papers beneath the white ones, older layers showing at the torn edges. Petra leaned close despite themselves. Older faces. Dozens of them. Years of them. Some of the vellum had gone brown and brittle, portraits fading to ghosts of ghosts. The court had been posting these for a long time. The court had been *hunting* for a long time.

Ivo's face was not among them. Petra checked twice, hating themselves for checking. Whatever Ivo had been—the previous heir, the coward, the courier who'd bled out against the boundary stones with a map in his hands—he had never made it onto the board. Either he'd hidden too well, or the court had never bothered to want him back.

*The heir comes home,* he'd whispered. He hadn't said *welcome* home.

"Looking for someone?"

Petra spun. A woman stood at their shoulder—middle-aged, flour on her forearms, a baker's apron, a smile that reached her eyes and stopped precisely at the surface. She nodded at the board. "Most folk who stand that long are looking for someone."

"Just reading," Petra said. Their voice came out flat and wrong, and they heard it come out wrong, and the woman heard it too.

"Mm." She looked at the board, then at Petra's face, then at the board again, and something moved behind her eyes—something quick and calculating and gone. "Funny thing about these notices," she said, conversational, wiping her hands on her apron. "My neighbor swears the faces change. Says the ink moves at night. Says the court redraws them as the goods travel, so the picture stays true." She smiled again. "Wouldn't know myself. Never seen one move."

"I wouldn't know either," Petra said.

"No," the woman agreed pleasantly. "Course not." And she walked away toward her stall, unhurried, and did not look back, and somehow that was more frightening than if she'd screamed.

* * *

Petra bought supplies with hands that wanted to run: bread, cheese, a skin of wine, oats for Bristle, a second cloak in a color nothing like their own. They paid with valley coin—Maren's emergency purse, silver stamped with the Hollow Gate's seal—and watched the moneylender's apprentice turn one piece over with a frown before shrugging and biting it. Old silver. Pure silver. It passed, but the frown lodged under Petra's skin like a splinter. Everything they carried was wrong here. Everything they *were* was wrong here.

They left through the north gate at the gray hour before dusk, when the watch changed and the gatekeeper yawned, and they did not stop until Ferrow's Crossing was a smudge of chimney smoke behind them and the marsh road—or the reed road, or possibly the tooth road—opened ahead in the failing light.

Camp, when they finally made it, was joyless. Cold ground, no fire, bread eaten in the dark. Petra sat with their back against Bristle's warm flank and took out the map, because the map was the only thing in the world that was theirs, and unfolded it across their knees.

The main route to Mount Ceren ran bold in faded ink: south along the King's Way, then east at the shrine of some saint Petra didn't recognize, then into the highlands. Eleven days, maybe twelve, for a rider pushing hard. The solstice was nineteen days off. It could be done. It could be done, if nothing went wrong, and everything had already gone wrong twice before breakfast.

It was the margins that stopped them.

They'd skimmed them before—in the valley, in the terrible first hours after Ivo died, when the words swam—but tonight, alone, with the bounty notice fresh behind their eyes, Petra read them properly for the first time. Ivo's handwriting was small and slanted and got smaller as the notes went on, as though the writer were running out of room and courage at the same rate.

*Do not take the King's Way past Harrow Ford. Collectors wait at bridges. They do not eat, sleep, or tire. They count.*

*The court does not post the notices for the reward. It posts them to teach the roads your face. By the third town, strangers will know you better than your mother did.*

*I hid for six years. Six years, and the only reason they lost me is that I stopped being anyone. No name, no fire, no face. You cannot hide a hole by standing in a crowd. You must become the dark it sits in.*

*If you are reading this, I am sorry. I was supposed to come home and I ran. Whoever you are, you are braver than me or you have less choice, and either way the map will hold. Trust the thief in Ashmarket. Trust no one else. Trust me least of all.*

Petra sat very still for a long time.

*A thief in Ashmarket.* So Ivo had known. Had planned for this, years ago, laying escape routes like snares and then fleeing from his own traps. And the rest of it—the roads, the counting, the lesson of the notices—landed now with the weight of confirmation. The board in Ferrow's Crossing hadn't been a warning. It had been a *net*. Every eye that had lingered on Petra since the gate, every too-long stare, every pleasant question—the court hadn't needed to send collectors yet. It had deputized the entire world.

Paranoia came up in Petra like floodwater, cold and total. Every traveler on the horizon was a hunter. Every kindness was a hook. They caught themselves cataloguing shadows, flinching at owl-calls, gripping the knife Maren had packed until their knuckles ached, and somewhere in the middle of the long dark night of it, crouched in the reeds with their own face drying on a notice board miles behind them, Petra hit the bottom of the fear and found something underneath it.

Anger. Clean and banked and burning steady.

The court had sold them before they were born. The valley had caged them to pay for it. Maren had loved them and lied to them in the same breath, and now the whole outer world was being taught their face so that any stranger could sell them back like a runaway ox. Every part of Petra's life had been arranged by other people for other people's purposes, and the one thing—*the one thing*—no one had arranged, no one had purchased, no one had posted on a board, was what Petra did next.

They folded the map. They checked Bristle's girth in the dark by touch. South, then, but not the King's Way—there'd be another road, smaller, meaner, unwritten. There was always another road. And beyond it, Ashmarket, and a thief who owed Ivo something, or owed the world something, or could be made to owe Petra.

Let them post the face, Petra thought, swinging up into the saddle as the sky went from black to iron. Let the whole world learn it by heart.

By solstice, they'd learn what it cost to try and collect.

Bristle picked her way south through the reeds, and behind them, far off on the night horizon, a light kindled on the road to Ferrow's Crossing—small, gray-white, and moving. Not a lantern. Lanterns flickered.

Whatever that was, it was counting.
