## 7. The Thief Who Owes a Debt

Sable Venn had three rules, and she'd broken all of them before noon.

Rule one: never work a job you don't understand. Rule two: never trust a client who won't show their face. Rule three—her mother's rule, the one that had kept her alive through six years in Ashmarket's underbelly—*never, ever steal for the same buyer twice.*

She was crouched on the roof of a dye-house in Lowmarsh, watching a soul-broker's counting house through a gap in the chimney pots, and she was thinking about rule three with the particular bitterness of someone who had already broken it four times.

The broker's name was Quillon Fess. He dealt in luck and small futures, the kind of magic that came bottled and cheap. His counting house was a squat stone building with iron shutters and a door that only opened for people who could pay. Sable had stolen from it twice already. The first time had been easy money. The second time had been leverage—a ledger page she still kept folded against her ribs, because Quillon's handwriting appeared in it next to a debt that belonged to her brother.

The third time, she told herself, would be the last. She just needed to see what he kept in the back room.

She was halfway down the drainpipe when the stranger walked into the square below her.

Sable noticed them the way she noticed everything: in pieces, catalogued without effort. A traveler in road-dusted clothes, moving wrong for this part of town—not slinking, not swaggering, just *walking*, like they hadn't yet learned that walking openly in Lowmarsh was an invitation. No visible weapons. A satchel worn high and tight against the body, the way people carried things they couldn't afford to lose. And eyes that kept snagging on everything—the luck-coins strung over the fountain, the weather-seller hawking a jar of clear morning, the truth-knots braided into the tavern lintels—as if magic itself were new to them.

Which was impossible. Nobody in the Marches looked at magic like that. You grew up with it the way you grew up with rain.

*Outsider,* Sable thought, and then, because her trade had made her cruel and curious in equal measure: *Interesting outsider.*

She should have gone through the window. She had maybe forty minutes before the counting house changed its watch, and Quillon's back room wouldn't wait. Instead she found herself drifting along the rooftops, following the stranger at an angle, telling herself it was reconnaissance. Telling herself a lot of things, lately.

The stranger stopped at the notice board outside the tollhouse annex, and Sable saw their whole body go rigid.

She knew what was pinned there. She'd seen the notices go up three days ago, fresh ink on grey paper, and she'd laughed at first because the reward was absurd—*a soul unpriced, terms negotiable*—and then she'd stopped laughing when she read the description. *No magic-scent. Unknown origin. Carries documents.*

The stranger stood in front of their own face and didn't move for a long time.

Sable dropped from the roofline into the shadow of the annex wall, close enough now to hear the stranger breathing. Up close, the wrongness was worse. There was nothing to *read* off them—no aura, no hum, no scent of craft. In a city where every person glowed faintly with what they were, this one was a hole shaped like a person. It made Sable's teeth ache.

"You're staring at yourself," Sable said.

The stranger flinched hard enough that Sable almost felt bad about it. Almost. "I'm not—" They turned, and up close their face did something complicated: fear, calculation, and underneath both, a stubbornness Sable recognized because she wore the same one every day. "Those aren't me."

"No," Sable agreed. "They're a drawing of you. Which is worse." She nodded at the notice. "Grey paper means Undercourt bounty. The collectors don't post those themselves—they hire it done. Someone local is being paid to watch for you, which means by tomorrow there'll be three more boards with your face on them, and by the week after, every cutpurse between here and the mountains will know exactly what a person with no magic-scent looks like."

The stranger's hand went to their satchel. Not a weapon draw—an instinctive protection. "Who are you?"

"Sable Venn." She let the pause stretch, enjoying the way uncertainty sat on their face. "And you're either very brave or very stupid to be standing in the open. Which is it?"

"I'm looking for passage east," the stranger said, and here was the interesting thing: they lied badly. Their voice stayed level but their eyes went to the left, hunting for the truth and not finding it. "Toward Mount Ceren."

"Nobody goes toward Ceren. People go *around* Ceren, the way water goes around a stone." Sable stepped closer, lowering her voice. "But you're not going toward Ceren. You're going *under* it. Aren't you."

Something shifted in the stranger's posture. Cornered-animal to something colder. "Why do you say that?"

"Because I've seen that map." Sable watched them go very still. "The one in your satchel. Blood-marked, old vellum, with writing in the margins that isn't writing—it's script. Court script. I'd bet my hands on it."

Now the stranger moved like a fighter after all—one hand shooting out to grab Sable's wrist, grip stronger than their frame promised. "How do you know that?"

"Ow. Because I stole for the Undercourt once." Sable didn't pull away; pulling away showed fear, and fear was currency she refused to spend. "Two years. Retrieval work. Little jobs—documents, keys, one memorable night involving a judge's spare face. I was good. Then I got smart, which is different from good, and I left." She twisted free. "You want to know how I know court script? Because I carried it. Page by page, out of places that kill thieves. So either you tell me what's under that mountain that matters to you, or you find another guide, and I promise you, whoever else offers will sell you to the collectors before you clear the city walls."

The stranger studied her for a long moment. Weighing her. Sable let them—she knew exactly what they'd find, because she'd built it: a scrappy, sharp-tongued street thief, harmless as a stray cat. What they wouldn't find was the ledger page against her ribs or the name written on it in Quillon Fess's careful hand.

*Tommin Venn. Soul sold, price paid, delivery solstice.*

Her brother. Sold by his own hand, two years ago, to pay a debt Sable had brought home on her shoulders like a dog she couldn't stop feeding. He'd signed the contract with a smile, because Tommin smiled at everything, even the end of himself.

"I'm Petra," the stranger said finally. "And I'm going under Ceren to take something back. Something that belongs to me." A beat. "My soul."

Sable blinked. Of all the answers she'd braced for, that wasn't one. "Your soul."

"It was sold before I was born. There's a bargain—a valley, a gate, generations of keepers. My family bought safety with me. The collectors are coming to collect at solstice." Petra's jaw tightened. "I mean to get there first."

Sable whistled low. "That's either the saddest story I've heard this year or the most expensive lie, and honestly I can't tell which." But she could. That was the trouble. The blood-marked map, the hollow where a magic-scent should be, the way the bounty notices described a person who was *terms negotiable*—it all fit. This was the heir. The one the grey-robes had been whispering about since the notices went up. The one whose retrieval would set Sable up for life.

Or the one whose cause might actually matter.

She hated that the second thought had snuck in. Sentiment was a leak in a boat; she'd watched it sink better people than her.

"Here's my offer," Sable said. "Passage east. I know the roads the collectors don't watch, the tollhouses that take coin instead of confessions, the fords that aren't drowned. I can get you to the Drowned Stair by dark of the moon. In exchange—"

"In exchange for what? I have almost nothing."

"You have access." Sable held up a finger before Petra could argue. "Undercourt inventory. Every soul in those vaults is shelved, labeled, logged. You're going to be inside. All I want—I want you to find one more jar while you're stealing yours back. One name. Tommin Venn. Bring him out with us, and I'm yours until the job's done. Locks, lies, roads, all of it."

Petra's expression flickered—something painful crossing it, quickly buried. "Your brother."

"My brother." Sable kept her voice flat, which took work. "He sold himself to cover debts I made. So technically he's my fault, and I collect my faults. That's the deal. Take it or don't, but decide fast, because that notice has your face on it and I'm not the only one in this square who reads."

Petra looked at the board again—at their own inked eyes staring back—and Sable watched the decision happen behind them, the same way she'd once watched Tommin sign his name: someone choosing a terrible thing because every other thing was worse.

"All right," Petra said. "Your brother comes out too. But I'm not promising miracles. If the vault doesn't have him—"

"It has him. Everything's shelved down there. Everything." Sable stuck out her hand, and Petra shook it, and she felt the strange null-coolness of their skin and suppressed a shiver. "Congratulations, keeper. You just hired the second-best thief in Lowmarsh."

"Who's the first?"

"Currently in prison, thanks to a job I didn't plan well. Another reason I owe the universe some penance." Sable jerked her chin toward the alley behind the annex. "Come on. We leave tonight, before the watchers change shift, and we do it through the tannery district because nobody follows anyone into a smell like that."

They walked. And Sable, three steps ahead, doing her best impression of a woman with no ulterior motives, felt the ledger page press against her ribs with every stride.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said. Here was the thing she'd tell no one, maybe ever:

Quillon Fess wasn't just a broker. He was a *finder*. When the Undercourt wanted someone located—someone with no magic-scent, someone carrying documents—his was one of the names the grey paper went to. Sable had seen the request slip across his desk last month, before she'd known why it mattered. She'd even read the terms.

*Delivery preferred alive. Solstice deadline. Bonus for the map.*

She was leading the Undercourt's most valuable fugitive straight toward the man who'd pay best for them, and she was doing it because the route east ran through Quillon's territory, and because she'd convinced herself she could walk that knife-edge without falling.

Two liars, she thought, glancing back at Petra, who was watching the rooftops with the wariness of someone learning a whole new world's dangers. Two liars walking east, each holding back the one truth that would end the other's trust.

*Just until the Stair,* she told herself. *After that, I'll tell them. After that, it won't matter.*

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, a boy of perhaps fifteen peeled away from the notice board and began to follow—grey ribbon tied around his wrist, the mark of Quillon's runners, his steps light and patient as a debt accruing interest.

* * *

The tannery district at dusk smelled like a crime against nature, which was precisely why Sable loved it. Lime pits and rotting hides drove off everyone with money or sense, leaving the alleys to rats, smugglers, and one very specific drainage tunnel that Sable had personally widened with a shovel over the course of a damp and miserable winter.

"The tunnel runs under the east wall," she explained, prying up a grate hidden beneath a stack of curing frames. "It lets out past the tollhouse ring, in the reed flats. From there we pick up the drover's road. Three days to Thornford, if the weather holds, and I've paid the weather not to."

"You can *pay* weather?" Petra asked, easing down into the tunnel after her.

"You can pay anything. That's the whole problem with this world, keeper." Sable lit a stub of candle and started walking, bent double. "Money, memory, luck, love—all currency. The trick is knowing what you're spending and what it costs the other person."

"That sounds like something a broker would say."

"Sable Venn, saying broker things." She put mock horror in her voice. "Careful, or I'll start charging you by the mile."

Petra laughed—a short, startled sound, like they'd surprised themselves. In the candlelight, for half a second, they looked young. Younger than Sable, somehow, though she doubted that was true. People who'd grown up inside oaths aged strangely.

They walked in silence for a while, boots splashing shallow water, and Sable found herself talking anyway, because silence gave her too much room to think about ledger pages and grey ribbons.

"How does it work?" Petra asked eventually. "Stealing souls. Do they... take you somewhere? Is there a ritual?"

"There's a desk." Sable's laugh came out bitter. "That's the worst part. No smoke, no chanting. A nice office above a nice shop, a contract in plain language, a signature. Tommin thought he was pawning a *piece* of himself—you know, like the street sellers claim. 'Sell your bad luck, keep your dreams.' But the contracts don't take pieces. They take the root. He signed, and he walked out smiling, and by the time I understood what he'd actually given up, the debt was registered and final." Her voice hardened. "The courts are civilized about it. That's how they win. Nobody fights a polite man with paperwork."

Petra was quiet for several steps. "My grandmother sold me before I existed. She thought she was saving everyone."

"And was she?"

"The valley never fell. Nobody starved, nobody was taken. Twenty years of peace." Petra's voice caught on something. "I used to think she was the strongest person I knew."

Sable knew that tone. It was the sound of someone standing in the ruins of a story they'd built their life on. She could have offered comfort. Instead she offered honesty, because it was rarer and she was feeling reckless.

"For what it's worth—my mother sold *me* a piece once. Not my soul. My name. Gave it to a river-god to settle a gambling debt, so for six years I legally didn't exist, and I learned every back way through every wall because doors wouldn't recognize me." She shrugged. "Families are just the first people who sell you. The question is whether they do it lovingly."

"That's horrible."

"That's Tuesday." Sable held up the candle as the tunnel forked. "Left here. And keeper—if we're going to do this, you need to learn to lie better. Your eyes go left when you fib. Any collector worth their grey robe will read you like a broadsheet."

"They won't need to read me. They'll smell that I'm *nothing*."

"Nothing has advantages." Sable tapped her own temple. "No scent means no craft can grip you. Charms slide off. Compulsions fail. Half the traps in the Undercourt run on compulsion, which means you're the only one of us who can walk through them sober. You're not a flaw in the design, keeper. You're a loophole."

Petra stared at her. "Has anyone ever told you that you're a strange comfort?"

"Frequently. Usually right before I rob them." She grinned, and meant maybe a third of it.

The tunnel ended at a rotted ladder and a hatch crusted with reed-mud. Above, the sky was going violet, the first stars pricking through over the flat black line of the east. Somewhere out there, three days' ride away, the mountains waited with their polite, civilized hunger.

Sable climbed up first, pushed the hatch, and froze.

Lanterns. Four of them, spaced across the reed flats in a careful arc—the professional spacing of people who'd done this before. And in the center of the arc, unhurried, a figure sitting on a camp stool as though waiting for a theatrical performance to begin, grey ribbon bright at his wrist.

Quillon Fess's youngest runner looked up at her and smiled with genuine, friendly apology.

"Evening, Miss Venn," he said. "Mr. Fess would like a word about your outstanding balance. He says—" the boy consulted a card, though Sable was certain he'd memorized it— "*bring the heir and the map, and we'll call everything square. Even Tommin's.*"

Beside her, in the hatch, Petra went utterly still.

Sable stood in the reeds with her heart hammering and her mind doing what it did best: running cons at speed. Every exit. Every lie. Every version of the next ten seconds.

There weren't enough.

"Well," she said lightly, to Petra, without turning around. "Funny story. Remember how I said families are the first people who sell you?"
