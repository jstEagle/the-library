## 8. Ashmarket

The road to Ashmarket smelled of woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath—burnt sugar, maybe, or the ghost of it. Sable had told Petra to expect a town. What they found instead was a wound in the hills, a bowl of land where the trees grew crooked and pale, and in the bottom of the bowl, tents and stalls and lanterns strung on wires of copper, thousands of them, burning without flame.

"They're memory-lights," Sable said, walking with her hood up and her hands in her pockets like she owned the place and was embarrassed about it. "Every lantern's someone's best day. Wedding mornings. First snows. The day a child was born. People sell the big ones when they're desperate, and the brokers string them up so everyone can see what's for sale."

Petra stopped at the lip of the hill and looked down at all that borrowed light.

Below, the market moved like a slow river. Vendors called out from stalls stacked with glass bottles, each one glowing faintly from within—a blue one here, a gold one there, a green that pulsed like a heartbeat. Buyers held bottles up to their eyes. Some wept. Some laughed. One woman stood perfectly still for a long moment, then set her bottle down with great care, as if returning something fragile to its shelf, and walked away lighter than she'd come.

"That's how it works?" Petra asked. "You just... drink someone else's life?"

"You rent it, mostly. Buying outright is expensive." Sable glanced sideways at them. "And you'd know that soon enough, if you're still planning what I think you're planning."

"I need the location. The Drowned Stair, the dark of the moon, whatever the ritual needs. The collectors will be at every crossroads between here and Mount Ceren by now. Someone in this market knows the way under the mountain, and no one gives away knowledge for free."

"No," Sable agreed. "No one gives away anything for free here. That's rather the point."

* * *

They descended into the market as the light failed—which, Petra realized after a while, meant nothing at all changed, because the lanterns burned regardless, and the sky above the bowl had gone the color of old pewter. The noise rose around them: haggling in three languages, a fiddler playing a tune that made Petra's chest ache before they understood why, a crier announcing auctions of "pristine childhoods, unspoiled, verified."

"Rules," Sable said quietly, steering them through the crowd with a hand at their elbow. "One: don't touch anything glowing unless you mean to buy it. Two: don't tell anyone what you are. Three—" she hesitated, "—don't let anyone look too closely at your eyes. Memory-brokers can read residue. If they see you've never sold anything, never bought anything, never even dreamed near magic, they'll know you're from the null-lands, and there are people in this market who'd pay good coin for a soul with no scent at all. Clean stock."

Petra's skin crawled. "Clean stock."

"It's a compliment here. Try not to take it personally."

The stalls blurred past. A man sold bottled courage off a cart, three swallows for a silver piece. A woman with silver-threaded braids traded in last words—she claimed to have collected the final sentences of forty dying kings. A child no older than ten sat behind a table of small brown vials labeled in careful letters: *one ordinary afternoon*, *the smell of rain on a roof*, *mother humming*. Petra stared at that table longer than they meant to.

"The poor sell small," Sable said, following their gaze. Her voice had gone flat, careful. "It lasts longer that way. Sell your wedding day and you'll grieve it forever. Sell an afternoon of rain and you only feel a little thinner."

"And the rich?"

"The rich buy." She nodded toward a pavilion at the market's heart, larger than the others, draped in deep violet silk, its lanterns burning white and steady while everything around them flickered. "The House of Ledger. That's where the real brokers work. That's where you'll find someone who knows what lies beneath Ceren—and where you'll pay through the nose for it."

* * *

The broker who received them was named Vess, according to the brass plate on her desk, and she was the oldest person Petra had ever seen—not wrinkled, exactly, but worn smooth, like a stone that had been in a river so long it had forgotten being a rock. Her eyes were pale grey and utterly patient. When Petra entered the violet pavilion, Vess did not look up from her ledger for a full minute. When she finally did, she looked at Petra the way a jeweler looks at an uncut stone.

"Sit," she said. "You want information. Everyone who comes in wanting information has money, and everyone who has money is lying about how much. So let's skip to the part where you show me what you actually have."

"I have coin," Petra said.

Vess smiled without warmth. "You have eleven silver pieces and a ring that isn't yours, hidden under your glove. Coin won't cover what you're asking. Sit down."

Petra sat. Across the room, Sable leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed, radiating the studied boredom of someone who wanted very badly to listen.

"Now." Vess folded her hands. "You want the way beneath Mount Ceren. Don't bother looking surprised; I've sold that route twice this year, and both buyers were headed for the solstice opening, and neither came back. I can give you the path—the Drowned Stair, the dark of the moon, the toll you'll owe at the threshold. It's worth, in fair trade, four hundred crowns. You don't have four hundred crowns. But you do have something rarer than coin."

She reached beneath her desk and produced a small glass bottle, empty, its stopper wrapped in lead foil. She set it on the desk between them with a click.

"A memory," Petra said. Their mouth had gone dry.

"A memory. Something true. Something that costs you." Vess's voice softened into something almost kind, which was worse. "That's the secret nobody tells you about this place, keeper's child. We don't buy recollections. Anyone's head is full of those, worthless as gravel. We buy weight. Show me a memory you could live without and I'll offer you bread money. Show me one you can't, and we'll talk about mountains."

Petra's hand drifted, without permission, to the seal-ring under their glove.

* * *

They almost walked out twice.

The first time was when Vess explained the mechanics: the extraction took minutes, the loss was permanent, and no, there was no way to get it back, not ever, not with all the magic in the world. "What's given to the ledger stays in the ledger," she said. "We're not thieves, whatever they say about us. Thieves leave you hoping."

The second time was when Petra closed their eyes and went looking, deliberately, for the first time in their life, through everything Maren had left them—and found that the deepest thing, the heaviest thing, the thing with the most weight, was also the thing they could least afford to lose.

It was an evening in early autumn, nine years old, maybe ten. Maren had caught them sketching the gate-seals in the dirt instead of doing chores, and instead of scolding, she had knelt down beside them in the dust and taken the stick from their hand. *If you're going to draw them,* she'd said, *draw them right.* And she had shown them—her rough fingers over theirs, tracing each sigil in order, speaking the words low, half-whispered, the way you speak things you're afraid of waking. The first seal for keeping. The second for knowing. The third for naming what stands at the door. Petra had gotten the third one wrong three times and Maren had laughed—not her usual laugh, but a young one, startled out of her—and the evening light had come through the valley in that particular gold it only wore in autumn, and Petra had thought, with the whole certainty of a child: *this is the best hour of my life.*

They had been right. They simply hadn't known yet that they would end up selling it.

"There's a memory," Petra said at last. "Of my grandmother teaching me the gate-seals."

Vess went very still. Then, slowly, she opened the ledger again and turned to a fresh page, and her pen hovered. "The gate-seals," she repeated. "The Hollow Gate seals."

"Does it matter?"

"Everything matters, or nothing does." Vess looked up, and for the first time her patience cracked into something sharper—hunger, quickly mastered. "A memory of the Hollow Gate's working, carried out of the null-lands in a living head. Do you understand what that's worth? There are collectors in this market tonight who would empty their coffers for a single syllable of that oath. There are courts that would—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. Folded her hands again. "I can't offer you four hundred crowns for it. I can offer you more than that. I can offer you the mountain."

"The route. Everything. The Stair, the moon, the tolls, the ritual's terms."

"The route, complete, verified against my own sources. Plus one thing more." Vess leaned forward. "There's a man in this market who came back from beneath Ceren. The only buyer I've ever sold that route to who returned. He's been trying to sell me his memories of the journey for a month, and I haven't bought, because his price is insane and his mind is half-gone. But he knows things. Buy his memories and you'll walk into that court knowing what waits there. Consider it a bonus for the quality of your goods."

Petra's heart was pounding. It was everything they needed. It was laid out on the desk like a feast.

"And the cost," they said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "When it's done. Will I know it's missing?"

"Oh, yes." Vess said it gently, the way a surgeon says *you'll feel some pressure*. "You'll know something was there. A shape with nothing in it. You'll remember that you loved her—you'll remember loving her your whole life—but the evening itself will be gone. The dust. The stick. Her laugh. You'll keep the fact and lose the hour." She slid the empty bottle an inch closer. "Most people find, afterward, that they wish they'd chosen differently. And most people, offered the choice again, choose the same. That's what desperation is."

Across the room, Sable had uncrossed her arms. "Petra," she said quietly. "Don't. We'll find another way. There's always another way."

"There isn't," Petra said, "and we both know it. Solstice is nineteen days away."

They thought of Maren sitting hollow-eyed in the valley house, her memories siphoned into grey robes, her seventy years of fierce love scraped down to blankness. They thought of Ivo bleeding out against the boundary stones, spending his last breath to pass the burden on. Every step of this road had been paid for by someone else. At least this time, Petra thought, they would be the one paying.

At least this time it would be theirs to give.

"Do it," they said.

* * *

The extraction chair was old leather, cracked and re-stitched many times, and it smelled faintly of other people's lives. Vess worked with a pair of thin silver instruments and a focus Petra couldn't follow—one moment she was adjusting a lens, the next she was inside their head, gentle as fog.

*Think of it,* she murmured, though her lips didn't move. *All of it. Don't hold back. If you ration it, I'll take the dregs, and the dregs hurt worse.*

So Petra gave it up willingly, which was the only mercy available. The dust. The gold light. Maren kneeling, her braid coming loose, the stick moving under both their hands. *The third seal for naming what stands at the door.* Getting it wrong. Getting it wrong. Getting it—

The laugh. Startled, young, unguarded.

—and then a sensation like a page turning, and the evening was on the other side of it.

Petra sat in the chair, breathing. The pavilion looked the same. Vess was stoppering a small bottle that glowed warm gold, autumn-colored, and setting it into a velvet-lined case with the reverence of a priest handling relics. Petra watched the little light settle among the other lights and felt a shape open inside them, clean-edged and empty, like a tooth lost from the back of the mouth. They knew something had been there. They knew they had loved her—they loved her still, the love was intact, vast, undiminished. But somewhere in the middle of that love there was now a hole exactly the size of one golden evening, and they would carry it for the rest of their life, and they would never once be able to visit it.

"Well?" Vess asked, watching their face with clinical interest. "How bad?"

"Like losing a tooth," Petra said. "You keep poking at where it was."

"Mm. That's the honest ones' answer. Liars say it doesn't hurt." She pushed a folded paper across the desk—the route, written in a tight, precise hand, along with a slip of paper bearing an address in the market's eastern rows. "Kestrel Row, the blue door with no handle. Ask for Tammas. Tell him Vess sent you and that his price is finally acceptable—he'll understand. He'll sell you what he saw under the mountain." She paused. "Fair warning, keeper's child. He came back wrong. Whatever the court showed him, it's still showing him. Don't buy more than you need."

Petra stood. Their legs held. Outside the pavilion, the memory-lanterns flickered in their thousands, and every one of them was somebody's irreplaceable hour, hanging on a wire for strangers to browse.

Sable fell into step beside them. She didn't say *are you all right*, which Petra was grateful for. Instead she said, "You didn't hesitate."

"I hesitated plenty. You just weren't watching at the right moment." Petra unfolded the route map as they walked, scanning the tight handwriting. *Drowned Stair. Dark of the moon. Six days hence.* Their stomach tightened. "Six days. We have six days to reach the mountain."

"And a madman to see first." Sable glanced back once at the House of Ledger, her expression unreadable. "You know what bothers me? Vess knew what the seals were worth the second you said it. Which means she knows what you are. Brokers like her don't sit on information like that, Petra. They sell it."

As if summoned by the words, a hush moved through the market behind them—not silence, but a ripple, heads turning, conversations dying mid-sentence. Petra followed the crowd's gaze toward the eastern edge of the bowl, where two figures in grey robes stood at the treeline, motionless, unhurried, immune to the press of bodies parting around them.

Collectors. Here. Already.

And beside them, holding a lantern that burned cold white, stood a broad-shouldered man in the battered coat of a justiciar, watching the crowd with the weary, methodical eyes of someone hunting a specific face.

His gaze swept the market, passed over Sable, passed over the vendors, and landed on Petra—and stayed.
