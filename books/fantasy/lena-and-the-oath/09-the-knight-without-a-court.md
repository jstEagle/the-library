## 9. The Knight Without a Court

The warrant arrived at dawn, and it was blank.

Dain Voss had spent twenty years reading warrants. He knew the weight of good vellum, the bite of a true seal, the particular way a lawful order sat in the hand like a blade balanced for throwing. This one weighed right. The seal was genuine—he pressed his thumb to it and felt the faint warmth of Undercourt wax, the kind that never fully cooled. But where the charges should have been, where his name should have been, the parchment held nothing at all.

He turned it over. Blank on both sides. A warrant to arrest no one, signed by no one, sealed by something that was not quite a someone.

His clerk, a nervous boy called Pell with ink-stained fingers and a habit of hovering, stood in the doorway of the justiciar's office in Ashmarket's garrison house. "It came by grey rider," Pell said. "No escort. No message. Just—"

"I can see what it is." Dain set it on the desk beneath the window, where the early light could reach it. Twenty years of morning light had faded the wood around that spot into a pale rectangle, the shape of every warrant he'd ever laid there before carrying it out into the world to ruin someone's life or save it, depending on the day, depending on the law.

"Sir," Pell said carefully, "does it mean anything?"

Dain almost laughed. It meant everything. It meant the end of everything he was, delivered with the quiet efficiency of an institution that had never once needed to raise its voice at him.

"It means," he said, "that I am no longer a justiciar."

* * *

He had been nineteen when he took the oath. He remembered the cold of the oath-stone under his knees, the weight of the iron band they'd pressed against his brow—the *warrant-mark*, burned into the skin above his left ear in a thin silver line that only showed when he lied. Every justiciar wore one. It was the whole point of them: a court's man who physically could not speak falsehood while wearing the mark. Dain had worn his like a second spine for two decades. It had straightened him through bribes offered and refused, through lords who wept and peasants who spat, through the long gray middle years when he'd stopped believing the law was just and started believing only that it was the law, which was, he'd told himself, the next best thing.

The mark had never once gone dark.

That morning, washing, he caught his reflection in the basin water and saw the line above his ear flickering. Not silver. Not dark. Flickering, like a candle in a draft, like a thing being argued over somewhere far away.

By noon it was gone entirely, and the blank warrant lay on his desk, and Pell kept asking questions Dain couldn't answer because answering them would require him to understand what had happened first.

He understood by evening. Ashmarket was a city of brokers, and brokers talked, and what they talked about traveled faster than any grey rider. By the time the taverns filled, the story had reached even the garrison: the Undercourt had purchased a judge. Not a petty magistrate—a High Arbiter, one of the nine whose word was final from the Salt Coast to the Weeping Woods' edge. Arbiter Caelen Mor, who had signed more justiciar warrants than any living man, who had personally confirmed Dain's own commission twenty years ago with a handshake and the words *be the law's hands, boy, and it will never fail you.*

Arbiter Mor had sold himself. Whole. Name, memory, judgment, soul—bottled and shelved beneath Mount Ceren, price undisclosed, buyer patient as winter. And every warrant issued under his authority, every commission he had ever granted, every oath sworn on his seal, was now void. The law did not recognize dead men, and the law did not recognize the sold, and so the law did not, as of this morning, recognize Dain Voss.

He sat in his office after dark with a single lamp burning and read the blank warrant again, though he no longer needed to. He knew what it was now. It wasn't an arrest order. It was a courtesy. The Undercourt's way of saying: *you are nothing, and we wanted you to know it formally.*

Twenty years. Two decades of refusing bribes in a city built on bribery. Of sleeping alone in garrison rooms because a justiciar with the mark could not afford even the appearance of a debt. Of burying three fellow officers—one to fever, one to a knife in a debtor's alley, one to her own hand after she'd condemned an innocent man and the mark had shown the lie to everyone in the courtroom. He had given the law everything a man could carry, and the law had been bought out from under him like a horse at auction, and the buyers hadn't even bothered to write his name on the paper.

Pell appeared in the doorway again, wringing his hat. "There's—sir, there's a woman asking for you at the gate. Says you owe her a truth."

"Everyone owes someone a truth. Show her in or turn her away, but stop standing there like a man waiting for the floor to open."

"She says—" Pell swallowed. "She says her name is Sable. And she says if you want your judge back, she knows where he is."

* * *

Sable was not what he expected, which was itself unexpected; Dain had thought he'd stopped expecting things from people years ago. She was small, sharp-eyed, dressed like a person who had practiced looking unremarkable until she'd gotten bored of it. She sat across from his desk without being invited and put a scrap of parchment down between them, weighted with a luck-coin that was probably counterfeit.

"That's a copy," she said. "Of a map. The original belongs to someone else, and before you ask, no, I didn't steal it. Recent improvement. Don't get used to it."

Dain looked at the markings. He didn't recognize the script, but he recognized the *style*—the formal, interlocking lines of documents that bound. Contract-script. He'd seen fragments of it on seized soul-broker papers over the years, always incomplete, always deniable. This was not incomplete.

"This is Undercourt script," he said.

"Knew you'd know it. That's why I'm here." She leaned forward. "Here's the situation, justiciar. There's a keeper out of the Hollow Valley—no magic, null-land, you've heard of it—and the Undercourt owns their soul. Bought before they were born. Solstice deadline. They're going under Mount Ceren to take it back, and I'm going with them, because my brother's soul is on those same shelves. And we need a third thing we don't have."

"A soldier."

"A *law*. Or what's left of one." Her eyes were unblinking. "You can't fight collectors with steel. You can't argue with judges using swords. But you spent twenty years making oaths stick, and I'm told there's a right in the old contract-law—any bargain may be contested by trial, even theirs. Especially theirs. They wrote the rule themselves, back when they were sure no one would ever dare use it." She tapped the map. "We're going to dare it. We need someone who knows how trials work. Someone the courts can't buy, because they've already been thrown away."

Dain sat very still. Outside, Ashmarket's evening noise rose and fell—carts, cries, the weather-sellers hawking tomorrow's rain. A city running on small magics and smaller dishonesties, all of it lawful, all of it licensed, all of it suddenly meaningless to him.

"You said the keeper owes me a truth," he said finally. "What truth?"

"They'll tell you themselves. I don't sell other people's confessions." A ghost of a smile. "Only my own, and only at a profit."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you stay here, with your blank warrant, in a garrison that stops paying you within the week, serving a law that no longer exists." She stood, unhurried. "I've watched men face that choice before, justiciar. Most of them pretend it isn't a choice at all. Take a day. We leave at moonrise tomorrow either way."

She was gone before he could answer, which he suspected was the point.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat in his office with the lamp trimmed low and took inventory of his life the way he'd once inventoried evidence: item by item, weighing each against the rest.

Item: the warrant-mark, gone. He touched the place above his ear perhaps forty times that night. The skin was smooth. Nothing. He was, for the first time since he was nineteen, a man capable of lying—and found that he had nothing he wanted to say with the new freedom.

Item: Arbiter Caelen Mor. Dain closed his eyes and saw the old man's study, the smell of pipe-smoke and sealing wax, the dry voice that had corrected Dain's first flawed indictment with the patience of a mason squaring a stone. *The law is not what powerful men want, Voss. It is what remains when everyone wants something.* And now that voice was in a jar beneath a mountain, and the law was what remained of nothing at all.

Item: the innocent man. Because if he was taking inventory, he would take all of it. Ten years ago. A miller named Osten Bray, condemned on testimony Dain had known was purchased, prosecuted anyway because the alternative was prosecuting a baron, and the law in those days still bent toward whoever paid its keepers least. Dain had told himself the evidence was sufficient. He had told himself it so many times that the mark above his ear had stayed silver, because the mark judged the words, not the heart, and Dain had chosen words that were technically true. Osten Bray had died in the salt mines four years later. Dain had attended the trial, the sentencing, and none of the funerals.

He had built twenty years of clean service on top of that one rotten timber, and tonight, with nothing left to protect, he finally admitted what he'd always known: he had not served the law. He had served the law's *shape*, its robes and seals and forms, and told himself the shape was the substance. The Undercourt had done nothing to the law that the law's own servants hadn't been doing for centuries. They had simply bought it openly, at scale, with better bookkeeping.

And that was the thought that got him up out of the chair, that sent him to the armory for his sword and the old traveling cloak he hadn't worn since the border campaigns. Because if the shape was gone—if the seals were void and the warrants blank and the judges bottled—then whatever justice remained in the world was going to have to exist without a shape for a while. Raw. Unlicensed. Carried by people with no institution behind them at all.

He had spent his whole life believing that justice outside the law was just vengeance wearing a borrowed coat. He still believed it. But the law was dead, and vengeance at least was honest about what it wanted, and somewhere between those two failures there might be a third thing worth kneeling for.

He wrote his resignation on the back of the blank warrant—*I resign a commission that no longer exists*—and left it on the desk for Pell, along with the luck-coin Sable had used as a paperweight, which he had examined and confirmed was counterfeit, and which he kept anyway, as a reminder that he was now in the business of accepting worthless currency and making it count.

* * *

They gathered at the west gate at moonrise: Sable, checking and rechecking a satchel of tools; the keeper—Petra, they were called, a name like a struck flint—with storm-grey eyes and a map-case strapped tight across their chest and the look of someone walking on ice over deep water; and a third figure Dain hadn't been told about, a wiry young man with a caravan guard's scars who introduced himself only as Ren and avoided Dain's eyes.

Petra studied Dain the way he'd seen magistrates study the accused. "Sable says you know contract-law. Old contract-law."

"I know how oaths bind and how they break. Whether that helps us under a mountain, I can't promise."

"We're not looking for promises." Something moved behind their eyes—not trust, nothing so generous, but a kind of grim accounting. "We're looking for someone who won't fold the first time the court offers him something. Everyone folds eventually. I need to know what you fold *for*."

Dain considered lying, discovered he no longer had the mark to stop him and no longer the desire to try, and answered plainly.

"Innocent people," he said. "I folded once for a guilty man's gold, ten years ago, and a miller named Osten Bray died in the salt mines because of it. That's the truth, unsealed and unsworn. If you want a man who's never bent, keep looking. If you want a man who knows exactly what bending costs and has decided to spend what's left of his life standing up—I'm what the law discarded, and I'm yours until this is finished."

Silence. Somewhere above the gate, the moon slid free of cloud, and Dain saw Petra exchange a look with Sable—some silent negotiation conducted in a currency he didn't yet know.

"The court will offer you your judge back," Petra said quietly. "When we're beneath the mountain. They'll offer you Arbiter Mor, restored, whole, his seal valid again, if you walk away. I need to know now, in the moonlight, before they can dress it up: what will you say?"

Dain looked east, toward the distant smudge of Mount Ceren against the stars, where a good old man sat on a shelf in the dark believing whatever he'd been made to believe.

"I'll tell them," Dain said, "that a judge who can be sold was never mine to begin with. And then I'll cite their own law at them until they wish they'd never written it down."

Petra held out a hand—not a magistrate's handshake but a traveler's grip, rough-palmed and brief. "Then welcome to the party nobody invited us to."

They passed through the gate together, four shadows on the north road, and behind them Ashmarket's lights glittered like a case of bottles, each one holding somebody's something, and Dain Voss walked away from twenty years of service feeling—for the first time since the oath-stone—unburdened and unarmed and dangerously, wonderfully free.

They made six miles before Sable, ranging ahead, came back at a run with her face pale in the dark.

"Roadblock ahead," she breathed. "Grey robes. Four of them. And justiciar—they're not stopping travelers." Her eyes found Petra's. "They're checking faces. They've got your likeness on a bounty board, and they're asking every soul on the road one question: *have you seen the heir?*"
