## 10. Three Names on a Map

The map had been lying to us for three days.

Not lying, exactly. Hiding. Ivo's blood-marked parchment looked like a merchant's route chart—trade roads, river crossings, the little inked hills that cartographers draw when they've never actually climbed one. Sable had stared at it a dozen times, turning it toward firelight and daylight and once, memorably, moonlight held in a silver dish she'd bought from a weather-seller. Nothing.

Then, on the third night, camped in the ruin of an old shrine with Dain's back against a fallen pillar and rain needling through the broken roof, I spilled tallow on it.

I want to say I did it on purpose. I didn't. My hands were shaking—I'd seen a grey robe at the edge of the market that afternoon, or thought I had, and since then my body had decided fear was a full-time occupation. The candle tipped, wax splashed across the lower corner, and where the wax cooled, the blood-marks rose up dark and glistening, as if the map had been waiting all along for something warm to wake it.

"Sable," I said, and my voice came out strange. "Sable, look."

She was asleep sitting up, which I'd learned was how she slept, one hand always near her knife. She was awake before I finished her name. Then she saw the map, and everything lazy about her burned away at once.

"Blood-script." She crouched over it, careful not to touch. "Undercourt script. It only shows to warmth. Body heat, candle flame—" her eyes flicked to me, quick and assessing, "—blood. Whoever made this wanted it readable only by someone carrying it close to their skin."

I had been carrying it against my chest for three days. Wrapped in linen, yes, but close.

Dain leaned forward into the light. He didn't say anything at first; he never did. He watched the way he watched everything, like a man counting exits even in a ruined shrine. Finally: "What does it show?"

What it showed was this: our road, the one we'd been walking, bending south and then simply stopping at a ring of mountains drawn in that risen-dark ink. And beneath the largest mountain—a peak rendered not in ink but in what looked like burn scars, little cratered circles pressed into the parchment—there were stairs. Hundreds of them, descending in a spiral, down and down past the edge of the drawing until the parchment itself ended, as if the mapmaker's courage had run out before the stairs did.

Beneath them, in a spidery hand that might have been Ivo's and might have been older: *Mount Ceren. The Drowned Stair. Only when the moon is blind.*

"The dark of the moon," Dain said.

"Nine days," Sable said. She knew the moon's calendar the way I knew the valley's seals—without thinking, without wanting to. "Next blind moon is nine days off. We can make Ceren if we push."

"And if we miss it?"

"Then we wait a month." She met my eyes. "A month of collectors, a month of bounty notices, a month of every soul-broker between here and the coast knowing there's a price on an heir with no magic-scent. We don't miss it."

I traced the spiral of stairs with one fingertip, not quite touching. Somewhere down there, in the dark under the burn-scarred mountain, was a jar with my name on it. My true name—the one Maren had never used, the one I hadn't known existed until Ivo whispered it into the storm. *Lena.* A name like a key, or a leash.

"We go," I said. It didn't feel like courage. It felt like stepping off a ledge I'd already fallen from.

* * *

We pushed hard the next two days, and the road punished us for it.

It was hill country now, treeless and wind-scoured, the kind of land where you could see a rider coming an hour before he arrived—and be seen just as long. Dain taught me to read the horizon the way Maren had taught me to read the gate: not looking directly at anything, letting the edges of my vision do the work. "Direct attention draws attention," he said. "Out here, that's not philosophy. It's survival."

He'd changed since Ashmarket. Or maybe I'd finally started seeing him clearly. The warrant that had made him a justiciar was void now—bought out from under him by the same court we were robbing—and there was something almost frightening in how completely he'd reorganized himself around that loss. He still moved like law. He still spoke like law. But the law he served now had no statutes, and he seemed lighter and more dangerous for it.

Sable, meanwhile, had become our map. Not Ivo's map—her own, the mental one she carried of every broker's territory, every tollhouse, every stretch of road where collectors could travel openly. She rerouted us twice without explaining why, and both times, later, we found signs she'd been right: hoofprints too heavy for merchants, a wayside inn with its shutters freshly painted grey.

Grey. Everything came back to grey.

On the second evening we found the notice nailed to a milestone. My face again—or the face they imagined I had, softer than mine, younger, the face of someone who hadn't yet learned what their grandmother had sold. Beneath it, in careful court-hand:

*The heir comes home willingly, or is brought. Reward commensurate. The debt does not age.*

"The debt does not age," Dain read aloud, and something in his jaw tightened. "That's a legal phrase. It means the contract survives death of the parties. They're telling you running forever won't save you."

"They're telling me they're patient," I said. "I know. I grew up on patience. The whole valley runs on it."

Sable peeled the notice off the stone, folded it, and tucked it away. When I raised an eyebrow, she shrugged. "Paper's paper. Good for starting fires, better for forging passes. And I like knowing exactly what they think you look like." A pause, and then, quieter, in the voice she used maybe once a day: "It's wrong, you know. The face. You look nothing like someone who'd be caught."

I didn't know what to do with that, so I said nothing, and she pretended she hadn't said it, and we walked on. That was how the three of us communicated now—in the gaps between words, in things folded up and put away. But I noticed, that night, that Sable took first watch instead of last, and that Dain slept facing the road instead of away from it. Small adjustments. The slow, unspoken architecture of people becoming a unit.

* * *

The ambush came on the fourth day, at a place called the Sorrel Ford, though I learned its name only afterward, from Dain, who learned names of places the way other men learned prayers.

We were crossing the shallows—Sable ahead, me in the middle, Dain behind with his hand resting on the warrant-stub he still wore at his belt like a relic—when the world went quiet.

Not silent. Quiet. There's a difference, and the valley had taught it to me young. Birds don't stop singing when a hawk passes; they change key. This was that. The reeds along the bank stopped moving against the wind. The water kept flowing but stopped sounding. And downstream, half-hidden in the willow-shadow, three figures stood perfectly still, grey robes dark with river-damp, faces like unfinished sketches.

Collectors. On our side of the ford. Waiting.

They shouldn't have been able to move so fast. That was the thought that ran through my head, absurdly, as Sable hissed a curse and Dain's sword cleared its sheath with a sound like tearing cloth. They shouldn't—but of course they could. The roads bent for them the way they couldn't bend for us. Every mile we'd gained, they'd spent effortlessly.

"Heir." The word came from the center figure, flat as slate dropped on stone. It wasn't a voice so much as the memory of one. "The solstice comes. Come home."

"Come home," echoed the second.

"Come home," said the third, and all three stepped forward into the shallows, and the water around their ankles went still and grey as pewter.

"Dain," Sable said, very calmly, "tell me you have a plan."

"I have a direction," Dain said. "Plans are for people with time." He pointed—not at the far bank, where the road continued open and inviting and surely watched, but upstream, where the willows thickened into something darker. A treeline. Old forest, deep forest, the kind of place even this lawless country avoided. "There. The Weeping Woods. No collector crosses that border."

"Why not?" I was already moving, because Dain had given a direction and some part of me had apparently decided, without consulting me, that his directions were law.

"Because the trees remember," Sable said grimly, splashing past me. "Everyone who died under them. All of it. Forever. Ask me how I know."

"How do you—"

"Later!" Dain said, and then the world broke open.

The lead collector moved. I will see it in my dreams for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be: no run, no lunge, just a sudden terrible *nearness*, as if distance itself had been negotiated away. One moment it stood mid-ford; the next, its grey sleeve was sweeping toward my face, and Dain's blade met it with a shriek of metal that sounded wrong, high and thin, like the steel was screaming.

"GO!" Dain roared, and shoved me so hard I went down in the shallows, gasping, the map a bruise against my sternum. Sable hauled me up by the collar with a strength I wouldn't have credited her slight frame, and we ran—up the bank, through the reeds, into the willows, while behind us came sounds I refused to turn and catalog. Steel on grey flesh. Dain breathing like a bellows. And under it all, that flat voice, repeating, patient as winter: *"Come home. Come home. Come home."*

The treeline swallowed us.

* * *

The forest closed over our heads like a lid.

Light changed first—went green and dim and underwater-feeling, filtering through a canopy so dense the rain barely reached us. Then sound changed. Our footsteps landed soft. Our breathing seemed absorbed. Even Sable's muttered cursing came out muffled, apologetic, as if the woods disapproved of profanity.

And then I felt it, and stopped dead.

The trees were watching. Not metaphorically. Not the ordinary animal prickle of being observed. The trunks around us—vast, silver-barked, hung with trailing moss like funeral veils—had a presence to them, a weight of attention, old and sorrowful and utterly without mercy. Bark rippled faintly, here and there, in patterns almost like faces. Almost like grief.

"This is the border?" I whispered.

"This is the doorstep," Sable said. Her bravado had thinned; she stood with her arms wrapped around herself, and for the first time since I'd known her, she looked genuinely afraid. "The Weeping Woods proper starts deeper in. Where we're standing, they're just... noticing us." She swallowed. "Deeper in, they remember you. Whether you want them to or not."

Behind us, at the treeline, the light flickered grey.

I turned. Three figures stood at the forest's edge, motionless, robes dripping. They did not step forward. They stood the way a creditor stands at a debtor's door—present, patient, making the silence itself a demand. The lead one raised its sketch-blank face, and though it had no features I could read, I felt its attention settle on me like a hand.

"The woods are not yours, heir," it said. "Nothing is hidden that the court cannot wait beside."

"Then wait," I said.

My voice surprised me. Steady. Valley-steady, gate-keeper steady, the voice I used for caravans that tried to linger and traders who lied about their cargo. Somewhere in the last four days, without noticing the moment it happened, I had stopped being a person who fled and started being a person who advanced along a chosen line of retreat.

The collector tilted its head. And then—it should have been impossible, but I heard it anyway, felt it in my teeth—something very like amusement passed through it.

"Nine days," it said. "The moon forgets soon. So will your friends. So will you." It folded its hands into its sleeves. "We are patient. Be certain you are."

Then the greyness of them simply... dimmed, the way a coal dims, and they were gone—not departed, I understood somehow, but *withdrawn*, folded back into whatever elsewhere they occupied when they weren't haunting the edges of my life.

Silence, real silence, returned to the treeline.

"Well," said Sable shakily. "That was horrible. Well done."

"You told them to wait," Dain said. He emerged from the willows bleeding from a long shallow cut along his forearm, sword sheathed, expression unreadable. "To their faces."

"It seemed polite."

Something happened to his mouth then. It took me a moment to recognize it, because I'd never seen it before: Dain smiled. It was small and crooked and gone almost immediately, but it was real. "Polite," he repeated, as if tasting the word. "Right. Politeness. I'll note it in the record." He glanced at the dim green depths of the forest, and his smile faded into something warier. "We have six days to cross these woods, and I don't love our odds. The trees remember everyone who died beneath them. Which means somewhere in there, they remember me."

"Me too, probably," Sable said. "Thief. Lots of corpses in my line of work. Mostly not mine, mostly not on purpose, but the trees don't care about intent."

They both looked at me.

"What?" I said.

"The trees remember everyone," Sable said gently. "Including you. Including—" she hesitated, "—including whatever they show you. Everyone sees something in there. Their worst betrayal, the stories say. Something true enough to break them."

I thought of Maren. Of her hollowed eyes, empty as washed river stones. Of the oath she'd sworn before I was born, selling a child who had no voice to consent. If the woods wanted to show me a betrayal, they would not have to work hard.

"Then we walk fast," I said, "and we don't stop for ghosts."

But even as I said it, the forest breathed around us—slow, vast, sorrowful—and I understood that the Weeping Woods did not care what we intended. It remembered. And tomorrow, whether I willed it or no, it would remember me.

Ahead, deep among the silver trunks, something glimmered pale between the trees. Moss-light, maybe. Moonlight, maybe.

Or maybe a face, waiting patiently in the dark, wearing one I loved.
