## 11. The Weeping Woods

The trees started crying the moment we crossed their shadow-line.

Not weeping in any way I'd seen before—no rain, no sap, no morning dew. The trunks themselves glistened, dark streaks running down grey bark like tears down a face that had forgotten how to stop. Sable rode at the front of our little column with her hood up and her jaw set, and when Petra asked what the place was called, she answered without turning around.

"The Weeping Woods. Locals don't come in. They say the trees remember."

"Remember what?"

"Everyone who ever died beneath them." She nudged her mare around a root as thick as a fallen man. "And they like to share."

Dain muttered something about superstition. He'd been muttering it since Ashmarket, since the memory-sellers, since every new wonder of the outer lands proved him wrong again. But even he went quiet when we passed the first of the standing stones—a grave-marker, or something older than graves—with a face carved into it so worn by centuries of grief-water that only the eyes remained, wide open, staring up into the canopy.

I kept my hands busy checking the map-case strapped under my coat. That was my habit: keep your hands busy, keep your mouth shut, keep moving. Twenty years of thieving had taught me that stillness invited trouble, and silence invited worse. But there was no stealing to do here, no locks to pick, no pockets to lighten. Just the trees, and their tears, and the slow drip of water that sounded almost like whispering if you let your mind wander.

Petra's mind wandered. I could see it happening—the way they kept glancing up at the canopy, the way their fingers drifted toward the seal-ring on their thumb. Maren's ring. The grandmother they'd left hollowed on a keeper's cot three valleys back.

"Stay close," I said. "Don't touch anything."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were." I didn't mean it unkindly. "Everyone does. That's how the woods get you."

* * *

We made camp in a clearing where the tears pooled in a shallow basin at the center, black and mirror-still despite the drizzle. Dain took first watch because Dain always took first watch—it was the only prayer he knew anymore. Petra curled up against their pack with the map spread across their knees, tracing the route to Mount Ceren for the hundredth time, and I sat with my back to a tree and told myself the warmth seeping through my coat was just bark.

It wasn't bark. The tree was warm the way a body is warm.

I moved. Fast. Crossed the clearing and put my hand flat against the trunk of another one—cold, dead, ordinary—and then pressed my palm to the first tree again just to be sure. Warm. Faintly pulsing. Like resting your hand on the chest of something sleeping very deeply.

"Sable." Petra was watching me now, map forgotten. "What is it?"

"Nothing. Trees are trees."

But the tree beneath my palm pulsed once more, and somewhere high above us, in the dripping canopy, something sighed.

Dain looked up from his watch-post. "Did you hear—"

The whispering started after full dark.

At first I thought it was the water. The drip off the leaves had a rhythm to it, a cadence, and tired minds make music out of anything. But then the whispers formed words—not words I could catch, not yet, but words shaped in a voice I knew. A voice I'd spent eleven years trying to forget.

My brother's voice.

Tam. Saying my name the way he used to say it when he was small and I'd come home from a job with coin enough for bread. *Sable. Sable. Sable.* Not accusing. Worse than accusing. Hopeful.

I got up and walked the perimeter of the camp twice, checking snares I hadn't set, until the voice faded or I walked far enough from whatever called it. When I came back, Petra was awake, sitting up in the dark with their eyes too bright.

"You heard it too," they said.

"Heard what?"

"Don't." Their voice cracked, just slightly. "Please don't do that. Not you."

*Not you.* As if I'd earned some exemption from lying. As if two weeks on the road made me honest. I almost laughed. Instead I sat down across the fire-ring from them, close enough to speak low, far enough to keep my hands visible—an old habit from negotiations that could go either way.

"What did yours say?" I asked.

Petra wrapped their arms around their knees. For a long moment I didn't think they'd answer. Then: "Nothing yet. Just... the trees are warm. Like something's inside them. Waiting."

"It's waiting for all of us, then."

"That doesn't scare you?"

I fed a stick into the dying fire and watched it catch. "Everything scares me. That's why I'm still alive."

* * *

The woods took Dain first.

He woke us near dawn—or what passed for dawn under that canopy, a grey-green dimness like light filtered through drowned glass—shouting at someone who wasn't there. By the time Petra and I reached him he'd drawn his sword and was holding it against empty air, arm shaking, face grey as ash.

"She was right there," he said. "The widow. From Corvane. The man I condemned—her husband—he was innocent, and she stood right there and she *asked me*—" His voice failed. He lowered the sword slowly, like a man setting down something heavier than steel. "Twenty years. Twenty years I told myself the law was the law, and the law doesn't weep. This forest disagrees."

None of us said anything comforting, because none of us had any comfort to give. That was the thing about our little company: we were three people held together by necessity and mutual damage, and comfort was a currency none of us carried.

We broke camp fast. The sooner we were through these woods, the better—for all of us. Except the woods had other ideas.

The path we'd been following simply ended. Not faded, not overgrown—ended, cut clean as if by a knife, the ground beyond it rising into a wall of briars that hadn't been there yesterday. Behind us, the way we'd come had changed too; I recognized nothing, though we'd walked it not six hours past.

"It's herding us," Dain said grimly.

"Toward what?" Petra asked.

I knew. I'd known since the first night, since the warm trees, since Tam's voice in the dripping dark. The locals' warning hadn't been about the trees remembering the dead. It was about what the trees did with the living.

"They show you something," I said. My mouth was dry. "A memory. Your worst one. The one you've built your whole life around never looking at directly. And they make you look."

"How do you know?" Petra's eyes were sharp on mine.

Because I'd stolen from a soul-broker's library once, years ago, and read a traveler's account of these woods in a book I was supposed to be burning. Because everyone who grew up in the outer lands knew the stories, the way valley children knew not to whistle at night. Because some part of me had felt this coming since we crossed the treeline, and had been quietly, desperately hoping I was wrong.

"Lucky guess," I said.

Petra didn't believe me. I watched them not believe me, and said nothing, and hated myself a little more efficiently than usual.

* * *

It took Petra at midday.

We were walking single-file through a stand of ancient oaks when they stopped—just stopped, mid-stride, like a puppet whose strings had been cut. Their eyes went fixed and silver, reflecting light that wasn't there, and their lips moved around words too quiet to hear.

I caught them before they hit the ground. Lowered them gently against the warm trunk of an oak whose tears ran faster now, streaming, as if the tree itself were grieving on Petra's behalf. And I knelt there, holding their shoulders, watching their face twist through expressions I'd never seen on them—horror, denial, a grief so raw it looked like it might split them open—and I understood that behind those silver eyes, the forest was showing them their worst betrayal.

Maren. It had to be Maren. The grandmother who sold their soul before they drew breath, who taught them seals and rules and love in equal measure, who lay hollowed in a bed three valleys away with her memories siphoned into a collector's jar. Whatever the woods were showing Petra, it wore her face.

"No," Petra whispered. "No, she wouldn't—she *loved* me—"

The trees wept harder.

I shook them. Gently at first, then not gently at all. Around us the forest had gone silent—even the dripping had stopped, as if the whole wood were leaning in to listen. Dain stood at the clearing's edge with his sword drawn, watching the treeline, giving us what privacy a man can give when he expects an ambush every moment.

"Petra." I gripped their face in both hands. "Whatever you're seeing, it isn't real. It's the woods. It lies the way I lie—beautifully, convincingly, with just enough truth in it to make you swallow the hook."

Their eyes found mine. Still silver. Still somewhere else.

"She signed it," Petra breathed. "Before I was born. She signed it and she smiled and she—she chose the valley over me, she chose the *gate*, she—"

"Listen to me." I put everything I had into my voice, every scrap of sincerity I'd ever faked, and meant it for the first time in years. "Mothers and grandmothers sell children futures every day in this world. Some sell them to brokers like mine. Some sell them to armies, to marriages, to gods. It doesn't mean they don't love you. It means love isn't always enough to save anyone—including the ones doing the loving. Maren made a monstrous choice because every choice she had was monstrous. That's not betrayal. That's just being alive in a world like this one."

Something shifted behind their eyes. The silver began to fade.

"And anyway," I added, because sincerity sitting alone makes me itch, "if she truly betrayed you, she'd have handed you to the collectors herself instead of getting her mind emptied defending you. Sentimental old women don't do that."

The silver cleared. Petra blinked up at me, wet-eyed, shaking, present. For a long moment neither of us moved.

"You lied to me," they said hoarsely. "In Ashmarket. About why you really joined us. You said it was the debt, the passage, the—"

"I did lie."

"But not about this." They searched my face. "You're telling the truth right now. How do I know which is which?"

"You don't." I released them and stood, brushing off my knees. "That's the fun part."

* * *

The woods saved me for last. Of course they did. The trees had been warming themselves around me for two days, learning my shape, learning where the soft places were.

It happened at dusk, in a hollow where the tear-pools gathered deep enough to reflect the canopy overhead. I went down to fill the waterskins—ordinary task, ordinary evening—and saw her in the water before I saw her anywhere else.

Myself. Standing on the far side of the pool, wearing my face, wearing my smile, the good one, the one I use when I'm selling something. And beside her, in the reflection, a collector in grey robes, its faceless head bent attentively as she spoke.

"—the gate-heir walks with me willingly," my reflection said. "Three days' grace and I'll deliver them to you at the foot of Mount Ceren. In exchange, my brother's jar goes free. Untouched. Full."

The collector inclined its head. The bargain was struck. And reflection-Sable turned, smiling her selling-smile, and walked out of the water's memory—

I stumbled back. Fell. Scrambled up and away from the pool as if it had teeth, heart slamming, breath coming in ragged pieces. Because the worst part—the part that made the woods worth their name—wasn't the vision itself.

It was that I couldn't swear it hadn't happened. Couldn't swear it *wouldn't*. The broker in Ashmarket had offered me exactly that deal—Petra's life for my debts erased—and I'd stalled, and warned the group, and done the right thing. Mostly. Eventually. After weighing it. After letting the scales sit heavy on the wrong side for one long, shameful moment while I thought about Tam, about his jar on some court shelf, about what I'd give to hold him whole again.

The trees remembered everyone who died beneath them. Now they remembered me too—remembered the traitor I carried inside me, the one who did the arithmetic of loyalty and found it wanting. They showed it back to me in still water, and the water didn't lie, because water never does. Only people do.

"Sable?"

Petra stood at the top of the hollow, silhouetted against the last grey light. Watching me. Watching the pool. Putting it together with the sharp, quick mind I'd been lying to since the day we met.

"What did it show you?" they asked quietly.

I opened my mouth to lie. It would have been easy—one smooth sentence, a deflection, a joke. My whole life lived in sentences like that.

Instead I heard myself say: "Me. Selling you."

The silence between us stretched thin as wire.

"And the worst part," I went on, because apparently the woods had pried the lid off something in me that wouldn't shut again, "is that I've already been offered that exact bargain. In Ashmarket. The broker—I told you I stalled him, and I did, but Petra, I *weighed it*. I stood there and did the sums with your life on one side and my brother's on the other, and for one moment the sums balanced. One moment. That's all it takes. That's all it's ever taken."

Petra came down into the hollow. Slow steps. Careful ones, the way you approach a wounded animal—or a trap. They stopped an arm's length away, close enough that I could see the tear-streaks of the trees reflected in their eyes.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because the woods were going to tell you anyway. In their own time, in their own way, probably at the worst possible moment." I laughed, and it came out broken. "I figured if I had to be betrayed, I'd rather do it myself. At least then I control the timing."

"That's not betrayal. Telling the truth isn't—"

"Isn't it? You needed to trust me. We're walking into the Undercourt together, into a place where one moment of doubt gets us all shelved in jars, and you needed to trust me, and I just handed you a reason not to." I spread my hands. "There. Betrayed. Congratulations."

For a long moment Petra said nothing. The trees wept around us, soft and ceaseless, and somewhere above, the canopy dripped its endless grief onto ground that remembered everything.

Then Petra did something I didn't expect. They pulled the map-case from under their coat—the blood-marked map, Ivo's legacy, the reason for all of this—and held it out to me.

"If you were going to sell me," they said, "you'd have done it already. You've had a dozen chances. Every one of them cheaper than the next will be." Their voice was steady. "The woods showed you what you fear, Sable. Not what you are. There's a difference. I've spent my whole life learning it."

I took the map. My hands weren't steady.

"Besides," Petra added, and there—there it was, the ghost of a smile, tired and real, "you're a terrible liar when you're tired. Everyone knows you stall before you confess. You've been stalling for two days."

Behind us, Dain called a warning from the ridge: lights in the trees, moving in formation. Grey robes. The collectors had found our trail again—and ahead, through the parting briars, the woods were finally showing us a way out: a narrow descent into rock, stairs carved into the mountainside, leading down toward a darkness that smelled of deep water and old stone.

The Drowned Stair. Two days early, and hunted, and out of options.

Petra grabbed my wrist. "Run now. Doubt me later."

So I ran.
