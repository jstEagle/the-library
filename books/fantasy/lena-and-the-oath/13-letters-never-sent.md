## 13. Letters Never Sent

The waystation squatted at the bend of the old trade road like something that had given up standing and was merely resting on its own bones. Its roof had half-collapsed into a fan of rotten thatch, its door hung from one hinge, and the hearth inside was black with years of cold. But it had walls, and walls meant a night without watching the treeline, and after three days of Sable's shortcuts and Dain's grim arithmetic about pursuit, Petra would have traded their grandmother's seal-ring for four walls and a roof that leaked only in one place.

They built a small fire in the hearth anyway—Dain insisted, saying cold camps made careless sentries—and Sable rigged her luck-coins in the doorway so they'd chime if anything crossed them. The coins were ugly little things, stamped with a merchant's smiling face, and they caught the firelight like a row of tiny grinning teeth.

"First watch is mine," Dain said. He'd taken up his post by the broken window with his warrant-staff across his knees, though the staff was void now, its authority burned away by a court that had sold its own judge. He still held it like it mattered. Petra was beginning to understand that Dain held everything like it mattered—that was the whole of him, a man built out of duties that no longer existed.

"Second," said Sable, already curling up near the fire with her cloak over her head. "Wake me if you die."

Petra sat against the far wall with Ivo's map spread across their knees.

They had been carrying it for weeks now. They had traced its roads with their fingers until they knew every line, had memorized the route to Mount Ceren, the mark of the Drowned Stair, the little symbols Sable read like scripture. But they had never truly read it—not all of it. The margins were crowded with writing, cramped and slanting, the handwriting of someone who wrote fast and hated what he wrote, and Petra had avoided it the way you avoid a room where someone died.

Tonight there was nowhere left to avoid.

The fire cracked. Outside, wind moved through the bare trees with a sound like slow breathing. Petra turned the map over and began at the top left corner, where the ink was oldest.

*If you are reading this, then I failed twice.*

Petra's breath went shallow.

*I was meant to be the heir. Before you. There was a keeper before your grandmother's daughter—a man named Aldous Vasquez, my father's brother by blood and by oath both, though the oaths mattered more. He chose me when I was nine years old. He told me the valley would need me someday, and he taught me the seals the way you teach a child to swim: gently, in shallow water, never letting them see how deep it goes.*

*Then I saw the ledger.*

*Every oath has a price written somewhere, and keepers are not shown the book. I found it in his strongbox while he slept. One soul per generation, pledged before birth. And this generation—the entry had a name already inked in, waiting. Mine.*

*I ran. I want that written plainly, so whoever comes after me knows exactly what kind of person carried this map. I did not stay and fight. I did not warn anyone. I took a horse and I crossed the gate and I told myself the court would simply choose another child, that the debt was the family's and not mine, that running was survival and survival was not a sin. I was nineteen years old and I believed every word, because believing them was easier than breathing.*

*The court does not choose another child. Understand that. The debt follows the name, and the name follows the blood, and if the named one flees, the collectors simply wait a generation and take the next born to that blood. My cowardice did not erase an entry in the ledger. It moved it. It moved it down one line, to a child not yet born, because the court is patient and ledgers do not grieve.*

Petra stopped reading. Their hands had gone cold despite the fire.

Down one line. To a child not yet born.

To them.

They thought of Maren in the doorway of the keep, all those years, checking the seals at dusk with her iron rules and her iron silences. *Never cross the stones. Never speak to strangers past the treeline. Never ask what the gate keeps out.* Petra had thought it was protectiveness. It was a wall built around a debt, brick by brick, rule by rule—an entire childhood constructed to keep one child ignorant of the price already paid for them.

And it had worked, until a dying courier bled out against the boundary stones and pressed a map into their hands.

*You will hate me,* Ivo had written, further down. *I hope you hate me. Hatred means you're alive to feel it, and alive is more than I managed to be for twenty years.*

* * *

Petra didn't remember deciding to write. Their hands simply found the stub of charcoal they used for sketching, and a sheet of the cheap paper Sable carried, and before they fully understood what they were doing, they had written:

*Maren.*

The single word sat on the page like a wound.

She couldn't read it. That was the thing that made Petra's throat close even as they kept writing. The letter could be perfect, could be eloquent, could say everything—and it would sit folded in Petra's pack forever, because Maren was a hundred miles behind them, hollowed, her memories siphoned into grey-robed hands, her eyes empty as winter pools. Even if a letter could reach her, the woman who would have read it was gone. What remained remembered nothing of the sacrifice, nothing of the bargain, perhaps nothing of Petra at all.

They wrote anyway.

*I'm sorry I left without telling you. I know you'd say I told you nothing because there was nothing to tell—you raised me on rules instead of reasons, and I obeyed them all, and I hated you a little for every one. I'm not sorry for the hating anymore. I think maybe you needed me to hate the rules, so I'd never look behind them.*

*The map belonged to a man named Ivo. He was supposed to be me, before me. He ran, and his running is why the debt came to me, and I've been trying all evening to decide whether that makes him a monster or just a frightened boy who grew into a frightened man. I think I've decided it makes him both, and I think—I think that might be what you were too. A frightened person who loved me so much she built a cage around me and called it safety, and never once let herself say the true thing out loud because saying it would make it real.*

*Here is the true thing, then, since you can't say yours: I'm going to Mount Ceren. I'm going to take my soul back. And when it's done, I'm going to come home, and I'm going to rewrite the oath, and no one born in that valley will ever be sold again. Not me. Not the next one. Not ever.*

*You taught me the gate-seals. You made me practice them until my hands knew them better than my name. I used to think it was because you wanted a successor. Now I think you were arming me the only way you knew how.*

*Come back to me. However they hollowed you, whatever they took—come back. I'm not done needing you.*

*—Petra*

The charcoal snapped under their thumb on the last stroke. Petra stared at the letter, at the smudged, urgent handwriting that looked so much like Ivo's margins it hurt, and folded it along the creases Sable used for her contracts—precise, deliberate, the folds of someone who handled paper that decided fates.

They tucked it into the inner pocket of their coat, over their heart, where the blood-marked map had ridden for weeks.

Some letters aren't sent. Some are carried.

* * *

"You're crying or freezing," Sable said from her corner, without opening her eyes. "Either way, stop."

"I'm reading."

"At night. By firelight. With your face doing that." She cracked one eye. "Doing what?"

"Nothing."

"Mm." She sat up, unhurried, and crossed to look at the map spread between them. Her eyes went straight to the margins—of course they did; she'd spent years stealing documents whose margins were worth more than their faces. "You finally read it, then."

"How did you know?"

"Because you've been carrying that map like it was a weapon. Weapons you study. Letters you avoid." She tapped the oldest ink with one finger. "Ivo's hand. I'd know it anywhere—he did work for my old employer once, years back. Fast writer. Hated every word." Her finger paused. "He came back, didn't he? In the end."

"He came back." Petra's voice came out rougher than intended. "Twenty years after he ran. He crossed the boundary stones knowing what the null would do to him, knowing magic would burn him alive from the inside, and he walked far enough in to find me and die doing it."

"To pass it on."

"To pass it on." Petra looked at the fire. "He could have died anywhere else. Anywhere in the world, comfortable, anonymous, with his guilt packed away in a drawer. Instead he spent his last strength crawling to a valley that unmakes people like him, to hand a stranger a map and two words." *The heir comes home.* "That's not courage. Courage would have been staying, at nineteen, and fighting. This is what guilt looks like when it finally grows teeth."

Sable was quiet a moment. For a woman who talked like money changing hands, her silences always cost more than her words.

"My brother used to say guilt is just love with nowhere to go," she said at last. "He said it about our mother. Then he sold his soul to pay her debts, and I learned guilt has plenty of places to go. It goes to brokers. It goes to courts." She shrugged, the motion too casual to be honest. "Maybe it also goes back through gates, sometimes. Maybe that's the one direction that costs the most and buys the least."

"It bought me time."

"It bought you a burden." She tapped the map again. "Same thing, most days." She stood, stretched, and jerked her chin toward the corner where Dain sat his watch. "Sleep. Third watch is yours, and you'll want your wits. Tomorrow we hit the toll road, and the toll road out here doesn't take coin."

"What does it take?"

Sable's grin had no warmth in it at all. "Confessions."

She rolled back into her cloak. Petra sat a moment longer, looking at the map—at Ivo's cramped, self-loathing hand filling every margin, twenty years of guilt written small enough to hide, and hidden so poorly that whoever carried it next would have to read every word.

*You will hate me. I hope you hate me.*

"I don't," Petra said quietly, to a dead man, to a burning courier, to the boy who fled and the man who crawled home. "I think I understand you. And I'm going to finish what you couldn't start."

The fire settled. Outside, the wind changed direction, and beneath it—faint, at the very edge of hearing—came a sound that did not belong to any weather.

A bell. Far off, on the toll road, ringing once.

Sable's luck-coins chimed in the doorway, all at once, like teeth beginning to grind.
