## 14. The Broker's Bargain

Sable had spent eleven years learning to read rooms, and the Gilded Antler read wrong from the moment she walked in.

It wasn't anything she could name at first. The inn sat on the crossroads at Marrowfen like it had always been there—low-beamed, smoke-stained, smelling of tallow and spilled ale. The fire burned. The potboy moved. The dice game in the corner clattered along with the particular rhythm of men losing money they'd already counted as gone. Everything ordinary.

Except that every ordinary thing was *arranged*.

The fire was lit too evenly. The potboy wiped the same stretch of counter in the same direction, over and over, a man performing memory of work rather than work itself. The dice players never laughed. And the landlord—the fat, bald, red-faced landlord polishing tankards behind the bar—was wearing a face Sable knew better than her own mother's, because she'd watched him buy it off a dying man in Ashmarket six years ago.

Quill.

Her old employer stood behind the bar of an inn he didn't own, in a body that wasn't his, pouring drinks for customers who weren't drinking, and he hadn't looked up yet because he wanted her to have three full seconds to understand that she'd been expected.

Sable's hand found her knife without her telling it to. She let it stay there, under her coat, thumb against the worn bone handle. Old habits. The good ones were all old.

"Quill," she said pleasantly. "You've gotten fat."

"I've gotten *comfortable*." He looked up then, and his eyes did that thing they always did—went soft and warm and utterly bottomless, like a well you could drop a coin into and never hear it land. "Sit down, little wren. Have a drink. It's been what, two years?"

"Two years, four months." She slid onto the stool across the bar, keeping the counter between them, keeping her back to the wall. "You kept count."

"I keep count of everything. It's why I'm rich and you're—" he gestured at her with the tankard cloth, taking in the road dust, the patched coat, the knife-scarred knuckles, "—*this*."

"This is freedom."

"This is debt with better scenery." He set a cup in front of her and poured something amber into it. She didn't touch it. He didn't expect her to; it was theater, and they both knew their lines. "Three hundred crowns, Sable. That was the number when you ran. Interest has been accruing."

"Interest doesn't accrue on debts to dead men."

"Dead men don't pour drinks." He smiled with the stolen face, and it was horrible how easily the fat cheeks arranged themselves around his expression, like a glove turning inside out. "I heard you crossed the Weeping Woods with interesting company. A gate-keeper. No magic-scent at all. Grey robes have been asking after them in every market from here to the Stair. There's a price on that head now that would clear your ledger twice over and leave enough for a house by the sea."

Sable turned the cup slowly on the counter, watching the amber catch the firelight. Buying time. Thinking.

Here was the shape of it, laid out plain: Petra Vasquez, walking beside her on the road, trusting her—or trying to, which was worse, because trust offered freely was so much easier to spend than trust bought with blood. Dain grinding his whetstone every night like penance. Three names on a map, one dark moon away from the Drowned Stair. And Quill sitting here in his borrowed flesh, offering her exactly the thing she'd run from him to escape: a clean slate.

All she had to do was sell one person.

She'd sold people before. That was the thing nobody understood about her—not Dain with his justiciar's arithmetic, not Petra with their keeper's earnestness. She had sold people before, more than once, and slept fine afterward, because sleep was a skill like any other and she'd practiced.

But her brother's soul was in a jar beneath Mount Ceren, and the only reason she was on this road was that a gate-keeper with no magic-scent had looked at her across a campfire and said *I'll get him out if I can*, and meant it, and Sable had felt something crack open in her chest that she'd spent eleven years bricking over.

"Who else knows they're here?" she asked.

"Everyone I've told." Quill refilled a glass no one had drunk from. "The collectors move slowly through settled lands—they don't like crowds, too much living noise. But they're patient. They'll be at the Fen by tomorrow night whether you say yes or no. The only question is whether you're standing next to your friends when they arrive, or standing somewhere very far away with a paid-off ledger and my blessing."

"And if I say no?"

He set down the cloth. For a moment the warmth drained out of the borrowed face entirely, and she saw the thing underneath, the actual Quill, the one who'd taught her lockpicking with a belt buckle and mercy with a lesson she still dreamed about.

"Then I sell them anyway, and I add the cost of this conversation to your account."

There it was. Knife-point arithmetic. Loyalty on one side, truth on the other, and Quill smiling between them like a moneylender at a funeral.

Sable picked up the cup. Held his eyes over the rim. Drank it all down—it was good, of course it was good, he never wasted good drink on anyone he didn't intend to keep—and set it back on the counter with a click.

"Give me until moonrise," she said. "I'll give you an answer at the crossroads shrine. Alone."

"Moonrise." He considered her, head tilted, the fat face doing its best impression of a man weighing profit against patience. "Moonrise. And Sable—if you're thinking of running, remember that I know where your brother sleeps at night." He smiled. "In a jar. On a shelf. In the dark. Jars are very easy to shelve somewhere *worse*."

She stood, dropped a coin on the counter out of pure spite, and walked out into the grey afternoon without letting her shoulders climb toward her ears until she was three streets gone.

* * *

She told herself she was going to warn them straight away.

That lasted the length of Marrowfen's high street. Then it became: first she'd scout the roads, confirm where the collectors would come from, gather facts so the warning would be useful instead of just frightening. Then it became: she should decide what she was going to say about *why* Quill had found her, because Dain would ask, Dain always asked, and the true answer—I owed him three hundred crowns and he owns a piece of everyone I ever failed—was not an answer she could afford to give while they needed her to be the one who knew the back ways.

By the time she reached the millhouse outside town where they'd made camp, she had constructed a version of events with all the dangerous parts sanded smooth. Quill had approached her. He'd offered her a deal. She'd refused it. She'd come straight to tell them the collectors were coming tomorrow night.

Every word of it true except the ones that mattered.

Dain sat sharpening his sword by the millrace, because of course he did; the man treated his blade like a rosary. Petra was crouched over Ivo's map, weighting the corners with stones, tracing the route to the Drowned Stair with a charcoal stub, lips moving as they memorized distances. Firelight caught the ink lines on their wrist—the blood-mark Ivo had left them, the mark that said *heir*.

Sable stopped at the edge of the light.

"Collectors," she said. "Tomorrow night. Marrowfen. Quill sold our location—he's got an inn there now, or he's wearing a man who does. They'll come through the fen causeway after dark, when the roads empty."

Petra looked up. Charcoal on their fingers, map-light in their eyes. "How do you know?"

"I went into town for supplies. Saw the notice boards. Heard talk." Smooth. Practiced. A lie built out of true bricks. "And Quill's face was everywhere. He's been following us since the Woods."

Dain had stopped sharpening. He was looking at her the way he looked at evidence—patiently, thoroughly, with the terrible attention of a man who had spent twenty years catching liars for a living before the law itself was sold out from under him.

"You went into town," he said, "for supplies."

"That's what I said."

"We have supplies."

"We have *adequate* supplies. There's a difference." She grinned at him, the old easy grin, the one that had gotten her fed and forgiven in a dozen towns. "What is this, justiciar? Cross-examination by campfire?"

But Dain didn't smile back, and Petra had gone very still over the map, and Sable felt the ground shift under her feet the way it did when a floorboard gave warning before it broke.

Because Petra wasn't looking at her anymore. They were looking at their own wrist—at the blood-mark—and their brow had furrowed, and they said, quietly, almost to themselves:

"The mark's been warm since noon."

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, when Dain had taken first watch and Petra lay wrapped in their cloak pretending to sleep—Sable sat by the banked fire and did the accounting she'd been avoiding since the Gilded Antler.

Here was what she knew about herself, honestly, in the dark, with no audience: she had taken the meeting. She had sat down. She had drunk the drink. Some part of her—some part with her voice and her cleverness and her hunger for a clean slate—had genuinely weighed it. Had thought: *three hundred crowns gone, a house by the sea, and Petra will understand eventually, because everyone understands eventually, because survival is the one story everybody believes.*

And here was what she also knew: she had refused. She had asked for moonrise not because she needed time to decide but because she needed time to lie well, and lying well took preparation, and she had prepared, and the lie had held through supper and through watch-change and through Petra's quiet questions about supply routes.

So why did she feel like she'd already lost?

Because of the mark. Because Petra had shown her, an hour ago, hushed by the embers: the blood-lines on their wrist glowing faintly warm whenever Sable came near, warmer still when she spoke. Ivo's blood, doing whatever blood-marks did—remembering, perhaps, or *listening*. And Petra had looked at the mark, and then at her, with those keeper's eyes that had spent a lifetime reading seals and boundaries and things that were not what they appeared, and had said nothing at all.

That was the worst of it. Not accusation. Silence. Petra giving her room to tell the truth, the way you leave a door open for a cat that may or may not want to come in.

Sable stared into the coals and understood, with the clarity that only arrived after midnight, that she had maybe an hour left before moonrise, before the crossroads shrine, before Quill sent someone to collect her answer—and that whatever she did in that hour would be the truest thing about her, more true than eleven years of thieving, more true than any lie she'd ever told well.

She rose. Crossed the camp. Crouched beside Petra, who opened their eyes immediately—had probably never been asleep at all.

"I lied to you," Sable said.

Petra waited. Didn't move. The mark on their wrist glowed faintly, steadily, like a coal refusing to die.

"Not about the collectors. They're coming tomorrow night, that's real, that's urgent. But I didn't hear it from notice boards." Her throat was dry. She made herself keep going, because stopping was the same as running and she was done running from things smaller than death. "Quill found me. My old employer. He offered to erase my debts—all of them, everything—in exchange for handing you over. And I sat there and I drank his wine and I *considered it*, Petra. I want you to know that. I want you to know exactly what I am before you decide what I'm worth."

The silence stretched. Somewhere out in the dark, an owl called, and Dain's silhouette by the millrace didn't shift, which meant he was listening, which meant he'd probably known, which meant—

"You lied," Petra said at last, "and then you came back and unlied. At midnight. When no one was making you."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because—" and here it was, the thing under the thing, the jar on the shelf in the dark, the brother whose name she hadn't spoken aloud in front of these two because speaking it made it real, "—because I've sold people before. People who trusted me. And it worked, and I ate well, and I slept fine, and there is a hole in the world shaped like every one of them and I dug every spadeful myself. And you looked at me across a fire and promised to try for Tam, and you *meant* it, and I realized I would burn down every clean slate in creation before I'd add your face to that hole." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "So yes. I considered selling you. And then I chose not to. Both of those are true. You don't get one without the other. That's the whole of me."

Petra sat up slowly. In the ember-light their face was unreadable—keeper's training, gate-keeper's stillness—and Sable braced for the verdict, for the knife or the exile or the cold nod that was worst of all.

Instead Petra held out their marked wrist, palm up, an offering.

"It's warm near you," they said. "It's been warm since we met. Ivo's blood knew you before I did—knew what you'd do, maybe, or what you'd choose. I decided to trust the mark." A pause, and something wry and tired moved through their eyes. "I'm deciding to trust you instead. It's harder. Do better than the blood."

Sable took the wrist. Her hands were shaking. Eleven years of steady hands, ruined by a gate-keeper's forgiveness.

Above the trees, the moon was climbing toward full rise, and somewhere down the dark road to Marrowfen, a fat man in a borrowed face was checking his hourglass, waiting for an answer at a crossroads shrine that was never going to come.

Which meant, Sable thought grimly, rising to wake Dain and plan a fight they couldn't win on ground they hadn't chosen, that Quill would take her silence for betrayal—and the collectors wouldn't wait for tomorrow night after all.

Far off across the fens, thin as a needle, a horn sounded once and stopped.

They were already coming.
