## 15. The Drowned Stair

The entrance to the Drowned Stair was a crack in the mountain's root, no wider than a coffin lid, hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall that had been falling so long it had forgotten how to be water.

Petra stood before it in the blue hour before dawn, breath steaming, the map open against their chest where the wind kept trying to take it. Ivo's blood-marked lines glowed faintly in the cold—a trick of the ink, Sable said, or a trick of the blood—and the final marking pulsed like a slow heartbeat: *down*.

"It's called the Drowned Stair," Sable said, "because people drown on it."

"Charming."

"I'm not finished. It's called the Drowned Stair because the steps are under water, and the water is full of the last thoughts of everyone who ever drowned there. You don't drown because you can't breathe." She pulled her collar up against the spray. "You drown because you stop wanting to."

Dain said nothing. He had been saying very little since the Tollhouse, since his confession had been marked *paid* in light that didn't fade. He checked the straps of his pack, then checked them again, the way he did when silence was the only prayer left to him.

Petra touched the place behind their ribs where the sold memory used to live. It was a strange sensation, missing something they could no longer name—like pressing a tongue to a gap where a tooth had been. They knew *that* Maren had taught them the gate-seals. They knew the knowledge had been taken at Ashmarket, traded for a single answer about the solstice ritual. But the shape of what was gone was itself gone. Grief without an object. A door painted on a wall.

"Last chance to turn back," Sable said, though her grin said she hoped they wouldn't. She needed this stair as much as Petra did. Her brother's soul sat on a shelf beneath their feet, in a jar, in a court that wore dead faces for jewelry.

"We're not turning back," Petra said, and stepped through the frozen fall.

The ice parted around them like a held breath released.

* * *

The first step was wet stone, slick as glass, and the dark below was not empty.

It was lit.

Not by torchlight—by *thoughts*. Pale shapes drifted in the flooded shaft like lantern-fish, each one glowing with someone's final moment. A woman's voice, repeating a child's name over and over, soft as snowfall. A man humming half a lullaby, the melody fraying at the edges. A soldier counting—*one, two, three, four*—as if numbers could hold back the water. The thoughts sank slowly, endlessly, down a stair that spiraled into the mountain's heart, and the water they floated in was warm as bathwater and black as spilled ink.

"Don't touch them," Sable whispered. She had gone pale, which for Sable was its own kind of terror. "Don't listen too long. They're not ghosts exactly. They're *ends*. And ends want company."

They descended.

The steps were cut for taller folk than any of them, or perhaps for no folk at all; each one required a stretch and a grip on the guide-chain bolted into the wall, and the chain was cold enough to burn. Water rose past their ankles on the third landing, past their knees by the tenth. Somewhere above, the entrance had sealed itself behind them—Petra heard the frozen waterfall knit closed with a sound like a struck bell—and after that there was only down.

The thoughts thickened as they went deeper. Older ones, dimmer ones. Petra passed a glow shaped like a wedding vow, spoken into rising water: *I do, I do, I do*. They passed a merchant calculating debts he would never settle, lips moving around sums that meant nothing now. They passed a child's thought that was only a color—the particular gold of late afternoon on a wheat field—and Petra had to press a hand over their own mouth to keep from weeping, though they couldn't have said why. The color was just a color. It was also everything.

"This is what the court does," Dain said quietly. His voice carried strangely in the flooded shaft, flattened by stone and water. "This is what it *keeps*."

"You've seen souls bottled before?" Sable asked.

"I've seen warrants executed on people who owed them." He climbed another step. Water sheeted off his shoulders. "I told myself the law was the law. That someone above me had weighed it." He looked down into the drifting lights. "No one weighed this."

Petra was about to answer when they saw it.

Below them, caught in an eddy where two flights of stairs met, a thought burned brighter than the rest. Not pale like the others but *warm*—amber, honey-colored, flickering like lamplight through kitchen smoke. Petra knew it before they understood it. The body knows what the mind has lost. Their feet were moving before their fear caught up, splashing down two steps, three, reaching out—

"Sable," Dain warned.

"I see it." Sable's hand closed on Petra's shoulder, hard. "That's yours, isn't it. From Ashmarket."

It was Maren.

Not Maren herself—Maren was a hundred leagues north, hollowed and breathing, remembering nothing of her sacrifice—but the *memory* of her, the one Petra had sold, and here in the drowned dark it had come loose from whatever ledger held it, drifting down the stair like all the other lost things. In the amber glow Petra could see the shape of the scene inside it: a kitchen. A table scarred by forty years of knives. Maren's hands moving over a ring of iron seals, and her voice—

The voice reached Petra across the water, thin as smoke, and spoke the words.

"*Ash keeps what fire forgets. Salt holds what sea releases.*"

Petra's whole body went rigid. Those were seal-words. Those were *the* seal-words—the missing syllables of the Hollow Gate's binding, the ones they'd traded away in Ashmarket without knowing what they were trading, the hole in their own mind that had ached like a phantom limb ever since. Here they were. Right here. Close enough to touch.

"Take it," Sable breathed. "Whatever it costs—Petra, *take it back*."

Petra reached into the warm water.

Their fingers closed around the memory, and it was like closing a hand around a handful of hearth-light—it had weight, warmth, the texture of a specific evening, flour on the table, Maren's thumb brushing flour from Petra's cheekbone the way she always did, absent, while her mouth recited the old words. For one impossible heartbeat Petra had all of it back. The kitchen. The smell of bread. The exact cadence of Maren's voice saying *again, slower, the gate doesn't forgive a rushed oath*—

And then it came apart.

It came apart the way the drowned thoughts came apart, the way every light in this black water was coming apart, slowly, endlessly, syllable by syllable. The flour dissolved. The table dissolved. Maren's hands thinned to outlines, to suggestions, to a warmth that was already cooling against Petra's palm. The seal-words repeated once more—*salt holds what sea releases*—and then the second line frayed mid-word, and the first line after it, and what remained in Petra's cupped hands was a glow with nothing left inside it, a lamp burning with no one home.

"No," Petra said. "No—come *back*—"

"Petra." Dain's voice, low and steady, the voice he must have used on grieving families for twenty years. "Look at it. Truly look."

They looked. The memory wasn't returning to them. It was *leaving*—drifting down the stair with all the others, joining the slow procession of ended things, sinking toward whatever floor the mountain had. What was lost could echo. It could surface. It could even, for one merciful moment, be held. But it could not return. The sale at Ashmarket had been real. The memory belonged to the water now, the way the drowned belonged to the water, and no amount of reaching would make a sold thing unsold.

Petra knelt in the flood on the landing, holding the fading ember of their grandmother's kitchen, and mourned.

Not loudly. There was no time for loudly, and besides, the stair was full of people who had died mid-sentence; who were they to wail about a memory? But their shoulders shook, and the water took the tears without comment, adding them to everything else it carried down.

Sable crouched beside them. For once she didn't joke. "I sold my brother's name once," she said. "To buy us both a night's lodging when the collectors were close. He laughed about it for years—called himself the man with no name, made up a new one every week." Her jaw tightened. "I'd give anything to hear him complain about it again. Anything except what the court is asking."

"What's it asking?" Petra wiped their face with a numb hand.

"That I become her." Sable nodded upward, toward the sealed entrance, toward the world. "Someone who sells people. That's the price of getting things back, down here. Everything costs the same coin: you."

Dain stood watch at the edge of the landing, hand on his voided warrant, watching the drowned thoughts drift past. "Then we pay differently," he said. "All three of us. We find another coin."

The ember in Petra's hands guttered once, twice—

And before it went out entirely, Petra did the only thing left to do. They *listened*. One last time, deliberately, memorizing—not the kitchen, not the flour, those were beyond saving—but the words. The seal-words. *Ash keeps what fire forgets. Salt holds what sea releases.* Two lines of the binding, recovered as echo instead of possession, written into the only ledger that couldn't be robbed: their living mind.

"There were four lines," Petra whispered as the light finally died between their fingers. "I know there were four. I remember knowing."

"Then you'll find the other two somewhere better than a flooded stair," Sable said, and hauled them to their feet. "Come on. The court's shelves are full of things people gave up. Maybe your grandmother's voice is on one of them."

That was a cruel hope and they both knew it. But it was a hope, and it moved their feet.

* * *

They climbed down for what felt like a day and might have been an hour. Time ran strangely in the water. Hunger came and went without pattern. The guide-chain grew warmer the deeper they went—which was wrong, Petra knew it was wrong, deep places are cold places, but the mountain's heart was not stone all the way through. Something down there generated heat the way a body does. Something down there was *alive*, or had been, or had eaten enough living things to run warm.

The thoughts changed character as they descended. Near the top they had been ordinary deaths—accidents, floods, missteps. Deeper, they became sentences. Petra passed a glow that was only the word *traitor*, repeated in a woman's furious voice until it wore smooth as river stone. Passed another that was a judge's pronouncement, delivered calmly into rising water: *the debt is affirmed; let the heir be collected*. Passed a dozen more in the same formal cadence, different voices, same verdict, stretching back further than any calendar Petra knew.

"These aren't accidents," Dain said grimly. "These are executions. The court has been drowning people on this stair for centuries."

"Debtors," Sable said. "Souls who tried to renege." She pointed with her chin at a cluster of lights circling a landing below, moving in a slow, deliberate pattern. "Look. They're not sinking anymore."

She was right. The oldest thoughts, the deepest ones, had stopped falling. They orbited the stair's final flight in a wide, silent spiral, hundreds of them, thousands, a galaxy of last words turning around a fixed point—and at the center of the spiral, at the bottom of everything, there was a door.

It stood at the foot of the last step, half-submerged, carved from a single slab of black stone, and it was covered in script. Undercourt script—the same flowing characters Sable had once stolen for, the same markings Ivo had copied onto his map in blood. But where the map's version had been a key, these were a lock, and the lock was *open*. The door stood ajar by exactly the width of a hand, and through the gap poured light the color of candleflame seen through amber wine, and warmth rolled out of it like a tide, and beneath the warmth, faint but unmistakable, came music.

Strings. Distant, elegant, patient. A court at its ease.

"The thoughts are guarding it," Petra realized aloud. "Or honoring it. Or—" They looked at the spiraling dead, at the last words circling the door like moths around a lamp, and understood. "Or they're waiting. All these centuries, they've been waiting for someone to go in and end the thing that drowned them."

"Careful," Dain murmured. "Hope is how that court feeds."

But Petra was already wading down the final steps, water climbing their thighs, their waist, the amber light gilding the surface of the black flood. Behind their ribs, the two recovered seal-words sat like swallowed coals. *Ash keeps what fire forgets. Salt holds what sea releases.* Half an oath. Half a weapon. Enough to recognize the rest when they saw it—maybe. Enough to walk into a hall of bottled souls and bargain with the thing that owned them.

Maybe.

At the threshold, Petra paused with one hand on the black stone, and looked back once—up the endless spiral, past the drifting lights, toward a valley they could no longer see and a grandmother who no longer remembered why she'd bled for it.

"I'm coming back with more than echoes," they told the dark. "I don't care what it costs."

The door swung wider at their touch, smooth as if oiled that morning, welcoming as a host.

And from within, the music stopped—as if the court had heard them, and was pleased, and had been waiting all along.
