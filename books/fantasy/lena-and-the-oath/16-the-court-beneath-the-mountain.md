## 16. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The Drowned Stair ended where no stair should end: in light.

Petra had expected darkness at the bottom of the world. They had expected the cold of deep water still clinging to their skin, the echo of those drowned last thoughts still whispering against their skull—*the seal-words, the seal-words*, Maren's voice given back to them as a ghost that could not be held. Instead, the final step rose out of black water onto pale stone, and above them stretched a cavern so vast it had weather. Clouds moved up there, slow and silver-grey, though there was no sky behind them—only rock so high it forgot it was rock.

And beneath those false clouds: the Undercourt.

It spread across the cavern floor like a city that had grown rather than been built. Terraces of white stone descended in wide arcs toward a central hall whose spires were carved from a single vein of crystal, glowing faintly from within, the way a lantern glows through paper. Bridges arched between towers without visible support. Water ran through channels cut into every street—not grey floodwater but something clearer than glass—and everywhere, everywhere, there were people. Or shapes that walked like people. Their movements were smooth as poured honey, their clothing rich without color, and none of them looked up at the false clouds. None of them seemed to look at anything at all.

"They're not souls," Sable said quietly at Petra's shoulder. She had gone very still on the last step, one hand on her knife out of habit rather than intent. "Souls don't get bodies down here. Those are the clerks. The staff." A pause. "Or they were people once. Down here it's hard to say where one ends."

Dain said nothing. He had not spoken since the Stair. Petra kept catching him touching the empty place at his belt where his warrant had hung for twenty years, the way a man touches a missing tooth.

"Stay close," Petra said. "Don't sign anything. Don't accept anything. Don't—" They hesitated. "Don't drink the water."

Sable's mouth curved, thin and humorless. "You're learning."

They descended together into the city of the dead's creditors.

* * *

The streets smelled of nothing, which was somehow worse than any smell. Petra had spent their whole life in a valley where magic could not exist, and they had thought they understood emptiness. But the Hollow Gate's null was a silence; this was a held breath. Magic saturated everything here—it pressed against Petra's skin like warm water, seeped through their boot soles, pooled in the corners of their eyes until every lamplight wore a halo. And yet the air carried no scent at all. No bread, no smoke, no rain. As if the court had decided smell was an extravagance.

The bottled souls were everywhere.

That was the thing no one had told them—the thing Ivo's map had only hinted at in its cramped, dying script. Petra had imagined a vault, a treasury, some guarded inner sanctum where the jars were kept. Instead the souls were displayed. Shelves lined the terraced streets, rising fifteen feet high, carved directly into the white stone, and on those shelves stood bottles of every shape: long-necked flasks, round-bellied jugs, slender vials like the ones apothecaries used for poisons. Each held a soft light, and each light was a different color—not the colors of fire or gemstone but the colors of feeling. A warm amber that made Petra's chest ache with homesickness just to glance at. A cold blue-green that tasted of grief across twenty feet of open air. A flickering violet that seemed, when Petra wasn't looking directly at it, to be laughing.

Small brass plaques were set beneath each bottle. Petra made the mistake of reading one.

*Marisol of the Fenlands. Purchased 61 years before present. Balance outstanding.*

The amber light inside the flask shifted as Petra read, pressing toward the glass toward them, and Petra felt it—a tiny, desperate warmth, like a hand brushing theirs in a crowd. They jerked back and nearly knocked over a passing clerk, who turned a face of perfect mildness toward them, murmured "Mind the shelves, honored guest," in a voice like a lullaby, and flowed on.

"Honored guest," Sable repeated under their breath. "That's new. Usually we'd be 'intruders' by now."

"We were invited," Petra said. The words came out flat. "Ivo told me. The debt calls its own."

They passed shelf after shelf after shelf. Thousands of lights. Tens of thousands. Some of the bottles were ancient, their plaques green with age, their contents dimmed to embers. Some were new—the glass still warm, the light inside still thrashing. Petra walked faster and made themselves stop counting, because counting was a kind of mourning and they could not afford to mourn ten thousand times before whatever came next.

But then, on a terrace near the crystal hall, on a shelf set slightly apart from all the others, on a small stand of dark wood like the ones that hold relics in village churches—

They saw it.

A jar. Round-bellied, plain, unremarkable except for the light inside it, which was unlike any other light on any other shelf. It was not amber or blue or violet. It was the color of the valley at dusk in autumn, when the light came low and gold over the eastern ridge and caught the dust above the gate-stones. It was the color of home.

The plaque read:

*Petra Vasquez. Born to the keeping line. Pledged before birth. Balance: due at solstice.*

Their own name. In their grandmother's handwriting—or a perfect copy of it, the careful letters Maren used for the ledger of gate-seals, the same slight hook she put on the z.

Petra did not remember crossing the terrace. They were simply there, both hands flat against the glass, and the light inside surged toward their palms like a dog pressing against a door it knows. Warmth flooded up their arms. Not magic exactly—something older, something that knew them, that recognized them the way their own reflection does. For one dizzy moment Petra felt twice as large, twice as real, as if half of them had been standing in this cavern all along, waiting in a jar, and now the halves were touching through three fingers of enchanted glass.

*Come home,* the light said, without words. *Come home, come home, come—*

A throat cleared behind them. Courteous. Patient. The sound of someone who has waited sixty years and can wait sixty more.

Petra turned.

* * *

The creditor was seated on a bench beside the shelf, as if it had been sitting there the whole time—as if it had perhaps always been sitting there, waiting for this exact conversation the way a farmer waits for rain. It wore the shape of an elderly person, dignified and spare: a long coat of undyed wool, hands folded atop a walking stick, a face lined like a well-read book. Its eyes were the only wrong thing about it. They were the grey of the collectors' robes, but deeper, as if the grey went all the way down, and looking into them was like looking into the Hollow Gate itself—that same sense of standing at the edge of something that does not reflect you back.

"You must be Petra," it said. Its voice was warm. Genuinely warm, or crafted so perfectly that the difference no longer mattered. "I confess I've looked forward to this meeting for a very long time. Do sit. Your legs must ache after the Stair. It always aches, the first descent."

"I'm fine standing."

"Of course. The young always prefer standing." It gestured gracefully at the jar between them. "May I say, yours is a particularly fine vintage. Keeping-line souls always are. All that discipline, all that quiet devotion—it concentrates beautifully. Your grandmother's was lovely too, in its day. Though hers, of course, was never for sale." A small, regretful smile. "Only the child's. That was the arrangement."

"You keep saying *arrangement*," Petra said. Their voice sounded steadier than they felt. Inside, everything was screaming—their soul was three inches from their hand, thrashing against the glass, and they could not take it, they could not even touch it again, because the clerk-shapes had begun to drift closer along the terrace edges, unhurried as tide. "My grandmother sold me. Before I was born. You call that an arrangement."

"I call it what it is." The creditor tilted its head, and there was something almost gentle in the motion. "Your grandmother came to us in a famine winter, carrying your mother, terrified, with a valley full of families about to starve or flee or do worse to one another. She asked for protection. We offered terms. She haggled—oh, she haggled wonderfully, your Maren; I've rarely enjoyed a negotiation more. She won concessions no keeper before her ever managed. Immunity for the whole valley. Permanence. No collector may cross while the debt stands unpaid." The grey eyes gleamed. "She bought you a childhood, Petra Vasquez. A whole life lived in safety, free of charge, on credit. And now the bill has come due, and you stand here calling it horror. Tell me—which of us is being uncivil?"

"The part where I'm a *vintage*."

"A figure of speech." It waved a hand, mild. "Would you prefer *asset*? *Holding*? Words are just containers, like the bottles. What matters is what's inside, and what's inside you is extraordinary." It leaned forward slightly, and its voice dropped into something like confidence, like intimacy, like a grandfather at a fireside. "Do you know how many souls pass through this court? Tens of thousands. And do you know what most of them are? Sold by others. Gambled away, traded, seized as debts owed to third parties. They arrive frightened and they stay frightened and most of them are never claimed at all." It tapped the walking stick once against the stone. "Yours was different. Yours was pledged by love. Misguided, costly, terrible love—but love nonetheless. In sixty years of business, I have learned to tell the difference. Your light burns differently. Anyone can see it."

"That doesn't make it fair."

"No," the creditor agreed pleasantly. "Fairness is not among our services. We offer certainty. Certainty is far more valuable, and considerably rarer." It rose, unfolding joint by joint, taller than it had seemed while seated, and gestured toward the great crystal hall below, where light spilled gold across the terraces. "But you didn't come all this way to discuss philosophy. You came because you believe the debt can be broken. Everyone who descends the Stair believes that. It's why we leave the Stair open, in fact—we find hope produces better-quality souls than despair. Despair goes flat in the bottle."

Petra's stomach turned. "You let Ivo come down here."

"Ivo." The creditor savored the name. "Yes. He fled his calling thirty years ago, ran to the edge of the magic lands, and spent three decades discovering that a pledged soul cannot outrun its pledge. He grew ill. He grew guilty. And in the end he crawled back across the boundary stones to press his burden into your hands, because guilt, too, is a kind of hope." The grey eyes settled on Petra, patient as sediment. "He died at your treeline believing he had saved you. He hadn't. He had simply delivered you. The map, the heir, the grand quest—all of it, every step, has been the debt collecting itself. You did not escape the bargain, child. You are *walking* it."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water, and the ripples went out and out. Petra thought of Ivo bleeding against the boundary stones. Thought of Maren's face when the truth finally broke open between them. Thought of every mile, every ally, every cost—Ashmarket, the memory of Maren's hands teaching the seals, sold away and echoing now only as a ghost in drowned water. Had it all been the road the court had already paved?

"Then why," Petra said slowly, "am I still holding my own soul? Why is the jar still on the shelf? If the debt collects itself, collect it. Take me. It's solstice in four days."

Something flickered across the creditor's face—the first expression Petra had seen on it that wasn't composed. It lasted less than a heartbeat, and then the courtesy closed over it like water over a sinking stone.

"Because," it said, "the court honors its own forms. A debt of this magnitude requires trial. Presentation of the original instrument. Witness. Judgment." It smiled, and the smile was warm, and the smile was terrible. "You invoked nothing, demanded nothing, and yet you shall receive the full ceremony. Consider it a courtesy extended to a keeping-line soul. Or consider it pride. Even we have our rituals, and your grandmother's oath is one of our treasures. It will be read aloud tomorrow, in the Hall of Instruments, in the voice of the one who swore it."

"Maren." Petra's throat went tight. "Her voice is hollowed. The collectors took it."

"Did they?" The creditor began to walk, unhurried, expecting to be followed, and Petra followed because the alternative was to stand alone on a terrace with ten thousand bottled dead. "Then it will be an interesting hearing indeed. Come. Your companions are already being shown to quarters—they'll be pleased to know the thief's brother's soul is in excellent condition, and the justiciar's old warrant has generated considerable discussion among the junior judges. Everyone here is so looking forward to tomorrow." It paused at the head of the great stair leading down into the crystal light, and turned, and for a moment the elderly shape seemed thinner, as if the person-shape were a coat worn over something with far too many joints.

"One piece of advice, freely given, as a mark of respect." The grey eyes held Petra's. "Tomorrow, when the oath is read and the judgment comes—do not do what Ivo did. Do not run. Souls that run go flat." Another smile, warmer still. "And yours is far too fine a vintage to waste."

Below them, the Hall of Instruments blazed like a lantern, and somewhere inside it, on a shelf of treasures, an oath signed in blood sixty years ago waited in the dark to speak with a dead woman's stolen voice.
