## 17. The Trial of Debts

Dain had argued in a hundred courtrooms, and every one of them had been built to make the truth easier to hear. Benches for the accused's family. Windows cut so that daylight fell across the witness stand, because even the most corrupt magistrate liked to pretend that lies withered in the sun.

The Undercourt was not built for truth. It was built like a throat.

The hall beneath Mount Ceren curved inward on all sides, walls of black stone veined with something that pulsed faintly, slow as a sleeping heartbeat. There were no windows. The light came from below—from channels cut into the floor where a pale luminescence drifted like milk stirred into water. And the benches, the galleries rising tier upon tier into the dark above, were full. Not with people. Dain had spent twenty years learning to tell people from the things that wore them, and what filled those galleries was the latter: collectors in their grey robes, judges whose faces he almost recognized and then didn't, things that might once have been petitioners before the court finished digesting them.

At the center of it all stood the shelf.

It ran the length of the hall, taller than three men, and every inch of it was jars. Souls, bottled. Some glowed warm as hearth-coals. Some flickered like dying candles. Some were dark, and Dain found he could not look at those for long without his chest tightening, because he knew what a soul looked like when it had stopped hoping anyone would come for it.

And there—third shelf from the top, small enough to cup in two hands—burned a light the color of a valley morning. Petra's soul. Labeled in silver script with a name Petra had never been allowed to know they had.

*Vasquez, born of Maren. Pledged before breath.*

Dain straightened his back. His warrant had been voided eleven days ago; his oath had died the moment the Undercourt bought his own judge out from under him. He had no standing here. No rank. No law at his back but the one he carried inside his ribs, which was the only kind that had ever mattered.

"Petra Vasquez," said the creditor.

The entity stood opposite them across the floor of pale light, and Dain hated it more than he had ever hated anything, precisely because it was courteous. It wore the shape of an elderly man in scholar's robes, kind-faced, patient, the way a well-kept ledger is patient. Its voice never rose. It had never needed to.

"You have been brought home," it said. "The debt is due at solstice, which is now two nights distant. You may spend those nights in comfort, if you wish. Many heirs have."

"I invoke the Trial of Debts."

The words came from beside Dain, and even now, hearing them, his heart lurched. Petra stood with their chin up and Maren's seal-ring clenched in their fist, and their voice did not shake, though Dain could see the effort it cost the way he could see a rope straining under load.

Silence took the hall. Not empty silence—the silence of ten thousand grey heads turning at once.

The creditor tilted its head, and something moved behind its kind old face, something old and cold recalculating. "That right," it said slowly, "has not been invoked in four hundred years."

"Then perhaps you've forgotten how it works." Dain stepped forward before his fear could argue him out of it. "I haven't. I enforced oaths for twenty years, and the first law carved above my order's door—the *first* law, older than your shelves, older than this mountain's name—was this: *any contract may be contested by trial.* Any. A bargain made in secret, over an unborn child, without the knowledge or consent of the bound party—" he gestured at Petra, "—is exactly the kind of contract that right exists to test."

"The consent was given," the creditor said mildly. "By the keeper Maren Vasquez, freely and with full understanding."

"Given *for* someone else. Given *over* someone else." Dain let his voice carry up into the galleries, into all those listening dark faces. "You claim to be a court. Courts weigh. So weigh it. If your bargain is fair—if it is everything you believe it to be—then a trial can only confirm it. What does a patient creditor have to fear from its own rules?"

He saw it then, just for a moment: the flicker. Not anger. *Inconvenience.* The look of a creature that had expected obedience and received procedure instead.

The galleries murmured like wind through dead reeds.

"Very well," the creditor said. "The Trial of Debts is granted. One advocate for the debtor. One reading of the original instrument. The court will summon its witnesses." Its kind eyes settled on Petra, and its smile was genuinely warm, which was the most horrifying thing Dain had seen all day. "Choose your ground carefully, keeper-heir. Trials in this court are binding on both parties. Lose, and the debt is sealed beyond any future contest. Forever."

"I know," Petra said.

Dain did not look at them. He had told them the same thing on the Stair, in the drowned dark, with the last thoughts of dead souls glowing around their ankles, and Petra had answered: *Then we don't lose.* He had believed them then. He chose to believe them now.

* * *

They were given one hour to prepare. The court provided a chamber—a courtesy, the creditor called it—and left them alone in a round stone room lit by a single jar mounted in the wall, its glow the color of deep water.

Sable paced. She had not stopped pacing since they'd descended past the last of the flooded steps, and her hands kept moving too, flexing, closing, finding nothing to hold. Her brother's jar was somewhere on those shelves. Dain had watched her find it. He had watched her press her palm flat against the glass until a collector pried her away, and he understood now why she had come all this way, and he understood, too, the exact shape of the temptation the court would offer her before this was done. He had spent twenty years watching people break. He knew the sound a person makes just before.

"We need a strategy," Sable said. "Not a speech. A strategy. That thing has been doing this since before my grandmother's grandmother learned to steal."

"It will produce the oath," Dain said. "Maren's oath. Read aloud, in her own voice—that's how these trials work. The instrument itself is testimony. I've seen lesser courts do it with written confessions." He turned to Petra. "When you hear her voice, it is going to feel like the floor opening. It will be her voice and it will not be her, and the thing wearing it will say words she swore with love in her heart, and the court will use that love against you. Can you stand there and listen to that?"

Petra was quiet for a long moment. In the jar-light their face looked young and old at once.

"When the collectors hollowed her," Petra said finally, "she looked at me. She couldn't remember my name anymore—I watched it go out of her like a candle—but she looked at me, and she *held on*, Dain. Whatever's left of her down here, whatever they summon—it held on then. It'll hold on now. And if it can't—" Their jaw tightened. "Then that's one more thing the court owes me."

Dain nodded slowly. Twenty years of watching people break, and here was someone deciding, deliberately, in front of him, not to.

"One more thing," he said. "The trial binds both parties. That cuts both ways. If the court loses, the judgment binds *it*. So we don't argue that the oath was cruel—we argue that it was *void*. Cruelty isn't a legal flaw. Flawed consent is. We keep it narrow. Keep it clean. Promise me you'll let me do the arguing, and no matter what you hear from that stand, you don't speak until I give you the signal."

"And what's the signal?"

"You'll know it," Dain said grimly. "It'll be the moment I'm losing."

Sable snorted despite herself. Petra almost smiled. It wasn't much. But in the throat of the mountain, it was enough to build on.

* * *

The trial convened in the great hall, and Dain stood alone on the pale-lit floor while the galleries pressed close above him like a tide deciding whether to come in.

The creditor presided from a seat of black stone—not a judge's bench, Dain noted, but a *throne*, and he filed that away, because a throne was a confession. Courts served something. Thrones only served themselves.

"The instrument," the creditor said, and the hall changed.

The light in the floor-channels dimmed. The pulse in the black walls slowed. And from the shadowed archway at the far end of the hall, a figure came walking.

Grey-robed. Barefoot. Moving with the careful, halting gait of someone carrying something fragile that they could no longer see.

Dain heard Petra's breath catch beside him and did not dare turn his head.

They had brought Maren.

Not her body—her body lay in the valley, breathing, empty. This was the rest of her. The memories the collectors had siphoned, worn like a garment by whatever walked in her shape, her face slack and smooth as river-worn stone, her eyes fixed on nothing. The galleries leaned toward her the way flowers lean toward sun. Even the jars on the great shelf seemed to brighten, feeding on her proximity.

"The witness," the creditor announced gently, "is the oath-taker herself. Or such portion of her as the court holds in trust."

"You're desecrating her," Dain said.

"I am *preserving* her," the creditor replied, with real, infuriating sincerity. "Everything given to this court is preserved. Nothing is wasted. That is more than the surface world can promise anyone. Now." It lifted one hand. "Read the instrument."

The thing that had been Maren Vasquez opened its mouth, and Maren's voice came out.

Dain had met her only days ago, in the valley, when she'd stood between Petra and the collectors with fury in her like a drawn blade. He had heard that voice crack with rage and grief. Now it came out level, unhurried, reciting—and that was worse. Because it was still her voice. Every cadence. The slight roughness at the bottom of it. The way she pronounced *valley* like a word she'd built her whole life around.

*"I, Maren Vasquez, keeper of the Hollow Gate, being of sound mind and full knowledge, do pledge the following:"*

Petra's hand found Dain's sleeve. He felt the tremor in it and did not pull away. Let them hold on. That was the point of sleeves.

*"That the valley shall remain empty of magic, wholly and forever, so long as the debt stands paid. That the peace of my people shall be purchased as it was purchased before me, as it was purchased before that, in the coin this court accepts."*

A pause. The hollowed thing's chest rose and fell.

*"I pledge the child unborn to me. My blood. My heir. Their soul, entire, to the Undercourt, due and deliverable upon the winter solstice of their twenty-fifth year. I give this freely. I give this knowing what it costs. I give it because I love them, and because love is the only currency I have that the world below will take."*

The voice stopped. The silence afterward had weight, physical weight, pressing down on the hall like snowfall.

Dain had prepared himself for many things. He had not prepared himself for that last line. *Because I love them.* Spoken in her voice, in this place, it was a knife turned in the wound—and it was also, he realized with a cold drop through his stomach, the strongest card the court held. How did you argue against an oath sworn in love? How did you call a mother's sacrifice void without making the sacrifice itself meaningless?

The creditor spread its hands, beneficent as sunrise.

"There it is," it said to the galleries, to the hall, to Dain. "Full knowledge. Free consent. Given in love. Four hundred years of contracts, advocates, and I have rarely handled one so clean. What flaw does the debtor's counsel find in it? Speak, justiciar. Your order wrote half the laws this court honors."

Every eye in the mountain was on him. Somewhere above, Sable stood rigid in the gallery. Beside him, Petra had gone very still, and Dain knew—they both knew—that the oath had done exactly what the court intended. It had made the floor open under them.

But Dain Hartwell had spent twenty years in courtrooms, and he had learned one thing the corrupt magistrates never did: the truth does not need to be pretty to win. It only needs to be *precise*.

He stepped forward onto the pale light, and his voice rang off the black walls, steady as a hammer.

"I find the flaw in the first line," he said. "*Being of sound mind and full knowledge.* Full knowledge of what, precisely? Did Maren Vasquez know that the valley's null would hollow her when she resisted? Did she know that her memories—the very mind that gave that consent—would become this court's property, held in trust, wheeled out whenever the debt needs polishing?" He pointed at the grey-robed figure swaying gently in the archway. "You accepted payment twice. Once in the child's soul, and once in the oath-taker's self. The contract names one price. A contract that takes more than it names is not a bargain. It is theft with paperwork."

The galleries rustled. The creditor's smile thinned by a degree no ordinary eye would catch. Dain's would.

"And further," he pressed, turning, letting his voice climb into the tiers, "the contract binds a party who gave no consent and possessed no knowledge—because they did not yet exist. You may answer that custom permits it. Custom permitted a great many things my order burned out of the law, one clause at a time. But here is the question this court must answer, the only question, and I put it to you before every soul in this hall, bottled and free alike:" He drew a breath. "If a debt can be created without a debtor's knowledge, sold without their consent, and enforced by hollowing everyone who ever loved them—then what, precisely, distinguishes this court from the thieves it claims to regulate?"

Silence. Real silence this time, vast and dangerous.

The creditor regarded him for a long, long moment. When it spoke again, its voice was still courteous, but the warmth had gone out of it like heat leaving a corpse.

"That," it said, "is a question of philosophy. Trials weigh *contracts*. And the contract stands. Unless the debtor can show a flaw in the consent itself—not the price, not the manner, the *consent*—the oath is valid, the debt is due, and the trial concludes at midnight tomorrow." It rose from the throne of black stone. "The court grants the debtor one night to find their flaw. Use it well."

As it turned to go, it paused, and looked back at Petra with those kind, bottomless eyes.

"For what it is worth, keeper-heir—I hope you fail. I have read your soul. It is a fine one. It would be a shame to dissolve it."

It swept from the hall, and the galleries began to empty, and the thing wearing Maren's memories turned with its halting gait and shuffled back toward the dark archway from which it had come.

And Petra broke.

Not loudly. They simply moved—one step, two, toward the archway, Dain grabbing their arm, and the sound they made was small and terrible, a single syllable of her name bitten in half. "She's in *there*—Dain, that's *her*, we can't just—"

"No." He held on. His grip was iron; twenty years of dragging the condemned had taught him how. "Listen to me. Listen. That is bait. The moment you cross that archway, the trial ends, the debt seals, and everything we just did becomes decoration. *That is what it wants.*"

"But it's *her*—"

"It's what's left after the court ate her." He said it brutally, because gentleness would lose this fight. "And you want to free her? Then win. Win tomorrow, break every debt on those shelves, and every stolen memory walks home—including hers. That is the only door out of this mountain that leads anywhere you want to go."

Petra stood trembling in his grip. Above them, Sable had come down from the gallery and stood waiting, silent, her eyes flicking once—just once—toward the great shelf, toward a particular jar burning low and blue.

Then Petra went still. The trembling stopped the way a storm stops—not gradually, but all at once, the air going flat and hard.

"All right," they said quietly. "One night to find the flaw." They turned to Dain, and their eyes in the jar-light were the coldest he had ever seen them. "So tell me, justiciar. You've read every oath your order ever enforced. Where does a contract like mine break?"

Dain opened his mouth—

And the lights in the floor went out, every channel at once, and from the darkness of the archway came the sound of Maren's voice, no longer reciting, no longer hollow, screaming.
