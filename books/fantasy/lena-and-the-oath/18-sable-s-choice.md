## 18. Sable's Choice

Sable had spent her whole life learning the exact weight of a lie—how much it cost to carry, how much it fetched on the open market—and so she knew, standing in the gallery of the Undercourt while Dain argued for Petra's soul like a man defending a drowning stranger with his bare hands, that the voice in her ear was not lying to her.

That was the worst part.

*You want proof?* The creditor's advocate stood beside her in the shadow of a pillar carved from black salt, and it wore no face at all, just a smooth oval like an egg left too long in the dark. *Look up.*

She looked up.

Above the dais, suspended in silver chains from a vaulted ceiling that seemed to breathe, hung the shelves. Thousands of jars, thousands of lights, each one a soul dimmed and bottled and labeled in script that hurt to read. Somewhere among them was Tam.

Tam, who had been eleven when he sold himself to pay their mother's debts. Eleven, because Sable—who had been fifteen and already good at stealing—had been caught, and the broker who owned her string had offered a choice: Sable's hands broken so she could never pick another lock, or her brother's name entered into the ledger. She'd chosen. She'd told herself every night since that there had been no real choice at all, but the Weeping Woods had shown her the truth of it, hadn't it? The trees remembered. She had signed. She had wept while she signed, and the tears had changed nothing.

*Tam's jar,* the faceless advocate said, and something in the air beside her ripened into the smell of rain on hot stone, which was how Tam used to smell coming home. *Third shelf, western wall, marked with his true name. Yours, freely given. No debt. No price. All you must do is this:*

The trial below reached a crescendo. Dain's voice cracked across the hall like a whip—"The oath was sworn over an unborn child! No court in any age has honored a contract made with someone who could not speak!"—and the judges stirred on their thrones, wearing the faces of dead men and women, wearing them slightly wrong, like masks hung by children.

*When they run,* the advocate said, *and they will run, you will slow them. A dropped torch. A false turn. A word in the wrong ear. That is all. And your brother walks out of here whole.*

Sable looked down at Petra.

Petra stood at the center of the trial floor with their chin up and their hands shaking—they thought nobody noticed the shaking, but Sable noticed everything; it was her trade—and they were doing the single stupidest, bravest thing Sable had ever witnessed. They were contesting a contract inside the creditor's own hall, on the creditor's own terms, armed with nothing but a disgraced justiciar and a stolen memory that came back to them as echoes instead of words. They had sold their best memory of Maren to buy information, and the loss still showed on their face some mornings, a small confusion where certainty used to live.

They trusted her.

That was the thing Sable kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. After Ashmarket, after the waystation, after the broker's offer she'd stalled on and warned them about—after everything—Petra still turned to her first when the map needed reading. Still slept with their back to hers in the cold. Still said *we* as if it were a fact rather than a gamble.

And the woods had shown her what she was. Not might be. *Was.* Sable sells people out. It is what Sable does. The trees didn't lie; they only remembered.

"Answer me," the advocate said gently. "Or shall I take your silence as the answer I expected? Everyone answers eventually, thief. Most of them say yes."

Below, one of the judges raised a hand, and the doors at the far end of the hall ground open, and Maren Vasquez walked in.

Not walked. Was *walked*, like a puppet with its strings held by something behind the curtain of the air. Her eyes were the hollowed grey of old ash, and when she opened her mouth, her voice came out flat and borrowed, reciting the original oath in her own cadence, her own accent, the valley vowels Sable had heard Petra slip into sometimes when they were tired. Every word of it landed in the hall like a stone into still water. *I pledge that which is not yet born, that which will carry my blood and my keeping, to the Court Beneath, in exchange for the silence of the valley, until the solstice seals it.*

Petra made a sound Sable would hear for the rest of her life. Small. Like a door closing very far away.

Dain kept arguing. That was Dain—he argued the way other men prayed—but Sable could see the shape of the ending now, and so, clearly, could the court. The judges were patient. Patience was their native language. They would let him finish, let the ritual of justice play out like a mummer's show, and then they would collect, because the oath was real, and oaths were the one currency this place never devalued.

Which meant the escape would happen soon. Which meant the advocate's offer was live *now*.

"Yes," Sable said.

The word came out of her smooth and easy, the way all her best lies did.

The advocate rippled with something like pleasure. "Wise."

"Show me his jar first." She kept her voice bored, transactional, a broker's voice, a liar's voice. "I don't work on promises. You know that about me. Your brokers taught me."

A chain sang somewhere above, and a jar descended through the breathing dark into the advocate's waiting non-hands, and there he was.

Tam.

Not the man of twenty-three he would have been—the soul in the jar was preserved at the moment of its taking, and souls apparently aged like wine in reverse—so what floated in the pale glass was eleven years old, thin, gap-toothed, scowling at the world with the fierce offended dignity of a boy who had been told he was saving his sister and had decided to believe it. His light was gutter-low after twelve years in the bottle, but it flickered when Sable leaned close, the way candle flames lean toward a draft, toward a door, toward home.

"Sable," she whispered, testing the weight of it. "His name is Tam. Say it."

"Tam," the advocate repeated, courteous as a banker. "Yours. On completion."

She nodded. She smiled her market smile, the one that had gotten her out of a hundred rooms and into ninety more she should have avoided. And inside her chest, behind the smile, behind the lie, she set the truth down carefully where she could reach it fast:

*There is no version of this where I hand you Petra.*

She had known it since the words left her mouth. Maybe before. Maybe in the Weeping Woods, when the trees showed her the betrayal and she had screamed at them that they were wrong, and the trees had done what trees do, which is remember, and wait.

* * *

The trial broke the way dams break—not gradually, all at once.

It was Dain who tore it. He did something no one in ten thousand years of Undercourt procedure had apparently thought to forbid: he demanded the oath's *original instrument* be produced for inspection, and when the clerks—grey, silent, faceless as the advocate—brought forth a scroll of skin that predated the valley itself, he read aloud the clause no one had ever bothered to read aloud, the one written in the margin in a different hand, older and smaller: *This pledge binds the bloodline of the keeper, save where the keeper's heir speaks the seal-words freely given.*

Freely given.

Petra went very still, and Sable saw them understand it in the same instant she did—Ivo's map, the margins, the dying courier's last gift. The seal-words weren't a key the court could confiscate. They were a gift, and gifts couldn't be ungiven, and Petra carried them in their blood like a second heartbeat.

The hall understood it a half-second later, and the half-second was enough for chaos.

The judges rose. The chains above began to swing, jars knocking against jars with a sound like distant bells mourning. Collectors poured in through doors that hadn't been doors a moment ago, grey robes billowing around bodies that were only mostly bodies, and Dain roared "RUN, YOU IDIOTS" with the full authority of twenty years of enforcing order, which was apparently exactly enough authority to break it.

They ran.

Sable ran with them, and the advocate's voice ran beside her, silk-smooth even now: *Any time, thief. A stumble. A shout. Point them down the wrong stair. Your brother waits.*

They burst through the eastern colonnade, Petra clutching their own jar—their own *soul*, labeled with a name Sable had never heard them use—to their chest like a child, Dain covering the rear with a torn-off piece of balustrade wielded like a mace, and ahead of them the corridor split: left, toward the great stair they'd come by, drowned and rising now with the court's alarm; right, toward a smaller passage Sable had mapped in her head during the trial, a servant's route, narrow, unguarded, faster.

*Left,* said the advocate, sweet as rot. *Tell them left. The servants' stair floods at the first bell. Tell them left and they drown, and you lose nothing, and Tam goes free.*

Petra skidded to a halt at the fork and looked at her. Looked at her the way they always did, with that terrible open trust, that assumption of *we*. "Sable—which way?"

The bell began to toll. Water was already sighing somewhere below the floor.

Every lie Sable had ever told had been cheap. That was the secret of lying nobody admitted: it was easy, it was *lighter* than truth, it cost nothing at the moment of telling and only billed you later, in the dark, with interest. She had built her whole life on that arithmetic. Sell the brother or lose the hands. Stall the broker or lose the road. There was always a reason, always a knife at the throat of the choice, always no real decision at all.

But there was no knife here. There was only a boy in a jar and a person with shaking hands asking her which way, and the arithmetic laid out plain at last, in a hall lit by ten thousand stolen lives:

Some things don't balance.

"Right," Sable said. "Servants' stair. Move."

She felt the advocate go still beside her, felt the attention of the whole breathing hall pivot onto her like a lens focusing, and she heard—not in her ears, in the marrow of her—*Thief. You are forfeiting—*

"Yeah," Sable said. "I know."

And then she did the thing she'd been carrying since the jar descended. She turned, and she ran *back*—back into the swinging dark, back under the shelves, back along the western wall while Dain bellowed her name and Petra screamed it—and she leapt onto the third shelf's iron rail with a thief's grip and a thief's timing, catching the swaying chain one-handed as the collectors closed in from both ends of the aisle.

The jar was warm. Of course it was. It had been waiting twelve years for her.

"Hey, Tam," she whispered. "Sorry I'm late."

Then she threw it down.

Not set it down—threw it, hurled it against the stone floor with every ounce of strength grief and fury and love could put behind her arm, and the glass burst like a struck star, and the light inside it didn't die.

It *rose*.

Eleven years old forever, gap-toothed, fierce—it came up out of the broken vessel like smoke from a snuffed fire, unfurling, expanding, filling the aisle with a brightness that had no source and needed none, and Sable felt it recognize her, felt it press against her like a forehead against hers, the way he used to when he was small and scared and pretending not to be.

And then, because a soul cannot return to a body twelve years drowned, because dissolution is what freedom means down here, the light began to come apart—gently, in long bright threads, like breath fading off a window—and Sable held her arms around it anyway, held the shape of it as long as holding meant anything, and she laughed, or sobbed, or both, and said, "Go on, then. Go on. You're paid off. Nobody owns you. *Nobody owns you.*"

The last thread of light touched her cheek, cool as rain on hot stone.

Then it was everywhere and nowhere, and the nearest shelf of jars—all along the western wall, hundreds of them—flickered in answer, like candles catching from one another, like a message passing hand to hand down a row of prisoners: *it can be done, it can be done, it can be done.*

The collectors stopped moving. For one full second, the entire Undercourt stared at the empty space where a debt had been, and Sable understood, with a thief's clarity, that she had just committed the only crime this place had no law for.

She had destroyed property by setting it free.

The screaming started in the walls.

* * *

She was still laughing—ugly, gasping, alive—when Dain hauled her off the shelf by the collar and Petra grabbed her wrist, and the three of them ran together down the servants' stair as the whole mountain came apart around them, shelves toppling, jars bursting in cascades of liberated light, the judges' borrowed faces sliding off their skulls like wet masks.

"You absolute fool," Dain was shouting over the roar. "You had him *free and clear*, you had the deal—"

"I know," Sable said.

"He was *right there*—"

"I know." She was crying and grinning and running all at once, and it felt like the truest thing she'd ever done with her body. "That's why I broke it. Don't you get it? Anything they give me, they own. Anything they *give* anybody, they own—that's the whole rotten ledger, that's the whole court. So I took it off the books." She looked at Petra, running beside her, jar clutched to chest, eyes wide. "Your brother's soul isn't in a jar anymore either, is it? I felt it. When mine broke, yours answered."

Petra's hand flew to the glass at their chest. Through the pale curve of it, faint as dawn under a door, their own light was burning brighter than it had any right to.

"The debts are linked," Petra breathed. "If one breaks clean—if one's freed without being *paid*—"

"Then the ledger's wrong," Sable finished. "And ledgers hate being wrong."

Above them, far back in the collapsing dark, the great bell tolled again—once, twice—and began, horribly, to toll *backward*.

"That'll be the solstice count breaking," Dain said grimly, shouldering open the door at the stair's bottom, onto cold wind and starlight and the black shoulder of Mount Ceren falling away beneath them. "Whatever you two just did, the court knows it. And everything down there that still has legs is going to come up those stairs after us."

Sable spat blood, grinned, and drew her knives.

"Then let's be gone before it does," she said. "We've got a gate to reach—and for once in my life, I intend to keep my word."

Behind them, the mountain's peak split with red light, and the pursuit began.
