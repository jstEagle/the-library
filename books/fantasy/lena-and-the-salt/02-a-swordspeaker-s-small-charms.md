## 2. A Swordspeaker's Small Charms

The charms were lies, and Emrys Dunmore had made his peace with that a long time ago.

He sat behind his stall in the Tanners' Row market with his hood up and his hands folded, watching the morning crowd shuffle past like water around a stone he wished would stop existing. Before him, laid out on a square of faded velvet, were forty-one luck-charms: knucklebones wrapped in red thread, river-stones drilled and strung on leather cords, iron nails bent into crooked rings and sealed in wax the color of dried blood. Each one had cost him a copper's worth of materials and an hour of careful, meaningless work. Each one was worth three coppers to someone desperate enough to buy hope from a stranger with a scarred face and a voice too quiet for the size of him.

None of them worked. That was the point. That had always been the point, since the day the last of the true charms went cold in his hands and the order that made them burned its own books to ash.

*Hollow things,* his old master would have called them, if his old master were alive to call them anything. *A swordspeaker who sells empty charms is a priest selling pictures of bread.*

His old master had also died face-down in a ditch outside Corvane with a witch-hunter's arrow between his shoulders, so Emrys tried not to weigh his opinions too heavily.

"Two coppers," said a woman at the front of the stall, holding up a knucklebone charm and squinting at it the way people squinted at things they wanted to believe in. "You said three."

"I say three to everyone," Emrys said. "You look like you've had a hard month."

She looked at him then — really looked, at the burn-scar down the left side of his jaw, at the grey in his beard that made him older than his thirty-four years — and something in her face softened into the particular pity strangers offered him, which he hated more than fear. She paid two coppers. He took them without meeting her eyes.

"For your daughter," she said, tucking the charm into her shawl. "The fever ward at Saint Aldery's. If it doesn't work—"

"It's a knucklebone and some thread," Emrys said flatly. "It works exactly as much as you do."

But she was already walking away, clutching the lie against her breastbone like a second heart, and he watched her go with the taste of old ash in his mouth. This was his penance, or as close to one as the world allowed him: to sell false magic in a city where magic was outlawed, to take coins from the frightened and the grieving, and to know — only he, in all of Vell, perhaps only he — precisely how much nothing he was giving them.

Three years since the Breaking. Three years since he had stood in the covenant hall beneath Greywater with a usurper's blade at his throat and a usurper's promise in his ear, and spoken the unbinding words in a voice that shook, and felt every god in the world go silent all at once, like a house full of people holding their breath.

He had believed he was saving the free cities. He still told himself that, most mornings, when the dreams came. The god-war was coming; the old pacts were fraying; ten thousand would die in the first season alone. Better a quiet world than a burning one. Better silence than slaughter.

Better, apparently, to become a man who sold knucklebones to mothers with sick daughters.

Emrys rubbed his scarred jaw and reached under the counter for his flask. It was nearly noon. The sun had burned off the harbor fog, and the market smelled of tar and frying fish and the particular brine-rot of Tanner's Row, which no amount of salt ever quite covered. Salt. Even here, even now, everything came back to salt. The free cities ran on it — preserved their fish, paid their tariffs, swore their small oaths over it. *Bread and salt*, the old greeting went, though nobody remembered anymore what the salt had once meant, what it had once carried, what it had been for.

He remembered. That was his curse among curses. He remembered what salt had been before it was only seasoning.

He was reaching for a charm to rearrange — any motion, any small labor to keep his hands from shaking — when the flare happened.

It began, as flares always did, in the bones. Not his bones. The stall's. The wood of the counter hummed like a struck bell, low and sweet, a note he hadn't heard in three years and knew instantly, horribly, the way a drowning man knows water. Then the velvet square lit from beneath, not with light but with *attention*, as though something vast and patient had turned its face toward the little table and everything on it.

And the charms woke up.

All forty-one of them. The knucklebones rattled in their threads. The river-stones glowed faintly, a pearl-grey luminescence like moonlight through fog. The iron rings warmed until the wax seals softened and began, very slowly, to weep. And through it all ran a sound Emrys had spent three years learning to live without: a singing, thin and high and unmistakable, the sound of the old covenant answering, the sound of salt remembering what it was for.

A customer two stalls down dropped a basket of eels. Somewhere a child began to cry. And Emrys sat frozen behind his humming, glowing, impossible stock of hollow lies, and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling:

*That's not mine.*

Because it wasn't. He had checked these charms a hundred times. Hollow, all of them, dead as river stones — this was not power leaking from him, because there was no power left in him to leak. This was something else. Something near. Something *awake*. A wellspring opened somewhere in the city, close enough that its overflow had pooled into the nearest empty vessels, and his stall full of beautiful fakes had been the emptiest thing in reach.

The gods were answering prayers again. Through salt, through stone, through whatever cracked vessel lay open nearest the source — and the source was close. Two streets, maybe three. He could feel the direction of it the way he'd once felt the pull of iron, back when feeling such things hadn't been a hanging offense.

Which meant, he realized with a cold slide of dread, that anyone else trained in the old arts could feel it too.

He looked up.

Across the market square, by the fountain where the fishwives argued about prices, stood three figures in grey cloaks. Wardens of the Purified Covenant — witch-hunters, though the law called them something gentler. They weren't arguing about prices. One held a ledger. One held a long brass instrument like a weathervane, its needle swinging, swinging, and then settling — settling toward the north, toward Tanner's Row, toward the little stall with the velvet cloth and the forty-one charms that had no business shining like votive lamps.

Toward him.

Emrys had run from better men than these, and worse ones, and he had learned the geometry of flight the way other men learned scripture. His body moved before his mind finished the arithmetic: sweep the charms into the satchel — the velvet too, the velvet was evidence — kick the brazier sideways to scatter the crowd — *go*.

He was three stalls away when the first Warden's voice cracked across the square like a whip.

"The seller of charms! Stop, in the name of the Usurper's grace!"

He did not stop. He went through the fish-market at a dead sprint, shoulder-checking a handcart of mackerel into the path of the nearest Warden, vaulting a stack of lobster pots, taking the alley behind the smokehouse at a speed that tore his hood back and left his scarred face bare to the morning. Behind him: boots, shouting, the high whine of the brass instrument being swung like a compass needle finding north. Finding *him*. Because his satchel was full of singing stones, because he had been fool enough to stand at the center of the flare, because after three years of being no one he had been seen, marked, *named* — *the seller of charms* — and names, in the Usurper's Vell, were death warrants waiting for ink.

He cut left at the tannery, right at the drainage channel, down the stairs that stank of offal and into the warren of cellars beneath the fish-quarter, where the ceiling beams dripped and the dark smelled of ice and old blood. He ran until his lungs burned, then walked, then stopped, pressed against a wall of wet stone in the black, listening.

The boots passed overhead. Faded. Gone, for now.

In the darkness, his satchel was still glowing. Softly, gently, the way a banked fire glows — the river-stones shedding their pearl-grey light through the canvas seams, patient as tide. Emrys sank down against the wall with the bag in his lap and opened it, and the light spilled up over his scarred face, and he let himself look at what he had spent three years refusing to look at.

They were still singing. All of them. Quietly. Hopefully. Like voices through a door left ajar.

*Something woke in Vell.* He'd heard the Wardens' ledger phrase repeated in tavern gossip for weeks now, half rumor, half joke. He had told himself it was nothing — a smuggler's candle, a hedge-witch's herb-charm, the usual noise of a city full of desperate people. He had told himself the covenant was broken beyond mending, because he had broken it, and a man cannot bear to believe his great sin might be undone by someone else's small miracle.

But this was no hedge-witch. This was no smuggler's trick. This was the deep current itself, the old answering-voice, awake and *reaching* — reaching out through salt and stone and the hollow hearts of empty charms, searching for hands to hold it. And it was young. That was the thing that undid him, sitting in the dark with the light on his face: the signature felt *young*. New-awakened. Untaught. Untrained. Alone, in a city crawling with hunters, carrying a gift that the Usurper had spent three years and ten thousand arrests making certain no one would survive possessing.

Someone out there had just done the most dangerous thing a person in Vell could do.

Someone out there had prayed, and been answered.

Emrys closed the satchel, muffling the light, and sat a moment longer in the dripping dark with his guilt rising in him like floodwater — because he knew what came next, had always known, had known it the day he spoke the unbinding words: that the silence would not hold forever, that the gods would find another door, and that when they did, the man who had locked the last one would owe whatever remained of his ruined life to holding the new one open.

He stood. He settled the satchel across his back, where the stones sang softly against his spine like a heartbeat not his own.

Then he climbed the cellar stairs toward the fish-quarter, toward the north streets, following the scent of answered prayer — and did not yet know that the prayer had come from a salt-seller's daughter, or that her door was already being kicked in.
