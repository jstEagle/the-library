## 6. The Road of White Fields

The salt flats began where the road gave up.

Lena felt the change before she saw it — a thinning in the air, as though the world had been wrung out and hung to dry. For three days the Brine Witches' galley had crawled up the Greywater estuary, hugging the reed-shadows, running dark by night. Then the river shallowed, and Captain Ossa stood at the tiller with her jaw set like a cleat, and said the word none of them wanted: *overland*.

"We beach her at Sallow Creek," Ossa had said, spitting into the brown water. "Take the flats on foot. Two days' walk, maybe three if the goat argues." The goat, whose name was Turnip and whose loyalty was absolute and whose sense of direction was not, had blinked at her with an expression of profound offense.

Now Lena walked on a world the color of bone.

The flats stretched to every horizon, cracked white earth crusted with salt so old it had forgotten water. It glittered under a high pale sun, beautiful and terrible, like standing inside a dead star. Every footstep rang faintly against the crust — a hollow, ceramic sound, as if they were walking across the lids of enormous buried jars. Behind them, the galley sat beached at Sallow Creek, half-hauled above the tideline, camouflaged under nets and bleached canvas. They would come back for her, Ossa promised. Ships could be repaired. People, less easily.

"Keep to my steps," Emrys murmured, falling in beside her. He had wrapped his scarred hands in linen against the salt-burn, and he moved with that careful economy of his, weight always balanced for a fight that hadn't started yet. "The crust lies over sinkholes. Old brine wells. Step where I step."

"I'm not a child," Lena said.

"No," he agreed quietly. "You're considerably more important than a child, which is why I'd prefer you unswallowed."

Ahead of them the crew strung out in a loose line: Ferro the exiled priest, who had spent the morning arguing theology with the sky; the twins, Brack and Mollie, who smuggled everything from silk to saints' relics and trusted nothing that couldn't be bribed; and Ossa herself, walking point with a harpoon over one shoulder and her grey braid whipping in the flat wind. Turnip trotted at Lena's heel, hooves clicking on the crust, occasionally pausing to lick the ground and looking personally betrayed by the taste.

They made good distance through the morning. By noon the sun hammered down and the glare came off the flats in blades, and Lena's eyes streamed until Ferro gave her strips of smoked leather to tie over her face, slitted like a mask. She must have looked, she thought wildly, like something out of the old stories — a masked pilgrim crossing the white fields to the drowned shrine. In the stories, though, nobody's feet blistered. In the stories there was always shade.

It was Turnip who heard them first.

The goat stopped mid-stride, ears swiveling forward, and made a low sound Lena had never heard from him — not a bleat but a groan, deep in the chest, the sound of an animal that has smelled death on the wind. A heartbeat later Ossa's fist went up, and the whole line froze.

"What is it?" Lena whispered.

Ossa didn't answer. Her head tilted, listening. And Lena heard it too — or rather felt it, a tremor coming up through the soles of her boots, through the salt crust itself. Hoofbeats. Many. Not approaching along the flats but *under* them, muffled, rhythmic, patient.

"The causeway," Emrys breathed. His face had gone the color of the ground. "There's an old salt-road under the crust, from the mining days. They're riding it. They knew our route."

"They couldn't have—" Mollie began.

"The luck-charms," Lena said, and her stomach dropped through the world. She turned to Emrys. "At Vell. When my gift leaked into your stock. The hunters traced it then — they've been able to *taste* me ever since, haven't they? Every time I listen to the pans, every time I answer—"

"Don't," Emrys said. "Don't finish that thought on open ground."

But the thought finished itself, cold and complete: they had never been running toward Greywater. They had been running *ahead* of a scent, and the scent was her.

The riders broke the crust two hundred yards out.

It happened all at once — the white field split along a seam no eye could see, and horses surged up out of the earth like the dead climbing out of graves, salt spraying in sheets, witch-hunters in their ash-grey coats rising with lances couched. There were twenty of them. Thirty. Their leader wore a mask of beaten tin shaped like a stern face, and when he raised his arm the whole line wheeled toward Lena as one animal, as if she were the only fixed point in a spinning world.

"To the ship!" Ossa roared. "Back to the ship! *Run!*"

They ran. The flats betrayed them at every stride — crust cracking, sinking, the buried brine wells yawning where the riders' weight had shattered the surface. Ferro went down to his knees in a hole and Brack hauled him out by the collar. Mollie threw a knife that spun away uselessly in the glare. And behind them the hoofbeats grew, and grew, and Lena understood with a clarity that felt like falling that they would not reach the creek. The riders were faster. The flats were wider. The day was longer than their legs.

An arrow took Ossa through the side.

Lena saw it happen as if underwater — saw the captain stagger mid-stride, saw the second arrow punch into her shoulder, saw the harpoon fall from her hand and ring against the salt like a struck bell. Ossa did not cry out. That was somehow the worst part. She simply folded, one knee, then both, her grey braid spilling across the white ground, and she looked up at Lena with an expression that was not fear and not pain but *instruction* — the look of a woman giving an order she knew would be her last.

"Go," Ossa mouthed.

Something in Lena tore loose.

Later she would not remember deciding. She remembered only stopping — turning in the blinding white while arrows hissed past her ears, while Emrys screamed her name in a voice she had never heard him use — and kneeling beside the captain, and pressing her bare hands to the salt crust, and doing the thing the law of three years had forbidden, the thing her father had burned his pans to prevent, the thing she had sworn on the night market to keep small and secret and safe.

She called.

Not the way she had before — not leaning close to a crusted pan, whispering, listening for a voice like wind through a keyhole. She called the way you call when someone you love is bleeding out under your hands. She called with everything, with her whole chest, with the nameless ache that had lived in her since the first voice answered through the salt, and the flats *heard* her.

Every grain of it.

The white fields lit from beneath. Light ran through the cracks in the crust like fire through paper veins, racing outward in every direction, and the air filled with a sound like ten thousand pans singing at once — the old voices, the deep ones, rising through the salt that had held their memory longer than any stone. Lena felt them pour toward her, felt them gather at her hands where Ossa's blood soaked into the ground, and she screamed the only prayer she had:

"*Save her.*"

The gods answered.

The wound did not close — they were not that kind of power anymore, broken and threadbare as they were — but the bleeding slowed, and slowed, and stopped, and warmth spread through Ossa's body like sun through a window, holding her at the edge of the dark instead of letting her fall in. Enough. Enough to carry her. Lena sobbed once, raggedly, and felt Emrys haul her up under the arms, and the world became motion again — Ferro carrying the captain, Brack dragging the harpoon, Turnip braying defiance at thirty armed men as though he alone could hold the line.

And in the ringing silence after the light faded, Lena heard the other thing.

Far behind them, faint as a bell across water: horns. One, then another, then a dozen, spreading outward across the flats in every direction — hunter-signals, relay-calls, the ash-grey network waking all at once. Because they had heard it too. Every hunter within fifty miles had felt the flare of true covenant-magic roll over the white fields like a tide, and every one of them now knew exactly where the last anchor of the old gods stood, and exactly how badly she wanted.

Emrys pulled her onward, and his face when she glanced up was worse than fear. It was grief. He had seen this before, she realized. He had seen what it cost, back when the world still burned with this light — and he had helped end it because he believed the cost was always paid by people like Ossa, lying in the salt with arrows in her side.

"She'll live," Lena gasped. "Tell me she'll live."

"She'll live," Emrys said. "Because of you. And now, little salt-girl, we have until nightfall before every sword in the usurper's army knows these coordinates. So we run, and we don't stop, and we pray—" his voice cracked on the word, the first prayer he'd spoken in three years, "—we pray the creek holds."

Behind them, the tin-masked rider did not pursue. He stood his horse amid the scorched circle where the light had passed, and slowly, deliberately, raised one gauntleted hand — not in threat, but in salute. As though acknowledging, at last, that the hunt was over and the finding had begun.

The horns sang on across the white fields, and the crew ran into the dying light, carrying their wounded captain toward the black line of the estuary, where the beached galley waited like a promise that might already be too late to keep.
