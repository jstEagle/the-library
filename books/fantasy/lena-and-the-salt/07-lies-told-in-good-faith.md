## 7. Lies Told in Good Faith

*Lena*

The truth came to her the way salt comes to bread — quietly, invisibly, until it was everywhere and could not be picked out.

They had made camp in the ruins of a toll-house on the Greywater road, three days after the ambush, three days since Ossa had taken the spear meant for Lena's back. The captain lay propped against a saddle in the corner of what had once been a customs office, her wound wrapped in linen gone rust-brown at the center, her color the grey of old ash. She refused to complain. That frightened Lena more than screaming would have.

Lena sat apart with her father's smallest pan balanced on her knees. The crust had thinned over the miles — the gods' voices came fainter now, as if they too were wounded — but she pressed her palm flat against it each night anyway, the way you press your ear to a door.

Tonight she heard not a god but a memory.

It rose through the salt like something drowned surfacing: a chamber of white stone, cold as a cellar. A circle of robed figures, their faces shadowed by hoods. And in the middle of them, kneeling, sword laid across his palms like an offering —

Emrys Dunmore, younger by three years, unscarred, speaking words she knew from her mother's lullabies. Words that were not a lullaby at all. Words of unbinding.

*"I renounce the covenant. I speak its ending. Let the doors be shut."*

The vision shivered. Through the salt she heard the old god's voice — the deep one, the one that called itself Brine-and-Bone — groan like a ship breaking up. *He did not know,* it said, or seemed to say, or perhaps she only needed it to say. *He did not know what he unmade.*

But he had spoken it. He had knelt there, in that white room, and ended the age with his mouth.

The pan slipped from her knees and rang against the flagstones. Across the ruined office, Emrys looked up from sharpening his sword. Their eyes met across the firelight, and she saw him read her face the way he read weather — and go still, the way the air goes still before hail.

"You saw," he said. Not a question.

She stood. Her legs felt borrowed. "You told me the oath-speakers died out. You told me you'd been cast out for *failing* them."

"I did fail them." He set the whetstone down with exaggerated care, the way a man moves when he knows any sudden thing might end him. "Lena—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it. "Don't say my name like you've earned it. You *ended* them. The gods went silent because of you. My whole life I thought — I thought the silence was just how the world was. And it was *you*. You put it there."

"It wasn't—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "Sit down. Please. Let me tell all of it or none of it will make sense."

"Nothing makes sense," she said. "That's the one true thing anyone's told me in weeks."

* * *

*Emrys*

He told her all of it, because she deserved the weight of it entire, and because lying to her now would be like lying to the sea.

Three years ago, the free cities had been weeks from war. Not a border squabble — a god-war. The old covenants had frayed; the gods had grown hungry and quarrelsome; each city's priesthood claimed its patron's favor and armed its faithful. Emrys had seen the first skirmish at Marrow Ford. He had counted the bodies afterward, four hundred of them, half of them children who'd been given swords and told heaven would turn the blades aside. Heaven had not bothered.

Then the usurper — Aldric Vane, then merely a clever man with a clever proposal — had come to the order of swordspeakers with proof: a rite, sealed and witnessed, by which the covenant could be dissolved. Shut the doors. Silence the gods. Take the divine out of men's hands, and take the excuse for holy slaughter with it.

"They showed us the rite worked," Emrys said. His voice came out flat, worn smooth by three years of telling himself this part. "They showed us a captured godlet, some river-thing, small and mean, and they bound it silent in front of us. And they said: this is mercy. This is the last spell anyone will ever need. No more wars fought in heaven's name. No more children promised miracles and handed steel instead."

"And you believed them."

"I *wanted* to believe them." He met her eyes. It cost more than he expected. "That's the confession, girl. Not that I was deceived. That I was tired, and grieving, and they offered me peace like a cup of water, and I drank without asking where they'd drawn it."

"But the rite — it didn't do what they said."

"No." He looked into the fire. "It didn't close the doors. It *broke the lock*. A closed door can be opened again. What I spoke that day shattered the hinge. The covenant didn't end — it *wounded*, and everything since has bled through the wound. Vane has spent three years harvesting that brokenness. Every silenced prayer, every unanswered plea, every priest who lost his god overnight — it all pools somewhere, and he drinks from it. His armies don't march on bread alone, Lena. They march on grief."

Silence filled the toll-house, thick as floodwater. In the corner, Ossa watched with half-lidded eyes, saying nothing, letting it happen.

"So when you found me," Lena said at last, very quietly, "when you took me out through the fish-cellars while they dragged my father away — you knew. You knew exactly what they wanted me for. Because you're the one who made me the *last*."

"Yes."

"And you didn't tell me."

"I was going to. When we were safe. When it wouldn't—" He stopped, because every version of that sentence was a lie dressed in good intentions, and they both knew it.

"When it wouldn't cost you anything," she finished for him. "When I'd already trusted you enough that the truth couldn't hurt you."

She turned and walked out into the dark, and he let her go, because a man who had broken a world had no right to chase the pieces.

* * *

*Lena*

The rival guide found her at the edge of camp, which was either remarkable luck or proof that the man had been circling them for a day like a gull after offal.

His name was Corven Hale, and he wore road-dust like cologne and smiled like a merchant counting your purse. He'd appeared that morning with six mules and a story: he knew a southern route to Greywater, wide and safe, past the usurper's pickets entirely. For a price. He'd made the offer to Ossa first; Ossa had told him, in Ossa's way, to fertilize a field with himself. But now he found Lena alone under the cold stars, and his smile softened into something almost kind.

"You learned tonight," he said. "About the swordspeaker. Word travels fast among people who lie to each other."

"We don't lie to each other," Lena said. "That's rather the point."

"Mm." He offered a flask. She didn't take it. "Girl, I'll speak plainly, since plainly is all I can afford. You're carrying something Vane wants badly, and the man escorting you is the reason Vane wants it. He broke the covenant. He's the most hunted man in the free cities, and every league he walks beside you paints a target on your spine. I can get you to Greywater by week's end. Clean roads, no hunters, no ghosts. Just you, the shrine, and whatever it is you mean to do there." He paused delicately. "Whatever it costs."

"And what does it cost?"

"Your company. Only that. Leave tonight, leave *him* behind, and my mules are yours."

She looked south, where he pointed, and the road did look clean — pale ribbon of moonlit gravel running away from everything that hurt. Away from Emrys's scarred face and his careful hands and the white stone room she could not stop seeing. Away from the sound of her father's voice calling her name as the hunters' cart rolled him off. Away, if she was honest, from Ossa's grey face and the growing stain on the bandage, from the whole slow weight of other people's wounds.

It would be so easy to stop being responsible for anyone.

Corven watched her weigh it, and his smile sharpened by a hair. "You're thinking he deserves it. He does, probably. But girl — think what he *knows*. Three years inside Vane's machine. The rite, the harvest, the roads his armies use. Throw that away out of hurt, and you're not punishing him. You're punishing everyone downstream of Vane's armies. Which is everyone."

"That's a merchant's argument," she said. "Buy cheap, sell dear."

"All arguments are merchant's arguments. Some just hide the scale better." He shrugged. "You have until dawn. I ride early."

He melted back toward his mules, and Lena stayed under the stars a long while, doing sums she hated.

* * *

*Lena*

Ossa was awake when Lena finally came back inside. Of course she was. Wounded women sleep like cats, in snatches, with one ear open.

"Corven talked to you," the captain said. It wasn't a question. She shifted, winced, and jerked her chin at the space beside her. "Sit. You'll wear a groove in my floor otherwise, and I'm fond of this floor. It's the first roof we've had since the flats."

Lena sat. The fire had burned low. Somewhere outside, the goat was chewing on something it shouldn't, rhythmically, absurdly, and the sound kept the silence from becoming unbearable.

"He's not wrong," Ossa said. "About Emrys being dangerous to travel with. About the roads being cleaner without him. Corven lies about prices and flatters ugly women, but he reads a map truer than any man I've sailed with." She paused. "So. Are you going?"

Lena stared at her. "Aren't you supposed to talk me out of it?"

"I'm supposed to keep you alive long enough to reach Greywater. Talking you out of things is above my pay." Ossa's eyes glinted, fever-bright but steady. "But I'll tell you what I know, and you can do your own arithmetic. I served under Emrys Dunmore for nine years, back when the order still had a name worth saying. I was there at Marrow Ford after. I helped count those bodies. And I watched him kneel in that white room because I was standing guard outside it, under orders, hating every second." She drew a breath that cost her. "He broke the covenant, girl. That's true, and it's the worst thing about him, and it isn't even the *most important* thing about him."

"What is?"

"He's spent three years trying to die of guilt, and failing at it, because he's too stubborn to do it quickly. And then a salt-girl walked into his stall with a singing pan, and for the first time since Marrow Ford, the man had a reason to live that wasn't penance." Ossa's hand found Lena's wrist — dry, strong, trembling slightly from the fever. "Here's the arithmetic nobody teaches you. Family you're born into breaks like anything else. Your father's in a cart because blood was all the law needed to find him. But family you *choose* — that you build joint by joint, out of trust and argument and showing up anyway — that holds weight no bloodline can. Emrys lied to you. Weigh it. Then weigh that he pulled you out of the fish-cellars with hunters at his heels. Weigh that he hasn't slept properly in three weeks because he stands watch over your tent every night. Weigh that when the truth finally crushed him, he told all of it rather than a comfortable piece."

"He told it because I caught him."

"Aye. Most truths come out that way. Doesn't make them less true." Ossa released her wrist and settled back against the saddle. "Corven offers you a clean road. Clean roads are for people with nothing to carry. Go back to sleep, salt-girl. Dawn's coming, and I suspect you've a swordspeaker to forgive, and forgiveness is slow work. Best start early."

Lena didn't sleep. She sat by the dying fire with her father's pan in her lap, and near morning, when the sky went the grey of Ossa's complexion, she pressed her palm to the crust one more time.

The voice that came was thin, distant, pained — but it was there. *Little anchor,* said Brine-and-Bone. *The water rises. The drowned shrine stirs. Come quickly, and come together — the gate opens for no one who arrives alone.*

Outside, Corven Hale was saddling his mules, checking the southern road, waiting with his patient merchant's smile.

And Emrys Dunmore stood at the eastern edge of camp, facing the direction of Greywater, sword drawn, watching the horizon where — far off, at the very limit of sight — dust was rising. Not six riders. Not sixty.

An army's worth of dust, moving toward the drowned shrine, and between it and them: three days, one wounded captain, one traitor-turned-guide, and a girl holding a pan full of gods.
