## 8. The Drowned Shrine at Greywater

They smelled the shrine before they saw it.

Not rot, exactly — Lena had grown up around rot, fish-rot and weed-rot and the sweet black stink of a tide pool left too long in the sun. This was older than any of that. It was the smell of stone that had been drinking water for a thousand years, of drowned cedar and cold silt and something beneath all of it, faint as a memory of incense: myrrh, or whatever the old priests had burned before anyone decided burning was against the law.

The road came down out of the reed hills in long switchbacks, and at each turn Greywater opened wider below them — a lake the color of pewter, still as poured metal, ringed with dead rushes. And rising from its center, no more than a broken crown above the surface: the shrine. Two horns of weathered granite, a sliver of archway between them, everything else gone under.

Lena stood at the rail of the mule cart and felt the salt crusts in her pack go warm against her back.

"They're singing," she said.

Ossa, propped on the cart bed with her wound bound and her face gray as the water, lifted her head. "The pans?"

"The whole lake." Lena pressed her palm flat to the boards. She could feel it through the wood now, through the wheels, through the soles of her boots — a hum like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. "It's been waiting. All this time, it's been *waiting*."

Emrys reined the mules to a stop and looked down at the water for a long moment. In three weeks on the road Lena had learned to read his silences the way she read salt — by texture, by weight. This one was heavy with something he wasn't saying.

"Say it," she told him.

"The vanguard crossed the ford at Millbrook two days ago." He didn't look at her. "If they pushed hard, they're close. If they pushed very hard—"

He didn't finish, because he didn't have to. The sound came across the reeds a heartbeat later: the thin bright clatter of harness, the creak of many boots on wet ground, a horn blown twice — not a hunting call. A mustering call.

"There," Emrys said, pointing with his chin to a stand of alders at the lake's near shore. "Cart goes in the trees. We walk the rest."

* * *

They carried Ossa between them, her arm over Emrys's shoulders and Lena's, her boots dragging furrows in the mud. She had refused to stay behind with the cart and the goat — "You'll lose the goat, you'll absolutely lose the goat, and then where are we" — and none of them had been fool enough to argue with her. The goat, whose name was Duchess and whose loyalty had survived smugglers, witch-hunters, and one memorable encounter with a river ferry, trotted behind with her ears pricked toward the water as if she heard the singing too.

The shoreline path was white with crusted salt, leached up out of the flooded ground over years and years, and every step Lena took, the crusts crackled softly underfoot like slow applause. The hum grew. It got into her teeth. It got into the space behind her eyes where the voices lived, and there they were again — not words yet, but presence, vast and patient and terribly glad.

*She comes,* something said, in the tone of an old woman remarking on rain. *The salt-girl comes.*

Behind them, along the shore, torches were kindling.

"She can't walk fast," Emrys said quietly. It wasn't complaint; it was arithmetic. Lena had watched him do this arithmetic for three weeks — distances, hours, arrows, odds — and she hated it and relied on it in equal measure.

"Then you'll hold them," Ossa said. "That's the arithmetic, isn't it? That's what you've been carrying since Vell."

"I'm one man."

"You're the last swordspeaker." Ossa stopped, and made them both stop, and turned her gray face up to look at him with eyes that had gone fierce as a gull's. "I served your order when you were still wet behind the ears, Dunmore. I watched men do what you're about to do, and I watched most of them die doing it, and I never once saw one regret it. So don't you dare stand there telling me the odds. Give me the goat and get to the waterline."

* * *

The gate was real. That was the thing that undid her.

From the hills it had been a broken crown, a ruin, a pretty shape against the sky. Up close, waist-deep in the shallows with the lake lapping cold at her ribs, Lena could see that the archway between the two granite horns was not empty. Water ran through it, yes — but the water *ran*, in a place where the lake lay still everywhere else. It fell in a slow silver curtain, upward and downward both, and through it she could see light that did not come from the gray afternoon sky. Warm light. Lamplit. The color of kitchens.

"It's still lit," she whispered. "After all these years, someone kept the lamps."

"No one kept anything." Emrys stood beside her in the shallows, water streaming off his sword arm, and his voice was rough. "It keeps itself. That's what a covenant is. It isn't a wall someone maintains. It's a door someone agreed to leave open." He paused. "And then someone closed it. Me. I closed it, and even so—" he gestured at the falling silver, the lamplight, the impossible running water — "even so, look. Three years of dark, and it's still trying."

The torches were closer now. She could hear individual voices shouting orders, the squelch of a hundred boots finding the shore path. Somewhere behind her, steel cleared a scabbard, and then another, and Ossa's voice rang out over the water, cracked and clear and laughing: *"Come on, then! You've come all this way!"*

"Lena." Emrys caught her shoulder. His hand was shaking; she noticed, and pretended not to, because he would have wanted her to. "There's a rite. There was always a rite. My order guarded its knowledge and I'll give it to you now, all of it, and may every god we broke forgive me for how late it comes."

"Tell me."

"The gate opens to inheritance. Memory, freely carried. The old families sent their heirs down with the memories of everyone who trusted them — fathers, mothers, grandsires, whole dynasties of faith, layered into one mind until the child walked under the water already knowing what love weighed." His grip tightened. "You're not an heir. You don't have dynasties. But trust doesn't care about blood, Lena. Trust is trust. If anyone trusts you — truly, freely — you can carry their memory down. That's the rite. That's all it ever was."

"And if I drown?"

"Then the covenant stays broken, the usurper wins, and I will spend what's left of my life making sure he regrets it." He almost smiled. "But you won't drown. The gods have been listening to you since a night market in Vell. Do you really think they'd let you sink?"

She thought about lying. About being brave in the loud way, the way Ossa was brave, the way the smugglers sang going into battle. But standing in the cold water with the lamplight falling through the silver curtain, Lena found she only had one honest thing in her.

"I'm terrified," she said.

"Good," said Emrys. "Terror means you understand it. Now come here, and let them give you what they've got."

* * *

They gathered at the waterline while the hunters formed their lines on the shore path — Ossa leaning on her spear, the smuggler twins Perrin and Sable with their knives out and their grins terrible, old Brother Anselm with his exiled priest's hands folded like a man at prayer, and Duchess the goat, absurdly, standing at the front as though she intended to fight the entire vanguard personally.

"Take my hands," Lena said. "All of you. Whoever's willing."

And they gave it to her.

It came like warmth first, then like weight. Ossa's memory: a galley deck at dawn, salt bread broken and shared, the word *aye* spoken over twenty years of storms. Perrin's: a cell in Vell, and a girl selling salt who bought a prisoner's freedom with a lie and a coin. Sable's: laughter in a rigging, and the decision, quiet and enormous, to follow a stranger into the reed hills because she listened to pans. Anselm's: forty years of prayers thrown at a silent heaven, and the day a salt-seller's daughter answered one. Even Duchess — and Lena laughed through her tears at this — even the goat gave her something, a simple animal certainty, warm and brown-eyed and absolute: *this one is mine; I follow this one.*

And last, Emrys. His memory came heaviest of all, because it was newest and rawest and given with his whole ruined heart: a boy swearing oaths he didn't understand, a man breaking them to save cities he loved, three years of ash — and then a market stall, a flare of true magic, and the sudden staggering hope of a drowning man seeing a light he believed he'd forfeited forever. *I trust her,* the memory said, in his voice, over and over, like a man learning a word. *I trust her. I trust her.*

The weight of it should have crushed her. Instead it held her up, the way ballast holds a boat upright in weather. She stood in the shallows with six lives folded into her chest and understood, suddenly, what the rite had always meant. Not burden. Ballast. You did not sink under the weight of being trusted. You floated on it.

On the shore, the first arrow loosed and Ossa's spear rang answering steel, and Emrys drew his sword, and the air itself began to remember how to speak.

Lena turned to the gate.

The lamplight fell through the silver curtain, warm as a kitchen, patient as a grandmother. Beneath it the water waited, deep and dark and full of singing.

"I'm coming," she told it. "And I'm bringing everyone."

She filled her lungs, and stepped under.
