## 9. An Inheritance Freely Given

The water of Greywater was not cold. That was the first mercy, and Lena clung to it as she stood at the drowned shrine's threshold with the flood lapping at her knees. The shrine had sunk a century ago, when the river changed its mind and swallowed the road to it, and now its bell tower rose from the green water like a broken finger pointing at a sky that had stopped listening. Beneath her feet, down a stair the gods had once meant pilgrims to walk dry-shod, the old doors waited — salt-crusted, silt-heavy, sealed.

Behind her, on the shore, Emrys was arguing with the horizon.

"They'll be here before dusk," he said. "Vanguard riders. I counted their dust an hour ago. You have until they arrive, and no longer."

"Then stop talking and let me work." Ossa lay propped against a fallen pillar, her wound bound in sailcloth gone brown, her face gray as ash but her voice still carrying the authority of a woman who had captained ships through blockades. "Girl. Come here. Before you go under."

Lena waded back. The Brine Witches had carried her the last three miles through the marsh, Pirro with the goat bleating indignantly on his shoulders, Sefa holding the salt pans above the waterline like relics, which, Lena supposed, they were. Now they gathered around Ossa, and the captain took Lena's wrist in a grip that had lost none of its strength even if everything else had.

"You'll walk into that water carrying us," Ossa said. "That's how the rite works. Not your memories. Ours. Everyone who trusts you gives you what they remember, and you carry it down like ballast, and you spend it at the door."

"I know."

"Do you know what it costs?" Ossa's eyes were steady. "The memories don't come back. Whatever we give you, we wake up without. Sefa won't remember her mother's face. Pirro won't remember why he stopped drinking. I won't remember—" she paused, and for the first time since Lena had known her, the captain looked uncertain, "—I won't remember my order. The swordspeakers. What they were before they broke. And I think maybe that's a kindness, but it's yours to take, not mine to give. So I'm giving it. Understand? Freely. Nobody's making me."

Lena's throat closed. She looked around the circle: Pirro, jaw set, already nodding. Sefa, crying silently and nodding anyway. Even the goat stood regarding her with its flat yellow patience, and she had the sudden wild certainty that animals remembered too, and gave too, in their own tongueless way.

And Emrys, apart from them all, arms crossed, watching the road.

"He can't give," Ossa said quietly, following her gaze. "He hasn't got any trust left to give. He spent it all three years ago."

"I know what he did," Lena said. "I know what he broke."

"And you're still here."

"I'm still here."

Ossa smiled, thin and pained. "Then go spend us well, salt-girl."

* * *

Emrys heard the water close over her head and felt something tear loose in his chest — not grief exactly, though grief lived in it, but the older thing underneath grief, the thing he had been running from since the night he spoke the unbinding and watched the sky go silent.

They came out of the dust at dusk, just as he'd promised. Twelve riders first, then the witch-hunters' wagons, then the banners — the usurper's white hand on black, limp in the windless evening. Forty men. He'd held worse odds once, back when the words still worked. Back when the words were his to speak.

Now there was one sword-speaker left in the world, standing in a marsh with a rusted blade and a crew of smugglers behind him, and the last anchor of the covenant was drowning herself fifty feet below his feet.

"Hold," he said to the Witches, and stepped forward alone.

The vanguard reined up. Their officer — young, polished, frightened in the way of men who have been told stories about what happens at Greywater — called out the formal challenge. *In the name of the Usurper Aldric, Third of His Line, heir to the silence of heaven—*

"There is no silence of heaven," Emrys said, and drew.

The blade came free of its scabbard singing, and the song did not stop where steel-song always stopped. It went on. It deepened. Three years of ash fell away from the old words and they came back to him whole, rising through him like water finding its level, and Emrys Dunmore, last of the swordspeakers, spoke for the first time since the breaking:

"*Stand.*"

The word hit the riders like a wave. Horses screamed and sat back on their haunches. Two men were thrown. The officer's sword arm locked rigid at his side, and every man on the line felt the truth of the word settle into his bones: *stand*, and stand they did, because the word was true, and true words are law.

But forty men, and one of him. He could feel the cost already, blood warming his lips, the world going bright and brittle at the edges. Swordspeaking spent memory too — that was the price the old order never advertised. Every word he spoke now, he paid for in himself. He could feel whole years going gray. The name of his first teacher. The taste of his mother's bread. He spoke anyway.

"*Kneel,*" he said, and half of them did.

The wagons came on. From within them came the hunters — not soldiers but the other kind, the ones with iron nails in their boots and hymns of unbinding stitched into their hems, walking untouched through the field of kneeling men. They had protections against the spoken word. Of course they did. The usurper had built his whole kingdom on the corpse of magic; he knew its shapes.

"Emrys Dunmore," the lead hunter said, almost gently. "The traitor who ended the gods. Come to end them properly this time?"

"Come to keep my promise to a girl," Emrys said, and raised his blade, and began to speak the long forms, the battle-cant, the words that turned a single man into a wall.

Each word cost him a season. He gave them gladly.

* * *

Down in the dark, Lena walked.

The stair went further than it should have. The water pressed on her ears, her chest, her eyes, and through the pressure she felt them arriving — the memories, given freely, settling into her like stones into a net. Sefa's mother's face, warm-lit and laughing. Pirro's daughter, the reason he'd stopped drinking, small hands and a gap-toothed grin. Ossa's order: white cloaks, dawn drills, the oath spoken in unison while the mountains rang. The goat's whole simple world of grass and road and the smell of Lena's coat.

She carried them down. Ballast. She would spend them at the door.

The doors were taller than a house, and carved with the covenant itself — two figures, god-shaped and human-shaped, hands meeting in the middle, and between their hands a space shaped like nothing Lena had ever seen except, suddenly, she had: it was shaped like a pan of salt, crusts broken, ready to receive whatever was poured into it.

She put her palm against the stone.

*I'm here,* she said, in the way she had learned to say things through salt. *I brought people.*

The dark answered. Not one voice — many, layered, the way the market crowd sounded from far off, the way rain sounds. The old gods, all of them, or what remained of them: hungry, patient, diminished, waiting a hundred years for someone to knock.

*Little anchor,* they said. *You come alone?*

"No," Lena said aloud, bubbles streaming from her lips, and it didn't matter that water filled her mouth; the gods heard meaning, not sound. "I come full. Hold out your hands."

She opened the net.

The memories poured out of her and into the seam between the carved figures — Sefa's mother, Pirro's daughter, Ossa's white-cloaked dawns, the goat's green fields — freely given, freely spent, and the doors drank them the way parched ground drinks rain. The carvings began to glow. The two figures' hands crept toward each other across a hundred years of silence.

*This is the rite of dynasties,* the gods murmured, wondering. *Blood calling to blood, heir to heir. Who taught you to bring strangers' trust instead?*

"Nobody taught me," Lena said. "That's the point. That's the whole point. You were bound to bloodlines, and bloodlines can be stolen, and broken, and bought — that's how he did it, that's how the usurper claims you, isn't it? He holds the last blood-oath. He thinks he owns the door."

Silence, vast and listening.

"So don't bind to blood," Lena said. "Bind to this. To what I'm holding right now. People who owe me nothing, giving me everything, because they chose to. Bind the covenant to consent. To freely given trust. Let anyone open the door who opens it honestly. Then he owns nothing. Then nobody owns anything. Then everyone can hear you — anyone willing to listen."

The pressure of the water seemed to hold its breath.

*It will change everything,* the gods said. *Magic loose in the world, lawful to all. Your usurper's armies will not kneel to it. Some will curse you.*

"Some already do."

*And the door, once opened this way, cannot be closed again. Not by us. Not by you.*

"I know," Lena said, and thought of her father's burned pans, of the night market, of salt singing softly to anyone willing to listen. "Open it anyway."

The hands met.

Light went through the water like sunrise through a struck bell.

* * *

Above, Emrys was on his knees in the shallows, and did not remember getting there. The hunters stood over him. His sword arm wouldn't answer. He had spoken himself nearly empty — he couldn't recall his own name, only that it mattered, only that somewhere below him a girl needed him to have kept standing, and he had, he had, until he couldn't.

The lead hunter raised an iron knife. "For the usurper," he said, "and the quiet he bought you—"

The lake stood up.

Not a wave. The whole body of Greywater rose — a wall of green glass thirty feet high, lit from beneath by gold, and in the light a figure walked up out of the depths: a girl in a soaked wool dress, barefoot on the water's surface, hair drifting like weed, eyes full of borrowed dawn. Behind her, fainter, vast, other shapes moved in the deep — antlered, winged, crowned, the old gods rising like fish coming home to spawn.

Every hunter's iron protection failed at once, because iron wards against the old binding, and the binding was no longer old. It was new. It was consent, and consent cannot be warded, only refused — and not one soul on that shore, hunter or soldier, kneeling or standing, refused it.

The wall of water came down gentle as snowfall.

When it cleared, the hunters' weapons lay dissolved to rust. The banners hung blank. Somewhere far to the east, in a palace built on silence, the usurper Aldric woke screaming from a dream of hands letting go of his, and found that the oath in his blood had become only blood, and the throne he'd claimed by it was only a chair.

Emrys looked up at Lena where she stood on the water, glowing, ordinary, seventeen years old.

"You did it," he managed.

"We did it," she said. "All of us. Even you." She knelt beside him in the shallows, took his ruined hand, and pressed something into it: a crusted pan, cracked open, its salt shining. "I saved you a piece of the door. Listen."

He listened. And through the salt, faint and clear, a voice he hadn't heard in three years said his name — not in judgment, not in summons, but the way family says it across a kitchen table — and Emrys Dunmore, last of the swordspeakers, put his forehead against the wet stone and wept like a man coming home.

Far off along the road to Vell, in a thousand kitchens, a thousand pans began, very softly, to sing.
