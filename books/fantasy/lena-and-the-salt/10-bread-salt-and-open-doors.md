## 10. Bread, Salt, and Open Doors

The salt sang to me before I was awake.

Not loudly — it never did anymore, not since Greywater — but softly, the way a kettle hums just before it finds its voice. A thin bright thread of sound rising from the pans cooling on the sill, from the crusts in the barrels downstairs, from the white fields beyond the walls where the evaporators caught the morning light like a hundred small mirrors laid out for the gods to admire.

I lay in my old bed, in my old room, with my father's cough and my father's kettle sounds drifting up through the floorboards, and I listened.

*Good morning,* said the salt. Or something like it. The gods had never learned to speak in words, exactly; they spoke in tastes, in pressures, in the particular ache behind the sternum that meant *we are here, we are glad, you are not alone.* Three months ago that ache had been a terror I hid under my apron. Now it was as ordinary as bread rising, as gulls arguing over the fish-market scraps, as Da humming off-key while he stirred the morning porridge.

Ordinary. That was the miracle no one had expected. Not the fire from the sky or the armies turning to dust or the usurper's tower cracking open like an egg — those had been terrible and bright and were already becoming story. But this: magic come home lawfully, quietly, folded into the daily grain of things. A covenant bound not to bloodlines or oaths or dynasties but to freely given trust, which meant it lived everywhere trust lived. In kitchens. In market stalls. In the space between two strangers who decided, against all sense, to believe each other.

I got up. I dressed. I went down to sell salt.

* * *

Vell's night market had reopened on the first day of the thaw, and by spring's end it had grown fat again — fatter than before, if anything, because half the free cities wanted to see the place where the last anchor of the old covenant had once sold her wares from a crooked little stall between the candle-seller and the woman who sold pickled eels. They came expecting a shrine. They found a girl with chapped hands and a goat named Turnip who ate ribbons out of their hair if they stood still too long.

"Salt," I called, the way Da had taught me when I was seven, the way his father had taught him. "White salt, grey salt, salt from the singing pans—"

That last part was new. Da had fought it at first. *We don't sell wonder, Lena-girl,* he'd said, wringing his cap in his hands the night before we reopened the stall. *Wonder isn't ours to sell.* But then old Marta from the candle-stall had bought a crust of pan-salt for her grandson's fever, held it to her ear the way I'd shown her, and heard — she swore it, weeping into her apron — her dead husband humming the lullaby he used to sing. After that Da stopped fighting it. He carved the sign himself, badly, the S leaning like a drunkard: **MARCHETTI SALT — IT LISTENS BACK.**

Business, as Emrys would say with his dry ghost of a smile, was obscene.

"Two measures," said a voice at my elbow, "and whatever the goat is having."

I didn't have to turn around. I knew Ossa's tread the way I knew the tide — heavy on the left, light on the right, the leg she'd nearly lost on the white fields still learning its business. She came to the market every seventh night now, ostensibly to buy salt, actually to sit on my stool and eat roasted chestnuts and tell increasingly improbable stories about the Brine Witches' glory days to anyone who'd listen. Her crew had scattered after Greywater — honest berths, mostly, though Brack had taken up pearl-diving and sworn on his mother's grave it was more dangerous than smuggling ever was.

"You're late," I said, measuring. "Chestnuts are gone."

"I'm never late. The world is early." She dropped onto the stool, groaning theatrically, and scratched Turnip's ears. Up close I could see the new lines around her eyes, the ones that hadn't been there in autumn. Healing made its own weather. "How is he?"

I didn't ask who. There was only one *he* these days.

"Better. He walked to the harbor and back yesterday without the stick." I tied off the paper twists of salt. "He complained about the price of fish the whole way. Da says complaining is how you know he's mending."

Ossa snorted. "The man survived three witch-hunters and a collapsing shrine. Fish prices will finish him yet."

* * *

Emrys Dunmore came to the stall at moonrise, as he always did, when the crowd thinned and the lanterns burned low and the market smelled of tallow and brine and cooling bread from the baker's ovens across the lane.

He looked better than he had in the shrine's aftermath — anyone would have, compared to a man carried bleeding up from drowned stone with swordspeaking still crackling off him like static off wool. The healers said the gift had nearly unmade him, that holding the old words in a body three years out of practice was like pouring a river through a dry channel. But the channel was filling now. His color was back. His hands, scarred and steady, could hold a cup without trembling.

What hadn't come back was the oath. And that, it turned out, was the whole point.

"The council approved it," he said, settling onto the crate beside the stall like a man announcing a death in the family. "The school. The old swordspeaker hall on Tanner's Row — they're giving it to me. For teaching."

"For teaching," I repeated, because he seemed to need to hear it aloud.

"Anyone who wants to learn. No bloodline tests, no oaths, no binding." He turned his cup in his hands — tea, these days; he'd given up the spirits in the long convalescence, another quiet surrender I pretended not to notice. "Words given freely, returned freely. That's what you built at Greywater, Lena. Someone has to teach people how to carry it without breaking themselves."

"And they chose you."

"They chose the only disgraced swordspeaker in the free cities who happens to be alive and ambulatory." But his mouth twitched. "Ossa voted for me four times. I'm told that's illegal."

"It's Ossa. Everything she does is illegal. It's why we love her."

He laughed — short, rusty, real. Then he went quiet, looking out over the market, at the candle-sellers and the eel-woman and the children chasing each other between the stalls, and I watched something move across his face that I recognized now, after everything: the old guilt, arriving and being let go, arriving and being let go, like a tide he was finally learning to swim in rather than drown beneath.

"I keep thinking about the man I was," he said. "Three years ago. What I would have done, standing here, hearing that salt sing. I'd have reported it. Smashed the pans myself." His thumb traced the scar along his jaw. "And then I think — that man is the one who most needs to be standing in a classroom on Tanner's Row. Because there will be others like him. Frightened people, coerced people, people who break things believing they're saving them." He looked at me. "You forgave me faster than I've managed to forgive myself. I'm going to spend the rest of my life trying to deserve both."

"You don't deserve forgiveness," I said, and watched him flinch, and then smiled so he knew the rest was coming. "Nobody does. That's what makes it a gift instead of a wage. You taught me that, actually. Or Ossa did. Honestly, between the two of you it's hard to sort out who corrupted whom."

* * *

Da came home on a Tuesday in high summer, walking through Vell's south gate with a freedman's papers in one hand and nothing else, because everything else had burned with the pans.

They'd released the prisoners from the usurper's work-camps within weeks of the covenant's reforging — there was no legal ground left to hold them on, no witch-law, no broken-magic statutes, nothing but the plain fact of men and women who had been taken for loving the wrong gods or the right gods or simply being related to someone who did. Da had come back thinner, greyer, with a limp the cold had left in his knee and a silence where his easy talk used to be. But on his first night home he had walked into the kitchen, seen the pans crusted white on every surface, and stood there a long moment with his hand pressed flat against the salt.

Then he'd asked, very quietly, "Do they still answer?"

And I'd shown him how to listen.

Now he worked the stall with me some evenings, and other evenings he sat by our door with a mug of barley tea and talked to neighbors about the weather and the herring run and whether the council's new tax on lamp-oil was tyranny or mere incompetence. Ordinary grievances. Ordinary life. He'd earned every hour of it, and I never told him how many nights I'd lain awake in smugglers' bunks and drowned shrines imagining this exact evening, this exact tea, this exact ordinary man safe behind his own door — because grief spent twice is still grief, and he'd paid enough.

But sometimes, when the market noise swelled and I glanced back at our house on Saltpan Lane, I saw him listening to the crusts on the windowsill, head tilted, eyes closed, smiling at whatever the old gods murmured to a tired salt-boiler at the end of a long day.

*Welcome home,* they said. *We kept your chair.*

* * *

On the eve of the harvest fair, we did what the free cities had begun doing everywhere: we set bread and salt on the doorstep, and we left the door open.

It was Ossa's idea originally — or she claimed it was, though Emrys insisted the rite predated even the old covenant, some first-faith custom from before the realms were realms. Bread for the body's welcome. Salt for the soul's. An open door so that any traveler, any stranger, any god might enter and be fed. Under the usurper it had been outlawed; a locked city was easier to rule. Now the lanes of Vell glowed with them every festival night — hundreds of doorways, each with its small white offering, each threshold bare of lock or bar until morning.

Our household gathered at ours: Da with his tea, Ossa with her chestnuts and her fourth retelling of the Battle of the White Fields (in which her role grew more heroic with each telling), Turnip attempting to eat the offering bread, and Emrys, standing a little apart in the doorway with the evening light on his face, looking out at the street full of open doors.

"One year," he said. "One year since the night market. Since the pan that answered."

"One year since you sold me a luck-charm that actually worked," I said, "and then ran away from the consequences like a sensible criminal."

"I prefer 'withdrew strategically.'"

The salt on our doorstep caught the last of the sun and threw it back in a soft white shimmer, and beneath the market noise and the gulls and Turnip's chewing, I heard it — faint, patient, glad. Not a command. Never again a command. Just the old gods, unbound and unbowed, keeping company with whoever cared to listen.

*More doors tomorrow,* they said. *More hands. More names we haven't sung in a thousand years.*

I bent and broke the bread, and set a piece in Emrys's hand, and one in Ossa's, and one in Da's, and one on the step for whatever wanderer came next — because that was the covenant now, the whole of it, small enough to fit on a doorstep and wide enough to hold the world:

Given freely. Returned freely. Doors open.

And far down the lane, past the last of the lanterns, a traveler was coming — dusty, hooded, carrying something wrapped in oilcloth that glimmered, even at that distance, like salt.
