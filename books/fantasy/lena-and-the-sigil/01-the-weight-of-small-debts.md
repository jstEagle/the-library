## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The inventory of the Ledgerwrights' guildhall fit, these days, into three shelves and a locked drawer that had once held nine.

Ines Underhill counted them anyway. Counting was the work, and the work was the only conversation she had ever been certain of.

"One jar of bottled thunder," she said aloud, copying the entry in her small, careful hand. "Half full. Contents sulky."

The thunder had been bottled in her grandmother's time, off the back of a storm-catcher who'd owed the guild a debt and paid it in weather. It had gone flat over the years, the way old thunder does, and now when you uncorked it you got mostly a grumble, like an uncle asked to dance. Still, it was impossible, and it was theirs, and so it went in the ledger: *one jar, half full, contents sulky.*

"Three feathers from the wings of a bird that never landed." These were genuine relics, plucked—so the old ledger claimed—from a creature that had spent its whole existence in the act of arriving. They were used for oaths. You swore on one, and if you lied, the feather simply declined to have existed, taking your oath with it. There were only three left because people kept lying, which Ines felt said something about the world that the ledgers were too polite to record outright.

"A key that opens any door except the one you most need."

"A coin that always comes back, no matter how you spend it." This last was the guild's pride and its curse both. The elders had spent it forty years ago on a debt to the crown, and it had come back, as coins of its kind do, and been spent again, and come back again, until the crown's collectors had begun to take it personally.

And then the drawer. The drawer held the things too dangerous or too sad to shelve: a mirror that showed you your mother's face at whatever age she'd been happiest (the glass had cracked during some long-ago grief, and now showed everyone's mother slightly out of focus); a rope with eleven knots, each of which untied one lie; and a small tin of sleep, harvested from the last dreamless night anyone in Vell had ever sold.

Ines wrote it all down. Her pen scratched. Somewhere above her, the elders were shouting.

* * *

The shouting came down through the floorboards the way water comes through a roof—steadily, in the same old places, wearing the same old grooves deeper every year.

"—owe the crown for the harbor levy, and we owe it since the spring, and Maren keeps saying we'll pay it in *services*, and the crown does not want our services, the crown wants coin—"

"The crown," said Elder Maren, in the voice of a woman who had been interrupted by better arguments than this and survived them all, "can want what it likes. We are the Ledgerwrights. We do not pay debts in coin. We pay them in what is owed."

"What's owed is four hundred marks!"

Ines climbed the stairs with her ledger under her arm, partly because someone ought to bring the tally, and partly because the counting room, without her in it, was just a room.

The council chamber smelled of tallow and old argument. Three elders sat around the great table where twelve had sat in her grandfather's day: Maren, sharp as a fishhook; Elder Corvin, who was mostly beard and grievance; and Elder Hask, who was ninety-one and spent most meetings asleep with his eyes open, a skill he insisted was not sleeping but *listening with patience.*

They stopped talking when she came in. They always stopped talking when she came in. It was the particular silence of adults deciding a child cannot hear something, applied to a woman of twenty-three who kept every account they had.

"The tally," Ines said, setting her book on the table. "Since we're discussing the crown's levy. We can cover two hundred and ten marks in goods, if they'll take the feathers, which they won't. One hundred and sixty in coin, if we sell the bottled thunder, which we shouldn't. And nothing at all," she added, before Corvin could suggest it, "from selling the return-coin, because selling it is how we got into this."

Corvin's beard bristled. "Nobody asked the junior keeper to—"

"She's right about the coin," said Hask, eyes still open, apparently listening with patience even now.

"I'm the youngest keeper the guild has ever recorded," Ines said. "That's not a boast. It's a fact in the third ledger, page one, in my own hand. Somebody in this room should be doing arithmetic that assumes there will be a guild next year."

The silence that followed had a different texture than the first one. Maren looked at her for a long moment—not unkindly, but the way you look at a candle burning in a house that might be condemned.

"There will be a guild next year," Maren said, "because there has always been a guild next year."

"That," said Ines, "is not how ledgers work."

She gathered her book and went back downstairs before anyone could decide whether she'd been insolent. On the stairs she permitted herself exactly four steps of satisfaction—Hask had agreed with her, publicly—and then put it away, because satisfaction was a luxury item and she'd never learned where the guild sourced it.

* * *

The trading floor opened at noon, and by half past, Ines had logged a man trading his ability to whistle for passage on a ship he was already too seasick to enjoy; a widow trading the smell of her late husband's coat for one more winter of warm feet; and a fisherman who wanted to trade away the memory of what he'd seen beyond the harbor wall, and was refused, gently, because some memories are load-bearing.

It was good work. She believed that. Every trade was written fair, witnessed twice, priced honestly. When people left the guildhall, they left lighter, and the lightness was real even when the price was strange.

But nobody traded *with* her, exactly. They traded with the guild, and she happened to be holding the pen. Elders negotiated the large bargains. Ines recorded them. She had recorded eleven hundred and six trades and made none, and lately she had begun to feel like a scale that weighed everything and owned nothing.

The bell over the door rang at dusk, in the gray hour when the lamps were lit and honest folk were home.

The child who came in was perhaps eight, soaked to the knees, with the particular stubbornness of someone who has rehearsed a speech the whole way there.

"I want to trade," she announced.

Ines set down her pen. "We're open. What do you have?"

The child produced, from her pocket, a memory. Children carry them loosely, like river stones. "I remember my birthday. The one with the honey cake. All of it—the cake, the song, my da laughing with crumbs on his chin." Her chin wobbled and steadied. "I want bread. A loaf. And not the end-pieces."

Ines did not ask why. That was the first rule, and the kindest one. But she saw it anyway, the way keepers see things: the child's coat was a grown man's coat cut down, and there had been no honey cake this year, and probably no da either, and the memory hurt worse than hunger and was easier to hold than either.

"The standard rate for a full childhood memory is a week of meals," Ines said. "But birthdays with honey cake run higher. I can offer a loaf today and a standing claim on the kitchen through the winter."

The child considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Can I keep the part where he laughs?"

Ines's pen paused.

Most keepers would have said no. A partial trade fouls the ledger; memories don't carve cleanly, and what's left behind goes sour. It was in the rules. Ines had copied the rules herself, in her own careful hand, and knew them better than anyone alive.

"No," she said. "You keep all of it. That's not how I'm writing this one."

The child frowned, suspicious of kindness, as children in Vell rightly were. "Then what am I trading?"

"You're trading a copy," Ines said, and it was true, in the way guild truths were true: the memory would be taken, written fair, and returned to her dreams each night for as long as the guild stood to honor it. "And I'm paying a loaf and a winter. Both sides get more than they gave. That's called a good bargain. You'll know them again when you see one."

The child handed over the memory—it weighed almost nothing, the way important things don't—and received her loaf, and ate the heel first, defiantly, on the doorstep, watching Ines the whole time as though daring her to object.

When the door closed, Ines sat alone in the lamplight with the trade unwritten.

Here was the trouble. Trades were recorded under the name of the elder who brokered them. Maren's name filled pages. Corvin's. Even Hask's, entered post-nap. Ines's own name appeared only in the margins—*recorded by*—a ghost's signature on other people's lives.

She thought of Maren upstairs, insisting there would always be a guild next year. She thought of the third ledger, page one, where her title lived like a plant in a dark corner, growing toward a window that wasn't there.

So Ines Underhill did a small, dishonest, entirely honest thing.

She wrote the trade fair and complete—*one memory, honey cake and laughter, against one loaf and a winter's claim*—and beneath it, where the brokering elder's name belonged, she wrote:

**Brokered by Ines Underhill, keeper.**

Her first trade. Her name, in ink, attached to something that mattered—a child fed, a laugh preserved. It was possibly against regulation. It was certainly against hierarchy. She found, examining the feeling closely, that she did not care even slightly.

She was blotting the page, absurdly pleased, when the bells of the Fish Gate began to ring out of pattern—fast, arrhythmic, wrong—and boots sounded in the street outside, many boots, in step, and a crier's voice rose over the rooftops of Vell, reading words she couldn't yet hear but already, in her bones, dreaded.
