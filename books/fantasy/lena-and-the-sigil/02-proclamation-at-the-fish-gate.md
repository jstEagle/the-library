## 2. Proclamation at the Fish Gate

The decree went up at the Fish Gate on a Tuesday, which Ines thought was a coward's choice of day. Tuesdays were market days; the whole city would see it before the fish had finished smelling like themselves.

She was there to buy ink—ordinary ink, the kind that stayed where you put it—and she stopped in the crowd with her basket on her hip and read the proclamation twice, because the first time she assumed she'd misread it.

*By order of His Majesty's Crown: all persons possessed of unlicensed gifts, workings, or unnatural aptitudes shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those who register will be examined and, if found harmless, released. Those who do not will be taken.*

"Taken," said the woman beside her, tasting the word like bad oysters. "Taken where?"

Nobody answered. Nobody ever answers that kind of question out loud anymore.

The paper was fresh, the wax seal still glossy as a beetle's back. Below the royal arms someone had already scratched a small mark in charcoal—a spiral, quick and furtive, the kind of mark a child makes when they're testing whether anyone is watching. Ines looked around for the child and saw no one. She looked back at the spiral and felt something cold walk down her spine, because she recognized the hand. Not the child's hand. The *style* of it. The Ledgerwrights used that spiral in their margins to mean *a debt deferred*.

Someone was telling the gifted of Vell: we know, and we are waiting.

She bought her ink. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Underneath the steadiness, though, something had shifted its weight, the way a floorboard shifts before it gives.

* * *

"You're late," said Elder Corvane, not looking up from the ledger he was pretending to read. He'd been on the same page since she arrived, which meant he hadn't read a word of it, which meant he was frightened, which meant he'd heard about the proclamation hours ago and told no one.

"The Fish Gate has a new decoration," Ines said, setting down the ink. "Thirty days."

"I know how long thirty days is." Corvane closed the ledger with unnecessary force. He was the youngest of the elders at sixty-one, which told you everything about the state of the guild. The others were scattered across the city in safehouses that had stopped being safe sometime last winter, and every week another name in the roster went quiet, the way lamps go out along a street—one, then two, then a darkness with gaps where light used to be.

"Have you told the others?" she asked.

"There are four others. Two are deaf in one ear and the third believes the crown has been after him since the reign of the old king's grandmother." Corvane rubbed his eyes. "And the fourth is you, Ines, who is nineteen and records other people's bargains because she isn't allowed to make any."

That landed harder than he probably intended. It usually did. Corvane was not cruel; he was simply accurate at inconvenient moments, which in a guild of truth-keepers amounted to nearly the same thing.

"The apprentices—" she began.

"Are gone. Sent east, sent south, sent wherever their families could send them. What remains here is inventory and paper, and paper is now the most dangerous thing in this building." He stood, joints cracking like green kindling. "Come with me. There's a decision to make, and I find I don't want to make it alone, which is either wisdom or cowardice and I've stopped being able to tell."

* * *

The ledger room smelled of tallow and old vellum, and it held, by Ines's last count, eleven hundred years of bargains. Impossible things traded for impossible prices: a shadow sold back to its owner at interest; three days of someone else's luck; the memory of a song nobody else had ever heard, purchased by a dying woman so she could die humming it. The guild had never dealt in power, whatever the crown believed. It dealt in *weight*. Things people couldn't carry alone.

Corvane went to the oldest shelf—the one behind the loose panel, the one Ines had been forbidden to touch since her first day—and took down a ledger bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth.

"No," Ines said, before she knew she was going to say it.

"It's the names," Corvane said quietly. "Every client, every broker, every keeper who ever signed. Eleven centuries of them. If Renn's hunters get into this room, they won't need to hunt. They'll just read."

"So we hide it. We've hidden it for eleven hundred years—"

"We hid it from kings who wanted to *tax* us." He was already carrying it toward the hearth, and his hands were shaking, and Ines understood with a lurch that this was why he hadn't wanted to be alone. "These men want to erase us. You can't hide a book from someone who intends to burn the shelf it sits on."

"Then bury it. Sink it. Give it to the tide-wights, they love a good deposit—"

"Ines." He said her name the way you set down something heavy. "Half of these names belong to dead people whose grandchildren live in this city. Ordinary people. Bakers. If the ledger burns, the debts burn too, and some of those debts are protections. Some of those families are *safe* because a bargain says they must be."

"And if it doesn't burn, those same families get taken."

They looked at each other across the ledger room, and the fire crackled in the hearth like an eavesdropper.

In the end they compromised, which is what Ledgerwrights do instead of winning arguments. Half the book. The elder names, the dead names, the ones whose protections had long since lapsed into folklore. Corvane tore the binding himself, wincing at each rip like a man pulling teeth, and fed the pages to the fire while Ines stood witness, because a destruction of record required a witness and she was, as always, the one available.

The burning names smelled like nothing at all. That was the worst part. She'd expected the past to smell like something.

* * *

It was during the second armful that Elder Maren arrived.

Maren was eighty-three, mostly deaf, and had spent forty years brokering bargains in the salt quarter, and she came through the door mid-sentence as deaf people do when they believe everyone has been shouting without them.

"—told Hesper to move the strongbox, and she hasn't, and now the decree is up at the Fish Gate and you're all sitting here like mushrooms—"

"Maren." Corvane turned, a page half-burned in his hand. "We're handling it."

"*Handling* it." She spotted the fire, the torn binding, the ash. Ines watched understanding arrive on the old woman's face like weather. "You're burning it. You're burning *our book*."

"The parts that kill people—"

"Our book!" Maren's voice cracked upward into a register Ines had never heard from her, thin and furious. "I signed my name in that book when I was fifteen years old. My mother signed it. You don't get to decide which centuries matter, Corvane, you weren't even—give it to me. Give me the rest of it. I'll sink it myself, I'll—"

"Maren, sit down—"

"Don't you tell me to sit—"

And then Maren stopped. Mid-word, mid-gesture, one hand still raised toward the ledger. She didn't fall so much as pause, standing very straight, her mouth open on the shape of a word that never came out, and then she went down sideways onto the reading table, scattering ink pots, and Corvane shouted, and Ines caught her—caught her, actually caught her, arms under the old woman's shoulders—and felt the weight of her go entirely, completely absent.

The physician came, eventually, and said the words physicians say. A vessel in the brain. Quick. She wouldn't have known.

But Ines had been holding her when it happened, and she knew that wasn't quite true. Maren had known. For one instant, mid-argument, mid-fury, the old woman's face had gone calm as a frozen pond—as if she'd seen, in the space between one word and the next, exactly what was coming and decided not to waste her last breath finishing the sentence.

* * *

They buried Maren quickly and quietly, in the salt quarter, in ground that technically belonged to no parish, which meant it belonged to them. Four mourners. Ines counted them and hated herself for counting—it was the guild habit, tallying everything—but grief and arithmetic had shared a bed in her chest for so long she no longer knew how to separate them.

It was afterward, clearing out Maren's rooms above the old counting-house, that Ines found the sigil.

Not *found*, precisely. It fell.

Maren's coat hung on its hook by the door, the heavy winter coat she'd worn every day for twenty years, and when Ines lifted it down to fold it, something slid from the inner pocket, bounced once off the floorboards, and spun to rest against her boot.

A disc of dark metal, no bigger than a coin but heavier than it had any right to be. One face smooth. The other carved with a door—not a picture of a door, but the *idea* of one, rendered in lines so old the style predated the city itself. And around the door's edge, in script Ines could read only because she'd spent her life reading ledgers: *freely given, freely kept.*

Her mouth went dry.

She knew what it was. Every apprentice knew the story the way children know the story of the moon: the Hollow Sigil, the key of keys, the thing the first keepers made when the world had more doors in it than it does now. She'd assumed it was a story. Guilds ran on stories the way ships ran on ballast—you carried them so you didn't tip over.

But here it was, warm from an old woman's pocket, and Ines understood suddenly why Maren had argued so hard about the ledger. Why she'd come storming in. Why her last unfinished word might have been *give it to me*—not the book.

The sigil. Maren had been carrying it. Perhaps for years. Perhaps she'd meant to pass it on and had run out of time in the middle of a sentence, the way everything in this guild was running out of time.

Footsteps on the stairs. Corvane, coming up, calling her name.

Ines looked at the sigil in her palm. Then she did the thing she'd done her whole life, the thing the guild had trained into her bones without ever meaning to: she made a record. Silently, instantly, in the ledger behind her eyes, she wrote the transaction down.

*One sigil, received. No price paid. Witness: myself.*

Then she dropped it into her apron pocket, picked up the folded coat, and turned to answer Corvane with a steady voice and an empty face.

Because invisibility had been safety, once. But the decree at the Fish Gate had a spiral scratched beneath it in a hand she recognized, and somewhere in this city a hunter with a patient smile was already reading names, and Ines Underhill—who had recorded a thousand bargains and never been allowed to make one—was holding the only key to the only door, and had just entered it into evidence.

She was, she realized as she climbed down the stairs toward Corvane's worried face, no longer invisible at all.

She was the most dangerous page left in the book.
