## 3. What the Map Remembers

Tomas Vey had been thrown out of better rooms than this one.

He sat in the back of the Saltcellar tavern with a mug of small beer going warm and a sheet of good paper under his elbow, and he drew. He had been drawing it for three nights now, in pieces, so that no single page would tell the whole story: a street called Candlemakers' Row, which began where Cooper's Lane bent and ended at a wall that was not there anymore, except that it was, if you knew how to look. The crown had unmade Candlemakers' Row eleven years ago. There had been a fire, an inquiry, a decree, and then there had been no Row at all — no record, no ruin, no name on any map printed since. The people who had lived there were reassigned to other streets in the ledgers, given other histories, and most of them had been grateful enough to accept. It is easier to remember what you are told than to insist on what you saw.

Tomas had seen. Tomas had drawn it, once, in the royal atlas itself, in his own careful hand, and that was why he no longer worked for the crown and why his name appeared on a list pinned inside the guardhouse with a reward figure beneath it that he considered frankly insulting. Eleven crowns. He had mapped the drowned coast of Sarn before anyone told him Sarn was not to be mapped. He had charted the shifting isles off Vell's harbor so accurately that ships stopped being lost, and they gave him eleven crowns and a cell with a view of the exercise yard.

So now he drew in taverns, in pieces, for himself, because a man who cannot stop doing the thing that ruined him is either a fool or an artist, and Tomas had long ago settled on fool as the more comfortable word.

The trouble with the Saltcellar was the boy.

He was maybe ten, selling roasted chestnuts along the benches, and he kept drifting past Tomas's table and slowing, the way people slow past something they can't explain. On the fourth pass, the boy set down his basket, leaned over, and said, "That's not a real street."

"It's a very real street," Tomas said mildly, not looking up. "It's simply not an official one."

"No." The boy tapped the paper with a chestnut-blackened finger. "I mean it's not anywhere. I've walked Cooper's Lane. It just ends at a wall. But your drawing has the wall *open*."

Tomas looked up. The boy was looking at the map the way you look at a window when you expected a mirror — with that slight, terrible tilt of the head that meant he was seeing through it. Tomas had met perhaps six people in his life who could do that, and two of them had been hanged.

"Boy," he said quietly, "how much for the rest of the chestnuts?"

"Four coppers."

"I'll give you eight, and you'll take them and go home by the long way, and tomorrow you'll have never seen me."

"That's a bad bargain," the boy said. "You're paying extra for me to forget something interesting."

"Yes," Tomas said. "Welcome to adulthood."

But it was too late, of course. Because that was when the door of the Saltcellar opened, and the cold came in wearing a gray coat, and behind the gray coat came two more, and the boy went pale in a way that had nothing to do with chestnuts.

"Tomas Vey," said the first hunter, reading from a paper, though he was looking directly at him, so the reading was theater. "By order of the Crown Commission on Unregistered Gifts—"

Tomas did the arithmetic every hunted man does: exits, distance, witnesses, the weight of the chestnut basket versus the weight of regret. Two exits. Hunters between him and both. A room full of tavern-goers performing the ancient civic art of seeing nothing.

Then the boy, gods bless him and keep him, upended the entire basket of chestnuts across the floor.

It bought four seconds of chaos — hunters stepping wide, a fishwife swearing magnificently, hot chestnuts skittering like small brown animals with somewhere urgent to be. Tomas took his map, left his coin, and went over the back bench toward the kitchen door, which was the worst door, which meant it was the door nobody guarded properly.

He got through the kitchen, through the yard, over a fence that tore his coat, into the tangle of lanes behind the cooperage where the city grew old and crooked and forgot its own geometry. Behind him, boots. Ahead of him, a wall.

Not a metaphorical wall. An actual wall, brick, higher than his raised hand, closing off the lane — except that Tomas knew this lane, had drawn this lane, and there was no wall here. There was a door here. A narrow door in a narrow gap between a chandler's shop and a house that leaned like a drunk uncle, a door he had put on a private sketch years ago because it had always struck him as odd: a door with no building above it, no lintel, no windows flanking, just a door standing in a gap as if the buildings on either side were politely pretending not to notice it.

Except tonight the gap was walled. And the door was gone.

Behind him, closer, the boots. And ahead — Tomas closed his eyes, did the thing he was punished for doing, the thing that had cost him everything, and *looked*. Not with his eyes. With whatever it was in him that maps answered to. The lane remembered itself around him. He felt the shape of the missing door the way your tongue finds the socket of a lost tooth: a hollow, an insistence, a place where something used to fit and still wanted to.

"There," he whispered, and put out his hand, and drew — not on paper, in the air, in the dark, the line of the doorframe, the panels, the iron ring — and the wall *argued* with him, brick by brick, but walls are only memory made heavy, and his memory was better.

The door opened under his hand onto darkness that smelled of ink and tallow and old paper, and Tomas Vey, disgraced cartographer, fell through it, pulled it shut behind him, and stood in the black listening to hunters curse on the far side of a wall that had, moments ago, been very committed to existing.

* * *

A candle came toward him down the corridor of shelves.

Tomas pressed himself against the shelving, which was a mistake, because the shelving was full of books that were not entirely books — he could feel them the way he felt maps, humming faintly, like struck bells that hadn't quite finished ringing — and one of them hummed *louder* as he touched it, a rising note, and the candle swung toward the sound.

"Don't touch anything," said a voice. Young. Female. Steadier than it probably felt. "Some of them bite. Some of them worse."

"I didn't—" Tomas began.

"You touched shelf nine. Everyone touches shelf nine. Shelf nine is a problem we've been meaning to solve." The candle arrived, and behind it arrived a young woman with ink-stained fingers and the particular posture of someone who has recently inherited a great deal of responsibility and none of the authority to match. She looked at him — really looked, top to bottom, cataloguing — and then her gaze snagged on his hands, on the ghost-lines of graphite ground into his knuckles, and her expression changed.

"You're a mapwright," she said.

"Cartographer."

"You're a *mapwright*," she repeated, with the emphasis of someone correcting a euphemism, "and you just opened a door that hasn't opened in forty years by *drawing it in the air*." She lowered the candle slightly. "Who taught you?"

"The Royal Survey," Tomas said. "Before they taught me the difference between what exists and what is permitted to."

Something moved behind her eyes at that — recognition, quick and quickly hidden. She glanced at the door behind him, at the wall beyond it, then back.

"They're hunting you."

"They're hunting everyone this month. I'm merely well-listed." He held out his hands, palms up, the universal gesture of the unarmed and exhausted. "Look — I'll go back out the way I came, if the way will let me. I didn't know this place existed. I won't say it does now. That's rather my specialty."

"Is it," she said. Not a question. She studied him another moment, and then, apparently deciding something, stepped aside and lifted the candle toward the shelves. "Ines Underhill. Keeper of the Ledgerwrights' guildhall, such as it is. Which means, at present, keeper of a hall containing me, three elders who don't agree about anything, and roughly four thousand objects the crown would burn us for."

"Ines Underhill," Tomas repeated slowly. The name turned over in his memory and came up wet. "The Ledgerwrights. You trade in impossible things."

"We *record* trades in impossible things. We don't make them. Making them is how guilds get burned down." She said it lightly, but her hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to her collarbone, where something hung on a cord beneath her shirt — he caught the glint of metal, quickly tucked away. "And you're Tomas Vey. The Sarn charts."

"You know the Sarn charts?"

"I've read them." She turned and began walking, expecting him to follow, which after everything seemed suddenly the most natural instruction in the world. "Or rather, I've read the ledger entries *about* them. The guild brokered the survey commission, sixty years ago. Your fee is recorded. Your disgrace is recorded too, I'm afraid. Entry 4,112. 'The crown repudiates all findings; the mapwright is remanded.' We wrote it down because we write everything down." She glanced back at him, and there was something almost apologetic in it. "Even the things people would pay a great deal to have unwritten."

They came out into a wider room — a counting room, or what had been one, desks under dust sheets, a great ledger open on the central table with a stone keeping its place. Candles everywhere, more than prudence allowed, as if someone had been afraid of the dark lately.

Tomas stopped in the doorway and breathed it in. Ink. Tallow. Old vellum. And underneath it, faint but unmistakable to him, the same hum he'd felt on the shelves — the whole room resonating faintly, like a harbor at low tide, full of things held just below the surface.

"You feel it," Ines said, watching his face. "Don't you. The inventory."

"I feel a room that remembers things rooms shouldn't."

"My grandmother said the guildhall sits on a debt the city owes and refuses to acknowledge. She was being poetic. Probably." She pulled the dust sheet off a chair and pushed it toward him. "Sit. You look like a man who's been drawing Candlemakers' Row in taverns."

Tomas went very still. "How—"

"Because the boy with the chestnuts came here an hour ago, shaking, saying a man at the Saltcellar was drawing a street that isn't there, and that the drawing *opened*, and that the hunters came, and that he'd taken your money and wished he hadn't." She sat down across from him, folded her ink-stained hands, and looked at him with an expression that was, he realized, the exact twin of his own in mirrors: the look of someone who has spent a lifetime being careful and is tired of mistaking it for safety.

"So," Ines Underhill said. "Tell me about the door you drew in the air. Because there's a sigil in this building that opens a door like that, and until tonight I thought we had the only key."

Tomas sat. Outside, somewhere over the rooftops, the hunters were still searching a lane that had decided, obligingly, to forget him.

"Where do I start?" he asked.

She reached across the table and turned the great ledger toward him, to a page near the end, where a fresh entry waited in a young, careful hand:

*Tomas Vey, mapwright. Entered the hall pursued. Entered freely.*

"Start there," she said. "Everyone does."
