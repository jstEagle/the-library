## 4. Inventory of Ghosts

The oldest ledger lived in the deepest shelf of the guildhall's cellar, behind a lock that had rusted shut sometime before Ines was born, and it took her the better part of a morning and one sacrificed hairpin to open it.

She told herself she was doing inventory. That was the honest word for it, or near enough: Elder Voss was dead, Elder Maren had fled north with a satchel of deeds and no explanation, and the remaining elders were too busy arguing about which debts could be paid with what remained of the treasury to count anything as unfashionable as ghosts. Someone had to know what the guild still held. Someone always had to know.

So: inventory.

The cellar smelled of salt and old paper and, faintly, of tallow candles burned by hands long since scattered into ash or exile. Ines carried down a single lamp and a fresh quill and sat on an upturned crate in her gray wool dress with her sleeves pushed to the elbow, and she opened the first ledger—the *first* ledger, the one with no title on its spine because it predated titles—and began where all good accounting begins: at the beginning.

The early pages were beautiful. Whoever had kept them had a hand like falling water, each entry balanced to the half-penny, each impossible thing described with the sober precision of a grocer counting flour.

*One winter, extended by six weeks, sold to the village of Harrowmere. Paid in full: the village's collective memory of the summer that should have followed.*

*One voice, borrowed from a dead singer, lent to a mute girl for the length of one wedding. Returned slightly worn.*

*One door, painted onto a wall, made passable for a single night. Buyer's name withheld at buyer's request. Fee: a secret, freely given, never recorded.*

Ines read with her chin in her palm and forgot, for whole minutes at a time, to be afraid. This was the guild as it had been meant to be—not the dwindling, frightened thing she'd inherited, haggling over bread-memories in a city filling up with witch-hunters, but something vast and careful and strange. A guild that traded in impossibility and kept its books anyway. She loved it the way you love a grandparent's stories: knowing they are not entirely yours, wanting them anyway.

Then the handwriting changed.

It happened gradually, the way rot does. The water-fall hand gave way to a stiffer script, then a cramped one, then—around the middle of the book—to entries written by several different clerks, some of them blotched, some of them hurried, as though the writers kept expecting to be interrupted.

And the entries themselves changed too.

*By order of the Crown's Envoy and for the consideration hereinafter named, the Guild of Ledgerwrights agrees to broker the following instrument between His Majesty's government and the government of Sarn—*

Ines stopped reading.

She knew the word *Sarn*. Everyone in Vell knew it the way you know the shape of a missing tooth: there was a country that used to be across the narrow sea, or perhaps along the coast, or perhaps—it depended who you asked—a day's ride east, before the marshes. Maps disagreed. Elders, when pressed, would smile vaguely and say *Sarn? I hardly recall*, and the strangest part was that they seemed to mean it. There was a hole in the world where Sarn had been, and everyone stepped around it without noticing they were stepping.

She had assumed, in the vague way children assume things and adults never bother to correct, that Sarn had simply *ended*. Countries did that. Wars, plagues, bad harvests. The world was full of edges where things stopped.

The ledger said otherwise.

She turned the pages slowly now, the lamp flame leaning as if it too wanted a better look. The treaty ran eleven pages. It was drafted in the guild's own house style—considerations listed, conditions numbered, every clause initialed in the margin by a clerk whose initials Ines recognized, because those same initials appeared in ledgers only two generations old, in the training exercises she herself had copied as an apprentice. *M.O.* Master Oviden. The founder of the modern guild. The man whose portrait hung in the entry hall looking wise and mildly apologetic, as portraits of accountants generally do.

The consideration—the payment the guild received for brokering the treaty—was recorded on the final page in Oviden's own hand, and it was this:

*For services rendered, the Crown grants the Guild of Ledgerwrights in perpetuity: the Hollow Door, and sole keeping of the sigil that opens it.*

Ines sat very still on her crate.

Below that, in smaller writing, almost squeezed into the margin as though Oviden had hoped future readers wouldn't look too closely:

*Note for my successors. The Door answers to our sigil alone. Its price is fixed and has ever been so: a true name, freely given, by the one who turns the key. No coin substitutes. No memory substitutes. A name is the one thing a person owns outright, and the Door will accept nothing less. May we never have cause to use it. — M.O.*

*May we never have cause to use it.* And yet the sigil hung on its cord against Ines's breastbone even now, taken from a dying elder's fingers, warming faintly against her skin like a sleeping animal. And yet the treaty existed. And yet Sarn did not.

Because that was the clause she made herself read three times, tracing each word with her fingertip as if touch might change it. The treaty did not destroy Sarn. Nothing so crude. The treaty *removed* it—from maps, from memory, from the agreement of the world itself—so thoroughly that even the people of Sarn, presumably, had forgotten they existed. Except. Except the treaty's final provision, the one the crown had insisted on, the one the guild had recorded without comment:

*A single passage shall remain, that any who come to remember may leave. The Door shall stand where Sarn stood, answering to the keepers' sigil, until such time as the instrument is fulfilled or undone.*

A door out of a country that had been erased. For anyone who managed, against all odds, to remember.

Ines thought about the hole in the world. She thought about how, when she was nine, she had found a child's shoe on the harbor stones—a small leather shoe, salt-stiffened, washed up from nowhere—and how the harbormaster had looked at it for a long moment with an expression she hadn't understood then, and had thrown it back into the sea, and had said, gently, *nothing washes ashore from nowhere, girl,* and had forgotten her own sentence before he finished saying it.

Her eyes were burning. She let them. Nobody watched the cellar; nobody had watched the cellar in years; that was rather the point of cellars.

Here was the arithmetic she could not avoid, and Ines Underhill was her mother's daughter and the Ledgerwrights' last keeper both, and she did not flinch from arithmetic. Her guild had built the cage. Her guild had kept the key. Every elder who smiled vaguely at the name Sarn, every map that disagreed with itself, every harbormaster throwing a child's shoe back into the sea—they were all, every one of them, standing inside a bargain their predecessors had struck, spending the interest on a debt they'd been carefully prevented from remembering they owed.

And the crown's hunters were in Vell now, posting proclamations and taking the gifted away in wagons, and Ines had assumed—she saw it clearly now, how neatly she'd assumed it—that the hunt was fear. Superstition. Kings frightened of things they couldn't tax.

But kings did not send patient men with courteous manners to seize ledgers over superstition. Kings sent them over *keys*.

The guild wasn't being hunted. The guild was being *repossessed*.

She laughed—one short, appalled note that bounced off the cellar walls and came back sounding like someone else—and pressed her hand flat against the old page, over Oviden's hopeful marginal note. *May we never have cause to use it.* Well, Master Oviden. Here we are, three generations later, with hunters at the Fish Gate and your successors scattered like dropped coins, and I am the last person alive who knows what we're holding.

Grief and purpose arrived together, the way they do, braided so tightly she couldn't tell which strand was which. She grieved for Sarn—for the shoe, for the harbormaster's gentle forgetting, for a whole country walking unremembered somewhere on the other side of a door her guild owned. And she grieved for the guild itself, the beautiful careful strange thing she'd loved since childhood, which turned out to have a body under its floorboards after all. But underneath the grief, steady as a keel: purpose. Because a debt you discover is a debt you can pay. A complicity you inherit is a complicity you can end. That was the whole of the Ledgerwright creed, the first thing taught to apprentices: *every entry can be balanced. It may cost everything. Balance it anyway.*

Ines took out her quill.

She did not write in the old ledger. The old ledger belonged to the people who had signed the treaty, and she would not add her hand to theirs. Instead she opened her own book—the new one, the one she'd begun the night she recorded a child's bread-memory under her own name—and on a fresh page she wrote, in her best falling-water hand:

*Inventory of ghosts, taken this day by Ines Underhill, keeper. Item: one country, erased, location uncertain, population unknown. Item: one treaty, criminal, unsigned by any party who understood it. Item: one Door, hollow, answering to the sigil in my keeping. Price of opening: a true name, freely given.*

She paused, quill hovering, and then added:

*Liability accepted in full by the undersigned.*

She signed it. Her true name, the whole of it, the one she'd been saving all her life the way misers save coins—*Ines Amalia Underhill, daughter of Petra Underhill, keeper of the Ledgerwrights of Vell.* Writing it felt like setting down something heavy and picking up something heavier, and lighter, somehow, at the same time.

She was blotting the ink when she heard the footsteps overhead—quick, uneven, not the shuffling tread of any elder—and then a crash, a shout, and the unmistakable sound of a door slamming somewhere in the guildhall that she was quite certain had no door in it.

Ines blew out the lamp, tucked the old ledger under her arm, and climbed toward the noise with the sigil warm against her chest and her heart going like a hammer.

Someone had just found the guildhall the way Tomas Vey found streets: by drawing what shouldn't exist.
