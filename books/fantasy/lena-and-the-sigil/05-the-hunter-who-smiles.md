## 5. The Hunter Who Smiles

Tomas Vey had spent eleven years learning to make himself small on paper, and he had thought the skill would transfer to life. It did not. Life, it turned out, had a compass.

He heard about it first in a tavern near the ropewalks, from a fishwife who spoke of it the way sailors speak of weather—matter-of-factly, with a professional respect for things that can kill you. *The captain carries a needle that doesn't point north,* she said, wiping her hands. *Points at people. My sister saw it swing toward a boy selling charms off Salt Lane, and the captain smiled at him like he'd found a coin in his soup.*

The boy was taken before sundown. The charms were just charms—dried lavender and string. That was the worst part, Tomas thought later. The compass didn't care whether the gift was real. It cared that you might believe you had one.

* * *

Captain Aldous Renn conducted his interrogations in the customs house, which was a courtesy in itself; other hunters used cellars. He arranged chairs in a semicircle and offered tea, and he remembered names—not just the names of the people he questioned but their children, their dead wives, the shops they'd inherited from their fathers. He asked after a bad knee with such genuine sympathy that people found themselves telling him things they hadn't meant to know they knew.

Tomas watched him once, from the gallery above the customs floor, under the pretense of delivering survey documents he had, in fact, forged an hour earlier for exactly this purpose. Renn was a broad, tidy man with grey at his temples and the patient hands of someone who had never once needed to hurry. His coat was plain. His boots were good. And on the table beside him, next to the teapot, sat a brass compass the size of a plum, its lid open, its needle trembling.

It did not point north. Tomas could tell because he had spent his whole life knowing where north was—it was the first thing a cartographer learned and the last thing he ever forgot, the way a priest knows where the altar stands even blindfolded. The needle swung lazily, ignoring the world's honest geometry entirely, and when a clerk crossed the floor below carrying a basket of oranges, the needle leaned toward him like a flower toward sun.

The clerk was probably innocent. Probably. Renn noted something in a little book anyway, smiling as he wrote, and the smile was the thing Tomas couldn't stop thinking about afterward. It wasn't cruel. That was the horror of it. It was the smile of a man doing work he believed in, thoroughly and gently, the way a good surgeon believes in amputation.

*You're not hunting monsters,* Tomas thought, gripping the rail. *You're hunting the possibility of them. And you'll take everything that might grow into one, just to be safe.*

Below, Renn looked up—directly up, into the gallery, into Tomas's eyes—and raised his teacup an inch, as if in greeting.

Tomas left by the back stairs and did not run until he reached the street.

* * *

"He remembers faces," Ines said. She was at the guildhall's long table with three ledgers open around her like fortifications, and she had not looked up when he came in, which was her way of saying she'd heard his footsteps on the stairs and identified them as safe. "That's worse than cruelty. Cruel men forget. Courteous ones file."

"Then we should be grateful he's courteous," Tomas said, hanging his wet coat by the fire. It had started raining, the fine cold rain of late autumn that seemed less like weather than like the city itself developing a nervous condition. "A cruel captain burns the quarter down. This one will simply knock on every door, drink every cup of tea, and write down every name, and in ten years there won't be a gifted soul in Vell who isn't registered, taxed, or gone."

"And the sigil?"

"Still pointing at us." He nodded at her pocket, where she kept the elder's brass ring wrapped in wool. He'd seen the compass react to nothing else the way it must react to that—the Hollow Door's key, humming its quiet impossible hum against her hip. "Whatever that thing is, it's loud, Ines. Loud enough that a needle across the harbor might lean toward this building one day."

She finally looked up. She had ink on her cheekbone, always, no matter how often she wiped it; Tomas had privately concluded she produced it the way snails produce shell. "Then we move it," she said. "Or we move ourselves. Or—" she hesitated, and he watched her decide, visibly, to trust him with the rest, "—or we learn what it opens before anyone else does. I've been reading the old ledgers, Tomas. There are things in them I need to show you. But not tonight. Tonight I need to finish cataloguing, because if the guild is going to die, it's going to die with its books balanced."

There it was: the stubbornness that he was beginning, against all sense, to find dear. "Balance your books," he said. "I'll go out tomorrow. There are people who owe me favors, and I mean to spend every one of them before Renn spends them for us."

* * *

The favor he spent was Merrit's.

Merrit Cole had been a junior surveyor in the royal cartography office back when Tomas still had a title, a desk, and a future. When Tomas fell—when his maps began showing streets that officially did not exist, and the office decided the problem was the mapmaker rather than the city—Merrit was the only one who'd shaken his hand on the way out. He was a round, cheerful man who whistled while he worked and drew coastlines so lovingly that gulls had been known to land on them.

Tomas found him at the tide-survey shed and laid it out plainly: a false trail, drawn in Tomas's own hand, convincing enough to pull a compass off the guildhall's scent. A map of "gifted persons," scattered addresses, half invented, half real-but-harmless—a widow who talked to her bees, a ferryman who claimed to smell storms coming. Nothing that would hurt them. Just noise. Static. Enough wrong signals to keep the needle spinning while the guild figured out what the sigil opened.

"I can copy his hand," Merrit said, turning the plan over with the seriousness he gave all maps. "I've watched him write. Little flourish on the capital letters, like he's signing each word personally. Give me two days."

"You don't have to do this."

"Tomas." Merrit grinned. "You drew me a street that doesn't exist and got ruined for it. Least I can do is draw some people who don't exist and get rich. Where's my cut?"

"There is no cut."

"Then where's my drink?"

"The drink I'll manage."

They laughed, and it was the last easy hour either of them would have for a long time, though neither knew it, which Tomas supposed was mercy of a kind.

* * *

The forgery was beautiful. Tomas hated how beautiful it was—Renn's hand rendered so faithfully that he felt like a ventriloquist, speaking with a dead man's mouth. They planted it where a hunter's runner would find it: tucked in the lining of a seized coat at the impound yard, folded around a scrap of paper bearing the guild's old mark, deliberately smudged.

For six days, it worked.

The compass needle spun. Renn's runners crisscrossed the city chasing bees-widows and storm-smellers, and the tea in the customs house went cold over maps of addresses that dissolved on inspection. Twice, Tomas watched from a distance as the captain stood in a street holding the compass, watching the needle waver between points like a man trying to choose between two lovers, and both times, the guildhall stayed dark and unremarkable behind him.

On the seventh day, Renn stopped wavering.

Tomas learned it the way you learn most disasters: too late, and from someone else's mouth. A runner from the ropewalks, breathless, finding him at the fish market. *The captain's at the tide-survey shed. He's asking who draws maps there.*

By the time Tomas got there—running, actually running, coat flying, past stalls and startled fishwives—the shed was surrounded. Not stormed. Surrounded, politely, the way Renn did everything. The captain stood in the doorway with the compass open in his palm, and inside, visible through the window, Merrit sat at his drafting table with his hands flat on the wood, very still, the way men go still when they understand that movement has a price.

Tomas stopped at the corner. Twenty yards of open street between him and the door. He had a forged pass in his pocket and a face Renn had already seen once across a teacup, and neither would survive twenty yards of that man's attention.

Inside, Merrit looked up. Through the glass, across the distance, he found Tomas standing frozen at the corner—and he smiled. The same grin as the tide-survey shed. *Where's my drink?*

And then, slowly, deliberately, Merrit Cole picked up the nearest thing to hand—a coastline chart, weeks of loving work—and began, in full view of everyone, to feed it into the stove.

Because a man who destroys crown property in front of a witch-hunter is a man with something to hide. Because a man with something to hide draws the compass needle like a lodestone. Because whatever Merrit knew—and he knew about Tomas, about the forgery, about the guildhall—he was choosing, right now, with a stove and a chart, to be the loudest possible signal in the room.

The needle in Renn's hand snapped toward the shed and stayed there.

Tomas understood it all in the space of a breath, and understood equally that there was nothing—nothing—in his considerable arsenal of impossible skills that could change what happened next. Some maps you cannot redraw. You can only stand at the edge of them and watch where the roads go.

They took Merrit out the front door, courteous as Sunday. He didn't look at the corner again. Renn paused on the step, consulted his little book, and wrote something down with that gentle, thorough smile, and then the party moved off through the rain toward the customs house, and the street closed behind them like water.

* * *

Ines found him that night in the guildhall's map room, sitting in the dark with the lamps unlit, surrounded by cities that existed and one that didn't.

"Merrit Cole," she said quietly. Not a question. News traveled; the guild's remnant had ears everywhere the crown didn't.

"He took the blame for the forgery. Made himself the signal so the needle would stop looking for the hand behind it." Tomas's voice came out flat, which surprised him; he'd expected it to break. "I built the trap, Ines. I drew every line of it. I told myself nobody would be hurt—that it was noise, static, harmless. I chose the widow with the bees. I chose the ferryman. I chose Merrit because he owed me a favor and I spent him like coin."

Ines pulled a chair around and sat opposite him, close, in the dark. For a long moment she said nothing, and he was grateful, because the alternative words—*it wasn't your fault*, the kind lie—would have been unbearable.

"We record debts here," she said at last. "All of them. Even the ones we can't repay. Especially those." She slid a ledger across the table toward him, open to a blank page, and set the pen beside it. "Write his name. Write what he paid and what it bought. That's what a Ledgerwright does with blood on her hands, Tomas. She doesn't wash it off. She enters it."

His eyes had adjusted to the dark enough to see her face now, and what he saw there frightened him more than Renn ever could: not pity. Recognition. She had blood of her own somewhere in those ledgers, older and stranger, and she was not offering him absolution. She was offering him company.

Tomas took the pen. His hand, which had never once shaken over a chart, shook now.

*Merrit Cole,* he wrote. *Who whistled at his work. Who drew coastlines gulls trusted. Who fed a map to a stove so a needle would look at him instead of us. Owed: everything. Paid: everything.*

Somewhere outside, the rain kept falling, and beneath the harbor, in the drowned quarter where Sarn used to be, something waited behind a door that answered to a brass ring wrapped in wool. And somewhere across the sleeping city, in a warm room in the customs house, Captain Aldous Renn dipped his pen and wrote a name in his own little book, and smiled.

Two books now, Tomas thought, setting down the pen. Two books, and only one of them could afford to lose.
