## 6. A Bargain in the Salt Cellar

The vote was held in the counting room, which was the only room left in the guildhall with enough chairs and no windows to shatter.

There were eleven of them now. Eleven, out of a guild that had once filled three floors and a courtyard with the sound of quills. Ines sat at the head of the table because the sigil was in her pocket and nobody had asked for it back, which was as close to an election as they were going to get.

"We should go," said Maren Oduya, who had been the guild's appraiser for forty years and could tell you what a lie weighed to the ounce. She laid her hands flat on the table. "Renn is working outward from the Fish Gate. He'll reach us within the month. The Door is the only way out that doesn't have a road at the end of it."

"And go where?" demanded old Pellam, who was mostly deaf and entirely stubborn. "Through a door into a country that stopped existing? That's not escape, that's just dying somewhere with better manners."

"It's not dying," Maren said. "It's *elsewhere*."

"Elsewhere," Pellam repeated, as if tasting something spoiled.

Ines let them argue. She had learned, in her nineteen years among the Ledgerwrights, that argument was how this guild loved each other—loudly, at volume, with citations. But she watched their faces while they did it, and every face said the same thing: *I am afraid, and I will call it principle until the fear gets too heavy.*

When they finally turned to her, she took out the sigil and set it on the table between a candlestick and a jar of pickled herring that someone had been hoarding against all sense.

"The Door answers to this," she said. "But there's a problem."

"There's always a problem," said Pellam, with something like comfort.

"The Door opens where Sarn used to be." She let that sit. Some of them flinched; Sarn was a word they'd been trained all their lives not to think too hard about. "The drowned quarter. Beyond the harbor wall. Where the water is."

Silence, except for the candle spitting.

"You're saying we swim," Pellam said at last.

"I'm saying we need help. There are things under the harbor that remember when it wasn't harbor. One of them owes the guild—or did, once. I found the entry." She tapped the ledger she'd brought down from the archive, open to a page so old the ink had gone the color of tea. "*Debt outstanding: one passage, safe conduct over or through water, payable to the bearer of the sigil.* No date. No expiry. Debts like that don't expire. That's rather the point of us."

Maren leaned forward. "And the price?"

"That's the other thing." Ines kept her voice level. "The wight won't take coin. It takes memory. Specifically—" and here she made herself say it plainly, because plainness was armor, "—memories of my mother."

* * *

They tried to talk her out of it for two hours, which she privately appreciated even though it changed nothing.

"She can't be the one," Pellam insisted. "She's the keeper. If anything happens to her—"

"If anything happens to me, the sigil goes to whoever catches it," Ines said. "That's also rather the point of us."

The truth, which she did not say aloud, was simpler and worse: the memories were hers to give because they were the only currency she owned outright. The ledgers belonged to the guild. The hall belonged to the dead elders' estates, tangled up in crown writs now. Even her name would belong to the Door, eventually, if chapter four of the old treaty meant what she thought it meant. But her mother's face, her mother's voice humming off-key in the kitchen, the smell of cedar and salt on her coat—all of that was Ines's alone, unrecorded, untraded, hers. Which meant, by the oldest law of the Ledgerwrights, it was spendable.

She hated that law. She understood it completely.

The vote passed at dusk. Nine for, one against (Pellam), one abstaining (little Corvin, who was fourteen and had already lost more than anyone should and didn't believe in choosing things anymore). They would go through the Door, all eleven of them, into whatever was left where Sarn had been, and they would carry the ledgers, and the guild would live.

Ines went down to the salt cellar to prepare.

* * *

The cellar ran beneath the whole guildhall, older than the guild itself, its walls crusted white where the sea had been seeping in since before anyone thought to mind. It was where the guild had always conducted its wettest business. Salt preserved; salt remembered. Ines had been told as a child that you could hear bargains being made down here if you pressed your ear to the wall, and she had spent one entire summer doing exactly that, hearing nothing, hoping desperately.

Tonight the walls were listening back.

She came alone, carrying a lantern, the sigil, and a small folded paper on which she had written, in her best hand, the terms she intended to offer. Preparation mattered. Her mother had taught her that—not with words, but with the sight of her laying out tools in neat rows before any job, however small, however tired she was. *A thing done properly honors the person you do it for,* she used to say, and then she'd hum, off-key, and Ines would roll her eyes, and both of them would pretend this wasn't the truest sentence either of them knew.

The tide-wight rose out of the flooded end of the cellar without ceremony, the way weather does.

It was smaller than she expected—no taller than a child, made of the particular darkness that water has when it's deep and thinking. Its face was a suggestion. Its voice arrived not through the air but through the stone, up through her boots, into her teeth.

**Bearer,** it said. **You are small.**

"I'm the keeper," Ines said. "Small runs in the profession."

**The last who stood here was tall and lied beautifully.**

"That would be Elder Voss. He's dead. His debts aren't." She held up the ledger page, copied fair. "One passage. Safe conduct. Payable to the bearer of the sigil. That's me."

The wight drifted closer. Where it moved, the salt crust bloomed and glistened, waking up.

**Passage for how many?**

"Eleven. And the ledgers. All of them."

**Heavy cargo.** A pause that felt like amusement. **Memories, then. Yours.**

"Memories of my mother," Ines said. "That's what I'm offering. Not all of them. I keep—" her voice caught, and she steadied it the way she'd been taught to steady ink lines, slowly, from the wrist, "—I keep the ones where she's teaching me something. Those are load-bearing. You can have the rest."

**Which rest?**

And there it was: the terrible arithmetic. She had spent the walk down cataloging, because cataloging was the only prayer she knew. Her mother laughing at the puppet show on Rope Street. Her mother braiding her hair badly and blaming the braid. Her mother asleep in a chair with the mending still in her lap. Her mother's hand on her forehead during the fever winter, cool as a coin. Her mother saying *you matter, little ledger, you matter to me*, which Ines had never told a living soul and which sat in her chest like a coal she'd been warming herself on for ten years.

She could not give that one. She knew it, and knew that knowing it made her a bad trader, and gave it up anyway to the category of things a person is allowed to keep.

"The ordinary days," she said. "Breakfast. Errands. The sound of her in the next room. The—her humming. All of it. Every day that wasn't special."

**Those are most of them.**

"Yes."

**You will remember that you loved her and not why.**

Ines's eyes stung. "Yes. That's the shape of it."

The wight was quiet for a long moment. Water sounds moved through the walls, patient, tidal.

**Why?** it asked, and it was not a merchant's question. It sounded almost—gentle. Almost sorry. **You could run instead. Overland. Some of you would live.**

"Some," Ines agreed. "And Renn would take the rest, and the ledgers would burn, and everything the guild ever was would end in a fire pit outside Vell." She lifted her chin. "This way, everyone goes. This way it *continues*. My mother spent her whole life keeping accounts for people who couldn't keep their own. She'd have called this a fair trade. She'd have—" the catch again, worse this time, "—she'd have been proud I drove a hard bargain. Keeping the load-bearing ones."

The wight regarded her. Or the idea of regarding.

**Freely given?**

"Freely given."

**Then hold still, little ledger.**

It reached toward her—or the water in the air reached—and Ines closed her eyes and thought, absurdly, *I should have written a receipt.*

Then her mother's humming was gone.

Not faded. Gone, cleanly, the way a tooth is gone: a shape left behind, aching at the edges, complete in its absence. Ines stood in the salt cellar holding a lantern and knew, with total certainty, that she had loved her mother more than anything in the world, and could not for the life of her remember the woman's voice. She remembered that the braid had been bad. She could not remember the hands that made it. She remembered the words *you matter* as if reading them off a page in her own archive—true, recorded, and no longer hers.

She felt smaller. That was the honest truth of it, the thing she would write later if she dared: not noble, not cleansed, just *smaller*, a book with pages torn out that still had to stand upright and be a book.

**It is done,** said the wight, and there was something in its voice now that hadn't been there before—something that might, in a larger creature, have been respect. **Passage for eleven, over or through, at the hour of your calling. Bring them beyond the harbor wall. The Door will find you.**

"And the debt?" Ines managed. Her voice sounded like someone else's.

**Paid.** The wight began to sink back into the flooded dark, then paused. **Keeper. The ones you kept—the load-bearing ones—I left those untouched. Not mercy. Interest.**

"What does that mean?"

**You will want them later. Debts of mine come due again, someday. Good evening, bearer.**

And the cellar was only a cellar, dripping and cold, and Ines was alone with a lantern and the enormous quiet where a voice used to be.

* * *

Tomas found her on the stairs. He had a way of appearing in doorways that suggested he'd been standing there deciding whether to exist, and tonight he looked like he'd decided against it several times before committing.

"You're pale," he said. "Even for you."

"It's done. We cross beyond the wall tomorrow night, at slack tide. Everyone goes." She started up past him, because moving was easier than standing.

"Ines." He fell into step beside her. "What did it take?"

"Memories of my mother."

He stopped walking. She kept going, so he had to catch up, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into that careful register he used for maps of places that shouldn't exist. "All of them?"

"All the ordinary ones. The good ones. The—" She waved a hand vaguely at her own chest. "The texture of her. I know I loved her. I can't remember loving her. There's a difference, apparently, and it turns out the difference is everything."

Tomas was silent for half a flight of stairs. Then, quietly: "My father used to sing while he drew coastlines. Badly. Off-key, always flat." He wasn't looking at her; he was looking at some middle distance where maps lived. "I haven't thought about that in years. I could have sworn I'd forgotten it. But there it is."

Ines stopped. Turned. Looked at him.

"Tomas Vey," she said slowly, "are you telling me a memory?"

"I'm telling you a fact about coastlines."

"You're lending me a memory. Of someone singing off-key. Because mine got eaten by a tide-wight twenty minutes ago and you noticed."

"I noticed nothing," Tomas said, with dignity, walking on. "Cartographers observe. It's different."

She followed him up into the dark of the guildhall, where eleven people were packing eleven lifetimes into satchels, and somewhere below the harbor the water was already rearranging itself to make room for them. It should have felt like victory. It felt like a receipt.

And it was only later—much later, when the lanterns were out—that Ines realized the wight had said *interest*, and that interest, in the language of her guild, was not a gift.

It was a hook.
