## 7. Renn's Offer

The summons came by way of a boy who smelled of tallow and fear, which in Vell meant he worked for the crown.

"You're wanted at the customs house," the boy said, not meeting Tomas's eyes. "Captain Renn. Tonight. He says—" the boy swallowed, "—he says to bring your instruments. He's heard you're good with maps."

Tomas looked at the boy for a long moment. *Heard from whom?* was the obvious question, but asking it would only tell the captain something about how Tomas spent his evenings, and who he spent them with. So instead he said, "Tell him I'll come," and gave the boy a coin, because a frightened child deserved at least that much kindness in a city gone mad.

* * *

The customs house had been built in the years when Vell believed itself important, all pale stone and tall windows meant to impress foreign merchants who no longer came. Now it was half-empty, its grand halls lit by a scattering of lamps, and Captain Aldous Renn had chosen the smallest room in it for his interview—a clerk's closet of a place, barely large enough for a table, two chairs, and a man like Renn, who somehow filled any space he stood in without seeming to try.

He was pouring wine when Tomas entered. Two cups. Of course there were two cups.

"Master Vey." Renn smiled the way other men shook hands—warmly, deliberately, as though warmth were a tool he kept oiled. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please. The wine is from Sarn."

Tomas stopped halfway to the chair.

"Ah," Renn said softly. "You noticed. Yes. There is still some left, if one knows where to look. The country is erased, Master Vey, not its cellars." He held out a cup. "I find that drinking it is a kind of... remembrance. Would you deny me that?"

Tomas took the cup. It would have been rude not to, and rudeness was a weapon he couldn't afford tonight. The wine was dark and tasted faintly of salt and plums, and it was very good, and he hated himself a little for enjoying it.

"You know why you're here," Renn said, settling into his own chair with the ease of a man who had never once been uncomfortable in his life.

"I imagine it's about maps," Tomas said. "It usually is."

"It is always about maps with you, isn't it?" Renn laughed—a real laugh, or such a good imitation that the difference didn't matter. "They took your credentials, did they not? Struck you from the Royal Cartographers' rolls. All because you drew a street that the crown had decided did not exist. What was it called?"

"Cooper's Row."

"Cooper's Row." Renn turned the name over like a coin he was testing for weight. "And the remarkable thing—the thing that got you disgraced rather than merely corrected—is that your street was *there*. Anyone could have walked it. You simply drew what was true, and the truth was inconvenient." He leaned forward. "That is a rare quality in a mapmaker, Master Vey. Most of them draw what they're told. You draw what *is*."

"And that's a crime now."

"That's a *gift*," Renn said, "and gifts are what we're here to discuss." He reached into his coat and set something on the table between them: a compass, brass-cased, its needle swinging lazily before settling—not north, but toward the harbor. Toward the guild quarter. Tomas kept his face still and thanked every god whose name he'd ever pretended to remember that he'd left nothing on his person tonight but chalk and a stub of pencil.

"Do you know what this points at?" Renn asked.

"Badly made magnets," Tomas said.

"Gifts," Renn repeated, unbothered. "People who can do what shouldn't be possible. It points at them the way lesser compasses point at iron. And do you know what it has been doing for three days, whenever I stand near the customs house?" He tapped the glass. "It swings toward *you*. Not strongly. Not the way it swings for the girl who keeps the ledgers—that one nearly drags my arm off. But steadily. Persistently. Like a dog that has decided to follow someone home." He smiled again. "You can do something impossible, Master Vey. Something quiet. I'd very much like to know what."

Tomas drank his wine and said nothing, which was itself becoming an answer, and they both knew it.

* * *

Here is what Tomas knew about Captain Aldous Renn, gathered from three weeks of listening in dockside taverns and reading the way hunters moved through the city like weather:

He was patient. That was the worst of him. Other witch-hunters kicked in doors; Renn knocked first, and apologized when he arrested people, and remembered their children's names. He had taken eleven gifted persons so far, and not one of them had been dragged—they had walked, most of them, looking confused, as though they'd been talked into it. As though they'd agreed.

He was also, by every account, honest. He did not lie to the people he hunted. He considered lying beneath his office. This made him more dangerous than any liar, because it meant that whatever he offered next would be real.

"Let me tell you a story," Renn said, refilling both cups without asking. "A story about a treaty. Very old. Signed by a king whose name has been struck from the rolls—which, you'll appreciate, given your history with the rolls. In that treaty, our crown traded away something enormous. A whole country, Master Vey. Its land, its people, its *memory*. Do you know what it received in exchange?"

"No," Tomas said carefully.

"A door," Renn said. "One door. A passage out of anything. Out of a siege, out of a plague, out of a country being unwritten. The crown's ancestors thought themselves very clever. They kept the key in a guild of tradesmen and forgot about it for two hundred years." His voice cooled by a single degree. "And then the crown needed it. There are exiles, Master Vey. People the present regime has decided no longer exist—yes, we do it too, we are not hypocrites, merely consistent—and those people need a way *out*, and the only way out is behind a door that answers to a sigil currently sitting in the pocket of a guild clerk who is, at this moment, hiding in a cellar with the last of her dying order."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, water lapped against the customs house pilings.

"So you see," Renn went on, gently, "this hunt was never about burning anyone. I have burned no one. I intend to burn no one. I am recovering crown property. The girl has something that belongs to the kingdom, and she is going to get people killed over it—people like the ferryman's son we took yesterday, who knew nothing except where she slept. People like your friend Odrik, the ink-seller, who vouched for you to the wrong person at the wrong time."

Tomas's cup stopped halfway to his mouth. "Odrik has done nothing."

"No," Renn agreed, with terrible mildness. "He hasn't. That is rarely relevant. But here is what *is* relevant." He produced a folded paper and laid it beside the compass. A warrant of clemency, sealed in red wax, bearing a name Tomas hadn't seen written down in six years: *Tomas Vey, restored to the Royal Cartographers, full rank and honors.*

"Full restoration," Renn said. "Your name back on the rolls. Your pension reinstated. Your street—Cooper's Row—officially redrawn into existence, with your signature on it, forever. All you must do is bring me the sigil. Or tell me where the girl keeps it. Or even just—" he spread his hands, magnanimous, "—stop standing between me and her. Walk away. Three gradations of the same offer, each more forgivable than the last. I am not a monster, Master Vey. I am offering you the chance to be innocent again."

* * *

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Tomas would carry afterward like a stone in his boot: for one whole breath, he wanted it.

Not the honors. Not the pension. He wanted the *name*. To walk through Vell and hear "cartographer" spoken of him without the sour aftertaste of disgrace. To have Cooper's Row exist again because he had insisted it existed, publicly, irreversibly. To stop being a man who lived in the gaps of official documents. He had spent six years learning to erase himself, and Renn was offering, in red wax, the opposite of erasure.

And underneath that—quieter, worse—was the arithmetic. If he gave up the sigil, Ines would lose the Door, yes, but she would live. She would be angry, magnificent, alive. Whereas if he kept refusing, the compass would keep swinging, the net would keep tightening, and one morning he would watch them take her the way they'd taken the ferryman's son, and his clean hands would not be clean, they would simply be empty.

Renn watched him think it, because Renn missed nothing, and something almost kind moved across his face.

"There it is," the captain murmured. "I've seen that look before. It's not shame, Master Vey. Don't mistake it for shame. It's relief. Someone is finally offering you a way to put the world down."

"The world isn't heavy," Tomas said.

"No?"

"Not the parts worth carrying."

Renn studied him for a long moment, and then, infuriatingly, laughed again—that warm, genuine-seeming laugh. "Oh, you're loyal. How rare. How *expensive*." He rose, collected the warrant, and—instead of tearing it up, which would have ended things neatly—folded it and slid it across the table anyway. "Keep it. Think it over. I'm a patient man; patience is the whole of my method. You have until the ice closes the harbor. After that, options narrow for everyone." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and added, without turning around: "For what it's worth, I believe she'll give you the sigil herself if you ask. Keepers always trade. It's the only language they speak. Good night, Master Vey."

* * *

Tomas walked home the long way, along the harbor wall, because the long way let him check whether he was followed (he wasn't; Renn didn't need to follow anyone, he had a compass) and because the cold helped.

The warrant sat in his pocket like a coal. Twice he took it out intending to drop it in the black water, and twice he didn't, and he examined himself honestly about why: because a forged map had once saved his life, and a piece of paper with a seal on it might someday save hers. Even the enemy's documents were tools, if you were the sort of man who could redraw what they said. He was that sort of man. It was, after all, his gift.

But the doubt Renn had planted—Renn, who did not lie—kept growing as he walked. *She'll give it to you herself. Keepers always trade.* Was that true? Had he seen her look at the sigil the way she looked at bread she couldn't afford? No. He had seen her hold it like a debt she intended to pay personally. But Renn didn't lie, and yet Renn had also never met Ines Underhill, youngest keeper of the Ledgerwrights, who had once recorded a child's memory-bargain under her own name just so the child wouldn't carry it alone. Some things could not be traded because they were already spent.

By the time he reached the salt cellar door and gave the knock—three short, one long—he had made his decision, and it surprised him by how little it felt like a decision. It felt like a fact about himself he'd only just read clearly, the way you suddenly notice a street has been there all along.

Ines opened the door, lamplight behind her, worry already on her face. "You're late. What did he want?"

"He offered me everything," Tomas said, stepping inside, out of the wind. "My name back. My work back. My whole life, unforgiven and refunded."

Her eyes searched his. "And?"

"And I need paper," he said. "Good paper, and your steadiest ink. Because I'm going to accept his offer—in writing, with a map attached. A map of exactly where the sigil will be tomorrow night." He managed something that was almost a smile. "The trouble with a man who never lies, Ines, is that he believes whatever you show him. And I can draw him anything."

She stared at him. "Tomorrow night. Tomas—tomorrow night is when we vote on the Door."

"Yes," he said, and the wind moaned along the harbor wall behind him, and somewhere out on the black water, the first thin skin of ice was already forming. "Which means we have until then to decide what I'm really going to draw."
