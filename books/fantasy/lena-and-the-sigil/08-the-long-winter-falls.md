## 8. The Long Winter Falls

The first sign was the gulls.

Ines noticed it on her morning walk to the fish market—she still went, though there was little left to buy and less coin to buy it with, because a keeper who stopped walking her rounds was a keeper who had already surrendered. The gulls hung over the harbor in a silent, bewildered cloud, refusing to land. Below them, where the water should have slapped black and restless against the pilings, the sea lay still as poured pewter.

By noon, it was ice.

Not the honest ice of a hard winter, which comes ragged and gray at the edges and creaks when you trust it. This ice had come all at once, smooth and white as fresh paper, spreading from the drowned quarter outward like ink bleeding through cloth in reverse. Children were already sliding on it near the Fish Gate, shrieking with delight, and Ines wanted to shout at them to get off, get off, don't you understand what's been taken from you—but of course they didn't. To them it was simply an early miracle. A gift.

She knew better than anyone alive what gifts cost.

"The tide-wight's route is closed," she said that evening, standing before the remnant of the guild in the salt cellar. Nine faces looked back at her. Once there had been forty. Once there had been elders whose names filled whole pages of the ledger without effort. Now there were nine, and half of them were asleep sitting up, because everyone was so tired that tiredness had become a kind of shared language.

Old Maren, who had been the guild's brewer of forgetting-teas back when such things could be brewed openly, squinted up from her chair by the brazier. "Closed how? The wight gave safe passage. A bargain's a bargain."

"A bargain holds until someone pays to break it." Ines spread the map Tomas had drawn—the real one, the one that showed Vell as it actually was, harbor wall and drowned quarter and all—and set a candlestub at the edge of the frozen water. "Renn has bought the winter itself. Or commanded it. I don't know which is worse. Either way, no one crosses the harbor now except over his ice."

Silence, except for the brazier's pop and the distant sound of the children still playing on their new miracle.

Brannic, the youngest of them besides herself, a lockboy with hands scarred from picking locks he'd been too honest to keep picking, said what they were all thinking: "Then we're sealed in with him."

"Not quite." Ines turned to the oldest ledger, the one bound in eel-skin that no one but a keeper could open without weeping. She had spent three nights with it since learning what the guild had done to Sarn, and she had not slept so much as dozed against its cover like a penitent against a door. "I've been reading the treaty entries. All of them. And there's something I got wrong."

Maren's eyebrows climbed. Ines was not in the habit of admitting error; keeping a ledger meant never writing anything down twice, which meant never being wrong out loud.

"The Hollow Door doesn't only answer to the sigil," Ines said. "It answers to the sigil *held*. Someone must stand inside the threshold and give their name to it—not once, the way I'd give mine to open it, but continuously. Like holding a latch. The name is the hinge. The moment the holder lets go, the Door closes wherever it happens to be, with whatever's halfway through it halfway forever."

"How do you know?" Brannic asked.

"Because the last time it was opened, the entry is signed twice. Once by the broker who made the bargain. And once—" she touched the faded line, the ink gone brown as old blood, "—once by the keeper who stayed behind in Sarn while the country was erased around her. Her name isn't anywhere else in any ledger. She wasn't traded. She wasn't paid. She just... stayed. Until there was no Sarn left to stay in."

The brazier crackled. Somewhere above them, the guildhall's ancient beams settled with a groan, the way old houses do when they're listening.

"So we find another way," Maren said flatly. "There's always another way. That's the whole point of us."

"There isn't." Ines heard her own voice go quiet and steady and was surprised by it. "I've looked. The Door opens where Sarn used to be—that's fixed, written into the treaty itself, a debt the guild can never discharge, only service. When the harbor was water, the wight could carry us to the spot and we could open it from a boat and be through before Renn finished his morning courtesy. Now the harbor is Renn's. He didn't freeze it to be cruel. He froze it because he worked out the same thing I did: the Door must be reached, and he owns every road to it."

"Except one," said a voice from the stairwell.

Tomas came down into the cellar with snow melting in his dark hair and his coat steaming, carrying a roll of paper under his arm like a wounded bird. He'd been out since dawn, and Ines had spent those hours pretending she hadn't been counting them.

"Except one," she agreed.

He unrolled the paper on the trestle table, weighting the corners with candlestubs. It was the guildhall itself—or rather, the guildhall as it had been a hundred years ago, drawn in Tomas's impossible hand, every vanished corridor and bricked-up stair rendered in ink that seemed to shift when you weren't looking directly at it.

"The guildhall was built on the Sarn quarter's edge," he said. "Before the erasure, this street ran down to the water. There was a gate in the old foundation—a customs gate, for goods coming in from across the sea. Goods from Sarn." His finger traced a line that ended, on his map, in a small careful archway. "The Door doesn't need the harbor. It needs the place where Sarn touched Vell. And one piece of that place is underneath our feet. Under the wine racks. Under the salt bins. Under, I'd wager, the very floor Maren spilled her first batch of forgetting-tea on."

Maren made a noise that might have been indignation or might have been agreement.

"But?" Ines said, because Tomas's face had the particular set it got when the good news came with teeth.

"But the Door opens *outward* from here the same as anywhere—it will still want to be held. And Renn knows the guildhall is the last stone in Vell with any Sarn in it. He doesn't need his compass anymore, Ines. He needs only to follow the cold." Tomas met her eyes. "When we open it, he'll come. Not maybe. Certainly. With everything he has."

* * *

They voted at midnight, because the Ledgerwrights had always voted at midnight—it was in the founding charter, along with several other provisions Ines suspected had been added during a particularly good vintage. Nine votes. Eight to flee through the Door and scatter beyond the crown's reach, carrying the ledgers, carrying the names, carrying the guild's inheritance somewhere it could grow again.

And one vote—one thin, stubborn, sleepless vote—to stay.

Ines raised her hand before anyone else could speak, which was cowardice of a kind, she knew. If she'd waited, Maren would have argued. Brannic would have volunteered, because Brannic was nineteen and immortal the way only nineteen-year-olds are. Someone would have offered, and she would have had to watch them offer, and she would have had to accept or refuse, and either would have broken something in her that she needed whole.

So she raised her hand first, alone, in the lamplight, and said: "It has to be me."

"No," said Maren, immediately, predictably.

"It has to be me, Maren. Listen. The holder gives a true name freely given—I'm the only one of us who's practiced giving mine. I've signed it under other people's bargains since I was twelve years old. I know its weight. Brannic would faint. You'd fall asleep, no offense, you fell asleep during the vote."

"I did not," said Maren, with dignity, her chin dipping toward her chest.

"And—" Ines made herself say the rest, made herself look at each of them, because a bargain spoken to the floor was no bargain at all. "And I'm the keeper. The ledgers are mine now. Elder Oskar put the sigil in my hand and then conveniently died before he could explain it, which I've decided to interpret as trust rather than cruelty. The inheritance is mine to carry. That's what inheritance *means*. It isn't the pretty things you're given. It's the bill that arrives later, with your family's name on it, and somebody in every generation has to pay."

"You could pay and come through after," Brannic said. "Hold it open, let us pass, then run through yourself."

"The Door closes when the holder lets go. Not before. Not after. If I let go, it shuts, and if I'm still on this side when it shuts—" She shrugged, and found the shrug easier than expected. "Besides. Someone has to seal it behind you. Properly. So Renn can't follow, and so the crown can never use it. That takes a keeper on the near side, working the sigil from outside while the last of you are through. Two jobs. One doorway. Do the arithmetic."

Nobody did the arithmetic. Nobody spoke at all, which in the salt cellar of the Ledgerwrights' guild was its own kind of tally.

It was Tomas who broke it. He had been standing apart, arms folded, looking at his map of the guildhall as though the answers were hidden in the linework, and when he finally turned around his face was doing something complicated that Ines chose, for her own survival, not to read too closely.

"When," he said. Not *if*. Not *should she*. Just *when*, as if the only question left was scheduling.

"Tomas—" she began.

"Three days," he said briskly, in the tone of a man laying out provisions for a journey. "Three days to cut through the old foundation, clear the customs gate, and move the wine racks—Maren, I'm sorry, your tea cellar is about to become a national monument. Three days for me to draw the anchoring map, because the Door won't hold its shape without one and I'm the only cartographer in Vell mad enough to make it. Three days for everyone to decide what they can carry and what they have to leave." He rolled up the map with a snap. "And three days for me to argue with you privately, Ines Underhill, which I intend to do at length and lose gracefully, so let's not waste the others' time pretending otherwise."

* * *

He walked her up from the cellar at dawn, past the sleeping guild, to the high window in the ledger room where she did her best thinking. Outside, the harbor gleamed white and perfect, and the children were out on the ice again despite everything, their laughter thin and bright across the frozen water.

"He'll take the city first," Ines said. "Before he comes for us. Renn is patient. He'll want us to hear it happening."

"Yes."

"And when he comes, it'll be fast."

"Yes." Tomas leaned against the window frame, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, which was as close as either of them ever admitted to wanting. "Ines. There's still the wight. Frozen harbor or no, tide-wights have broken thicker ice than this for lesser debts. If I went back to the shore, offered it something—"

"It would cost more than I have left, and you know it." She smiled, small and tired. "I already paid it my mother. I'm not spending you too."

Something moved through his face then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone before she could name it.

"Three days," he said instead, gruffly, and left her alone with the ledgers.

Ines sat down at her desk and pulled the oldest book toward her, the eel-skin one, and opened it to the page with the keeper who had stayed in Sarn. She read her predecessor's name again—Sefa Underhill, some cousin of cousins, four generations back—and felt, for the first time, not dread but a strange, settling calm, like a scale finding its balance.

Below Sefa's name, at the bottom of the page, there was room for exactly one more line.

Ines dipped her pen.

Outside, the ice sang—a long, groaning note, the sound of a harbor becoming a road, and somewhere far off across all that whiteness, she imagined she could hear boots beginning to march.
