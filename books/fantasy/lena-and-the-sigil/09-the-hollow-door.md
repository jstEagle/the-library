## 9. The Hollow Door

**Ines**

The sigil had never been heavy before. Ines had carried it in her pocket for weeks like a coin she was afraid to spend, and it had weighed nothing at all. Now, standing in the guildhall with smoke already threading through the floorboards and Captain Aldous Renn's boots audible on the cobbles outside—patient boots, courteous boots, the boots of a man who had never once needed to run—it weighed as much as a country.

Which was, she supposed, exactly what it was.

"Say it plainly," said Maren, the last elder still standing, her voice cracked from the vote they'd taken an hour ago. "The Door opens to a true name, freely given. There's no trick of phrasing. No loophole. We looked for one for three hundred years."

"I know," Ines said. "I read the ledger."

"Then you understand what you're about to do."

She understood more than that. She understood that her mother's face had gone soft and unfixed in her memory since the salt cellar, since the tide-wight had drunk those memories down like broth, and that what remained was a shape of love without a face inside it. She understood that she was about to hand over the rest.

What she did not say—and would not, because saying it aloud might make it a bargain rather than a gift, and the Door knew the difference the way a dog knows fear—was that she had been practicing this moment her whole life. Every entry she'd ever recorded under another keeper's name. Every bargain she'd witnessed but never been permitted to make. *I have spent twenty-two years being the person who writes things down,* she thought. *Tonight I am finally the thing written.*

Outside, Renn knocked.

Not battered the door. Not demanded. Knocked—the way a neighbor knocks when borrowing sugar. That was somehow worse than anything.

"Ines Underhill," came his voice through the wood, warm as a hearth. "I've come about the ledger."

Tomas appeared beside her, ink-stained to the elbow, his impossible map rolled under one arm like a dueling sword. He looked, she thought, terrible. He looked wonderful.

"You could still go through first," he said. "Hold the door open from the other side. I'll anchor it here."

"And leave you holding a burning building and a compass that points at you?"

"The compass points at gifts," Tomas said. "And I've never given anyone anything in my life. I'm famously selfish. Ask anyone."

"I'm asking you," Ines said, "and you're offering to die in my place, which is the least selfish thing I've ever heard, so your reputation is in tatters."

He almost smiled. The hall shuddered as something struck the outer door—not Renn's knock this time, but the ram of his men, done with courtesy now that courtesy had failed its purpose.

* * *

**Tomas**

The map was wrong. That was the whole point of it.

Every map Tomas Vey had ever drawn for the crown had been right, obediently right, right the way a dog is obedient, and it had nearly killed him. This map—the one he'd drawn over three sleepless nights in the guildhall's back room, while Ines slept sitting up against the ledger shelf—was wrong in every particular. The streets bent where streets should straighten. The harbor wall stood where the drowned quarter used to be, and also where it *still* was, if you knew how to look. It was a map of Vell as Sarn remembered it, and Sarn, being erased, remembered everything.

Anchoring the Hollow Door meant pinning it open with a place that didn't exist. The map was the only such place left.

The outer door split down the middle. Torchlight poured through the crack like spilled honey, and with it came the smell of pitch—they'd brought fire, then. Of course they had. You couldn't burn a guild that traded in impossible things, but you could certainly burn the building it kept them in.

"Hold the line!" Maren shouted, though there was no line, only forty frightened people pressed against the far wall, the ones who hadn't made it out in the earlier passages: a lampwright who glowed faintly in the dark, twin sisters who shared one shadow between them, an old man whose whistling made bread rise. The remnant. Ines's people now, whether she'd asked for them or not.

Renn stepped through the broken door himself, ahead of his men, brushing splinters from his coat with two fingers. Up close he was exactly what he'd always seemed: pleasant, tidy, a man you'd trust with your luggage. His compass hung at his belt, and its needle swung once toward the lampwright, once toward the twins, and then—slowly, hungrily—toward Ines.

"There she is," Renn said, and his smile was genuinely kind, which was the most frightening thing in the room. "Keeper Underhill. I confess I hoped we'd meet like this. Civilized. Before the fire gets ambitious."

"You're burning your own evidence," Tomas said. "The ledgers. Everything the crown claims doesn't exist—you're about to prove it exists by destroying it."

"The crown," Renn said mildly, "is very good at not existing, Master Vey. You'd know. You drew half its blank spaces." He turned back to Ines, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Last offer. Give me the sigil. Walk out. Everyone in this room walks out. I give you my word."

"He gives excellent word," Tomas murmured. "It just means nothing. Very economical."

But Ines was looking at Renn with an expression Tomas had learned to dread—the look she got when she found a debt no one else had noticed. "Your exiles," she said quietly. "That's what this is about. People the crown sent away, and now wants back, and the only door home answers to us."

Something flickered behind Renn's pleasantness. "You've been reading."

"It's rather my whole profession."

"Then you know I'm not the villain of this story, Keeper. I'm just the man who remembers what everyone else was paid to forget."

"Yes," Ines said. "That's what makes it so sad."

And she opened the sigil.

* * *

**Ines**

It was smaller than she expected, the Hollow Door. A plain archway in the guildhall's back wall, behind the shelf of ledgers nobody had dusted in a century, and when the sigil woke in her palm it lit the arch from within like a lantern behind paper.

The price came due immediately. She felt it as a question, patient and enormous, older than the guild, older than the treaty: *Who gives?*

"Ines Underhill," she said.

Her full name. Her true name, the one her mother had chosen and no clerk had ever recorded, because keepers' names were guild property and guild property went unspoken. Saying it felt like taking off a coat she'd worn so long she'd forgotten she was wearing it. Cold air. Strange lightness. Somewhere beneath the floorboards, the tide-wight stirred in its drowned quarter and turned its attention toward her like a lighthouse turning, and she thought—absurdly, warmly—*hello again. You got the memories. These are the rest of me.*

The Door began to open.

"NO—" Renn moved fast for a courteous man. His men surged forward. And Tomas, bless him, bless his ink-stained traitor hands, unrolled the impossible map across the guildhall floor, and the floor agreed to be what the map said it was.

The hall became the drowned quarter. Salt water stood ankle-deep where floorboards had been, and did not wet them. The arch of the Hollow Door rose out of it, stone slick with old light, and beyond the arch—not darkness, not void, but *Sarn*: fields that had been unwritten, a coastline that had been a rumor, air that smelled of rain on stone that had never stopped believing itself real.

"Through!" Ines screamed. "Now, now, NOW!"

They went. The lampwright first, glowing like a candle in fog; the twins, their single shadow stretched across two bodies and finally, joyfully, splitting in two as they crossed; the old man whistling, and somewhere in Sarn, unseen, a hundred loaves must have risen at once. Maren went last of the elders, pausing at the threshold to look back at Ines with an expression that contained, compressed into one glance, three hundred years of guild guilt and one small, fierce blessing.

The fire reached the ledger shelves. Centuries of bargains went up in a roar of heat, names curling off pages like sparks returning to the sky, and Ines watched it burn and felt—strangely—unburdened. Let the trades burn. They had all been debts. What she was building next would be a different kind of book.

Renn fought his way through the smoke to the threshold, and there he stopped, because the Door stopped him—not violently, but the way deep water stops a man who cannot swim. He stood at the edge of Sarn with his coat smoking and his compass spinning uselessly, and he reached across the threshold for Ines with both hands.

"Come *through*," he said, and for the first time his voice cracked, and she saw the exile underneath the captain, the child put on a ship, the man who had spent his whole life trying to walk home by force. "You don't belong to this country either. None of us do anymore. Come *through*, Keeper, and I swear on whatever's left—"

"No," Ines said gently.

And the Door refused him.

It was not dramatic. That was the astonishing part. His hands simply closed on nothing, the way hands close on rain. The threshold passed through his fingers like water through a net, because the Door was built on freely given things—it had been built on a treaty, after all, however wicked, and treaties are promises, and promises are gifts—and Renn wanted, oh, how he wanted, but wanting is not giving, and taking is the opposite of both.

The Door knows a freely given thing when it sees one. Ines had given hers. Renn had only ever tried to seize.

He sank to his knees at the threshold of the country that had thrown him away, and Ines—who had every reason to hate him, and found, examining herself honestly, that she mostly pitied him—crouched opposite him through the arch, close enough to touch, separated by everything.

"When the winter ends," she told him, "come find us. Not with soldiers. With your name in your mouth instead of your orders. We're starting a new ledger. It takes names back. It doesn't take them away."

"You're mad," Renn whispered.

"Almost certainly," said Ines. "It runs in the guild."

* * *

**Tomas**

The last thing through the Door was the map.

Tomas had planned it that way—had planned everything, because planning was the only prayer he knew—but plans have a way of meeting Ines Underhill and coming out changed. She was supposed to step through second-to-last. Instead she stood in the burning hall, holding the sigil, waiting for him with the patience of someone closing up shop after a very long day.

"Anchor's set," he shouted over the roar. "The Door holds until the map burns! Come *on*!"

"You go first."

"I am not leaving you in a burning building to hold a door alone—"

"Tomas." She smiled at him through the smoke, and it was the smile she wore when she'd found the true shape of a bargain, the one underneath the asking price. "Someone has to stay to seal it. If it closes empty, it closes forever, and Renn's exiles can never come home. If it closes from this side, held by the one who paid for it—" she lifted the sigil, glowing like a coal, "—it stays findable. Sealed, but findable. For whoever comes looking with clean hands."

"That's not in any ledger."

"I know," Ines said. "I'm writing it now."

He crossed the burning hall to her—salt water and fire together, an impossibility he intended to map later, if there was a later—and took her hands, sigil and all.

"Come with me," he said. It came out rawer than he meant it to. Twenty years of erasing himself, and this woman had redrawn him in a season. "There has to be another way. There's always another way. You taught me that."

"There is," Ines said softly. "This is it."

She kissed him—briefly, fiercely, a signature on a page—and pushed him gently backward through the arch, into Sarn, into the rain-on-stone smell of a country remembering itself.

From the far side, Tomas Vey watched the Hollow Door begin to close around the last keeper of the Ledgerwrights, standing straight in the heart of the fire with her name in her mouth and her chin up, growing smaller and brighter, like a star deciding where it belonged—

—and then, just before the stone met stone, the light stuttered.

Flickered.

And Ines stumbled *out* of the closing arch, coughing, singed, clutching the sigil to her chest, into the arms of a disgraced cartographer who very nearly dropped her in a tidal pool.

"What," Tomas said, when he could speak, "did you *do*?"

Ines grinned at him through the smoke, wild-eyed and alive, and held up the sigil—which was no longer a sigil at all, but a blank page, humming faintly, waiting to be written on.

"I told you," she said. "I'm writing it now."
