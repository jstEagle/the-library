## 10. Ledgerwright

The morning after the Door sealed, the guildhall smelled of smoke and salt and, improbably, of bread.

Ines stood in the ruined scriptorium with a broom she had found under a collapsed shelf and swept ash into a pile that immediately refused to stay a pile. The fire had taken the east wall, half the roof, and every ledger but one—the oldest one, the treaty ledger, which had been in her satchel when Renn's men came through the door. She supposed there was a lesson in that. The guild had spent three hundred years guarding its treasures behind locks and wards and ceremonial doors, and the only book to survive had done so because a frightened girl had shoved it into a bag.

She was still contemplating this when Tomas appeared in the doorway carrying two cups of something steaming and a loaf of bread balanced on his forearm like a serving tray at a very informal inn.

"Brindlemarrow baked," he said. "He says grief is no excuse for bad crust."

"Brindlemarrow," Ines said, "is ninety-one years old and has never baked anything in his life."

"He learned yesterday. He says it's never too late to acquire an impossible skill." Tomas handed her a cup. The drink inside was hot, sweet, and tasted faintly of the sea, which she chose not to ask about. "Also, the tide-wight is in the cellar again. It ate the last of the pickled herring and left a pearl on the barrel as payment. Brindlemarrow is furious about the herring and delighted about the pearl, in roughly equal measure."

"That's the most Vell thing I've ever heard," Ines said, and drank her strange tea, and felt—for the first time in longer than she could name—like a person rather than a function.

* * *

The country was learning to remember Sarn.

It happened slowly, the way ice goes out of a harbor: not all at once but in loosening pieces, groaning, reluctant, then suddenly all at once. The drowned quarter beyond the harbor wall began to dry. Fishermen reported streets under the shallows where streets should not be, and then—this was the remarkable part—reported them without crossing themselves or going quiet or looking over their shoulders for hunters. A woman who sold mussels on the Fish Gate steps stopped mid-sentence one morning, frowned, and said, "My grandmother came from Sarn. Why did I never say that out loud before?" And then she had gone back to selling mussels, because grief that arrives thirty years late still wants its breakfast.

The treaty was unwinding. Ines did not fully understand how—Tomas theorized that a freely given name, refused by the Door, had torn something loose in the bargain's architecture; Brindlemarrow theorized that treaties, like debts, expired when everyone who remembered signing them was dead or gone—but she understood the shape of it. The country had been holding its breath for a generation. Now it exhaled, and what came out were names.

They came to the guildhall. Not many at first—a trickle, then more. People who had lost things to the erasure: a surname, a street, a sister whose face they could describe but not place. They stood in the burned doorway, hat in hand, embarrassed, the way people are when asking for help, and said things like *I don't know if this is the sort of thing you do anymore.*

"It's exactly the sort of thing we do now," Ines told them. "It's the only sort of thing we do."

Because here was what she had decided, sitting in the ashes with the treaty ledger open on her knees while the refugees' boat passed beyond reach: the old guild had traded in impossible things, and every trade had a price, and the prices had accumulated like silt until the whole institution had drowned in them. Debts owed to crowns. Memories paid to wights. Names surrendered to sigils. She had spent her childhood recording bargains she was never allowed to make. She would spend the rest of it doing the opposite.

The new ledger would record nothing bought or sold. Only names restored, freely, to whoever had lost them—and each entry signed by the keeper who made it, so that the guild's inheritance would be carried forward as responsibility, not fled from as guilt.

She had the book bound by a binder off Tanner's Row who asked no questions, which in Vell was the highest form of craftsmanship. She ruled the pages herself. On the cover, where the old ledgers had borne the guild's sigil—a door within a circle—she had him stamp a simple open book instead.

"What do we call it?" Tomas asked, watching her rule the first page. "The guild, I mean. We can't keep calling it the Ledgerwrights. Half the city thinks we're tax collectors, and the other half thinks we're ghosts."

"Let them think both," Ines said. "It keeps the queue short." But she turned the thought over that night, and the next, and on the third morning she inked the answer into the front matter in her careful, upright hand:

*Ledgerwrights. Second Founding. Keeper: Ines Underhill.*

Her own name. Written by her own hand, in a book she owned, in a guild she had chosen—not inherited, not fallen into, not smuggled herself into through a child's desperate forgery. Chosen. It took her three tries to write it without smudging, because her eyes had developed opinions on the matter.

* * *

Winter broke in the second week of Thawmonth, which everyone agreed was suspiciously punctual, as if the season itself had checked the calendar and decided to behave.

The hall was rebuilt slowly and badly and with enormous joy. Brindlemarrow supervised from a chair he refused to leave, directing carpenters with a cane and commentary ("Left! Your *other* left, boy, were you raised in a barn—" "I was raised in Sarn, actually," the boy said, and the whole roof went quiet, and then Brindlemarrow said, "Well. Then you know better than most how a barn ought to stand," and they got on with it). The tide-wight took up semi-permanent residence in the repaired cellar, paying for its lodgings in pearls and unsolicited advice about structural drainage, and Ines learned to knock before entering her own cellar, which she considered a small price for the company.

Tomas drew maps.

Not the crown's maps, not maps of what officially existed. He sat by the new south window with its view of the drying flats and drew Sarn as it returned: street by street, well by well, cross-checked against the memories people brought them. His hands shook sometimes, when he worked too long—he had never quite forgiven himself for the friend Renn's compass had found, a fisherman named Osk, whose name was the third entry in the new ledger, restored to his family with an apology Ines had written and rewritten eleven times—but the shaking stopped when the lines came out true.

"Renn's gone west," Tomas said one evening, not looking up from a drawing of a market square that had been underwater since before either of them was born. "Osk's brother saw him in Corrow Ford. Still smiling. Still asking questions. But the compass doesn't point at anything here anymore."

"Because there's nothing hidden left to find?"

"Because everything's being unhidden faster than he can hunt it." Tomas set down his pen. "He'll be back someday. Men like him always come back. But he'll find a city that remembers, and a guild with its books in order, and—" he glanced at her, and the lamplight caught the corner of his mouth, "—a tide-wight in the cellar with strong opinions about trespassers."

"We should put that on the sign," Ines said gravely. "*Beware of Wight.*"

"I'll letter it myself."

She laughed, and he laughed, and neither of them said the thing they had both decided not to say aloud somewhere around the burning of the hall, because some bargains are best kept as standing offers rather than signed contracts, and because they had time now—that was the whole point, the entire miraculous point, of everything they had built. There was time.

* * *

On the first warm evening of spring, Ines opened the new ledger to its first page and read the entries aloud, as she did every week now, so the names would live in ears as well as ink.

Osk, the fisherman. Maren Sallow, whose grandmother's village had a name again—Cinderlow, on the Sarnish coast, where the mussel-seller's family had come from. A street in Vell called Lanternmakers' Row, restored to the city's memory along with the forty families who had lit it. And at the bottom of the page, in the space she had ruled for the keeper's own account, the words she had written on the founding day:

*Ines Underhill. Restored to herself. Price: none. Freely given, freely kept.*

It was not, strictly speaking, a restoration—she had never lost her name, only been forbidden to spend it. But the ledger did not distinguish, and neither, she had decided, would she. Every name in the book had been carried through the dark by someone. Hers had been carried by a girl who recorded debts she couldn't pay, and that girl deserved her page same as anyone.

She closed the book and blew out the lamp and stood a moment in the dark of the scriptorium, listening. Below, Brindlemarrow was arguing with the tide-wight about whether cellars required windows (the wight held that they did, on aesthetic grounds). Somewhere in the rebuilt dormitory, the Sarnish boy was teaching the other apprentices a counting rhyme in a language the country was remembering syllable by syllable. Out past the harbor wall, where the flats lay bare and gleaming under the rising moon, the first stones of Cinderlow's new seawall were being laid by hands that remembered why walls mattered.

Ines Underhill, youngest keeper of the Ledgerwrights, first keeper of the Second Founding, rested her hand on the closed ledger and felt the weight of it—not the weight of debts, for once, but the weight of a promise kept.

Tomorrow there would be more names. Tomorrow someone would stand embarrassed in the doorway, hat in hand, and she would say *come in, sit down, tell me what was taken,* and she would write it down, and it would be enough.

Through the window, far out on the flats, something glimmered—briefly, at the edge of the tide-line, where the water was beginning to return. A light, perhaps. Or a lantern. Or, just possibly, a door.

Ines reached for her coat, and her pen, and the ledger.

Some habits, after all, were worth keeping.
