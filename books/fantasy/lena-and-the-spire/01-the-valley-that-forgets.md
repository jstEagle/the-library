## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley had a way of making a man forget on purpose.

Sebastian Okafor had learned this in his first winter, thirteen years ago, when he woke one morning and could not remember the color of lightning. He knew the word for it — he could say *lightning* as easily as any farmer says *rain* — but when he closed his eyes and reached for the memory of it, the white-hot branching of it, the smell of ozone like a struck bell, there was nothing there. Just a smooth blank place in his mind, polished by absence, like a stone worn featureless by a river that no longer ran.

He had sat up in his narrow bed with his heart hammering and thought: *it's working.*

That was the whole point of Hollowmere. The valley forgot so that other things could forget him too.

* * *

Thirteen years was a long time to mend roofs.

He was good at it now. He had been terrible at it once — a young man's hands, all thumbs and blisters, hands that had been made for holding storms the way other men held cups of tea. But the valley had sanded those hands down into something useful and plain. They knew shingles now, and gutter-iron, and the particular complaint of a beam that had taken too much snow. They knew how to pack clay into a chimney crack and how to coax a stubborn window back onto its track. The villagers called him Seb, because Okafor was too long for their mouths and too foreign for their comfort, and he answered to it the way a dog answers to any name said kindly enough.

"Seb! The Harrow place is leaking again."

"Seb, you'll look at the mill roof before the thaw?"

"Seb, my boy says you can tell weather from the way the swallows fly. Is that true?"

To the last one he always said no. It was not entirely a lie. Any man who has lived outdoors long enough learns to read swallows. What he did not say — what he had not said in thirteen years — was that he used to read the sky the way priests read scripture, fluently, hungrily, in a language with no alphabet at all. He used to know a storm three days before it knew itself. He used to stand on high ground with his arms open and his name spoken aloud into the wind, and the wind would come down and put its mouth against his ear and tell him everything.

Now the swallows were just birds, and the sky was just weather, and the wind had nothing to say to him.

This was peace. He told himself this every morning, standing at his window with the kettle going, watching mist lift off the fields. This is peace. This is what I wanted. This is the price paid and the bargain kept.

The telling never quite took. It was like pressing a flower between pages and finding, years later, that it had gone to dust. The shape remained; the living thing did not.

* * *

Hollowmere sat in the cupped palm of two ridgelines, east and west, with the river Slade running through its middle like a lazy silver stitch. There were forty-one households, a mill, an inn with a crooked sign depicting a boar that might have been a bear, and a chapel whose bell had cracked sometime before anyone currently living was born, so that it rang flat and mournful no matter the occasion. Weddings sounded like funerals. Funerals sounded like nothing much at all.

The villagers were good people in the way that people are good anywhere — imperfectly, mostly, with lapses. Marta Voss kept the inn and kept, unofficially, the village's opinions, sorting them like grain: what to keep, what to feed the pigs. Old Tam Harrow believed in ghosts and would tell you about them at length if you made the mistake of buying him a drink. The Aldous children were feral in summer and nearly civilized in winter. Nobody asked Sebastian where he came from, which was either remarkable courtesy or a collective agreement so complete it had become invisible, like the air.

He understood why. In his first year, when he still flinched at thunder, old Widow Cern had watched him duck beneath her doorframe during a spring squall and had said, mildly, "Storms don't like this valley. Never have. They come to the ridge and then they—" she'd made a vague gesture, fingers opening like a bird releasing, "—they lose interest."

*Lose interest.* As if the storm were a suitor and the valley a cold-hearted beloved. He had laughed, and the laugh had hurt his ribs, because it was truer than she knew. Magic could not follow him here. Whatever old covenant lay under the soil of Hollowmere — and something did lie under it; he could feel the silence of it the way a deaf man feels the silence of a room — it smoothed power flat. A storm-caller in Hollowmere was a musician in a soundproofed cell. His gift did not die; it simply could not hear him, and he could not hear it, and between them stretched a distance wider than the sea he'd crossed to get here.

So the hunters, when they came sniffing — and they had come, twice, in the early years, riders in gray with their faces wrapped — found nothing. No resonance. No signature. Just a big quiet foreigner who mended roofs and overpaid for ale and kept to himself. The first pair had looked through him twice before moving on. The second pair had stayed a night at Marta's inn and left at dawn, and Sebastian had spent that night sitting upright in bed with a knife across his knees, listening to the rain fall softly, indifferently, on the thatch above him, and feeling nothing answer in his blood.

After that, the fear had faded the way the memory of lightning had faded. Not all at once. By degrees. Until one day he realized he had gone a full season without checking the road for dust, and he had stood in his yard holding a hammer, and he had not known whether the feeling in his chest was relief or grief.

Probably both. It was usually both.

* * *

His cottage stood at the village's western edge, last house before the pasture fences gave up and the hillside began. He had chosen it deliberately — close enough to be neighborly, far enough to be forgotten. It had two rooms, a garden he was competent with, and a chimney that smoked in wet weather no matter what he did to it, which he privately considered fair penance. On the mantel he kept a single object: a river stone, gray and ordinary, that he had picked up his first week in the valley for no reason he could name except that his hand had needed to hold something.

He did not keep letters. He did not keep keepsakes. Everything from before — the sea crossing, the cities where he'd slept in doorways with his collar up, the mountains where he'd learned the deep forms of his art from a woman with frost-white braids, and before all of that, the house where his grandmother had sung songs in a language she forbade him to repeat — all of it lived in the blank country behind his eyes, walled off, unvisited. He had made a discipline of not remembering the way other men made a discipline of prayer. Every morning: do not remember. Every night: do not remember. And in between, when a smell or a song or the particular slant of autumn light threatened to open a door — do not remember, do not remember, do not remember.

It worked the way dams work. Perfectly, until it didn't.

* * *

On the morning it began, he was re-thatching the south slope of Widow Cern's roof, and the light was doing something wrong.

He noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar hymn — not immediately, but with a slow crawl of unease along the back of the neck. The light over the eastern ridge had gone the color of pewter, though the sun was still up and the sky elsewhere was a clean, stupid blue. He straightened on the ladder, straw dust drifting off his shoulders, and looked.

A cloud was building over the eastern ridge.

Not building — *arriving*. It came up from beyond the hills like a thing rising out of deep water, vast and blue-black at its base, boiling silently upward into towers. It had the organized, deliberate shape of a storm that knows exactly what it intends. And it was coming toward the valley.

Sebastian stood on the ladder with a fistful of straw and felt his heart begin, for the first time in years, to move.

Below him, Widow Cern's voice floated up, unhurried: "That's a strange one. We don't generally get weather off the east." A pause. "They lose interest, storms. Always have."

But this one was not losing interest. This one was leaning against the boundary of the valley the way a traveler leans against a locked door.

He climbed down without finishing his work. His hands, he observed distantly, were steady, and he hated them for it. Thirteen years of quiet and the moment the sky turned interesting, his body remembered its old trade as if it had been yesterday. Some animal part of him — the part that had once stood on the spine of the Karagat Mountains with his arms spread and his grandmother's name in his mouth — that part was rising toward the surface like a drowned man, hands outstretched.

*Call it,* whispered something in him. *You can't call it. You can't stop it. Either way you're useless, and either way it's coming.*

He walked home fast, past the mill, past the inn where Marta was carrying chairs inside with a squint at the sky, past children who were still playing because children trust the world until taught otherwise. The cloud had doubled while he walked. Its shadow reached the eastern fields now, and the light in the shadow was green-gray, submarine, the light of places where things drown.

In his cottage he shut the door and stood in the middle of the room and did the only ritual left to him.

*Do not remember.*

But the storm did not care what he remembered. The storm was not his. That was the horror of it, the newness of it — for thirteen years the valley's silence had been a wall around him, protective, suffocating, and he had told himself the wall worked both ways, that nothing of his old life could reach in. Now something vast and patient was pressing against the wall from outside, testing it, and he understood with a sick lurch that the wall had never been for his protection at all. It had been a cage with him inside it. And cages only work until something outside wants in badly enough.

Toward evening the first thunder came, and it was wrong.

Thunder in Hollowmere should have been impossible — the valley ate such things, swallowed sound and fury alike into its soft gray silence. But this thunder arrived muffled, flattened, like drums played behind a heavy curtain, and beneath it Sebastian heard — or imagined he heard, and could not decide which was worse — a rhythm in it. A pattern. Three beats and a rest. Three beats and a rest.

His grandmother's calling-rhythm. The first thing she had ever taught him, tapping it out on the kitchen table with one blunt fingernail while the bread rose under its cloth. *Three and a rest, little storm. Three and a rest. The sky speaks in threes, and the fourth beat belongs to us.*

"No," he said aloud, to the empty room, to the stone on the mantel, to the curtain-muffled sky. "No. She's dead. She's thirty years dead. It's weather. It's only weather."

Outside, the light failed all at once, the way it does under a storm's shoulder, and the first drops hit the thatch like fingertips.

He lit a lamp because his hands needed occupation, and the flame bent sideways in a draft that shouldn't have existed, pointing — he noticed, and wished he hadn't — toward the eastern ridge. Toward the mountain that rose beyond it, the one the villagers called the Gray Wife and spoke of rarely and never after dark. Toward whatever lay beneath it.

The rain thickened. Somewhere up on the ridge, lightning flashed, and for one involuntary heartbeat Sebastian's whole body turned toward it like a plant toward sun — and felt nothing. A great silent nothing, a socket without a tooth, a name called in an empty house. The valley held its hand flat against his chest, gentle and absolute: *not here, not you, never again.*

And yet the storm leaned on the boundary, and the boundary groaned, and the thunder tapped its three beats and a rest, three beats and a rest, patient as a knock at a door.

He did not sleep. Near midnight, wrapped in a blanket in his chair with the lamp burned to a stub, he caught himself listening — truly listening, the old listening, the forbidden listening — to the voice of the rain on his roof. And underneath the rain, underneath the thunder's patient arithmetic, he heard something else arrive at the edge of hearing: small, quick, and utterly out of place in a valley where nothing was supposed to be able to cross the boundary at all.

Footsteps. Small ones. Coming down the hillside through the storm, toward the last house in the village, toward his door.

* * *

Sebastian set down the blanket and picked up the poker instead, because iron was honest, and went to see what the storm had sent him.
