## 2. A Knock at the Door

The storm came up the valley like something with a grudge.

Sebastian felt it before he heard it — a pressure behind his eyes, an ache in the old scar along his forearm where lightning had once written its signature and left him to read it for the rest of his life. Thirteen years in the valley had taught him that this was all he would ever feel of weather again: the anticipation without the answer, the itch without the scratch. The magic could not follow him past the boundary stones. It pooled against them like rain against glass, and on nights like this one he could almost see it there — a wall of grey fury standing at the mouth of the valley, pressing its face flat against the invisible membrane of quiet, wanting in.

It never got in.

He stood in his doorway with a mug of barley tea going cold in his hands and watched the ridge swallow the light. The village below — twelve houses, a mill, Marta's inn with its crooked sign of a leaping fish — had shuttered itself early. He'd seen lanterns go out one by one down the lane like eyes closing. Good. Let them sleep through it. Storms were only storms here. They could not be called, could not be bargained with, could not be *his*. That was the whole point of the place. That was why he had paid what he'd paid to come.

The first thunder arrived muted, as if the valley had wrapped the sky in wool.

He drank his tea. He told himself the tightness in his chest was only the cold coming on.

* * *

The knock came near midnight.

Three small raps, spaced too evenly, too politely — nothing like the frantic hammering a child lost in a storm ought to make. Sebastian set down the mug and listened. The wind worried the thatch above him; somewhere a shutter banged its slow iron rhythm against the wall of the empty Halloway house. Nothing else.

Again: three raps. Patient. Almost apologetic.

No one knocked on Sebastian Okafor's door. In thirteen years, the visits he'd received could be counted on one hand: the reeve, twice, about roof-taxes; Marta, once a season, with bread and gossip she pretended wasn't surveillance; a tinker who'd gotten turned around in the fog and left looking at him strangely, as if the fog had shown her something. The village had long ago settled into an arrangement with him — tolerated, unexamined, the way one tolerates a well that tastes faintly of iron. You didn't drink from it if you could help it, but you were glad it was there when your own ran dry.

He opened the door on the chain, out of habit older than the exile.

A child stood on his step.

She was small — eight years old, perhaps, though it was hard to tell with children who carried themselves like folded letters. Rain sheeted off the brim of a hood that had once been red and was now the color of old brick. She was soaked through in the way that meant she had been soaked for hours: hair plastered flat, lips gone pale, water still dripping from her sleeves onto his stone step in little ticking drops. Her boots were wrong for the weather — thin things, city-made, split at the seams. And her feet, he noticed, were bare inside them. She had walked out of her shoes somewhere behind her and hadn't stopped.

"Where's your mother?" he asked.

She looked up at him. Her eyes were dark and very steady, and they did not do what children's eyes did when they met a stranger — no flicker to the door, no calculation of escape, no quick inventory of the big man's size. She simply looked at him, the way you might look at a landmark you had traveled a long way to confirm existed.

"I said—" He stopped. There was no one on the road behind her. No lantern bobbing in the dark between the houses, no voices calling a name into the rain. The lane ran down toward the inn and up toward the ridge, and both directions held nothing but water and night.

He unlatched the chain. He would tell himself later that he didn't know why. But that would be a lie, and he had sworn off lies the day he'd sworn off everything else. He knew why. It was the steadiness. A frightened child runs from strangers or clings to them; this one had done neither. She had knocked three times, evenly, patiently, as though she had known he would open the door — as though the knocking were a formality, a courtesy observed between people who already understood each other.

She stepped inside when he stepped back, wiping her boots — the ruined, empty boots — twice on his mat before entering, which was more manners than most adults managed.

* * *

He sat her by the hearth in his grandmother's chair, which he had never once sat in himself, and wrapped her in the grey blanket from the chest at the foot of his bed. She took the blanket without thanks and without protest, pulling it around her shoulders with the economy of someone conserving warmth rather than seeking comfort. Steam began almost immediately to rise off her hair. He put water on for tea, then thought better of it and warmed milk instead, sweetening it with the honey he kept for coughs.

While it heated, he studied her sideways, the way you study a bird that has landed on your windowsill — any sudden motion and it will be gone, and part of you will grieve it.

There was nothing remarkable about her face. Round-cheeked, snub-nosed, a smudge of mud along one jaw where she'd fallen or knelt. Ordinary, except for the stillness. Children fidgeted. Children were made of motion the way rivers were made of water. This girl sat in the chair like a stone sits in a streambed — not fighting the current, simply being passed over by it. Even shivering, even blue-lipped with cold, she seemed somehow *settled*, as if the storm outside were a thing happening to other people.

And then there was the whistle.

It hung at her throat on a cord of braided sinew, and he had noticed it the moment she'd raised her chin to look at him — a small thing, bone-white, carved from the hollow wing-bone of some large bird, or so he'd assumed. It was the humming that caught him now. As the fire warmed the room, as the milk steamed on the hook, the whistle began — very softly — to sound. Not a note, exactly. A hum, low and round, like a finger run around the rim of a wet glass, like a hive heard through a wall. It rose and fell with no pattern he could find, and it did not stop when the girl moved, and it did not change when she pulled the blanket tighter.

There was no wind in the room. There was no breath in her, yet — her lips were closed, her chest barely lifting.

"Hold still," Sebastian said, and crouched beside the chair.

Up close, the whistle resolved into something worse than carved. The bone was old — old the way river stones are old, worn smooth by time rather than tools — and along its length ran lines cut fine as spider silk, spiraling from the mouthpiece down and around and down again, tightening as they went, until at the narrow end they coiled into a point. It was not decoration. He had seen carving meant to please the eye, and this was not that. This was writing. Or a map. Or a diagram of something that should not have a diagram.

The hum thickened as he leaned close. He felt it in his teeth. Felt it, absurdly, in the scar on his arm — a faint sympathetic itching, like the ghost of a limb remembering rain.

His hand rose before he decided anything, two fingers reaching to touch the bone—

The girl's hand closed around his wrist.

Her grip was astonishing. Small fingers, cold as the rain, and strength in them like a trap-spring. Not hurting him. Just stopping him, absolutely, the way a doorstop stops a door. She shook her head once. Slowly. Left. Right.

"All right," Sebastian said. "All right. I won't."

She let him go. The hum softened, sank, became almost subliminal — a sound you'd swear you'd imagined if you tried to describe it to anyone tomorrow. He straightened up, knees cracking, and busied himself with the milk because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

He poured it into his one good clay cup and held it out. She took it in both hands, blew across the surface — a proper, careful blow, three short puffs, the way his grandmother had taught *him* to cool milk fifty years and another lifetime ago — and drank.

"Name?" he asked.

She lowered the cup. Looked at him.

"Your name," he said, and tapped his own chest. "Sebastian." Then he tapped hers, gently, a question.

Nothing. Not refusal — refusal has a shape, a set to the jaw, a flinch away. This was different. She regarded his tapping finger with mild interest, the way one regards rain falling upward. Then she set the cup down on the hearthstone, precise as a clockmaker, and held out her right hand, palm up.

He gave her his hand, wondering. Instead of taking it, she pressed one fingertip to his palm and drew.

A spiral. One smooth stroke, beginning wide and winding inward, tighter and tighter, until at the center the line stood upright and climbed — a single vertical stroke rising from the coil's heart, taller than the spiral was wide, straight as a plumb line.

She did it twice more, exactly the same, exact as a stamp. Then she looked up at him with those steady dark eyes, and pointed at the mark in his palm, and then — slowly, deliberately — pointed out the window, into the dark, in the direction of the ridge. Of the mountains. Of whatever lay beyond the mountains, in the country he had spent thirteen years making sure he would never see again.

The fire popped. The whistle hummed its tuneless tune. Outside, the storm leaned on the valley and was refused, and refused, and refused.

"That's a tower," Sebastian said quietly. "Or a spring. Coiled water, coiled stone." His grandmother's voice surfaced in him unbidden, singing in the forbidden half-language she'd used for lullabies when she thought he was asleep — words that had no trade-tongue equivalent, words the priests of three kingdoms had burned people for writing down. *Spiral and pillar*, she'd sung. *The coiled tower. The Spire.* He had been six. He had thought it was nonsense, the way all lullabies are nonsense, until the day he learned otherwise and it cost everyone everything.

He closed his fist over the mark she'd drawn, as if he could keep it there or crush it out, and wasn't sure which impulse was stronger.

"You can't stay," he heard himself say.

She blinked at him, unhurried, and picked up her milk, and drank, and looked into the fire as though he had remarked on the weather — which, in fairness, he effectively had. The blanket had slipped from one shoulder. Mud was drying on her jaw. Somewhere under the hum of the whistle, the storm ground its teeth against the boundary stones, and Sebastian Okafor, who had given up storms and names and the right to be found, stood in his own kitchen feeling the exact shape of the lie he had just told.

He got the second blanket. He built the fire up. He put the last of the bread, and the honey, within her reach, and pointed to the settle by the hearth, and said, "Tonight. Only tonight."

She nodded once — gracious, almost, the nod of a duchess accepting an inadequate gift — and curled up in the settle with the whistle's cord tucked inside her fist, and was asleep between one breath and the next, the way only children and soldiers sleep.

* * *

He did not sleep.

He sat across the room with a lamp turned low and watched her breathe and told himself he was watching for danger — for whoever would come looking for her come morning, for the mother or father or master whose absence explained a barefoot child walking a mountain road in the teeth of a storm. Someone would come. Someone always came. That was the arithmetic of lost things: they belonged to somebody, and somebody always came, and the wise thing — the *only* thing — was to feed her, dry her, and hand her over at first light with a smile and a story about finding her on the road.

But the road had shown him nothing. And the whistle hummed on, soft as a sleeping animal, and the more he listened the more certain he grew that it was not humming *to* the room. It was humming outward. Through the walls. Through the rain. Through whatever invisible skin the valley wore, out into the dark beyond the boundary stones, carrying its small round note into the night like a hand raised in a crowd — *here, here, I am here* —

— and something, somewhere far away, he was almost sure, had begun to listen.

On the settle, the girl stirred. Her brow creased. Her lips parted, and from her throat came a sound that was not the whistle's hum and not quite a word — a syllable, low and ringing, in a language Sebastian had not heard spoken aloud since he was six years old and pretending to sleep.

Then she sighed, turned her face to the firelight, and was silent.

Sebastian sat very still in the lamplight, his heart going like a boy's, and understood that the valley had finally let something in.
