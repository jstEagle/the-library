## 4. What the Village Saw

Marta Halloway had kept the Drowned Bell for twenty-two years, and in that time she had learned to read a village the way other women read weather.

She read it now from her doorway at first light, a mug of barley tea warming her palms, watching the way folk moved down the lane toward the well. There was a hitch in their walking this morning. A looking-back. Old Tam Wicker, who had not spoken to Sebastian Okafor in eleven years except to complain about his fence line, was standing at the well with his bucket forgotten beside him, talking low and fast to the cooper's wife, and every few words his chin jerked toward the crooked house at the valley's end where the exile lived.

The child had been seen.

Of course she had been seen. You could not bring a soaked, silent girl through a village of forty-three souls on the worst night of the autumn storm without someone's candle catching on you. The miller's boy had sworn he'd watched the storm-caller carry something small up his path through rain that fell sideways, and by morning the story had grown a tail: the child had appeared out of the flood itself, they said, out of the lightning, born of it, delivered to his door like a debt come due.

Marta drank her tea and thought about debts.

* * *

The Drowned Bell filled slowly that day, the way it always did after a hard storm — men coming in to drink away the memory of collapsed sheds and drowned turnip rows, women coming to trade flour and gossip in equal measure. Marta worked the taps and the bread oven and listened with both ears, which was how an innkeeper survived.

By noon she had assembled the whole of it, the way you assemble a quilt from scraps.

The child had been seen at Okafor's window, pale as a fish belly, watching the village. The child had been seen gathering stones by the creek, arranging them in circles no one could explain. The child did not speak — this part was true; Marta had seen it herself — and a child who does not speak is either simple or cursed, and folk preferred cursed because it required less tenderness of them.

And beneath all of it, older than any of it, ran the other current, the one that never quite dried up in Hollowmere: *he* had been seen too. Seen hauling timber two-handed when one hand should have buckled. Seen standing in the doorway during the storm with the wind bending around him like water around a stone. Seen, thirteen years ago, arriving half-dead on the ford road with burn-scars up both forearms and a silence about him deeper than the child's, asking nothing, offering nothing, taking the ruined house at the valley's end because no one else would live there.

Storm-caller, they still said, when they said it at all. As if the word were a splinter too deep to work out.

"More ale, Tam?" Marta asked, appearing at his elbow before he could say anything louder.

Tam Wicker blinked up at her, caught mid-sentence, his mouth still shaped around the word *lightning*. "Aye," he said, deflating. "Aye, Marta."

She poured it slow. "You'll want to be careful with stories in here," she said, conversational, wiping the tap. "Stories are like sparks in a dry barn. One careless one and everything you own goes up."

"I only said what I saw."

"You saw a man carrying a child out of a flood." She set the jug down harder than necessary. "I saw the same thing, if we're keeping accounts. It's called rescue, Tam. We used to have a word for it."

"It's not natural, is what it is." He wouldn't meet her eyes now; he studied his ale like it held scripture. "Rain falling wrong around his house. My dog won't go past his fence. And now a mute girl turns up out of nowhere, the very night of the worst storm in — "

"The worst storm in years fell on everyone equally, Tam. It flooded the Barrow field and took half your roof and it didn't ask his permission first." She leaned in, lowered her voice so only he could hear, and made it gentle, which frightened him more than shouting ever had. "You were here thirteen years ago when the hunters came through asking after him. You remember what they looked like. You remember what they did to the Voss farm on their way out, just to be thorough. Now think carefully about whether you'd rather have a strange quiet man who mends your roofs for free, or whatever it is that hunts men like him — and what it does to villages that point."

Tam's throat moved. He drank.

* * *

But Marta was honest enough with herself to know that defending a man in his absence was the easy part. The hard part came at dusk, when she wrapped a loaf of dark bread and a wedge of cheese in cloth, told her serving-girl she was making a delivery, and walked the long lane to the valley's end herself.

The house looked the same as it always did: crooked, over-mended, stubbornly standing. Smoke rose straight from its chimney into windless evening air. But as she drew near she saw the door stood open, and Sebastian Okafor sat on his own step with a small girl beside him, and he was teaching her — of all things — to mend a fishing net, guiding her small hands through the knots with a patience Marta had never once heard in his voice, because she had rarely heard his voice at all.

He rose when he saw her. He always rose. Thirteen years and he had never once let her stand in his doorway while he sat.

"Marta." Her name in his mouth was careful, like something borrowed.

"Sebastian." She held up the bundle. "Bread. And I've come to look at her, and you know it, so let's not waste the light pretending otherwise."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where one might have lived, in another man, in another life. He stepped aside. "There's tea. It's terrible. She picked the herbs herself, so we're both going to drink it and say nothing."

The inside of the house surprised her, though she couldn't have said why. She had imagined squalor, maybe, or bare walls, or the chalkless emptiness of a man waiting to run. Instead there were mended things everywhere — chairs, shutters, a spinning wheel rebuilt from three dead ones — and on the table, spread out with a care that made her chest ache, a child's supper laid ready: bread torn small, milk in a cup with a chipped rim turned toward the wall so the chip faced away from the drinker.

The girl sat very straight on her stool, watching Marta with eyes the color of river slate. Up close she was thin in the way of children who have gone hungry politely, without complaint, for longer than anyone should. Her hair had been washed and combed — recently, clumsily, by large hands learning the work. Around her neck on a cord hung a little whistle of yellowed bone, and Marta felt her eyes snag on it the way a sleeve snags on a nail, though she couldn't have said why.

"She doesn't speak?" Marta asked quietly.

"Not yet." Sebastian set down the tea. "She draws instead. Spirals. Towers." He hesitated, and Marta watched him decide to tell her the truth, watched it cost him something. "Last night she talked in her sleep. Words I didn't know. Words I *did* know, from somewhere I'd rather not say."

Marta sat back. Outside, the last light was going copper on the ridge, and somewhere a nightjar started its churring call, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, which suddenly felt like a courtesy extended rather than a fact guaranteed.

"Sebastian." She said his name plainly, the way no one else in the village bothered to anymore. "Two things, and you're going to hear both. First: whatever she is, whoever she is — she's fed, and warm, and someone combed her hair, and I'll fight any soul in Hollowmere who says a word against it. That's the first thing."

His shoulders came down a fraction. He hadn't known he was braced. She could see that he hadn't known.

"Second thing." She folded her hands on the table, and made herself hold his eyes. "Three days ago, before the storm, two riders stopped at my inn. Cloaks the color of wet slate, faces masked to the eyes. Polite. That's the worst of it — polite as priests. They asked had I seen a man pass through. They described him." She nodded at Sebastian. "Grey eyes, burn-scarred forearms, calls storms. They used the old word for it. They said he might be traveling with—" she glanced at the girl, and chose the riders' own phrasing because there was no gentler version— "*a vessel.*"

The silence that followed had weight. The fire ticked. The girl looked between them, reading their faces the way mute children learn to, missing nothing.

"They said 'might be,'" Sebastian said at last. His voice was flat and very controlled, the voice of a man holding a door shut against weather. "Past tense. Traveling."

"They were riding east when they left. Toward the ford road." Marta stood, because sitting felt too much like settling in. "Riders come back, Sebastian. Riders that polite always come back. Whatever you're doing here — whoever she is — you should know the village can't hide what it doesn't understand. Fear fills in the blanks faster than kindness does. I'll keep filling those blanks myself as long as I'm able. But I'm one woman with a taproom and a loud voice, and fear has better lungs."

He walked her to the door. At the threshold he stopped, and for a moment the mask slipped and she saw underneath it not the cold stranger of village legend but something younger and far more tired — a man who had spent thirteen years holding his breath.

"Marta. If they come back. If it comes to choosing —"

"Don't." She cut him off, sharper than she meant to. "Don't you dare give me instructions for abandoning you. I've buried a husband and two sons, Sebastian Okafor. I know exactly what loss costs, and I've decided I won't pay it early, on credit, for a thing that hasn't happened yet." She pressed the bread bundle into his hands, then — before she could think better of it — reached up and touched the girl's cheek, cool and smooth as riverstone. The child leaned into it, just slightly, just for a heartbeat, the way a plant leans toward light.

Marta walked home in the blue dark with her heart hammering and her jaw set, and behind her the crooked house at the valley's end put out its lamp, one small gold square of light in all that gathering black.

* * *

She was wrong about one thing, though she wouldn't learn it until later, and it would keep her up nights forever after: the riders had not ridden east.

That same evening, while Marta scrubbed her taproom floor and Tam Wicker told his softened version of the story to anyone buying, the miller's boy came running up the lane from the ford, white as whey, breath ragged, bursting through the Drowned Bell's door without knocking for the first time in his life.

"Marta," he gasped, gripping the doorframe. "Marta — there's horses on the far bank. Two of them. Standing in the shallows. Just *standing* there, facing the village, and they haven't moved since sundown, and there's no one on their backs."

Outside, far off across the dark fields, at the edge of hearing, something began to hum — thin and high and patient, like a bone whistle blown by no living mouth.

It sounded almost like it was coming from the direction of the valley's end.
