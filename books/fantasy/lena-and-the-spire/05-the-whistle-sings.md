## 5. The Whistle Sings

The whistle sat on my mantel for six days before it sang.

I want to say I didn't think about it during those days — that I mended the widow Hearn's fence rail and re-thatched the lean-to and went about the small, deliberate business of my exile without once glancing at that pale curve of bone where it rested on the shelf between a chipped clay jug and a stub of tallow candle. But that would be a lie, and I have told myself enough lies in this valley to fill a second lifetime.

The truth is I thought about it constantly. The way you think about a tooth that aches only when you bite down. The way you think about a door you've nailed shut and can still hear breathing behind.

It was not an ugly thing. That was part of the trouble. Bone whistles made by village hands are crude — knucklebones drilled through, strung on leather, good for calling pigs or children home at dusk. This was neither. It was long and slender, curved like a heron's neck, polished to a soft ivory sheen, and carved along its length with a spiral that wound from mouthpiece to end in one unbroken line. A coiled tower. The same spiral Lena had drawn in the ash of my hearth the night she arrived, over and over, until her fingers were gray and the pattern had worn itself into the stone.

I had tried to put it out of the house. I carried it to the woodpile with every intention of burying it under the split logs, and I stood there in the cold with my arm cocked back like a man about to throw a curse into a well, and then I didn't. I brought it back inside. I set it on the mantel. I told myself it was because destroying evidence of what she was would be cruel to her later, when she might need to know what she was. I told myself a great many things.

The girl herself gave no sign of caring whether I kept it or burned it. Lena — I had settled on the name by the third day, plucked half from a dream and half from nowhere, and when I said it aloud she had looked up at me with those dark, patient eyes and touched her own chest once, twice, as if testing whether the word fit — Lena was quiet the way deep water is quiet. She ate what I gave her. She swept my floor without being asked, badly, pushing the broom in great enthusiastic arcs that scattered more dust than they gathered. She followed me to the fence line and watched me work with her chin on her forearms, and sometimes, when she thought I wasn't looking, she drew spirals in the dirt with a stick. Small ones. Always the same spiral. Always wound tight, like something holding itself together.

And always, always, the whistle stayed within her reach.

* * *

On the sixth evening the sky did something it had not done in thirteen years.

I was outside splitting kindling when I felt it — or rather, felt the absence of it, which is a stranger sensation than most people could imagine. In this valley, weather is weather. It comes down off the western hills dumb and ordinary, rain and wind and the occasional honest thunderstorm that belongs to no one. But for thirteen years I had lived behind a veil, and anything with teeth could not reach me through it. So when the air over the ridge began to gather itself — when I felt, faint as a memory of a memory, pressure building in the clouds like breath held behind a closed door — I knew it was not gathering for its own sake.

Something was pulling on it. Something on the other side of the veil, testing the weave.

The light went strange. Greenish, bruised, the color of pond ice. The sheep in Marta's lower field stopped grazing all at once and stood facing the ridge, ears forward, in that collective animal attention that means the world has gone wrong in a way their bodies understand before their minds do. My hands, wrapped around the axe handle, began to ache. Not the ache of labor. The ache of longing. Thirteen years of silence, and my bones still remembered how to listen for storms the way a deaf man's chest remembers music — through vibration, through the floorboards of the self.

I stood very still and let the feeling pass through me and out the other side, the way I had taught myself to let everything pass. It did not quite pass. It pooled in my boots, in my molars, in the scar tissue along my left forearm where lightning had kissed me once, long ago, and left its signature.

Then the pressure eased. The green light drained back out of the sky like water finding a crack. The sheep lowered their heads. And I went inside, and Lena looked up from the floor where she was drawing her spiral, and I saw that she had been watching the sky too — had been watching it, I realized, with recognition rather than wonder, the way you watch a familiar face across a crowded market.

She pointed at the window. Then at herself. Then she shook her head slowly, and her expression was so carefully blank that it frightened me more than any fear could have.

* * *

It happened two nights later, and afterward I could never decide whether it had been accident or invitation.

I had banked the fire low. Lena slept in the alcove off the main room, in the bed I'd built for her from pine and an old feather tick, and I sat in my chair by the hearth doing what I did most evenings: nothing, deliberately, with great discipline. Whittling a spoon I didn't need. Listening to the house settle. Keeping my mind as smooth and empty as a frozen pond.

The sound woke me from that practiced emptiness — though I hadn't been asleep, so perhaps *woke* is the wrong word. Perhaps it *cut* me loose.

A note. Thin and high and sweet, like a finger run around the rim of a glass, except it did not fade the way glass-singing fades. It held. It held and held and held, and underneath the sweetness was something else, something that made the hair on my arms lift and my heartbeat stumble — a hum, a drone, a resonance that seemed to come not from the whistle but from everywhere, from the walls of the house, from the ground beneath it, from the air itself, as if the whole valley were a bell that had just been struck.

Lena stood in the middle of the room. She was in her nightdress, barefoot on the cold flagstones, and the bone whistle was at her lips, and her eyes were open but not looking at anything in this world. Moonlight came through the window in a hard silver bar and lay across the floor between us like a blade laid down.

"Lena." My voice came out rough, unused to urgency. "Put it down."

She didn't hear me. Or she heard me and couldn't stop, which is worse. Her cheeks were hollowed with the effort of blowing, though her chest barely moved — it was as if the whistle drew breath *through* her rather than receiving it, as if she were less a player than a doorway. The spiral carvings along its length had begun to glow. Faintly. A pale luminescence like moonlight caught in milk, traveling up the spiral from the mouthpiece toward the end, winding, climbing, a light with somewhere to be.

And then the valley shuddered.

I don't know how else to describe it. There was no earthquake; the cups on my shelf did not rattle. But I felt it in my spine, in the roots of my teeth, in the old lightning scar that flared white-hot down my arm — a tremor running through something vast and invisible, the way a sheet shakes when someone snaps it flat. The dampening field. The veil. Whatever name you give the woven thing that has kept this valley deaf and mute and safe for longer than the village has kept records — it *shuddered*, and through the gap in its weave, for one single heartbeat, the world outside poured in.

Oh, God. The world outside.

It came back to me all at once, the way floodwater comes back to a dry riverbed — not gradually but totally, erasing the dryness as if it had never been. Every storm on the face of the earth, and I felt them all. The slow gray rain worrying the coast two hundred leagues west. A thunderhead standing over the mountains like a dark god deciding. Winds I had known personally, winds I had named in my grandmother's tongue, still circling the high passes where I'd left them, patient as dogs. The whole breathing, turning, electric body of the sky, and threaded through it — this was the part that broke something open in my chest — threaded through it was *me*. The shape I had left in the world's weather. The storm-caller-shaped absence, thirteen years wide, and every wind I had ever bent recognized me in the same instant, turned toward me like a thousand faces turning in a crowd, and *reached*.

I was on my knees. I don't remember falling. My hands were pressed flat against the flagstones and the stones were singing, actually singing, a fine high vibration, and tears were running down my face and I did not know if they were grief or joy and I suspect now, thirteen years wiser, that they were both, that grief and joy are the same water wearing different banks.

For one heartbeat I was whole. For one heartbeat I was the thing I had been made to be, the heir of a long line of sky-speakers, the man whose grandmother had sung storms down out of a hostile heaven and taught him to do the same, and the power came home to me like a bird to a roost, and it was *magnificent*, and I wanted it, God forgive me, I wanted it so badly I would have torn the veil down with my bare hands—

And then I felt the other thing.

Far away. Farther than far. Below the horizon of the world, below the roots of mountains, in whatever deep place the Spire-folk had built their court of glass and lanterns — something turned over in its sleep. Something vast and patient and *attentive*, and it felt the whistle's note thread up through the earth the way I felt the storms thread down through the sky, and it paused, the way a spider pauses when the web speaks.

It listened.

And then — I swear this on whatever is left of my soul — it began, very slowly, to move.

The heartbeat ended. The whistle's note collapsed inward like a star dying, and the glow ran backward down the spiral and gutted out, and the veil snapped shut over the valley with a sound I felt rather than heard, a sound like the whole sky closing a book. The pressure vanished. The singing stones went silent. The storms — my storms, my beautiful, terrible, waiting storms — receded beyond the weave, and the absence rushed back into the space they'd occupied, and I knelt on my cold floor in the dark, hollowed out, gasping, poorer than I had been a minute before, because now I knew exactly what I was missing.

Lena lowered the whistle. She swayed. I lunged forward and caught her before she fell, and she was limp as a dropped scarf, and for one horrible moment I thought the whistle had taken something out of her that wouldn't come back.

Then her eyes focused on my face, and she blinked, and she looked down at the bone whistle in her hand as if seeing it for the first time. Her brow creased. Slowly, deliberately, she turned the whistle over, studying the spiral, and then she looked up at me again — and her expression was not fear.

It was apology. Deep, grave, adult apology, in a child's face, for something she had not chosen to do.

"It's all right," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle like green wood. "You're all right. We're all right."

Even as I said it, I was looking past her shoulder, through the window, at the ridge. At the night beyond the ridge. At the dark country where the veil ended and the world began, and somewhere beneath that world, something enormous had opened one eye.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat by the dead fire with Lena's sleeping weight finally warm against my chest — she had cried herself out against my shoulder, silent tears, the weeping of someone who has never been allowed to make noise about her pain — and I stared into the ashes and did arithmetic with dread.

The whistle was a key. That much was certain. Whether Lena had blown it on purpose or whether it had used her, I couldn't say, and I suspected the distinction mattered less than I wanted it to. What mattered was this: the valley's protection was not absolute. It was a weave, and weaves have gaps, and the whistle had found one. For one heartbeat, the thing beneath the mountain had heard this valley. Had heard *her* — because I did not fool myself that the distant attention had been for me. I was a stray ember. She was a beacon. Whatever the court below the mountain was, it had felt its property sing.

Because that was the other thing I understood, sitting there in the dark with my arms around a child who was not mine and who was, I feared, everyone's: the whistle had not merely made noise. It had answered something. Notes do not hang in the air the way that one hung unless they are completing a chord begun somewhere else. Somewhere far below, something had been sounding a single tone for years — maybe decades — waiting, and tonight, in a mute valley at the edge of the map, a bone whistle had finally given it the harmony it was listening for.

And I had felt it begin to move.

Toward what? Toward whom? I had no answers, only the animal certainty that sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone: the clock I had not known was ticking had started ticking tonight. The quiet I had mistaken for peace had just revealed itself as a held breath, and somewhere, something exhaled first.

Lena stirred against my chest. In her sleep, her lips moved, shaping sounds without voice — and the shapes were not the shapes of any language I knew, but they were shapes I *recognized*, from songs my grandmother had sung with the shutters barred, from words written in a hand that shook as it wrote them, forbidden syllables from the language before kingdoms.

In her sleep, the child was answering the mountain.

Outside, far to the north, past the ridge, past the veil, thunder rolled — long, low, and deliberate, three beats and a pause, three beats and a pause, like a knock at a door at the bottom of the world.

I held her tighter, and waited for morning, and morning was four days away from changing everything I believed about why she had come to my door.
