## 6. Thirteen Turns

The letter came out of the wall the way water comes out of stone — slowly, and only where you least thought to look.

I had been patching the chimney that morning, the third repair since the storm two nights past, when my trowel knocked against something behind the flue stones that was not mortar and not brick. A hollow. I pried loose the stone with the flat of the blade, expecting birds' nests, old soot, the usual archaeology of an old house.

Instead: oilcloth, tied with waxed string gone amber with age, and inside it a bundle of letters bound in ribbon the color of dried blood.

My grandmother's handwriting. I knew it before I saw a single word — the sharp downstrokes, the letters leaning forward like runners at a starting line. Thirteen years I had lived in this house without knowing her letters slept in its walls. Or perhaps some part of me had known and had chosen, as I chose so many things, not to look.

I sat down on the cold hearthstone with the bundle in my lap and did not open it for a long time. The fire had burned to embers. Outside, the valley lay washed and green under a sky scrubbed clean by rain, and somewhere below, Lena's voice — no, not her voice; she had none to give — but the sound of her moving through Marta's kitchen next door, the clink of bowls, the small domestic percussion of a child being useful. She had been with me nine days now. Nine days of silence and spiral drawings and porridge eaten with fierce concentration, and already the house had rearranged itself around her the way a river rearranges itself around a stone.

I untied the ribbon.

* * *

There were eleven letters. The earliest were dated the year before I was born, and they were ordinary — complaints about weather, about my grandfather's stubbornness, about the price of lamp-oil in the low country. My grandmother, Orla Coldharbour, writing to her sister in a voice dry as August grass. I read them the way you reread a dead relative's face in a photograph, hunting for what you remember and finding mostly what you'd forgotten.

The tenth letter was different.

It was dated thirty-one years ago, and the handwriting had changed — smaller now, cramped into the corners of the page as though the words themselves were trying to hide from what they said. There was no salutation. No sister's name. It began mid-confession, the way nightmares begin mid-fall.

*I have done a thing,* it said, *and I will write it here because I cannot say it aloud, and because paper keeps secrets better than priests.*

*You know what the winter was. You know we ate the seed-corn and then the dog and then nothing. You know your brother's children were dying one by one like candles pinched by an unseen hand. And you know — because Mother told us both, and swore us to it — what waits beneath the mountain, and what it asks, and what it gives.*

*I went down. I took the river road and paid the river its toll and walked into the dark where the lantern-moths light the way for those who are expected. And I stood before the three faces, and I asked for my family's survival, and they asked what I would give.*

*A soul, they said. That is the price. One soul, freely given, freely signed.*

*And I thought — let it be mine. Let them have mine. I am old, I am tired, my soul has more yesterdays than tomorrows in it. But the Ledger-Keeper turned the pages and turned them and looked at me with its kind, terrible eyes, and it said: Not yours. Yours is nearly spent. We take vessels, not dregs. A soul must be young enough to be worth the hollowing.*

*So I gave them a name instead. A descendant's name. A soul not yet born, or barely born — the books do not care, they say. The debt passes down the bloodline until it ripens. They wrote it in their ledger in ink that moved like something swimming, and they sealed it with glass, and they told me the terms as gently as a midwife tells a mother when the pains will come.*

*When the moon completes its thirteenth turn — thirteen full turnings of the year, sister, thirteen years — the vessel will be called home. Reclaimed. Hollowed. Filled with whatever the Court fills its vessels with, and set upon the mountain's shoulder as a lamp is set in a window.*

*And my family lived. Every one of us. Your brother's children grew fat and loud and ungrateful, which is the sound of children living. The farm fed us again. The winter passed and never came back so hard.*

*I tell myself I bought a life with a life. I tell myself the Court would have taken payment from someone eventually, better it be a name I choose than a harvest they reap. I tell myself many things at night, and none of them fit, and still I sleep, which is its own damnation.*

*Do not look for the child. Do not learn the name. If you never know, you can never tell, and if you can never tell, maybe the debt sleeps past its due date the way debts sometimes do.*

*Burn this.*

She had not burned it. She had hidden it in a chimney, in a house she could not have known I would one day inhabit — unless she had. Unless some part of her, in the last years, had understood exactly who would come knocking on exile's door, and had left the truth where only he would find it.

I read the letter twice. Then a third time, my lips moving around the words without sound, as if speaking them silently might change them, the way saying a storm's true name backward is supposed to unmake it.

Then I went through the eleventh letter, the last one, dated six years later, two years before her death. It was short. Her handwriting had gone thin as frost-fern.

*The moon turns,* it said. *I count the winters now the way I used to count coins. Twelve are spent. I have tried to buy it back. I went down the river road again, an old woman with nothing left to offer, and the Smiling One received me — it always smiles, sister, even when it refuses — and it told me the contract cannot be voided, only fulfilled. It told me there is one way a sale dies: if the seller takes back what was sold, freely given back. But my soul is already theirs, you see. I signed it too, that first winter, as surety. I am collateral on my own crime.*

*Unless.* And here the handwriting shook so badly the word was nearly illegible. *Unless someone bearing my name-magic — the storms, the calling, the blood-gift that runs in our line — stands before the Court and spends it. The Ledger-Keeper said such a thing once, in a moment of what I mistook for kindness. Name-magic can unseal what name-magic sealed. But it would cost everything. Everything, and likely fail.*

*I have a grandson. He has the gift, God help him, and I taught him the songs anyway, because a gift unused curdles. If he ever reads this — Sebastian, if you ever read this — I am sorry. I am sorry in the place where words don't reach. I sold a child's soul to keep a family breathing, and the child was yours, though you did not exist yet when I signed, and the child is not you. The child is the one the books named in your stead. Find her. She will be found regardless. Better she should be found by you.*

*Burn this.*

I did not burn it either.

* * *

I sat on the hearthstone until my legs went numb, and the light through the window crawled from morning toward noon, and the embers died completely, and still I did not move. The letters lay open on my knees like a wound I couldn't stop looking at.

Thirteen turns. Thirteen full turnings of the year since the signing — and the signing had been thirty-one years ago, and thirty-one minus thirteen was eighteen, and eighteen years ago a child had been born somewhere beneath the shadow of the mountain, or near it, a child whose name I did not know, whose face I had never seen, whose soul had been spent before her first breath by a woman dead six years.

A child who would be eight years old now.

Eight years old. Soaked to the skin. Standing at my door in the worst storm the ridge had thrown in a decade, carrying a bone whistle that hummed without wind, drawing spirals like coiled towers in spilled flour and river mud and the condensation on window glass.

A child who spoke in her sleep in a tongue older than kingdoms.

"No," I said aloud, to the empty kitchen, to the dead fire, to my grandmother's ghost. "No, that's coincidence. That's—"

But the word wouldn't finish, because there was no word for it. Coincidence was too small. Miracle was too clean. What it was, was arithmetic. Thirty-one years. Thirteen turns. Eight years old. Silent as the grave because the grave was coming for her, and some instinct older than language had told her to run, and her feet — her small, bare, blistered feet — had carried her not away from the mountain but toward the one man in the world whose blood was knotted into her sentence.

She hadn't found me by accident. Children don't find anything by accident. Something in her — the sold thing, the signed-for thing, the soul that knew its own paperwork — had steered her down off the mountain and across the low country and up into the one valley in the world where magic could not follow, following the thread of her own stolen self the way a compass follows north.

And I had taken her in. Fed her. Named her. Started — God forgive me — to love her, in nine days, the way you love a splinter working toward your heart: painfully, inevitably, already part of you.

Four weeks. The moon hung outside right now, a paring of silver over the eastern ridge, and I counted without wanting to: new moon to new moon, four turnings of the week, and then the thirteenth turn complete, and the debt come due.

They would come for her. Not hunters with masks — or not only hunters. The Court beneath the mountain kept its contracts the way winter keeps its appointments. When the turn completed, something would simply arrive, patient and inevitable, and it would hold out its hand, and in its hand would be a ledger, and in the ledger would be my grandmother's swimming signature, and there would be nothing in all the world I could do about it.

Nothing except what my grandmother had written in her shaking hand. *Name-magic can unseal what name-magic sealed.* My storms. The gift I had buried for thirteen years under mortar and mend-work and deliberate forgetting. The gift that, the moment I carried it past the valley's edge, would blaze up like a beacon and mark me for every hunter who had ever hunted me.

To save her, I would have to leave the quiet. To leave the quiet was to be seen. To be seen was—

I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes until I saw stars that had nothing to do with magic.

* * *

Marta found me there in the late afternoon. She came in without knocking, the way she did, carrying bread wrapped in cloth and wearing the expression of a woman who had decided hours ago to ask a question and was only waiting for the right opening.

"You've been sitting in the dark since morning," she said, setting the bread on the table. "The girl's been fretting. Kept looking at your door like it might swallow her."

"I'm fine."

"You're sitting in a cold house in the dark reading old letters." She nodded at the bundle. "Bad news travels even through wax and ribbon, apparently."

"Marta." My voice came out strange, flattened, like a thing stepped on. "The night she came. The storm. Did anyone see where she came from? Which direction?"

"North and east, the boys said. Down off the high pasture side." Marta's eyes narrowed. She was not a stupid woman; she had run an inn through two bad winters and a tax collector's visit, and stupidity does not survive that. "Why?"

"And how old would you guess her?"

"Seven. Eight." A pause. The bread sat untouched between us, filling the kitchen with the smell of warmth neither of us felt. "Sebastian. What's in those letters?"

I looked at her — my neighbor, my only friend in thirteen quiet years, the woman who had defended me to gossips and brought soup when I had fever — and I could not tell her. Some truths are contagious. You hand them to a person and suddenly they're holding them too, and the Court's ledger does not distinguish between the debtor and whoever stands beside him reading over his shoulder.

"My grandmother's affairs," I said. "Old business. Nothing that touches the village."

"Liar," Marta said, without heat. "But all right. I've known you long enough to know when a door's locked on purpose." She gathered her shawl, then stopped at the threshold, her back to me. "Whatever it is — and it is something, I've got eyes — you should know the girl sat on your step for an hour this afternoon. Just sat there, watching the sky. Watching the moon come up over the ridge, pale as it was in daylight. And she had her whistle in her hands, and she was turning it over and over, and she looked—" Marta searched for the word and didn't like any of the ones she found. "She looked like someone counting."

She left. The door swung shut. The house settled around its silence.

* * *

I found Lena at the well behind the house, as the sun went down.

She was crouched at the edge of the trough where the overflow pooled, drawing in the water with one finger — spirals, always spirals, the coiled tower, the mark I had seen in flour and mud and breath-fog. The evening light caught the water and made her drawings gleam and vanish, gleam and vanish, each one erased almost as soon as it was made, and she kept making them with the patience of someone practicing a prayer.

"Lena." The name I had given her, from the old songs — *lena*, little light, the ember carried in a cupped hand through the dark. She looked up. Her eyes were gray as storm-light, and just now they held something I had not seen in them before, something that made my chest tighten: recognition. As if she had been waiting all day for me to come out, and had known why.

I couched beside her at the trough. The water was cold enough to ache. Above the eastern ridge, the moon was rising — a thin silver paring, one night old, beginning its slow climb toward fullness and roundness and, four turnings from now, completion.

"That's the first turn," I heard myself say. My voice sounded far away, like it belonged to someone standing behind me. "In four weeks it finishes its thirteenth."

Lena went very still.

Not frightened-still. Still the way the air goes still before lightning — charged, attentive, listening with its whole body. Her finger hovered above the water, the unfinished spiral trembling on the surface between us.

And I understood, with a certainty that arrived whole and terrible like a storm front, that she knew. Whatever she was, however much the mountain had kept from her, she knew what the moon's turning meant. Perhaps she had always known. Perhaps it was why she had run. Perhaps it was why she had come to me — not to the village, not to the road, not to any of the ten thousand safer doors in the world, but to mine.

Her hand closed around the bone whistle at her throat. In the last of the light, the bone seemed to glow faintly, the way things glow when they remember being alive.

"Lena," I said, and my voice cracked on the second syllable, and I did not care. "Whoever you are. Wherever you came from. Whatever's coming for you when that moon comes full—" I swallowed. "You're not facing it alone. Do you understand me? Whatever my grandmother did, whatever was signed and sold and sealed before either of us was born — I'm going to find a way to unmake it. Even if it costs—"

I stopped. Because the rest of the sentence was *even if it costs me everything*, and saying it aloud felt like signing something.

Lena looked at me for a long moment. The moonlight strengthened. Somewhere far to the east, beyond the ridges, beyond the low country, beneath the mountain, I imagined — I could not help imagining — a ledger opening, a pen of glass dipping into ink that swam, a page turning toward a date circled in advance.

Then the girl did something she had never done.

She opened her mouth — and this time it was not sleep, not the unconscious murmuring I had heard through the wall on her second night, that flowing syllabic tide of the language before kingdoms. This time she was awake, and her eyes held mine, and she spoke deliberately, carefully, the way a person steps onto ice, testing every word before trusting it with her weight.

Three words. Only three. In the old tongue, soft as ash falling:

*"Vessha nor olen."*

*I know what I am.*

The wind came down off the ridge and moved the water in the trough, erasing the last spiral, and Lena closed her mouth, and the silence rushed back in — but it was a different silence now. A chosen one. A door left open a crack, with lamplight showing through.

She knew what she was. And tomorrow, or the day after, or whenever her courage finished gathering — she would tell me the rest.
