## 7. Lena Speaks

The words lived in my chest like birds in a closed room.

I had carried them down the mountain, through the pine-dark and the scree and the long cold water of the river shallows, through three nights of sleeping with my back against stones so they could not take me from behind. I had carried them into the valley where the air went strange and soft and nothing followed me — not the hunters, not the humming, not the pull at the base of my skull that had been my whole life before. And still the words stayed shut inside me, beating their wings.

Because words are how they find you.

That is the first thing they teach you beneath the mountain, though no one calls it teaching. You learn it the way you learn hunger. Every vessel learns it. Speak, and the glass roots taste your voice in the dark. Speak, and the Ledger-Keeper's pen scratches a little faster somewhere far below. The children who had been there longest had gone quiet all the way through, like wells with no bottom echo, and I had promised myself, running, that I would never be a well. I would be a river if I could. Rivers make noise. Rivers get to move.

But rivers also get caught.

* * *

The man — Sebastian, though I did not know his name yet, only that he moved like someone who had once been struck by lightning and was still listening for the second bolt — set a bowl of barley soup in front of me on the third evening and sat across the table with his own, and did not eat.

He watched me instead. Not the way the Host watched us, with that warm terrible attention, all lamplight and appetite. He watched me the way you watch weather. Like something might come over the hills that he already half-remembered.

I ate the soup. It was too hot and too salted and the best thing I had ever tasted, because everything outside the mountain was the best thing I had ever tasted: bread with a crust that cracked, milk that smelled of the cow it came from, apples with bruises on them, real bruises, from falling off a real tree. The world above ground was sloppy and generous and I loved it with a love that frightened me, because loving things is how they hollow you fastest. They do not take what you do not hold.

The whistle sat on the table between us. My whistle. Bone, old as old, carved with a spiral that turned inward forever, the way the Spire turned downward. It hummed sometimes when the wind was wrong, or when the wind was right; I had never learned which. The man's eyes kept going to it and then away, quick as a tongue finding a broken tooth.

"You drew this," he said at last, and pushed a scrap of birchbark across the table. On it, in charcoal gone grey-brown, was the spiral I had drawn the first night, the one I draw everywhere without meaning to, the way other children draw suns. "I've seen this mark before."

I said nothing. I had said nothing for eleven days by then, counting from the door in the storm, and my silence had grown a skin around me, thin and strong as ice on a rain barrel. Underneath it I was all movement, all noise, all the time. That is the secret no one tells you about mute children: we are the loudest ones in every room. We are shouting constantly. You simply cannot hear the language.

He waited. He was good at waiting. I think he had practiced being alone until waiting became a kind of breathing.

Then he did the thing that undid me.

He began to hum.

It was low and shapeless at first, just sound, but after a moment it found a pattern, and the pattern found a rhythm, and the rhythm was one I knew — knew the way you know your own heartbeat, knew it in the marrow the mountain had almost finished drinking. A cradle-song. An old one. Older than kingdoms, older than the valley, older than the glass roots winding down through the dark under the world. My grandmother-of-songs, the woman whose voice lived in the walls of the nursery levels, used to sing its bones to the youngest vessels when the lantern-moths dimmed and the small ones cried for mothers who had sold them.

She sang it wrong, that woman-in-the-walls. Or maybe he sang it wrong. Maybe everyone sings it wrong now except the ones who were taken first. But the bones were right, and the bones reached through my silence like fingers through water, and I felt my mouth open before I had decided anything at all.

"*Sessurath imma vael,*" I said.

The song stopped.

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the valley's soft dead air pressed against the windows, holding its breath the way it always did, forgetting everything the way it always did, and for one moment I hated it — hated the forgetting field, hated the safety of it — because I had crossed the whole world to be somewhere nothing could follow me, and here was the proof that I had brought the following thing inside my own ribs.

The man had gone very still. Stillness has many flavors. His was the flavor of a man hearing a voice from a grave he dug himself.

"Say it again," he whispered.

I should have gone back under the ice then. I want you to understand that. I stood at the edge of speaking the way you stand at the edge of a frozen lake, knowing the weight of yourself, knowing the black water underneath, and every sensible part of me said: *be silent, be stone, be safe.* Silence had kept me alive through the ford and the fens and the long white flank of the mountain. Silence was the only armor I owned.

But he had given me soup with too much salt in it because he did not know how children like theirs salted things. He had put a blanket over me in the storm-night and pretended to be asleep while I shook. He had looked at my spiral drawing not with hunger, not with ledger-eyes, but with grief — an old, personal grief, the kind that has a name written inside it.

And the song. He had sung the song.

Trust is not a feeling. I learned that later, from him, though he never said it in those words. Trust is a decision made with your hands shaking. So I let the ice crack, and I spoke.

"*Sessurath imma vael. Sessurath korrin vael-eth.*"

*The song is wrong. The song was wrong even then.*

His lips moved, shaping sounds without voice, translating, and I watched him arrive at the meaning the way you watch someone wade into cold water — flinch, flinch, go under. When he looked up at me his eyes were wet and furious and afraid, all three at once, which is a very human combination and one I had never seen beneath the mountain. Nothing there felt three things at once. That was the whole point of them.

"That's the tongue," he said. "That's — my grandmother sang in that tongue. When she thought I was asleep. She said it was forbidden, that it came from before, from the Spire-folk, from—" He stopped himself. His hand had curled into a fist on the table, and I saw the knuckles whiten, and I understood that his grandmother was a wound in him the way the mountain was a wound in me. Everyone in this story is carrying somebody else's bargain. It took me years to see that clearly. That night I only saw that his fist was shaking.

"*Vael-eth sessurath,*" I said gently, because the old tongue has gentleness built into it, built into the grammar itself — you cannot say anything harshly in it, the language refuses — "*korrin imma.*" *Your song. Tell me.*

"I can't tell you what I don't know." He dragged a hand down his face. "Thirteen years I've been in this valley and I have spent every one of them not thinking about that language, and now a child walks out of a storm humming it, and—"

I held up my hand. It surprised both of us, I think, that small brown hand rising between us like a fence gate closing. He stopped talking. Good. Talking was his way of hiding; I could see that already. Everyone hides in whatever language they have.

I got up, took the charcoal stub from the birchbark, and turned it over. On the smooth back side I began to draw.

Not the spiral this time. I drew the mountain — but not the mountain as travelers see it, green-shouldered and cloud-crowned. I drew it cut open, the way I knew it, a great dark tooth rooted in the world, and inside the tooth I drew the roots: glass roots, silver-pale, branching down and down and down, and at the ends of the branches, small circles. Dozens of them. Hundreds.

I tapped the smallest circle at the deepest point. Then I pointed at myself.

* * *

He did not speak for a long time. The fire burned down a log's worth. Outside, the valley night did its patient nothing, windless and forgetful, and I missed wind. You cannot imagine how much I missed wind, having been raised in air that never moved unless the Court wished it.

"The circles," he said finally. "Those are people?"

I nodded.

"And the deep ones—" His throat worked. "The smallest ones. At the bottom."

I nodded again.

"What are they for?"

Ah. There it was. The question I had run four hundred miles rather than answer, and here it was, sitting in a warm kitchen smelling of barley and woodsmoke, asking politely. I could have lied. I could have drawn something else, some softer story. But lies are a kind of debt too, and I had sworn on the river shallows that I would never owe anyone anything ever again, not even kindness, not even protection. If he was going to shelter me, he would shelter the truth, all its teeth showing.

So I drew a lantern.

A simple lantern, the kind he probably hung by his own door: a cage of metal around a flame. Then I drew the flame leaving the cage, drifting upward, and the cage remaining — empty, lit by nothing, shaped exactly as before.

Then I pointed at the empty cage. And then, slowly, watching his face the whole way, I pointed at my own chest.

I will tell you what happened in his face, because I watched it the way you watch a storm come over a ridge — layer by layer, each one darker. First confusion. Then the arithmetic starting behind his eyes. Then the arithmetic finishing. Then something that was not confusion or arithmetic at all but a raw animal horror, the kind the body knows before the mind consents to.

"They take it," he said hoarsely. "They take the — the soul. Out of children. And keep the child."

I made the smallest nod. In the old tongue there is a word for yes that literally means *the water goes that way*, and I said it: "*Naeth.*" The water goes that way.

"Why?" His voice cracked on it. "What in all the hells do they *want* with—"

I spread my hands. I didn't know. That was the worst of it, the part I had never managed to explain even to myself: the vessels were never told the purpose. We were fed and clothed and sung to and measured, our hums recorded, our dreams written down by moth-servants with ink that glowed faintly, and none of us knew what we were *for*. You can be a harvest and never see the table.

But I could show him what I knew. And I knew one more thing, the thing that had put my feet on the road, the thing that had made me a runner instead of a well.

I touched my own face. Both cheeks. Then I drew one of the circles from the mountain — a mid-depth one, not the smallest — and beside it I drew a face. A smiling face. Then another smiling face, identical. Then another. Ten faces, ten smiles, exactly the same smile, the same curve, the same nothing behind the eyes.

I pointed at the smiles. Then I pointed at the circle. Then I drew a line from the circle to the smiles, and I made the line thick, and I looked at him until he understood.

"Some of it stays," he breathed. "They don't take all of it. They leave enough to walk and smile and work, and they take the rest, and the rest is—" He couldn't finish. He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Gods below. Gods below and above. I've heard stories. Marta tells a story about a village over the eastern ridge where everyone smiles the same, everyone, and she always laughed when she told it, she thought it was a fairy tale—"

He dropped his hands and stared at me, and I stared back, and the firelight moved on both of us, and I realized my cheeks were wet. I hadn't known I was crying. Crying is loud in its own language; I suppose I had been speaking it for some time.

"*Imma naeth vael,*" I said, and my voice came out rough and small. *I am not that water. I will not go that way.*

"No," he said fiercely, to me or to the kitchen or to thirteen years of his own silence. "No. You won't."

* * *

We talked until the candle drowned in its own wax. Well — he talked, and I drew, and the old tongue came out of me in fragments whenever drawing failed, and slowly, stitch by stitch, he learned the shape of my running.

I showed him the nursery levels, warm and golden and full of singing, because that is the truth too and I refuse to make the mountain only a monster; monsters that are only monsters are easy to flee. I showed him the measuring days, when the moth-servants held their cool hands against our sternums and listened, and wrote down numbers that glowed. I showed him the older children, the ones past their twelfth year, who had begun to go quiet in a way that had nothing to do with silence-as-armor. Their quiet had a floor to it. You could drop a coin down their quiet and never hear it land.

I showed him the night I understood my turn was coming.

The moon counts differently beneath the mountain — they keep a moon of their own, a great pale disc of glass hung in the highest root-vault, and it turns through its phases faster than the sky-moon, thirteen turns to the sky's one, and when your thirteenth turn completes, the measuring stops being measuring. I had watched two others reach it. I remembered the ceremony: lanterns, songs, the Host's beautiful warm face, the child walking down the spiral stair holding a moth-lantern, everyone waving, everyone smiling —

Everyone smiling the same.

I had not understood, before that night, why the families who visited their sold children always smiled that way. I had thought it was joy.

I drew him the spiral stair. I drew the small figure descending. And then, because he needed to understand what I had done — what it had cost, what it meant, that I was not a runaway but a blasphemy — I drew the same figure climbing *up*, and I drew the doors I had passed, and the sentries of living glass whose song could taste a vessel's voice at forty paces, and the fen, and the ford, and the long white shoulder of the mountain under a sky-moon I had only ever seen in drawings, so wide and so wild and so *free* that I had lain on my back in a sheep-field the first night and wept at it.

He listened to all of it. That is the truest thing I can say about him, the thing I would say at his grave if graves could hear: he listened. Not the listening of someone waiting for his turn to talk. The listening of a man taking a weight onto his own shoulders to see how heavy it was, so he would know what he was asking me to carry alone.

When I finished, the candle was a puddle and the window had gone from black to the deep blue that comes just before the birds start lying about morning.

"Lena," he said.

I blinked. He had never called me anything. I had never told him a name — names are dangerous, names are hooks, the Ledger-Keeper's pen writes names and names become debts — and yet he said it, softly, testing it, and it fit me like the blanket in the storm.

"I heard it," he said, before I could panic. "In your sleep. The second night. You talk in your sleep — in the old tongue, mostly, but that word came out plain as bread. Lena." He rubbed the back of his neck, suddenly awkward, suddenly young. "I shouldn't have — I know what names are worth, better than most. If you want me to forget it, I'll—"

*Don't*, I wanted to say. *Don't forget it. No one has ever kept a piece of me before. Everything I am is written in ledgers under the mountain. Be the one place I'm written in charcoal, on birchbark, where the rain can take it if it wants.*

But I was tired of the ice, and tired of the water underneath it, and trust had already cracked me open that evening, so I did the bravest thing I did in that whole long year, braver than the fen, braver than the glass sentries, braver than the door in the storm.

I answered him in his language. My first words to him in the tongue of the valley, the tongue of kingdoms, the tongue they could follow — and I gave them anyway, deliberately, the way you give someone your knife handle-first.

"My name is Lena," I said. My voice cracked in the middle of it, rusted from disuse, and I didn't care. "And they are coming for me. The turn doesn't stop because I ran. The moon keeps turning whether I watch it or not."

The fire had burned to embers. In the ember-light his face did something complicated and settled, the way water settles when a stone finally lands.

"How long?" he asked.

I held up my hands. I counted out the turns I had left the way the moth-servants counted them, folding fingers down one by one, and when I was done, four fingers stood and the rest were folded, and we both looked at them for a long time.

Four turns of the glass moon. Four weeks, near enough, in the counting of the sky.

Somewhere out beyond the valley's soft forgetting edge, in the world where voices carry and ledgers wait, I felt — or imagined I felt, though I have never since been sure it was imagination — a faint, patient scratching. Like a pen. Like a pen somewhere far below the world, writing my name one more time, and underlining it.

Sebastian rose and began, quietly, to bank the fire. And as he bent to it, his eyes went — once, quickly, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth — to the bone whistle lying on the table, humming its noteless note in the dead air of the valley that forgot everything.

Almost everything.
