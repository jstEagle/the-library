## 9. Leaving the Quiet

The morning they left, the valley did not try to stop them.

Sebastian had half expected it to. Thirteen years of living inside that hush had taught him to think of it as a thing with opinions — a great soft animal curled around the village, breathing slow, swallowing sound and sorcery alike. He had imagined, in his darker hours, that if he ever tried to leave it would tighten around him like a fist. That the quiet would take offense.

But the valley only went on forgetting, as it always did. The mist lifted off the millpond in long gray ribbons. A heron stood in the shallows with the patience of a saint. Somewhere behind him a door banged, and Marta's voice carried across the square, scolding a boy for stealing plums, and none of it meant anything except that morning was morning and the world was ordinary.

He was grateful for that, he decided. He would rather be unremarked than mourned.

He had spent three nights packing, though there was little enough to pack. His grandmother's letters went into an oilskin wrap first — all eleven of them, tied with the same fraying cord she'd used, each one handled so many times the folds had gone soft as cloth. He knew them by heart and still could not stop reading them, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. *Do not go up the mountain, Seb. Do not answer what calls you by name.* And beneath that, in ink gone brown: *I am sorry for what I made you inherit.*

He hadn't understood that line until four days ago. Now it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

The whistle went next. He wrapped it separately, in wool, though he suspected it didn't need protecting from anything in this valley. It hummed even through the layers — a thin, patient note, like an insect trapped against a window. Lena kept reaching for it whenever it was out of his hands, her small fingers opening and closing with a want so naked it hurt to watch. So he let her carry it herself, on a cord around her neck, tucked under her shirt. It seemed right that she should hold the one thing that had come with her out of the mountain's country.

Everything else fit into a single pack: two shirts, a blanket, a skillet, a coil of good rope, a week's bread wrapped in linen, the mending kit he'd used on half the roofs in the village. Tools. He'd been a tool-user for thirteen years, not a wonder-worker, and his hands had learned the honest grammar of nails and shingles and hinge-pins. He packed those tools the way another man might pack relics.

Lena sat on the doorstep while he worked, watching the road.

She had not spoken since the night at the ford — since the wordless rush of the old tongue, her hands flying, her eyes fierce with terror and trust in equal measure, telling him about the mountain, the hollowing, the room where they kept the vessels-in-waiting until the moon finished its counting. She had run. She, a child of eight years with a bone whistle and a spiral mark, had run from the court beneath the mountain, and every step of that flight had been an act of courage Sebastian doubted he could have matched at twice her age.

But she spoke to no one else. Not to Marta, who had wept over her and stuffed her pockets with honey-cakes. Not to the innkeeper's children, who had tried for days to teach her skipping games. She watched them all with those dark, old eyes and kept her silence like a coat she'd grown into.

This morning she wore the coat loosely. She kept looking down the road — the road that led out — and then back at the cottage, and then at Sebastian, and something in her face asked a question she wouldn't voice.

"You can stay," he said, for the third time that morning. He crouched in front of her, knees cracking — thirteen years of roof-work took their toll. "Marta will take you in. The whole village would fight for you, I think. You'd be safe here. Safer than anywhere."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached out and touched the oilskin bundle where it bulged against his shoulder — the letters — and then pressed her palm flat against his chest, over his heart, and left it there.

*You,* that touch said. *I'm staying with you. Wherever you're going.*

Sebastian exhaled slowly through his nose. "Stubborn," he said, in the old tongue, because some words belonged to that language and no other. Her mouth twitched. Almost a smile.

* * *

Marta found them at the edge of the square, as he'd known she would. She came down the lane with her shawl pinned crooked and her jaw set in the way that meant she'd already lost the argument with herself and was furious about it.

"I told you not to go," she said.

"No," Sebastian said. "You told me the hunters passed through asking after a storm-caller. You told me the mountain doesn't forget. You never once told me not to go." He shifted the pack higher on his shoulder. "You know me too well to lie that badly."

"Someone has to say it anyway." She looked past him at Lena, and her voice gentled in a way few people ever heard. "Child. You keep him out of trouble, you hear me? He's hopeless alone. Can't cook. Mumbles in his sleep. Talks to his tools."

Lena stepped forward and hugged her, quick and fierce, and Marta's breath caught audibly. When the girl pulled back, she pressed something into Marta's hand — one of the honey-cakes, saved from her pocket, offered back. Marta laughed, wet-eyed, and closed her fingers around it like it was a king's ransom.

"And you—" Marta rounded on Sebastian, poking a finger hard against his sternum. "Thirteen years I've watched you mend other people's roofs and never your own heart. You think I don't know why you came here? Why any of us come here?" Her voice dropped. "The valley takes our sharp edges, Sebastian. It took yours. And now you're walking out to get them back, and God help me, I'm glad, because a man shouldn't live half-made just to stay safe."

"Marta—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't thank me, don't apologize, don't make a speech. Just—" For a moment the iron went out of her and she was only a tired woman on a cold morning. "Come back. Both of you. That's all. Come back."

He embraced her briefly, her shawl smelling of woodsmoke and bread, and then it was done, because if he let it become more than brief he would not leave at all.

They walked out through the eastern gate as the sun cleared the ridge. No one followed. But when Sebastian looked back, once, at the bend where the road dropped toward the ford, he saw figures standing along the palisade — the smith, the miller's wife, the boy with the stolen plums, a dozen others, silent, watching them go. Not a farewell. A witness. The village had seen them off, and the valley would forget them by evening, but for this one moment they were remembered.

Lena raised her hand. So did Sebastian.

Then the road bent, and home was gone.

* * *

For six miles the road climbed gently through pastureland, and Sebastian felt nothing.

That was the strangest part, he thought later. He had braced himself for the boundary the way a man braces for cold water — a held breath, a clenched jaw, waiting for the flood. He counted fenceposts. He listened to Lena humming tunelessly beside him, matching her steps to his without being asked, and he catalogued the ordinary sounds of an ordinary morning, hoarding them. The last quiet things. The last hour of being nobody.

The pastures gave way to gorse and bracken. The gorse gave way to pine. The air cooled and thickened, and the birdsong changed — different thrushes up here, harsher, wilder. And still nothing. The valley's dampening field, whatever ancient working had laid it down, apparently faded by degrees rather than ending at a line, and Sebastian began — foolishly, shamefully — to hope that maybe the magic had died in him after all. Thirteen years. Maybe it had starved quietly in its cage and he would walk out of these hills as merely a man, a roofer, a father-of-sorts, and the hunters would never look twice at—

The pines ended.

There was no warning. One stride he was among the trees; the next, the forest simply stopped, cut off in a clean arc as though a giant had drawn a circle around the high country beyond, and beyond that arc lay open moorland rolling away toward the distant blue shoulders of the mountains, and the sky above it was enormous, and the wind hit him full in the face.

And the wind *knew him.*

It happened between one heartbeat and the next. There was no slow return, no gentle reawakening. Thirteen years of dammed storm broke all at once, and the magic came back into him like a river into a cracked bowl.

He went down on one knee. He heard Lena cry out — heard it strangely, from far away, because his own blood was suddenly roaring louder. Every nerve lit at once. It was not pain exactly, or not only pain; it was sensation returning to a limb long asleep, that terrible bright flooding of pins and needles magnified a thousandfold, running through marrow and muscle and the deep channels of him that had lain fallow since he was twenty-six years old. He tasted copper. He tasted lightning. The hairs on his arms rose and the air around him crackled with a smell like the moment before a summer cloudburst — ozone and hot stone and green rain.

*Come home,* said the wind, in the voice wind has spoken to his family for nine generations. *Come home, come home, come home.*

And the sky answered before he asked.

That was the part that undid him. He wasn't calling. He wasn't reaching. He was on his knees in the bracken with his head bowed, wanting nothing, and the clouds were already gathering — he felt them assembling overhead with the eager obedience of hounds hearing their master's boots on the stair. Pressure built. Light flickered inside the cloud-bellies, pale and wrong for midmorning. The temperature dropped five degrees in as many breaths. Somewhere behind him, thunder cleared its throat.

*Lena.* He grabbed for her hand blindly, found it, gripped hard. *Control it. Control it or you'll mark us both for every hunter from here to the sea.*

He wrestled it down. It was like wrestling a living thing — like holding a wolf's muzzle shut while it strained toward a scent. Breath by breath, heartbeat by heartbeat, he dragged the storm back from the edge, unspun the pressure, soothed the charge out of the air. The flickering light inside the clouds dimmed. The wind lost its hungry voice and became only wind again.

When it was over he was shaking, soaked in sweat despite the cold, both knees in the wet bracken, and the sky overhead was merely gray and innocent, and Lena was crouched in front of him with both her small hands on his face, searching his eyes.

He managed a laugh. It came out ragged. "Well," he rasped. "I'm out."

She didn't smile. She looked at him with that grave, ancient attention of hers, and then she did something he hadn't seen her do before: she drew back her lips and showed him her teeth — deliberately, comically, a child's grimace — and pointed up at the sky, and then at him, and shook her head slowly.

*I saw that,* the gesture said. *I see what you are. And I'm not afraid.*

Something in his chest cracked along an old fault line. He pulled her against him, one-armed, and felt her small weight settle against his side as naturally as if she'd always been there.

"Yeah," he murmured into her hair. "Me neither. Mostly."

* * *

They walked the moorland through the afternoon, and Sebastian learned what it meant to be a storm-caller in open country again.

It was not freedom. That was the bitter joke of it. In the valley he had been caged but invisible; out here he was free and blazing like a beacon. Every step he took, the world noticed. Birds went quiet when he passed, not because of the girl but because of him — they felt the charge in him the way animals feel weather coming. Puddles trembled with rings though there was no rain. Twice, static crawled up his arms when his mood darkened, and he had to stop and breathe it flat, sweating, cursing softly, while Lena stood watch beside him with her hand on the whistle through her shirt, ready.

By late afternoon the moor had begun to rise toward a saddle of dark stone, and the mountains stood clear ahead at last — real mountains now, not blue suggestions, their upper slopes banded with cloud that did not move quite the way clouds should. Somewhere under one of those peaks, glass roots ran through the dark like veins, and lantern-moths drifted through halls of singing souls, and a ledger existed with his grandmother's signature in it, and a debt with his child's name written where his family's should have been.

He stopped at the top of the saddle and looked back.

The valley was a smudge of green behind them, already softened by distance into something like a memory. He thought of Marta's crooked shawl. Of the boy with the plums. Of thirteen years of mended roofs and refused remembrance, of loneliness so old he had mistaken it for peace. He had walked out of that quiet believing he was choosing exposure over safety, danger over comfort.

Standing here, with the storm-magic coiled and restless in his chest and the sky taking note of his every mood, he understood it was worse than that. He hadn't traded safety for danger. He had traded invisibility for a bonfire. Every hunter who had ever sought the exile Okafor would feel him pass like a change in the weather — because that was precisely what he was now. A change in the weather. Walking. Talking. Carrying a child the mountain wanted back.

Lena tugged his sleeve. She pointed east, where the moor descended toward a thread of silver — a river, catching the last light — and along it, tiny with distance, the smoke of chimneys. A road. Traffic. People.

People meant witnesses. Witnesses meant word traveling. Word meant hunters.

It also meant the only way to the mountain, and the moon overhead was already fattening toward its thirteenth turn, four weeks and closing.

Sebastian settled the pack, checked the whistle's cord at Lena's neck, and started down toward the river and the road and everything waiting for them there.

Behind them, far to the north, almost too far to be sure — a single roll of thunder answered his passing, though he had called nothing.

Something had heard him come out of hiding.

And it was not the valley.
