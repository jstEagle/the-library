## 10. Road of Small Kings

The trade road announced itself before we saw it.

We heard it first — a low, rolling murmur like a river that had learned to talk — and then the smell: woodsmoke and tallow, wet wool, onions frying in old fat, the sweet rot of overripe fruit left too long in a cart bed. After nine days of walking through country so empty that our own footsteps sounded like accusations, the noise of other people hit me like weather.

Lena stopped at the crest of the hill and gripped my hand.

I felt her flinch through her fingers. She had not been among crowds since the mountain, I realized. Perhaps never among crowds like this — not the careful, quiet village she'd fled from, but this: a road that moved like an animal, all hide and muscle and appetite. Carts ground along ruts worn knee-deep by centuries of wheels. Drovers shouted. A man led six mules loaded with salt blocks, each one stamped with a sigil I didn't recognize, and behind him came a woman selling ribbons off a pole she carried like a lance, and behind *her* a knot of pilgrims singing something tuneless and devout, and threading through it all children with trays of roasted chestnuts who scattered like sparrows when a wagoner cracked his whip.

"Stay close," I said, and then felt foolish, because where else would she go?

She didn't let go of my hand anyway. Her grip was small and dry and very certain, and I found I was grateful for it in a way I didn't examine too closely.

The road ran east toward the mountains, which stood on the horizon now like a row of filed teeth. Three days, the innkeeper at the last village had said, if the passes stayed open. Four if the weather turned. I looked at the sky — high, pale, harmless — and did not trust it. The sky and I had an understanding, or we had once. Out here beyond the valley's edge, I could feel it again, that faint pressure at the base of my skull, like a door left unlatched in a house I used to own. The storms knew where I was. They were patient. They had been waiting thirteen years; they could wait three days more.

I only hoped nothing else on this road was waiting for us.

* * *

We made camp that first night in the lee of a ruined waystation, one of the old toll-houses from before the Kingdoms consolidated the roads, its roof fallen in but its walls still standing shoulder-high. Lena built the fire while I saw to the boots — hers had begun to come apart at the seams, and I had waxed thread and an awl and the stubbornness of a man who has mended roofs for a living. She worked quickly, competently, feeding twigs to the flame in the order they'd burn best, and I thought, not for the first time, that whoever had raised her before the mountain had taught her useful things before they taught her silence.

She was watching the road when I looked up. Not the fire. The road.

Traffic had thinned after dark, but not stopped. Travelers on the trade road moved by night as often as day — it was cooler, and safer in some ways, though I'd have argued the point. A line of lanterns bobbed along the far verge, and Lena's head turned to follow them the way a cat's head follows a bird.

Then she went rigid.

I followed her gaze. Among the lanterns, one light had detached itself from the rest and was coming toward us. It moved without hurry. It did not waver, though the ground was broken and dark, and as it drew nearer I saw why: it wasn't carried. It floated, waist-high, ahead of a small covered cart drawn by a single white ox whose horns had been painted with tiny blue spirals.

The cart stopped twenty paces out. The lantern settled to the ground beside it, gently, like a bird coming to roost.

"Well," said a voice from within the cart, rich and amused and older than it had any right to be. "A fire, a child, and a man mending boots by moonlight. Either I've found honest folk or excellent bait. In my experience the two are rarely distinguishable."

The curtain over the cart's doorway twitched aside.

The woman who emerged was small — smaller than me, smaller than some children — and wrapped in layer upon layer of shawls, each a different color, none of them matching, all of them magnificent. Coins and charms and bits of bone hung from her hems and chimed when she moved. Her hair was white as frost and bound up with pins made of yellowed ivory, and her face was brown and seamed and sharp as a hatchet, with eyes the color of good honey that missed absolutely nothing. She looked at our fire. She looked at our boots. She looked at Lena, and her eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch, and something in my chest went cold.

"Away from the fire, little one," she said pleasantly, to Lena, in a language I did not know — and then, seeing Lena's blank face, she tried another, and another, working through tongues the way a merchant works through coin, until she reached one that made Lena's breath catch audibly.

"No," I said, and stood up, and put myself between them, feeling absurd — a roofsman squaring off against a grandmother-sized peddler — but doing it anyway. "Whatever you're selling, she's not buying."

The old woman laughed. It was a wonderful laugh, warm and unkind at once, like brandy cut with vinegar. "Selling? Boy, I haven't sold anything in forty years. People give me things. Information mostly. Sometimes teeth." She lowered herself onto the waystation wall with a rustle and clatter of charms, arranged her shawls, and regarded me over her folded hands. "But you're right to be rude. Rudeness is cheap armor. Wear it proudly."

"We're travelers," I said. "Poor ones."

"All travelers are poor. That's what makes them travelers instead of kings." She nodded at the road. "Though between us, there are more small kings on this road than on any throne. Every drover with a route no one else wants is a king of his mile. Every toll-keeper, every ferryman, every woman who knows which pass closes first in autumn. Little kingdoms, little crowns, little laws. And every one of them would sell a child with the right mark for less than you'd think." Her honey eyes flicked to Lena, then back to me. "Which brings us to the question I stopped to ask, since courtesy demands I ask before I take: who marked her?"

"Marked her?"

"Don't." The word came out flat, and for a moment the amusement drained out of her face entirely, and I saw the thing underneath, the thing that had kept her alive on this road for forty years. "Don't insult us both. I watched her look at my lantern the way a deer looks at a snare. I watched you put your body between us before I'd said ten words. And I can smell the mountain on her from here — cold stone and lantern-moths and old bargains. So. Who marked her? Or did she mark herself, which would be worse, and braver, and none of my business except that it's now traveling down my road under my sky, and I make it my business when things travel under my sky."

I should have lied. Thirteen years in the valley had taught me the value of a plain face and a plainer story. But there was something about being seen so completely that made lying feel like handing her the rope.

"She came to me," I said finally. "Out of a storm. I don't know who marked her. She can't tell me. She doesn't speak — "

"She speaks," the old woman said. "Just not in any tongue you'd know. Not in any tongue most folk know." She tilted her head, studying Lena with new interest. "Unless I'm wrong about the spiral."

Lena's hand had gone, without my noticing, to the collar of her shirt — to the mark below her collarbone, the coiled spiral she'd drawn on my door the first night, the one I'd seen inked faintly into her skin and told myself was a birthmark. She hadn't shown it to anyone. She hadn't shown it to *me*, not really; I'd found it once while washing fever-sweat from her shoulders and she had covered it immediately, both hands, like someone shielding a wound.

Now she looked at the old woman, and slowly, deliberately, pulled her collar aside.

The spiral caught the firelight. It was finer than I remembered — lines within lines, a tower coiled around itself, winding inward toward a center that seemed, when I looked too long, to be slightly deeper than skin ought to allow.

The peddler-queen went very still. Even her charms stopped chiming.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor brave stupid man."

"That's the second time someone's called me stupid this month."

"And they'll keep calling you that until it stops being true, which it won't, because men like you don't learn, they just get older." She beckoned, two fingers, imperious. "Come here, child. Let me see properly. I won't touch. Touching marks is how you end up owning pieces of people, and I gave up owning pieces of people decades ago — bad margins."

Lena glanced at me. I hesitated, then nodded. She crossed the firelight and stood before the old woman, chin up, hands at her sides, and I marveled again — eight years old, and she faced strangers the way soldiers face gates.

The peddler-queen leaned close. She did not touch the mark. But she held her palm above it, an inch away, and closed her eyes, and her lips moved soundlessly, counting something, and the fire burned lower and lower and neither of them seemed to notice, and I noticed, and my hands wanted to shake, and I made them not.

"There are eleven coils," the old woman said at last, opening her eyes. "Do you know what that means?"

"No."

"It means she's nearly finished." She sat back, and for the first time she looked tired — genuinely tired, ancient, a woman carrying a weight she'd set down for no one in forty years. "Boy. Sit down. You're not going to want to hear this standing up, and I'm not going to enjoy saying it either way, so we may as well both be comfortable."

I didn't sit. "Tell me what the mark is."

"The mark is a ledger." She said it simply, the way you'd say *the sky is up*. "Everything the Court beneath the mountain does, it writes. They don't keep their accounts in books, boy — books burn, books get lost, books get argued with. They keep their accounts in *people*. That spiral is a contract, written in the only ink that can't be forged. Each coil is a term. Eleven terms fulfilled." She looked at Lena with something terrible and gentle in her face. "One remaining."

The wind moved through the ruined waystation. Somewhere down the road, a mule complained. My voice, when it came, sounded like someone else's. "Fulfilled by whom?"

"Does it matter?" She shrugged, and her charms chimed, and the sound was suddenly unbearable. "A bargain's a bargain. Bloodline, blood-price, blood-debt — the words change, the arithmetic doesn't. Someone in her line signed something, and the signature runs in the veins like dye in water, and it pools in the youngest vessel it finds. That's her. That's always how it works. The Court doesn't hunt children, boy. It *waits* for them. Patience is the cruelest thing about it. That, and the paperwork."

"When?" I asked. I already knew. Some part of me had known since the letter, since the thirteenth turn, since the night I'd read my grandmother's handwriting and felt the floor of my life tilt. But knowing a thing in your bones and hearing it said aloud by a stranger on a dark road are different animals, and the second one bites.

"Soon," she said. "Sooner than comfortable. You're heading toward the mountain — don't bother denying it, the road only goes two places and you're not dressed for the coast." She studied me. "You think you can bargain with them. I can see it in your jaw. You've got a plan, and the plan involves talking, because men like you always believe that underneath everything, even monsters want to be reasonable."

"Doesn't everyone?"

"No." She said it without cruelty, which made it worse. "Most things want to be *fed*. Reasonableness is just the shape hunger wears when it's patient." She rose, shawls chiming, and began rummaging in her cart, muttering. "But I didn't stop to lecture you. I stopped because of the mark, and because of what I owe an old debt, and because — well. Because I'm soft, and I'll deny it if you repeat it."

She turned back holding something small: a strip of leather, plain and dark, punched with a single hole, threaded on a cord.

"A gift," she said. "Not for sale. For sale it would cost you everything you have, which is why I'm giving it away — gifts are how you stay richer than your customers." She held it out to Lena. "Wear this. Under your clothes, against the mark. When the Court's people look at you — and they will look, child, they will *look* — this will blur what they see. Not hide it. Blur it. A smudged ledger buys time. Time is the only currency worth anything on this road."

Lena took it carefully, the way you take something hot. She looked at me. I nodded. She tied it around her neck and tucked it beneath her shirt, over the spiral, and something in her shoulders loosened half an inch.

"Now," the old woman said, and her voice changed — dropped its performance, dropped its music, became simply an old woman's voice, tired and clear. "The advice. The thing I actually stopped for. You'll get one piece from me, and it will be the most valuable thing anyone gives you on this entire journey, including the leather, including anything the river gives you, including anything you steal." She pointed one gnarled finger at me, then swung it to Lena. "*Never show the court your true name.*"

I frowned. "I don't — "

"You do. Everyone does. Your true name isn't what your mother called you across a field. It's the name the world keeps for you — the one written in whatever ledger holds your kind. Storm-callers have them. Vessels have them. Anyone with magic in their blood has one, and the Court can *read* them, and a name read aloud by the right mouth is a leash." Her eyes were fierce now, burning in the firelight. "They will ask you for it politely. They will ask you for it kindly. They will offer you tea and cushions and hospitality so warm you'll forget you're in a hole in the ground. And somewhere in the middle of all that warmth, someone will say, *and what shall we call you, friend?* — and the moment you answer truly, you belong to them. Give them a lie. Give them a dead man's name. Give them the name of a mule you once disliked. But never — never — the true one."

"And if they already know it?" I asked quietly.

The old woman looked at me for a long moment. Then at Lena. Then back at me, and her expression was the worst thing I'd seen all night, worse than the hunters at the ford, worse than the storm on the ridge: it was pity, offered without anesthesia.

"Then you hope," she said, "that you find the flaw before they use it. There's always a flaw. Contracts are made by hungry things, and hungry things are careless. But you won't find it by being honest, and you won't find it by being brave, and you certainly won't find it by standing in front of a child like a fence post." She gathered her shawls. "Find it by being clever. Be clever, boy. It's the only prayer this road answers."

She climbed back into her cart. The curtain swayed. Then it stilled, and her voice came out once more, softer:

"Little one."

Lena stepped forward. The old woman's hand emerged through the curtain, holding a dried fig, and Lena took it, and then — quick as a wren — pressed something into the old woman's palm in return. I couldn't see what. The hand withdrew. There was a pause, and then a low laugh, startled and genuine.

"Well," said the peddler-queen. "Well, well. Keep that hidden better than your name, child." And the curtain fell.

The lantern lifted from the ground. The white ox leaned into its yoke, and the cart rolled away down the dark road, charms chiming, growing smaller, until the lantern was one star among stars and then not even that.

Lena stood watching after it, her fist closed tight around the fig.

"What did you give her?" I asked.

She opened her hand — no. She didn't open her hand. She shook her head, small and firm, and tucked the fig into her pocket, and looked up at me with an expression I was beginning to know well: the look of someone keeping a secret not to deceive me, but to protect me from the weight of it.

I let it go. I was learning to let things go. It was either wisdom or exhaustion, and on the trade road the two taste identical.

* * *

We walked the next two days inside a crowd, and I hated it, and I was grateful for it, and both feelings taught me the same lesson: the world outside the valley was wider than I remembered and crueler than I'd ever known.

I had thought exile had shown me hardness. Thirteen years of mending roofs, of being the stranger at the well, of watching conversations stop when I approached — I'd believed that was hardship. It was nothing. Hardship was the woman at the watering trough who sold drinking rights by the cupful and watered her mules first. Hardship was the gibbet at the crossroads with three bodies on it, one of them small, and the placard nailed above them reading DEBT in letters painted fresh, because the paint had to be refreshed weekly or travelers might forget what happened to those who couldn't pay. Hardship was watching Lena watch the gibbet, and watching her understand it, and knowing that whatever the mountain had planned for her, the world below the mountain had already rehearsed it in a dozen cheaper forms.

Small kings everywhere. A toll-king at the bridge who charged double for anyone with dust of the eastern roads on their boots. A ferry-queen on the shallows who took payment in secrets when coin ran short — I paid her a secret, a small one, the name of the village where I'd bought my awl, and watched her file it away in whatever ledger she kept behind her eyes, and understood that I had just become a debt on someone's books. Everything was ledgers. Everything was owed. The whole world outside the valley was one vast account book, and every soul in it was either a creditor or collateral.

And through it all, Lena walked beside me with her hand in mine and her eyes open, missing nothing, cataloguing everything, and I began to understand that she wasn't frightened of this world. She was *studying* it. The way you study a disease you've been diagnosed with.

On the third morning the mountains filled half the sky.

They came up over the horizon the way a wave comes up over a swimmer — not gradually, but all at once, as if they'd been waiting for us to look away. Grey and black and streaked with snow, they shouldered into the clouds, and at their feet the trade road split: one branch running south along the foothills toward the passes, the other bending north and upward, narrowing, climbing, toward a notch in the ridgeline that might have been a pass or might have been a wound.

At the fork stood a marker-stone, older than the Kingdoms, carved with directions in four scripts, three of them dead. The fourth script was a spiral.

Lena stopped in front of it. She stared at the spiral for a long time. Then she reached out and laid her palm flat against the stone, the way you'd touch a grave, and her lips moved — silently, or almost silently, shaping words in the tongue before kingdoms, the tongue I'd heard her speak in her sleep.

When she took her hand away, she looked up at me. And for the first time since the ford, since the hunters, since we'd fled the quiet valley with its truce broken behind us, she smiled.

It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone who has just confirmed something they suspected, and confirmed it is worse than feared, and has decided to walk toward it anyway.

Above the notch in the ridge, where the northern branch of the road climbed toward the mountain's shadow, the clouds had begun to gather. They were a long way off. They were moving slowly. But they were gathering, and they were dark, and they were not natural weather — I knew that the way I knew my own heartbeat, the way I knew the pull behind my breastbone that had been growing stronger every day since we crossed the valley's edge.

Something beneath the mountain had felt us coming.

And it was rolling out the welcome.
