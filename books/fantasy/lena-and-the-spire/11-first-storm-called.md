## 11. First Storm Called

The road east of the valley had a smell Sebastian had forgotten he knew: dust and pine resin and the faint iron tang of distance, of places where the air was not filtered through a dampening field that smothered every wild thing. For thirteen years he had breathed valley air — thick, quiet air, air like wool wrapped around a bell so it could not ring. Now each breath felt like standing at the lip of a well and hearing water move far below.

Lena walked beside him with her hands clasped behind her back, the bone whistle hanging against her chest on its cord of braided hair. She had not blown it since the night it sang. She did not need to. He caught her sometimes touching it the way other children touched a locket, or a scar.

They had been three days on the trade road, following the slow southward curve of the hills toward the mountain's shadow. The peddler-queen's cart had turned off yesterday at a shrine to some small god of crossroads, and she had left them with dried plums, a warning about the pass, and a look at Lena that Sebastian still hadn't decided how to read — pity, or recognition, or appetite.

*Never show the court your true name.* He kept turning the advice over like a stone with something alive under it.

The sky had been building all afternoon. That was the thing about leaving the valley: weather became a language again. In thirteen years of exile, clouds had been only clouds — scenery, shade, an occasion for mending roofs. Out here they were sentences. The great grey towers stacking up over the western ridge said *I am coming*, and the wind that flattened the grass in long silver strokes said *soon*, and somewhere beneath his breastbone, in a place he had boarded shut like a window in winter, something old and enormous stirred in its sleep and said *yes*.

He hated it. He hated how much he didn't hate it.

"Keep close," he told Lena, though she was already close, walking in the pocket of his shadow the way she'd learned to do in markets and crowds. She looked up at him. Her eyes were the grey-green of deep water over stone, and they went to the stormclouds, then back to him, then to his hands — which had begun, without his permission, to flex.

He shoved them into his pockets.

*You are a man mending roofs,* he told himself. *You are a man who keeps bees for a neighbor who doesn't like him. You are no one.*

But the storm kept talking, and part of him — the part he'd starved for thirteen years and never managed to kill — kept answering.

* * *

The ambush came at the hour when the light goes copper and everything looks like it's already burning.

The road dipped through a cutting between two hills, a place where the pines crowded close and the ditches ran deep with last week's rain. Sebastian had noticed it half a mile back and disliked it, and had said nothing, because there was nowhere else to go and saying *I dislike this ditch* would have frightened Lena more than the ditch itself. So when the first arrow buried itself in the trunk of a pine two feet from his head with a sound like a door being kicked open, he was ashamed to find that some cold, competent corner of his mind had already drawn the map: three in the trees ahead, one on the ridge behind, probably two flanking in the fern-brake to the left where the ground cover moved wrong.

"Lena." He pulled her against his side, into the lee of his body. "Don't run. Whatever happens, don't run."

She pressed her face into his coat but did not cry out. Of course she didn't. She was the child who had run down from a mountain full of smiling horrors without making a single sound the whole way to his door.

"Evening," called a voice from the trees ahead, pleasant as a landlord's. A man stepped out onto the road, and four more resolved around him out of the green gloom — big men, road-worn, armed with the confident shabbiness of people who had done this many times and never once been interrupted. The speaker had a crossbow held low and easy, and a smile that showed too much gum. "Bad stretch of road, friend. Toll's whatever you're carrying. Coin, boots, the girl's cloak if it's good wool. Nobody needs to get wet."

Sebastian realized the man meant the coming rain. It was almost funny. Almost.

"We've little," Sebastian said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. Underneath the level voice, his blood had gone strange — not fast, but *deep*, like a river suddenly remembering it used to be a glacier. "Some food. A few coins. Take them and we'll walk on."

"Oh, I don't think so." The gum-shining smile widened. "See, my associate up on the ridge says you've got a look about you. Foreign look. And foreign folk on this road are usually running *from* something, which means somebody somewhere would pay to know which way you went. So maybe we take the coins *and* the information. Maybe we take the girl a ways and ask her questions. She's a quiet one, isn't she? Quiet ones always know things."

Something happened in Sebastian's chest.

It was not anger, exactly. Anger was hot and human and he knew its shape well. This was colder and older — a pressure change, a dropping of the barometer inside his own ribs. Thirteen years of mended roofs and refused memories sat on that pressure like stones on a cellar door, and the stones did not move, but the door groaned.

"The girl," Sebastian said, "goes nowhere."

"Friend," the bandit said, almost kindly, "there's five of us and one of you, and the one of you is soft as valley butter—"

The lightning did not wait for permission.

That was the thing Sebastian would remember afterward, lying awake while Lena slept against his side: that it did not wait. He had spent thirteen years believing his power was a thing he carried, a tool in a box, and that opening the box would be a decision, deliberate as drawing a knife. It was nothing of the kind. The storm above the ridge had been speaking to him all afternoon, and all afternoon he had been answering without knowing it, and now the conversation simply — concluded.

The world went white.

There was no gap, no gathering, no dramatic pause. One moment the gum-smiling man was raising his crossbow, and the next the sky reached down through the cutting and touched the pine beside him, and the tree exploded into burning splinters, and the thunder arrived not as sound but as a physical fact, a fist of pressure that slammed the air flat and threw two of the bandits off their feet and rang in Sebastian's teeth like the whole world had become a struck bell.

And oh — oh, it was sweet.

That was the sickening part. Not the power itself, not even the violence of it, but the *rightness*. Thirteen years of drought ended in one swallow. Every nerve in his body lit up like the cutting, like the tree, like the white-blind air, and the storm poured into the channel it had carved in him long ago and found the channel exactly as it had left it, wide and waiting, and Sebastian stood in the middle of the smoking road with his arms half-raised and his hair lifting off his skull and felt — for one obscene, glorious heartbeat —

*felt like himself.*

Then the heartbeat passed, and he saw what himself looked like.

The pine was a torch. Rain had begun to fall in fat, stunned drops that hissed where they hit the coals. The gum-smiling man lay on his back ten feet from where he'd stood, crossbow flung away, clothes smoking faintly at the seams, alive — Sebastian could see the frantic bellows-work of his breathing — but looking up at Sebastian with an expression that had shed every trace of the landlord's pleasantness. It was the expression of a man who has seen what lives under the world.

The others were scrambling. One had made it three steps toward the fern-brake before his legs stopped agreeing with each other. Another had dropped his blade and was backing away with both palms out, mouthing something — a prayer, or a bargain, or just *no, no, no* over and over.

"On the ridge," Sebastian heard himself say, and his voice came out with the storm still in it, doubled and vast, a voice with a floor under it. "Leave. All of you. If I ever see any of your faces again, I won't aim at a tree."

They left. They left with a speed that would have been comic in any other context — men falling over each other in the ditch, one losing a boot in the mud and not stopping for it, the ridge-top archer vanishing so completely and so fast that Sebastian wondered if he'd imagined him. Within a minute the cutting held only Sebastian, Lena, a burning tree, and the rain, which was arriving properly now, in earnest, drumming on the leaves and steaming off the split trunk.

Sebastian lowered his arms.

His hands were shaking. Not with exhaustion — there was no exhaustion. That was the horror of it. He felt *wonderful*. He felt like a man stepping out of tight boots after a day's march, like the first breath after a fever breaks. The storm rolled overhead and every drop of it was his, known to him, mapped in his blood; he could feel the whole grey engine of it turning across the hills, could feel the next fork of lightning coiled in the cloud-base like a muscle waiting for the word. It would come whenever he asked. It would come *gladly*.

Thirteen years, and it hadn't diminished. It hadn't forgotten him. It had only waited, patient as debt.

He was going to be sick.

He made it two steps off the road before his stomach won, and he knelt in the wet ferns and brought up nothing but bile and the plum he'd eaten at noon, retching while the rain soaked through his coat, while the storm hummed its enormous satisfaction all around him, while his skin crawled with borrowed glory.

A small hand settled between his shoulder blades.

He hadn't heard her approach. Of course he hadn't. Lena crouched beside him in the ferns, soaked to the skin, her dark hair plastered to her forehead, and rubbed slow circles on his back with the grave competence of someone who had perhaps tended sick people before, or perhaps simply understood, in the wordless way she understood most things, that this was not a moment for words.

"I'm all right," he rasped, which was a lie, and then, because lying to her had begun to feel like a kind of theft: "No. I'm not. I'm — glad. Do you understand? I'm glad it came back. That's what's wrong with me."

Lena studied him. Rain ran off her chin. Then she lifted her free hand and pointed, deliberately, at the burning pine — at the black wound the lightning had torn in the world to keep a bad man from doing a bad thing. She pointed at it, then at Sebastian, then she pressed her small fist against her own chest, over the bone whistle, over whatever lived beneath it that the court beneath the mountain wanted to scoop out and wear.

*It is the same for me,* she was saying. *The thing that saves us is the thing they want. Being dangerous is not the same as being wrong.*

Or perhaps she was saying nothing of the kind. Perhaps she was only eight years old and pointing at fire because fire was astonishing. But her eyes held steady on his, grey-green and unblinking, and there was no fear in them. That was the part that undid him more than the vomiting had. Five armed men, a tree struck to embers, a man kneeling in the mud with storm-light still crawling faintly under his skin like veins of ore — and the child watched him the way she watched rain: with attention, with patience, with something that was not quite wonder and not quite trust but was, terrifyingly, willing to become either.

"You should be afraid of me," he said hoarsely.

Lena tilted her head. Then, slowly, she shook it — once, firmly, the way she shook her head at offers of food she didn't want, at suggestions she intended to overrule. She took her hand off his back, wiped the rain from her own face with her sleeve, and stood, and held out her hand to him. To help him up. Eight years old, half his size, offering to haul him out of the ferns.

He took it. Her fingers were cold and small and absolutely steady.

* * *

They walked until dark, because staying near the burned tree felt like staying at the scene of something, and made camp in the lee of an outcrop where the rain couldn't reach. Sebastian built a fire with flint and steel, deliberately, clumsily, taking three times longer than the old way would have taken, because he needed to prove to himself that he still knew how to make fire the way ordinary men did — by friction and patience and small human stubbornness.

The storm moved off eastward, grumbling, reluctant, like a dog called home from a hunt. He could feel it going. He could feel it the way he'd once felt a toothache: constantly, intimately, against his will. Somewhere over the far hills it would spend itself on empty fields, and he would feel that too, and tomorrow morning the sky would be scrubbed clean and innocent, and none of it would be his business anymore except that all of it would always be his business, forever, because you could board up a window but the wall was still yours.

Lena ate her share of the dried plums and then, instead of sleeping, sat cross-legged and watched him across the fire with that unnerving patience of hers.

"What?" he said finally.

She raised one finger. Pointed at the sky, where the last of the storm glowed dimly behind the eastern ridgeline. Then she pointed at him. Then — and this she did slowly, carefully, choosing the shapes of it — she drew in the air with her finger a spiral, the coiled-tower mark she'd scratched on his door the first night, the mark that meant the Spire, that meant the mountain, that meant everything hunting them both.

Then she opened her hand. Let it fall open, empty, fingers loose.

*I have a thing inside me too,* she was saying. Or asking. *Does having it make us theirs?*

"No," Sebastian said, before he knew he was going to speak. The word came out rough. He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight jumping in his eyes. "Listen to me. What they want — what the court wants — is not the thing itself. It's the *having* of you. The owning. A storm isn't cruel because it strikes a tree, and you aren't a vessel just because there's more in you than fits. They want you to believe the shape of you belongs to them because they can use it. That's the whole trick. That's the entire trick, Lena. Everything else is paperwork."

She considered this with the seriousness she gave to everything, chewing a plum stone absently. Then she pointed at him, and then at the eastern ridgeline where his storm was dying, and raised her eyebrows.

"And me?" he said. "What about the fact that I liked it?"

Lena shrugged. It was a small, devastating shrug — the shrug of someone for whom the category *things that frighten me* had been reorganized long ago, and lightning did not make the list.

"You'll understand when you're older," Sebastian muttered, and was rewarded with the ghost of her almost-smile, the rare one, the one that started and stopped like a candle deciding whether to catch.

She slept eventually, curled against his side under his coat, the whistle rising and falling with her breath. Sebastian stayed awake and watched the fire and did not sleep, because every time he closed his eyes he felt the channel again, wide and waiting, and heard the storm's patient voice saying *whenever you want, whenever you want, we never stopped listening.*

Toward dawn he dozed despite himself, and dreamed of his grandmother's kitchen, and in the dream she was singing one of the forbidden songs in the tongue before kingdoms, and mid-verse she stopped, and looked at him with an expression of terrible kindness, and said: *Now you know why I sold it.*

He woke with the sun in his eyes and Lena already up, standing at the edge of the outcrop, looking north along the road they would have to take.

He joined her. The road ran down out of the hills toward a river valley, green and gold in the early light, smoke rising from chimneys — a village, the first real settlement since the peddler-queen's turn-off, a place where they might buy bread and horses and a night behind a locked door.

Except that as the light strengthened, Sebastian saw that the smoke rose from every chimney at exactly the same height, perfectly uniform, like threads cut by the same shears. And the fields, when his eye followed them, were planted in rows so precise they might have been ruled. And no dogs barked. No birds wheeled over the yards. No children's voices carried on the morning air, though the village was plainly large enough to hold them.

Lena's hand found his. Her grip was cold.

She was staring at the village with an expression he had never seen on her face before — not fear, exactly, but recognition, the grim familiarity of someone meeting, in the flesh, a thing she had only ever seen in nightmares. Her lips moved. No sound came out, but he was close enough to read the shape of the word, and it was a word in the old tongue, and he had learned enough of it from his grandmother's songs to know what it meant.

*Hollow.*

The road down to the village was the only road forward. Sebastian looked at it for a long moment, feeling the ghost of the storm ache in his teeth, feeling Lena's small cold hand tighten around his fingers.

"Well," he said quietly. "We knew the world out here was crueler than the valley. Let's go see how much."
