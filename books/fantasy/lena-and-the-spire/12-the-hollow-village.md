## 12. The Hollow Village

The road bent toward the river on the third morning, and Lena felt the wrongness before she saw it.

It came to her the way cold comes through a floorboard — not all at once, but rising, patient, certain. She had learned to read the world in pressures and silences, in the small languages of wind and water that needed no mouth. The old tongue had words for these things. *Vess* — the hush before rain. *Kellith* — the stillness of a held breath. What pressed against her now was neither. It was the silence of something already taken.

She walked behind Sebastian, as she always did, close enough that his shadow touched her feet when the sun leaned west. He had begun talking to her without expecting answers — about the road, about the weather, about the way his grandmother used to salt fish and curse the gulls in two languages. He talked the way some people hum: to fill a space he was afraid of. Lena didn't mind. His voice was rough and low and it made the world feel like it had edges.

But this morning he had gone quiet too, and when she looked up at him she saw that he had felt it also. His hand kept drifting to his chest, where the storms lived under his skin like caged birds, and his eyes moved along the rooftops ahead with the wariness of a man reading a letter he already dreaded.

The village sat in a fold of land where the river widened and slowed, all pale stone and dark timber, smoke rising from chimneys in thin, obedient lines. From a distance it looked like any of a hundred villages they had passed since leaving the valley. It looked like home, almost. That was the first lie of it.

They came down the hill along a rutted cart track, past a shrine with a weather-worn figure whose face had been smoothed away by centuries of rain. Lena stopped at the shrine. She did not know why, at first. Then she understood: the statue's face was not worn away. It had been *made* smooth — deliberately, lovingly, by hands that had sanded every feature into a soft blank oval. Someone had wanted no face here. Or someone had wanted only one face, everywhere, forever.

Her fingers found the bone whistle at her throat and closed around it. It was warm. It was always warm now, since the night she had blown it and the valley had shuddered and something far away had turned its attention toward them like an eye opening. She had not blown it since. But it remembered her grip. It remembered everything.

"Stay close," Sebastian said. His voice was quiet in the way that meant loud things underneath.

She nodded. He had taught her the valley signs for yes and no and *wait*, and she used them now, though her hands were not steady.

* * *

The first person they met was a woman drawing water at the well.

She was perhaps thirty, broad-shouldered, with flour dusted on her forearms and a smock the color of unbleached linen. She looked up as they approached, and she smiled.

Lena had seen ten thousand smiles. She knew their grammar the way she knew the grammar of storms. There were smiles of greeting and smiles of fear, smiles that hid teeth and smiles that hid grief, smiles offered upward to strangers and downward to children. Every smile was a sentence. Every smile said something.

This woman's smile said nothing.

It was perfectly symmetrical. It lifted both corners of her mouth to precisely the same height. Her eyes crinkled at the corners in exactly matching arcs, and her cheeks rose in identical rounded curves, and the whole arrangement of her face was so *correct* that it looked less like an expression than like a mask carved by someone who had studied expressions for years without ever once having felt one.

"Good morning," the woman said. Her voice was pleasant. Warm, even. "Travelers! How wonderful. You must be tired. The bread is fresh today."

Every word landed at the same pitch. Not flat — that would have been easier, that would have been merely strange. Flatness could be exhaustion or sorrow or fear. This was worse than flat. This was *level*, the way water is level, finding its one true surface and holding it without effort, without variation, without the tiny human tremors that make a voice a living thing.

Sebastian answered her. Lena watched his mouth move — greetings, thanks, questions about the road ahead — but she barely heard him. She was watching the woman's face while Sebastian spoke, and she saw the thing that made her stomach turn over slowly, like a stone rolling downhill.

The woman's smile did not change. Not once. Not when Sebastian mentioned the flooded ford, not when he asked after lodging, not when he thanked her. The smile held its perfect shape the way a painting holds its shape, and behind it the woman's eyes were — 

Lena searched for the word and found it in the old tongue before she found it in any language Sebastian spoke.

*Empt.*

Not empty. Empt was emptier than empty. Empty meant something had been removed and a space remained, aching, waiting to be filled. Empt meant the space itself had been taken. It meant looking into a room and finding not bare walls but no room at all — just the idea of a room, drawn on air.

The woman turned back to her well-rope with the same smile fixed in place, hauling water in slow, even strokes, each pull identical to the last, and Lena understood that the woman would haul water with that same smile until someone told her to stop, and then she would stop, and she would smile at whatever came next with exactly the same face.

Lena's hand tightened on the whistle until the bone edges bit into her palm.

*That is what they will make of me,* she thought. *That is the shape they have chosen for my face.*

* * *

There were more of them in the square.

A man splitting wood — smiling, each stroke of the axe falling at the same rhythm, the logs stacking themselves into a pile so neat it looked drawn rather than built. Two children playing a game with stones — smiling, laughing at intervals so regular that Lena counted them, and yes, the laughter came every eleventh throw, never the tenth, never the twelfth. An old man on a bench outside the inn, smiling at the street with his hands folded, and when a cart rattled past he turned his head to follow it, smoothly, and smiled at the cart, and when the cart was gone he turned back and smiled at the street again, and there was nothing else in him at all.

The village was full. Full of people. And empty.

"Something's wrong with them," Sebastian murmured. He had crouched beside her, pretending to adjust her bootlace, his voice pitched under the ambient sounds of the square. "All of them. Like—" He stopped. Lena watched him struggle with it, watch him arrive at the truth the way a man arrives at a cliff edge and feels the ground go soft. "Like they're being *worn*. Like the people are coats and something else is walking around inside them."

Lena shook her head slowly. She tugged his sleeve and pointed, first at the woodcutter, then at herself, then drew in the dust with one finger the spiral — the coiled tower, the mark of the Spire — and then she flattened her hand across the spiral, smoothing it away, and looked up at him.

He stared at the erased spiral for a long moment.

"No," he said. It came out of him like something torn. "No, that's not— you're not—" He caught himself, lowered his voice further, further, until it was barely breath. "They took part of them. Partially. They left enough to work and smile and say good morning." His jaw worked. "Why leave anything? Why not take it all?"

Lena knew. She had known since the mountain, since the long dark corridors where she had listened through doors, since the night she had run. She took his hand and pressed it flat against her own chest, over her heart, and then pressed his hand against her cheek, and then she pointed at the woman at the well, and then she made the gesture she had no words for: she cupped her hands as if holding water, and poured most of it out, and kept only a little sloshing in the bottom of her palms.

Enough to carry. Enough to serve. Enough to smile.

Sebastian went gray.

"They keep the vessel useful," he whispered. "Until it's needed. They don't hollow you all at once — they hollow you in *installments*. They take a piece, see how much is left, take another." He looked around the square with new horror, at the smiling woodcutter and the counting children and the bench-old-man, and Lena saw him doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. "How many pieces does a village make? How many souls does it take to fill whatever they're filling down there?"

Lena didn't answer. She couldn't. But she looked at the children with the stones, and she thought about the palace corridors, and the other vessels-in-waiting she had left behind — the ones who had already forgotten how to want — and she felt the horror rise in her not as fear but as something colder and more permanent. Recognition. This was not a warning. This was a *portrait*. Somewhere beneath the mountain, in a case of glass roots, there was a hollow shaped exactly like her, waiting to be filled, and every smiling person in this square was a rehearsal for it.

One of the stone-playing children looked up.

The child was maybe six years old, a girl with mud on her knees and a gap where a tooth should be, and for one moment — one single, flickering moment — the smile slipped. What was underneath it was not sadness, exactly. Sadness would have been a mercy. What was underneath was confusion, a small animal bewilderment, the look of someone waking in a house where all the furniture has been rearranged in the dark. The girl's eyes found Lena's, and in them Lena saw a question forming, slow and drowning, like a bubble rising through deep water:

*Was I ever somebody different?*

Then the smile rose back up over the girl's face like water closing over a stone, and she threw her eleventh stone, and laughed on schedule, and turned away.

Lena's legs nearly gave out. Sebastian caught her elbow, steadied her, and she realized she was shaking — not weeping, she hadn't wept since the mountain, weeping required a self that could afford the loss of water — but shaking, a fine continuous tremor from the bones outward.

"We're leaving," Sebastian said. "Now. We'll go around, take the far bank, sleep in the open—"

"Lodging," said a voice behind them, pleasant and level. "You'll want lodging. The inn has rooms. Fresh bread today."

The well-woman stood behind them, bucket in hand, water slopping gently over her knuckles without her noticing. Her smile had not moved. But her head was tilted — slightly, precisely — and her eyes were fixed not on Sebastian but on Lena. On the whistle at Lena's throat.

"The inn," the woman repeated. "Rooms. You'll be comfortable. Everyone is comfortable here."

And Lena heard it then, underneath the level warmth, underneath the pleasant pitch — faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall. Something else speaking through the woman's mouth. Not the woman. Something using the woman the way a glove is used. Something that had noticed the whistle, that had noticed the girl who carried it, that was saying *stay, stay, stay* in a voice borrowed from a hollowed stranger, because it could not come out into the daylight itself, because whatever wore this village wore it from below, from the direction of the mountain, from the direction of home.

Lena stepped backward. One step. Two. She put Sebastian's body between herself and the woman, and she pulled the hood of her cloak up over the whistle, and she did not run, because running would be an answer, and she would not answer.

"Thank you," Sebastian said carefully. "But we're expected in Corlevan by nightfall."

"There is no hurry," the woman said, smiling. "No one hurries here. No one needs to. Everything stays."

*Everything stays.* The words went through Lena like a hook through cloth. She thought of the archive she had heard whispers of beneath the mountain, the singing glass, the shelves upon shelves of kept things. *Everything stays.* That was the whole creed of the court beneath the mountain, spoken through the mouth of a flour-dusted woman at a well three days' walk from its gates. Nothing was lost down there. Nothing escaped. Everything stayed, arranged and labeled and singing softly in the dark forever.

Sebastian took her hand. His palm was sweating. His grip was iron.

"We'll be going," he said.

The woman watched them go. Lena did not turn around, but she felt the smile on her back the whole length of the square, warm as sun, patient as stone, and she heard the woodcutter's axe keeping its perfect time behind them, and the children laughing on the elevens, and somewhere a door opening and a voice saying good morning, good morning, good morning, in voices that were all the same voice wearing different throats.

* * *

They did not speak until the village was a smudge of smoke behind them and the river ran loud between them and it.

Then Sebastian sat down on a fallen tree at the roadside and put his face in his hands, and Lena sat beside him and waited, because she had learned that men like Sebastian broke quietly and needed witnesses who wouldn't make noise about it.

"I've seen dead villages," he said finally, through his fingers. "Plague towns. Burned-out holds. I walked through a place once where everyone died in a single night, and it was terrible, but it was *over*, it was finished, the horror had an ending you could stand at the edge of." He dropped his hands and looked at her, and his eyes were wet and furious. "That wasn't death. Death leaves you alone. That was— it's still happening, isn't it? Right now, while we sit here. They're still in there, smiling, working, hauling water. Part of them is still alive in there, watching through the windows of their own faces, and it can't get out, and it can't die, and it can't even *want* to get out anymore because wanting was the first thing they took—"

He stopped. Swallowed. Looked at her.

"And that's the shelf they're saving you for."

Lena held his gaze. Then, slowly, deliberately, she did something she had not done since the mountain. She reached into the collar of her dress and drew out the bone whistle, and held it up between them in the gray afternoon light, and let him look at it — really look — the fine spiral grooves cut into the bone, the way it seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it.

Then she pointed at the whistle. Pointed south, toward the mountain, toward the spire that coiled beneath it like a root of glass. Pointed at the whistle again. And then she pressed the whistle hard against her own chest, over her heart, both hands wrapped around it, and shook her head — *no* — with a force that shook her whole body.

*This is mine. It knows me. Whatever they put in me, whatever they hollow out, this remembers me. This is the part they cannot carve away, because it was given, not taken. Given freely. By someone who chose me.*

She didn't know yet who had given it. She didn't know yet why the bone was warm, or what song slept inside it. But she knew the difference between a thing taken and a thing given, because she had lived on both sides of that line, and she would die on the giving side of it if she had to.

Sebastian looked at the whistle for a long time. Then he reached out and covered her hands with his own, wrapping his fingers around hers around the bone, so that for a moment the three of them — the man, the girl, the whistle — were bound together in one small knot of warmth.

"Whoever you were before," he said quietly, "whoever gave you that — they're the reason you're still *you*. And I swear to you, on my grandmother's grave and on every storm I've ever called, that I will find out who they were. And I will find out what you are. And I will get you out of this. Not hollowed. Not shelved. Not smiling at strangers from a well. *Out.*"

Lena believed him. That was the terrible thing, the wonderful thing — she believed him completely, the way she had never believed anyone, because he had nothing to gain from lying to her and everything to lose from keeping his promise, and he kept talking anyway, kept promising anyway, kept spending himself on her behalf like a man burning his own furniture to warm a stray.

She leaned into his side, and he put his arm around her, and they sat on the fallen tree while the river ran past, carrying its cold cargo of melted snow toward the sea, remembering nothing, forgetting nothing, asking nothing of anyone.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a drystone wall, off the road, with a small fire that Sebastian built with the anxious competence of a man who trusted flames more than people. Lena lay with her head on his rolled cloak and watched the stars come out over the eastern hills — and beyond the hills, faint as a bruise, the mountain.

She was almost asleep when the humming started.

It came from the fire, at first — or she thought it did, a thread of sound woven into the crackle, too melodic for burning wood. Then she realized it was coming from the whistle, resting against her collarbone, singing to itself in the dark the way it sometimes did, a sound below hearing, more felt than heard, a vibration that traveled through bone to bone.

And beneath the whistle's hum, from far away, from the direction of the mountain, something hummed *back*.

Lena sat up. Across the dead fire, Sebastian was awake too, propped on one elbow, staring south. His face was lit orange and shadow, and on it was an expression she had seen only once before — the night in the valley when the whistle had sung and the dampening field had shuddered and he had felt his storms answer from across the distance.

"It knows we're coming," he breathed. "Or it knows *you're* coming. It's been listening the whole way, hasn't it? Through them. Through the village. Through every smiling mouth between here and the gates." He looked at her, and his voice cracked down the middle. "Lena. How far does it reach?"

She didn't know. But she lay back down and pulled the cloak to her chin and looked at the mountain, and she thought about the girl with the gap-toothed smile and the question drowning in her eyes, and she thought about the shelf beneath the world where everything stayed, and she thought about the hollow shaped like her, waiting, patient, already half in love with the idea of her arrival.

And then, in the old tongue, in the language before kingdoms, in the language the mountain had tried to keep for itself, Lena whispered into the dark:

*I am coming. But I am not coming to stay.*

Far to the south, the humming paused.

As if it had heard.

As if, for the first time in a thousand years, something beneath the mountain had been *answered* — and did not know what to do about it.

In the silence that followed, the bone whistle grew very cold against her skin, and did not warm again until dawn.
