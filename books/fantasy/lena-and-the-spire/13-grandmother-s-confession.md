## 13. Grandmother's Confession

The letter had been waiting for him for thirteen years.

Sebastian had carried it through every season of his exile without once opening it — sewn into the lining of his coat when he fled the lowlands, transferred from coat to coat as the winters wore the old ones out, moved at last to the bottom of a cedar chest where he kept the things he could not look at and could not throw away. It lay there beneath a folded map of a country he no longer trusted, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, tied with a length of blue thread that had once been the color of his grandmother's eyes.

He had told himself he would open it when he was ready. He had told himself that for thirteen years, the way a man tells himself he will swim again after nearly drowning, standing on the bank, watching the water move.

Now Lena was asleep in the loft above him, breathing in the slow rhythm of a child who had finally stopped listening for footsteps, and the fire had burned down to a red heart of coals, and Sebastian sat on the floor of his own house with the oilcloth packet in his hands and understood that *ready* was a door that would never open on its own. You had to put your shoulder to it.

The blue thread came away easily. Thirteen years, and it had not rotted. His grandmother had knotted it with a storm-caller's fingers — knots that held against anything but the hand that made them.

He unfolded the paper.

Her handwriting filled the first page edge to edge, small and slanted, the letters leaning forward like runners at a starting line. She had always written the way she spoke: fast, certain, leaving no room for doubt or for reply. He could almost hear her voice rising off the ink — that low, smoke-cured voice that had taught him the names of clouds before it taught him the names of colors.

*My Sebastian,* it began. *If you are reading this, then you have found the girl, or she has found you, which is the same thing. The Spire-folk do not wander. They are drawn.*

His hands went cold around the paper.

*There is no gentle way to write what I have to write, so I will not try to be gentle. I was never any good at it, and you always saw through me anyway. I sold a soul to the court beneath the mountain. Not mine. Mine they would have taken gladly, and I would have given it, and there would have been an end to it, clean as a cut. But the court does not want clean cuts. The court wants bloodlines. A soul given by one generation binds the generations that follow, because a family is not a row of separate candles — it is one flame passed hand to hand, and what is owed to the flame is owed to every hand that carries it.*

*So I gave them a descendant's soul. Unborn, unnamed, unchosen. I signed for a child who did not yet exist, because the price of my family's survival was a soul of our blood, freely offered, and I was the only one of us still alive to offer it.*

Sebastian read the paragraph three times. The words did not change. The fire did not warm him. Outside, the valley lay under its blanket of forgetting, the stars sharp and indifferent over the ridge, and somewhere in the loft above him a child breathed in her sleep who had been sold before she was ever conceived.

He thought he had known grief before. He had buried a wife; he had buried his grandmother; he had buried, in a sense, himself, the day he walked into this valley and let the quiet close over his storms like water over a stone. But all those griefs had been for things taken from him. This was a different shape entirely. This was grief for something he had *done* — done in the only way a man can do a thing before he exists, which is to inherit it, to carry it in his blood like a seed, to be the hand the flame was passed to.

*I know what you are thinking,* the letter went on, as if she were sitting across the coals from him, watching his face. *You are thinking: how could she? And you are right to think it. Do not let anyone — do not let me, even in memory — talk you out of your anger. Anger is the honest answer. I am owed your anger, and I will collect it gladly if it keeps you from drowning in the other thing, the worse thing, which is understanding. Because here is why I did it, Sebastian, and I will tell it plain:*

*The winter your mother was born, the blight came down out of the high passes and took every field we had and half the cattle, and then it took people. Your grandfather first. Then your aunt. The village elders went up to the mountain to beg, the way people used to do in the old days, before everyone decided the court was a story to frighten children. And the court heard them. The Host received them in the glass roots and fed them honey-wine and asked, kindly, what their suffering was worth to them. And the Ledger-Keeper named the price: one soul of the petitioner's blood, freely given, payable when the moon completes its thirteenth turn from the signing.*

*I signed. They brought the contract down the mountain on a ribbon of living glass, and I pressed my thumb into it, and it sang when it took my mark — sang like a wet finger on a bowl's rim, one clear note, and I have heard that note in my dreams every night since. The blight lifted within the week. Your mother lived. Your father lived. You were born eight years later, in a green spring, in a world the court had bought back for us at a price I chose not to count.*

*I counted it later. Oh, I counted it later.*

The fire ticked. Somewhere far off on the ridge, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the sea, and Sebastian's skin prickled with the old reflex — the reach toward the storm, the gathering in the wrists — before the valley's dampening field pressed it flat, the way it had pressed everything flat for thirteen years. Even his magic was not allowed to grieve here.

He turned the page. Her handwriting had changed — smaller now, tighter, the letters crowded together as though she were trying to fit too much truth onto too little paper, or as though her hand had begun to shake.

*Here is the part I could not say while I lived, and I am sorry for it, because cowardice delayed is not courage, it is only debt with interest.*

*When I signed, I believed the court would choose the vessel themselves. That is what the Ledger-Keeper implied — that the debt would fall where the court willed it, and I would never know which grandchild or great-grandchild would pay it, and perhaps that mercy was its own cruelty, because not knowing let me sleep. But that is not what happened. What happened is that the contract asked me to name the line. To choose the branch of the family that would carry the debt. And I — *

There was a long gap in the writing, a smear where the ink had blurred, whether by water or by a trembling hand he could not tell. When the letters resumed they were very small.

*I chose your line. Your mother's line. Because your mother was strong, and your father was steady, and I told myself that strength and steadiness would be needed in whatever child grew up carrying this — that a hard fate should go to the ones best able to bear it. That was the lie I told. The true reason is smaller and uglier: your line was the line I loved most, and I wanted the debt to stay near me, where I could watch it, where I could spend my whole life trying to find a way to unsign what I had signed. Love does not always aim well, Sebastian. Sometimes love aims at the thing it cannot bear to lose, and calls the wound protection.*

*And then I found the way. Or I thought I did. In my last year, when my hands were failing and I knew the thirteenth turn was coming due, I went back up the mountain alone — this time not to petition but to bargain. I offered my own soul, freely given, in exchange for the release of the debt. An even trade: one soul of the blood for another soul of the blood. The Smiling One listened to the whole of it with that face that never changes, and then the Ledger-Keeper opened the book and showed me the clause, and I will write it here exactly as it stands, so that you will not waste your years hunting it as I wasted mine:*

*A soul may be substituted for a soul, but only by the seller, and only before the debt falls due. Once the turn comes, the vessel belongs to the court entire — body, breath, and name — and no trade, no plea, no payment shall alter it.*

*I was too late by eleven days. Eleven days, Sebastian. I have lain awake counting them the way a miser counts coins.*

*So I did the last thing left to me. If I could not free the debt, I could hide the debtor. I took the whistle — you will know it, bone, older than any kingdom, it hums when the Spire-folk come near — and I sent word into the world through channels I will not name, asking the old allies of my girlhood to watch for a child who drew spirals. And I wrote this letter, and I hid it where only you would ever find it, because you are the only one who would ever need to.*

*The girl's name is Lena. She will be small, and silent, and she will speak in her sleep in the tongue before kingdoms, because the Spire-folk dream in it and cannot help themselves. She is your blood, Sebastian — your mother's mother's line, doubled back through cousins the way mountain families double. She is the debt I made. She is also, I have come to believe in the nights when belief is possible, the best thing I ever made, though God knows I made her the way a flood makes a shoreline.*

*Do not hate her for what I did. Hate me. I can bear it; I am dead, and the dead are built for bearing. But do not give her to them. Whatever it costs you — and it will cost you everything, I know the shape of these bargains better than anyone living — do not walk her up that road on the thirteenth turn. Run. Hide her deeper than I hid you. Break the valley's truce if you must, and let your storms come home to you, and let every hunter in the lowlands come sniffing after the lightning. A marked man who is free is worth ten safe men who are hollow.*

*Or — and here is the thought I swore I would not write, and write it I must, because you deserve every tool even the terrible ones — or go up yourself. Go up before the turn. Face the three faces. Find the flaw. There is always a flaw; contracts are written by hands, and hands err, and pride makes them err in patterns. I failed to find it in one lifetime. Perhaps you will fail too. But you are storm-blooded, and storms do not recognize closed doors.*

*Forgive me. Forgive me or don't. I have written both possibilities at the bottom of this page and torn away the one I could not live with, and what remains is what remains.*

*Your grandmother, who loved you badly and completely,*
*Maren Okafor*

Sebastian sat for a long time without moving.

The coals settled. The wind on the ridge rose and fell. Above him, Lena turned over in her sleep, and the floorboards creaked under her small weight, and the sound went through him like a hook through cloth.

*She is your blood.*

He looked at his own hands in the firelight — broad, scarred, mender's hands, exile's hands — and tried to see the connection, the thread running backward through his mother and her mother and the cousins doubled like mountain paths, forward into a child of eight who drew spirals and slept in a dead language. He could not see it. Blood was not a thing the eye could find. But he felt it suddenly, the way you feel weather coming in an old scar: a pressure, a certainty, a dull ache where the joining was.

Lena was his kin. His grandmother had sold his kin. His grandmother had sold *him*, in the only way a person can sell someone not yet born — had reached down the years with a trembling thumb and pressed it into singing glass, and the note that glass had sung had echoed forward thirteen years to become a silent child knocking on his door in a storm.

The rage came up in him slowly, the way groundwater rises in a cellar — not dramatic, not sudden, just level after level of cold rising, filling rooms he had thought were empty. Rage at Maren, who had loved badly and completely and had said so herself, who had chosen his line because she loved it most, which meant that love itself had been the instrument of the harm. Rage at the court, whose kindness was arithmetic, whose faces smiled and tallied and hosted while children were weighed against harvests. Rage at the village below, sleeping its forgetting sleep, safe inside a truce paid for by other people's souls. Rage, most bitterly, at himself — thirteen years of mending roofs and refusing to remember, thirteen years of calling his loneliness peace, while somewhere in the world a child grew toward a reckoning he might have gone looking for if he had ever once opened this letter instead of hiding it in cedar and oilcloth and blue thread.

He had been ready to forgive her. He realized that now. He had spent years building a soft place in himself where her memory could rest — the woman who taught him cloud-names, who braided lightning into lullabies, whose hands smelled of rain and pipe-smoke. He had curated her. He had kept the songs and burned the silences.

And she had known he would. That was the cruelest intelligence in the whole letter — that she had written *do not let me, even in memory, talk you out of your anger.* She had foreseen her own sanctification and left a nail in it, deliberately, so that his grief could not smooth her into a saint. Even from the grave she refused to be easy to love. Even from the grave she was handing him the sharpest thing she owned.

A floorboard creaked overhead. Then another. Small feet, bare, moving to the ladder.

Sebastian folded the letter along its old creases with hands that were not quite steady, and he was still holding it when Lena's head appeared at the top of the ladder — hair loose, eyes heavy, the bone whistle clutched against her chest the way other children clutched blankets. She looked at him kneeling by the dead fire with papers in his hands, and something moved behind her eyes: not fear, not quite. Recognition, maybe. Children always know when a house has changed temperature.

She came down the ladder without a sound — she never made a sound on those rungs, as if gravity made exceptions for her — and stood beside him, and looked at the letter.

"Lena," he said. His voice came out rough, as if the rage had scoured it on its way up. "Come here."

She came. He drew her in against his side, and she allowed it — she had begun allowing it, in the last weeks, the way ice allows the thaw, grudgingly and then all at once. Her hair smelled of woodsmoke and the lavender Marta had put in her bath. Her heartbeat, against his ribs, was quick and light and utterly ordinary, a child's heartbeat, and that ordinariness undid him more than anything in the letter. This was what the court had priced. This breath, this warmth, this small stubborn animal aliveness. They had weighed it against fields and cattle and called the balance fair.

"I have to tell you something," he said. "And I'm going to tell it straight, because you've earned straight words, and because everyone else in this story has lied to you — before you could even walk."

She tilted her face up. In the dark her eyes caught the last coal-light, two embers' worth of attention.

"Before you were born," he said, "before your mother was born, a woman sold a soul to the court under the mountain. To buy her family's life. And the soul she sold—" His throat closed. He made himself go on, because she was watching, because she deserved the whole weight of it and not the softened version. "The soul she sold was yours. It was always going to be yours. She wrote it into a contract before you existed, and the moon has been walking toward that night ever since, and there are four turns of it left."

Lena did not pull away. That was the thing about her that continually astonished him — she absorbed blows the way deep water absorbs stones, without surface drama, everything sinking straight down to some floor he could not see. She looked at the letter in his hands for a long moment. Then she reached out and touched the paper — touched the signature, the faded blur where tears or a shaking hand had smeared the ink — and her brow furrowed, and she made a small sound in her throat. Not a word. A question.

"Yes," Sebastian said quietly. "It was my grandmother. My blood and yours, tangled together like roots. She's the one who sold you." He laughed, and there was nothing of laughter in it. "And she's the one who hid you, too. Both things are true. She spent her whole life trying to unsign what she signed, and she ran out of moons."

Lena studied him. Then, slowly, she raised one hand and laid it flat against his chest, over the sternum, the way she had done once before — the night the whistle sang, the night something far away had turned its attention toward the valley like an eye. She kept it there. And he understood that she was not comforting him. She was *checking*. Listening to whatever she listened to, in whatever language she listened in, for the thing underneath his words — the rage, the grief, the inheritance — to see if it was aimed at her.

"It isn't at you," he said. "None of it. Hear me. Whatever happens on that mountain, whatever the ledger says, whoever's thumbprint is on that glass — you are not a debt. You're a girl. Debts are numbers. You are not a number."

Something shifted in her face — the smallest loosening, a knot giving way a single degree. She withdrew her hand, stepped back, and looked at him with an expression he had learned to read the way sailors read a horizon: trouble assembling somewhere beyond sight.

Then she pointed. At the letter. At the window, and the black ridge beyond it where the mountain stood invisible in the dark. At herself. Back at him.

"No," Sebastian said, before he fully knew what he was refusing. "Not yet. Four turns, Lena. We have time to think—"

She stamped her foot — the first flash of temper he had ever seen in her, small and fierce and human — and pointed again, harder, jabbing the air between his chest and the dark window as if she were sewing the two together with her finger. *You. Them. Me. Now.* Her eyes were blazing, and her mouth worked silently around words she would not or could not spend, and then she did the thing she had done only once before, the thing that had cracked his heart open the first time:

She spoke. One word, in the tongue before kingdoms, low and resonant, a word that seemed to make the coals lean in their bed.

He didn't know its meaning. But he knew its tone. Everyone knew that tone, in every language there had ever been. It was the tone of *now*.

Outside, far to the north, past the ridge, past the boundary of the valley's quiet — thunder rolled. Long, low, patient. The kind of thunder that was not weather.

The kind that answered a call.

Sebastian looked at the letter in his hands, at his grandmother's final smudged line, at the child standing rigid with urgency in the fire-dark, and felt the last of his thirteen peaceful years close behind him like a door.

"We leave tomorrow," he said. "Before dawn. Get your shoes."

Lena was already climbing for the loft.
