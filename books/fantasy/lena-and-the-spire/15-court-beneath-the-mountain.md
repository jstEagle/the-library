## 15. Court Beneath the Mountain

The door in the mountainside was not a door.

That was my first thought, standing before it with Lena's small hand cold in mine and the river's toll still aching behind my eyes — a smooth-edged absence where my wedding day had been, like a tooth gone from a familiar mouth. The entrance to the Court beneath the mountain was an arch of black stone that had never been carved, because stone does not grow in arches, and yet this one had grown, the way a tree grows around a fence post, swallowing the shape of something older. No hinges. No gate. Only a darkness so complete it seemed less like shadow than like soil — the dark of somewhere things were meant to take root.

Lena stopped at the threshold and would not go further.

She did not pull away from me. She simply planted her feet on the wet granite, and her grip tightened until her knuckles went white, and she looked up at me with an expression I had learned to read over these weeks of silence made speech: *I know this place. I know what waits inside it.*

"We can still turn back," I said, though we both knew we couldn't. The twelfth turn was waning above us, a fingernail of moon caught between two peaks. Four nights remained. Perhaps fewer. The Court had already shown us it did not keep honest time.

She looked at the darkness for a long moment. Then she raised her free hand and pressed it flat against the black stone, the way you might touch the flank of a horse you feared but intended to ride anyway.

*It knows we're here,* she signed, then spoke aloud in the old tongue, her voice thin as thread in the mountain cold. "It has known since the ford."

"I know," I said. And I did know. That was the worst of it. From the moment I had turned those masked riders back with a sliver of hoarded storm — from the moment the valley's truce broke and my magic came flooding home to me like a dog that had waited thirteen years at a gate — I had felt it: a attention, vast and patient, turning toward us the way a sleeper turns toward a sound. Not pursuit. Not yet. Something worse. *Expectation.*

"Stay close," I said. "Say nothing unless I ask. If anything happens—"

She squeezed my hand once, hard, cutting off the instruction. She had heard all my instructions. She had memorized them, I suspected, the way she memorized everything — the spiral mark on her wrist, the songs in her sleep, the whistle humming against her chest even now with no wind to move it. Lena did not need my contingencies. She needed only to know that I meant them.

We stepped through together.

* * *

The dark took us gently, which frightened me more than any violence could have.

There was no passage, no tunnel, no winding descent such as the miners' tales promised. There was simply a moment when the night closed around us like water, and then a moment more when light began — and the light was wrong, or rather, it was right in a way no surface light ever is. It came from below. It came from everywhere below, rising, soft as lamplight through honey, and by it I saw what we stood upon.

Glass roots.

They ran beneath our feet like the veins of some buried leviathan — great translucent channels, thick as a man's waist in places, branching and rebranching downward into depths the light could not plumb. They glowed faintly from within, a warm gold shot through with threads of green and violet, and where they crossed they knotted themselves into structures — walls, buttresses, stairways — as if the whole mountain's interior had been grown rather than built, cultivated over centuries by hands patient enough to garden stone. When I shifted my weight, the root beneath my boot sang. A low, clear note, like a finger run round the rim of a cup.

Lena crouched and laid her palm against it. Her lips moved without sound. Reading it, I realized. Or listening.

"It says welcome," she translated quietly, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone translating a threat into politeness. "It says the Court has been waiting a long time for guests."

"Guests," I repeated.

The word echoed strangely. Not because the space was vast — though it was; when I lifted my eyes I saw the roots arcing overhead in vaults and ribs, a cathedral grown rather than raised — but because the echo came back *warmed*. As if the mountain itself had taken my suspicion and returned it softened, sweetened, made agreeable. I had spent thirteen years in a valley that ate echoes. I knew what honest silence sounded like. This was not it.

We descended. There was no choice about it; the path of glass roots sloped always downward, broadening, and the light grew richer, and the air grew warmer, and smells began to reach us that had no business being real — woodsmoke, bread, cut grass, rain on hot stone. The smells of the surface. Of home. Someone, or something, had decided what we would want to smell, and had arranged it.

And there were moths.

They came up out of the deep in slow spirals, hundreds of them, thousands, wings wide as my spread hand and pale as paper, each one carrying its own small lantern of light in the hollow of its body. They did not flutter the way surface moths do, frantic and blind. These flew with intention, in processions, in patterns — and as we walked, the patterns rearranged themselves around us, forming corridors of light, arches of light, and once — briefly, before I could decide whether I'd imagined it — the shape of a doorway held open in welcome.

"They're beautiful," I said, because Lena was looking at them with an expression I couldn't parse, and because saying nothing felt more dangerous than saying the wrong thing.

"They're servants," Lena said. "Or they used to be." She watched one settle on a root beside her path, folding its luminous wings. "Grandmother Ashen — the woman who kept me, before — she said the moths remember everyone who comes down. Every guest. Every payment." Her eyes came up to mine. "They'll remember you, Sebastian. Even after everything else forgets."

I thought of the river, and the smooth absence where my wedding day had been, and said nothing.

* * *

The city opened below us all at once, the way a valley opens when you crest a ridge — except that here the ridge was inside the mountain, and the valley was a hollow the size of a drowned cathedral, and every part of it was alive with light.

Glass roots cascaded down the far walls like frozen waterfalls. Terraces had been grown along their lengths, and on the terraces stood buildings — or things shaped like buildings, their walls half-glass, half-black stone, hung with lanterns that were not lanterns but clusters of dormant moths, sleeping in chandeliers. Streets wound between them, paved in root-wood polished smooth by generations of feet. And everywhere the moths rose and fell in their slow processions, so that the whole city seemed to breathe light — inhaling gold, exhaling violet — a thing not inhabited so much as *ensouled*.

People moved through it. That was the detail my mind kept snagging on. People — men and women in fine wool and stranger fabrics, children running errands, vendors at stalls selling fruit I didn't recognize — living ordinary lives four hundred fathoms under stone, under a sky of glowing root. None of them looked up at us. None of them looked at each other, quite. Their faces were pleasant. Their movements were smooth. And it took me the length of a street to understand what was wrong, and when I understood it, my stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking water.

No one was hurrying. In a city of any size, on any road, at any hour, someone is always hurrying. Here, no one was. Everyone moved at exactly the same unrushed pace, as if the whole population shared a single heartbeat and none of them wished to be caught out of step with it.

Lena saw it too. Her hand found mine again, and this time she was the one gripping.

"Don't look at them long," she murmured. "Some of them are vessels. The ones that were partly taken. If you look at them long, they look back, and—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It isn't good to be looked at by them."

A woman at a fruit stall smiled at us as we passed. It was a lovely smile. It arrived all at once, fully formed, like a mask being donned, and it did not waver or vary as we went by, and I made myself look away before I could see whether it stayed on her face after we'd passed.

"The Host will want to greet us himself," Lena said. "He always greets new arrivals. It's considered an honor." Her jaw was tight. "It isn't."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I lived here." She said it simply, without drama, the way you'd say *I lived near the sea*, and the simplicity of it was the cruelest part. "Before they chose me for the thirteenth turn. Before I ran. I was four when I came down. I don't remember the sky from then. I only remember the light that comes from underneath."

I stopped walking. Around us the moths adjusted their patterns, patient, accommodating, closing the corridor of light behind us and opening it ahead, herding us gently forward without ever seeming to push.

"Lena." I crouched to her height, heedless of who might see. "If there is any part of this place that frightens you beyond bearing — any door we should not pass, any face you cannot look at — you tell me. Sign it, whisper it, draw it in the dust. I don't care how. But you tell me. We survive this together or not at all, and I will not have you carrying the map alone because you think it protects me."

Something moved across her face — fear, yes, but under it something fiercer, something that had carried an eight-year-old girl out of this mountain and across a country of hunters to a stranger's door. She nodded once.

Then she pointed, past my shoulder, down the great central avenue of the city, to where the glass roots converged.

"They're coming," she said. "Both of them. The Host and the Ledger-Keeper. The Smiling One doesn't come to gates." A pause, and then, quieter, with the weariness of someone much older than eight: "The Smiling One comes when he wants something."

* * *

They came in state, though their state was quiet.

The Host was tall — taller than any man I'd met on the surface, and lean in the way of things grown rather than built, his limbs a little too long, his proportions a little too graceful, as if he had been designed by someone working from a description of a man rather than a man himself. He wore robes the color of deep water, and his face was kind. That was the first thing, and the most terrible thing: his face was genuinely, warmly kind, the face of a beloved uncle, of a physician come with good news, and it did not flicker or perform. Whatever else the Court was, the kindness on the Host's face was real, and I understood in that moment that this was the most dangerous thing about him.

Beside him walked a smaller figure, wrapped in grey, whose face was hidden behind a visor of smoked glass — the Ledger-Keeper, I knew without being told, because in one gloved hand the figure carried a book. The book was large as a paving stone, bound in something pale, and it hummed. Not metaphorically. It hummed, a chord of many voices, very faint, and I recognized the quality of that sound with a lurch, because I had heard it before — in the archive-dreams Lena described, in the singing of the bone whistle, in the glass roots underfoot. The sound of contained souls. The book was full of them. The book was *made* of them.

Behind them came six moth-lantern bearers in silver, walking in perfect step.

"Sebastian Okafor." The Host's voice was rich and unhurried, and he pronounced my name carefully, savoring it, the way one savors a vintage brought up from a cellar. "Storm-caller. Son of Adaeze. Grandson of Yewande Coldharbour, of the line that bargained." He spread his long hands. "Welcome. Welcome beneath the mountain. You honor us."

Every instinct I owned screamed at once. *You honor us.* Thirteen years of exile, of hunters at my heels, of a name spoken only in whispers — and the first words the Court gave me were words of gratitude, delivered on a carpet of light, in a city dressed in my favorite smells.

"You expected us," I said.

"Of course." The Host smiled, and the smile reached all the way to his eyes, which was somehow worse. "You crossed the River That Remembers. You paid the toll. Do you imagine, storm-caller, that a soul passing our river goes unremarked? The moment your memory touched the water, the roots sang your arrival from the ford to this very square." He gestured gracefully at the glowing ground. "We have been setting places for you since Tuesday. Though I suppose you've lost track of the days. The surface does that to people. Makes them feel time is theirs." His head tilted, birdlike, gentle. "Time was never yours, Sebastian Okafor. It was only lent."

Beside me, Lena had gone very still. I put my hand on her shoulder and felt her trembling, and felt, too, the effort she was making to stop it.

"She eats first," I said. "And sleeps. Whatever business you believe you have with us, it keeps until she has eaten and slept. I did not walk out of exile and under a mountain to negotiate with a child on an empty stomach."

Silence. Somewhere in the vaulted dark above, a thousand moths shifted their pattern, and the light of the whole city changed color for a heartbeat — gold to violet and back — like a great eye blinking.

And then the Host laughed. It was a wonderful laugh, warm and genuine, and it terrified me more than anything I had seen since the arch.

"Oh, *good*," he said, pressing his long hands together in delight. "Oh, that is *good*. A grandmother sells a soul and the grandson arrives making demands on its behalf. Yewande, you sly old storm — you knew, didn't you? You knew what blood would come down here after your debt." He shook his head fondly, as if remembering an old friend. "Yes. Yes, of course. Eat, sleep, rest. The Guesthouse of Roots has been prepared — the best chambers, the ones with the skylights of living glass. Anything you require, you have only to ask and it is already done." He stepped aside, sweeping his arm toward a side avenue where a procession of moth-bearers had already assembled, waiting, as if they had known the shape of my demand before I made it.

Because they had. That was the message under all of it, warm as the bread-smell, bright as the lantern-light: *we knew, we know, we will always know.*

The Ledger-Keeper had not spoken. As we turned to follow the bearers, the grey figure opened the great humming book across both gloved arms, and the smoked-glass visor tilted down toward its pages, and I heard — clearly, unmistakably — a new line being written, the scratch of a pen that sounded almost like a sigh.

"One matter," the Host called softly, just as we reached the edge of the light.

I turned.

His kind face was kind still. Kinder, if anything. He looked at me the way a gardener looks at a seedling that has finally broken soil after a long winter.

"The Smiling One asks that you join him tomorrow evening," he said. "Alone." His gaze slid, brief and gentle, to Lena, and back. "He so rarely extends the invitation twice."

* * *

The chamber they gave us was beautiful, and that was the last straw.

It was grown, not built — a room of curved glass-root walls with a ceiling of living lattice through which the city's light filtered down in shifting gold, and two beds heaped with furs, and a table set with food I recognized from no market and every dream: dark bread still warm, honey comb, pears, a stew that smelled like the one Marta made on festival nights. On the table beside the food lay a folded cloth, and on the cloth lay a child's nightdress, sized for Lena, sewn in the style of the Spire-folk, in the exact faded blue of the one she'd been wearing the night she appeared soaked at my door.

They had studied her. They had studied *us*. Every comfort offered was a demonstration: *we see you, we know you, resistance is a formality.*

Lena stood in the middle of the beautiful room and did not touch any of it. She looked at the nightdress for a long time. Then she picked it up, deliberately, and folded it back along its original creases, and set it in the corner, face-down, the way you turn a photograph to the wall.

"Eat," she said to me, pushing the bowl of stew toward my hands. "Sleep if you can. I'll watch first." She climbed onto the window-side bed and sat with her back straight and the bone whistle in her lap, and her eyes on the door.

I ate, because she was right, and because refusing their food would cost strength I would need. The stew tasted exactly like Marta's. That was the point. I ate it anyway.

The light in the lattice ceiling dimmed by degrees, courteous as dusk, and the sounds of the city settled into a hush, and still Lena watched the door. Once, near midnight by the ache in my bones, she spoke without turning around, her voice very small in the beautiful room.

"He's going to offer you something tomorrow. The Smiling One. He offers everyone something." Her fingers found the whistle's holes, resting there without blowing. "Whatever it is — whatever he shows you, whatever he promises — remember that he smiles because smiling works. Not because he's happy."

Below us, faint through the living floor, the glass roots sang their endless chord of welcome.

And somewhere deeper than that — deeper than the city, deeper than the archive I could feel humming under everything like a heart under ribs — something laughed softly, once, and was quiet.
