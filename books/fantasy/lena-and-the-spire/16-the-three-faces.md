## 16. The Three Faces

They were led down through the palace of glass roots as honored guests, and that was the worst of it.

Sebastian had expected chains. He had expected the cold arithmetic of guards with lantern-moths swarming their shoulders, the clink of iron, a cell carved from living stone. Instead a servant — one of the moth-people, its face smooth as river stone and faintly luminous, its hands too many-jointed to be anything but kind in their precision — bowed them into a corridor of translucent root-glass and said, in a voice like wind through wheat: *The Court will see you now. Please walk slowly. The roots are listening.*

Lena's hand found his. Her fingers were cold, but her grip was certain. She had not spoken since they entered the city beneath the mountain, and Sebastian understood why. This place was made of her language. Every archway hummed with the old tongue, every lantern-moth's wingbeat carried a syllable of it, and the whole vast dark seemed to breathe in sentences she alone could parse. She was not silent from fear. She was silent the way a person is silent when walking through a house where every wall is whispering her name.

The audience chamber opened around them like the inside of a bell.

It was enormous — a cavern ribbed with roots of glass that rose from the floor and branched against the ceiling, each one filled with slow-moving light the color of honey and bruise. Lantern-moths drifted between them in lazy constellations. The air smelled of warm wax and turned earth and something older underneath, something like the inside of a sealed letter. And at the center, on three thrones grown rather than built — the glass had curled up out of the floor like saplings and hardened into seats — sat the three faces of the Mountain Court.

Sebastian had heard stories, of course. Everyone who traveled the trade roads had heard stories. But the stories had prepared him for monsters, and what he saw instead was worse, because it was almost ordinary.

The first face was an old man, or shaped like one, robed in grey wool with a hearth-smoke smell that reached Sebastian even across the distance. His features were soft, grandfatherly, arranged around an expression of such genuine welcome that Sebastian's chest ached with the memory of kitchens he'd eaten in during his first year of exile, before the villagers learned to keep their doors shut. This, he knew without being told, was the Host.

The second face was neither old nor young, neither man nor woman — a figure of neat proportions wrapped in a robe covered edge to edge in writing so small it looked at a distance like texture, like moleskin, like frost on a window. Its eyes were the eyes of someone reading. Even now, looking at them, Sebastian felt *read* — measured, weighed, entered into a column. The Ledger-Keeper did not smile. It simply attended.

And the third —

The third face was beautiful, and it was smiling, and the smile was the problem. It sat upon a young body dressed plainly, almost humbly, in undyed linen, with dark hair loose about the shoulders, and its smile was warm and wide and perfectly, precisely calibrated, like an instrument tuned to the exact frequency of trust. The Smiling One looked at Lena the way a gardener looks at a fruit nearly ripe.

*Come closer,* said the Host, and his voice was every voice that had ever said *sit down, child, you look tired.* *You've walked far, and the road above is cruel this season. We have soup.*

Sebastian did not move. "We're not staying."

"Of course you are," said the Host, mildly, as if Sebastian had agreed. "Everyone stays. That is rather the point of a court."

A moth-servant glided forward bearing a tray — actual soup, steaming, smelling of leeks and barley and something sweetly spiced, the kind of food that existed only in memory or in places like this. Lena stared at it. Sebastian stepped slightly in front of her.

"We came to talk about the contract," he said. "Nothing else."

Something flickered across the Host's face — not anger, never anger; something more like a father's fond disappointment — and then the warmth returned, undimmed, as though it had never left. "Very well. Business first. Hospitality after. Though I'll warn you, stranger, we are very good at hospitality. It has been our ruin more than once." He gestured, and the glass roots overhead dimmed by degrees until the light gathered low, intimate, the light of a fireside rather than a throne room. "Ask your questions."

"The thirteenth turn," Sebastian said. "Four weeks. You mean to take the child."

"Take." The Ledger-Keeper spoke for the first time, and its voice was the sound of a quill crossing paper, dry and exact. "An imprecise verb. We *receive*. What is owed is delivered. There is no taking in it."

"A soul was sold," the Host said gently. "Thirteen years ago, by a woman of the storm-line, in exchange for the survival of her blood. The terms were standard. The vessel would live safe and hidden until the moon completed thirteen turns from the signing — long enough for her to grow, to be loved, to be a person and not merely a payment. Then she comes below, and the debt is settled, and her family's line stands clear forever." He spread his soft hands. "Where is the cruelty in that? Tell me where, precisely, and I will amend the practice tomorrow."

Sebastian's jaw ached. He realized he was grinding his teeth. "She's a child."

"She is a *term*," said the Ledger-Keeper, without malice, which was somehow infinitely worse than malice. "Terms do not suffer. Terms are fulfilled. You are imagining cruelty because you are imagining a person being harmed. No person is harmed. A vessel is emptied — painlessly, I assure you; the extraction is gentler than sleep — and the soul within is entered into the archive, where it sings in perpetuity, preserved beyond decay, beyond grief, beyond the indignities of flesh. Mortals call it death when it happens slowly. We have merely made it punctual."

"And the hollowed villages on the road," Sebastian said. "The ones full of smiling people who don't remember their children's names. Was that punctual too?"

For the first time, something moved behind the Ledger-Keeper's reading eyes. Not guilt. Recalculation. "Partial extractions," it said. "Debts settled in installments. The village of Ashenmoor sold its surplus — souls beyond what each household needed to remain itself. They are not hollowed. They are *thriftier*." It tilted its head. "You disapprove."

"I—" Sebastian stopped. Rage was rising in him like weather, and there was no storm here to carry it, no sky to throw it at, only these three patient faces and the humming glass. He made himself breathe. Made himself think the way he'd thought in the valley for thirteen years: slowly, carefully, mending one shingle at a time. "I want to see the contract."

"Why?" asked the Smiling One.

It was the first word the third face had spoken, and the room changed around it. The lantern-moths stilled mid-drift. The honeyed light in the roots dimmed a shade deeper. The Host's warmth and the Ledger-Keeper's precision both bent subtly toward the Smiling One, the way water bends toward a drain.

Sebastian met the smile and held it. "Because contracts can be read."

"They can," the Smiling One agreed, delighted, as if Sebastian had performed a clever trick. "That is their tragedy and their beauty. Everything is written. Nothing is hidden. We are not the villains of your road-stories, storm-caller — we have never once lied to anyone. Lying is so *crude*. We simply write, and wait, and let mortals sign." It rose from its throne — a small motion, unhurried — and the glass roots nearest it leaned away, very slightly, like grass before wind. "Bring the book," it said to the air, and the air obeyed.

What came was not carried. It grew. From the floor beside the Ledger-Keeper's throne a root of glass swelled upward, unfurling leaf after leaf of thin translucent page, and on those pages writing surfaced like fish rising in a pond — dense, minute, immaculate. The Ledger-Keeper extended one neat hand and a single leaf detached and drifted across the chamber into Sebastian's grasp.

He read.

The script was the old tongue, and he could read it — badly, laboriously, the way his grandmother had taught him before he understood what the lessons were *for*, before the songs she sang over the wash-basin resolved into clauses. The letters swam and steadied. He read aloud, slowly, translating as he went, because he wanted the words in the open air where Lena could hear them too:

*"In the year of the long frost, Maren Coldharbour, called the Storm-Singer, of sound mind and desperate circumstance, does sell unto the Court Beneath the Mountain one soul of her bloodline, unnamed at signing, to be named by descent: the first-born vessel of the fourth generation, to be delivered upon the completion of the moon's thirteenth turn, in consideration whereof the said bloodline shall be preserved, protected, and passed over by all famine, war, and fever..."*

"Preserved," Sebastian said. "Protected. Passed over." He looked up. "My mother lived through the fever-year. My uncle walked through three wars untouched. I thought we were lucky."

"You were *paid*," said the Ledger-Keeper.

"And the fourth generation—"

"—is the girl," the Smiling One finished softly. "Yes. Your grandmother was precise. She specified descent, not identity. She could not know which grandchild the debt would land upon. Neither could we. The contract named a vessel; the world supplied one." The smile deepened by a degree, and Sebastian felt it like a change in pressure. "Fate is very obliging to paperwork."

Lena tugged his sleeve. When he glanced down, she was looking at the leaf, and her lips were moving — reading it faster than he ever could, fluently, natively, her finger tracing clause after clause. Then she stopped. Her finger rested on a line near the bottom, and she looked up at him, and her eyes were wide and fierce and wet all at once, and she tapped the line twice.

Sebastian bent over the page. The script there was older, cramped, written in a different hand — amended, initialed, initialed again. He read it three times before the meaning broke over him like cold water:

*"Should the seller, prior to delivery, freely release the sold soul back unto itself, the sale is void and all consideration forfeit."*

His heart began to hammer. "This clause. Who wrote it?"

"The seller," said the Host, and for the first time the warmth in his voice thinned, just barely, like ice over moving water. "Your grandmother insisted upon it. She wept, actually. Very dramatic. We allow sellers their amendments — it costs us nothing, because no one ever uses them." He sighed, a great gusty, disappointed sigh. "Do you know how many mortals have stood where you stand, storm-caller, in thirteen hundred years, and demanded to read the contract? Hundreds. Do you know how many found a way out in the text?" He held up one soft hand and closed it into a fist. "None. Because the way out always requires giving back something more precious than what was bought, and mortals, given the choice between another's freedom and their own comfort, choose comfort. Every time. It is the most reliable law we know."

"Then you understand my position," Sebastian said quietly. "I've read it. I intend to use it."

The silence that followed was total. Even the roots stopped humming. Three faces regarded him — one with pity, one with calculation, one with that unbearable smile — and somewhere far above, muffled by miles of stone, thunder rolled. Sebastian felt it in his teeth. Felt it *answer* him, faintly, the way a dog answers a whistle only it can hear. His storms had come back to him at the valley's edge and they had not left; they sat in his blood now like a second heartbeat, and the mountain's deep stone carried their voices down to him, and he wondered — not for the first time since crossing the boundary — whether the Court had chosen this chamber for exactly that reason.

"You cannot void it," the Ledger-Keeper said at last, returning to its papers. "The clause requires the seller's free release. Maren Coldharbour is dead these nine years. The dead cannot release."

"Can't they?"

The Host's eyebrows rose, kind and terrible. "Ohh," he breathed. "Oh, you *are* your grandmother's grandson. She had that same look. That look that says *there is a door here and I will find it if I have to wear my hands to bone.*" He shook his head slowly. "Listen to me, Sebastian Okafor. Exile. Storm-caller. Last of the line that signed. Hear an old host's honest counsel, offered freely, which is rarer under this mountain than gold." He leaned forward on his throne of grown glass, and the firelight of the roots caught the tears standing unashamed in his eyes — real tears, Sebastian was almost certain, and that certainty frightened him more than any threat could have. "The turn comes in four weeks whether you find your door or not. Stay with us until then. Eat our food. Sleep in our guest-hall. Let the girl see the gardens — she is Spire-born; she has never seen the gardens meant for her. Perhaps, given time, given kindness, she will walk down willingly on the appointed night, and spare everyone the ugliness of collection. Willing vessels sing sweeter. Everyone says so."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no," said the Host, spreading his hands, wounded and forgiving in the same gesture. "And we wait four weeks, and then we stop waiting. We are very patient, storm-caller. Patience is the oldest magic there is, and unlike yours, ours never burns out."

The Smiling One laughed — a bright, genuine, charming laugh — and clapped its hands once, and the lantern-moths resumed their drifting as though a spell had broken. "Enough! You'll frighten our guests, Host, with all this doom spoken gently. The man has had a long road and a hard read, and the child hasn't touched her soup." It descended from its throne and crossed the chamber toward them, unhurried, and Sebastian put his arm out and drew Lena behind him, and the Smiling One stopped exactly at the edge of his reach and inclined its head, gracious, amused, as if he'd shown it a dance step. "Guest-hall, east root, third gallery," it said to the air, and a moth-servant materialized at Sebastian's elbow, bowing. "You will want for nothing. Truly nothing. That is not a threat, whatever your valley instincts tell you. It is simply the truth about us: we give everything, and everything is given *on account*."

It turned to go, then paused, and looked back over its shoulder at Lena — only at Lena — and its smile softened into something that looked, for one vertiginous moment, almost like tenderness.

"Sleep well, little vessel," it said in the old tongue, the language before kingdoms, the language of her dead people. "The mountain has missed your voice."

Then the three faces faded back into the root-light, thrones and all, as though they had never been more than reflections in glass, and the moth-servant touched Sebastian's arm with its many-jointed hand and gestured gently toward the east gallery, and there was nothing to do but follow.

* * *

The guest-hall was beautiful, and Sebastian hated it with his whole body.

Their chambers opened off a gallery of pale root-glass hung with woven lights. The beds were deep and warm. Water ran cold and clean from a spout carved as a sleeping bird. On the table waited bread still warm from an oven somewhere, fruit out of season, a small pot of honey, and two cups of milk — one sized for a child. Someone had laid out clothes folded with obsessive care. Someone had placed, on the sill of the one window (a window looking out onto darkness and the far glow of the lantern-fields), a single sprig of some flowering thing that did not grow beneath mountains, fresh as morning.

Everything wanted to be loved. That was the trap of it. Every object in the room was a small argument for staying.

Lena stood in the middle of the chamber, turning slowly, cataloguing it all with Spire-born eyes. Then she walked to the window, picked up the flower, considered it, and set it outside the door on the stones of the gallery — deliberately, neatly, the way one returns a borrowed thing.

She came back to Sebastian and took his hand, and signed against his palm, quick and sure, the private grammar they'd built on the road: *Safe?*

"No," he said honestly. "But we're alive, and we're together, and I read the clause." He crouched to her height, holding both her hands now. "Your grandmother's clause — Maren's clause. A soul freely released voids the sale. The Ledger-Keeper says it can't be done, because the seller is dead." He watched her face. "But I heard singing in the archive-road as we came down. Did you? Glass that sings?"

Her breath caught. She nodded once.

"Maren sold more than she told anyone," Sebastian said slowly, feeling the shape of it assemble in the dark, clause by clause, the way a storm assembles on a ridge. "She was desperate, but she was careful — careful enough to write herself an exit into a contract with the Court. People like that don't leave one door. They leave two." He squeezed her hands. "Tomorrow I find the archive. Tonight—" he glanced at the too-comfortable bed, the too-kind food, the listening walls, "—tonight we sleep in shifts, and we touch nothing we're given, and we remember that hospitality is just a ledger with better manners."

Lena smiled — a small, sharp, entirely un-hollowed smile — and signed one last word against his palm.

*Storm?*

"Not yet," Sebastian murmured, and felt, far above them through miles of patient stone, the thunder answer anyway — soft, distant, waiting, like a dog at a door it knows will open. "Soon."

Out in the gallery, past their door, the sprig of mountain-flower lay on the cold glass stones. And as the lights dimmed for sleep, its petals turned — slowly, without wind — to face the room where the vessel lay, and kept watch.
