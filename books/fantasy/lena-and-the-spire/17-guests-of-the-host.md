## 17. Guests of the Host

The palace beneath the mountain did not sleep, and neither, it seemed, did its kindness.

I woke on the second morning in a bed too soft to be trusted. In the valley, beds were straw and wool and the smell of Sebastian's woodsmoke baked into the blankets. Here the mattress sighed when I moved, as if it had been waiting all night for the chance to comfort me, and the linens smelled of something sweet I could not name — not flowers exactly, more like the memory of flowers, pressed flat and kept in a book until only their ghost remained.

A dress had been laid out at the foot of the bed. White wool, fine-woven, with silver thread at the collar worked into spirals. My spiral. The mark that coiled up my arm like a tower seen from above, like the whistle's song made visible. Someone had measured me while I slept, or known me before I ever arrived, and both possibilities sat in my stomach like swallowed stones.

There was food on the table by the window. There was always food on the table by the window.

I ate because refusing felt dangerous in a way I couldn't explain, and because the bread was warm, and warmth is hard to argue with when you are eight years old and your whole world has been a road. Honeyed bread. Fruit from some season that didn't exist under the mountain — pale gold pears that tasted of sunlight remembered rather than sunlight lived. A cup of milk so fresh it must have been poured minutes ago, though I had heard no cow, no cart, no door.

That was the trick of the place, I understood even then, in the wordless way children understand things. Everything arrived as if wished for. And wishes, in the court beneath the mountain, were not free. They were invoices written in a hand too beautiful to read.

* * *

Sebastian was already awake when I found him in our rooms — *our* rooms, they had given us two chambers joined by an arch hung with lantern-moths, as if we were honored guests and not what we both knew we were. He stood at the arch with his arms crossed, watching the moths drift through their slow orbits. They were lovely the way deep water is lovely: blue-green wings, bodies like drops of amber, and inside each body a small light that pulsed in time with no heart I could hear.

"They never land," he said quietly, when he saw me. "Did you notice? They fly all night and they never once land."

I hadn't noticed. Now I would never stop noticing.

He crouched to my height. Thirteen days on the road had taught me his face the way I'd learned the valley's paths — every crease mapped, every weather. This morning his weather was storm-colored. "Lena." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "Last night I heard someone walking in the corridor. Slow steps, back and forth, back and forth. When I opened the door, there was no one. But the stones were still warm."

I touched his hand. It was the language we'd built between us, his words and my hands, and it had carried us across a kingdom's worth of distance. *I know,* I signed against his knuckles. *They are being kind at us.*

He almost smiled. Almost. "Kind *at* us. That's exactly it." He straightened, and his jaw did the thing it did when he was deciding to be brave instead of afraid. "We're guests, then. Let's be good ones. Good guests learn where the doors are."

So we went walking.

* * *

The palace grew out of the mountain the way roots grow out of soil — which is to say, it *was* roots. Great columns of glass, smooth and faintly green, rose from the floors and branched across the ceilings, and between them the walls were stone worn soft by centuries of lantern-light. Everything curved. Nothing in the palace met at a right angle; corners bloomed instead of ending, corridors widened like throats opening to swallow. Walking through it felt like walking through the inside of a shell, and I kept waiting for the sea-sound, for the great pull of something drawing us deeper.

The Host's servants found us within moments of leaving our rooms. Of course they did. They were moth-kin — small, quick people with dust-pale skin and eyes that caught the light like the insects they tended — and they materialized out of side passages with trays and towels and small courtesies, bowing, always bowing, their faces arranged in expressions of gentle welcome that never quite reached their eyes. Their eyes stayed busy. Their eyes counted.

"Would the little lady care to see the gardens?" one asked me directly, kneeling so we were eye to eye. Her voice was kind. Her hands were steady. Only the pulse at her throat betrayed her, quick as a bird's. "The Court thought — that is, the Host thought — a child might find the glass gardens pleasing. Other children dwell here, lady. Companions, if she should want them."

Other children.

I looked at Sebastian. He was looking at the servant with an expression I'd seen him wear at Marta's inn when a stranger asked one question too many about where we'd come from. But he knelt beside me and said, "Would you like that, Lena?" and underneath the words, in the pressure of his hand on my shoulder, he said the other thing too: *If you go, I go. If you run, I run.*

I nodded. Because I wanted to see them — the other children, the ones like me, the vessels-in-waiting whose existence I had fled down a mountainside to escape. And because I wanted to know what they knew. Prisoners who share a cage share everything else.

* * *

The gardens were real, in the way nothing else in the palace was quite real. A cavern open to some fissure in the mountain's shoulder, where a thread of true daylight fell through a crack far overhead and struck a floor of pale sand. Things grew there — white-rooted trees with leaves like blown glass, flowers that chimed faintly when the air moved, moss that glowed a soft green-gold along the stones. Lantern-moths drifted among the branches but would not cross into the daylight column, circling its edge like moths around a flame they both loved and feared.

And there were children in the garden. Seven of them.

They were playing. That was the first thing I saw, and for one bright moment my heart lifted — children, playing, running through the sand and the light, laughing —

Except they weren't laughing.

They were moving. That was the accurate thing. One boy rolled a glass ball along a groove in the sand, over and over, the same groove, the same speed, his face smooth as the ball itself. Two girls wove flower stems into crowns with fingers that never fumbled and never hurried, and when a crown was finished they set it down in a row — there were already dozens of crowns in the row, wilting and fresh together — and began another. A smaller child sat beneath a glass tree turning a smooth stone in circles on her palm, around and around, her eyes fixed on the middle distance where nothing was.

None of them looked up when we entered. None of them looked at anything, really. Their eyes were open and their bodies moved and somewhere behind all of it, the wanting had been taken out, the way you take a bone out of a fish and the fish still holds its shape.

I had run down a mountain to escape becoming hollow. I had not known hollow had a face. Now I was looking at seven of them, and every one of those faces was calm, and fed, and dressed in white wool with silver spirals at the collar, and safe, and empty.

The smallest girl looked up. Just her eyes, not her head — the stone kept turning in her palm. Her gaze passed over Sebastian without pausing, the way water passes over a rock. Then it reached me, and stopped.

She saw the spiral on my arm where my sleeve had slipped. She saw the bone whistle hanging at my throat. Something moved behind her eyes — not light, not yet, but the memory of light, the shape a fire leaves on the wall after it goes out.

Her lips formed a word. No sound came. But I knew the tongue. I had spoken it in my sleep before I ever spoke it waking; it was the language before kingdoms, the Spire-folk's tongue, the one Sebastian's grandmother had sung in secret and I had been born carrying like a stone in my chest.

*Run*, her mouth said. Or maybe: *run while*. The old tongue was like that — one word holding three meanings, depending on how much of your soul was left to hear them.

Then her eyes slid away, back to the middle distance, and the stone went on turning, and she was gone again, the way a candle is gone when the wind finds the wick.

* * *

I don't remember deciding to sit down among them. I was simply there, in the sand, in the column of thin mountain daylight, and the boy with the glass ball was rolling it past me, and I put my hand in the groove and stopped it.

He stopped too. Not startled. Stopped, the way a clock stops, and waited.

Up close I could see it better: the fine silver stitching at his collar matched mine stitch for stitch. His spiral mark showed at his wrist, fainter than mine, like a scar half-healed. He was maybe ten. He had a smudge of sand on his cheek that no one had wiped away, and somehow that smudge hurt worse than anything else in the garden — proof that no one here was tended *as a child*, only kept *as a vessel*. You wipe a child's face. You merely dust a jar.

I held out my hand, palm up, the way you offer anything to anyone in any language.

He looked at my palm. He looked at my face. And then — slowly, slowly, like a door opening in a house long shut — he set the glass ball into my hand.

It was warm. That undid me. The ball was warm from all those hours of his turning, warm with the only living thing left in him, and I closed my fingers around it and felt tears come hot and fast, and the boy watched me cry with an expression of mild, patient curiosity, as if crying were a custom of some foreign country he had heard described.

Behind me I heard Sebastian's breath catch. He had followed me in, of course. He stood at the edge of the daylight with his fists at his sides, and I knew without looking that his face was doing the thing it did when grief and fury wore the same expression, and that he was memorizing every child in this garden the way he memorized roads — because Sebastian Okafor did not forget debts, and these seven were becoming his.

One of the girls with the flower crowns drifted nearer. She knelt beside me and began, absently, to braid a stem into my hair, her fingers deft and empty. As she worked, she hummed. Three notes, over and over. I knew the tune. Every Spire-born child knows it before they know their own name; it is the lullaby the mothers sang before the mountain took the mothers too.

I hummed it back.

The girl's hands stopped.

For a moment — one moment, no longer — her face broke. Not like glass breaking; like ice breaking, like a river in spring, the surface cracking to show the current underneath, wild and cold and alive. Her eyes filled. Her mouth trembled around a sound that might have been a name, hers or someone else's, lost so long ago even she no longer knew whose it was.

Then the current went under again. The ice sealed. She finished the braid, patted my hair once — gently, gently, the way you pat a grave — and returned to her row of crowns.

But she had heard. I had seen it. Somewhere down in the dark of her, past all the careful hollowness, something had heard the lullaby and turned toward it like a plant toward a window.

*They can still hear*, I told Sebastian later, in the old tongue, my hands shaking too badly for the valley signs we'd invented. *They're not gone. They're buried. They're buried and they can hear.*

He crouched in front of me in our moth-lit room, and his eyes were wet and hard at once, which I had not known eyes could be. "Then we dig," he said.

* * *

That evening the palace gave us a feast.

Of course it did. The Host himself presided — all three of the Court's faces were present, but the Host was the one who poured the wine and asked after our comfort, the Host whose voice was honey warmed over a slow fire. The table stretched longer than any table had a right to be, laden with foods I had no names for, and the lantern-moths descended low enough to turn every dish to jeweled light, and the Smiling One smiled, and the Ledger-Keeper recorded, and I sat beside Sebastian with the warm glass ball hidden in my pocket and my hand around it like a hand around a coal.

"To our guests," said the Host, lifting his cup. "May your stay be long."

Beside me, very softly, Sebastian did not drink.

And across the length of that glittering table, the Ledger-Keeper set down her pen — the first time I had seen her hands go still — and looked at Sebastian the way a tide looks at a sandcastle, and said:

"Master Okafor. The books wish to speak with you. Tomorrow. At your convenience."

Her smile was perfect. Her voice was silk over iron.

"There is a matter of payment we should discuss."
