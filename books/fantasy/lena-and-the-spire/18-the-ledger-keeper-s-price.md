## 18. The Ledger-Keeper's Price

The Ledger-Keeper's office was not a room so much as a throat.

Sebastian felt it the moment he crossed the threshold — the way the walls curved inward above and below, glass roots arcing like ribs, lantern-moths drifting in slow spirals through air that tasted of ink and cold stone. Somewhere far beneath them, water moved in channels he could hear but never see, a sound like the mountain swallowing.

Shelves rose on every side. Not shelves of books — though there were books too, their spines stitched from something pale and veined — but shelves of glass. Thousands upon thousands of shards, each one no larger than a thumb, each one humming faintly as he passed, like struck wineglasses heard through a wall of sleep. Some sang high and thin. Some droned low, almost mournful. A few, near the ceiling where the light of the moth-lanterns barely reached, had gone silent altogether, and Sebastian found he could not look at those for long.

The Ledger-Keeper sat at a desk that seemed grown rather than built, its surface a single sheet of smoked glass polished to a mirror's patience. It was neither man nor woman, nor anything so simple as either. Its face was smooth as an egg, featureless except for two slits of light where eyes might have been, and when it moved — sorting, writing, turning pages with fingers too long and too many — it moved with the unhurried grace of something that had never once been interrupted.

It did not look up when they entered. It finished the line it was writing first. Then, and only then, it set down its pen — a quill cut from a moth's wing, still faintly luminous — and folded its hands.

"You wish to amend an entry," it said. Its voice was dry paper, patient arithmetic. "Sit."

There were two chairs before the desk. Lena took one without being asked; she had taken to the Court's hospitality with the wary calm of a cat entering a house full of dogs, watching everything, touching nothing. She climbed into the chair and pulled her feet up under her, the bone whistle clutched in one fist against her chest. Her eyes went to the singing shelves and stayed there, tracking the hums the way other children tracked birds.

Sebastian remained standing.

"I want to make a substitution," he said. "One soul for another. Mine for hers."

The Ledger-Keeper regarded him for a long moment. The light in its eye-slits did not flicker, but something behind it shifted — a recalculation, perhaps, or merely the mountain settling.

"State your name," it said.

"Sebastian Okafor."

"State your lineage."

"My grandmother was Ysolde Okafor. Of the storm-line." He said it evenly, though the words scraped going down. "You'll find her in your books. You'll find what she signed."

"I know what she signed," the Ledger-Keeper said. "I have read it eleven thousand times. I read all entries eleven thousand times. It is how I know they are true." It turned, then, in its chair — the motion fluid, jointless — and extended one long hand toward the wall of glass behind it. "But you are not here to amend her entry. You are here to amend *hers*."

Its finger indicated Lena.

Lena did not flinch. But her grip on the whistle tightened until the bone creaked, and the faint hum inside it changed pitch — a half-tone higher, like a held breath.

"The vessel," the Ledger-Keeper continued, "is entered under contract 9,441-of-the-Deep-Shelf. Seller: Ysolde Okafor, storm-line, freely given consideration. Consideration received: passage of the Okafor bloodline through the Famine Years unharvested. Payment due: one soul of the seller's blood, at the completion of the moon's thirteenth turn following the seller's death." It paused. "The turn completes in nineteen days."

"I know," Sebastian said. "I've come to pay early. In full. My soul instead of hers. Same bloodline — I'm her grandson. The debt is owed by the blood; here is the blood, standing in front of you, offering itself freely. That should be worth more than a child who doesn't even understand what she's carrying."

He had rehearsed it on the walk down from the guest quarters, through corridors of glass root that glowed faintly where the walls were touched, dimming again behind them like footprints closing over. He had rehearsed it in the bath the Host had drawn for him, in the bed too soft to sleep in, staring at a ceiling woven of living crystal that pulsed very slowly, like the underside of a heartbeat. He had rehearsed it until the words wore smooth as river stones.

The Ledger-Keeper let him finish. Then it opened a drawer.

From the drawer it lifted a book. Not glass — actual paper, old paper, gone the color of weak tea, bound in leather that had been oiled and re-oiled across centuries until it shone like a beetle's back. It laid the book on the smoked-glass desk and turned pages with terrible gentleness, each leaf whispering against the next, until it arrived at the page it wanted and turned the book around to face him.

"Read," it said.

Sebastian leaned over the desk.

The handwriting was his grandmother's.

He knew it the way you know a voice through a closed door — the strong downstrokes, the little hook she put on her capitals, the way her *y*s descended like lightning about to strike. He had seen it on recipe cards and letters and, once, on the flyleaf of a songbook she had burned the same winter she gave it to him. Thirteen years since he'd seen it last, and his chest seized anyway, tight and sudden, as if a hand had reached into him and closed around something soft.

*Ysolde Okafor*, the signature read. Beneath it, in a different hand — older, colder, the letters like frost ferns — ran the terms. And beneath *that*, in yet a third script, dense and small as insect tracks, the clauses.

His eyes found the clause he feared most before he could stop himself looking for it.

*No substitution shall be accepted. No soul may stand surety for another. The debt binds the blood, and the blood alone shall answer; and of the blood, the vessel chosen at the signing shall answer first, last, and only.*

"There," the Ledger-Keeper said softly. It sounded, if anything, regretful — the way a scale is regretful when it tips. "You see the difficulty."

"She didn't understand what she was signing." Sebastian's voice came out lower than he intended. "She was starving. Her whole village was starving. Your court came to her in the famine years and offered—"

"Passage unharvested," the Ledger-Keeper agreed. "For four generations. It was generous. Harvests are our right, in the old law. We suspended them. That suspension had a price, and she named this price herself — she chose which soul would answer. Not us. *Her.*" One long finger touched the signature, feather-light. "We do not choose the vessels, storm-caller. We only collect what is freely promised. It is the difference between us and the things your stories warn you about. The things in the stories do not keep books."

Sebastian stared at the signature. His grandmother's hand. Steady, even here, even at the bottom of a bargain with the dark beneath the world. Had her pen shaken? He looked for tremor in the strokes and found none, and somehow that was worse than trembling would have been. She had known exactly what she was doing. She had written his family's future in her calmest hand and walked away, and lived thirty more years, and sung him lullabies, and taught him forbidden songs in a tongue she had no right to remember, and never once — not once in all his childhood — had her voice caught on the weight of it.

Or maybe it had. Maybe he simply hadn't been old enough to hear.

"Then void it," he said. "Unmake the contract. Return what she sold."

"We cannot." The Ledger-Keeper began, already, to close the book. "A soul freely given becomes ours absolutely. To return it would be to unmake ourselves. The Court is built on given things, storm-caller. Every root of glass in this city grew from a promise kept. If we broke one promise, we would break all of them, and everything you see around you—" it gestured, unhurried, at the singing shelves, the ribbed throat of the room, the moths drifting like slow snow — "would fall silent forever. You are asking me to commit suicide for the sake of a child. It is a moving request. The answer has been no for nine hundred years."

"Then I'll find another way."

"Many have looked." The Ledger-Keeper set the book back in its drawer and shut it with a click that echoed strangely, twice, as if the room repeated it to itself. "Scholars. Kings. A saint, once, though she was less pleasant about it. The contracts of the Deep Shelf hold. It is why we keep them there." It folded its hands again. "Is there anything else? The Host will be wounded if you leave without eating. The Host is always wounded. It is the Host's nature."

Sebastian didn't move.

He stood in the throat of the mountain, in the smell of ink and cold stone, and listened to the thousands of souls singing on their shelves, and felt despair arrive in him not like a wave but like a tide — slow, total, rising past his knees, his waist, his chest, filling the space where his breath should be. Nineteen days. Nineteen days and then men made of courtesy would come for Lena with soft voices and gentle hands, and they would hollow her out the way the hollowed villagers had been hollowed, smiling their identical smiles, wanting nothing, remembering nothing, and the part of her that drew spirals and gripped bone whistles and watched storms without fear would be poured into glass and shelved in the dark and sung at by moth-light for nine hundred years.

And he could not buy her free. He could not trade for her. He could not fight his way out — he had seen what the Court's riders were, and he was one man with a whistle and a child. He could not run; the mountain's roots went deeper than roads. Every door he pressed his hands against was locked, and the locks were not malice. They were *bookkeeping*. That was the horror of it, the thing that made the tide rise past his chin: there was no villain here to hate. There was only a ledger, and his grandmother's steady hand at the bottom of a page, and a clause written in frost-fern letters that said *the blood alone shall answer.*

"Lena," he said quietly. "Look at me a moment."

She dragged her gaze from the shelves. In the moth-light her face was pale and grave, eight years old and older than that, and she looked at him the way she had looked at him the first night, soaked on his doorstep — waiting, watchful, deciding whether the person in front of her was shelter or weather.

He knelt beside her chair so their eyes were level. He spoke in the old tongue, the language before kingdoms, the one his grandmother had sung and Lena dreamed. His accent was poor. Hers was perfect. It didn't matter.

*"I'm not leaving without you,"* he said. *"Whatever the books say. Whatever they tell me is impossible. Do you understand me?"*

Lena studied him. Then she reached out and touched his cheek with two fingers — the gesture she made sometimes, wordless, that he had come to understand meant *I believe you* — and after a moment she answered, also in the old tongue, her voice small and clear as a bell rung underwater:

*"My grandmother's grandmother wrote nothing. But my mother told me once, before they took my voice: the ones below cannot write a thing that isn't true, and they cannot make a truth that can't be broken. Everything they build has a seam. Even the deep shelf. Especially the deep shelf."*

Sebastian went very still.

"That's a story," he said aloud, in the common tongue, hoarse. "Children's talk."

"It is a *song*," Lena said, in the common tongue too, deliberately, so the Ledger-Keeper would hear. Her eyes never left his. "Songs live longer than signatures."

The Ledger-Keeper had gone motionless in a new way. Not the patience of arithmetic now. Something more like attention.

"Children repeat what they overhear," it said. "Vessels especially. They spend years in these halls before their turn comes, and they hear the servants talking, and servants gossip. It means nothing. The Deep Shelf has no seams. I have checked it eleven thousand times."

"Then you won't mind if I check it twelve thousand and one," Sebastian said.

He straightened. The tide of despair was still in him, cold and high — but tides, he knew better than anyone alive, tides *turn*. He had spent thirteen years learning what it was to stand in stillness while power gathered somewhere out of reach. He had thought that lesson was about loss. Standing now in the ink-and-stone dark, with his grandmother's calm signature burning behind his eyes and a child's borrowed hope ringing in his ears, he understood it had been training. Stillness was not surrender. Stillness was the moment before the strike, when you learned the shape of the storm you meant to call.

He could not substitute his soul. Fine. He would not substitute it.

He would *steal*.

Not today — today he would bow to the Host, eat the offered meal, smile the required smiles, be the grateful guest while he learned the halls, the guards, the rhythms of the festival the servants whispered was coming when the twelfth turn waned. Today he would be harmless. And somewhere between now and the thirteenth turn, he would find the seam in the deep shelf, and he would put his hands into it, and he would tear.

"Thank you for your time," he said to the Ledger-Keeper, and inclined his head with the exact courtesy the Court used, the courtesy he was beginning to understand was a kind of armor worn by everyone down here, on both sides of every desk.

The Ledger-Keeper watched him with its slit-lit eyes. When it spoke, its dry-paper voice had acquired, very faintly, the quality of a warning.

"The archive is sealed, storm-caller. The archive is always sealed. And the glass remembers hands." It paused. "Enjoy your supper."

Sebastian took Lena's hand. Her fingers were cold; he warmed them without thinking, the way he'd done every night since the valley, a habit that had grown into love while he wasn't watching. Together they walked back down the throat of the room, past the singing shelves, past the silent ones near the ceiling that he still could not look at — though now, as he passed, he made himself look. Made himself count them. Made himself memorize their silence, the way a soldier memorizes the faces of the dead so that later, when it matters, he knows exactly what he is fighting for.

At the door, Lena stopped. She turned back, and she blew — very softly, barely a breath — across the bone whistle in her fist. No sound came out that any ear in the room could catch. But every shard on every shelf shivered at once, a thousand souls singing one startled note, and the Ledger-Keeper rose halfway out of its chair for the first time in nine hundred years.

Lena smiled — the first true smile Sebastian had seen from her since they came beneath the mountain — and took his hand again, and led *him* out.

Behind them, in the dark, the Ledger-Keeper opened its drawer, took out its book, and began — for the twelve-thousand-and-first time — to read.
