## 20. What Lena Wants

The palace of glass roots never slept, but it pretended to.

Lena had learned that in her first night beneath the mountain. The lantern-moths dimmed in slow waves, like breathing, and the corridors went soft and blue as the inside of a shell, and somewhere far off the roots sang to themselves in voices too low for waking ears. It was beautiful. That was the worst part. Everything here was beautiful, and everything here was a trap with the lid left open so you would climb in yourself.

She could not sleep in the bed they had given her. It was too wide, too warm, too full of feathers that smelled of some flower she did not know. She had slept on floors all her life — the cold shelf behind Marta's woodpile, the hollow under Sebastian's stairs where she had dragged her blanket the first week because beds were for people who believed they would still be there in the morning. So she lay on the floor beside the bed, in the strip of moonless dark between the moth-light and the wall, and she listened to the palace pretend, and she thought about what she had seen today.

The other children.

There were seven of them in the east wing, behind a door that was never locked, which was its own kind of horror. They sat in a round room with cushions the color of cream and honey, and when Lena came in they all turned their heads at once, gently, like flowers following a sun that wasn't there. Their eyes were fine. Their hands were clean. Someone combed their hair. And when Lena asked them questions — *what is your name, what did you love, what do you want* — they smiled at her with the same smile, all seven, the same curve of the same mouth, and one of them said, *I am happy here,* in a voice as smooth and empty as a river stone.

*I am happy here.* Seven mouths. One sentence. No child behind any of them.

Lena had run from the room. She had run until the roots caught her, cradling her against the walls like a mother who would not let go, and she had stood there shaking, pressing her palms against the warm glass, understanding at last — fully, in her body, in the place where words are made — what *hollowed* meant. Not dead. Dead would have been kinder. Hollowed meant someone else lived in the shape of you and smiled your smile and wanted nothing, ever again, because wanting was the part they took.

That was the fate waiting for her when the thirteenth turn came. Unless Sebastian found another way.

Unless Sebastian gave himself instead.

She had watched him all evening across the banquet table, and she knew him well enough now to read the thing he was not saying. He held his cup and did not drink. He answered the Host's courtesies in short, careful words. And every few minutes his eyes came to her — quick, guilty, measuring — the way a man looks at a door he is deciding whether to walk through. She knew that look. She had spent eight years learning to read people because she had no voice to argue with them, and Sebastian Okafor was a man building a sacrifice in the quiet of his own chest and calling it a plan.

No.

She would not let him.

So when the moths dimmed to their deepest blue and the palace's pretending-sleep settled over the halls, Lena rose from her floor-nest, took the bone whistle from beneath her shirt where it lay warm against her skin, and went to find him.

* * *

His door was not locked either. Nothing here was locked; locks were for places that feared you, and the Court feared nothing, because everything here eventually walked in willingly. That was the whole architecture of the mountain, Lena understood now. Not walls. Open doors.

Sebastian was sitting by the window — a window of thin glass root that looked out onto the cavern dark, where the lantern-moths drifted in their thousands like a sky that had fallen and refused to lie down. He had his grandmother's letters spread on his knees, though he was not reading them. He was just holding them, the way you hold a wound.

He heard her come in — of course he did; he always heard her, even before she learned to make sound — and he looked up, and his face did the thing it always did when he saw her: the hard lines around his mouth loosening, just slightly, like ice remembering it used to be water.

"You should be asleep," he said softly. "It's late. Even by mountain time."

Lena crossed the room and sat down across from him, cross-legged, the way they used to sit by his hearth in the valley, back when the world was only one valley wide. She put her hand flat on the floor between them. Then she looked at him, steadily, and made the choice she had been circling for days.

She spoke.

Not in signs. Not in drawings in the ash. In the old tongue, the language before kingdoms, the language that lived in her bones before anyone taught her anything — the language the Spire-folk had sung into their children so that something of them would survive even the selling.

"*I have been thinking,*" she said.

Sebastian went very still. He had heard her speak this way only twice, and each time it had shaken him, she knew — hearing the dead tongue come out of a living child's mouth, hearing his grandmother's forbidden songs answer him from an eight-year-old's throat. But he did not interrupt. He had learned that about him, too. When something mattered, Sebastian Okafor got quiet and let the world talk.

"*I saw the children in the east wing,*" Lena said. "*Seven of them. They smile with one smile. They say one sentence. I looked into them and there is no one home, Sebastian. There is a coat hanging on a hook, and the coat says it is happy.*"

"Lena—"

"*Let me finish.*" Her voice was small in the big glass room, but it did not waver. Smallness was not weakness; she had proved that her whole life. "*You are going to tell me tomorrow that you have found a way. I have watched you reading the letters. I have watched you counting on your fingers when you think I'm sleeping. You are going to offer them yourself. Your soul for mine. Or your magic. Or your name. Something of yours, in my place.*"

He said nothing. His silence said everything. His silence was a confession written in a language even the river of names could read.

"*I know why,*" she went on, gentler now, because this next part was not anger — anger was easy, and this was harder than anger. "*Because you think you are already lost. Because you were exiled, and alone, and you think a man with nothing left can afford to pay with what remains. Because you held me when I knocked on your door soaked through, and you decided I was yours to protect, and you have never once asked whether I agreed to be protected at this price.*"

"That isn't—" He stopped. Started again. "Lena, you're a child. You don't have to understand this. You don't have to carry it."

"*I carried it up a mountain,*" she said. "*I carried it out of the Spire's shadow, through rivers and roads, while hunters rode behind me. Do not tell me what I can carry.*"

The moths outside drifted. The roots sang their low, sleepless song. Somewhere far above, stone shifted against stone with a sound like a giant turning over in sleep, and neither of them flinched; they had been beneath the mountain long enough now that its body had become almost familiar.

Sebastian set the letters aside. He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and for a moment he looked every year of his exile — tired in the marrow, worn smooth by thirteen years of weather he had not been allowed to feel.

"I sold myself once already," he said quietly. "Did you know that? Not to the Court. To the quiet. Thirteen years ago I walked into that valley and I buried my storms and I told myself it was peace. It wasn't. It was just a smaller cage with better light." He shook his head. "And then you knocked on my door, and for the first time since I buried it, I had a reason to want my magic back. Not for me. For you. Don't you see? If giving it — if giving *anything* — keeps you whole, then it isn't a loss, Lena. It's the first useful thing my power has ever done."

Lena felt tears rise and forced them down. Tears were rain, and rain was his, and she would not put her grief on him tonight like a burden dressed as gratitude.

"*Then hear me,*" she said. "*Hear me the way you heard the storm on the ridge the first night I came. Hear me and do not turn away.*

"*If you give yourself for me, I will be whole. I know that. I will walk out of this mountain with my soul in my chest and my name in my mouth, and I will grow up, and I will laugh, and I will want things — and every single thing I ever want, for the rest of my life, will be built on top of you. On your emptiness. On your smile that is really seven smiles wearing one face.*"

Her voice cracked, and she let it.

"*I would rather be hollow.*"

The words fell into the room like stones into a well.

Sebastian's breath went out of him. "Don't say that."

"*I mean it.*" She leaned forward and took his hands — his big, scarred, weather-mended hands, hands that had fixed her roof and braided her hair badly and held the bone whistle for her when she dreamed. "*Listen to what I am saying, because I have thought about this longer than you think. The children in the east wing owe nothing. That is the only freedom they have left — they cannot be hurt by a debt because there is no one left inside them to owe it. But if you empty yourself to fill me, then I will spend my whole life owing a ghost. Every joy I have will be a coin I stole from your pocket. Every time I laugh, I will wonder if it is the laugh you paid for. You would not be saving me, Sebastian. You would be hollowing me slowly, from the inside, with love instead of knives — and I would feel every year of it, awake, forever.*"

"That's not how it would be—"

"*How would you know?*" she asked, and her voice rose, and she let it rise, because she had been silent for eight years and she had earned this volume. "*Have you ever been owed to? Have you ever been the reason someone else went dark? I have. I was the reason my own people sold themselves, generation after generation, payment after payment, and I ran rather than be the coin. Do you think I ran all this way just to watch you volunteer to be spent?*"

He was crying now. She had never seen him cry. In the valley he had been the still point of her world, the man who mended roofs and refused to remember, and she had half-believed he was carved from the same quiet as the mountains. But here he wept without shame, silently, the way men cry who learned young that tears change nothing.

"I couldn't watch them take you," he whispered. "I couldn't stand in that hall tomorrow and listen to the Ledger-Keeper read your name out of a book and do nothing. What kind of— what kind of father does that?"

The word landed between them like a struck bell.

*Father.* He had never said it aloud. She had never dared to hope it in more than fragments. And it would have been so easy — so sweet, so warm, so exactly the belonging she had walked across a kingdom starving for — to let it stand, to let it pull her toward him, to say *yes, and fathers save their children, that is what fathers do.*

But that was the trap with the lid left open. Everything in this palace was.

"*Yes,*" she said softly. "*You are my father. Not because blood says so. Because you chose me, and I chose you, and choice is the only inheritance worth anything. And because you are my father, I am telling you the truth no one else in this mountain will tell you:*"

She gripped his hands tighter.

"*A father who makes himself nothing to save his child does not save the child. He only teaches her what she costs. And I refuse — I refuse — to learn that lesson. I refuse to be the debt that emptied you. If the choice is your wholeness or mine, then I choose mine to stay unspent, and I will hate the spending of yours every day I live, and there is no wholeness in a life built on hating what saved it.*"

The roots hummed. A moth pressed itself against the window glass near Sebastian's shoulder, wings opening and closing like a slow heartbeat, as if it were listening. Perhaps it was. Everything here listened. Let it carry this back to the three faces. Let the Host and the Ledger-Keeper and the Smiling One hear that their vessel had a will, and that her will was iron.

Sebastian bowed his head over their joined hands. For a long time he said nothing, and Lena let the silence be, because she had learned silence from the best teacher in two kingdoms.

"If they take you," he said finally, hoarse, "and I did nothing—"

"*Then you will grieve,*" she said. "*Truly, terribly, for a long time. And you will be whole while you grieve. And one day you will mend a roof for some other soaked-through child who knocks on your door, and you will tell them about me, and it will hurt, and it will also be true. That is a real life. That is what I want for you — a real life, even if it is one I am not in. Better that than a hollow girl serving tea to a hollow man in a house built out of a debt.*"

"And if I find another way?" he asked. "Not substitution. Not trade. Something else — something none of us has thought of yet. Will you let me look?"

Lena considered him for a long moment. Outside, the false sky of moths wheeled slowly, and the palace breathed its beautiful, lying breath.

"*Look,*" she said at last. "*Look with everything you have. But promise me the boundary, Sebastian. Promise me on the old tongue, which does not let a speaker lie. Promise me you will not pay with yourself. Find a door that opens without a key of flesh. If you cannot find one — if tomorrow there is no way but your way — then you will stand beside me when they come, and we will face it together, and neither of us pays alone.*"

He was quiet for so long she thought he would refuse. She watched the war move through his face — the protector's instinct against the father's love, the man who fixes roofs against the man who has finally learned that some things cannot be mended by breaking the mender.

Then he lifted his head, and he spoke in the old tongue, haltingly, the accent of his grandmother's songs surfacing after thirteen drowned years:

"*I will not pay with myself. I will find a door that opens free, or I will stand beside you. This I swear, and the old tongue holds me.*"

The words settled into the glass of the room like frost forming — visible, somehow, faintly silver, the way oaths spoken in the first language leave their mark on the air. Lena let out a breath she felt she had been holding since the east wing.

"*Thank you,*" she said. And then, because love is not only solemnity, she climbed onto the too-wide bed beside him and leaned her head against his shoulder, the way she used to during thunderstorms in the valley — except now the thunder was inside him, and she was not afraid of it.

They sat like that while the moths dimmed toward their false dawn.

It was Lena who noticed it first — a wrongness in the roots' song, a new thread woven into the low music, high and sweet and deliberate. A summons. She sat up. Down the corridor, soft footsteps were approaching, unhurried, courteous, terrible: the particular gait of a servant who has never once needed to hurry because everyone in this mountain comes willingly.

The footsteps stopped outside the door.

And a voice like warmed honey through the glass said, "Forgive the hour, storm-caller. The Smiling One asks — begs — for a word with you. Alone. Tonight, before the Ledgers open."

Lena's hand found the bone whistle at her throat and closed around it, and the whistle, silent for weeks, began — very faintly — to hum.
