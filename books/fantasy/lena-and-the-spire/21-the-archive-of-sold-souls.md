## 21. The Archive of Sold Souls

The moth came to him in the hour the palace pretended to sleep.

Sebastian had not truly slept since they arrived beneath the mountain — how could he, with lantern-moths drifting past the high windows of their guest-chamber like slow golden snow, and the whole city humming a note too low for the ear and too deep for the body to ignore? He lay on silk that felt like an apology and listened to Lena breathe in the bed beside his own, her small chest rising and falling with the steadiness of someone who had decided, once and already, what she was willing to lose.

The knock, when it came, was not a knock at all. It was a tap of something soft against glass — three times, patient, deliberate.

He rose without waking her. He had learned in thirteen years of exile how to move through a dark room as if he were part of its darkness, though the irony of that skill, here, in a place where his storms were locked behind his teeth like swallowed coals, did not escape him.

In the corridor stood one of the servants — the same one who had poured his wine at the Host's table, the one whose wings carried a pattern like frost ferns rather than the gilded filigree of the others. It stood barely taller than Lena, wrapped in grey robes that seemed woven from the memory of fog, and its face was a smooth oval of pale chitin with two eyes like drops of amber holding ancient light.

"You are awake," it said. Its voice was dry and small, like pages turning. "I hoped you would be. The sleeping are harder to help."

"Help," Sebastian repeated quietly. "That's a dangerous word in this house."

"Yes." The moth tilted its head, and the amber eyes caught the corridor's faint glow. "I have served the Court for four hundred years. I have watched them take souls the way farmers take grain — gratefully, efficiently, without malice, which is worse than malice. Malice can be hated. This—" it gestured with one delicate limb at the palace around them, the glass roots glowing faintly blue-green in the walls like veins of frozen river, "—this cannot even be resented properly. It simply *is*."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you looked at the lanterns during dinner," the moth said, "the way a man looks at caged birds. And because your grandmother's shard sings loudest on the nights before a turn completes. I thought you should know that."

The floor did not move, but Sebastian felt it move anyway — a lurch in the blood, in the hollow where his magic used to sit like a second heart.

"My grandmother's shard," he said. "You mean her soul is *here*."

"I mean," said the moth, "that the archive keeps everything the Court has ever been paid. And your family has paid twice."

* * *

They went down.

Down past the guest levels with their warm lamps and their too-soft carpets; down past the kitchens where dough was being worked by hands that never tired because they had nothing left in them to tire; down a stair of black stone worn concave by centuries of feet, until the warmth of the palace fell away behind them like a coat shrugged off, and the cold of the deep mountain took its place — a clean, mineral cold that tasted of iron and old rain.

The moth moved ahead of him, its grey robes whispering against the steps, and it never once looked back to see if he followed. That, more than anything, convinced him. The Court's creatures performed loyalty. This one simply walked, and trusted him to walk too, or didn't care either way, and both possibilities felt more honest than anything the three faces had offered him across their polished table.

"The archive is not secret," the moth said, its voice floating back up the stair. "That is the first thing you must understand. Nothing here is hidden. The Court does not need to hide. Who would come? Who would dare? The archive is guarded by indifference, which is stronger than any wall."

"And if we're seen?"

"We will be seen," the moth said simply. "It is the seeing that matters. A servant walking a guest through the deep levels on the night before a festival — no one will think twice. Everyone is busy preparing the moth-lanterns. Everyone is busy being beautiful." There was something in its voice then, thin and bitter as a wire. "Four hundred years, storm-caller. Do you know what it is to be decoration?"

Sebastian didn't answer. He thought of thirteen years of mending roofs. Of being useful and invisible and calling it peace.

"Yes," he said finally. "I think I do."

The stair ended at a door that was not a door — an archway of living glass, grown rather than built, its surface moving so slowly it might have been still: a ripple frozen over a hundred years. Beyond it, the dark opened out, and out, and out.

* * *

The archive was a cavern the size of a cathedral buried in the mountain's root, and it was full of light.

Not lamplight. Not firelight. The light came from the shelves themselves — thousands upon thousands of them, rising in terraces into the black overhead like the inside of a shell, and on every shelf, rows of glass shards, each one no larger than a hand's length, each one glowing with its own color and pulsing with its own rhythm. Blue ones that flickered quick as a frightened heartbeat. Gold ones that burned slow and steady as hearth-coals. Green ones, violet ones, one far shelf of white so pure it hurt to look at.

And they were singing.

Not words. Not music, exactly. A hum — layered, interwoven, thousands of voices braided into one vast chord that filled the cavern the way water fills a well. It was the note Sebastian had been hearing since he arrived beneath the mountain. He had thought it was the city itself. It was this. It had always been this. The whole court beneath the mountain was lit and warmed and *animated* by the sound of stolen souls, the way a mill is turned by a river.

Lena's soul would be a shard on one of these shelves. He understood that now, viscerally, in his spine. She would hang here in the dark and sing forever, and the Court would call it rest.

"They call it the Quiet Library," the moth said, standing beside him at the archway. "The Court's joke. Because the souls cannot speak. They can only sing what they were." It gestured outward with one limb. "Every shard is a person. Everything they loved, everything they feared, everything they chose — compressed into glass and pitch. The Ledger-Keeper says it is preservation. The Host calls it hospitality. The Smiling One—" the moth paused, "—the Smiling One doesn't explain anything. That is how you know the Smiling One is the most honest of the three."

"How do you find one shard among all this?"

"You listen." The moth turned its amber eyes up at him. "Souls recognize their blood. Stand in the center and open yourself, and yours will answer. But be warned, storm-caller — everything answers, a little. You will hear ten thousand lives brushing against yours. Most men weep. Some men go mad. One man, two hundred years ago, tried to smash the shelves, and the shards sang his name to the guards before his hand touched the first one." A beat. "Do you still wish to continue?"

Sebastian looked out over the terraces of light. Somewhere in that ocean of glow was a woman named Ysolde Coldharbour, who had sung forbidden songs to a boy in a kitchen that smelled of bread and woodsmoke, who had taught him that storms were not violence but *attention*, who had died — or hadn't she? — while her family survived on the strength of whatever bargain she'd struck beneath this mountain.

He had come down here hating her a little. He was only now admitting that to himself. Hating her for selling a descendant's soul, for writing letters instead of warnings, for leaving him a debt shaped like a child with silent eyes.

"Show me the center," he said.

* * *

The center of the archive was a circle of bare black stone where no shelves grew, as if the glass roots themselves refused to touch it. The singing was loudest there — or perhaps it only felt that way, the way a name feels loudest when it is called in an empty room.

"Stand still," the moth murmured. "Breathe. Let them see you."

Sebastian closed his eyes.

At first there was nothing but the chord, vast and impersonal, the sound of the mountain's stolen heart. Then, slowly, threads began to separate from the weave — the way that, standing in a crowd, you begin to pick out individual conversations. A woman's laugh, gold-colored, patient, fifty years dead and still amused by something. A soldier's shard thrumming with marching rhythms. A child's — a *child's*, small and silver and quick, and Sebastian's stomach turned over and he made himself keep breathing, made himself keep standing, because turning away from this was what the Court counted on. Indifference was their wall. He would not add himself to it.

Then, beneath everything, low and unmistakable, a thread of storm-grey caught him.

It didn't shine like the others. It was a dull, restless color, the grey of cloud before hail, and it pulled at him the way weather pulls at a scar. His skin prickled. For one wild half-second he thought his magic had returned — that the valley's dampening field had somehow followed him down here, or that the archive itself had cracked him open — and then he understood.

It wasn't his storm answering.

It was hers.

He crossed the floor before he knew he was moving, following the grey thread through the terraces, up two shallow flights of shelving, past shards that leaned their light toward him curiously as he passed — and stopped before a niche near the top, where a single shard sat slightly apart from its neighbors, angled as if it had been trying for years to turn and face the door.

Grey as stormlight. Rough-edged, unpolished, as if it had refused to be made beautiful. And singing — oh, it was singing, a low rolling melody with thunder under it, and threaded through the melody, faint as a voice heard through a wall, *words*.

Sebastian reached out with a hand that shook and laid his palm flat against the glass.

The song resolved.

*"—told him not to come, stubborn boy, told him the valley would keep him safe, told him—"*

Her voice. Her actual voice, worn and warm and cracked at the edges, the voice that had sung him to sleep through the worst years, coming out of a piece of glass in a cave under the world.

Sebastian sank to his knees in front of the shelf, and the grief that came out of him was not quiet. It was a storm with nowhere to go, all thunder and no lightning, and he knelt there in the singing dark and let it break over him while the moth-servant stood watch at the terrace's edge, grey robes still, amber eyes turned away to give him the mercy of not being seen.

* * *

It took him a long time to hear what she was actually saying.

The shards did not narrate, he realized. They *were*. Each one sang the shape of the life inside it — its loves, its terrors, its great unfinished business — circling them endlessly, the way a trapped bird circles a window. His grandmother's shard circled one thing. Over and over, in fragments, in storm-rhythms, the same cluster of memories:

*"—signed it twice, fool woman, signed my name and then gave the rest freely, they never asked, they never dreamed—"*

*"—a debt needs a debtor, but a gift needs no one—"*

*"—if my blood comes looking, tell the glass, tell the glass—"*

Sebastian pressed his forehead against the shelf's cool edge. His mind was moving fast now, faster than it had moved in thirteen years, the old sharpness returning like feeling flooding back into a numb limb.

She had sold herself.

Not a descendant. Not Lena. *Herself.* The letter in his pack — the confession he'd read by candlelight, the one that had named Lena as the payment, the debt passed down the bloodline — that was the contract the Court held. But his grandmother had done something else, secretly, afterward or alongside it. She had come back down here, or sent word, or found some crack in the Court's careful machinery, and she had given her own soul *freely* — not taken, not traded, *given* — and the Court had accepted it, because the Court accepted everything, because the Court could not imagine that a gift might be a weapon.

*"A debt needs a debtor, but a gift needs no one."*

His lips moved around the words. Something was forming in the back of his mind, some shape made of contract-law and storm-logic, some flaw in the ledger —

"There is a clause," the moth said quietly, from behind him. It had approached without sound. "In the oldest book. The one the Ledger-Keeper keeps beneath the others and pretends is decorative. My people bound the contracts, four hundred years ago, before we were ornaments. We remember what the Court forgets on purpose." The amber eyes reflected ten thousand shards of light. "A soul freely given back by its seller voids the sale. All sales. The whole line of them. It is the one law the Court fears, and so it is the one law they never speak."

Sebastian stared at it. "You're telling me how to destroy them."

"I am telling you," the moth said, "what your grandmother died trying to leave where her blood could find it. Whether you use it is your storm to call." It paused, and something almost like gentleness entered the dry page-turning voice. "But understand this, storm-caller. The clause requires the seller's soul to be *released* — returned, freed, given back to sky or soil. And a shard can only be released by one thing."

"Name me."

"Not a hand. Not a key. Not a bribe." The moth's wings stirred, shedding a fine dust that glimmered as it fell. "The shards are held by the Court's own binding, and the binding answers only to power older than the Court's founding. Power that was here before the first contract was signed." The amber eyes fixed on him, unblinking. "Storm-magic, storm-caller. The old kind. The kind that was calling thunder when this mountain was still soft. The kind that lives now, so far as I know, in exactly one living creature — and he is kneeling in front of me, and he gave it up to hide in a valley."

The cavern sang around them, ten thousand souls, and among them his grandmother's grey shard rolled its slow thunder, and Sebastian Okafor felt the shape of it settle over him at last — the whole terrible architecture of it, the bargain beneath the bargain, the inheritance beneath the debt.

To free her, he would need his storms.

To reach her, he would need to steal her out of the heart of the Court's own vault.

And somewhere above them, in a guest chamber hung with lantern-light, a girl was asleep who had exactly twenty days left before the moon finished its thirteenth turn.

"When," Sebastian said, rising, wiping his face with the back of his hand, his voice coming out level and cold and more like his grandmother's every second, "is the moth-lantern festival?"

The moth-servant smiled — or did the thing its face did instead of smiling.

"Tomorrow night," it said. "When every eye in the Court is turned upward toward the light."
