## 22. Grandmother's Voice

The archive did not breathe.

Sebastian had noticed it first as an absence — the way a room without windows is an absence of sky — and then he could not stop noticing. Every other place beneath the mountain had a pulse to it: the slow swell of the lantern-moths' wings, the wet green shimmer of the glass roots drinking light that came from nowhere, the distant chime of the Court's many voices moving through their corridors like wind through wind-chimes. But here, in the long hall where sold souls were kept, there was only stillness, and the singing.

The shelves rose higher than his eyes could follow, curving away into dark that the moth-servant's lamp could not reach. Each shelf held rows upon rows of shards — slivers of glass no longer than a finger, each one mounted in a cradle of woven root-fiber, each one singing.

Not loudly. That was the horror of it. They sang the way snow falls: continuously, softly, without any need for anyone to listen. Thousands of voices braided into one low tone that Sebastian felt in his back teeth more than heard, a chord so old and so patient it seemed less like music than like geology.

"They are not unhappy," the moth-servant said, reading his face. Its voice was dry paper and moth-dust, and its great dark eyes caught the lamplight like drops of oil. "I want you to understand that before you go further. What is kept here is kept *well*. The Court is cruel in its accounting, but it is not careless. A cracked soul sings badly, and bad song offends the Ledger."

"And if no one ever listens?" Sebastian asked. His voice came out rougher than he intended. The air down here tasted of cold stone and something sweeter underneath — lily-of-the-valley, impossibly, deep under a mountain. "If no one ever comes?"

The moth tilted its head, antennae swaying. "Then they sing for the shelves," it said. "The shelves do not mind."

It turned and moved deeper into the hall, its lamp swinging, and Sebastian followed because there was nothing else in the world left to do.

* * *

He had come in through the servant-passage behind the lantern-nursery, in the hour the Host called the *dimming*, when the palace's lights were lowered and even the Court's faces rested — or pretended to. The moth-servant — it had given him no name, only the gesture of touching two fingers to its own thorax, which he understood to mean *you may call me by this* — had led him through corridors that smelled of wax and warm chitin, past sleeping nurseries where moth-larvae curled like commas of pale fire.

Every step, Sebastian had waited for the alarm. Every step, the living glass in the walls had hummed against his skin, tasting him, and every step it had let him pass. He did not know yet whether that was mercy or design. He suspected, with the Smiling One's grin still burned into the back of his mind, that it was design.

Now the shelves closed around them, and the singing thickened, and the moth stopped before a section marked by nothing at all — no sign, no number, only a slight widening of the aisle, as if the room itself had made space for what was kept there.

"Your blood-name," the moth said. "Say it inwardly. Not aloud. The shards answer to intention, not sound."

Sebastian closed his eyes. *Okafor,* he thought. *Storm-caller. Son of Adaeze, son of the woman who wrote letters she was afraid to send.*

The singing changed.

It was subtle at first — a single voice rising out of the chord the way a single face surfaces out of a crowd — and then it was not subtle at all. One shard, three rows up and six cradles along, began to burn. Not with light, exactly. With *recognition*. The glass warmed from within until it glowed the color of a storm-cloud's underside, that bruised silver-green he had spent thirteen years trying not to remember, and the song that came out of it was no longer part of the chord. It stood apart. It sang alone.

His grandmother's voice.

Sebastian's knees nearly went. He caught himself against the shelf, and the shelf — the living wood-and-glass of it — seemed to lean into his hand, steadying him, and he could not decide whether that was kindness or the archive simply keeping its inventory upright.

"Take it down," the moth said quietly. "You may hold it. Holding is permitted. Only *keeping* is theft."

*Only keeping is theft.* He would turn those words over later, again and again, looking for the trap inside them. Later. Now he reached up with both hands, the way you reach for something hot, the way you reach for a child out of water, and lifted the shard from its cradle.

The moment it left the shelf, the singing all around them faltered — a heartbeat of silence rolling down the aisles like a wave — and then resumed, and Sebastian understood that the archive had *noticed*, and had decided, for now, to say nothing.

The shard was warm as a held hand. And it sang.

* * *

Her voice came in pieces, the way memory comes, the way a letter comes after years in a drawer — and he realized that was exactly what it was. This was her letter. The last one. The one that had never been sent because there had been no one left to send it to, because the boy she was writing about had been four years old and hiding under a table when the hunters came for his mother, and by the time he was old enough to read, she had been dust and debt and a name no one spoke above a whisper.

The glass did not speak words at first. It sang *shapes*. He felt them rather than heard them: weariness first, vast and gentle; then love, sharp as a fishhook; then a specific, deliberate attention, the feeling of someone choosing their words with care because the words would have to last.

Then, slowly, the shapes became language. Not the common tongue. Not quite the old tongue either, but the border-country between them — the dialect of grandmothers, the language of lullabies and curses, and Sebastian, who had learned it kneeling at her knee while she shelled beans and told him stories she swore him to secrecy over, understood every word.

*Little storm. If you are hearing this, then you found your way down, which means you were foolish, which means you are mine.*

He laughed, and it broke in the middle, and the moth-servant looked away with the tact of the long-serving.

*I sold a soul to buy our survival. You know this, or you will. What you do not know — what I made certain no ledger would show — is whose soul I sold first.*

The shard flickered. For a moment the storm-light in it guttered like a candle in wind, and Sebastian felt the whole archive lean closer, thousands of shards straining on their shelves, listening to one of their own speak the unspeakable.

*I offered myself. Do you hear me, child? I walked into that court with my name in my mouth and I said: take mine, take the storm-magic, take the whole line of it back to the first thunder. And the Ledger-Keeper looked at me with its kind, terrible face and said: a soul freely offered is worth nothing. It must be owed. It must be bound by blood and debt and necessity, or the giving means nothing.*

*So I did the arithmetic no grandmother should ever have to do. I looked at my children, and my children's children, and I chose the one whose life the bargain would ruin least — or so I told myself, in the dark, for forty years. I bound the debt to the bloodline and I let the Court choose how it flowed downhill. And it flowed, little storm. It flowed downhill the way water does. To the smallest. To the youngest. To the girl born mute with a spiral on her wrist who sings in her sleep in the tongue before kingdoms.*

Lena.

The word formed in him without sound. Lena. The child who had knocked on his door soaked through with rain that should never have crossed the valley's boundary, carrying a whistle that hummed without wind, drawing spirals on his floor with a wet finger. The payment. The payment his own grandmother had signed into being before he was old enough to hold a spoon.

Rage came first — hot, total, aimed at a dead woman — and then grief arrived and put its arms around the rage like a friend restraining a drunk, and the two of them stood together inside his chest while the shard went on singing.

*Do not hate me. Or hate me, but hate me usefully. Because I did not stop when the contract was signed. I had forty years, and I am a stubborn woman, and I found the flaw.*

The storm-light in the glass flared bright enough to throw his shadow huge and wavering across the shelves.

*A contract of the Court is a circle drawn in law older than law. It holds because everything inside it is owned — the debt, the vessel, the term, the reckoning. But there is one thing the Court cannot own, because owning it would unmake the Court itself. A gift freely returned. Hear me: if the seller takes back what was sold — freely, knowingly, without price or trickery — the circle breaks. Not bends. Breaks. The sale voids. Not paused. Voided. Every clause, every term, every thirteenth turn — ash.*

*But they knew I would find this, someday, and so they built the lock into the lock. A soul can only be returned by its seller. My body is forty years of rain on a hillside. So the return must come through what remains of me in the world — and what remains of me, little storm, is my magic. My storms. The name-magic I carried, that ran downhill just like the debt did, that sleeps in your bones right now whether you have called it in thirteen years or thirteen hundred.*

*Only a bearer of my storm may give my soul back. Only my soul, freely returned, voids the sale. Do you see the shape of it? They built a door that only opens from the inside, and then they buried the key in the one person they thought would never come down.*

The shard dimmed. The last of her song came soft, almost a whisper, and Sebastian bent close to the glass as if she could see him, as if forty years and a mountain were nothing at all.

*I am sorry. I am sorry. I would burn every year of my afterlife to unsign that page. Since I cannot — since the Court keeps even regret in its ledgers — I leave you the door instead. It will cost you everything you are, and I know what that is worth, because it was worth everything to me too, once, and I spent it on the same errand.*

*Spend it better than I did, little storm. Spend it on the girl.*

The singing stopped.

The shard went quiet in his hands — not dead, he could feel that, not hollowed, but *waiting*, the way a held breath waits — and the silence it left behind was so complete that Sebastian could hear his own heart, and beneath it, faint and far above, the memory of rain on a valley roof.

* * *

He sat down on the archive floor. There was no dignity in it and he did not look for any. He sat with his back against the shelf that had held her for forty years, and the shard in his cupped palms, and he wept the way men weep alone in the dark underground: silently, thoroughly, without any hope of stopping soon.

The moth-servant waited. It was very good at waiting. Perhaps that was the whole of its employment.

When he could speak, he said, "She sold herself too."

"Yes," the moth said.

"She tried to sell herself first, and they refused, so she built the debt instead — and then she spent forty years finding the way to break what she'd built." He turned the shard over. The storm-light stirred under his thumb like a fish under ice. "And the way to break it is me. It was always me. She hid the key in her own blood and trusted the flood to carry it somewhere it might be used."

"The Court believes the flaw is theoretical," the moth said. "A story sellers tell themselves. In eleven thousand years of the Ledger, no soul has ever been freely returned. The seller always wants something in exchange — life, mercy, another's freedom. Wanting poisons the giving. The circle holds." Its antennae curved forward, and in the lamplight its eyes were ancient and unreadable. "They do not believe a man exists who would give his storm-magic — the whole inheritance of his line, the only thing standing between him and every hunter who ever sought him — for a child he has known one season, and ask nothing back."

"Then they haven't met my grandmother," Sebastian said, "and they haven't met her grandson."

He stood. His legs shook and held. Around him the archive sang on, thousands of souls in their cradles, patient as snowfall, and for the first time since he had descended into this hall he looked at them not as a wall of horror but as a congregation — witnesses, waiting, every one of them proof that the Court's arithmetic had a remainder it had never been able to carry.

"How," he said. "How does it work. The rite."

"You know already," the moth said. "You have known since you were a boy kneeling at her knee. Storm-magic is not lightning. Lightning is only what it looks like when it passes through the world. Storm-magic is *pressure* — the weight of the sky, the charge before the strike, the name the air answers to. Pour it out. All of it. Into the glass, through the glass, into her. Hold nothing back, because the giving must be free, and a free gift that keeps a coin in its fist is not free." The moth's wings opened slightly, pale as onion-skin. "And when it is done, there will be no storm in you. Not resting. Not sleeping. Gone. The hunters will lose your scent, yes — but so will the sky."

Sebastian looked down at his hands. Thirteen years he had hated them for their emptiness, for their patience, for mending roofs and hauling water and lying flat and quiet while the weather passed over without once turning its head toward him. Thirteen years he had called it peace and known it was exile.

And now he stood in a hall of stolen souls holding the one shard in the world that could open the door, and the price of opening it was the last thing he had left of her — of himself — of the boy who had once stood on a ridge with his arms flung wide and taught the thunder his name.

*Inheritance,* his grandmother's voice seemed to say, from somewhere under the silence of the glass. *Is not what you are given. It is what you are for.*

"All right," he said, and his voice did not shake, which surprised him. "All right. Then we do it tonight. Now. Before the twelfth turn wanes and they—"

"No," the moth said.

The word fell into the archive like a stone into a well.

"Not here. Not in the dark, in secret, like thieves of grief." The moth stepped closer, and its lamp threw its shadow tall and thin against the singing shelves. "The rite must be performed openly, or the Court will call it theft — and a stolen return is not a free return, and the circle will hold, and you will have spent your storm for nothing and woken every hunter in the mountain besides. It must be done in the sight of the Three Faces. On the mountain's shoulder, under the open sky, where the old law can watch. Which means—" the moth's voice dropped to moth-dust and paper, "—you must take her out of this hall first. Past the living glass. Past the alarms. During the festival of lanterns, when ten thousand moths rise at once and the Court's senses drown in their own beauty."

Sebastian stared at it. "You're asking me to steal her."

"I am asking you," the moth said gently, "to do the one thing the Court has forbidden in eleven thousand years. Freely. Knowingly. Asking nothing back." Its wings trembled once, a motion like a shrug, like a prayer. "I have carried lanterns in this dark for nine hundred years, storm-caller. Let me carry one true thing."

Far above them, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the first notes of the festival began — a sound like bells learning to sing — and in Sebastian's hands, faint as a pulse, the shard woke and glowed, and his grandmother's storm answered the distant music the way it had once answered the horizon.

Three nights. One festival. Ten thousand moths.

And between him and the door, every alarm in the mountain.
