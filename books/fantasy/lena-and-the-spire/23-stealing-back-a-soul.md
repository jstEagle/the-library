## 23. Stealing Back a Soul

The festival of the moth-lanterns began at dusk, and beneath the mountain, dusk was a rumor.

There was no sky down there — only the vast root-vaults of glass arching overhead like the ribs of some buried god, hung with ten thousand lanterns in which pale moths beat their wings and burned without burning. The light they gave was not warm. It was the color of milk held up to the sun, the color of a memory you could not quite reach, and it fell over the Court's city in slow, drifting pools that moved as the moths moved, so that the whole underground world seemed to breathe.

Sebastian stood at the edge of the Host's great terrace with Lena's small hand swallowed in his own, and watched the city put on its celebration.

The vessels-in-waiting had been dressed in white. He saw them now, lined along the processional way, children in gowns stitched with glass thread, each one smiling the same smile, each one holding a lantern they did not look at. Their eyes were the worst part. Not empty — Lena's would have been empty, if she'd been hollowed — but *waiting*, like rooms swept and aired for guests who were expected to arrive soon and stay forever.

"Don't look at them," he murmured.

Lena squeezed his hand once. She didn't need to say anything. She never needed to say anything; the old tongue lived in her fingers as much as her throat, and her fingers said: *I know. I remember. I was almost one of them.*

Above the terrace, the three faces of the Court presided from their thrones of woven root-glass. The Host poured wine for honored guests who had no mouths to drink it. The Ledger-Keeper sat with the great book open on its lectern, quill scratching even now, recording joy the way other courts recorded taxes. And the Smiling One — the Smiling One was looking directly at Sebastian, and smiling, which meant nothing and everything, and had meant nothing and everything since the night it had offered him Lena's place and his magic forever.

*One night,* Sebastian had asked. The Smiling One had given him three. Generosity, or a trap with a longer snare. He had not decided yet. He suspected he was not meant to decide. He suspected the deciding itself was being weighed on scales he could not see.

Tonight, then. While the whole court drank light and called it mercy.

His grandmother's shard was kept in the Archive of Sold Souls, below the Hall of Ledgers, behind a door of living glass that opened only to the touch of one who carried name-magic. That should have stopped him. It nearly had, the first time the moth-servant — Vess, she had whispered her name was Vess, though names were currency here and she gave hers away like a beggar giving a coin she couldn't spare — had told him how to reach it.

*It opens to name-magic,* she had said, folding and unfolding her dusty wings in the shadow of a corridor, her lantern-eyes darting toward every passing footstep. *But it does not open to the name-magic of those who belong to the Court. You are a guest. You are outside the books. For one more night, you are outside the books.* A pause, her wings stilling. *I have served here four hundred years, storm-caller. I have carried souls to their shelves. I have heard them sing. Do you know what it is, to carry a soul that is singing?*

He hadn't answered. There was no answer.

*They sing quieter every year,* Vess had said. *Yours has been singing since before you came. She sings a lullaby. I know it because I have heard it through the shelves for sixty years, and last month, when your child walked past the archive door, the singing changed key.* Her wings had shivered. *I would like to hear it end well. Once. Before I am shelved myself.*

Now the festival drums began — deep, resonant things made of hollowed root, struck by servants whose faces were smooth as river stones — and the procession of vessels started its slow walk toward the Host's throne, where they would be blessed, and praised, and thanked for what they would give, and none of them would understand a single word of the ceremony because understanding was the first thing taken.

Sebastian turned from the terrace with Lena and walked, unhurried, into the crowd.

* * *

The corridors below the festival were lit only by the bleed-through of moth-light through the glass roots, and the roots themselves seemed drowsy, drunk on the ceremony above. Sebastian had learned in three days of careful wandering that the palace was not built but *grown*, and that it slept lightly, the way an old dog sleeps — one ear always open. Every pane of glass in every wall was a listening thing. The Court heard through its own house.

That was why they needed the whistle.

Lena walked beside him with the bone whistle hidden in the sleeve of the gray traveling dress Marta had packed for her, back when leaving the valley had still felt like a plan rather than a plunge. The whistle hummed faintly against the cloth. Even muffled, even here, it made the air around it feel *wrong* in a way Sebastian's bones remembered from the valley — the same wrongness as the dampening field, the same hush-that-was-not-silence. The Court's senses slid off it. He had watched a servant pass Lena in a corridor and simply not see her, gaze sliding away like water off oiled wood.

"Which way?" Lena signed. They had built the signs between them over weeks of road and fear, a private grammar of finger and thumb, and Sebastian found he thought in it now more easily than in speech.

"Down. Past the hall of ledgers." He kept his voice below the drums. "Vess says the door remembers my grandmother's blood. It will open for me."

"And after?"

"After, we run."

She looked at him then, in the half-dark, with that gravity she carried like a stone in her chest — eight years old and older than the mountain. *You said we wouldn't lie to each other anymore.* It wasn't a sign. It was just her eyes.

"We might not make it," he admitted. "If the alarms come—"

"They'll come." No doubt in it. She had seen the living glass wake once already, when a servant dropped a tray near the archive stair; the walls had rippled like the skin of a struck drum, and the sound that came out of them had been a chord made of every stolen soul in the building, screaming in harmony. "When they come, I'll play. You carry me if I fall."

"You won't fall."

"You don't know that."

"No," he said. "But I'm allowed to hope badly. It's a father's privilege."

Something crossed her face — quick, bright, quickly hidden — at the word *father*. He had used it twice before, carelessly, testing. Each time she had gone very still, the way a wild bird goes still when the window it has been battering suddenly opens. She didn't sign anything now either. She just took his hand again, and they went down.

* * *

The Hall of Ledgers at night was worse than by day. By day it was merely terrible: shelf upon shelf of the great book's ancestors, bindings of pressed bark and wire, ink that gleamed wetly no matter how old the page. By night, with the festival drums thrumming down through the roots like a heartbeat heard through a wall, the hall seemed to be *breathing*. The ledger on its lectern had been left open. The quill scratched on, unheld, recording something. Sebastian did not let himself read what.

The archive door waited beyond, set into a wall of glass grown thick as tree-trunk, and it was exactly as Vess had described: a door of living glass veined with slow silver light, featureless until you stood close, and then — faces. Hundreds of faces, sleeping in the depth of it, the makers perhaps, or the kept. As Sebastian raised his hand, the faces stirred. Not waking. Turning. Toward him. Toward the blood in him.

He pressed his palm flat.

For a moment nothing. Then the silver veins flared, and a voice that was not a voice moved through the door like wind through wheat, tasting him, reading him the way the river below the mountain had read him — except this reader did not want a memory. It wanted a *name*.

*Okafor,* it breathed, inside his skull, gentle as frost forming. *Storm-line. Daughter-of-the-line: Sefa Okafor, sold freely, kept sixty years, singing. Son-of-the-line: Sebastian, exiled, entered, guest. Blood answers blood. What do you ask of the kept?*

Every instinct he owned screamed that this was the trap, that the door was the Court's mouth, that the Smiling One was somewhere above wearing its smile like a lamp. But there was no other way, and the drums were counting down the festival, and somewhere on a shelf below his feet his grandmother had been singing a lullaby for sixty years to no one.

"I ask to see her," he whispered. "Sefa Okafor. Shelf of the freely sold."

The door considered him. The sleeping faces opened their eyes — all of them, all at once, hundreds of pairs of patient ancient eyes — and Sebastian understood that they were not wardens. They were witnesses. Everything done at this door was seen, and remembered, and someday accounted.

Good, he thought. Let it be witnessed.

The door opened without a sound.

* * *

The Archive of Sold Souls was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. Shelves rose in ranks into the dark, and on every shelf, rows of shards — glass the size of a fist, the size of a heart, each one holding a soft internal glow that pulsed slowly, slowly, like breathing. And they sang. All of them. Not aloud, not in any language, but in the way a struck glass sings if you stand close enough and are quiet enough to hear the ringing continue after the sound should have died.

Sixty years of souls. Six hundred. Some of the glows were very faint. Some had gone out entirely, and those shelves were the ones Vess could not walk past without stopping.

Lena's hand tightened in his. She was staring up at the ranks with her lips parted, and Sebastian realized she could hear them clearly — better than he could — the dead tongue rising off the shards like heat off stone. Her free hand rose halfway toward the nearest shelf, some instinct of greeting, of kinship, and Sebastian caught it gently and shook his head.

*Not yet,* he signed. *Soon.*

They went down the aisle Vess had described — third rank, the low shelves, where the oldest freely-given souls were kept apart because they sang loudest and the Court disliked loudness. And there, third shelf, between a shard that glowed the green of spring storms and one that had gone dark as river stone, sat a shard of ordinary, stubborn, work-worn light. Amber. Warm. Utterly unlike everything around it, the way his grandmother had been utterly unlike everything around her: a woman who sang forbidden songs in a kitchen full of steam and dared the world to object.

The singing changed key the moment he knelt before it. He heard it this time, plain as a voice across a table. A lullaby. His lullaby. The one she had sung to a boy who talked to thunder, in a tongue before kingdoms, while rain wrote its long gray letters down the windows.

"Sefa," he said. His voice cracked on it. "Nana. I'm here. I'm so late."

The shard pulsed. Brightened. Dimmed. Brightened — and the rhythm of it was unmistakable now, the rhythm of a heart remembering it had a listener.

He lifted it from its cradle of woven root.

The alarm woke like a scream made of winter.

* * *

Later, Sebastian would try to describe the sound and fail. It was not loud, exactly. It was *total*. Every wall of glass in the archive flushed white, then red, then white, and the chord that came out of them was every soul on every shelf crying out at once — grief and terror and a horrible, hopeful *recognition*, because the shard in his hands was singing louder than it had sung in sixty years and every kept soul in the dark knew what that meant. Someone had touched a soul and not put it back. Someone had committed the one crime this place had a word for.

The floor rippled under his feet. Roots stirred in the walls like muscles flexing. Somewhere far above, the festival drums faltered.

"Lena!"

She was already running. They burst from the archive into the ledger hall and the hall was waking around them, panes flushing, the great book snapping shut on its lectern with a crack like a jaw, and behind them the aisle of shelves began to *close*, roots knitting across the gap where his grandmother's shard had sat, sealing the theft into the record of the room.

Then Lena put the whistle to her lips and blew.

No sound came out — and all the sound in the world came out. The note that wasn't a note tore through the hall, and the living glass *shuddered*, every flushing wall stuttering mid-alarm, every sensing vein going blind at once. Sebastian felt it in his teeth, in the old scar-tissue of his exile: the valley's hush, the dampening field, the thing that had kept his storms from finding him for thirteen years — concentrated in a child's breath, in a bone that remembered a tower. The Court's house had ears everywhere. The whistle made them all deaf.

"What do you see?" Lena gasped, lowering it, wide-eyed. Speaking aloud cost her; she rationed words like bread in a famine. "I can't tell if it worked—"

The corridor ahead filled with moth-servants, dozens of them, wings blazing, drawn by the alarm — and then, as the whistle's silence rolled over them, they simply *stopped*, hovering, turning in slow confused circles, lantern-eyes flickering, unable to fix on the two intruders standing ten feet away. One of them looked directly at Sebastian and looked *through* him.

"It worked," Sebastian breathed. "Run anyway."

They ran.

The palace fought them the way a body fights fever — not with malice but with reflex. Stairs lengthened between one step and the next. Corridors leaned, trying to pour them back the way they'd come. Glass doors sealed ahead of them and Lena blew the whistle and the seals forgot themselves, sagging open like hands unclenching in sleep. Twice, root-tendrils whipped down from the vaulting to snatch at them, and both times the whistle's dead note made the tendrils flinch aside as if scalded.

And through it all, the shard in Sebastian's coat sang. Louder now. Unafraid. His grandmother's lullaby climbing over the alarm-chords like a woman singing over a storm, the way she had all his childhood, the way she had taught him — without ever saying it — that a voice raised in love was louder than any thunder.

They reached the festival terraces and the light hit them like a wave: ten thousand moth-lanterns, the whole court assembled, the three faces risen from their thrones, every eye in the underground city turning—

—and sliding away. Because Lena was running beside him with the whistle still sounding its soundless sound, and the Court's senses broke against her like surf on a sea-wall, and in the confusion of ten thousand turning heads, two more figures were simply two more guests, hurrying, anonymous, toward the root-tunnels that led up and out.

Only the Smiling One saw them. Of course it did.

It stood at the head of the terrace, above the rows of white-clad vessel children, and as Sebastian passed the edge of the crowd with his daughter's hand in his and his grandmother's soul against his heart, the Smiling One's gaze found his and held it — and it did not raise an alarm. It did not move at all.

It winked.

Cold walked down Sebastian's spine colder than the archive. *Escape that comes too easily is a door left open on purpose.* But there was no time, and the tunnels were ahead, and the twelfth turn was waning somewhere above them in a sky he hadn't seen in days.

They plunged into the root-tunnels as the first true alarm finally tore loose behind them — a sound like the whole mountain clearing its throat to speak their names.

Behind them, the Court woke fully. Ahead, the tunnels climbed toward the surface, dark and branching and alive.

In his coat, his grandmother sang on, steady as a hearth-fire, as if she had known all along that someone was coming for her — and had simply been keeping the song warm until he arrived.
