## 24. Flight Through Glass Roots

### Sebastian

The alarm of living glass did not sound like bells.

It sounded like a choir learning to scream.

Sebastian felt it in his teeth before he heard it with his ears — a high, crystalline keening that rippled outward from the archive in waves, each one sharper than the last, until the whole palace beneath the mountain was ringing like a struck goblet. Around him, the great root-tunnels of glass lit up vein by vein, amber light flooding through walls thin enough to show the shadows of moth-servants scattering on the far side, their wings flashing panic-signals in stutters of silver.

He ran.

The shard was inside his coat, wrapped in three layers of linen against his chest, and it was warm — warmer than body-warm, warm the way a hearthstone is warm at midnight, and it sang against his sternum in a voice he knew. His grandmother's voice. Not words yet, not quite, but the hum she used to make when she kneaded bread, when she braided her hair, when she thought no one was listening. Thirteen years since she died and here she was, pressed against his heart, singing him onward through a palace that wanted very much to swallow them both.

"Left," Lena said.

She had said it in the old tongue first, then caught herself and said it again in the trade-tongue, and Sebastian marveled even now, even running, at how quickly she had learned to spend words like coins after hoarding them so long. She was ahead of him, bare feet silent on the glass, the bone whistle clenched in one small fist. She had blown it once, back in the archive, when the alarms began — one long note that had made every lantern-moth in the chamber turn its head at once, confused, listening for a wind that did not exist. It had bought them ten heartbeats. Ten heartbeats was a corridor's length. A corridor's length was everything.

They plunged left, down a tunnel that sloped and narrowed, the glass roots here grown wild and knotted, branching into chambers where pale gardens grew in soil that glittered like crushed stars. The light followed them. Of course it followed them. This whole place was an eye.

"The Host will be waking," Sebastian panted. "He never truly sleeps — he only *receives*."

"Then we go where receiving is hard." Lena skidded to a stop at a junction where four tunnels met, and pressed her palm flat against the glass wall. She closed her eyes. Her lips moved without sound, shaping syllables in the language before kingdoms, and Sebastian watched — watched with the hair rising on his arms — as the glass *leaned* toward her. Just slightly. The way a plant leans toward light. The way a dog leans toward the hand it trusts.

"You can hear which way is safe," he said.

"I can hear which way is *listening*." She opened her eyes, and in the amber glow they were the color of deep water over old stone. "This one. But we must run faster than polite."

They ran faster than polite.

* * *

### Lena

The palace dreamed in colors, and the colors were all wrong.

Lena had known this place before she could name it. She had been carried down these tunnels as a smaller child, half-asleep against a shoulder that smelled of stone and lilies, and some part of her had memorized everything: the way the third root-branch forked around a chamber full of hanging cradles, the way the air grew sweet near the nurseries, the way the sweetness sat on the tongue like fruit gone quietly rotten. The Court kept its vessels close. The Court kept everything close.

Now she ran through her own memory with a stolen soul singing against a man's chest, and the palace woke around them like a body waking from anesthesia — slowly, and then all at once, and then angry.

Behind them, the keening changed pitch. Lower now. Wider. Lena understood it the way she understood weather: this was no longer the archive crying out about theft. This was the palace itself being asked a question by its masters, and answering. *Where are they. Where are they. Where.*

"They're closing the upper branches," she called back. "I can feel the doors thinking shut."

"Doors don't think."

"Theirs do."

They burst into a gallery Sebastian would remember later whether he wanted to or not — a long hall where the glass roots arched overhead like the ribs of some buried leviathan, and between the ribs hung lanterns by the hundred, and inside each lantern burned not flame but a moth the size of a sparrow, wings patterned in eyes. As they passed beneath, every eye-wing opened at once. Every moth turned. The whole ceiling became a single watching face, and Lena felt Sebastian flinch beside her and reached up without looking and found his hand, and gripped it, and did not let go.

"It's all right," she said, and was surprised to find she meant it. "They're only moths. They're only ever asking."

"Asking what?"

"*Are you lost? Are you lost? Are you lost?*" She blew the whistle instead of answering — a short note this time, sharp as a snapped twig — and the moths shuddered, their patterns scrambling, dozens of tiny faces suddenly unsure which direction lost went. In that stuttering confusion they ducked through a side arch, down, always down, following the slope of the mountain's buried bones toward the surface and the cold.

The tunnel narrowed. The glass grew older here, cloudier, shot through with veins of white like frost in a windowpane. Lena's breath came out in ghosts now. Somewhere above them, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, wind moved over the mountain's shoulder, and she heard it and nearly wept, because wind meant sky, and sky meant *away*, and away was a word she had not owned since the night she climbed out of the nursery window with nothing but the whistle and her name held tight between her teeth.

Sebastian stopped so suddenly she walked into his back.

Ahead, the tunnel forked around a chamber, and in the chamber stood the Smiling One.

* * *

### Sebastian

Of the three faces of the Court, Sebastian had feared the Ledger-Keeper most, because the Ledger-Keeper's cruelty wore arithmetic, and arithmetic does not tire. He had distrusted the Host, because hospitality that perfect is a net woven one courtesy at a time.

But the Smiling One terrified him in a way he had no clean words for, because the Smiling One's face was kind, and kindness from a thing like this was either a lie or a leash, and both fit equally well.

It stood in the side-chamber with its hands folded — a tall figure whose outline kept trying to settle and couldn't, robes shifting like smoke deciding to be cloth, features arranged in an expression of such gentle amusement that Sebastian's skin crawled from scalp to sole. Its smile did not move. That was the horror of it. The smile was not an expression; it was architecture.

"There you are," the Smiling One said warmly, as if greeting guests who had merely wandered off during dinner. "And there it is." Its gaze dropped to Sebastian's chest, to the warmth beneath the linen, and the smile deepened by a fraction that somehow communicated *delight*. "You've made rather a mess of our quiet evening, storm-caller. The archive is inconsolable. Living glass, you understand — it feels everything. Right now it is feeling *you*, your heartbeat, your fear, the salt on your skin. It will remember this night longer than you will."

"Then it can remember us leaving," Sebastian said. His voice came out steadier than his hands. He put himself between the Smiling One and Lena without deciding to; his body simply knew its work. "Step aside."

"Oh, I don't think I'll need to." The Smiling One tilted its head, and for just a moment the smile flickered — not away, but *inward*, like a lamp turned down to hide how bright it burned. "Do you know what I admire about you, Sebastian Okafor? No — don't answer. Let me admire in peace. Thirteen years you sat in your little forgetting valley, and everyone believed you'd buried yourself alive. Even the Ledger-Keeper believed it. Even I half-believed it, and I believe almost nothing." It unfolded its hands. "But a man who steals a soul out of love — that man has not buried anything. He has been keeping it warm."

Every instinct Sebastian owned screamed at him that this was a trap wearing conversation. He looked for the trap. He looked behind the figure, at the tunnel beyond it — open, unguarded, sloping up toward a gray suggestion of daylight. He looked at the Smiling One's empty hands. He looked at the watching moths along the ceiling, which had gone utterly still, as if awaiting a cue.

"Walk," the Smiling One said, and gestured graciously at the open passage, and its smile was a door held open, and Sebastian had lived long enough among dangerous things to know that an open door offered by a smiling creature is more frightening than any barred one. "Go on. Up. The twelfth turn is waning; you haven't much moon left to outrun. We wouldn't want you to miss what comes next."

"What comes next?" Lena asked, small and clear.

The Smiling One looked at her then — truly looked, and for one suspended instant the smile *did* move, softening into something that might have been, in a better world, tenderness.

"Why, the thirteenth," it said. "Isn't that what all of this has been about?"

Sebastian grabbed Lena's hand and ran.

* * *

### Lena

He let them go. He *let* them go, and Lena's blood knew it the way prey knows it, the way a hare knows the hawk that watches without diving.

She ran anyway. There was nowhere else to run *to*.

The tunnel climbed. The glass thinned and roughened, giving way to raw granite veined with quartz, and the air came colder and wetter and tasting of pine and snowmelt, and Lena drank it like a woman saved from drowning. Behind them the keening faded, softened, and — this was the part she would not tell Sebastian, not yet, maybe not ever — behind them the keening did not sound like pursuit at all.

It sounded like patience.

Roots gave way to stone stairs worn smooth by centuries of feet, and the stairs gave way to scree, and then there was no roof over them anymore, only night, vast and black and needled with stars, and the mountain's shoulder falling away below them in dark folds of forest, and the moon — the moon hanging low and swollen over the western ridge, silver going to shadow along its lower edge as the twelfth turn waned toward its end.

Lena stopped and turned and looked back the way they had come.

The mountainside was quiet. No riders. No hunters pouring from hidden gates. No living-glass tendrils reaching up through the rock to drag them home. Only wind combing the high grass, and somewhere far below, a fox screaming its thin night-song, and the palace beneath them holding its breath.

"It's too easy," Sebastian said. He was bent double, hands on knees, breath sawing. The shard against his chest had begun, faintly, to sing aloud — a thread of melody escaping the linen, his grandmother's voice rising through fabric and cold air like steam. "Chases aren't supposed to end like this. Doors aren't supposed to stand open. It *wanted*—"

"I know what it wanted." Lena touched the whistle at her throat. "It wanted us to run. You don't chase something you've already caught."

Sebastian straightened slowly. In the moonlight his face was hollowed and gray, thirteen years of exile carved fresh into it, and when he spoke his voice was very quiet. "Then what do we do?"

Lena looked at the moon. The last sliver of the twelfth turn was thinning as she watched, shadow eating silver, and beneath her fear she felt something else stirring — the old tongue rising in her throat on its own, words she hadn't chosen, a counting-rhyme the nursery children had sung under the mountain without knowing what they counted:

*Twelve turns wane and twelve turns wake,*
*twelve turns carry what the thirteenth takes...*

"We walk," she said. "We walk all night, and we don't stop, and we get as far from this mountain as our legs will take us before the moon finishes dying." She took his hand again — his hand was freezing, and hers was not, and she found that strange and didn't examine why. "And when the thirteenth turn comes, wherever we are—"

She didn't finish. Above the western ridge, the final crescent of the twelfth moon slid into shadow, and the night went dark as the inside of a closed mouth.

And in the silence that followed, from everywhere and nowhere — from the stones, from the stars, from the singing shard against Sebastian's heart — came a sound like a page turning.

Somewhere beneath the mountain, a ledger opened.

The thirteenth turn had begun early.
