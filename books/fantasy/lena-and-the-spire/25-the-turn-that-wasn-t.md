## 25. The Turn That Wasn't

The river was called the Sorrowless, and it lied.

Sebastian had known that the moment he'd knelt on its bank and let a lantern-moth priest lift his wedding day out of him like a splinter drawn from flesh. *Sorrowless*, the maps said — because what you surrendered to its current, you could no longer grieve. But grief, he had learned in thirteen years of exile, does not leave when its object is forgotten. It only goes feral. It waits in the body like a storm behind glass, and one day the glass cracks, and you feel all of it at once without knowing why your hands are shaking.

He felt it now.

They had made camp a half-mile downstream from the crossing, in a hollow of birches where the ground was soft with fallen leaves and the river's murmur came to them muffled, like voices through a wall. Lena slept with her back against his pack, the bone whistle tucked into her fist even in sleep, her thumb resting over its spiral carving as if she feared someone might take it while she dreamed. Above them, through the black lace of branches, the moon hung fat and white and wrong.

Twelve turns complete. The thirteenth waxing.

Four nights, by the old reckoning. Perhaps three. Sebastian sat with his grandmother's letters spread across his knees and could not read a word of them, because every time he looked down at the paper his eyes slid up again to the moon, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

*It's too easy,* he kept thinking. *It's been too easy since the glass roots.*

The Smiling One had watched them go. Not pursued them. Watched — with that face like a door left open onto a warm room, that expression Sebastian still could not decide was kindness or appetite. The alarms of living glass had shrieked through the palace, and then, between one heartbeat and the next, they had simply... stopped. As if someone had put a hand over the Court's mouth. As if someone had wanted two fugitives to reach the surface with their stolen shard intact and their lungs full of night air and their hearts foolish with hope.

Hope, Sebastian thought, was the most expensive thing the mountain sold. And it gave it away free.

Lena stirred. In sleep she made small sounds now — she had begun to, in the weeks since she'd first chosen to speak to him, as though her voice were a muscle remembering itself. Tonight the sounds were not peaceful. Her brow furrowed. Her lips moved around syllables of the old tongue, the language before kingdoms, and even asleep she shaped it more beautifully than any living speaker he'd ever heard, each word round as a river stone.

He reached over and smoothed the hair from her forehead, and she quieted, and he went back to watching the moon.

That was when the memory came back.

It did not arrive all at once. It arrived the way dawn arrives — a grayness first, a sense of something standing just beyond the trees of his mind, and then color bleeding in, and then the whole sky of it, sudden and unbearable.

A kitchen with blue tiles. Flour on someone's knuckles. A woman laughing with her head thrown back, saying his name — saying *Sebastian, look at the mess you've made of me* — and sunlight through a window doing something impossible to her hair, and the smell of bread, and the weight of a ring he'd spent three months' wages on, and the vow he'd spoken in a chapel so small the echo came back before he finished speaking it—

He was on his feet before he knew he'd stood. The letters scattered. His hand was pressed flat against a birch trunk and the birch bark was rough under his palm and real, and the river was murmuring, and Lena was blinking up at him awake now, alert the way she was always alert, a deer-child rising out of sleep already listening.

"Seb-as-ti-an," she said carefully, in the accent of the old tongue, which had never had to bend itself around his name before. "What is it?"

"I remember." His voice came out scraped. He turned to look at her and found his own face reflected in her wide dark eyes, and he must have looked wild, because she sat up slowly, the way you sit up near a frightened animal. "My wedding day. Lena, I remember my wedding day."

She frowned. She had been there at the crossing; she had watched him kneel and watched the moth-priest draw the memory out in a thread of silver light and drop it into the current, where it had unspooled and dissolved like sugar in water. She had held his arm afterward while he wept without knowing what for. She understood better than anyone alive what the river took, because the river had tried to take from her and failed — had reached into her and found nothing it could hold, nothing it could dissolve, and had recoiled.

"The river gave it back?" she asked.

"The river doesn't give things back." He crouched, gathered the scattered letters with hands that would not quite steady. "That's the whole horror of it. That's why they call it the Sorrowless. What goes in stays in. The moth-priest told me — he said the memories become part of the river, they feed the reeds, they—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "It's not the river. It can't be the river."

"Then who?"

And there it was. The question he had been circling all night like a moth around a flame it did not trust. He lowered his hands and looked east, toward the mountains, where the moon was climbing.

"The Court," he said. "The Court gave it back."

* * *

They walked downstream in the gray hour before dawn, following the river's voice, because Sebastian needed to see it. Needed proof, or disproof, or anything at all that was not the shape of his own mounting dread.

The mist lay on the water in long white banners. The reeds along the bank stood motionless, and yet the mist moved against the current, drifting upstream, which mist should not do, which nothing should do, and Sebastian stopped at the edge of the reed-bed and felt the hair rise on his arms.

"Lena. Look at the water."

She looked. Her breath went out slow.

The river was flowing backward.

Not violently — not a flood running the wrong way — but gently, patiently, the way a hand smooths a wrinkled sheet. The current that had carried his wedding day east toward the sea was now carrying it west, back upstream, back toward the crossing, back toward the mountain's shadow. And in the water, here and there, silver threads moved beneath the surface: thin, bright, deliberate. Memories. Dozens of them. Hundreds. He could almost make out shapes in them — a face here, a doorway there, a snatch of music rendered in light. Every payment the river had ever collected, being returned.

"It's calling them home," Lena whispered. Then she corrected herself, and the correction was worse: "No. It's *being made* to call them home."

Sebastian waded in to his ankles before he thought about it. The water was cold enough to ache, and when he crouched and let his fingers trail in the current, one of the silver threads drifted toward him, curious as a fish. He caught it — or it let itself be caught — and for a moment it lay across his palm like a strand of light, and inside it he saw:

Not his memory. Someone else's. A man's hands building a cradle, sanding the wood smooth, humming. A woman heavy with child, laughing at him from a doorway. A name — *Alric* — spoken twice, once in love and once in grief.

The thread shivered in his grip, wanting to move on, and he opened his fingers and let it flow.

"They're all going back," he said. He was surprised how calm his voice sounded, when everything under it was screaming. "Every soul that ever paid the river. It's returning them."

"Why?"

"Because the contract isn't ending, Lena." He stood. Water streamed off his boots. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered — or he imagined it muttered; his storms were still with him out here beyond the valley, coiled and eager, answering his fear before he asked them to. He pushed the feeling down. "Because the thirteenth turn isn't four nights away. It's *now*. It started the moment we climbed out of that tunnel with the shard in my coat, and the Court isn't trying to stop us. The Court is *reclaiming early*. Everything. Everywhere. All the debts come due at once."

Lena had gone very still. In the pre-dawn gray her face was pale as the mist, and her hand had found the whistle at her throat without her seeming to notice.

"Hollowing," she said. The word in the old tongue was older and worse — a word that meant *the pouring-out*, a word that had been carved somewhere, he suspected, into the walls of the Spire before the Spire fell. "All of them. Everyone who ever sold anything."

"Not everyone." He was grasping and he knew it. "The ones whose contracts are written. The ones in the ledger. My grandmother's bargain — the vessel clause —"

"You don't know that." Her voice cracked, and she hated herself for cracking, he could see it. Eight years old and she already hated her own fear. "You're guessing. You always guess and then you're right and I wish you weren't."

He crouched to her level. Upstream, the mist rolled on, patient as bureaucracy. "Then we stop guessing," he said, "and we start moving. Marta and the others are a day behind us with the iron bells. If the thirteenth turn has begun, the village is in the path of it — the valley's dampening field won't hold against a reckoning, it was never meant to, it was meant to keep out *stray magic*, not—"

"Not the Court coming to collect," Lena finished.

The sun broke the ridge behind them then, and the light came down the river valley in a long gold flood, and it should have made everything kinder. Instead it showed Sebastian what the mist had hidden: that the banks of the Sorrowless, for as far as he could see in both directions, were lined with people.

Travelers. Peddlers. Pilgrims. Families with carts. They stood at the water's edge in the growing light, silent, all facing upstream, all watching the silver threads pass. Some were weeping — he could see that even at this distance, shoulders shaking, hands pressed to mouths. Others stood perfectly still, and those were the ones that frightened him, because they wore the same smile. Identical. Serene. The smile of the hollow village, the smile of people whose souls had been partially taken and partially replaced with something smooth and compliant.

One of them turned. An old woman, sixty years of weather in her face, wearing a peddler's patched cloak. She smiled at Sebastian with that borrowed serenity, and then — for just a moment, like lightning inside a cloud — something else moved behind her eyes. Something terrified. Her mouth shaped words at him across the water and the wind took most of them, but he caught the shape of them anyway, because some words are shaped so plainly that even silence speaks them:

*Run.*

Then the serenity closed over her face like water over a stone, and she turned back to the river with all the others, and the mist rolled on upstream toward the mountain, carrying everything home.

* * *

They ran.

Not toward the village — not yet. First to the crossing, because Sebastian needed one thing from the moth-priest's shrine that stood there: answers, or a map, or failing both, the priest himself. The shrine was a low dome of woven reeds and river-glass, and when they burst through its curtain of hanging bells, they found it empty. The little altar was cold. The offering bowls were overturned. On the floor, arranged with terrible neatness, lay the priest's robes, folded, and atop them a single lantern-moth, dead, its wings gone gray as ash.

Beside the robes, a ledger lay open.

Sebastian had seen ledgers like it before — in the Court beneath the mountain, in the Ledger-Keeper's hall of glass roots, where every sale of every soul was recorded in script that seemed to crawl slightly when you weren't looking directly at it. This one was smaller, a field copy, the kind a traveling collector might carry. The last entry was fresh, the ink still shining.

He read it aloud, because Lena could not read the Court's script and he needed to hear it in the air, needed it to be real and therefore fightable:

*"By order of the Three Faces, in anticipation of the Thirteenth Turn, all outstanding collections are hereby accelerated. The river shall return what it holds, that the debtors may be found in possession of their whole selves at the hour of reckoning. Nothing owed may be collected from an empty vessel. Let every debtor be made whole — that they may be made ours."*

The words sat in the air like frost.

"Made whole," Lena repeated. Her voice was very small. "So they give everyone back their memories first. So the taking hurts more."

"So the taking is *legal*," Sebastian said, and heard in his own voice the cold clarity that had once made him a storm-caller the Mountain Court had feared. "That's what this is. The contract requires a whole soul freely given. My grandmother's loophole — a soul given back voids the sale — they've closed it. They're not returning anything out of mercy. They're restoring inventory. Every person who ever paid this river is about to stand at the hour of reckoning with their entire self present and accounted for, and the ledger will say the debt stands, and there will be no argument left. No flaw. No escape."

He looked down at Lena, and she looked up at him, and between them passed the thing neither of them said: *and the newest entry in that ledger, the freshest debt of all, is a girl with a bone whistle and a soul the river couldn't hold.*

Except — he frowned. Turned back to the page. Read the final line again.

*"Nothing owed may be collected from an empty vessel."*

An empty vessel. The phrase snagged in him like a hook. Because Lena was not empty — she was the opposite of empty, she was so full of the old tongue and stubbornness and terrible beautiful courage that the river itself had recoiled from her. But the Court's books did not record courage. The Court's books recorded *status*. And in the Court's books, Lena was listed as a vessel — a container awaiting filling — and containers, by definition, were...

"Your soul," he said slowly. "When your family sold it. When it was sold *for* you, before you could consent. Where did it go?"

Her eyes widened. "To the archive. With the others. You heard them singing—"

"Singing. Yes." His mind was moving fast now, leaping gaps, landing badly but landing. "But the archive keeps the *shards*. The glass. And the glass sings because the soul is in it. Except yours wasn't in the archive, was it? We looked. The Ledger-Keeper showed me the books — your line, the debt, the vessel clause — but there was no shard for you. No glass with your name on it. Your soul was never stored. It was *assigned*. Held in reserve. Waiting for the turn." He gripped her shoulders, gently, feeling her tremble like a plucked string. "Lena. If the thirteenth turn is happening now — tonight, tomorrow, not in four nights — then somewhere beneath that mountain, right now, they are preparing to pour your soul back into you. Whole. Legal. Irrevocable. And then hollow you around it."

Outside the shrine, the river murmured on, carrying its cargo of returned lives toward the mountain. And above the eastern ridgeline, impossibly early, impossibly wrong, the moon was setting — and as it sank, Sebastian saw with a lurch that its lower edge was already thinning. Waxing. Turning.

Somewhere in the folds of his coat, wrapped in wool, his grandmother's stolen shard began — softly, sweetly, horribly — to sing.
