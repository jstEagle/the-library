## 26. Allies on the Road

Marta had never liked the way Sebastian Okafor walked.

It was a small thing to notice after thirteen years of sharing a village with a man, but she noticed everything about her guests — it was how an innkeeper survived, reading the weight in a stranger's step before he ever reached the bar. Most men walked like they were going somewhere. Sebastian walked like he was trying not to disturb anything on his way there, as if the ground were a sleeping animal and his boots might wake it. Even now, even after all this time, he moved through the valley like a man apologizing for existing.

She'd told herself for years that it was just his manner. A quiet man's quiet walk. But quiet men didn't flinch when thunder rolled over the ridge and then went silent, as if the storm itself had been muzzled. Quiet men didn't stand at their windows during the worst weather of the year with their hands pressed flat against the glass like they were holding something back — or holding something in.

So when he left — when Marta woke on the third morning after the riders came through the ford and found his shutters closed, his hearth cold, and a neat stack of coins under her inkpot that covered what he owed and then some — she did not tell herself he'd be back.

She packed a bag instead.

* * *

"You've gone soft in the head," said Old Bram, when she stood in the square at dawn with her pack on her shoulder and Sebastian's direction fixed in her mind like a splinter. "The man's kept himself to himself for thirteen years. Whatever business he's got with the mountain, it's his business."

"His business," Marta repeated. She looked around the square, at the faces gathered there — because of course they'd gathered; a village always gathers when someone is about to do something foolish, drawn like moths to the promise of spectacle. "And when the mountain's business comes here instead? When those masked riders come back through our ford looking for him and don't find him? You think they'll tip their hats and ride on?"

Nobody answered. The wind moved through the barley on the south slope, and the sound it made was too much like whispering for anyone's comfort.

"He took in that child," said Tam Weller's wife, Ilsa, from the edge of the crowd. She had her youngest on her hip, a girl of three who stared at Marta with the solemn, unblinking attention of the very young. "The mute one. Fed her. Kept her warm through the storm-night. Nobody asked him to."

"He did," Marta agreed. "And nobody asked us to do this either." She shifted her pack. "That's rather the point."

It was Ilsa who stepped forward first. Then Tam, scowling at his wife as though she'd volunteered him personally, which, in the grammar of marriage, she had. Then Old Bram, muttering something about fools and graves and how at his age he'd earned the right to choose his own. By the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge, eleven of them stood in the square with packs and walking sticks and the particular stubborn silence of people who have decided something and will not be talked out of it.

Eleven villagers. Going after a storm-caller who had spent thirteen years making sure no one would ever need to go after him.

Marta looked at them and felt something in her chest that was not quite hope and not quite fear but lived in the neighborhood of both.

"Well," she said. "Best not keep him waiting."

* * *

They followed the river road north, past the ford where the riders had turned back, and Marta tried not to look at the water too long. Something had happened there. The whole village knew it without knowing how they knew it — the way you know a room has been argued in before you enter it. The reeds along the near bank were bent flat in a perfect circle, as if the wind had knelt there. And the water, which should have been brown with autumn silt, ran clear as glass for thirty paces downstream before remembering how to be a river again.

Ilsa touched the iron bell hanging at her belt — one of the old chapel bells, cracked in the year of the fever, too damaged to ring true and so left to hang forgotten in the belfry until Tam climbed up and cut it down two nights ago.

"You really think these will work?" Marta asked her, low, as they walked.

"I think," Ilsa said, "that my grandmother used to say the mountain-folk can't abide a broken note. That they hear everything, everything, everything, and a bell that rings wrong is like a shout in a sickroom." She shrugged. "Maybe it's nonsense. But my grandmother also said never to buy eggs from a man with clean boots, and she wasn't wrong about that either."

There were nine bells between them, pulled from the chapel and the mill and old Widow Hearn's gatepost, where one had hung for sixty years against nothing anyone could name. They clanked softly with every step, out of rhythm with each other, a small discordant procession. Marta found the sound less annoying than comforting. It was honest noise. It was the noise of ordinary things refusing to be quiet.

The child's trail was easy enough to follow, once you knew what to look for. Not footprints — the road was hard clay — but other signs. Where they had camped, the grass grew in a spiral. Marta saw it at the second site and made everyone stop and look: a whorl of green turning inward on itself, tight as a snail shell, while all around it the grass lay flat and ordinary.

"That's her doing?" asked young Piet, who was seventeen and had volunteered mostly, Marta suspected, for the adventure of it.

"That or the boy," said Bram. "Or the man himself."

"No," Marta said quietly. She crouched and put her hand above the spiral without touching it. There was a warmth coming off it, faint as breath on a windowpane. "He mends roofs. He fixes fences. In thirteen years I have never seen him leave so much as a crooked nail behind him. This isn't his." She stood. "This is the child. And we should be careful, because whatever she is, the mountain wants her badly enough to send riders."

They walked on. The bells clanked. Somewhere ahead, beneath a mountain none of them could yet see, a girl who spoke in the language before kingdoms was walking toward the thing that wanted to hollow her out.

* * *

They caught up on the fourth day, at the place where the trade road bent around a hill of black stone and the air changed.

Marta felt it first as a pressure in her ears, like descending into a cellar. Then the bells began to hum — not ring, hum, a low uneasy drone, each one finding a different pitch, so that the whole company sounded like a hive disturbed. Piet stopped walking. Bram spat. And around the bend of the road, silhouetted against a sky bruised purple with evening, came figures.

For one horrible heartbeat Marta thought: *riders*. Her hand found the handle of the little skinning knife she carried and had never once used on anything larger than a hare.

But the figures were on foot, and stumbling, and one of them was large and broad-shouldered in a way she'd know anywhere, because she'd watched him carry her delivery crates up her steps for thirteen years without ever letting one touch the ground.

"Sebastian!" she called, and her voice cracked on his name like a green branch.

He stopped. Turned. And the look on his face — Marta would remember it the rest of her life — was not joy. It was horror. Pure, undiluted horror, the face of a man watching a cart roll toward the edge of a cliff with people in it.

"What are you doing here?" His voice carried down the road, rough with something between anger and fear. "Go back. All of you — turn around, go home, now—"

"We're not going home," Marta said, and kept walking.

Beside him stood the child — Lena, though he hadn't named her that in front of the village, though Marta only knew it because she'd heard him say it once through an open window last spring, soft as a prayer. The girl watched the approaching villagers with wide dark eyes, and her small hand was wrapped around a bone whistle, and the whistle was humming in harmony with the bells, and Marta thought: *of course. Of course it is.*

Behind Sebastian and Lena, half-hidden in the shadow of the black-stone hill, stood others. Travelers. A woman in layered shawls with coins sewn into the hems. Two men with the rope-scarred hands of teamsters. A boy of maybe twelve leading a donkey loaded with pots. Refugees, or pilgrims, or simply people who had been on the road when the sky started doing what the sky was apparently doing now.

Because the sky was doing something. Above the black hill, clouds had gathered in a slow rotation, turning like a wheel, and at their center was a darkness that was not cloud at all but absence — a hole in the evening, patient and vast. Every few heartbeats, light flickered inside it without sound. Lightning that never became thunder. A storm being swallowed somewhere, or stored, or saved.

"The thirteenth turn," Sebastian said, when Marta reached him. He was gripping her shoulders, actually gripping her, and his hands were shaking. "Marta. Listen to me. The moon completes its turn in six days, and when it does, the Court comes for her — not just riders, not just hunters, the Court itself, and everything under its shadow answers to it. Anyone standing near her when it happens—" He stopped. Swallowed. "You have to take your people home."

"They're not my people," Marta said. "They're ours. That's the whole trouble with villages, Sebastian — you live in one long enough and the fool thing starts thinking it has opinions."

"Marta—"

"Thirteen years." She said it loudly enough for the whole road to hear, because some things needed witnesses. "Thirteen years you mended our roofs and dug our graves and sat with our dying so they wouldn't go alone, and you think we didn't notice? You think we didn't know exactly what you were? We knew. We knew and we let you stay, every single year, and do you know why?"

He didn't answer. Behind him, the child watched Marta with an expression far too old for her face.

"Because a man is what he does when no one's making him," Marta said. "And you, Sebastian Okafor, spent thirteen years being kind with no reason to be. So no. We're not going home. We're coming with you, and you can be angry about it the whole way if it keeps your legs moving."

The wind off the black hill gusted then, and it smelled of hot glass and deep stone, and every bell on the road rang out at once — a broken, jagged, glorious chord, nine cracked voices crying discord into the gathering dark.

And the hole in the sky flinched.

Marta saw it. Saw the slow wheel of cloud stutter, saw the soundless lightning gutter like a candle in a draft. Saw, on the road below, the travelers cross themselves or clutch charms or simply stare, and saw the child Lena lift her head toward the wounded sky with something that was almost — almost — a smile.

"It hears us," Ilsa whispered beside her. "The bells. It actually hears us."

"Of course it does," said Old Bram, shouldering his pack and starting up the road. "Everything up there listens. That's its whole problem." He glanced back at them, one eyebrow raised, daring anyone to comment on the fact that a seventy-year-old man was walking toward a god's front door. "Well? Are we standing about all night, or are we going to go make some noise?"

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the black hill, villagers and travelers together, and Marta learned the shape of the road behind them. The peddler-queen — for that was what the shawled woman was, though she wore no crown, only forty years of roads in her spine and a name she gave as Sefa, Queen of Nothing You Can Tax — had been guiding stragglers away from the mountain for days. Word traveled strange and fast along the trade roads: the Court was calling in old debts early, the river had given back a memory, the moon was hurrying.

"The Court doesn't hurry," Sefa told them over the fire, turning her cup in her hands without drinking. "That's what frightens me. Bureaucracy is patient the way stone is patient. If the Ledger-Keeper is moving before the appointed hour, it means the books have stopped balancing. It means something has gone wrong in the arithmetic." Her eyes, dark and quick, found Sebastian across the fire. "Something you did, perhaps. Or something she did." A nod toward Lena, who sat close against Sebastian's side, the bone whistle cradled in both hands like a sleeping bird.

"She ran away from them," Sebastian said. "Before I ever met her. Whatever's gone wrong in their arithmetic, she started it."

"And you finished it," Sefa said, "when you crossed the boundary and let your storms come home to you. Don't look surprised, storm-caller. The sky tells stories to anyone who knows how to listen, and I've been listening for forty years. There is a man-shaped silence walking toward the mountain, and inside the silence, thunder." She smiled, thin as a knife. "The Court can hear you coming, Sebastian Okafor. The question is whether it can hear anything else."

Marta watched him absorb that — watched his jaw tighten, watched his hand settle protectively on the child's shoulder — and understood, finally, the full shape of what they had walked into. Not a rescue. Not really. A negotiation, maybe, or a war conducted in contracts and clauses, in names and ledgers, in the terrible polite language of things that owned people.

And here they were: an innkeeper, a stonemason, a grandmother of four, a boy who'd never seen anything worse than a broken leg, carrying nine cracked bells and a bag of bread and the unshakable conviction that you did not let your people walk into the dark alone.

"Get some sleep, all of you," Marta said, standing, brushing off her skirts. "Tomorrow we climb."

Lena tugged at her sleeve. Marta looked down. The child held up a handful of smooth pebbles, arranged carefully in her palm, and pointed — at the fire, at the bells, at each sleeping villager in turn — and then pressed the pebbles into Marta's hand and closed Marta's fingers over them.

It took Marta a moment. Then she laughed, short and startled, and felt the laugh crack something loose in her chest that had been tight since the morning she'd found the cold hearth.

"A counting," she said. "You're giving me a counting. One pebble per soul, so I can keep track." She looked at the eight stones in her palm, then at the child. "Nine," she corrected gently, and picked up a ninth pebble from the ground, and set it in the girl's own hand, and folded the small fingers closed over it. "Yours counts too. It counted from the first night you knocked on his door."

Lena stared at the stone in her fist. And in the firelight, for just a moment, her eyes filled — not spilling, not yet, but filling, like a cup set under a slow tap — and she looked up at Marta with an expression that needed no tongue at all, in any age of the world.

Above them, the hole in the sky turned and turned. Below them, in the roots of the world, something ancient heard nine cracked bells and did not understand them, and hated what it could not understand.

And Marta sat down between the child and the storm-caller, put another branch on the fire, and kept watch.
