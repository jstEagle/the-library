## 28. Voided, Not Ended

The mountain let us go the way a fist lets go of something it has decided not to crush — slowly, finger by finger, as if it might change its mind.

We came down out of the glass roots in the last grey hour before dawn, Lena's hand locked in mine, her other hand still curled around the bone whistle. Behind us the palace of lantern-moths glowed like a wound under the skin of the world, and I kept waiting for the shout, the alarm to rise again, the roots to close around our ankles. They did not. The tunnels simply emptied themselves behind us, corridor after corridor, until we stood blinking on the mountainside with real wind in our faces and frost forming on our eyelashes.

The shard was inside my coat, against my chest, wrapped in three layers of wool. It was warm. It had been warm since I took it from its cradle of black glass, and it grew warmer whenever my heart beat faster, which meant that for most of the descent it had been nearly hot enough to scald. My grandmother sang inside it. Not words I could make out — not yet, not without the rite — but a low thread of melody, patient as groundwater, as if she had been waiting thirteen years and could wait thirteen more without complaint.

She had waited longer than that, I reminded myself. She had waited through her own death, folded into singing glass in an archive beneath the earth, sold twice over: once by the Court's ledger, once by her own secret hand. She had given herself so that the contract would have a flaw in it, a hairline crack running through all that beautiful cruelty, and she had left the crack for me to find.

* * *

We made camp at the tree line, where the pines bent away from the mountain like men flinching from a blow. Lena built the fire without being asked — she was quick with fires now, quick with everything, as though the weeks on the road had taught her hands what her voice had never learned to say. She got the flames going small and clean, then sat beside me and leaned her weight against my arm, which was how she said many things.

I unwrapped the shard.

In the firelight it looked like nothing — a sliver of smoked glass no longer than my thumb, ugly, chipped at one end. But when I held it up, the light moved wrong inside it. It pooled. It gathered. And the song rose, faint but unmistakable, a woman's voice humming a tune I knew from before I knew anything else: the lullaby my grandmother used to sing while she shelled beans, the one she claimed was nonsense and I now understood had been old-tongue all along, every syllable of it, hidden in plain sight in a farmhouse kitchen while the storms answered her grandson's temper outside.

Lena watched it the way she watched all magic — without fear, without hunger, with the flat clear attention of someone who had seen what magic did to people who wanted it too much.

"It worked," I said, because saying it aloud made it more true. "The rite worked. The debt is voided."

The words sat between us and neither of us believed them entirely. That was the trouble with bargains made by things like the Court beneath the Mountain: they were never finished, only paused. You did not escape them. You survived them, and survival felt so much like victory that you forgot to check over your shoulder.

I checked over my shoulder anyway. Habit. The slope above us was empty, silvered with frost, utterly silent.

That should have been my first warning. The mountain is never silent. Even in exile, even through the valley's dampening field, I had always been able to feel it — a pressure at the edge of hearing, like a word someone is about to say. Now there was nothing. The silence had a shape, and the shape was listening.

* * *

It came for us at midmorning, when the sun had burned the frost off and the pines were dripping.

Not riders this time. Not hunters. The Smiling One came alone, walking up the trail as if out for a stroll, dressed in travelling grey with its hands clasped behind its back, and I want to tell you that I reached for a storm or a stone or even just Lena's wrist to run — but the truth is I only stood there, because some part of me had known since the tree line that this conversation was coming, and dread, when it arrives on schedule, loses half its terror.

"Sebastian Okafor," it said, and my name in its mouth sounded like a coin being bitten. "And the vessel. And—" it tilted its head, and its smile deepened by a degree that I felt rather than saw, "—the grandmother. How industrious you've all been."

"You can't have her," I said. "The sale is voided. A soul freely released by its seller ends the contract. Your own archive says so. Your own ledger says so."

"Oh, it does." The Smiling One strolled closer, boots silent on the frozen mud, and crouched down to Lena's level with the unhurried grace of someone greeting a child they already owned. Lena stepped back into me. I put my hand on her shoulder and felt her trembling start and stop, start and stop, like a bird deciding whether to fly. "The Ledger-Keeper is beside himself. Do you know what a voided contract does to the books? Pages rewrite themselves. Ink runs uphill. He hasn't slept — well. He doesn't sleep. But he has been very agitated, in his way."

"Then we're done."

"We are done with one thing." It rose, brushed off its knees, and turned that face to me — that warm, reasonable, terrible face. "But you took something, Sebastian Okafor. You didn't ask. You didn't petition. You crept into the Archive of Sold Souls during our festival, while our people danced, and you stole a shard of living glass out of its cradle."

"She stole herself first," I said. "She sold herself to buy the flaw. That makes it hers."

"Mm. A lovely theory. The older law disagrees." The Smiling One began to walk a slow circle around our little camp, around the dead fire, around the shard in my hand, and as it walked it spoke in the tone of a schoolmaster reciting something it had recited ten thousand times. "Before the ledgers, before the vessels, before the Court had faces at all, there was one law under the mountain, and it is carved into the first root, and it says this: *what is taken must be returned, or what was taken from will be taken in turn.*"

I felt the cold go through me before I understood it.

"The valley," I said.

"The valley," it agreed pleasantly. "Your dampening field. Your quiet village. Your innkeeper who defended you, your neighbours who taught the girl to bake, your children who will grow up thinking thunder is only weather. War comes to places like that slowly, Sebastian Okafor. It doesn't arrive with horns. It arrives with a bad winter that lasts two years. With wells that sicken. With children born hollow-eyed. We are very patient, and very thorough, and we have done it before." It smiled wider. "Or."

"Or?"

"Or the shard goes back into its cradle, and the voided contract stands — the Ledger-Keeper's books be damned — and everyone lives exactly as long and as hollow as they were always going to." It spread its hands, presenting the choice like a merchant presenting scales. "Return what was stolen. Walk back down. Give her up."

Lena's hand found mine. Her grip was fierce. She shook her head, hard, once, twice — *no, no, no* — and when I looked down at her I saw she had gone pale as the frost, and that her jaw was set in the particular way it set when she was about to do something brave and stupid, like blow the whistle, like run, like offer herself.

"No," I said — to her, not to it. Then, to the Smiling One: "You're lying. Partly. If war were truly yours to unleash, you'd have unleashed it the moment the alarm rang. You need the shard returned *willingly*. That's the catch, isn't it? The old law wants a return, not a repossession. You need me to walk down that mountain on my own feet."

The smile did not waver. But something behind it shifted — a weight redistributing, a card turning over.

"You always did read contracts well," it said softly. "Your grandmother was the same."

* * *

It left us then — it simply wished us good day and walked back down the trail, whistling three notes of my grandmother's lullaby, which told me everything about how much it knew and how little it feared. Lena and I sat by the dead fire for a long time after it was gone.

She wrote in the dirt with a stick. She had gotten fast at this, our private second language, faster than speech almost: *Don't go. Run. The whistle — I can hide us, I did it before.*

"I know you can," I said. "For a while. Maybe a long while. But hiding isn't living, little one, and I've done thirteen years of it already. I know exactly what it costs, and I know it compounds." I crouched in front of her and took both her hands. They were cold. I warmed them the ordinary way, with my own palms, the way any father could. "Listen to me. The contract is voided. Whatever happens next, that is done — your soul is yours, bought by no one, owed to no one. That was the whole war, and we won it. What's left is only the shape of the retreat."

Her eyes were wet and furious. She pulled one hand free and wrote, pressing hard enough to break the stick: *They'll keep you.*

"They might," I said honestly. She deserved honesty; everyone had lied to her for eight years, and look what it made. "But here's the thing about me, Lena. I spent thirteen years learning how to stay in one place without being broken by it. Most men can't do that. I can. And I'm not going down there empty-handed, either. I'm going down there holding the one thing their oldest law can't argue with: a willing return. There's power in that. Even the Smiling One has to bow to the first root."

*They'll take the shard and keep you anyway,* she wrote.

"Then they'll have broken their own oldest law in front of a witness," I said, "and the first root remembers everything. That's why the law is carved instead of written, little one. Stone doesn't get rewritten by frightened ledger-keepers." I smiled, and found, to my own surprise, that the smile was mostly real. "Besides. You forget what I am."

She frowned.

"I'm a man who walks toward storms," I said. "It's the family trade."

* * *

We argued until the light went gold and then went blue. She exhausted every argument the dirt could hold — *run north, run to the sea, the whistle, the whistle, MARTA WILL COME* — and I countered each one, and in the end what settled it was not an argument at all. It was the shard. It had grown cold in my hand, colder than the evening air, and when I lifted it to show her, the song inside it had changed. Quieter. Sadder. A woman's voice singing a lullaby to a child who wouldn't listen.

"She knows," I said quietly. "Whatever she is now, wherever she is in there — she knows what I'm about to do, and she's telling me it's right. Or telling me it's foolish. With her it was always the same song."

Lena looked at the shard for a long time. Then she did something I did not expect. She reached up and pressed her palm flat against the glass, and closed her eyes, and hummed — the lullaby, note for note, the old tongue rising out of her throat unbroken, the language before kingdoms, and the shard *answered*. It flared warm. It harmonized. For three heartbeats the mountainside held two voices singing one song across the gap between the living and the sold, and then it faded, and Lena opened her eyes, and she nodded. Once.

Permission. From both of them.

I wrapped the shard back in its wool, tucked it against my chest, and shouldered the pack. Lena packed her own bag — she'd insisted on carrying her own since the ford — and we stood at the edge of the firelight looking down the trail, at the darkening folds of the mountain, at the faint glow far below where the glass roots began.

"One condition," I told her. "You don't come past the tree line. You wait here. Marta may already be on the road — if anyone comes, you blow the whistle and you run for the valley and you don't look back. Swear it."

She swore it the way she swore everything: by drawing the spiral in the air between us, the coiled tower, the mark of the Spire-folk. Then, before I could turn away, she caught my sleeve and pulled me down and kissed my cheek, quick and fierce, and spoke her first word of the day aloud, in the old tongue, in a voice like a small bell:

*"Come back."*

Two words. I carried them down the mountain the way other men carry torches.

* * *

The trail down was longer than the trail up. That is always the way of returning; the road that fled beneath your feet on the way out measures every step on the way back. I walked through the blue dusk and then the full dark, and the glow of the glass roots rose around me like the memory of a fire, and the moth-lanterns drifted up out of the depths to meet me — dozens of them, then hundreds, a slow ascending river of pale wings, and not one of them touched me. They escorted me. They had orders, I supposed. Or courtesy. Under the mountain, I had learned, those were often the same thing.

At the threshold of the root-tunnels I stopped and took out the shard one last time.

"Grandmother," I said. "If this goes wrong — if they keep me, if the law bends the way laws bend for people like them — find her. However you can. Sing loud enough that she hears you, and teach her the rest of the lullaby, because I only ever learned the first verse, and I think—" my voice did something I hadn't authorized, and I let it, "—I think she's going to need the ending someday."

The shard pulsed against my palm, warm as a sleeping bird, and I swear to you it hummed the next line. The one I'd never known. As if it had been saving it.

I put it away. I squared my shoulders like a man walking into weather, which was the only kind of squaring I'd ever been taught. And I walked down into the mountain — not dragged, not driven, not traded — a free man, walking freely, carrying back what was stolen, to face whatever the three faces had prepared for a man who had beaten their contract and stolen their glass and dared, in defiance of everything they were, to love something enough to give himself away.

The first root waited below, carved with the oldest law. And I intended, before this was finished, to read every word of it myself.
