## 29. Before the Three Faces Again

They took his hands.

That was the first thing Sebastian understood about being tried by the Court Beneath the Mountain: it was not a matter of standing in a dock or answering to a bench of robed judges. It was a matter of hands. Two moth-servants — the same gentle, grey-veined creatures that had guided him through the archive, though this one would not meet his eyes now — wrapped his wrists in ribbons of woven glass, and the ribbons drank what little warmth he had left and gave nothing back. His hands had been empty since the mountain's shoulder. Empty of storms, empty of thunder, empty of the low electric hum that had lived in his bones for thirty years before the valley and thirteen after it. But the ribbons did not care whether the hands held anything. They cared only that they were *his* hands, and that he was walking down of his own will, which was somehow worse than chains.

Lena tried to follow. Of course she did. She got three steps past the threshold of the great root-hall before the glass roots themselves rose from the floor like slow green waves and folded around her, not cruelly, not even firmly — the way a hedge closes around a garden. She pressed her palms flat against them. Through the translucent wall of her prison he could see her mouth moving, shaping words in the old tongue, and though no sound reached him he knew every one of them. *Don't. Don't. Don't.*

"I'll come back," he said, and hated how small it sounded in a hall built for voices like avalanches.

The roots did not move. The moth-servant touched his elbow, feather-light, and gestured downward, and Sebastian walked.

* * *

The hall beneath the palace was older than the palace above it, older than the glass roots, older perhaps than the mountain itself, if mountains could be said to have an inside the way people do. It was a hollow shaped like the throat of a bell, and its walls were not stone but compressed dark — a blackness so old and so patient that it had texture, like velvet rubbed the wrong way for ten thousand years. Lantern-moths hung from nothing at all, drifting in slow constellations, and their light fell upward instead of down, so that Sebastian felt lit from below, shadowed from above, presented rather than seen.

At the center of the floor stood three chairs.

He had met the Three Faces in their audience chamber, amid cushions and honeyed wine and hospitality dressed in silk. This was different. Here there were no cushions. Here the Host sat on a chair of pale root-wood with his hands folded in his lap like a man waiting for a train he knew would be late. The Ledger-Keeper occupied a chair whose legs were made of stacked ledgers — actual books, bound in leather gone soft with age, fused into furniture by some patience Sebastian did not want to imagine. And the Smiling One —

The Smiling One stood beside her chair. She did not sit. She never seemed to sit; she seemed instead to be perpetually in the act of deciding to sit, and finding the decision more entertaining than the act.

"Sebastian Okafor," said the Host, and his voice was warm bread, was hearth-smoke, was every kind voice Sebastian had ever half-trusted. "You came back."

"You left me very little choice," Sebastian said. His voice cracked on the second word. Thirteen years of silence had not prepared him for speaking without power behind his tongue; he had forgotten how naked a human voice was when it carried only itself.

"We left you every choice," said the Ledger-Keeper. Her voice was dry paper turning. She opened a book on her knee — one of thousands, each spine labeled in a script that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts. "You could have stayed in the valley. You could have let the turn pass and given us the vessel as the contract required. You could have accepted my colleague's offer and kept your magic forever." A page turned. The sound of it went through the hall like a knife through silk. "Instead you stole from our archive, fled across our river, burned out your name-magic on a dead woman's glass, and walked back down to face us with nothing in your hands but your own stubbornness."

"Yes," Sebastian said.

The Ledger-Keeper looked up. For the first time since he had met her, something moved behind her eyes that was not arithmetic. "Yes," she repeated, as though tasting a foreign word. "No argument. No justification. No plea."

"Would it change what's written?"

"No."

"Then I'll save us both the ink."

Somewhere above them, faint as a memory, Lena's whistle hummed. The whole hall shivered. The lantern-moths scattered and regrouped, and for one heartbeat the upward light guttered, and in that heartbeat Sebastian saw the walls for what they were: not darkness, but *glass*, miles of it, black glass packed with singing — the archive went deeper than he had dreamed, the sold souls were not kept in a room, they were the room, they were the mountain's marrow, and every one of them was singing the same wordless note of waiting.

Then the light steadied, and the walls were darkness again, and he wished he had never seen.

* * *

"The charge," said the Host, "is theft of a soul-shard from the Archive of Sold Souls."

"The charge," said the Ledger-Keeper, "is violation of contract clause nine, bloodline binding, the Okafor line, signature Marta-Okafor-senior, hereinafter the Seller."

"The charge," said the Smiling One, and her smile widened by a degree that should not have been possible on a human face, "is love."

Silence. Even the moths seemed to hold their drift.

"That is not a charge," Sebastian said.

"It is the oldest one," said the Smiling One. She began, at last, to circle him — slowly, her bare feet making no sound on the glass floor, her robes the color of dawn pretending to be night. "Every crime in our books, if you read far enough back, is love with the wrong address. Theft: love of a thing over its owner. Treason: love of one crown over another. And theft of a soul-shard—" she stopped before him, close enough that he could see that her smile did not reach her eyes, because her eyes were busy doing something else entirely, something that looked almost like grief, "—love of one soul over the ledger. The most common crime in our history. And the rarest, in its true form."

"In its true form," Sebastian repeated.

"Most who steal souls steal them *back*," she said. "To keep. To wear. To feed on. Grief makes monsters of the bereaved; you would be astonished — or perhaps not — how many widows have come down these stairs to carve their husbands out of the glass and carry them home in jars." Her head tilted. "You did not take your grandmother's shard to keep her. You took it to free her. You poured your own magic into the release — the last of it, all of it, thirteen years of stored storm spent in a single rite — and then, when we invoked the old clause, when war stood at your valley's door, you did not run, did not hide behind iron bells and dampening fields and the villagers who love you." She spread her hands. "You walked back down. Powerless. To be judged by the thing you robbed."

"I walked back down," Sebastian said quietly, "because you threatened my home."

"Mm," said the Smiling One. "That is what you believe. Hold on to it a moment longer. It is about to become very precious to you."

* * *

The Host rose from his chair of pale wood, and when he rose, the hall changed. Sebastian could not have said how — the proportions were the same, the moths the same, the dark the same — but it felt suddenly smaller, suddenly *attended*, the way a forest feels attended when you realize the birds have gone quiet.

"Sebastian Okafor," the Host said. "Before we pass sentence, the Court offers you the truth. Not as mercy. Truth is never mercy. We offer it because the contract requires full disclosure at judgment, and we are — whatever else we may be — creatures of contract." He folded his hands again. "Ask your question. Any question. It will be answered truly. That is the law."

Sebastian's mouth was dry. He thought of a hundred questions — why me, why her, why the whistle, why the river took his wedding day and could not hold Lena, why the Smiling One had let them go through the glass roots with that terrible easy grace — and found that underneath all of them was only one question, the one he had been carrying since the archive, since the shard had sung his grandmother's name in a voice he remembered from childhood kitchens:

"Why did you chase us?"

The Smiling One's smile did not waver. But everything behind it did — some vast interior architecture shifting, settling, like snow sliding off a roof.

"Ah," she said softly. "There it is."

"Answer him," said the Ledger-Keeper, and closed her book.

The Host looked at the Smiling One. Some silent negotiation passed between the three faces — Sebastian caught fragments of it, the way one catches fragments of conversation through a wall: a tightening at the Host's jaw, a flicker of the Ledger-Keeper's pen-fingers, the Smiling One's shoulders rising and falling once in something that was almost, almost a sigh.

And then she laughed.

It was not the laugh he had heard in the audience chamber, warm and dreadful and hospitable. This laugh was smaller, wearier, and infinitely more human, and it frightened him more than everything else in the mountain put together, because it was the laugh of someone setting down a weight they had carried so long they had forgotten it was not part of their body.

"Do you know what we are?" she asked.

"The Court Beneath the Mountain," Sebastian said. "Keepers of sold souls."

"Keepers," she agreed. "Custodians. Wardens. Jailers, if you prefer the honest word — we prefer it ourselves, in private." She drifted closer, circling again, and the upward light made her shadow climb the walls like a second, taller woman. "But custodians of *what*, Sebastian Okafor? Souls? Yes. But souls cannot be kept unless they are first surrendered. And souls are never surrendered freely. Not truly. They are sold in desperation, traded in fear, gambled away in grief — always under duress, always under the weight of circumstance, and a soul taken under duress does not rest. It *chafes*. Have you wondered why the archive sings? Why the glass hums day and night? It is not contentment. It is fourteen thousand souls pressing against the inside of their prisons, whispering *this was not fair, this was not fair, this was not fair.*"

Her voice dropped, and the hall leaned in to hear it.

"A soul given freely cannot be kept at all. Freely given, freely living — such a soul passes through our hands like water and leaves no stain. Do you understand? For six hundred years the Court has searched for one — one single seller who would give a soul away in love rather than in fear. One free gift. Because a free gift is the only thing in existence that can void a sale retroactively — that can crack the ledger open along its spine and let the light in. And in six hundred years, across eleven kingdoms and every desperate bloodline that ever signed with us, we found no one. Until your grandmother."

Sebastian's heart stopped, started, stopped again.

"She sold herself," he said. "I heard her. In the archive. She added her own shard to the payment — secretly —"

"She did," the Smiling One said, and for a moment her smile was gone entirely, and what lay beneath it was so tired and so old that Sebastian's knees nearly buckled. "A woman who sold a grandchild's soul to save her family, and then, unable to live with it, sold her own to soften the price — hoping no one would ever notice, hoping the two shards would press against each other in the glass until they wore each other thin. She was *almost* the one. Almost. But she gave in guilt, and guilt is only fear wearing its Sunday clothes. It was not enough. It has never been enough." The smile returned, transformed now, unbearable. "So we waited. Contracts ripen. Debts descend. Bloodlines braid themselves down through the generations toward the moment we designed them for."

"Designed," Sebastian whispered. "The thirteenth turn. The vessel. Lena — none of it was —"

"It was a test," said the Ledger-Keeper, and her dry voice had lost its dryness; it sounded, for the first time, like a woman reading her own confession. "The vessel-system, the hollowing, the reclaiming — cruelty, yes, real cruelty, we do not pretend otherwise — but also a sieve. Every generation, the debt comes due, and every generation we watch what the debtor does. Most pay. Most hand over the child and go home and do not sleep well and die eventually, and their souls come to us too, in time, and we file them beside the ones they sold. Some flee. Some fight. Some bargain, and bargain, and bargain, trying to keep both the child and their power, their name, their comfort." She opened her book again, to the last page, and Sebastian saw that it was blank. "In six hundred years, no debtor has ever done what you did. Given everything. Asked nothing. Walked back down with empty hands."

"The chase," Sebastian said. The words came slowly, each one a stone turned over. "The hunters at the ford. The clause about war. The Smiling One letting us go through the roots. The twelfth turn ending early. All of it —"

"Was pressure," said the Host gently. "We needed to know what you would do when every door was closing. Kindness proves nothing; anyone is generous when the sun shines. We needed the dark. We needed you hunted, exhausted, magicless, alone — and still choosing to give."

"And the war clause," Sebastian said. "If I hadn't come back —"

"There was never going to be a war," said the Smiling One. "The clause is a hinge, not a hammer. It exists to bring the debtor back down the mountain. It has brought every debtor back down the mountain for six hundred years. You are the four hundred and eleventh." She paused. "You are the first to arrive with nothing left to lose and nothing left to want — except her."

Above them, muffled through miles of root and glass, Lena's whistle sang again — long, wavering, a note like a lighthouse searching. The lantern-moths all turned toward the sound at once, hundreds of small faces, and Sebastian understood suddenly that they were not decorations. They were messengers. They were the Court's ears. And every one of them was straining toward a mute child playing a bone whistle in a garden of glass roots, because in six hundred years, nothing down here had ever sounded like that — like someone calling not to take, but to make sure you were still there.

"What happens now?" Sebastian asked.

"Now," said the Host, "the Court keeps its oldest appointment. The one we have been preparing for since before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath." He descended from the dais, one step, two, until he stood level with Sebastian, and this close Sebastian could see that the Host's warm eyes were wet. "A free gift voids the sale. Your grandmother's soul, released by her own blood, freely — that cracks the ledger. But a ledger, once cracked, does not mend. Everything filed under duress becomes contestable. Fourteen thousand souls, Sebastian. Fourteen thousand sales, every one of them about to be reopened."

"And the Court," said the Ledger-Keeper, rising at last from her chair of books, "must decide what it becomes when its shelves stand empty."

The Smiling One came to stand before him, and she took his ribbon-bound hands in hers, and her grip was astonishingly gentle, and her smile — her true smile, the one under the smile — was the loneliest thing he had ever seen.

"So," she said, "before we rewrite the world, we must settle one final account. Yours. And I warn you, Sebastian Okafor, son of a storm-caller, grandson of a woman who loved badly and bravely: the price we are about to ask will cost you the one thing you have left."

The lantern-moths dimmed. Somewhere far above, the whistle fell silent, and the silence was worse.

"The ledger is open," said the Ledger-Keeper, and turned the blank page toward him. "Tell us your name."
