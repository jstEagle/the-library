## 30. The Bargain Rewritten

The trial chamber had not changed since the last time Sebastian stood in it — or perhaps it had changed in every way that mattered, and only its bones remained. The glass roots still arched overhead like the ribs of some buried leviathan, and the lantern-moths still drifted through them in slow constellations, but their light was different now. Softer. Warmer, if a light born underground could be called warm. It fell on the three faces of the Court like candlelight on masks, and for the first time since he had descended beneath the mountain, Sebastian did not feel that the light itself was watching him.

His grandmother's shard hung at his throat, wrapped in wool, still faintly singing. He could feel it against his sternum — a vibration more felt than heard, like a hymn remembered by the body after the mind has forgotten the words. Her soul. Freed by his own hands, poured out of his own storms, spent down to ash and silence. When he reached for the place inside himself where the weather had always lived, he found nothing there. Not emptiness exactly. More like a room after the furniture has been carried out: the shape of what had been, printed in dust.

He was, for the first time in thirteen years, entirely ordinary. And he had walked back down into the dark anyway.

* * *

The Host spoke first, as the Host always did.

It sat at the center of the crescent dais, robed in grey silk that moved like water finding its level, and its face — the Court's faces were not faces so much as agreements about what a face should be — wore the expression of a landlord greeting a tenant who has paid early. "Sebastian Okafor," it said, and the name rang through the chamber like a coin dropped on marble. "Storm-caller. Grandson of Adaeze Okafor. Seller of souls." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "And, as of last night, thief."

"I don't deny it," Sebastian said. His voice sounded strange to him without thunder behind it. Smaller. Human. "I stole her back. I'd do it again."

"We know," said the Ledger-Keeper.

That was the second face, seated to the Host's left with an open book across its knees — a book whose pages turned themselves, slowly, like something breathing in its sleep. The Ledger-Keeper's voice was dry as old paper. It had shown Sebastian the books once already, had traced his grandmother's signature with one long finger and explained, kindly, that bloodlines bind and substitutions are not permitted. Now it turned a page and regarded him with what might have been curiosity, if curiosity were a thing that could be kept in a ledger.

"The shard is freed," the Ledger-Keeper continued. "The soul of Adaeze Okafor is no longer held in our archive. It sings in the open air for the first time in sixty years. This presents us with a difficulty of accounting."

"Accounting," Sebastian repeated. Lena's hand was in his. She stood slightly behind his hip, the way she always did when strangers crowded close, and her grip tightened at the word. He squeezed back. *I'm here. I'm not leaving.* He could not say it aloud before the Court — words given to the Court became clauses — but he hoped the pressure of his hand said enough.

"You must understand how we see the world," the Host said, spreading its grey sleeves in a gesture that might have been apology. "Everything is owed or owned. Every soul that enters this mountain enters as a line in a book. Your grandmother sold a vessel — a descendant, unnamed at the time of sale, which was permitted then, though we have since found the practice... imprecise. That debt has passed through your family like an heirloom no one wanted. And now the collateral has walked out of our vaults in a boy's pocket."

"She wasn't collateral," Sebastian said. "She was a child."

"Yes," said the third face.

* * *

The Smiling One had not spoken until now. It never spoke until now. It sat to the Host's right, and where the other two faces were stillness, it was patience — leaning forward slightly, hands folded, wearing the expression of someone who has just heard good news and is deciding whether to share it.

Sebastian had learned, over the past days, to fear the Smiling One most of all. The Host was power; the Ledger-Keeper was law. But the Smiling One was the part of the Court that *wanted things*, and wanting was the root from which every cruelty in this place had grown — dressed, always, in kindness.

"The contract is void," the Smiling One said now, and its voice was almost gentle. "You should understand what that means, because I suspect you don't. A soul freely released by its seller's blood voids the sale — yes. But it does not simply erase the pages. It unbinds everything the sale touched. The vessel-child belongs to no one. Not to us. Not to you. Not to any ledger in this mountain." It tilted its head. "Do you know how long it has been since anything in our books belonged to no one?"

"Is that a riddle?" Sebastian asked.

"It's an admission," said the Smiling One. "We are very old, storm-caller, and we are very careful, and careful things accumulate. Debts. Vessels. Children who forget how to want." Something flickered beneath the smile — brief, unreadable, gone. "Your grandmother's freed soul settles the original debt. That much is arithmetic. But the theft remains. Living glass cried out. Our festival was broken. Our servants scattered like spilled salt. By the oldest clause — older than me, older than the Host, older than the mountain's name — a stolen shard demands return, or war comes to the valley above."

Lena made a sound. Small, low, in her throat — the sound she made instead of *no*. Sebastian stepped half in front of her before he knew he was moving.

"Then take me," he said. "Not the valley. Not her. Me. I stole it. I'll answer for it."

"That," said the Smiling One, "is what we hoped you would say."

* * *

The Host raised one hand, and the chamber changed.

Sebastian felt it rather than saw it — a settling, like the moment before snowfall, when the air decides what it will become. The lantern-moths drew inward, gathering above the dais until their light pooled there in a single slow-turning sphere. The Ledger-Keeper closed its book. The sound of it closing was enormous, though it was only a sound like a door.

"New terms," the Host said. "Hear them fully before you speak, because words spoken here are spoken forever."

Sebastian listened.

"The debt of Adaeze Okafor is settled by her own returned soul. Let it be written that she bought her family's survival twice — once in fear, and once in freedom — and that the second purchase holds." The Ledger-Keeper's pen scratched somewhere in the dark, writing it so. "The vessel known as Lena is struck from every record. She belongs to no court, no contract, no crown beneath or above any mountain. No hand may claim her. No tongue may bind her. If she wishes to walk out of this mountain tomorrow and never think of us again, the road will be open, and nothing will follow her, ever."

Something loosened in Sebastian's chest so suddenly that he nearly swayed. Lena's fingers went white around his.

"And in exchange," the Host continued, "you will surrender your name."

The words took a moment to arrive. When they did, they arrived all at once, the way his storms used to — a pressure first, then the breaking.

"My name," Sebastian said.

"Sebastian Okafor," the Ledger-Keeper recited, and each syllable landed like a stone in still water. "Son of Iheoma. Grandson of Adaeze. Storm-caller of the western valleys. Exile of thirteen years. The man who broke the valley's truce at the ford. The man who called lightning on the trade road. The man who stole from the archive of sold souls. All of it — every record, every ledger, every memory written in ink or glass or living stone — will be surrendered. You will be remembered by no record. No ledger. No tongue. The river that remembers names will forget yours. The hunters who sought you will find no trace that you ever existed to be sought. The village that sheltered you will keep its love for you, but not the shape of why." It looked up from the book, and for one strange moment its dry face seemed almost sorrowful. "You will be a man unwritten."

"And my magic?" Sebastian asked. His mouth was dry. "You've already taken—"

"Your magic burned out in the rite. That was yours to spend, and you spent it well. We claim nothing that is not offered." The Host folded its hands. "We claim only what you have not yet considered giving. Think, storm-caller. What is a name? It is the handle by which the world takes hold of you. It is the first word your mother gave you and the last word carved over your bones. It is how debts find their debtor, how oaths find their keeper, how love finds its beloved." A pause. "And it is how ledgers find their lines. Give us your name, and no contract can ever touch you again. No court. No hunter. No bargain made in fear by grandmothers trying to save their children. You would be free in the only way this world permits: by being unfindable."

* * *

Sebastian looked at Lena.

He had promised himself, walking back down through the glass roots with the alarms still ringing behind him, that he would not do this again — would not stand in front of one of the Court's offers and weigh pieces of himself on their scales. He had spent his storms. He had spent his exile. He had spent thirteen years of quiet and four weeks of terror and one wedding day dropped into a hungry river. There was supposed to be nothing left to spend.

But there was. There was the oldest coin of all.

"They'll forget me," he said quietly. Not to the Court. To her. "Marta. The villagers. They'll remember loving me, maybe — the Ledger says they keep the love. But not my face. Not my voice. Not the name they called me across the inn." His throat worked. "I came down here to make sure you'd never be hollowed. I didn't come down here to be erased."

Lena looked up at him.

She had not spoken since the ford — not in the old tongue, not at all — but she did not need to. He had learned her silences the way he had once learned weather: by attention, by patience, by standing very still and letting them tell him what they meant. This silence said: *I ran away from the mountain rather than be hollowed. I chose you. Choose what you choose, but know that I am watching, and know that whatever you give, I will carry it too.*

Then she did something that undid him completely. She let go of his hand, stepped forward, and faced the three faces of the Court alone — a girl of eight years, small and soaked-looking even in dry clothes, with a spiral mark on her wrist and a bone whistle hanging at her neck — and she looked at the Smiling One, and she smiled back.

It was not a friendly smile. Sebastian had never seen it before and understood it instantly: it was the smile of someone who knows exactly what is being offered and is not afraid of it, and the Smiling One — the face that wore mercy like a mask — actually leaned back.

"She speaks for herself," the Smiling One said softly. "How rare."

Lena reached up and took the whistle from around her neck. She held it out — not offering it, just showing it, the way you show a wolf your empty hands — and then she pointed at Sebastian, and then at her own chest, and then she pressed both fists together over her heart, the gesture she had taught him in the valley, the old-tongue sign that meant *belonging chosen, not inherited.*

Then she turned and looked at Sebastian, and nodded once.

*Yes. Do it. We will still be us.*

* * *

"How does it work?" Sebastian asked. His voice was steadier than he felt. "The surrender."

"You speak your name," the Host said, "and then you release it. Here, in the chamber where all names are kept. The river above will return the memory you paid it — consider that a courtesy, or an irony; we do not distinguish. And when you climb out of this mountain, the world will have a hole in it shaped exactly like you, and no one will be able to say what filled it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the old clause stands," said the Ledger-Keeper. "Return the shard to the archive, or war comes to the valley. Those are the only doors."

There it was, then. The same door it had always been, since the night a storm-caller's grandmother signed away a future she couldn't see: give something of yourself, or watch the people you love pay for it. Thirteen years ago, Adaeze Okafor had given a soul she didn't own. Sebastian Okafor would give the one thing that was truly, entirely his — and the difference between them, he thought, was that he was choosing it with his eyes open, and choosing it for love, and choosing it *last*, so that no one after him would ever have to choose at all.

"Marta won't know me," he said. It wasn't a question. He needed to say it aloud anyway, to hear how it sounded in the air.

"No," said the Host, with terrible gentleness. "But she will know that she is glad you came home. Some things live deeper than names. You of all people should know that — you spent thirteen years being loved by people who feared what you were."

Sebastian laughed. It came out cracked, half a sob, and the moths scattered briefly at the sound like startled fish.

"All right," he said. "All right."

He knelt so that he was eye-level with Lena. Up close he could see the storm-colored flecks in her eyes, the ones that had appeared the night the whistle sang, and he memorized them — deliberately, furiously, the way a drowning man memorizes the surface of the water. Whatever the Court took, it could not take this. Memory was theirs to erase from records and rivers and tongues. But the body keeps its own ledgers. His hands would remember braiding her hair badly. His shoulders would remember her weight falling asleep against them on the trade road. Somewhere deeper than any archive, a man who had no name would still turn his head at the sound of a whistle, still set two bowls on the table out of habit, still wake at dawn because a small voice in the next room was singing in the language before kingdoms.

"Whatever happens," he told her, "you are mine, and I am yours, and no book under this mountain changes that. Say it back to me."

Her mouth moved. No sound — she saved her voice for what mattered — but the shapes were clear: *Mine. Yours. Always.*

He stood. He faced the dais. The lantern-moths gathered close, and the Ledger-Keeper opened its book to a page near the end, and the Smiling One watched him with an expression that, for the first time, held no smile at all — only something old and tired and almost like respect.

"Say it," the Host said.

"Sebastian Okafor," he said.

The name left him like breath in winter — visible for a moment, then gone. He felt it go. Felt it lift out of him the way a storm lifts off a valley, taking the pressure and the weight and the terrible familiar hum of being *known*. The Ledger-Keeper wrote, and the pen-scratch was the loudest sound in the world, and with each stroke Sebastian felt another thread cut: the name his mother whispered over his cradle; the name shouted across courtyards in the years before the exile; the name the hunters cursed; the name Marta used when she slid him extra bread and pretended it was leftovers. Gone. Gone. Gone. Unwritten. Unremembered. A hole in the world shaped exactly like him.

When it was done, he stood in the chamber and felt — nothing. Not grief, not yet. Grief, he suspected, was waiting for him somewhere on the long climb into daylight, patient as winter. For now there was only lightness, terrifying and clean, like a sky after the last cloud has gone.

"The bargain is rewritten," the Host declared, and the glass roots rang with it, a deep bell-note that traveled up through the mountain's bones and out into the stone of the world. "The debt is settled. The child belongs to no one. The man—" it paused, consulting a ledger that no longer contained him, and something almost like wonder crossed its agreed-upon face "—the man belongs to no record. Rise, nameless one. Take your daughter. Go home."

* * *

They climbed.

The tunnels that had taken four days to descend let them pass in one, as if the mountain itself had decided to be finished with them — glass roots glowing brighter where Lena touched them, moth-lanterns drifting ahead of them like an honor guard, doors opening before they reached them. At the threshold where the underground city met the river-road, the Smiling One was waiting. Alone.

"One truth, freely given," it said, before Sebastian could speak. "You've earned at least that. The chase was never meant to catch you. The test was never the theft. We have waited a very long time — longer than your kingdom, longer than your tongue — for someone to come down here willing to give *freely*. Everyone else bargains, or begs, or burns. Your grandmother bargained. You gave. That is the only currency this mountain has never been able to mint for itself." Its smile returned, but smaller now, and sadder. "Spend it wisely, nameless one. And when the world asks you who you are—"

It stopped. Looked at Lena. Looked at the whistle at her throat.

"—perhaps someone will answer for you."

Then it stepped aside, and the night air of the surface world poured down the tunnel, cold and pine-sharp and full of rain that had not yet fallen, and Sebastian Okafor — no, not that, not anymore, that name was a line in a closed book under a mountain — the nameless man took his daughter's hand and walked out under the waning twelfth moon, toward the valley, toward home, toward the woman named Marta who would greet him with love and no reason for it.

Behind them, far below, a freed soul sang in the open dark.

Ahead of them, a small girl began, very quietly, to build a new name out of old words — one syllable at a time, testing each against the wind, saving the best of them for the morning she would finally say it aloud.
