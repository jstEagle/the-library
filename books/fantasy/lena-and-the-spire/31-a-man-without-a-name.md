## 31. A Man Without a Name

The last tunnel smells of rain.

I know rain the way I know the whistle — by its hum before it arrives. The air coming down through the crack above us is wet and green and full of growing things, and after so long beneath the mountain it tastes like a language I am only now remembering how to speak.

The man who is no longer anyone climbs ahead of me. He has no name now; I heard them take it. I heard the Ledger-Keeper's pen move across the page like a moth eating through wool, and I heard the sound the ledger made when it closed — a soft, satisfied thump, the sound of something being swallowed. The Court thinks that is the end of it. The Court believes that a name is a thing you can take the way you take a tooth: pull it out, leave a hole, let the rest of the mouth learn to work around the absence.

But the Court was never a child in a valley. The Court does not know what I know.

I know that names are not kept in ledgers. Names are kept in mouths.

* * *

We climb for most of the day. The glass roots thin as we rise, giving way to stone, then to stone furred with moss, then to a chimney of rock where the light comes down in a single pale column and moths — ordinary ones, brown and small, not lantern-moths at all — spiral up toward it. He goes first because he says his hands remember climbing even when the rest of him is tired. His hands are different now. I have been watching them since we left the Court behind us. They used to hum. Even in the valley, even with all that quiet pressed down on him like snow, his hands had a faint storm-sound in them, a far-off rolling that only I could hear because I had spent my whole life listening to things that were not speaking.

Now they are silent. Completely, terribly silent. When he reaches for a handhold, the rock does not lean toward him. When he pulls himself up, nothing answers. He is only a man lifting the weight of a man, and I can see that this frightens him more than the Smiling One ever did.

He looks back at me once, halfway up the chimney, silhouetted against the light.

"Almost," he says.

That is the first thing he says without a name, and I decide, right there, hanging from a root in the dark, that it will not be the last. A person who can still say *almost* is a person who still believes there is an *after*. I will build his new name out of words like that.

* * *

Near the top the chimney narrows, and we have to go one at a time, and for a little while I cannot see him at all — only hear his breathing, rough and human, and the scrape of his boots, and beyond both of those, faint as a memory of a memory, the mountain itself. The Spire is not dead. Everyone below pretends it is sleeping, but I grew up inside its dreams, and I know the difference between sleep and silence. It is listening to us climb. It felt my grandmother's soul pass out of its archive like a splinter leaving a wound, and it felt the contract tear, and it felt the Ledger-Keeper's pen scratch out a name — and somewhere in all that listening, I think, it made room for us. The Smiling One let us go too easily. I have not forgotten. Neither has the mountain.

But right now the mountain's attention feels less like a hand on my neck and more like an old woman watching children leave a funeral, making sure they eat something on the road.

I come out of the chimney into evening.

The sky is enormous. That is the first thing. After the Court's lantern-glow and the glass roots' cold shine, the sky is so wide it makes me dizzy, all bruise-purple at the edges and burning gold where the twelfth moon is going down fat and low over the hills. The second thing is the wind. It moves through the grass in long slow strokes, and every stroke carries a hundred small voices — insects, leaves, water somewhere below, the creak of a tree deciding which way to lean. Nothing down here is mute. Nothing down here has been hollowed.

The man stands beside me with his face turned up, and tears are running into his ears, and he does not wipe them away or pretend they are not happening. He just lets the sky do whatever it is doing to him.

"They took it," he says quietly. "My name. I keep reaching for it and it's — there's a smooth place where it used to be."

I take his hand. His fingers close around mine automatically, the way they have since the night of the storm, since the door, since the bowl of soup he set in front of a soaked silent child without asking her to be anything other than hungry. His hand is warm. His hand is only a hand. And it is enough. It has always been enough; he simply never got to find that out until now.

"I heard it," I tell him. "What they took."

"You did?"

"The old tongue doesn't live in ledgers." I tap my chest, then my throat, then my mouth. "It lives here. Here. Here. They can empty their books all they want. Books forget. Mouths don't. Not if someone loves the mouth."

He laughs — a cracked, surprised sound, half sob. "And you love my mouth?"

I make a face at him. He laughs harder. Somewhere below us, deep in the stone, I feel the mountain almost laugh too, a tremor like distant thunder that never quite becomes weather. Then it settles. We stand in the gold light and the wind combs through us, and I begin.

* * *

Here is what I do not tell him yet: I have been composing since the ledger closed.

Words come slowly in the old tongue. That is why it survived so long — every word has to be earned, carried, meant. There is no word in it for *casualty*, no word for *collateral*, no word for *payment*. The Spire-folk built their language back when language was still young and honest, and they built it out of what things *do*, not what things are called. So a river is *the-water-that-remembers*, and grief is *the-carrying-of-a-light-through-a-dark-house*, and a father —

Well. I will get to that word later. One piece at a time. That is how the old tongue works. You do not bestow a name; you grow one, syllable by syllable, the way a valley grows a tree, and each piece must be true or the whole name will rot.

The first piece I choose is *Vael*. It means *shelter-that-stays-standing-after-the-storm-has-forgotten-it-was-ever-there*. It is a long meaning for a short sound; that is normal. I say it aloud, testing it against the evening air, and the grass leans slightly toward me, which is the old tongue's way of saying *yes, that is a real thing, we acknowledge it.*

"Vael," I say again, louder, and point at him.

He blinks. "Is that — is that for me?"

"It's the beginning of you." I hold up one finger. "One part. Don't rush. Names grown fast fall over."

"Like houses."

"Like houses." He mended roofs for thirteen years in the valley. He knows exactly what I mean, and something eases in his shoulders, because this is a language he already speaks — the language of things built carefully, load-bearing, meant to last.

* * *

We walk down out of the rocks while the gold drains out of the sky, and we make camp in the lee of a hill where a hawthorn grows crooked over a spring. He builds a fire the ordinary way — flint, patience, cursing under his breath when the tinder dampens — and I watch him do it and add the second piece.

*Oren.* It means *hands-that-mend-what-the-world-breaks-without-being-asked*. I say it while he is blowing on the ember, and the ember flares, startled, and he jerks back and then stares at me.

"Did you just —"

"Vael Oren," I say. "Shelter-that-stays. Hands-that-mend."

He looks at his own palms as though they belong to a stranger. Thirteen years of mending roofs, patching walls, fixing Marta's inn gate, straightening the smithy's chimney, and he never once thought of any of it as magic. But the old tongue disagrees. The old tongue thinks fixing things is the oldest magic there is — older than storms, older than courts, older than the Spire itself. Someone had to mend the first broken world. That is in the songs my mother sang before they took her voice. That is in the bones of the language.

"That's two pieces," he says hoarsely. "How many are there?"

"As many as you've earned." I hold up two fingers, then fold one down. "Two so far."

"And how many have I earned?"

"All of them. But you have to be present for each one. That's the rule. A name given all at once is just a label. Grown slowly, it's a — " I search for the word and land on the old-tongue phrase directly, because there is no trading-tongue equivalent. "*Sennu-veth.* A rope braided while both people watch."

He is quiet for a long time. The fire pops. Above us the first stars come out, shy, then bolder, then everywhere, a spill of salt across black cloth.

"No one ever watched me that closely," he finally says. "Thirteen years in that valley and I don't think anyone looked at me long enough to name a fence post after me."

"Marta named her cat after you."

"She named her cat after my *storms*. There's a difference."

"There is," I agree solemnly. "Her cat is a menace."

He laughs again, and this time it comes easier, and I file the sound away. Laughter is the third piece. I already know what it will be.

* * *

I wake once in the night, the way I always wake — all at once, silently, listening. Old habit. In the Court you learned to sleep with one ear open or you woke up smaller than you'd been.

The fire has burned to red coals. He is sitting up against the hawthorn, awake too, looking at his hands in the coal-light, turning them over and over as if the silence in them might be a trick that will wear off.

"I keep waiting for it to come back," he says, without looking at me. He knows I'm awake; he always knows. "The storms. Like a limb that fell asleep. Pins and needles, and then — nothing. Just quiet." His voice catches. "I called lightning three days ago to save our lives and it came like it had never left. And now it's gone like it never was. I don't know which version of me was real."

I crawl over and sit beside him. The night is cold and clean-smelling. Far off, an owl asks its one question and gets no answer.

"In the old tongue," I tell him, "there is no word for *powerless*."

He turns his head. "There isn't?"

"The closest is *kel-vassa*. It means *one-whose-river-has-gone-underground*." I press my palm flat against the earth between us. "The river is still there. Underneath. Moving. Feeding roots it will never see. The Spire-folk believed that was not a lesser kind of river. They believed it was the kind that outlasts everything on the surface."

"You're saying I'm a groundwater deposit."

"I'm saying you're *kel-vassa*, and it's a beautiful word, and the Court has no translation for it because the Court only counts what it can see." I look at him steadily. "They took your name. They didn't take your rivers."

His eyes shine in the coal-light. He puts his arm around my shoulders and pulls me against his side, and we sit like that watching the coals breathe, and high above us the twelfth moon sets and the thirteenth begins its final climb, thin as a paring of bone.

Four nights. Maybe five. But the ledger is torn, and my grandmother's soul is free, and the Court's own faces said the words themselves: *the debt is settled; the child belongs to no one.* Whatever comes on the thirteenth turn, it will not be a reckoning. I hold onto that the way you hold a rope in wind.

* * *

In the morning we come over a ridge and see the trade road unspooling below us toward home, and the valley beyond it, a green cup holding its little cloud of morning mist. Home. My chest does something complicated at the sight of it. Four weeks ago I ran down a mountain with a bone whistle and no plan, and a stranger opened his door to me and never once asked me to be less strange.

He stops at the crest of the ridge. The wind comes up the slope and moves his hair, and he closes his eyes and lets it, and I understand that he is listening for thunder in it and finding none, and that this hurts, and that he is going to bear it anyway, the way he bears everything — quietly, usefully, with his sleeves rolled up.

So I give him the third piece.

"*Sova*," I say.

He opens his eyes. "What does that one mean?"

"*Laughter-that-refuses-to-die-in-a-cold-house.*" I say it plainly, the way the old tongue deserves to be said. "You laughed in the Court, Vael Oren Sova. Did you notice? Right in front of the Smiling One. Nobody laughs in front of the Smiling One. Their faces forgot how. Yours didn't."

He stands very still. The wind moves the grass around his boots in slow silver waves.

"Lena," he says carefully, "that's three pieces. How long does this go on?"

"For the rest of your life." I start walking down toward the road, because if I keep standing there looking at his face I will give him all the remaining pieces at once and ruin the ceremony. "Every year, maybe. Every kindness. That's how it works. By the time we're old you'll have a name longer than a winter, and I'll be the only one alive who can pronounce all of it, and everyone else will just call you *old man* and be jealous."

Behind me I hear him laugh — *sova*, exactly, laughter refusing to die — and then his boots catch up to mine, and we walk down together toward the road, toward the valley, toward the four nights left before the moon finishes turning.

I am carrying one more piece already, saved and sealed, the heaviest and the truest, the word I have not said in any tongue since the Court took my mother's voice:

*a-father-is-one-who-opens-his-door-in-a-storm-and-asks-nothing-back.*

When I give him that one, I will give it in front of the whole village, with iron bells ringing and Marta crying into her apron and the bone whistle hung by the schoolhouse door.

But not today. Today there is a road, and a long walk, and a man walking beside me whose old name is gone and whose new name is three words old and growing.

Today, when the peddler carts rattle past and a driver calls out, "Oi! You two — where from?" — the man opens his mouth and finds the smooth place where his answer used to live, and panics for half a heartbeat.

I squeeze his hand and answer for him, loud and clear, in the old tongue, pointing at him with fierce pride:

"*Vael Oren Sova.* From nowhere the Court can spell."

The driver squints, shrugs, rattles on. The man looks down at me with his whole ruined-and-remade face.

"That's going to get us asked a lot of questions on this road," he says.

"Good," I tell him. "Questions are how names spread. Mouths, remember? Not books."

And we walk west, into the long gold morning, a girl and her nameless-named father, while behind us the mountain watches us go — and, I would swear on my grandmother's freed soul, hums.
