## 32. The Long Walk Home

The first thing Sebastian learned about being nameless was that the world did not care.

Birds still sang at dawn. The river still ran cold and quick beside the road, carrying its cargo of melted snow down from the mountain's shoulder. His boots still needed mending, his stomach still demanded bread, and Lena still walked three paces ahead with her chin tipped up like a girl surveying her own kingdom.

But when he reached for the sky — the way he had reached for it every day of his life before the valley, the way a man reaches for a stair that is no longer there — nothing answered.

He had done it on the mountain's shoulder, in front of Marta and the villagers and the whole listening dark: poured thirteen years of hoarded storm into a single shard of singing glass, given it all away to free a woman who had been dead longer than he'd been exiled. And now the reaching found only emptiness, an absence shaped exactly like himself.

He tried not to do it often. He failed.

* * *

They walked south through country Sebastian had never seen, though he had crossed most of it once, long ago, running. It looked different without fear. Then, every hedgerow had been a place for hunters to wait; every crossroads, a question with teeth. Now the hedges were just hedges, gold-green in the late light, loud with sparrows. The crossroads were just mud and a leaning signpost pointing toward towns whose names he didn't know — which was, he supposed, fitting, since no ledger anywhere held his.

No one looked at him twice.

That was the strangest part. For thirteen years in the valley he had been *the storm-caller*, even muted, even harmless — the man the children dared each other to knock on the door of, the man Marta defended over ale while the innkeeper's other customers muttered. Before that, in the years of running, he had been worse than a name: he had been a bounty, a shape on the horizon, a reason for doors to close.

Now he was a tall, tired man walking with a small girl, and the world let him be exactly that.

A carter gave them a lift outside a town whose name Sebastian never learned, and the man talked the whole way about turnip prices and his brother's bad knee and whether the rains would come early this year, and at no point did he glance at Sebastian's hands, or ask where they'd come from, or go quiet in the careful way people went quiet around power. When they climbed down at the edge of his fields, he pressed half a loaf and a wedge of hard cheese into Lena's arms and said, "For the road," as if they were anyone. As if they were everyone.

Lena ate her share sitting on a milestone, swinging her legs, and watched Sebastian not eat his.

"You keep doing it," she said. She spoke the old tongue always now, even on open roads, because she said secrets deserved a language of their own. "Reaching."

"I'm not—"

"Your hand does the thing." She demonstrated: fingers curling slightly, palm turning up, the unconscious gesture of a man offering something to the air. "Like feeding a bird that isn't there."

Sebastian looked at his own hand as though it belonged to a stranger. It was a good hand. Broad-knuckled, scarred across two fingers from a roofing accident in his fourth year in the valley, steady enough to mend a kettle or braid a child's hair. It had called lightning out of a clear sky at eleven years old. It had killed men. It had, four days ago, saved a soul.

"It's like a limb," he said finally. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. "You don't miss a limb until you reach for something and remember."

Lena considered this with the grave attention she gave everything, her dark eyes catching the last of the sun. Then she took his hand — the reaching one — and folded the fingers closed, and held the fist between both of hers, the way you hold a wounded bird.

"Quiet hands," she said. "Are still hands."

It wasn't quite grammar. It was the old tongue working itself out in her mouth, meaning arriving sideways, the way meaning always arrived with her. But he understood. He turned his hand over inside hers until their palms met, and the emptiness where the storms had lived felt, for a moment, less like a wound and more like room.

* * *

The grief came anyway. Grief always came anyway.

It came on the third night, in a barn they'd been allowed to sleep in by a farmer who asked nothing, and it came so suddenly that Sebastian sat bolt upright in the straw with his heart slamming, certain for one disorienting second that the storm was already here, that the pressure behind his eyes meant thunder building somewhere north—

There was no storm. There was a barn full of sleeping cows and the small, even breathing of a child, and through the gaps in the boards, stars. Ordinary stars, indifferent, uncharged.

He sat in the straw and shook, quietly, the way he had learned to shake during the exile years — silently, so as not to wake anyone, so as not to be witnessed in the thing that was happening to him. Because that was what it was. Not weakness. Weather. A front moving through him that had nowhere to go, no lightning to become, no thunder to spend itself as. Thirteen years he had dammed it. Four days ago he had broken the dam deliberately, and now everything the dam had held back — not just the magic but the mourning under the magic, the mourning for his grandmother, for the family her bargain had bought and betrayed, for the man he might have been if souls could be inherited clean — all of it moved through him at its own pace, and he could do nothing but sit in the straw and let it rain.

Except.

Except that after a while, a small warm weight settled against his side, and a small voice said, "I know," and that was all. Lena didn't tell him it would pass. She didn't tell him he was brave or foolish or free. She leaned against him in the dark barn among the cows and breathed, and after a while her breathing slowed into sleep, and Sebastian put his arm around her and discovered that his body knew how to do this — how to be a shelter, how to hold warmth, how to be a wall against the ordinary cold of an ordinary night — and that none of it required a single volt of the sky.

Quiet hands. Still hands. Hands that could hold a sleeping child through the worst weather of his life, which was, as it turned out, weather made entirely of himself.

* * *

In the mornings, he discovered what quiet hands were for.

They were for whittling, which he took up again on the road because his fingers grew restless in the evenings, twitching toward gestures that meant nothing now. He carved Lena a fox from a piece of apple wood, and she named it in the old tongue — a word that meant roughly *the-one-who-listens-at-hedges* — and slept with it tucked under her chin.

They were for cooking, which he had never much done, since storms made excellent fires and he'd never needed patience. Over a small flame built the slow human way, he learned to brown onions without burning them, and Lena appointed herself critic-in-chief, delivering verdicts in the dead language with such solemnity that he laughed for the first time since the mountain — really laughed, helplessly, until his ribs hurt, while a nine-year-old informed him with great dignity that his stew tasted like *wet stone's regret*.

They were for mending. This was the oldest skill, the valley skill, the one he'd carried out of exile along with his grandmother's letters and nothing else. A farmwife's loom frame, cracked. A shepherd's crook. The axle of a tinker's cart, in exchange for a ride and a story, and the tinker told stories badly and paid them in dried apples and gossip, and Sebastian listened to gossip about lords and border skirmishes and a strange rumor out of the north — that the mountain court had gone dark, that its lantern-moths had scattered like sparks on wind, that travelers passing beneath the Spire now saw wildflowers growing up through the glass roots — and felt something in his chest turn over gently, like a page.

His hands fixed things. That was all they had ever needed to do, maybe. The lightning had been the least of it.

* * *

On the sixth day, hunters' roads crossed theirs, and Sebastian learned what hunted-by-nothing actually felt like.

Two riders came down the far side of a ford as Sebastian and Lena waded the near side — armed riders, cloaked against the drizzle, the kind of men Sebastian had spent a decade learning to read at distance: weight of the horse, set of the shoulders, angle of the head. Every muscle in him locked into the old arithmetic. *Can we run. Can I hide her. Can I—*

And then, with something like vertigo, he remembered: there was nothing left to read. No signature in the air above his head. No name in any ledger. If these riders were hunting anything, they were hunting someone else, and even if they weren't — even if some old warrant still walked the world with his old face on it — the face on it belonged to a man who no longer existed. The Court had seen to that personally. He had signed himself out of the world.

The riders passed within twenty feet. One nodded to him, the flat nod of a man acknowledging another man on a road, and rode on, and the water closed over their hoofprints, and Sebastian stood hip-deep in the ford with his heart going like a hammer and Lena tugging his sleeve saying, *come on, the current's strong here,* and understood that he was safe.

Not hidden. Safe. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole width of his new life.

He stood in the river a moment longer than he needed to, letting the cold water pull at his legs, and laughed once — a short, disbelieving sound — and Lena looked back at him from the bank with her eyebrows raised in perfect imitation of Marta, and he waded out to her, dripping, grinning like a fool.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing." He wrung out his coat hem. "I just forgot, for a second, that I was allowed to stand in rivers."

"That's very sad," Lena said, in the tone of a scholar recording a fact, and took his hand, and pulled him up the bank toward home.

* * *

Home. He noticed he had started thinking of it that way — the valley, the village, the crooked house with the roof he'd mended eleven times. Somewhere on the road home had stopped being a direction he'd fled and become a place he was returning to, and the change had happened so gradually he couldn't mark the day of it. Perhaps the day the farmer gave them bread. Perhaps the day in the barn. Perhaps the day he'd stood in a river laughing.

What he could mark was the evening the homesickness got ahead of them.

They had camped in the ruins of an old waystation, a roofless square of stones on a hill, and Lena had built a fire with the competence of someone raised by a practical man, and was teaching him — patiently, the way one teaches a promising but backward student — the names in the old tongue for the things the fire was doing. *Eating. Climbing. Singing-to-itself.* Sebastian repeated them badly and happily, and somewhere in the middle of it he realized his eyes were wet.

"The smoke?" Lena asked, concerned.

"Not the smoke." He fed a stick to the flames. "I keep thinking about my grandmother. Not the letter. Not the bargain." The shard was gone now, released, wherever freed souls went — but her voice still lived behind his sternum, that last sung line, the one that had played under everything he'd done since the archive. "She used to sing me songs in your language. In the old tongue. When I was small. I thought they were lullabies. They were probably warnings." He huffed. "I can't ask her now. I'll never know which verses were which."

Lena was quiet for a while. Firelight moved on her face.

"Sing me one," she said. "The one you remember. I'll tell you which kind it is."

So Sebastian sang — badly, off-key, in an accent fourteen years rusted — the fragment of a song his grandmother had sung over a cradle in a house that no longer existed, in a tongue that predated kingdoms, while the daughter of the Spire-folk sat cross-legged across the fire and listened with her whole body, the way she listened to everything, and when he stumbled to the end she was smiling.

"It's both," she said. "That's the trick of the old songs. They're lullabies *and* warnings. That's why they survived." She lay back against her pack, the carved fox under her chin. "Warnings are just lullabies for grown-ups. Someone loved you enough to do both at once."

Sebastian sat with that until the fire burned low, and then he banked it the careful human way, with ash and patience, and lay down beside his daughter — the word still new, still warm, still slightly unbelievable, like bread fresh from an oven — and slept without dreaming of storms.

* * *

They smelled the valley before they saw it.

It came on the ninth morning, cresting the last ridge of foothills: green and woodsmoke and wet earth and, underneath it, faintly, improbably, something floral that had no business growing this early in the season. Sebastian stopped on the ridge with his pack straps biting his shoulders and looked down into the bowl of the valley that had kept him, and Lena stopped beside him, and neither of them spoke for a long moment.

The dampening field had softened. You could see it from here — see it in the way the light lay on the land, no longer flattened and gray but touched here and there with a shimmer, like sun on moving water. Small magics seeping in, Marta had written in the letter they'd received at the last waystation, her handwriting blunt and delighted. Old Marta the skeptic, reporting miracles in a postscript. The well-water tastes of starlight, the widow Ansel swears. The Hendersons' goat levitates. Only a little. We are handling it.

And below, at the valley's mouth, where the road narrowed between the boundary stones — figures. More than a few. Waiting.

Sebastian's chest tightened with an old reflex, the reflex of the man who had entered this valley thirteen years ago with nothing and been permitted, grudgingly, to stay. *They'll want to know what I've brought back. What followed me home.*

"They're waving," Lena said. She was squinting down the slope. "Why are they all waving?"

Because, he realized slowly, as the figures resolved — Marta at the front with her arms already up, the innkeeper behind her, children, farmers, the widow Ansel holding what appeared to be a banner — they were not waiting to see what he'd brought back.

They were waiting to welcome him home.

Lena grabbed his hand. Her grip was small and strong and utterly certain, and down in the valley someone began ringing the inn bell, and the sound came up the hillside bright and human and utterly unmagical, and it was the best sound Sebastian Okafor — nameless, powerless, unexiled, father — had ever heard.

"Come on," Lena said, in the old tongue, in the new one, in the language of pulling. "They're waiting for us."

He let her pull him down the hill toward the noise, the bells, the banner, the changed and changing valley — and it was only halfway down, breathless, laughing, that he heard it: faint, beneath the wind, beneath the bells, a hum like a struck glass, rising from the bundle in his pack where he kept the one shard he had allowed himself to keep.

It was humming in time with Lena's laughter.

And it had never done that before.
