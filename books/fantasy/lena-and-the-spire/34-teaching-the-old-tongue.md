## 34. Teaching the Old Tongue

Winter came to the valley the way forgiveness comes — slowly, and then all at once.

I woke on the first morning of it to a silence so complete I could hear the snow landing. Not falling — landing. Each flake touched the roof of our cottage with a sound like a held breath being released, and I lay beneath my quilt listening until there were too many breaths to count and they became one long exhale over the whole valley. The mountains had put on their white hoods. The river had gone quiet under its skin of ice. Even the dampening field, the old magic that had guarded this place for longer than anyone remembered, seemed to sleep deeper in the cold, humming its low note beneath the frost like a cat purring in its dreams.

I was nine winters old now, though I had stopped counting them the way other children did. My years before the valley did not stack neatly. They spiraled. Like the mark on my wrist, like the tower in my drawings, like the tongue that lived behind my teeth.

The old tongue. The language before kingdoms.

That morning, for the first time, I would teach it to someone other than myself.

* * *

The schoolroom had been Marta's idea, though she pretended it had been mine.

"It's not a school," she'd said, arms crossed in front of her hearth three weeks earlier, when the first hard frost had driven everyone indoors and the village children had begun to fray at the edges like old rope. "It's a keeping-them-alive-until-spring arrangement. You'll sit them by your fire, you'll keep them from drowning each other in the horse trough, and if you happen to teach them something useful along the way, so much the better."

But I had seen her face when she said it. Marta's face was a ledger of its own, every feeling entered twice — once where the world could read it and once where only careful people could find it. And underneath her gruffness I found the entry she thought she'd hidden: *she was proud of me.* Proud that the strange mute child who had arrived soaked at Sebastian Okafor's door two winters past had become something the village could point to without crossing itself.

So we made a schoolroom of Sebastian's largest room, the one that used to be his workshop back when he still believed he would never touch magic again. We dragged in benches from the inn. Marta donated a slate board and an alarming quantity of chalk. Old Tam brought a stool he'd carved himself and refused to sit on it, insisting it belonged to the teacher, which meant me, which meant that for the first weeks I taught standing up because sitting on Tam's stool felt like wearing a crown I hadn't earned.

And Sebastian — my father, though we had not yet said the word aloud, though the whole village already said it for us — Sebastian carried in the last bench and then stood in the doorway with snow melting in his dark hair, looking at the room as if he couldn't quite believe what it had become.

"You're certain about this?" he asked me. He asked in the trade-tongue, but lately we slipped between languages the way other people slipped between rooms. "They're village children, Lena. Their parents remember what happened the last time someone in this valley spoke words that hummed."

"I'm certain," I said.

It was one of the small miracles of my life that I could say such things now. For two years after the mountain, after the Court, after the night my father poured everything he was into a shard of singing glass and came out the other side nameless and empty-handed and smiling — for two years my voice had come back slowly, like feeling returning to a frozen hand. First whispers. Then single words. Then, on a spring morning I still kept folded inside my memory like a letter, whole sentences, spoken to Sebastian over breakfast, in the language before kingdoms, because it was the only language that felt like mine.

He had dropped his spoon. That was how I knew it mattered.

* * *

Seven children came the first day. By midwinter there were eleven, and one of them was Sebastian.

He sat at the back on Tam's stool — Tam having finally relented and carved a second one — with his big weather-mender's hands folded in his lap like a boy at prayer. The village children giggled at him at first. Pip Ashdown, who was six and fearless, informed him that school was for children. Sebastian replied, gravely, that he had missed his schooling on account of being exiled, and Pip considered this so interesting that from then on Pip sat next to him every single day.

I began, as my own remembering had begun, with names.

Not their names — those they already had, shouted across the valley since birth. I began with the names of things. In the old tongue, everything that exists has a true name, and the true name is not a label but a description of wanting. Fire is not called fire. Fire is called *the hunger that dances*. Water is *the patient one who always finds the door*. Snow —

Snow took me a while to explain.

"Snow," I said, writing the shape of it on Marta's slate board, the characters flowing like something alive, "is the sky learning to be gentle."

Pip raised his hand. "Why does the sky need to learn that?"

"Because the sky remembers being storms," I said, and I saw my father go very still at the back of the room, and I wished immediately that I had chosen different words, and then I saw his face soften, and I understood that I had chosen exactly right. He nodded at me once, small and slow, the way he did when I said something true about him without meaning to.

We learned ten words that first day. The children repeated them wrong in eleven different ways, and I corrected them gently, and by the time the light through the frosted windows went blue and thin, all eleven of them could say *thank you* in a language older than any kingdom on any map, and none of them knew yet what a dangerous and holy thing they were carrying home in their mouths.

* * *

Some evenings, after the children had gone tramping home through the snow, leaving boot-prints like a sentence written across the yard, Sebastian stayed behind and we practiced alone.

Those were my favorite hours, though I never told him so directly. Telling him directly was still new to me; I hoarded my directness like a dragon hoards gold, spending it carefully. Instead I would say, "Again," and make him repeat the word for *home*, which was the hardest word in the old tongue and the one he wanted most.

The word for home was not a word for a house or a valley or even a family. The word for home was built from smaller sounds that meant *the place where your name is safe*. And because Sebastian had traded his name away — because no ledger recorded him, no tongue could hold him, no record anywhere in the world remembered that a man named Sebastian Okafor had ever existed — the word caught in his throat every time, like a fishbone.

"The place," I would prompt, tapping the sounds out one by one on the table, "where your—"

"Name." His voice would roughen on it.

"—is safe."

Silence. The fire ticking. Snow settling on the window with its sound of released breath.

"Do you think it counts," he asked me one night, very quietly, "if the name isn't real anymore? If there's nothing there to keep safe?"

I looked at him across the table — this enormous man, this exiled storm-caller, this thief of souls and giver of his own, this person the entire world had agreed to forget — and I felt the fierce, unreasonable anger that lived permanently in my chest on his behalf, the anger I had carried down out of the mountain in place of the soul they had wanted to take.

Then I did the thing I had been building toward all winter, the thing I had invented word by word in the dark of my bed, the inheritance I had decided to redirect.

I gave him a name.

* * *

It had started, properly, the week before, when I found the bone whistle.

I had not blown it since the flight through the glass roots, since the night its song had tangled the Court's senses into knots and bought us our escape. It lived wrapped in wool at the bottom of my chest of keepsakes, and some nights I dreamed of it humming without wind, and woke with my heart going like a rabbit's. But that evening, unwrapping it to show the children — because they had begged, because Pip had begged, because Pip's begging had the persistence of dripping water — I noticed something I had never seen before.

There were markings on it. Faint ones, worn nearly smooth, hidden in the grain of the bone. Characters. Old ones.

Characters I could read.

I didn't tell the children. I wrapped it back up with hands that were not entirely steady and finished the lesson with a story instead, one of the Spire-folk tales my sleeping mouth had been telling since before I could walk: the story of the bell-maker who cast a bell so beautiful that grief itself came down from the mountains to listen, and stayed, and became the bell's shadow, so that whenever the bell rang, somewhere far away, someone sad felt a little less alone.

"That's why bells matter," I told them. "In the old times, a bell wasn't for calling people. It was for telling the lonely ones: *you are heard.*"

Old Tam, who had come to sit in on the lessons because he claimed the walk kept his knees honest, wiped his eye with the heel of his hand and blamed the woodsmoke.

* * *

That night I turned the whistle over by candlelight and read the worn characters, and what they said was this:

*I ring for the forgotten. Let the forgotten ring me.*

The whistle had been made for exactly this. Not for escape — that had only been one use of it, one verse of its song. It had been made, ages ago, by someone in the Spire who understood that forgetting is a kind of burial, and that the buried deserve a bell.

So I did what the bell-makers do. I made a decision.

The next morning, I hung the whistle from the eave above our door, on a cord of braided red wool that Marta spun for me without asking why, because Marta understood that some questions are just gifts wearing disguises. When the wind moved — and the wind moved often in winter, coming down off the white-hooded mountains — the whistle hummed. Not its escaping-song, not the tangle-note that had confused the Court. A softer tone. A single clear thread of sound that the whole village could hear if they listened, and half of them did listen, and the half that did began, without anyone deciding it, to call it the school-bell.

When it hummed, children came. When it hummed, I opened the door. When it hummed on days with no lessons, it hummed anyway, for whoever needed hearing — for old Tam on his bad-knee walks, for the widow Brenna whose son had gone down the mountain years ago and not come back, for anyone the world had quietly agreed to forget.

Let the forgotten ring me. I rang them back.

* * *

On the evening I gave Sebastian his name, the whistle was humming low above the door, and the fire was down to its orange bones, and the snow had stopped its breathing outside, and I made him sit on Tam's stool facing me like a student, which made him laugh, which made it easier.

"In the old tongue," I said, "a name isn't given. It's grown. Word by word. Like a tree. Each part means something true about you, and together they mean *you*, and if the parts are true enough, the name holds."

"Lena." He said my name carefully, the way he always did, as if it were made of eggshell. "You've been building something. I've watched you counting words under your breath for a month. I'm not stupid."

"No," I agreed. "You're not stupid. You're *the storm that chose to be quiet*." I said it in the old tongue, and then again, slowly, so he could feel each piece land. "That's your first root-word. It's true. Everything else grows from it."

His eyes went bright in the firelight. He didn't speak. He had learned, in two years of living with me, that when Lena is giving you something, you hold out your hands and you don't ask the price.

"Second word." I reached across and touched his wrist, where the pulse beat steady and human and unmagical. "*The mender.* Because you fixed roofs for thirteen years in a valley that forgot you, and you never once fixed them badly, and fixing things is what your hands do even when they have no magic left. Especially then."

"Third word," I said, and here my voice wobbled, and I let it, because a name grown from pretending isn't a name at all. "*The one who stays.*"

I looked at him. He looked at me. Above us the whistle hummed its thread of sound for the forgotten, and the fire ticked, and somewhere out in the white silence a fox barked once and thought better of it.

"Say it," I whispered. "All of it. It's yours. Nobody sold it. Nobody stole it. Nobody wrote it in any ledger. It belongs to exactly one person in the whole world, and he's sitting on Tam's stool."

And Sebastian Okafor — nameless man, storm that chose to be quiet, mender, the one who stays — said his new name aloud in the language before kingdoms, all three roots braided together, and the sound of it filled the room the way water fills a dry cup, and above the door the bone whistle shivered on its cord and hummed one note higher than it had ever hummed before, as if it recognized him.

As if it had been waiting thirteen years and two winters to ring for him specifically.

* * *

Word travels fast in a small village in winter, faster than sledges, faster than gossip, almost as fast as Marta. Within two days, the children were demanding to know my father's secret name, and he — bless his stubborn modest heart — refused to tell them, saying that names grown in private stay strong in public, which was a thing I had actually said to him and which he was now quoting back to me as if he'd thought of it himself, which is, I am told, what fathers do.

But they heard him answer to it. That was unavoidable. When I called him across the schoolroom in the old tongue — *storm-that-chose-to-be-quiet, come see what Pip built* — he turned, every time, the way a plant turns to light, quick and helpless and glad. And the children, being children, absorbed the shape of the name the way snow absorbs footprints, and soon half of them could say it, mispronounced in eleven endearing ways, and the name that no ledger in the world contained was being shouted daily across a snowy yard by six-year-olds with runny noses.

I thought my grandmother's ghost might object. The Spire-folk had kept their tongue like a locked box; knowledge was currency, currency was power, power was the whole rotten architecture that had ended with children like me hollowed out and shelved. Every rule I had ever inherited whispered that the language before kingdoms should stay rare, guarded, expensive.

But I had seen what guarding it built. I had walked through the Hollow Village and smiled back at identical smiles. I had sat in the palace of glass roots among children who had forgotten how to want anything. Guarding was just another word for burying, and buried things don't stay buried — they get dug up by people like my grandmother, and sold.

So I taught it free. To anyone who came. To Pip and his sister and the widow Brenna and old Tam, who learned the word for *bell* first and wept openly and pretended it was the woodsmoke again. Ten words, then twenty, then fifty. Not the deep words — not yet, not the words that open doors — but the true ones, the gentle ones, the ones that describe the world as something worth describing.

An inheritance, redirected. My grandmother's tongue, unburied and handed out like bread.

* * *

It was on the last evening of winter's deepest cold that the moth came.

I want to say I sensed it first, and maybe I did — there was a moment during the lesson when every child went quiet at once, the way birds go quiet, and I looked up from the slate board and saw them all staring past me at the window. Against the darkness beyond the frost, glowing soft as a coal wrapped in silk, hung a lantern-moth. Its wings shed pale light onto the snow. Its antennae trembled with the cold, or with purpose, or with both.

The children pressed to the glass. Old Tam crossed himself with the wrong hand and settled for patting Pip on the head. And the moth, unhurried, drifted down through the freezing air and landed on the windowsill inside — on the exact spot, I noticed with a chill that had nothing to do with the season, where a certain shard of singing glass had sat for two years, humming when I laughed.

Clasped in its small forelegs was a letter. Sealed in glass.

The moth looked at me. There was no other word for it. Its eyes were black and ancient and familiar, and I knew it — of course I knew it. The servant who had guided my father through the archive of sold souls. The friend inside the enemy's house. It dipped its head, once, formally, the way one scholar greets another.

Then it set the letter on the sill, waited precisely long enough for me to step forward, and spoke — in a voice like dust settling in an empty library, in the language before kingdoms, in perfect, flawless, heartbreaking grammar:

"*Teacher. I have come to learn.*"

Behind me, eleven children held their breath. On his stool by the fire, my father rose slowly to his feet, and the whistle above the door began, very softly, to sing.
