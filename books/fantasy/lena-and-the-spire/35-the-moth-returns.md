## 35. The Moth Returns

The first frost of the year came in the night and laid itself over the valley like a hand laid over a sleeping child's mouth — gentle, deliberate, meant to keep something in. Sebastian woke before dawn to the particular silence of a frozen world, the kind of silence he had once hated with his whole body, back when silence meant the absence of storms. Now it was only silence, and he had learned, mostly, to live inside it.

He was at the stove when the light changed.

Not the light of morning. Morning came gray and slow through the east windows, the way it always did in the valley, filtered through the ridge and the pines and the low ceiling of cloud that seemed to have taken up permanent residence since the dampening field had begun to soften. This was different. This was a warmth at the edge of his vision, gold and trembling, and it was coming from the direction of the garden.

He knew what it was before he looked. Some part of him had known for three days — since the bees had refused to leave their hive, since Lena had stopped mid-sentence at supper and stood with her head cocked like a bird's, listening to something none of the rest of them could hear. Since the shard on the windowsill, the one he kept from the archive, had begun to hum in a register too low for waking ears.

He opened the door, and the moth came down out of the dark like a lantern being lowered on a chain.

It was larger than the lantern-moths of the Court, larger than any moth had a right to be, its wings the color of old paper and candlelight, veined with lines of faint silver script that shifted when he tried to read them. It had been years — four, perhaps five; he had stopped counting the way he had stopped counting so many things — since he had last seen it, perched on the rim of the Archive of Sold Souls, guiding him through the dark with the patient courtesy of a servant who had long ago decided which side of a question it stood on. It had aged, if moths aged. The silver of its wing-script had gone dull at the edges, and one antenna bent at an angle that looked less like injury than like the way an old man's back bends — from long labor, from weather, from carrying things.

In its forelegs, held with absurd delicacy, was a letter.

The envelope was glass. Not glass the way a window is glass, or a bottle — this was glass the way the singing shards of the archive were glass, thin as a wafer, warm to the eye, holding a light that had no obvious source. Sebastian stood on his own threshold with the frost breathing around his ankles and did not move for a long moment. He had learned, beneath the mountain, that gifts from the Court arrived dressed as kindness, and that the kindness was sometimes real and sometimes a trap, and that the only way to tell the difference was to open them and see what remained of you afterward.

But the Court was dark now. The travelers said so. Marta said so, with the flat certainty of a woman who had walked to the mountain's shoulder herself and looked into the mouth of it. The glass roots grew wildflowers. The three faces had gone — dissolved, departed, or simply stopped, the way a bell stops when the hand finally closes around it.

The moth waited. It did not tap its forelegs impatiently. It had never been impatient, that one. It had only ever been present, which in the Court's palace had been its own quiet kind of rebellion.

"Hello again," Sebastian said. His voice came out rough, unused to speaking to anything older than the stove.

The moth dipped once, a bow of exactly the depth it had offered him in the archive — no deeper, no shallower, a courtesy preserved like a pressed flower — and extended the letter.

He took it. The glass was warm. That was the first thing, and it undid him more than he expected: warm, as if it had been carried against a living body the whole way from the mountain, which perhaps it had. He turned it in his hands. There was no writing on the outside, no seal, no address. There was only a spiral, pressed into the glass in relief, the coiled tower-mark that Lena had drawn on his floor with a wet finger on the first night she had come to his door, soaked and silent, carrying a whistle that hummed without wind.

Inside the glass, faintly, something moved.

He did not open it there. Some instincts survive even the burning-out of magic, and his told him that this was not a letter to be read standing in a frozen garden with an old moth watching. He looked up at the moth, and the moth looked back at him with eyes like drops of amber in which a whole evening's light had been preserved.

"Will you come in?" he asked. "It's cold, and there's tea, and — " he stopped, because he was about to say *and Lena will want to see you*, and he realized he did not know whether the moth had come only for him.

The moth's wings opened and closed once, slowly, the way an old dog wags its tail — not enthusiasm, but acknowledgment. It lifted from his hands and drifted past his shoulder into the house, through the door he held open, and settled on the beam above the stove with the sigh of a traveler who has finally been permitted to put down a burden it has carried much too far.

* * *

Lena came down the ladder from her loft room before he had the kettle on, drawn — he was sure — by the same listening that had stopped her mid-sentence at supper three nights ago. She was fifteen now, tall in the way of someone still surprised by her own height, her dark hair loose and her feet bare on the cold boards because she had never once, in all the years he had known her, shown any interest in the small self-preservations ordinary people performed without thinking.

She saw the moth on the beam and stopped. Her hand went to her throat, where the bone whistle hung on its cord — she still wore it, though she had not blown it since the night in the glass roots, and he suspected she never would again except at the very end of the world.

"*Sela*," she said. Not a word he knew. Her old tongue had become, over the years, a language the whole valley was learning from her, but it had depths he had never plumbed, and this word came from one of them. Her voice, when she used the old tongue, had a different grain than when she spoke the valley speech — older, rounder, as though the words came up from somewhere beneath the floor of her.

The moth dipped its head to her. And then it did something Sebastian had never seen it do, not in the palace, not in the archive, not in all its patient service: it made a sound. A small, dry, rustling sound, wings against the beam, arrhythmic, deliberate. Lena listened to it the way Marta listened to the price of wool — closely, missing nothing.

"It's been looking for us," she translated, "since the mountain went quiet. It carried this through three storms and a winter that hadn't decided to be winter yet." She paused. Her brow furrowed. "It says it wasn't sent. It says it *left*."

"Left," Sebastian repeated.

Lena listened again, her head tilted, and something moved through her face — not fear, he realized. Recognition. "It was a servant of the Court," she said slowly, "but it was made from something else. From a moth that used to live in the wildflowers on the mountain's shoulder, before the Court dug down. It says everything the Court made was made from something the Court took. And when the Court went quiet, all the taken things remembered what they were." She looked up at him. "It remembered the flowers."

The kettle began to sing. Sebastian turned it off the heat, and for a moment he simply stood there with his hand on the handle, looking at the small rust-brown creature on his beam, this servant of a dead bureaucracy that had walked — had *flown* — out of the ruins of its own making to deliver a letter it had not been asked to deliver, to people it had not been ordered to find.

"Then it's not a messenger," he said. "It's a refugee."

Lena considered this, and shook her head once. "It says it's a *return*."

* * *

They opened the letter at the table, in the full light of morning, because Sebastian had learned that dread feeds on shadow and he was done feeding it. Lena sat across from him with her hands folded, watching. The moth had come down from the beam and settled on the table's corner, close enough to see, far enough to be proper, a chaperone of one kind or another.

The glass envelope did not break so much as open — the spiral on its face turned under Sebastian's thumb like a key, and the two halves parted along a seam he hadn't been able to find, and the light inside came out into the kitchen.

It was not a letter, not in ink. It was a shard.

He knew the shape of it instantly, the way you know the shape of a face you grew up under. A singing shard, one of the archive's glass vessels, in which the Court had kept the souls of the sold — but this one was not singing. It was *quiet*, in the way that a held breath is quiet, and inside it, moving slowly like a figure behind frosted glass, was a light the color of storm-light, green-gray and restless and unmistakably alive.

And then it began to sing.

Not with words at first. With a hum, low and rising, a melody Sebastian had not heard in thirty years and had never once forgotten — the tune his grandmother had sung over the wash-basin, over the stew-pot, over his sleeping head in the years before he knew that her songs were in the dead tongue of the Spire-folk, that they were forbidden, that she was doing with them something far more dangerous than lulling a child. The melody filled the kitchen the way cold fills a kitchen, from the floor up, and the frost on the windows bloomed outward in fern-patterns, and the fire in the stove stood up straight and burned a shade bluer, and Lena's hand found Sebastian's across the table and gripped it hard.

Then, inside the song, words.

*Little storm. Little storm of mine.*

Her voice. Older than he remembered it, frayed at the edges, but hers — the voice that had named the clouds for him when he was four, that had told him thunder was only the sky clearing its throat, that had sung the songs she must have known would one day hang her if the wrong ears heard them. The voice he had heard once before, four years ago, in the archive, among the singing glass — the voice he had spent a night with, and a rite, and every last drop of the magic in his body to free.

*I don't know if this reaches you. I don't know what reaches anywhere, now. The moth says it will find you. The moth has been kind to me, and I have learned not to be surprised by kindness in strange shapes.*

Sebastian's eyes were burning. He did not look away from the shard. He would not look away.

*They let me go, little storm. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The contract voided, and the Court — the Court that was — opened the archive and let us go. Most of us went out like lamps. That's what passing on is, I think. But some of us stayed, because we had things to say, and no one to say them to, and the glass keeps what the glass keeps. I stayed. I have been waiting for the moth to grow its courage, and it has, the dear slow thing, and so here is what I stayed to say:*

*You gave your storms for me. I know. I felt them go — felt them like a door closing in a house I was still standing in. I have been angry at you for it and proud of you for it in the same breath, the way you are about all the people you love best. But listen to me now, because I was sold twice, once by my own hand and once by my own foolishness, and I know more about bargains than any Ledger-Keeper who ever kept one:*

*Nothing given in love is ever truly spent. It changes shape. It goes into the ground and comes up as something no one planted. You look at your empty hands and see an ending. I look at them and see a field before the spring.*

The song slowed. The light inside the shard gathered itself, the way a speaker gathers breath.

*The valley is softening. I can feel it from here — the field that kept magic out is thinning like old ice, and what comes through first will be small things, green things, things with no names yet. When they come, little storm, do not be afraid of them. They are not your storms coming back. They are the world's, and the world is generous, and it has been waiting a long time for someone in that valley to teach its children the old tongue.*

*Tell the girl — tell my girl, because if you are raising her then she is mine too, that is how it works, blood is the smallest part of it — tell her the spiral was never a mark of ownership. It was a door. Doors only frighten the people who have forgotten they can open.*

The melody wound down then, note by note, like a spindle running out of thread, and the last of it was almost too faint to catch, and it was not in the old tongue and it was not in the valley speech but in something between, something private, something that had only ever been sung over one sleeping boy's head by one old woman who had sold a soul to save a family and then, secretly, in the archive's cold dark, sold her own to keep the debt from landing where she could not follow it:

*Sleep well. Grow tall. The sky remembers every name the ground forgets.*

The light inside the shard dimmed — but did not go out. It settled, banked, the way a fire settles when it is being kept rather than extinguished, and the singing stopped, and the kitchen was very quiet, and outside the frost was melting off the east windows in long bright runnels because the sun had come up while no one was watching.

Sebastian became aware that his face was wet. He did not remember it starting.

* * *

Lena did not let go of his hand for a long time. When she finally did, it was only to reach across and take the shard from where it lay on the table, cradling it in both palms the way you would hold a bird — carefully, and with the understanding that it might leave whenever it chose.

"She stayed," Lena said softly. "She could have gone out like a lamp, and she stayed. For this."

"For this," Sebastian said. His voice was wrecked. He did not trouble to repair it.

Lena turned the shard in her hands, and the banked light inside it stirred, and — faintly, so faintly he might have imagined it — it hummed. Not the song. Something smaller. A single held note, contented, the note a kettle makes when it is taken off the heat.

"She's warm," Lena said, wonderingly. "Sebastian — she's *warm*."

He looked at his granddaughter — at the girl who was not his blood and was entirely his blood, who had come to his door mute and soaked and carrying a whistle that hummed without wind, who had taught a valley to speak the language before kingdoms, who had refused to be saved if saving cost him himself and had thereby, in the end, saved them both. He looked at her, and at the moth on the table's corner, which had bowed its ancient head as the song played and had not raised it since, and at the shard glowing in her hands like a coal that had decided, against all likelihood, to keep burning.

"The travelers say the glass roots are growing wildflowers," he said. "Marta heard it from the wool-factor, who heard it from a carter who went up the mountain road in the autumn. Whole slopes of them, they say. Flowers that don't grow anywhere else in the world."

Lena looked up. She was quick — she had always been quick — and he watched the thought arrive in her face the way the frost had arrived on the windows, all at once and from the inside out.

"Wildflowers," she repeated. And then, in the old tongue, a word he had learned that winter and never forgotten: *selenna* — the flower that grows where something was taken, the first green to come back to a burned field. The flower that remembers.

"The taken things," she said, "remembering what they were."

The moth lifted from the table. It circled the kitchen once, slow, its paper-colored wings catching the morning light, its silver script bright again at the edges as though the journey had restored something in it rather than spent it — and then it drifted to the windowsill, to the empty space beside the other shard, the one that hummed when Lena laughed, and settled there with the finality of a creature that has arrived somewhere and intends to stay.

Lena laughed — sudden, startled, delighted — and both shards hummed, and the sound they made together was not a song and not a word but a chord, two notes that had been sung apart for a very long time and were, at last, in a kitchen above a softening valley, being sung together.

Sebastian put his hand flat on the table, on the warm wood, and breathed.

That was when the knocking started — not at his door, but up the lane, door after door, the quick urgent knocking of someone carrying news that would not keep. He heard Marta's voice, carrying the way it always carried, from the direction of the inn, and though the words were still too far to resolve, the tone was unmistakable.

It was the tone of a woman running.

Lena was already on her feet, the shard cradled against her chest, the whistle bright at her throat, her bare feet silent on the boards as she went to the window where the frost was melting and the whole valley lay open below them, gold in the early light, every rooftop smoking, every lane filling with people turning toward the mountain.

Sebastian rose more slowly, the way an old man rises, and followed her to the glass.

Far off, at the valley's eastern edge, where the ridge met the sky, the clouds were breaking — not drifting, not thinning, but *breaking apart*, peeling back from the peaks like a bandage coming off a healing wound, and through the gap, for the first time in thirteen years, he could see the morning star.
