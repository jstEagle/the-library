## 36. Storm Without Thunder

The first storm of spring came up the valley like a guest who had learned manners.

Sebastian felt it before he saw it — not as he once had, not as a shout in his blood or a hand closing around his spine, but as something gentler: a warmth in the old scar-tissue of him, a faint pressure behind his ears like the memory of deep water. He was mending the south fence when it began, wire and willow-withies in his hands, Lena's mended coat hanging on the gatepost where she'd abandoned it to go chasing frogs along the irrigation ditch. The sky over the western ridge had gone the color of pewter, and beneath it the air lay thick and green-smelling, full of thaw and turned earth.

Thirteen years in this valley, and then two more since the mountain. Fifteen springs. He knew every way a storm could arrive here: slinking along the river's course, shouldering over the pass in a grey wall, splitting into scattered grumbles that never amounted to anything. This one came the way storms used to come to him — deliberately, as if it had somewhere to be.

He set down the wire and stood very still.

*It knows me,* he thought, and the thought did not frighten him. That was the strangest part. Two years ago — even one year ago — any stirring of the sky would have sent a cold wire of terror through him, the old fear that power was a debt that always came collecting. But the winter had been long and quiet, and he had spent it learning to be a man whose hands were only hands, and something in him had settled like silt in still water.

The wind arrived first, moving up the valley floor in a long breath that bent the young barley and carried the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet. It passed through him, around him, and for one heartbeat — no longer than the space between two heartbeats — something in the storm *noticed* him.

Not called. Not summoned. Not seized. Acknowledged.

It was like standing in a crowded market and hearing, across the noise, someone speak your name without looking for you. Like a dog lifting its head at a window as an old friend passes in the street. The pressure in the air shifted, gathered itself toward him the way water gathers toward a low place, and then — gently, almost courteously — let go.

The first drops fell, fat and cold on the back of his neck.

Sebastian Okafor laughed. It startled him, the sound of it, rough from disuse, and he laughed again just to hear it, standing in the rising rain with wire cutters in one hand and his face tipped up to the grey.

He didn't reach for more. That was the discipline now, though it wasn't discipline anymore either; it was simply what he wanted, which was nothing, which was everything. Once, reaching had been reflex — the storm touched him and he touched back, lightning answering lightning, and there had been glory in it and ruin too, hunters on his trail like wolves drawn by blood. Now the storm brushed against whatever remained of him and moved on, and he let it move on, and the letting-go tasted like bread fresh from the oven, like sleep after a day of honest work.

Like enough.

* * *

Lena came running through the rain with her hood down and her arms out, mouth open to catch the drops, and he watched her come and thought, as he thought every day now, that there was no sight in any world he'd traveled that could match it.

She skidded to a stop in the mud at the fence line, soaked through her shirt, eyes bright as wet stones. She signed fast — her hands had grown fluent over the winters, a language all their own now, half the old tongue's gestures and half invention: *Storm. First one. Did you feel it?*

"I felt it," he said.

Her hands stilled. Her head tilted, the way it did when she was deciding how much truth a question deserved. Then, slowly: *What did it feel like?*

He considered lying, or softening it, and decided against both. She had earned every true thing he had. "Like being remembered," he said. "By something I used to be."

Lena looked at the sky. Rain ran down her face and she didn't blink it away. When she signed again, her movements were slower, careful, the way she moved when she was saying something that mattered: *Does it hurt?*

"No." And he tested the word inside himself as he said it, turning it over, checking for cracks. "No, little bird. It doesn't hurt. It's like — you know the scar on my arm? From the ford?"

She nodded.

"It's like that. It aches sometimes when the weather changes. But the ache isn't the wound. The ache is just the body remembering it survived."

She thought about that for a long moment, rain drumming on her shoulders. Then she reached out and took his hand — the left one, the one with the wire cutters still hooked in the fingers — and turned it over, studying the calluses, the burn scar along the thumb he'd gotten pulling bread from Marta's oven last midwinter. His hands. Ordinary hands. Hands that mended fences and kneaded dough and braided her hair badly on festival mornings because she liked them badly braided, she said, because it proved he'd done it and not her own patient fingers.

*Good,* she signed, one-handed, still holding him. *I like your hands better this way.*

"You've told me."

*I'll keep telling you until you believe it.*

He believed it most days. He told her so, and she gave him the look that meant she knew he was telling the truth today and might not tomorrow, and that she would be there tomorrow anyway, and they walked up through the rain together, leaving the fence half-mended, because some storms were worth standing in and fences kept.

* * *

That evening the whole valley smelled of wet earth and woodsmoke, and the village gathered under Marta's wide eaves the way it did whenever the weather drove them together — which was often, and which everyone pretended to mind. Sebastian sat near the fire with a bowl of barley stew and listened to the talk wash over him: lambing worries, the state of the mill race, Old Tam's claim that the storm had struck the standing stone on the ridge and split it clean down the middle, which nobody believed except everybody would walk up and check by morning.

"Split it, did he?" Marta snorted from the hearth, ladling stew like a woman dealing cards. "Tam couldn't split a loaf of bread."

"There was light," Tam insisted. "Greenish light. And a sound like — like a bell underwater."

"Aye, and there's greenish light in your cup, that's what there is."

Laughter went around the room, warm and easy, and Sebastian found himself smiling into his bowl. Two years ago these people had watched him cross back over the valley boundary with a child's hand in his and a hunted look still on him, and they had taken him in without one question asked aloud — Marta first, iron bells still packed in her cart, her jaw set like a fortification. They had never entirely stopped watching him, not in the first year. But watching had become something else somewhere along the way. Watching became waving. Waving became borrowing. Borrowing became this: a seat by the fire, a bowl in his hands, a place in the noise.

Marta dropped onto the bench beside him with her own bowl and a groan that suggested the bench had personally wronged her. "You're smiling," she said suspiciously.

"It happens."

"Not at you it doesn't. You smile like a man who's gotten away with something." She nudged him with her elbow. "Out with it. What happened on that fence line?"

He hesitated. Some things were hard to say even now, even here. But Marta had followed him to the mountain's shadow with iron bells and farmers armed with pitchforks and stubbornness; she had earned his truths more than most.

"The storm," he said quietly. "When it came over the ridge. It — acknowledged me. Just for a moment. Like it knew I was there."

Marta chewed on that along with her stew. "And is that good or bad?"

"I don't know yet. I think—" He turned the spoon over. "I think it's neither. I think it's just what's left. A scar that aches when the weather changes."

"Mm." She ate another mouthful. "My hip does the same. Doesn't mean I miss the fall I took off Harrow's cart."

He huffed a laugh. "No. I suppose it doesn't."

"But?" Marta was relentless when she chose to be; it was why she made such a formidable innkeeper and such a terrifying ally.

"But I don't hate it," he admitted. "The ache. I spent fifteen years hating everything about what I was — the power, the hunger of it, the way it marked me. And then I spent it all buying Lena free, and I thought I'd feel empty. Grieved. And I do, some days. Some days I wake up and the sky's building something enormous over the pass and every part of me that ever called lightning is leaning toward the window like a dog at a door." He stared into the fire. "But today it just... said hello. And I said hello back. And then we both went about our business. And that felt like — "

"Peace," Marta said.

"Peace," he agreed. "With an unfinished self. If that's a thing."

"It's the only kind there is," Marta said flatly. "Anyone tells you different, they're selling something." She pointed her spoon at him. "Now eat your stew before Lena eats yours. Girl's got hollow legs and no shame."

Across the room, Lena was indeed eyeing his bowl with strategic patience, and beside her sat three of the village children, cross-legged on the floor, watching her hands move as she told them a story in the old tongue — the story of the moth who carried letters, from the sound of the gestures, her fingers fluttering. The children had begun picking it up the way children pick up everything: effortlessly, greedily, without knowing it was supposed to be forbidden. A language before kingdoms, alive again in a farm kitchen, taught by a girl who had nearly been hollowed out of her own name.

Sebastian watched her, and the fire crackled, and the rain came down steady and kind on the roof, and he thought: *this*. Whatever the storm had acknowledged in him on the fence line, whatever small ember of the old power still smoldered under his skin like a banked coal — none of it was worth more than this bowl, this bench, this noise.

He ate his stew before she could steal it. Mostly.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned to embers and the villagers had straggled home through the thinning rain, Sebastian walked back along the lane with Lena under one shared cloak, their boots sucking in the mud. The clouds had broken apart in the east, and through the tear in them the moon showed — fat, silver, three nights short of full.

Lena stopped walking.

He felt her go still under the cloak, felt the change in her breathing, and followed her gaze up to the moon, and understood.

The thirteenth turn. Their thirteenth turn — the anniversary of the night the moon had completed its cycle and the contract should have claimed her, the night they had instead spent walking out of the mountain with a stolen shard singing in his coat and the Smiling One's laughter echoing up the root-tunnels behind them. Every year since, the turn had come round again, and every year they had held their breath without meaning to, and every year the moon had simply... passed. Full, and waning, and gone. No riders. No ledger. No reckoning.

But this year was different. This year, Marta had been making noises about a festival. Properly making noises — lists, committees, commandeering of ovens. A lantern night, she called it. For the children, she said, though everyone knew which children, and which night, and what it was really for: a reckoning turned into a feast, grief given candles to hold.

Lena's hands moved slowly in the dark, pale moths against the black. *Three nights.*

"Three nights," he agreed.

*Are you afraid?*

He considered it honestly, because she could tell when he didn't. Was he afraid? He searched the old places where fear had lived — the hollow behind the sternum where dread had nested for fifteen years, the flinch he'd carried toward masked riders and singing glass and the smell of lantern-moths. The places were still there. But they were swept. Emptied. Nothing nested in them now but ordinary human weather.

"No," he said, and found it was true. "The court is dark. The contract is ash. Whatever comes on the thirteenth turn now, we meet it as free people. Besides—" he bumped her shoulder with his, "—you'll have a hundred lanterns and the whole village behind you. Even if the mountain itself came walking down the valley, Marta would hit it with an iron bell and give it soup."

Lena laughed — her silent laugh, shoulders shaking, breath catching, the laugh she'd had since before he knew her, the one that needed no voice and lost nothing for it. She leaned into his side as they walked, and above them the moon slid free of the last cloud, washing the wet lane silver, and far to the west, over the shoulder of the mountain, the storm was finishing itself in a slow flicker of heatless lightning — silent, distant, beautiful, like a language spoken too far away to understand.

He watched it and felt the faint ache in him answer, once, softly, and fade.

*Hello,* it said, or seemed to say. *Still there?*

*Still here,* he answered, in the place where words weren't needed. *Going nowhere.*

At the gate of their cottage, Lena paused with her hand on the latch and looked back at the mountain — a black shape now, crowned with cloud, its glass roots long since gone to wildflower according to the travelers' tales. Something moved in her face, an expression he'd learned to read over two years of silence: not fear, not quite. Anticipation wearing vigilance's clothes.

Then she tugged his sleeve and signed, quick and certain: *On the turn-night, I want to light the first lantern myself. For the hollowed ones. All of them.*

"I know," he said. "Marta's already put you first on the list. There was no discussion. There's never any discussion."

*Good.* She pushed open the door, then stopped, one more sign, small and grave: *And Papa — if the storm speaks to you again... you can answer. Just a little. I don't think it would take anything from you. I think it misses you the way I missed you. Before.*

His throat tightened. He crouched, kissed the top of her damp head, tasting rain.

"Go dry off, little bird," he said. "We've a festival to build."

Inside, he banked the fire and hung their wet cloaks, and through the window the moon climbed, three nights from full, and the last of the storm whispered away over the ridgeline — thunderless, asking nothing, leaving behind only the smell of rain on turned earth and the sense, faint and unmistakable, that somewhere far above the valley, the sky had smiled at him and moved on.

Three nights, he thought, setting the latch. Three nights, and the moon would turn complete, and the village would fill with lanterns for children who had been sold and children who had been saved and children not yet born who would never know either weight.

He slept without dreaming of storms, and woke before dawn to the sound of Marta hammering a festival notice to his gatepost, and the first thing he saw when he opened the door was the paper fluttering in the new wind — and the second thing he saw was the bone whistle, hanging on its nail by the door, humming softly all on its own, though there was no wind inside the house at all.
