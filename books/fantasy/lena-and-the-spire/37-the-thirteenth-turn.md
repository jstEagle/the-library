## 37. The Thirteenth Turn

The moon had been climbing toward its thirteenth turn for a whole season, and the whole valley had been watching it the way you watch a wound close — not daring to hope too loudly, in case hope was the thing that tore it open again.

I watched it from the roof of Marta's inn, because roofs were where I did my best thinking. My father-figure — my *father*, I corrected myself, and the word still fit strangely and wonderfully, like a shoe that had been made for someone else but had stretched to me over the winter — stood below in the yard with his sleeves rolled up, hanging lanterns from the eaves. He could not reach the high hooks anymore without the ladder, and he would not ask for help, and so Marta stood at the bottom of the ladder holding it steady and pretending she wasn't, and he pretended he didn't notice her pretending, and this was how love worked in our village: quietly, stubbornly, with a great deal of pretending.

*Sebastian,* I thought down at him, using the name I had built for him out of old-tongue pieces, syllable by syllable through that first winter when the world had taken his real one away. In my head the name sounded like rain on a warm stone. Like a door opening onto a room full of light. The Court had unwritten him from every ledger and every tongue, but they could not unwrite him from me. Names are not kept in books. They are kept in mouths.

"Girl!" Marta called up to me. "If you're going to sit on my roof, you can at least be useful about it. Hand down the garlands."

I handed down the garlands. I had learned to speak with my hands long before I ever trusted my voice, and Marta read hands the way scholars read scripture. She caught the bundles of dried rowan and blue flax against her hip and squinted up at me with her one sharp eye and her one soft one.

"You're not afraid," she said. It was not quite a question.

I shook my head. Then, because lying to Marta was like lying to a river — pointless, and the river always knew — I held up one hand and tipped it back and forth. *A little.*

"Good," she said. "Only fools aren't afraid of thirteenth turns." And she went off toward the bake-house with her arms full, humming one of the songs the children had learned from me, sung wrong in all the places that mattered and right in all the places that didn't.

* * *

Here is what I remembered about thirteenth turns, from before.

I remembered the mountain's belly, which is where I had lived for as long as I could remember before I ran. I remembered glass roots growing down through the dark like frozen lightning, and lantern-moths drifting between them in slow gold rivers, and the sound the sold souls made when the Court walked past their shelves — a singing, thin and sweet, like wet fingers on the rim of a cup. I remembered being told, in the Host's warm voice, that I should be honored. That a vessel chosen to hold a soul was a vessel *kept*. That wanting nothing was a kind of peace.

I remembered the other children. That was the part I carried worst. There had been eleven of us in the vessel-ward, in beds of woven root-fiber, each with our own small shelf above our bed where our shard would go when the turn came. Some of them were older than me. One boy, Ferren, used to tap rhythms on the wall with his knuckles, and I tapped back, and for a while we had a language of taps that no one else knew. Then Ferren's turn came early — some ledger adjustment, some debt called in ahead of schedule — and after that he didn't tap anymore. He smiled. Everyone in the ward smiled the same smile after their turn came, the smile I had seen later in the hollow village on the road, forty faces wearing one face. Ferren sat by the window and looked at the glass roots and smiled, and I tapped our rhythm on the wall every night until my knuckles bled, and he never answered again.

That was what a thirteenth turn meant, in the place I came from. Not a reckoning, exactly. A *harvest*.

And tonight the moon would finish its thirteenth turn since the contract was voided, and here in the valley we were going to hang lanterns and eat honey-cakes and let the children stay up past dark, and the difference between those two things — between harvest and festival, between the mountain's belly and Marta's yard — was a man without a name who had traded everything he was so that I would never have to learn what the inside of a shard felt like.

So no, I was not afraid. But my hands shook a little anyway, stringing the last lanterns along the inn's ridge-line, and I let them shake. Marta said grief that got to move was grief that didn't rot. She said a lot of true things in a voice like a boot heel on gravel.

* * *

The whole village turned out at dusk.

I want to write down exactly how it looked, because I am a keeper of words now, and keepers are supposed to keep even the ordinary miracles — especially the ordinary ones. The yard of the inn and half the lane beyond it filled with people: farmers with scrubbed necks and their good coats that smelled of cedar chests, the miller's twins chasing each other under the trestle tables, old Tam with his fiddle tuning and retuning because tuning was mostly what he loved about music. Iron bells hung from the well-post — Marta's idea, from the road, from the night she'd followed us with the villagers and their ringing, clattering courage — though nobody said anymore whether the bells were for keeping the Court's hearing away or just for the sound. I think they liked the sound. I think the sound meant *we survived something*, and villages need to say that out loud sometimes, even if only in bronze.

At the center of the yard stood the lantern-pole. It had gone up three days ago, a young birch felled and stripped and set in the earth where the maypole went in summer, and from its crown hung a cage of white glass.

Not a shard. A *lantern* — blown new by the glazier's widow, who had wept while she worked, she told me later, because the shape of it kept coming out wrong until she stopped trying to make it anything but what it was. Inside the glass sat a single moth, one of the palace-bred ones that had followed the freed souls home across the mountains like gulls following a fishing boat, and which had stayed, because moths, whatever else they are, know where they are tended.

It was not one of the soul-shards. It was only light. That was the whole point. Tonight there would be no singing glass, no shelves, no vessels. Tonight the light would be free to fly away whenever it wanted, and everyone would watch it go, and that would be the ceremony.

My father came to stand beside me while Tam's fiddle found its feet and the dancing started in the trampled grass. He had put on his good coat too, the grey one, and his hair was combed, and he looked — I searched for the word in two languages — *unarmored*. That was it. All year he had been slowly setting down the things he'd carried: the storms first, then the exile, then the guilt, layer by layer, like a man unpacking a pack he'd worn so long his shoulders had grown around its shape. He still flinched sometimes when thunder rolled far off over the rim of the valley. But in spring, on the night of the first storm, he had stood in the doorway with his hand flat against the doorframe and told me, very quietly, that the storm had acknowledged him. Not answered. Acknowledged. Like an old dog lifting its head when someone walks past the gate. He hadn't reached for more. He'd come inside and finished his supper.

"Lena," he said now. His mouth still stumbled on my name sometimes — not because it was hard, but because names cost him something now, every one he spoke, and he paid gladly, and I saw him pay. "Are you ready?"

I nodded. Then I took his sleeve and pulled him aside, behind the woodpile, where the lantern-light didn't reach, because what I had to say was not for the festival.

*I dreamed about Ferren,* I signed. *Last night. He was tapping on the wall.*

His face did the thing it did when I mentioned the ward — a tightening around the eyes, the look of a man hearing a debt called in that he can never repay. He crouched down to my height. He always crouched. Even now that I was nearly up to his shoulder, he crouched, out of habit from the first winter, and I never told him to stop because I loved the habit more than I cared about the height.

"What did he tap?" he asked.

*Our rhythm. The one we made.* My hands hesitated. *But in the dream, when I tapped back — he answered. And then he said thank you. In the old tongue. And woke up.*

Sebastian was quiet a long moment. Somewhere beyond the valley rim, thunder muttered, far and polite, like a guest waiting to be invited in.

"The moth-servant's letter," he said slowly. "When the contract dissolved. Every soul in the archive was released. Every shard unmade." He looked toward the lantern-pole, at the little moth turning its gold circles in the glass. "You don't think—"

*I don't know,* I signed. *But tonight, when we light the first lantern — I want to say his name. Out loud. In the old tongue. All of them. Every child whose turn came before ours did.*

He put his hand on my head, the way he did, heavy and careful, like resting a palm on the crown of a small tree.

"Then we'll say them," he said. "All of them. Loud enough to reach wherever tapping gets answered."

* * *

Full dark came, and the moon rose fat and complete over the eastern ridge, one sliver short of its thirteenth turn, and the whole village fell silent the way snow silences a field.

Marta brought the taper. She had argued for days about who should light the first lantern — the mayor wanted it, the glazier's widow deserved it, Tam offered to light it with a burning fiddle-string, which satisfied no one — but in the end Marta had ended the argument the way she ended most arguments, by picking up the taper and saying, "The child lights it, or nobody does."

She pressed the taper into my hand. Its flame leaned toward the pole as if it too had somewhere to be.

I walked to the foot of the lantern-pole through a corridor of people, and every face in it was open, and none of the smiles were the same, and I understood for the first time in my life that this — the crooked, mismatched, individual ridiculousness of a hundred different human smiles — was the exact opposite of the hollow village, the opposite of the ward, the opposite of everything the Court had ever built beneath the mountain. Uniformity was their cathedral. Difference was ours.

I touched flame to wick.

The lantern caught with a soft breath of light, and the moth inside lifted, circled once, twice — and then the glazier's widow opened the little glass door at the top, as we had planned, as we had practiced, and the moth rose out of its cage into the open dark.

It flew a slow spiral upward, gold against the black, spiraling the way the coiled tower spiraled, the mark I had drawn on Sebastian's door the first night, the mark of the Spire-folk, the language before kingdoms — and then it straightened and flew south, toward the mountain, and did not come back, and everyone watched it go, and nobody spoke, and the silence was the fullest silence I have ever stood inside.

Then I turned to the crowd, and I spoke.

My voice is still a strange thing to me. For eight years I did not use it, and even now it comes out low and rough-edged, a voice that sounds like it has been carried a long way. But the old tongue does not care what your voice sounds like. The old tongue is older than voices. It only cares that you mean it.

"Ferren," I said. "Who tapped the wall. Who taught me that even in the dark, someone will answer."

And from the edge of the crowd, small and clear, came a knock of knuckle on wood — the miller's twins, who had learned the rhythm from me without my ever knowing it, tapping it back across the yard, and then another pair of hands joined, and another, until the whole village was keeping Ferren's rhythm on tables and fence posts and the sides of barrels, a heartbeat made of a hundred hands.

I said the next name. And the next. Eleven names from the ward, and then the ones Marta had gathered from travelers' tales, hollowed children from villages we'd never seen, and then — because Sebastian stood beside me and nodded, his eyes bright and wet — the names of the adults too, the ones whose souls had been sold generations back, whose singing glass had lined the archive shelves, including one voice I had heard myself through a stolen shard on a festival night: a woman singing a lullaby in a tongue she thought no one living remembered. *Grandmother Coldharbour,* I named her, and beside me my father bowed his head, and the village knocked her rhythm gently on the wood, and somewhere far off the thunder rolled once, soft, like acknowledgment.

We ran out of names before we ran out of night. So I said the last words I knew to say, the closing formula of the old tongue, the one that means roughly *you are counted, you are kept, you are released* — and the village repeated it after me, mangling the vowels beautifully, and the iron bells rang from the well-post, and the dancing started again, and the honey-cakes came out, and the thirteenth turn passed over our valley like a ship passing over a sleeping harbor, harmless, unhurried, carrying nothing but moonlight.

* * *

Much later — the fire burned to embers, the twins asleep under a table like two puppies, Marta snoring in her chair with her boots still on — I sat on the doorstep with my father, and we did what we always did on nights that were too big to talk about: we watched the sky.

The moon hung at the top of its arc, whole and silver, beginning its long slide down the other side of the world. Thirteen turns. Finished. Owed to no one.

"Do you remember," Sebastian said, "the first night you knocked on my door? I thought the storm had sent you. I thought you were a punishment."

I laughed — silently, the way I still laughed first, my shoulders shaking before my voice caught up. *You looked at me like I was a flood coming through the floorboards.*

"You were," he said. "The best kind." He was quiet a while, turning his cup of cider in his hands. "Lena. Can I tell you something I haven't said?"

I leaned my head against his arm. *Always.*

"When I gave up my name, I thought I was paying for you. Trading what I was for what you are. And for a long time that felt like a fair price and also like a wound." He looked up at the moon. "But tonight, listening to you say those names — I understood. You weren't the price. You were the *return*. Everything the Court ever took, everything my grandmother lost, everything the valley forgot — it all comes back through you. Through the old tongue. Through a school-bell made of bone. I didn't lose my name, girl. I invested it."

I sat with that. The embers ticked. Far off on the ridge, the dampening field shimmered faintly where it always shimmered, though all winter it had been softening — small magics seeping in at the edges, wildflowers blooming out of season in the hollows, old Tam's rheumatism easing on damp mornings, the well water tasting faintly of lightning. The valley was remembering itself. We were remembering ourselves. Perhaps those were the same act.

Then, down the lane, out of the dark between two houses, came a flutter of wings.

A moth. Palace-bred — I knew the deep-gold dust of their wings anywhere, knew it from the mountain's belly, from the lantern-rivers, from the letter sealed in glass. But this one did not circle the yard or settle on the lantern-pole. It flew straight to our doorstep, straight to me, and hovered at the height of my heart, and I saw that clasped beneath it, gripped in its small dark legs, it carried something that caught the ember-light and held it.

A shard. A small one, no bigger than my thumbnail, singing — very faintly, very sweetly, a few notes, over and over, like someone humming to themselves while they work.

And I knew the tune. I had known it since I was small enough to sleep in the vessel-ward. It was a tapping rhythm. Four beats, rest, four beats. Wall-wood, wall-wood. Someone answering, someone answering, someone answering at last.

Ferren's song. Come home.

Beside me my father had gone very still, and the wind moved through the valley without any storm in it at all, and the moth waited at the height of my heart with the soul of a boy who had forgotten how to want — except, it seemed, for one thing.

I reached out my hands to take it.
