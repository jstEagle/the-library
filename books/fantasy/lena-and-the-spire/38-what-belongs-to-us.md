## 38. What Belongs to Us

### Sebastian

The morning of the naming came in gold.

Spring had been working at the valley for weeks by then — prying the frost from the fence posts, greening the orchard rows, filling the ditches with a sound like small applause — but that morning it outdid itself. The light came down the eastern ridge in long slanted bars, and every one of them seemed to land on something Marta had hung: lanterns in the alder trees, ribbons of dyed wool strung between the well and the inn's porch, garlands of early meadow-sweet laid across the threshold stones of the meeting green.

I stood at my window with a shirt in my hands and could not make myself put it on.

It was only a shirt. Blue wool, mended twice at the collar, the same as any I'd worn for thirteen years of exile and one year of something else. But my hands kept stopping. There is a strange arithmetic to days like that — how a man can face a court beneath a mountain without flinching and be undone by a clean shirt and a morning full of neighbors.

Lena knocked on my doorframe the way she always did now: two knuckles, quick, an old habit from the mountain where silence had been survival. She was dressed in the dress Marta's daughters had sewn her, deep green with a collar worked in white thread, and around her neck, on its new cord, hung the bone whistle. She had refused to leave it behind even for a ceremony. I understood. Some things you carry not because they are beautiful but because they are proof.

"You're staring at that shirt like it owes you money," she said. Her voice had grown all winter — still quiet, still careful, but hers now, used daily, like a path that stays open because feet keep finding it.

"It's a fine shirt," I said.

"It's your only shirt."

"That's what makes it fine."

She grinned and came in and took it from my hands and held it out to me with the exaggerated patience of a child managing a fool, and I put my arms through the sleeves, and just like that the impossible morning became an ordinary one, which is the only kind I have ever survived.

Down in the village, the whole valley had turned out. That still astonished me when I let myself notice it. For thirteen years these people had tolerated me the way one tolerates weather — useful, distant, vaguely feared. Now they had hung ribbons. Old Tomas had swept the meeting green himself, though his back made sweeping a form of penance. The miller's wife had baked until her kitchen surrendered. Children ran underfoot wearing crowns of woven grass, and nobody told them to sit still, because this was a day that belonged to them too, though they didn't know why yet.

Marta found me at the edge of the crowd. She looked older than she had a year ago — we all did; the year had spent us freely — but her eyes were the same steady gray they'd been the day she'd stood between me and a mob of frightened farmers and said, *He mends roofs. What have any of you ever mended?*

"Nervous?" she asked.

"I've bargained with three faces of a deathless court," I said. "I called lightning out of a clear sky. I stole from an archive of singing glass."

"And none of that required a clean shirt," she said, "or standing in front of people who know you." She straightened my collar with two brisk tugs, the way she'd done for her own children a lifetime ago. "This is harder. This is the thing that matters. It's supposed to shake you."

The rite was old — older than the village, older than the kingdom's borders, dragged here generations ago by settlers who half-remembered it. In most valleys it had worn down to a few words and a handshake. But Lena had spent the winter teaching us the language before kingdoms, and the words had come back to the valley like water returning to a dry channel, and so today we would speak the rite properly, in the tongue it had been born in, the way it was meant to bind.

The village gathered in a circle on the green. Not a crowd watching — a circle enclosing, which is a different shape entirely. I had learned the difference beneath the mountain, where circles were drawn to keep things in. This one was drawn to hold something safe.

Lena stood across from me. The sun caught the whistle at her throat and it gave off a faint warmth-light, the way it sometimes did now, gently, like a sleeping animal's breath.

Old Tomas, because he was oldest, spoke the opening. His accent was terrible and he knew it and he said the words anyway, loudly, daring anyone to smile. *We stand on ground that remembers.* And the circle answered, forty voices rough and imperfect: *We stand on ground that remembers.*

Then it was mine.

I had rehearsed this. I had rehearsed it in the barn at midnight, alone, whispering to the goats, who were unimpressed. But standing there with the whole valley looking at me, the words went somewhere deeper than rehearsal had reached.

"In the tongue before kingdoms," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I let it, "there is no word for *orphan*. There is no word for *step*, or *half*, or *foster*. There is only *mine-by-choice* —" I said the old word, *séva-neth*, and heard it pass around the circle like a flame passed hand to hand, each person repeating it, learning it, keeping it — "*and it is the strongest word they had. Stronger than blood. Because blood is given to you, and anything given can be taken. But chosen—" I looked at Lena. She was crying already, silently, furiously, in the way of someone who has decided tears will not stop her. "Chosen is claimed. Chosen is defended. Chosen does not lapse."

I knelt. My knees complained. I ignored them.

"Lena," I said. "Child of no court, vessel of no one, daughter of the road and the river and this valley. I cannot give you my blood. I have something better. I give you my name."

In the old tongue, a name is not a label. It is a roof. To name someone into your family is to build walls around them out of everything you are — your history, your failures, your grandmother's guilt and your grandmother's courage, the storms you called and the storm you no longer carry. I spoke the words of the rite and felt them do what words do when they are true: they settled over us both like a roof going up beam by beam.

"I am Sebastian Okafor," I said — and the ledger beneath the mountain had taken that name, erased it from every record, every tongue, every memory outside this valley, but here, inside the circle, inside the ground that remembers, it still meant something, it still *held* — "and I say: whatever is mine is yours. My table. My door. My winters and my harvests. My mistakes, which are many, and my love, which is not."

The circle spoke the answering line, all of them, even the smallest children who'd been drilled all week: *What is his is hers. What is hers is his.*

Then it was Lena's turn to answer, and the green went very quiet, because everyone knew what she had prepared, and no one — least of all me — knew exactly what was coming.

### Lena

My heart was doing something embarrassing against my ribs.

I had spoken before crowds all winter. I taught the little ones their letters in the old tongue; I led the songs; last month I had stood up at the inn and told the story of the hollow village to thirty adults without my voice shaking once. But this was different. This was the sentence I had been building since the night I first climbed onto a stool in his kitchen and said, in a language dead everywhere but inside me, *I am hungry,* and he had gotten up without a word and fed me.

Sebastian knelt in front of me with his eyes wet and pretending not to be, and I thought: *he traded everything for me. His magic. His name. His whole self, piece by piece, handed over to three smiling faces under a mountain. And he thinks today is about what he gives me.*

So I was going to correct him. In front of everyone. In the old tongue, because some things must be said in the language they were meant for.

I took a breath and began.

"*A séva-neth neth-úra,*" I said. *Mine-by-choice, I claim him back.*

There was a ripple through the circle — people glancing at each other, sensing the rite had bent off its expected path. Sebastian's eyebrows went up. Good. Pay attention, old man.

"The rite says the parent names the child into the family," I said, switching to the common speech so no one would miss a word. "But I looked in the grammar. All winter, while you were all learning your letters, I was reading the old texts Marta bought off the peddler-queens. And the grammar says something the villages forgot." I lifted my chin. "It says the claiming goes both ways or it doesn't hold. A name given is only half a roof. The child must raise the other half. The child must say: *and I take you too. Your table, your door, your mistakes, your love. You are mine-by-choice also.*"

I knelt too, so we were eye to eye, kneeling on the spring grass in our circle of neighbors, and I said the words I had written and rewritten twenty times in my slate book:

"Sebastian Okafor. Man without a name, except the one I'm giving you. Storm-caller who doesn't call storms anymore, and is more than he was anyway. I take your table. I take your door. I take your grief for your grandmother, and your grief for your storms, and your terrible jokes, and the way you talk to the goats like they're colleagues. I take all of it. Whatever is yours is mine, and I will carry it, the way you carried me out from under a mountain."

His mouth did the thing it does when he's trying not to feel something enormous. I pressed on, because the last part was the part that mattered, the part I'd saved.

"And I answer to your name," I said. "Your family name. The one you gave me this morning in the rite. Say it again. Say it so the ground remembers."

And he did. He said it — *Okafor* — in the old pronunciation, the deep one, the one that means *root-that-holds-in-floodwater*, and I said it after him, loud, louder than I have ever said anything, letting it fill my mouth and the whole green: "*Okafor.*"

The valley said it with me. Forty voices, rough and imperfect and certain. Somewhere in the circle Marta was sobbing openly into her apron and daring anyone to comment. The children threw their grass crowns in the air, having understood approximately nothing except that throwing things was permitted, which was enough.

And underneath our knees, in the soil, in the old stone under the soil, I felt the valley *remember*. That is the only way I can say it. The dampening field that had kept magic out for thirteen years — softened now, thinned by my grandmother's freed soul passing over it, by the whistle's song, by a year of the old tongue spoken in kitchens — stirred like a sleeper turning toward a familiar voice. Not magic rising. Just the ground, paying attention.

The bone whistle hummed at my throat, warm as a held hand.

Later — after the feast, after the dancing, after old Tomas fell asleep sitting up with a plate balanced on his chest and no one woke him out of respect — I sat with Sebastian on the inn steps while the lanterns burned down. The moon was nearly round. One turn short of thirteen. But it was only a moon now, doing what moons do, counting nothing, claiming nothing.

"Do you know what I thought, that first night?" he said. "When you showed up soaked at my door with that whistle? I thought: *this is trouble, send her to Marta, keep your door shut, you've survived this long by keeping the door shut.*"

"Why didn't you?"

He was quiet a while. Firelight moved on his face, on the lines the last year had carved deeper.

"Because you drew the spiral," he said finally. "On the fogged glass. And I recognized it. And I realized I'd been drawing it myself, in the frost, for years, without knowing what it was. Some part of me had been waiting at that door the whole time." He glanced at me sideways. "Belonging isn't inherited, Lena. I learned that too late for my grandmother and just in time for you. It's built. Beam by beam. Like a roof."

I leaned my head on his shoulder, which I am tall enough to do now, barely, if he slouches, which he did.

Above us the almost-full moon cleared the ridge, and the valley lay quiet, and the whistle hummed its low contented note, and I thought: *this is what belongs to us. Not the house, not the name, not even each other exactly. This. The choosing, renewed daily, like tending a fire.*

That was when the moth came.

It came out of the dark above the orchard — a lantern-moth, one of the Court's, its glow soft amber instead of the cold silver they'd carried beneath the mountain. It circled the inn twice, unhurried, and landed on the gatepost in front of us, and folded its wings, and waited, the way a messenger waits who knows the message will change everything.

On its back, glowing faintly through the wing-membrane, was a seal of living glass.

Sebastian went very still beside me. Then he reached out, slowly, the way you reach toward something that might be a gift or might be a debt, and the moth stepped onto his palm without fear, and the seal opened like an eye.

Inside it, faint and far and unmistakable, someone began to sing.

My grandmother's song. His grandmother's voice.
