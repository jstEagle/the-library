## 39. The Spire's Echo

The travelers came in autumn, as they always did, when the pass was still open and the roads were thick with the smell of cut hay and woodsmoke. They came to the valley because the valley had become the kind of place travelers came to — which was a thing I had never expected, and which Marta never tired of reminding me she had predicted.

"The field is soft now," she'd say, leaning on her doorframe, watching strangers wander past her inn toward the orchards where small magics had begun to bloom. "I told you it would soften. Ten years ago I told you."

"You told me the field would *kill* them," I said. "You were very specific about the killing."

"I said it might kill them. There's a difference. A good innkeeper knows the difference between what might happen and what will." She handed me a basket of late plums because refusing her was impossible and we both knew it. "Go on. The girl's been asking after you all morning. She talks like a river that's learned it's allowed to run downhill."

Lena talked like that now. It still caught me sometimes, even after all these years — the sound of her voice rising over the garden fence, teaching the village children their letters in two tongues at once, the old words rolling beneath the new ones like stones under clear water. When she laughed, the whole valley seemed to lean toward her the way wheat leans toward sun.

And on my windowsill, the shard hummed.

* * *

It was a small thing, no bigger than a sparrow's egg, rough on one side where I had pried it from its setting in the archive of sold souls, smooth on the other where ten thousand years of lantern-moth wings had polished it glass-bright. In the early days I had kept it wrapped in wool at the bottom of a chest, afraid of it, the way you are afraid of a letter you have not yet opened. Then one evening Lena had laughed at something — I no longer remember what; some joke about Marta's cat and a stolen fish — and the chest had sung.

Not loudly. A single note, low and warm, like a finger run around the rim of a wet cup. Like someone humming in another room who is happy and does not care who knows it.

I had carried the shard to the window and set it there, and it had been there ever since, through fourteen winters, humming whenever joy passed close enough to touch it.

My grandmother's soul. Or what remained of her, freed from the Court's archive by my own hands, released by my own storms, spent down to nothing so that she could go wherever souls go when they are finally paid for and pardoned both. The scholars who came through now — and they came often, gray-robed men and women with ink-stained fingers who asked me questions I mostly declined to answer — said that a soul could not be destroyed, only moved. That what sat on my windowsill was not her, exactly, but her echo. The shape her gladness left behind in the glass.

I did not correct them. I liked the word *echo*. It meant something had been here, and mattered, and was gone but not erased.

Some mornings, before the village woke, I would sit with my tea going cold and hold the shard up to the light and talk to it. I told it about the harvest. About Lena's students. About the wildflowers.

Mostly, I told it about the wildflowers.

* * *

Because that was the thing the travelers came to tell us, every autumn now, with the particular excitement of people carrying news too large for their saddlebags: the mountain court was dark.

"Empty," said the first one who told us, a wool merchant with frost in his beard, sitting in Marta's common room with his boots steaming by the fire. "Not ruined, mind. Not broken. Just — empty. Like a house after everyone's gone to church. All the lanterns out. All those glass roots glowing pale instead of gold, and growing *up*, out through the mountain's seams, breaking the surface in a hundred places. And everywhere the roots come up—" He shook his head, hunting for the word.

"Wildflowers," Lena said quietly, from the corner where she sat mending a student's torn sleeve.

The merchant stared at her. "How did—"

"They always say wildflowers," she said, and smiled, and went back to her mending.

But I watched her hands. They had gone very still around the needle, the way they went still only when something old was moving under the floorboards of the present. Later, walking home along the orchard path with the first stars coming out, she took my arm — she was tall enough now, nearly, though I refused to acknowledge it — and said nothing for a long while, which for Lena was its own kind of speech.

"You should see it," she finally said. "Before winter closes the pass. You should see what they grew into."

"Lena—"

"I'm not asking you to go back *in*, Papa." She used the word deliberately, the way she always did, laying it down between us like a coin on a counter. She had chosen it herself, years ago, in the old tongue first and then translated, and no ledger anywhere in the world held a record of it, which was precisely the point. "I'm asking you to look at a mountain. From the outside. With your daughter. Before the snow."

We went in the last week of autumn, when the larches had turned the color of struck matches.

* * *

The road up was the road I remembered, which was the road I had spent thirteen years trying to forget and then eleven more learning to carry without flinching. The ford where the hunters had turned back. The ridge where I had called lightning for the first time in thirteen years and been sickened by how easily it answered. The bend where bandits had waited and where a girl of eight had watched me burn the sky and had not been afraid — had, in fact, looked at me afterward with an expression I could not name until many years later, when I understood it was recognition. As if she had known all along what lived in me, and had simply been waiting for me to know it too.

The river was low and quick and cold. We crossed at the shallows, and it did not ask anything of either of us. It had stopped asking, the moth-servant had written us years ago. The River That Remembers Names had gone quiet since the Court dissolved, holding its memories loosely now, returning them like a lender with no debtors. Travelers said you could wade across and come out remembering everything you'd ever surrendered, and that some people stood in the middle of it for hours, weeping, getting back pieces of themselves they'd forgotten they'd sold.

I thought of my wedding day sometimes. Not often. It came back to me whole, unasked-for, usually in spring: a green morning, rain threatening and never falling, a woman whose face I could see clearly now and whose name I could also see clearly, and who had died in the plague winter three years before I fled to the valley, which was why the memory had cost me less than it might have. Grief returned is a strange gift. You grieve twice, but the second time you get to be grateful.

Lena walked beside me with her hood down, letting the wind do what it liked with her hair, and the shard rode in my pocket, silent, waiting.

The mountain's shoulder came up gray and enormous against a sky the color of pewter, and then we rounded the last spur and saw what the travelers had been trying to describe for years and failing.

The glass roots had breached the surface everywhere. They rose out of the rock like the ribs of some buried cathedral deciding, after millennia underground, that it wanted sky. But they were not the gold-lit, hungry-looking things I remembered from beneath. They had gone pale — moon-pale, milk-pale — and translucent, and where each root broke the earth, flowers grew. Not planted flowers. Not tame ones. Wild things in every color the autumn hadn't yet taken: gentians blue as deep water, harebells, a kind of white poppy I had no name for, growing straight out of living glass as if glass were the most ordinary soil in the world.

And the court itself — we climbed high enough to see it, through a seam in the mountainside that had once been a gate and was now just an opening, hung with ivy — the court was dark and open and empty. The great hall where the three faces had received us with hospitality dressed as dread: bare. The archive where souls sang in their thousands: silent, the shelves standing in rows like beehives in winter. Lantern-moths still drifted in the deep places, but few of them, and slow, and when one passed close to Lena it circled her twice and settled briefly on her shoulder like a bird greeting an old friend before drifting away into the dark.

No Host to welcome. No Ledger-Keeper to count. No Smiling One to smile.

"They're gone," I said. "Truly gone."

"Not gone," Lena said. She was standing in the doorway of the great hall, looking out at the valley far below, at our valley, a smudge of smoke and orchard-green under the afternoon light. "Changed. There's a difference. You taught me that." She touched the wall, and the glass under her palm glowed faintly, the way a banked fire glows. "Something slept here a long time and did terrible things in its sleep. Now it's waking up as something else. Things do that." She glanced at me sideways, with the ghost of her old mischief. "You did that."

I had no argument. Thirteen years in a valley that forgot, and I had called myself peaceful, and I had been lying. Eleven years since, and I had called myself finished, powerless, a man with quiet hands — and I had been lying again, or at least telling only half the truth. What I had become was not the absence of the storm-caller. It was whatever the storm-caller was when you took away the storm and left the man. I was still learning his name. So, in a way, was he.

We ate bread and hard cheese on the mountainside among the wildflowers, and the wind smelled of stone and gentian, and the shard in my pocket stayed silent, because there was no laughter near it, only a kind of vast and gentle quiet that it apparently did not know what to do with.

On the way down, Lena picked one white poppy — just one, apologizing to the root as she cut it — and pressed it between the pages of her songbook, where it lives still, thin as breath and almost transparent, the way old memories get when they've been handled with love enough times.

* * *

Winter came, and the travelers stopped, and the valley closed itself like a hand around its warm small life. I taught fewer lessons now — my voice had developed a rasp the village children found hilarious and imitated mercilessly — but I still sat in on Lena's classes, in the back by the window, ostensibly to mend the shutters and actually to listen.

She taught the old tongue the way water teaches a stream bed: patiently, inevitably, by simply flowing where she flowed. The children learned the words for snow and bread and friend and stay. They learned the spiral, drawn in flour on the table, traced in the frost on windows — the coiled tower that had once been a brand of ownership and was becoming, syllable by syllable, a sign of home. The bone whistle hung above the schoolroom door, and when lessons ran long into the golden end of the afternoon, Lena would take it down and blow a single round note, and the children would pour out into the snow like spilled light.

Marta had died two winters ago, in her sleep, in the good bed above her inn, having arranged every detail of her own funeral except the weather, which disappointed her enormously — it was a beautiful day. The inn belonged to her nephew now, who kept her rules and her plums and her portrait, and who still set aside the corner table for me and Lena every market day without being asked.

It was in that corner, on the first market day of the new year, that the moth found us.

It came in through the door with the cold air, unhurried, wings dusted with snow, and the whole room went quiet the way rooms do when something impossible walks in wearing the ordinary like a coat. It flew the length of the common room, past the fire, past the staring faces, and landed on our table between the teapot and the salt, and folded its wings, and waited.

Then it opened them, and on the undersides, in characters of faint silver light, words appeared — the Court's old way of writing, the way the moth-servant had written to us for years, though the letters had grown sparser as the Court dimmed, and this was the first in almost three years.

Lena read it aloud, softly, so only I could hear, translating as she went, her voice steady and her eyes bright:

*"To the nameless man and the keeper of the tongue. The last lights are going out below the mountain, one by one, the way lamps are put out at the end of a feast — not in fear, but in fullness. What we were is ended. What we are becoming has no need of vessels, or ledgers, or names taken as payment. We return this final thing, which we have kept too long: the memory of why we began. It was not hunger, in the beginning. It was loneliness. We had forgotten. She helped us remember."*

The moth tilted its head, and the silver letters faded, and new ones kindled:

*"There is one more thing below, in the deepest root, where the first lantern was ever lit. It is singing. It has been singing for eleven years. It says it is waiting for its grandson to come and hear the end of the song. It says he will know the way. It says to tell him: the thirteenth turn was never a reckoning. It was an invitation."*

The letters went dark. The moth closed its wings, drank a drop of honeyed water Lena offered on her fingertip, and then rose, circled the room once — the children who had crept close gasped and laughed — and flew out into the blue snow-light, south, toward the mountain.

Lena and I sat for a long time without speaking. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, the whistle-note of a child calling another child to play rang off the frozen eaves.

"Papa," Lena said at last, and her voice was careful the way it was careful only when something mattered past the reach of carefulness. "Grandmother's shard. It's been humming more lately. Hasn't it."

It had. For months. Little runs of notes at odd hours, half-phrases, like someone practicing. Like someone remembering a song they'd known long ago and finding their way back to it verse by verse.

"It has," I said.

"And the letter says she's been singing for eleven years." Lena counted silently, her lips moving, and then she looked up at me, and I saw in her face the same expression I had seen on an eight-year-old soaked and shivering in my doorway all those years ago — recognition, arriving ahead of understanding. "Eleven years. Since the rite. Since you poured your storms into the glass and set her free." She reached across the table and took my hand, and her grip was strong, a woman's grip, a keeper's grip. "Papa. She didn't leave. She went *down*. Into the deep root. She's been singing to them — to the Court — all this time. Teaching them. Softening them. Every wildflower on that mountain grew from her song."

The shard. At home, on the windowsill, in the low winter light. Humming its half-remembered phrases. Waiting, perhaps, the way the letter said, for someone to come and hear the end of the song.

"We'll need provisions," I heard myself say, because some part of me had already stood up and put on its coat. "And the pass will close within the month. And I haven't traveled that road since—" I stopped. Started again. "Lena. Your students. Winter term."

"Winter term ends in nine days," she said, smiling now, tears standing bright in her eyes and refusing, with tremendous dignity, to fall. "And I am coming with you. Obviously. Someone has to translate. And someone—" she squeezed my hand, "—has to make you laugh on the road, so the shard can sing us the way."

That night I sat by the window a long time, tea cold, lamp unlit, the valley sleeping white and safe around me. On the sill, the shard caught the moonlight — waxing, I noticed, toward its turn — and hummed. Not the little contented chime it gave for laughter. Something longer. Something with a melody in it, rising and falling, patient as roots, sweet as an old woman's voice singing a forbidden song to a child who wasn't supposed to be listening.

I knew the song. My grandmother had sung it to me before I could walk, in the language before kingdoms, and I had forgotten it for fifty years, and now it came back to me whole, the way the river gives things back.

It was a lullaby. It had always been a lullaby.

And it was missing its ending — and the shard sang right up to the edge of the missing part, and paused there, and waited, the way a story waits, the way a door waits, the way a grandmother waits at a window for someone she loves to finally, finally come home.
