## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The rain came down like a debt collector on inventory night, and Gideon Winterbourne was counting other people's sins by candlelight when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. The kind a child makes when she has been taught that large knocks get doors opened faster, and has decided against it.

Gideon set down her pen. The Guild of Impossible Things kept its counting house in a converted lighthouse at the crooked end of Saltmere Street, and on the last night of the fiscal year the guildmaster locked the doors against exactly this sort of thing—petitioners, beggars, miracles. *No new impossibilities after dusk,* read the sign, in letters that had been repainted so many times they stood out from the wood like scar tissue.

She went to the door anyway. Nine years of exile had taught her that rules were mostly just fear wearing a uniform, and she had never once managed to be afraid of the right things.

The girl on the step could not have been more than ten. Rain sheeted off her in ropes; she was soaked to the skin and shivering with the deep, mechanical shiver of someone who had been cold for hours, not minutes. No coat. No shoes worth the name. Her hair was dark and plastered flat, and her eyes—

Her eyes were wrong for a child. Old the way wells are old. You look down into them expecting your own reflection and find instead the sense of depth, of water far below, of something that fell in once and never came back up.

"Guild's closed," Gideon said. "Come back at—"

The girl spoke.

Not common tongue. Not the harbor pidgin, not the high speech of the merchant houses, none of the languages Gideon had catalogued in nine years of ledgers. This was older. The vowels came from somewhere behind the teeth, resonant, each word shaped like it was being *kept* rather than said—a language built for contracts, for binding, for the moment when a soul is weighed against a price.

Gideon knew it because she had been forbidden to learn it.

Her grandmother had taught her in secret, in the years before everything, tracing characters in spilled tea on the tabletop: *This is the First Tongue, little storm. This is what they speak beneath the mountain. Never let them hear you know it.* Gideon had been seven. She had thought it a game. She had not understood until much later that her grandmother had been preparing an escape route, or a warning, or both.

The girl was still talking. Gideon caught fragments—*owed*, *come*, *the seal*, *solstice*—words she knew the way you know a face across a crowded room.

"You're speaking the First Tongue," Gideon said slowly. "Mute children don't usually—" She stopped. The girl's mouth had kept moving through all of it, but no sound had come out. Not one syllable. Gideon had *read* the words off her lips, translated them through a dead woman's lessons, and only now realized the street behind the child was utterly silent except for rain.

The girl touched her own throat. Shook her head. Then pointed past Gideon, into the counting house, at the great iron-bound ledger that lay open on the desk—the Ledger of Small Debts, the guild's book of minor obligations, favors owed and favors rendered, the small change of impossible commerce.

"No," Gideon said. "Absolutely not. That book is—"

The girl walked past her. Children are good at that, at simply declining to participate in an adult's refusal. She crossed the counting house floor trailing puddles, climbed onto Gideon's own chair like she owned it, and laid one small wet hand flat on the open page.

The ink moved.

Gideon had seen a lot in nine years among the Impossible Things. She had watched a man pay a debt in years instead of coin. She had catalogued a mirror that remembered forward. But she had never seen a ledger rewrite itself, and her first instinct—trained into her by the order that had cast her out—was to grab the girl's wrist and pull.

She didn't. Later, she would be glad. Because the entries were rearranging themselves around the girl's hand the way iron filings arrange around a lodestone, and what they were forming, line by line, in Gideon's own careful bookkeeper's hand, was a single entry:

*Received: one (1) soul, held as collateral. Debtor: Winterbourne. Creditor: the Court Beneath. Terms: payment due at solstice sealing.*

Below it, in ink that was still drying, a name wrote itself: **LENA**.

The candle guttered. Somewhere upstairs, the guildmaster's dog began to howl.

* * *

Gideon did the arithmetic of survival quickly. The guildmaster slept light and woke meaner. She doused the candle, wrapped the girl—Lena, the name fit her like a borrowed coat—in the spare cloak from the hook, and carried her down the spiral stair to the archive cellar, where the guild kept its oldest records and where, crucially, the floorboards above did not carry sound.

Lena did not struggle. She sat on a crate of unfiled miracles and watched Gideon with those well-deep eyes while Gideon lit a stub of tallow candle and went hunting through the W drawer.

Winterbourne. Her family. Nine years since she'd spoken the name aloud, and here it was in the guild's records, because the guild recorded everything, especially the things people paid it to forget.

The file was thin. That was the first bad sign. Important debts have thick files; thin files mean someone wanted the paper scarce. Inside: a single contract, copied in a clerk's hand from an original held elsewhere, dated two hundred and eleven years ago.

*Be it known that the House of Winterbourne, being sore pressed, does sell unto the Court Beneath the Mountain one soul, to be named at the creditor's pleasure, as collateral against sums advanced. The debtor's line shall remain enforceable so long as any Winterbourne lives.*

Any Winterbourne lives.

Gideon sat down on the cold stone floor without quite meaning to. The candle flame leaned. Above her, muffled, the dog had stopped howling, which meant the guildmaster was awake, which meant she had perhaps ten minutes.

Two hundred years. Her family had sold a soul to the thing under the mountain before her grandmother's grandmother was born, and the debt had sat there, patient as geology, waiting for the creditor to choose a name. And the court had chosen—or the debt had chosen—and a mute child had walked nine miles through a storm to the one door in the city where someone might recognize what she was.

Or had she been sent? Was Lena the collection agent, the trap, the engineered circumstance designed to put a Winterbourne's hands on a forbidden contract?

It didn't matter. That was the terrible discovery Gideon made sitting on the cellar floor with the paper trembling in her fingers: it didn't matter. Whatever Lena was—collateral, bait, victim, all three—the girl was ten years old and rain-soaked and looking at Gideon right now with an expression that needed no translation in any tongue.

*You will not give me back.*

"No," Gideon said aloud, in common tongue, then again in the First Tongue, the words rusty and strange in her mouth: *"No. I won't."*

Lena's eyes went wide. She reached out and took Gideon's hand—not the way children take hands, tentatively, asking. She took it the way you take an oath.

Then she pointed at the contract copy, and at the words *the original held elsewhere*, and made a gesture Gideon understood across two hundred years of family silence: *burn*.

Above them, the cellar door opened, and the guildmaster's voice came down the stairs like a dropped anvil.

"Winterbourne. Why is there an entry in my ledger that wasn't there at dusk?"

Gideon closed the file. She looked at Lena—at the debt come due, the soul her blood had sold, the small fierce gravity now sitting on a crate in her cellar—and felt the dread in her chest do something unexpected. It turned over. It rooted. It became something with teeth.

She stood up, and started up the stairs to lie to the most dangerous bookkeeper in the city.

"We need to talk about passage," she called back, "down the mountain road. Before solstice."
