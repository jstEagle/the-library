## 2. What the Mountain Owes

The guildmaster's office smelled of tallow and old paper, which was to say it smelled like every room Gideon had ever tried to disappear into.

"You want a descent permit." Master Quillon did not look up from his abacus. His beads clicked like teeth. "To the Undercourt. During sealing season."

"I want a descent permit, a guide who's been past the Second Gate, and the guild's seal on the passage writ so the toll-wardens don't eat us at the border of the dark."

"Bold inventory clerk we've hired."

"I keep your ledgers balanced to the copper, Quillon. I know exactly what this guild can afford."

That made him look up. He was a narrow man, all elbows and suspicion, with ink stains on his knuckles like old bruises. Behind him, rain still hammered the windows—three days of it now, since Lena had come through the door.

"She's in the scriptorium," Quillon said. "Rewriting things."

"I know. I've seen."

"A ledger that rewrites itself around a child is either a miracle or a liability, Winterbourne. The Guild of Impossible Things has survived four hundred years by treating both identically: as an entry to be resolved."

Gideon kept her hands flat on the desk. Steady. She'd learned that trick from her grandmother, who could hold lightning in her palm without flinching, and who had sold a child's soul to the dark under the mountain while smiling about it. "Then resolve me an entry. One descent. If I don't come back, write me off as a bad debt. You're good at that."

Something flickered across Quillon's face—not quite guilt. Guildmasters didn't have guilt; they had accounting. "There's another matter," he said. "A visitor. Arrived this morning, asking after you by your old name."

The bottom dropped out of Gideon's stomach. "What old name?"

"The storm one."

* * *

Lena sat cross-legged on the floor of the scriptorium, surrounded by open ledgers like a small island in a sea of paper. She wasn't touching them anymore. She didn't need to. The entries were moving on their own now, ink crawling toward her like iron filings toward a lodestone.

She looked up when Gideon entered, and her mouth opened, and what came out was not silence.

*Water-remember. Door-of-below. The-one-who-carries-me walks where I once walked. She does not know she is walking in my footprints.*

Gideon went cold from the scalp down. Not because Lena was speaking—the girl hadn't stopped speaking First Tongue since she arrived—but because the voice behind the words was wrong. Too deep. Too old. A woman's cadence wearing a child's mouth.

"Lena." Gideon crouched, slow, the way you approach something wild. "Who's talking?"

The girl blinked. Her own face came back—nine years old, chapped lips, eyes the gray of rain over slate—and she pressed her hand flat against her own chest, then pointed down. Through the floor. Through the stone. Through however many miles of mountain root lay between them and the court beneath.

*She is not talking,* said the other voice, gentle and terrible. *I am leaking.*

Then Lena shuddered, and the deep voice guttered out, and the girl was alone in her body again, breathing hard, staring at Gideon with an expression no nine-year-old should have to wear: the look of someone sharing a house with a stranger.

"It's getting louder," Gideon said quietly. It wasn't a question.

Lena nodded. Then she crawled to the nearest ledger—the guild's debt registry, the one Gideon herself maintained—and touched the page. Ink moved. New lines wrote themselves in a spidery antique hand:

*Winterbourne line: one (1) soul, held in trust. Payment due at solstice sealing. Collateral currently ambulatory. Do not damage collateral. Damage voids contract.*

"Do not damage collateral," Gideon read aloud, and tasted copper. Nine years ago she'd refused to weaponize a child for her order, and they'd cast her out for it. Now here was a child already weaponized—already spent, centuries before her birth, by Gideon's own blood. "They bound someone into you. Someone who died owing."

Lena tilted her head. Then, slowly, she raised her hands and began to shape words in the air, the way the First Tongue was meant to be spoken—not sound but gesture, meaning made visible:

*I dream her life. A baker's daughter. A wedding. A fever. They came with papers and she signed because her family was starving and the Undercourt pays debts in bread. She thought she was selling her death. She did not know they would keep her waiting.*

Gideon closed her eyes. "Waiting for what?"

Lena's hands hesitated. Then:

*For a Winterbourne to come collect.*

* * *

The rival was waiting in the guild's back stairwell, which figured. Vespera Thorne had always had a talent for being exactly where Gideon least wanted her.

Nine years hadn't softened her. Vespera still dressed like a knife pretending to be a woman—all black silk and silver pins, hair cropped short, a guild badge on her collar that Gideon knew for a fact she'd forged. They'd come up together in the counting-houses, competing for the same commissions, until Vespera had sold Gideon's route-map to a slaver consortium and called it business.

"Winterbourne." Vespera smiled with every tooth. "Heard you finally found trouble worth keeping."

"Thorne. Heard you finally got a badge. Stolen?"

"Found." She pushed off the wall, circling, the way she did when she was pricing something. "You're going down the mountain. Everyone's saying it. Sealing season, solo descent, no guide, no writ. You'll die in the Second Gate like everyone else who tries it without me."

"I'd rather die unaccompanied."

"Mm. But you won't die. You'll do worse—you'll fail, and the girl gets collected anyway, and you get to live with that forever. Which is why you need me. I've been past the Second Gate. Twice. I know which corridors lie and which doors take bribes." She stopped, close enough that Gideon could smell the ozone on her—storm-caller residue, faint but unmistakable. Vespera had dealings with the order. Of course she did. "One price."

"There it is."

"Your sigil."

Gideon's hand went to her collarbone without permission. Beneath the skin there, sunk deep as a splinter of God, sat the storm-mark: the coiled sigil every caller was branded with at initiation, the thing that let her pull lightning out of clear sky. It was the last piece of the life she'd lost. Even exiled, even stripped of rank, the sigil stayed. It was hers. It was the only proof that what she'd done—refusing them, protecting that child nine years ago—had cost her something real.

"No," Gideon said.

Vespera shrugged, turned to go.

"Wait." The word came out of Gideon like a tooth. She stood in the stairwell and felt the rain hammering the roof above them, felt the storm answering something in her blood, felt the sigil warm against her bone like a second heartbeat. Without it, she couldn't call lightning. And she was going to need lightning, down there, to burn a contract older than kingdoms—

No. That was the trap. That was the exact arithmetic the Undercourt wanted: keep the sigil, keep the tie to the order, keep the bloodline enforceable. The contract held *because* she was still a Winterbourne caller. Cut the thread, and the paper might burn.

Or might not. There was no way to know except to pay.

"How," Gideon said, "do you propose to take it?"

Vespera's smile went soft and almost kind, which was worse than the teeth. "The old way. We do it tonight, before a witness, with salt and consent. You speak the renunciation. I cut the anchor. It will feel like drowning." She paused. "It will feel like the order, actually. Same thing."

Gideon laughed despite everything—a short, cracked sound. "You've been waiting nine years to say that."

"Seven. I'm not patient, I'm thorough." Vespera produced a knife with a silver blade and a handle wrapped in storm-gray cord, and laid it on the stair between them like a card face-down. "Midnight. Bring the girl if you want. Children should learn what things cost."

When she was gone, Gideon stood alone in the stairwell a long time, listening to the rain.

Then she went to find Lena.

* * *

The girl was asleep in the scriptorium, curled among the ledgers like a comma in a sentence. When Gideon sat down beside her, Lena woke instantly—no child slept deeply anymore, not with a tenant in her soul—and watched Gideon's face with those slate-gray eyes.

Gideon took the girl's small hand and shaped the First Tongue against her palm, gesture by gesture, so there would be no mistake:

*Tonight, I give away my thunder. Tomorrow, I spend what's left of me buying you back. Understand?*

Lena's chin trembled. Then she answered, and her hands shook, and the translation rose off her fingers like heat:

*Why? You never met the baker's daughter. You owe her nothing.*

Gideon thought about it honestly, because the girl deserved honesty. About her grandmother's smiling ledgers. About the child she'd refused to burn, nine years ago, and the door that had closed behind her after. About a mute girl standing in the rain with a dead woman's soul stitched under her ribs.

*Because someone has to be the first Winterbourne who pays instead of collects,* Gideon signed. *Might as well be me.*

Lena stared at her. Then she did something she had never done in three days at the guild: she leaned forward and pressed her forehead against Gideon's shoulder, and stayed there, and the ledgers around them went quiet, every entry settling flat and still, as if the whole building were holding its breath.

At midnight, in the cellar, before salt and witness, Gideon Winterbourne spoke the renunciation in a steady voice while Vespera's knife found the anchor beneath her skin.

It felt like drowning. It felt like the order. It felt, worst of all, like freedom—

and as the sigil died, somewhere far above them, the storm stopped mid-raindrop,

and far below, in the court beneath the mountain, something ancient opened its ledger

and wrote, in fresh ink: *The collateral approaches.*
