## 3. The Court Beneath

The stairs down took three days.

Not because they were long—though they were, spiraling into the mountain's wet black throat like the inside of a shell—but because the Undercourt did not want them. Every hundred steps, the dark pressed back with a weight that had nothing to do with air. Gideon counted her heartbeats to keep from counting her regrets. Lena's small hand stayed fisted in her coat hem the whole way, and once, in a voice like cracked bells, the girl said: *the water remembers you.*

Gideon chose not to ask what it remembered.

They came out at last into a cavern so vast the ceiling was a rumor. Stalactites hung like the fingers of drowned giants, and between them drifted lamps without flames—cold blue lights that moved when you weren't watching and froze when you were. The floor was polished obsidian, and in it, reflected, Gideon saw not herself but every version of herself she had refused to become: the storm-caller in full regalia, the executioner, the woman who had said yes.

"Keep your eyes on me," she told Lena. "Only me."

The court received them the way a wound receives a needle.

* * *

The Archivist of Debts stood at the center of the cavern behind a desk of black glass taller than two men, and it had no face.

It had faces. Hundreds. A surface like hammered mirror, and in each fragment a different reflection waited—some hers, some not, some things she suspected had never been human at all. When it spoke, it spoke with her grandmother's voice, warm and dry as autumn paper, and that was the cruelest thing in the whole cold room.

"Gideon Winterbourne." The mirror-face tilted. "Nine years, four months, eleven days since you last spoke an oath aloud. We kept count. We keep count of everything."

"I've come for the contract," Gideon said. Her voice held. She'd have thanked the old training for that if the old training hadn't been part of the trap. "The Winterbourne debt. I'm here to settle it."

"There is no settling." The Archivist turned one mirrored hand palm-up, and above it coalesced a scroll so old its edges smoked faintly, inked in the First Tongue, sealed with a sigil Gideon knew better than her own heartbeat—the storm-crown of her order, pressed in wax mixed with blood. "There is only continuation. Your family understood this. Your grandmother understood this best of all."

"You're wearing her voice," Gideon said. "That's obscene."

"She sold it to us. Along with everything else." The mirrors rippled, amused. "Would you like to know what you actually purchased with your exile, child? Shall I read you the ledger?"

It didn't wait for permission. It never would have.

*"Item,"* it recited, grandmother-voiced, gentle as a bedtime story. *"One storm-caller, second rank, refusing lawful sanction. Exiled by order of the Conclave—on petition of the Undercourt. Item: exile maintained indefinitely, per terms of the Winterbourne instrument, which requires the debtor line be severed from protection, from standing, from recourse. An enforceable soul must be an unclaimed soul. You made her unclaimed, Gideon. You did our work beautifully."*

The floor tilted. Or Gideon did. She couldn't tell.

"They let me go," she said slowly. "They didn't cast me out. They—"

"They filed you," said the Archivist. "You were paperwork before you were a person. The girl is merely the final page." Its mirror-face bent toward Lena, and every reflection in it showed the same thing: a small figure wrapped in borrowed light, souls circling her like moths. "Come here, payment. The solstice seal is nearly due."

Lena stepped forward—and Gideon caught her arm.

"No."

The word came out as thunder wants to. Not loud. Inevitable.

* * *

That was when the guards came.

They poured from the dark at the cavern's rim—things in the shapes of soldiers, armored in slagged stone, carrying blades of frozen sound. A dozen. Two dozen. And ahead of them, descending the stair with rain still dripping off her cloak though no rain had fallen for three days underground, came Sera Vane.

Storm-caller enforcer. First rank. The woman Gideon had loved with the whole reckless violence of being twenty-two, and left without goodbye because goodbye would have meant explaining, and explaining would have meant Lena dying in a cell while the Conclave debated whether collateral had rights.

Sera looked older. Harder at the mouth. Her sigil burned silver at her throat, and her eyes found Gideon across the obsidian floor with an expression Gideon had rehearsed nine years of defenses against.

"Gideon Winterbourne," Sera said, formal, terrible. "By authority of the Conclave, you're coming home."

"Sera." Gideon put herself between Sera and Lena anyway. Old habit. Only habit. "Whatever they promised you—"

"They promised me nothing." Sera's jaw worked. "They told me where you'd be. That's all I needed." Then she turned—turned away from Gideon, away from the Archivist—and raised both hands, and the cavern filled with the smell of ozone.

Lightning crawled up the walls. Not at Gideon. At the guards.

"Hold the line," Sera said to the dark, in the flat voice of someone reading their own death warrant, "against me, then."

The first guard broke apart mid-stride.

What followed was not a battle; battles have tactics. This was a storm given a body and a grudge, Sera Vane standing alone on the obsidian with lightning sheeting off her shoulders, holding twenty things of frozen sound at bay because the woman she'd loved for eleven silent years had finally, finally come within reach and was doing something unforgivably brave. Gideon wanted to scream at her to stop, to run, to live. Instead she grabbed Lena's hand and ran toward the desk of black glass.

"The contract," Lena said—in common tongue, first time, cracked and urgent. "Burn it. *Burn it.*"

Gideon reached across the desk. The scroll fought her, wax seal biting her fingers like teeth, the First Tongue writhing off the page into her skin. And the Archivist's mirrors all turned to her at once, hundreds of reflections, and in every single one her grandmother smiled.

"You cannot burn what owns you," it said.

"I know," Gideon said. "That's why I stole this first."

She opened her fist. Inside it: the shard of sigil-stone she'd surrendered to the rival in the guild, bought back at a price she hadn't yet paid—a debt deferred, a favor owed, a storm-calling sigil surrendered forever. But the stone still held one stolen bolt. One. The last lightning she would ever call.

She thought of a rain-soaked child at a guild door. Of ledgers rewriting themselves around a name. Of Sera, bleeding light, holding the world off with her bare hands.

"For the record," Gideon said to the mirror-faced thing wearing her dead grandmother's voice, "I keep count too."

She drove the stone into the seal.

The lightning went through the contract like dawn through fog. The scroll screamed in the First Tongue—every soul bound inside it screaming with it, a chord of centuries—and then the wax shattered, the ink fled the page, and the seal broke with a sound like a mountain exhaling after nine hundred years of holding its breath.

The shockwave threw Gideon across the obsidian. The guards fell as one, slag armor sloughing into ordinary rock. The Archivist stood amid its collapsing mirrors, face fracturing into a thousand fleeing reflections, and the last thing it said—in no voice at all now, in static, in the sound of a ledger closing—was:

*"The debt transfers. Someone always inherits—"*

Then the lamps went out.

* * *

In the sudden honest dark, Gideon crawled toward the sound of breathing.

Found Lena first—whole, unbound, the circling souls around her rising off her skin like sparks off a fire, free, streaming upward through the stalactites toward a sky none of them would ever see but all of them trusted. Found Sera second, on her knees, scorched and laughing or crying, impossible to tell.

"You idiot," Sera whispered, gripping Gideon's collar. "You absolute—you could have come home. They'd have lifted it. All you had to do was—"

"That's why I didn't," Gideon said.

Above them, far above, through miles of stone, the solstice sun was rising. And in the dark, Lena took Gideon's hand and pressed something into it: a scrap of the burned contract's edge, one word surviving the fire in the First Tongue, glowing faintly.

Lena pointed at the word. Then at Gideon.

Gideon read it, and her blood went cold.

It was her own name.
