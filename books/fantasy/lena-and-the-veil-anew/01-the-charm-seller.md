## 1. The Charm Seller

The trick to selling luck in a city that has outlawed it is to make the customer believe they're buying nothing at all.

I learned that from my grandmother, who could have taught a masterclass in deception if masterclasses hadn't also been outlawed. *Never sell them magic, Paloma. Sell them confidence with a knot in it.* She'd say it while braiding charms out of river-grass and fishbone, her fingers moving with the absent precision of a woman who had once bound storms and now settled for binding headaches.

So when Brammel Ostwick — no relation to anyone important, dockhand, forty years of hauling rope, shoulders like a ship's anchor chain — stopped at my stall in the Saltmarket that grey morning, I did what I always do. I became a fraud.

"Morning, charm-seller." He said it the way everyone does, half greeting, half accusation, as though the word *charm-seller* were a legal category rather than an insult. Which, in Corvane, it technically was. Category Three Unlicensed Practitioner: fines, stocks, or the Bell Gate posting depending on your luck.

"Brammel." I nodded at his hands. "You've been working the *Merrow's Debt* again."

"How'd you know?"

"You smell like bilge and despair. Only one ship in this harbor produces both."

He laughed, which was the point. A laughing customer buys twice as much and asks half as many questions. I let him laugh while my hand drifted across the stall's meager inventory: knotted cords for safe childbirth, dried rowan sprigs for fever, little tin fish that were supposed to keep nets full. All of it harmless. All of it, by the letter of the Veil Edicts, criminal. And all of it, by the honest truth of my hands, fake — or close enough to fake that no witch-hunter would bother sharpening a knife over it.

"What have you got for luck?" he asked. "Real luck. Not the fish thing. The fish thing's rubbish, my wife says so."

"The fish thing works if you believe in fish."

"I believe in eating them."

"Then you're halfway saved already." I reached under the counter and brought out the piece I'd been saving. A cord, grey-blue, braided from harbor-grass and something else, tied off with seven knots. To Brammel it looked like every other cord on my table. What he couldn't see — what nobody could see, not even me, most days, because I kept it folded down small and quiet like a coin palmed in a closed fist — was the thread of true work running through it. A whisper of binding. A suggestion, really. Not enough to light a candle. Just enough to tip a scale.

Enough that when the *Merrow's Debt* took water next month, the leak would find the pump before it found the hold.

"Seven knots," I said, turning it in the light. "One for each day of the week. You wear it under your shirt where the foreman can't see it, and you don't untie it, and you don't tell anyone what you paid for it."

"How much?"

"For you? Six coppers."

"That's robbery."

"It's insurance. Robbery is when I take your money and give you nothing. This way at least one of us sleeps well."

He grumbled, counted coppers onto the cloth with the ceremony of a man paying off a debt to the crown itself, and took the cord like it might bite him. Then, because dockworkers are sentimental in the way stones are heavy, he paused.

"My girl starts at the ropewalk Monday," he said. "First work she's ever had. You got anything for that?"

I should have said no. I should have said the market watch was circling, that two stalls down old Petter had been fined a month's earnings last week for selling a *fever sachet*, a sachet, which contained thyme and optimism and nothing else. I should have been the careful, tired, invisible man I'd spent eleven years learning to be.

Instead I heard myself say, "Bring her by Sunday evening. After the bells. There's a knot for beginners."

Brammel grinned, pocketed his luck, and went off toward the harbor smelling slightly less of despair than before.

And I stood there in the cold, behind my table of lawful-ish nonsense, feeling the small warm coal of my gift settle back down into hiding, and thought: *that was stupid, Paloma. That was exactly the kind of stupid that gets people hanged.*

But the rope-girl would come Sunday. And I would tie her a knot. Because that's the other thing my grandmother taught me, the thing underneath the survival lessons, the thing I pretend isn't there so it doesn't hurt so much:

*We are Blackwoods. We do not stop working just because the world made the work illegal.*

* * *

Corvane in the morning is a city pretending it didn't used to be beautiful.

You can see it if you know where to look, and I always know where to look. The guildhouse quarter, shuttered and tax-rotted, its lintels still carved with sigils the masons weren't allowed to chisel off because the chiseling itself would have required admitting the sigils meant something. The fountain in Tanner's Square that runs dry every winter because the warming-writ that used to keep the pipes from freezing died with the last licensed hydromancer, and the Council decided pipes were cheaper than pardons. The way people walk past the old academy without looking up, the way you don't look at a grave you helped dig.

Eleven years ago, when I was nine, the Veil War ended. Nobody talks about how. The histories say the Crown prevailed, magic was bound, peace was restored. The histories are written by the same office that posts the edicts, so draw your own conclusions about their accuracy.

What I know is this: after the war, the gifted didn't disappear. We just learned to be furniture. The laundress on Veil Street who's never once shrunk a wool coat in twenty years. The midwife in Lowmarsh whose babies don't cry until they're safely delivered. The chandler whose candles burn exactly evenly, every wick, every time, which sounds like craftsmanship right up until you notice it's impossible.

We're everywhere. That's the secret nobody says out loud, because saying it out loud is how the hunts start. And we are all of us so tired. Tiredness is the tax we pay on existing. Every charm I sell, I sell with one ear cocked for boots on cobblestone. Every stranger who stops at my stall gets sorted in the first three seconds: customer, informer, or hunter. Customers haggle. Informers ask too many specific questions. Hunters don't ask questions at all; they just look at your hands.

My hands, for the record, are very good at looking stupid.

I closed the stall at noon, as the law requires — unlicensed trade must conclude by midday, another rule designed to make poverty inconvenient — and carried my unsold stock home through streets that smelled of coal smoke and low tide. Home is a room above a cooper's shop in Lowmarsh, twelve feet by fourteen, rent owed weekly, window facing a wall. It contains a bed, a stool, a kettle, and a locked box under the floorboard that contains everything I actually am.

I didn't open the box. I never open the box before dark. Daylight is for being nobody.

Instead I sat on the stool, boiled water I couldn't afford to flavor, and ate bread that had opinions about its own staleness. Through the wall I could hear the cooper's youngest practicing the fiddle badly, and I found myself, as I do most days, listening to it the way other men listen to music — with gratitude. Bad fiddle means ordinary life. Ordinary life means nobody's burning anything today.

It was around then that the boy from the third floor knocked. Tam, eight years old, gap-toothed, sent by his mother with two eggs and a question.

"Mum wants to know if you've got anything for the baby's coughing."

"Tell her honey and onion, steeped overnight."

"She tried that."

"Tell her to try it again and hum while she stirs. Humming helps."

That part wasn't even a lie. Humming helps. Not because of any gift in it — because a mother humming over a sick child is doing the oldest magic there is, the kind that predates guilds and edicts and witch-hunters, the kind nobody's managed to outlaw yet because it lives in too many kitchens. My grandmother believed that was the real reason the Crown feared the guilds. Not the fire-calling and storm-binding. The kitchens. You can arrest a mage. You can't arrest a thousand mothers humming.

Tam took his eggs' worth of wisdom and left, and I sat with my cold tea and felt the loneliness arrive on schedule, the way it does every afternoon between the market and the dark. It comes quietly. It doesn't announce itself. It just sits down across from you like an old friend who knows you too well to knock.

Here is the arithmetic of my life: I have a gift strong enough that the elders of my grandmother's guild would have called it promising. I use perhaps a hundredth of it, folded into knots and whispers, sold for coppers to people who think they're buying superstition. I am, by training and blood, the heir to one of the great working traditions of Corvane — and by law, I am a Category Three Unlicensed Practitioner who sells tin fish.

And there is no one — no one — I can tell.

Not the cooper, who thinks I'm a clerk between positions. Not Tam's mother, who thinks I'm odd but useful. Not Brammel, who thinks his luck came from grass and knots and my winning smile. Eleven years of folding myself smaller every year, and I've gotten so good at it that some mornings I wake up and have to check under the floorboard just to confirm there's still a me in here.

The tea went cold. The fiddle sawed on. Outside, the gulls argued over the harbor's leavings, and somewhere across the city the palace bells rang the hour, and I thought, as I think every day, *one more week. One more week of being nobody, and then maybe—*

Maybe what? That's the part I've never worked out. Maybe the edicts soften. Maybe the hunters retire. Maybe I die old and anonymous with my gift still folded in its box, unused, unlived, and they bury me under a stone that says PALOMA BLACKWOOD, SELLER OF TIN FISH, and that's the whole story.

I was washing the cup when I heard the drums.

* * *

You learn the sound of official misery fast in Corvane. Tax collectors come with a clerk and a yawn. Press-gangs come with ropes and shouting. But the proclamation drums — those come with silence. The whole street stops talking at once, the way a flock of birds goes quiet before weather, and everyone pretends to keep working while listening with their entire spine.

Two drummers and a herald came up Cooper's Row from the direction of the Bell Gate. Behind them walked four men in grey — not soldiers, hunters. You can tell hunters by the grey. Soldiers wear the crown's blue and look bored; hunters wear grey and look patient, which is infinitely worse. They moved down the row pinning broadsheets to doorposts with iron nails, and the cooper came out of his shop wiping his hands, and Tam's mother appeared in her doorway holding the baby, and nobody read the postings aloud because nobody wanted to be the first voice attached to the words.

I read them. Somebody has to.

BY ORDER OF THE CROWN: Let it be known that the period of grace granted to persons harboring unregistered gifts is ended. Henceforth, all persons possessed of unlicensed workings shall present themselves at the Hall of Registers within thirty days, to be assessed, listed, and bound to lawful service. Those failing to register will be deemed *concealed practitioners*, subject to seizure, trial, and such penalties as the law provides.

There was more. There's always more. Something about rewards for information, something about the duties of neighbors, something about how the Crown regrets the necessity. The Crown regrets things the way a knife regrets cutting.

I stood in the street with my empty cup still in my hand and read it twice, and around me the row did its careful pantomime of indifference. The cooper studied his doorway. Tam's mother studied her baby. Old Petter from the market, who lived two doors down, went very still in the way of a man doing rapid arithmetic with his own name.

Because here's what the broadsheet didn't say, and what everyone reading it understood anyway: *assessed, listed, and bound to lawful service* means the registers. And the registers mean lists. And lists, in a city with a Hollow Crown — though none of us called it that yet, none of us knew — lists are how you turn neighbors into informers and names into ash.

Thirty days. That was the message under the message. Thirty days to decide what you are: registered, or hunted.

The hunters finished nailing their notices and walked back down the row, unhurried, patient, grey. One of them — younger, sharp-faced, with the stillness of someone trained to watch rather than speak — let his gaze drag across the gathered street as he passed. Across the cooper. Across Tam's mother. Across me.

It caught on me for half a heartbeat. Half a heartbeat longer than the others.

Then he moved on, and the street exhaled all at once, and everyone began talking again in the bright, too-loud voices of people proving they have nothing to hide.

I went upstairs. I pried up the floorboard. I took out the box — plain wood, iron-bound, my grandmother's — and opened it for the first time in daylight, which tells you everything about how my hands were shaking.

Inside lay the things I'd carried out of the old guildhouse the night they sealed it: her journal, water-stained and dense with her cramped script. A ring of keys to doors that no longer existed. And a single pressed flower, rowan-blossom, eleven years brown, that I have never once been able to throw away.

I sat on the stool with the journal in my lap and didn't open it. Not yet. Opening it meant deciding something, and I wasn't ready to decide. Instead I listened to the bad fiddle through the wall, and the gulls, and the city going about its business of pretending, and I told myself the thing I tell myself every time the fear gets loud:

*You've survived worse. You're a Blackwood. Keep your head down, keep your knots small, keep being nobody.*

Down in the street, past my window that faces a wall, I heard boots. Several pairs. Moving with that patient hunter rhythm, unhurried, coming up Cooper's Row from the far end.

They stopped three doors down.

At old Petter's.

I heard the knock. I heard Petter's voice, high and reasonable, saying *of course, officers, of course, come in.* I heard Tam's mother pull her door shut, soft as a prayer. The fiddle through the wall stumbled and stopped.

They'd taken the fever-sachet man first. Of course they had. He'd been fined once already; his name was already in a book somewhere; the arithmetic of him had already been done. The grey men stayed inside Petter's rooms for less time than it takes to drink a pot of tea. When they came out, Petter came with them, coatless, hands bound in front of him with cord — cord, I noted distantly, of the exact weight and braid I sold by the spool — and they walked him down Cooper's Row toward the Bell Gate while the whole street watched through shutters and pretended not to.

Nobody shouted. Nobody followed. Nobody did anything, because doing something is how you end up walking beside him.

Including me. I stood at my window with my grandmother's box open at my feet and did nothing, and the doing-nothing felt exactly as heavy as it always does, which is to say like a second skeleton I carry under the first one.

That night I lit no lamp. I sat in the dark above the cooper's shop with the journal finally open on my knees, reading my grandmother's handwriting by the memory of light, and the first line under the front cover said:

*If you are reading this, Paloma, then the hiding has failed, and it is time you knew what we hid from.*

I turned the page.
