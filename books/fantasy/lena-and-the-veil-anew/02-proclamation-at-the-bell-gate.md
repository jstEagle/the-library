## 2. Proclamation at the Bell Gate

The edict went up at the Bell Gate on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were market days, the one morning of the week when Corvane pretended to be prosperous, when the fishwives shouted each other down over herring and the baker's boy ran hot loaves up Copperlane with his shirt untucked and his dignity somewhere behind him. If the crown had wanted its proclamation to arrive quietly, it would have posted it at dawn on a Sunday, when the only witnesses were drunks and priests, both of whom forget things professionally.

Instead they chose Tuesday. They wanted an audience.

I was three stalls down, selling luck-charms to a woman who believed her husband's gambling problem could be solved with knotted red thread, when the heralds came through the gate in their grey-and-gold livery. I heard them before I saw them—the crowd has a particular sound when authority arrives, a kind of collective inhale, like a house settling before something falls off a shelf.

"By order of His Majesty King Aldric," the first herald read, in a voice trained to carry over battlefields and probably used mostly for announcing tax schedules, "all persons possessing the gift of sorcery, hedge-craft, charm-work, or any art of the Veil shall present themselves for registration within fourteen days. Registration is mandatory. Registration is mercy."

He paused there, the way they'd rehearsed it, letting *mercy* sit in the air like a coin tossed into a well you couldn't see the bottom of.

"Those who fail to register will be hunted. Those who harbor the unregistered will be tried as accomplices. The Veil War is ended. The age of law has begun."

The crowd did what crowds do. It made a noise that wasn't quite a murmur and wasn't quite silence, the sound of two thousand people deciding simultaneously not to say what they were thinking. Somewhere near me a baby started crying and was shushed with real desperation, as though even an infant's opinion might be noted down.

The woman buying the luck-charm for her husband looked at me. Just looked. Her eyes went from my face to my hands—my hands, always the hands, because everyone knows charm-sellers work with their hands—and then back to my face, and she set the knotted thread down on my stall cloth very gently, the way you'd put down something that might be listening.

"I'll think about it," she said, and left without her change.

* * *

Let me tell you how registration works, or rather, how everyone in Corvane understood it would work, because no one had actually seen it done yet. You present yourself at the citadel. A hunter—a real one, not a clerk—examines you. They have instruments for this. Then your name goes into a book, and your gift goes into a ledger, and you are, from that day forward, a known quantity. A listed thing. An entry.

And here is the part nobody said out loud but everybody understood: books can be consulted. Lists can be sorted. When the harvest comes in thin, when the treasury needs filling or the king needs reassuring, someone opens the book and reads down the column of names, and the people whose names are written there discover that registration was never mercy. It was inventory.

My grandmother taught me that lesson before she died, though she taught it sideways, the way she taught everything, through proverbs and riddles and long silences that meant more than her words. *"A fish that lets itself be counted,"* she said once, mending a net in the doorway of the old guildhouse, *"has already agreed to be caught."* I'd been nine. I'd thought she was talking about fishing. She'd looked at me for a long moment with those black Blackwood eyes and then gone back to her mending, and it took me another twenty years and this exact Tuesday morning to understand she hadn't been talking about fish at all.

Fourteen days. That was the arithmetic of it. Fourteen days to decide whether to walk into the citadel and write my name in their book, or to run, or to hide so deep in the city's skin that no hunter could find me. And underneath all of it, the option I didn't let myself look at directly, the way you don't look directly at the sun or at grief: fight.

I packed up my stall early. Nobody bought anything else that morning. Luck-charms, it turns out, lose their market appeal when the whole city's luck goes bad at once.

* * *

Copperlane in the evening smells of tallow smoke and boiled cabbage and the river, which never quite forgives us for what we dump in it. My room sits above a chandler's shop at the lane's narrow throat, one window, one bed, one stove that works if you negotiate with it. The rent is honest and the landlady is not, and between those two facts I have built a life.

That evening I sat at my window with the shutter cracked and watched the neighborhood do what neighborhoods do after a proclamation: nothing, loudly. Old Vesna swept her step for the third time, sweeping nothing, sweeping the idea of dust. The Ferro brothers stood in their doorway speaking in the low rapid cadence of men deciding something. Down at the corner, the Widow Anselm lit a candle in her window—the old gesture, the guild gesture, the one that meant *a light burns here for the hidden*—and then, after a moment, blew it out again and drew her curtain. Even gestures had become dangerous by nightfall.

I should explain what I am, since the crown was so interested. Officially, I'm a charm-seller of the third-rate sort: knots and beads and dried herbs, sleight of hand dressed up as superstition, the kind of magic that works exactly as often as coincidence allows, which is to say often enough to keep customers and rarely enough to stay legal. Unofficially—unofficially, in a room with the shutters closed and salt at the sill—I am the last practicing heir of the Blackwood guild, and what I can do doesn't fit in a ledger. What I can do, the hunters would burn me for in the Gate Square while the herald read out my crimes in his battlefield voice.

So I sat in my dark room and did the arithmetic again. Fourteen days. One name. One choice.

And then I heard the boots.

You learn to hear them, in outlawed Corvane. Hunter patrols move differently than watchmen—watchmen shuffle, hunters *advance*, four abreast, heels striking in time, a rhythm designed to be heard, because half the hunt is fear and fear travels by ear. I counted them past my door: four sets, then a pause, then four more. Two squads. In Copperlane. At night.

Copperlane had never warranted two squads. Copperlane's crimes were unpaid rents and the occasional fistfight outside the Salt Cellar. Two squads meant they weren't patrolling. They were collecting.

I opened my window an inch. The night air came in cold and riverish, and with it, voices.

"—the boy, I said. The Aldous boy. Address was in the tip-book." A hunter's voice, flat as slate. "Third house past the cooper's."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the chandler's stock, through the earth itself.

Tam Aldous. The cooper's apprentice. Sixteen years old, maybe seventeen, all elbows and appetite, forever underfoot at the market, forever asking me questions about my charms with that hungry brightness kids get when they've caught the edge of something true. *How does it work, Master Paloma? Can you teach me? Is it like the stories?* And me, every time, giving him the same tired smile and the same tired lie: *It's knots and nonsense, lad. Buy one for your sweetheart.*

Because I'd seen it in him. Of course I'd seen it. A flicker when he handled my stock, a warmth in his palms that had nothing to do with the stove, the little unconscious way objects behaved around him—tools finding his hand, dropped things landing soft. The boy had the gift, raw and untrained and glowing out of him like a lamp through a curtain, and I had seen it and said nothing and sold him a love-charm and sent him home.

I told myself I'd protected him. I told myself ignorance was armor, that if Tam didn't know what he was, he couldn't be what he was. I told myself a great many things, sitting at that window with my heart going like a hammer in a bell-tower, while below me the boots stopped at the cooper's door.

* * *

They didn't knock quietly. Hunters never do; the noise is the message, and the message is for the whole street.

"Open in the name of the king!"

Light bloomed in the cooper's windows. Shouting—Mistress Aldous, her voice cracking up through its registers, the specific fury of a mother who knows exactly what's at the door and has decided to meet it standing. The cooper himself, big Renn Aldous, whose shoulders could bend iron hoops, saying something low and level that I couldn't catch, the tone of a man negotiating with weather.

Then the door gave—it sounded like it was opened rather than broken, which somehow made it worse—and the street filled with lamplight and shadow and the small, sharp geometry of a family coming apart.

I saw Tam before they brought him out. He was at the upstairs window, his mother gripping his arm, and even across the lane and through the dark I could see him *glowing*. Not visibly—not the way ballads describe it—but I could feel it from where I crouched, my gift reading his the way a tuning fork answers a struck note: terror ringing through raw power, the boy's gift flaring wild because he was frightened and no one had ever taught him the first thing about holding it. A cup on the cooper's windowsill lifted an inch into the air and hung there, trembling, until his mother pulled him back from the glass.

"That's evidence," said the slate voice below, satisfied. "Mark it down."

They brought him out the front door with his hands bound in front of him—bound in *cold-iron thread*, I realized, my stomach turning, the stuff weavers won't touch, the stuff that deadens the gift like a hand pressed over a mouth. Tam wasn't fighting. That was the worst of it. He was looking around at the street, at the doors cracking open, at the faces in the windows, and he was looking for help, the way a sixteen-year-old looks, the way someone looks who still believes the world is fundamentally on his side.

His eyes found my window.

I don't know what he saw. Darkness, presumably. A shutter cracked an inch. A neighbor's curiosity, nothing more. But I saw his face change—saw the belief go out of it, replaced by something older and colder, the expression of a boy learning in one evening what I'd spent thirty years learning by inches: that the world is not on his side, that the faces in the windows belong to survivors, and that survivors watch.

"Master Paloma," he said. Not loudly. Just clearly, across the lane, in the sudden quiet. "Master Paloma, tell them. Tell them what I am. Tell them I didn't mean anything."

Every eye on Copperlane turned toward my window.

I want to record, for whatever record survives, that in that moment I did not think about survival. I thought about moving—about flinging the shutter wide, about the working I could do with the salt in my sill and the fire in the chandler's stock below and the rage sitting in my chest like a second heart. I felt the power answer me, rise in me, the old Blackwood inheritance singing in my blood like a struck bell, and for one full second I was already halfway to the window.

Then I thought about Maren Ostwick's voice, calm as deep water: *One mage dies for every spectacle, and the spectacle buys nothing.* I thought about my grandmother's journal buried in the guildhouse cellar, about keys I didn't yet understand and a truth I hadn't yet confirmed, about the fact that dying gloriously on Copperlane would save Tam Aldous exactly nothing and would leave the Hollow Crown—I didn't know that name yet, not then, I knew only the shape of the wrongness wearing the throne—one enemy fewer.

I thought all of that in perhaps a heartbeat. And then I did the unforgivable, sensible thing.

I closed the shutter.

From outside, faintly, I heard Tam laugh. One short note, no humor in it, the laugh of someone crossing a line he can't come back over. Then the boots resumed, four abreast, advancing away down Copperlane with their cargo, and Mistress Aldous's voice followed them the whole length of the lane, calling her son's name like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing, until the night swallowed even that.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat on the floor with my back against the bed and the salt circle redrawn around me twice, which was pointless—no hunter was coming for me that night, I was nobody, I was a charm-seller, I was invisible—and held my grandmother's signet ring in my fist hard enough to leave its mark in my palm.

Here is what I understood, sitting in the dark while Copperlane grieved quietly around me: the invisibility was over. Not mine specifically—mine might hold another week, another month, if I was careful and lucky and ruthless. But the *era* of it. For thirty years the gifted of Corvane had survived by being small, by being deniable, by knots-and-nonsense and lowered eyes and candles lit and blown out again. The crown had tolerated us the way a man tolerates mice in the wall: beneath notice, beneath concern. Tonight the boots had come to Copperlane for a cooper's son who'd never cast so much as a cantrip, and tomorrow they would come somewhere else, and the day after somewhere else again, and the list they were building would grow longer and the net would draw tighter, and every one of us who stayed hidden would be hiding alone, each in our separate dark, waiting our turn.

Registration or the hunt. The book or the rope. Those were the choices the grey-and-gold men offered, and both roads led into their hands.

Which meant there was a third road, the one I'd spent my whole life refusing to look at directly. The road that ran through my grandmother's buried cache, through whatever she'd hidden beneath the rotting floor of the old guildhouse, through the truth she'd died rather than speak aloud—the truth Serah had called madness, the truth that had split our family down the middle like a felled tree. *If they ever post the lists,* she'd told me once, near the end, her hands shaking too badly to braid her own hair, *if they ever start counting us, Paloma, then dig. Promise me. Dig up what I buried and finish what I couldn't.*

I'd promised. Promises made to the dying are easy; they're made to someone who won't collect.

She was collecting now. From beyond the Veil, from wherever the dead keep their accounts, my grandmother was calling in a debt I'd signed as a grieving boy with no idea what I was agreeing to.

Toward dawn, when the sky over the rooftops went the grey of old ash, I got up, dressed, and took down from behind the loose brick the few things worth carrying: the signet ring, a knife, and the little iron key to the guildhouse cellar that I'd worn against my skin for years without ever once using it, the way you wear a question you're afraid to ask.

Below in the lane, Mistress Aldous stood on her doorstep in yesterday's clothes, staring down the empty street where the boots had gone. Nobody had come to comfort her. Nobody would. Comfort, like luck-charms, had lost its market overnight.

I pulled my hood up, slipped out past the sleeping chandler's shop, and turned my feet toward the river and the ruin of the old Blackwood guildhouse, walking fast, like a man late for an appointment he's dreaded his whole life.

Fourteen days, the herald had said. I intended to need none of them.
