## 3. What Grandmother Buried

The rain started an hour before I reached the old guildhouse, which felt about right. Corvane's weather had always shared my grandmother's sense of timing—dramatic, inconvenient, and impossible to argue with.

The Blackwood guildhouse sat at the end of Veilmaker's Lane like a tooth that refused to fall out. Three stories of grey stone, windows boarded, roof half-collapsed into the attic where I'd once slept as a boy and listened to Grandmother tell stories about the old days, when magic moved through the city like water through a riverbed and nobody pretended otherwise. The city had condemned the building eleven years ago. The city had also condemned me, in a quieter way, so I supposed we had that in common.

I stood across the lane under the dripping eaves of the cooper's shop for a long moment, letting the rain hide whatever my face was doing. Because here was the truth I'd been avoiding since the proclamation went up at the Bell Gate: I wasn't digging up a cache. I was digging up a decision. Grandmother had left it buried for a reason, and reasons buried by Odessa Blackwood tended to stay buried unless you were prepared for what came up with the shovel.

The neighbor boy's name had been Pip Wren. He was fourteen. He could make candle flames lean toward him when he hummed, nothing more, a party trick with a whisper of true gift underneath it, and they'd taken him in the night with a hood over his head and his mother screaming in her doorway until someone shut her up with a fist. By morning there was a notice on the Bell Gate with his name on it—not an execution notice yet, just a *detained pending examination*, as if the boy were a shipment of suspect cloth.

*Pending examination.* I'd turned that phrase over all day while selling charms to people who wouldn't meet my eyes. What did they examine? Where did the examined go?

I didn't want to know. That was the honest answer. I wanted to keep selling luck-charms and fever-knots and little braided protections that worked through suggestion and sleight of hand and maybe—one part in a hundred—a touch of something real, so faint that even I could pretend it wasn't there. I wanted to be a charm-seller forever, small and harmless and invisible.

But invisibility, I was learning, is a thing you rent, not own. And the crown had just raised the price.

So. The guildhouse.

I crossed the lane and let myself in through the cellar hatch, because locks had never much troubled a Blackwood and because the front door had been nailed shut by the city assessor with ceremonial thoroughness. Inside smelled of dust and mouse and old rosemary—Grandmother had hung bundles of it everywhere against damp, and eleven years later the ghost of it still clung to the walls like a stubborn opinion.

I lit no lamp. Instead I did the thing I never did indoors, the thing I kept folded up small inside myself like a letter I was afraid to open: I listened.

Not with ears. With whatever it is that lives under the breastbone in people like us. The guildhouse answered. Faintly, sleepily, the way a house answers when you come home after years—the floorboards remembering weight, the walls holding warmth that had no source. Bound into the very mortar, laid down by hands long dead, was a residue of working. My grandmother's working. It recognized me before I'd taken three steps, and something in my chest cracked along an old seam.

"Hello, house," I said quietly, and felt foolish, and did it anyway.

The cache was beneath the hearthstone in the back parlor. I knew this the way I knew the words to songs she'd sung me—not from being told, but from being told *so many times*, in that tone she used for things that mattered more than she wanted to admit. *"If ever the house must give up its heart,"* she used to say, ruffling my hair, *"you'll know where the heart sleeps."* I'd thought it was just a saying. Grandmothers collected sayings the way magpies collect buttons.

The hearthstone came up easier than it should have. There was a lip worn smooth at one edge, finger-worn, and when I set my hands in those worn hollows and pulled, the stone lifted on a counterweight mechanism some ancestor had built, silent as a held breath. Of course. Nothing about this house was accidental.

Below: a lead-lined box, wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath the box, filling the cavity like sand in an hourglass, a spill of grey ash that I chose not to examine too closely. Some inheritances you take one piece at a time.

I carried the box to the parlor table, wiped off the dust, and sat in Grandmother's chair—which some instinct told me was exactly what I was supposed to do, and which some other instinct said was a trap—and unwrapped the oilcloth.

Inside: a journal bound in green leather gone soft as skin. A ring I recognized, garnet set in silver, that she'd supposedly lost decades ago. A folded paper sealed with black wax bearing the Blackwood mark. And a key. Not iron, not quite bronze—an ugly, dense little thing, warm to the touch in a way metal has no business being warm, as though it had been held in a fist for a very long time.

I picked up the journal first. Cowardice or wisdom; with me the two have always shared a border.

Her handwriting filled the early pages—sharp, slanted, impatient, the hand of a woman writing fast because the world was writing faster. Dates went back past my birth, past my mother's, into years I only knew from history lessons that had been carefully gutted. And then, three pages in, a heading underlined twice:

**On the King.**

*I write this knowing that if the wrong eyes find it, I die badly and everything I've built dies with me. So I will write it plainly, because plain things are harder to twist.*

*Aldric is dead.*

*Not deposed, not exiled, not sleeping in some mountain fastness as the ballads would have it. Dead. Eleven years dead as of this winter. I know because I helped bury him. Not his body—his body sits on the throne and waves at festivals and signs edicts with a hand that has not warmed since the Veil War ended. I mean his soul. His self. The man.*

*What wears him is older than the war. We called it the Hollow Crown in the guild records, though the name is ours, not its. It came to Aldric during the last year of fighting, when he was desperate and the city was burning and it offered him victory at a price he did not understand until the price was paid. It does not possess the way demons are said to possess—no screaming, no contortion. It simply moves in and begins to eat. It feeds on bound magic, on gift, on the living current in gifted blood. The hunts are not the Crown's cruelty. They are its farming.*

I stopped reading. Set the journal down. Got up, walked to the window, looked out at the rain on Veilmaker's Lane, and made myself breathe.

Because here was the thing: I had heard whispers. Every charm-seller had. Drunk dockworkers talked about how the king hadn't aged a day in twenty years. Old women muttered that the royal physicians died young and often. But whispers are weather, and this was geology. My grandmother hadn't gossiped. She had *documented*. Dates. Names. Cross-references to guild records I'd assumed were burned.

And the hunts. *Its farming.* Pip Wren, fourteen, humming at candles, taken in the night—not because he was dangerous, but because he was *nutritious*.

I sat back down. I picked the journal up again, because the alternative was to put it back in the hole and nail the hearthstone down and go sell charms until they came for me too, and some stubborn Blackwood grain in me refused to let that sentence finish forming.

Further in, the handwriting changed—shakier now, pressed hard, written by lamplight in a hurry:

**On the Keys.**

*There is a way to unbind him. The binding that holds Aldric's soul apart from his body was worked with three anchors, because a binding of three cannot be broken by force from outside—it must be unlocked, each anchor in turn, and the unlocking must be done by willing hands with inherited gift. The guild bound him. I will not pretend otherwise. When Aldric understood what he had become, what he had allowed in, he came to us in secret and begged us for death, and death was beyond our art, but unbinding was not. We locked his soul out of reach of the thing wearing him, so that when the keys turn, what remains to be freed is still him.*

*Three keys. Salt, iron, bone.*

*The salt key lies where the sea took our chapel, in the drowned chapel of Saint Verity beneath the harbor mole. It does not rust and it does not rest. I have seen it in dreams for thirty years and it is tired of waiting.*

*The iron key the guild surrendered to the crown as part of the accords—we thought it safer in their vaults than in ours, God forgive us. It sleeps in the hunter citadel, in the deep vault, behind doors that open to hunter blood alone.*

*The bone key is the worst of the three, and I will write it once and never speak it aloud: it is a fingerbone of Aldric himself, kept in the royal reliquary among the sainted relics, venerated by the very court that destroyed him. They pray to the proof of their crime every feast day.*

*Whoever reads this: I am sorry. I am sorry that we were cowards in stages instead of brave in one motion. I am sorry that the bill has come due in your hands. But hear me—if you hold this book, you hold the only complete account of the binding in existence, and the Crown knows it. It has been looking for this book for eleven years. If it finds you before you understand what you carry, it will not kill you. It will use you. Understand the difference, and move quickly, and trust almost no one.*

*Trust Maren Ostwick. She is the last elder who remembers the rite entire. Find her even if finding her costs you everything else.*

I closed the journal and sat for a long time in my grandmother's chair with the rain ticking on the boarded windows.

Three keys. A drowned chapel. A hunter citadel. A palace reliquary. Any one of those sentences should have been enough to make me laugh and pour a drink. Together they read like a suicide note with footnotes.

And yet.

I looked at the key in the box—the warm, ugly, patient thing—and understood, with a lurch, that it wasn't one of the three. It was the key to *Maren*. To wherever Grandmother had hidden the last elder, or rather the token that would make Maren believe me, because a woman who'd survived eleven years of the Crown's searching would not open her door for a tearful nephew with a story. She'd open it for this. For metal warm as a living hand.

Grandmother had planned it all like a lock falling into place, and I resented her for it with my whole heart, because she'd known—she'd *known*—that someday the price would come due and that the one to pay it would be family, and she'd arranged everything anyway, gently, thoroughly, the way she'd arranged everything, including my childhood, including my fear, including the smallness she'd taught me to wear like armor.

"You could have told me while you were alive," I said to the empty room. The house creaked, noncommittal.

Then I opened the sealed paper, because apparently I was committed to bad decisions tonight.

One page. Her hand, steadier than the journal, as if this had been written early and kept for last:

*Paloma. If it's you, then you're reading this scared, and I want you to know that scared is correct. Brave people who aren't scared die stupid deaths. Be scared and do it anyway.*

*You'll wonder why you. You'll wonder why not Serah. Here is why: Serah believes the world is rules, and rules can be trusted if you follow them perfectly. You believe the world is people, and people can be helped even when the rules fail. The Crown lives on rules—registration, examination, order. It cannot digest someone who keeps choosing people over law. That is your armor, love. Wear it thin and it will save your life.*

*Keep Serah out of this if you can. But if you cannot—*

The page ended there. Ink blot. Either interruption or hesitation, and with Grandmother I'd never learned to tell the two apart.

I folded it away next to my heart, which felt less like sentiment than like following instructions.

* * *

Dawn found me still in the parlor, having read the journal twice more and slept perhaps forty minutes sitting up, dreaming of candle flames leaning toward a humming boy. I packed what mattered: the journal, wrapped again in oilcloth; the warm key on a cord around my neck, under my shirt, against my skin, where it beat faintly like a second pulse I decided not to think about; the garnet ring, because some things you carry whether or not they're useful.

At the hearth I paused. The cavity below the stone still held its spill of grey ash, and I finally let myself look at it properly. Ash doesn't keep for eleven years looking like that—grey and fine and *arranged*, raked level, deliberate. Something had been burned here on purpose, completely, by someone careful. Papers, most likely. Guild records. The copies, the dangerous ones, the ones that named names.

Or—my stomach turned—something else. Someone else's remains, given to the hearth of a guildhouse the way sailors give bodies to the sea. I raked the ash level again with the poker, the way you'd close a grave, and replaced the stone.

"Rest," I told whoever it was. "I'll try to be worth it."

Outside, the rain had stopped and Corvane was waking up its lies: bells, cart wheels, the Bell Gate criers already droning the registration deadline—*seven days remaining, seven days remaining*—in voices polished smooth as river stones. Seven days to sign my name on a list that piped people straight into the Crown's pantry.

I pulled my collar up and joined the current of bodies flowing toward the harbor district, one more invisible man in a visible crowd. Maren Ostwick. A laundress, the journal's margins said in a note added later, shakier: *Washhouse on Laundress Row, the one with the blue door. Ask for nothing. Show her the key.*

The blue door. The warm key. A dead king begging through thirty years of silence for someone to finish the job his rescuers couldn't.

I touched the key through my shirt and felt it pulse, patient as a tide.

Seven days. I'd better find a laundress.
