## 4. The Last Elder

The journal had ruined my sleep for three nights running.

Not because it frightened me—though it did, God knew it did—but because my grandmother's handwriting was so *calm*. Page after page of tidy script describing a parasite wearing the king's face like a coat, and she wrote it the way a grocer writes an inventory. *The Crown feeds at moonrise. The queen's eyes have gone flat. We must find the keys before winter.* No exclamation points. Not one. As if the end of everything were simply a chore to be finished before supper.

I'd read it four times by candlelight I couldn't afford, and each time I arrived at the same passage, the same line, underlined twice in ink gone brown as old blood:

*If I am taken, seek Maren Ostwick. She will know what to do. She always did.*

Maren Ostwick. The name meant nothing and everything. Every Blackwood child learned the guild's roll of elders the way other children learned prayers, and Ostwick was on it—third from the top, keeper of rites, which in guild language meant she was the one who decided what magic was allowed to exist. Grandmother had spoken of her exactly once that I could remember, when I was nine: *That woman could make water boil with a look, and she'd still scold you for wasting the firewood.*

Thirty years since the guildhouse burned. Thirty years since anyone had said her name aloud where hunters might hear it.

So on the fourth morning I did what any sensible man would do with a death sentence folded into his grandmother's journal: I went looking for a laundress.

* * *

Finding her took less skill than finding the journal had. The old guild network hadn't died in the Veil War—it had just learned to hold its breath. There was a woman named Bess who sold eels off a cart near Tanner's Row, and Bess had a habit of humming a particular tuneless song while she worked, three notes rising and falling, rising and falling. My grandmother used to hum it while shelling peas. It wasn't a melody. It was a signpost.

I bought two smoked eels I didn't want and asked, casually, whether she knew of a good laundress. Bess looked at me for a long moment—the look of a woman weighing how much trouble a stranger was worth—and then said, "Wash-house on Candle Lane takes in piecework. Ask for the one with the red hands."

"Red hands?"

"Lye," she said, and went back to her eels.

Candle Lane was a gutter of a street where the wash-houses breathed steam into the gray air all day, and the women who worked them aged in dog-years, hands cracked and bleached white by lye until the knuckles split and wept. I found her at the third house, at the far end of the row, a small square woman with iron-grey hair bound up under a kerchief, scrubbing a shirt against a washboard with the mechanical fury of someone who had been angry at fabric for decades.

Her hands were red, all right. But not lye-red. Lye bleaches; these hands were *stained*, the color soaked deep into the creases, rust-brown and old, and when she wrung the shirt I saw the water in her basin swirl briefly against the current of her scrubbing—a little eddy turning the wrong way, correcting itself, too fast to be sure I'd seen it.

You spend your whole life hiding a gift, you learn to see the hiding in others. It's like recognizing a limp.

"Mistress," I said. "I'm told you take in piecework."

She didn't look up. "Full till Friday."

"It's not washing I've come about." I set my grandmother's journal on the wet stone lip of her basin, wrapped in oilcloth. "It's this."

The washboard stopped.

For three full seconds the only sound on Candle Lane was dripping water and a cart rattling past at the street's mouth. Then she picked up the journal without wiping her hands, weighed it in her palm like a fishwife judging a catch, and said, "Blackwood binding. Ash-tanned hide. You dug where you shouldn't have."

"My grandmother told me to."

"Your grandmother told half of Corvane's dead to do things." She finally looked up, and her eyes were pale grey, the color of weather coming. "And now you've carried that thing through open streets to a wash-house, which means either you're very brave or you're very stupid, and in thirty years of watching fools, boy, I've learned they usually turn out to be both."

"You're Maren Ostwick."

"I'm Widow Corrigan, I take in washing, and if you don't move along I'll scream that a thief is bothering me, and the watch will come, and you'll have a very educational afternoon." She thrust the journal back at me. "Go home."

"I read what's in it." I kept my voice low and steady, though my heart was going like a drum in a parade. "About the king. About what's wearing him."

Something moved behind those grey eyes—not fear exactly. More like a door being barred from the inside.

"There's nothing wrong with the king," she said loudly, for the benefit of the yard, and then, barely a breath, pitched only for me: "Chapel of Saint Vessin. Dusk. Come alone and come clean—if you're followed, I'll know, and you'll wish the hunters had found you first."

Then she picked up her washboard and went back to murdering the shirt, and I understood I'd been dismissed.

* * *

I spent the hours until dusk doing the stupidest possible things: checking over my shoulder every ten steps, buying a meat pie and tasting nothing, standing in a shrine to the Unconquered Sun and trying to remember how to pray to a god whose church had helped outlaw people like me. The chapel of Saint Vessin sat in the shadow of the harbor wall, a squat little building the sea had been trying to eat for a century, its floor permanently damp, its saint's statue missing both hands—worn away, some said, by generations of desperate sailors gripping them.

She was already there when I slipped in, sitting in a front pew like a penitent, and the chapel was empty except for us and the smell of brine and old tallow.

"Sit," she said, without turning. "And ward yourself. Properly. None of that charm-seller nonsense."

I sat. "I don't know proper wards. My grandmother died before—"

"Of course she did. They always leave too late and teach too little." She turned then, and studied me the way a farrier studies a horse he suspects is lame. "Show me your gift."

"That's not—"

"Boy." Her voice didn't rise at all, and yet it filled the chapel like cold water filling a boot. "Three days ago the crown hanged a boy of sixteen off the Bell Gate for a cantrip that made beer foam. This morning you walked into my wash-yard carrying the Blackwood journal. Either you are the bravest fool in Corvane or the journal chose you, and I will not risk my neck on a maybe. Show me. Now."

So I did what I'd spent my whole life never doing. I opened the door I keep locked.

It's hard to describe what it feels like to stop hiding. Like unclenching a fist you've held shut so long you forgot it was a fist. The chapel's damp air warmed around me; the tallow smoke bent toward me; the sea beyond the wall seemed to lean in through the stones. Small things. Harmless things. But unmistakable, to anyone who knew what to feel for—and she would know.

Maren Ostwick closed her eyes. And then she reached into me.

I want to be honest about this, because people talk about magic like it's candles and starlight. What she did was more like a surgeon's hand going into a wound without asking permission. Something of hers slid along whatever-it-is inside me, the inheritance, the Blackwood thread, and it *hurt*—a bright grinding ache, like a tooth being sounded, like she was testing every strand of me for rot. I gasped. I gripped the pew. Somewhere distantly I heard myself make a sound I'm not proud of.

"Hold still," she said. "If you flinch, I'll know it's borrowed."

"It's not—" The words came out strangled. "It's mine. It's been mine since I was six."

"Since you were six." She kept working, that terrible gentle pressure, deeper now. "And no one trained you. No one warded you. You've been walking around Corvane for twenty years with an open hearth in your chest and a city full of hunters outside, and nobody thought to teach you so much as how to bank the fire?"

"They outlawed the teaching."

"They outlawed the teaching and so we let children burn unattended. Yes. That was wisdom." Her voice was bitter enough to strip paint. "Hold. Almost done."

And then she stopped, and withdrew, and the absence of her was almost worse than the presence—I felt scraped out, wrung like one of her shirts, trembling on the pew with sweat running down my spine.

But her expression had changed. The door behind her eyes had unbarring, just a crack.

"Well?" I managed.

"Well," she said, "you're Blackwood, right enough. Stronger than your grandmother, which is either a blessing or a catastrophe, and untrained, which is certainly a catastrophe." She folded her red-stained hands in her lap. "Now ask me what you came to ask."

The chapel felt suddenly very quiet. Outside, gulls argued over something dead.

"The journal says the king is dead," I said. "That something called the Hollow Crown wears him. That there are three keys that can unbind him. Is it true?"

Maren Ostwick looked at the handless saint for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman walking on ice she already knows is thin.

"Aldric has been dead eleven years," she said. "I helped carry his body out of that throne room myself, or what was left of it. What sits on the throne now is older than the kingdom. It came through during the Veil War, when the Veil was torn, and it has been feeding ever since—on the bound magic, on the gifted, on the city itself. Slowly. Patiently. The way rot feeds on a great house: nobody notices until the stairs give way."

"And the hunts? The executions?"

"Not hunts. Harvests." She said it the way you'd say *plague*. "Every gifted person taken is drained before the pyre. The registry isn't a list of criminals, boy. It's a menu."

My stomach turned over. I thought of the neighbor boy, Tam, with his little light-cantrips, dragged screaming into the night. I thought of the dockworker's luck-charm in my pocket, sold that morning, harmless, worthless—and how even worthless magic was worth killing for, if you were hungry enough.

"The keys," I said. "Are they real?"

"They're real. Salt, iron, and bone. Three locks on one binding, made when the guild still served the crown—when we still believed we were saving the kingdom instead of burying it." Her jaw tightened. "The salt key lies in the drowned chapel under the harbor. The iron key is locked in the hunter citadel's own vault, because the Crown trusts its dogs to guard its chains. And the bone key—"

She stopped.

"The bone key," I prompted.

"Is the worst of the three, and the safest, and I'll tell you where it lies when you've earned it. First you must decide." She leaned forward, and up close her eyes weren't cold at all—they were tired, tired the way only the very old and the very guilty get tired. "Understand what you'd be taking up, Paloma Blackwood. Your grandmother started this work and it killed her. The elders who came before her—dead, drained, scattered. I am the last, and I have survived thirty years by being *no one*: a widow, a washerwoman, a pair of red hands nobody looks at twice. If you walk out of this chapel on this road, you will be hunted by your own sister before the season turns. Do you know that? Have you worked that out yet?"

The name landed like a slap. "How do you—"

"I keep accounts. It's what elders do." She didn't soften. "Serah Villalobos-Blackwood, chief investigator of the hunt, the Crown's favorite hound. She'll be given your name soon enough, and she won't hesitate, because hesitation is the first thing they train out of them. So think carefully. Walk away, burn the journal, sell your charms, die old and ordinary in a warm bed. That life is still available to you. For perhaps another week."

I should have taken it. Any reasonable man would have taken it. I sat there in the damp chapel, scraped hollow and aching, thinking about a sixteen-year-old boy hanging off the Bell Gate for making beer foam, and about my grandmother writing her calm little inventory of the apocalypse, and about Serah, who used to braid my hair when we were small and who now wore the grey coat of the hunt.

"No," I said. "Tell me about the salt key."

Maren Ostwick watched me for a long moment. Whatever she was looking for, she must have found it, because something in her face settled—grief and resolve shaking down together like sediment in a jar.

"Your grandmother said you'd say that," she murmured. "She also said you'd complain the whole way, which I'm counting on, because a man who complains is a man paying attention." She rose from the pew, stiff-backed, every year of her thirty-year disguise in the movement. "Drowned chapel, under the old pilgrim pier, past the breakwater. The tide gives you one hour at slack water, no more. And Paloma—"

"Yes?"

"Whatever you find down there, however bad it gets—you do not use your gift openly. Not once. Not to save your skin. The moment you flare, every hunter in Corvane will feel it, and your sister will feel it first." She pulled her kerchief tighter and became, before my eyes, merely a laundress again, small and bent and forgettable. "We meet tomorrow, after dark, Tanner's Row. Bring the journal. And bring your complaints. I'm told you're good at those."

She walked out into the dusk, and I stayed a moment longer in the chapel of the handless saint, listening to the sea gnaw at the wall, feeling the place inside me where she had been—raw, tested, and, for the first time in my life, *known*.

Fear was still there. Fear would always be there. But underneath it now, something else had taken root, quiet and stubborn as a weed through cobblestone.

Purpose. Which, I was beginning to suspect, was just fear that had found somewhere to go.

Tomorrow, the drowned chapel. Tonight, I had a journal to study and a sister to try not to think about.

I failed at the second part before I even reached the door.
