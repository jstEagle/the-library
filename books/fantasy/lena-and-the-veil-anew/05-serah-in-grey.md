## 5. Serah in Grey

The charm-seller's hands were shaking, and I found that more convincing than anything he said.

"Third time this month," he kept saying. "Third time, Captain—Investigator—I mean—" He couldn't decide what to call me, which was fair. Most people in Corvane hadn't decided yet either. The grey coat did the deciding for them eventually. Grey like ash, grey like a locked door. People saw it and their sentences fell apart.

"Sit down," I said.

He sat. The interrogation room at the Bell Gate garrison had one table, two chairs, and a window too high to see out of, positioned so that the light fell on the prisoner and never on the questioner. Someone had designed it that way on purpose, and I tried not to think about who, or how long ago, or whether the person had gone home afterward to a family and slept well.

I laid out his inventory on the table between us. Eleven items, catalogued by my sergeant with the meticulousness of a man who wanted to be noticed by someone above him. Knotted cords. Dried lavender in cloth squares. A river-stone with a hole through it, worn smooth, the kind children find and keep for years without knowing why.

"These are toys," I said.

"Yes." Eager. Too eager. "Yes, exactly, Investigator, they're—they're folk things, tourist things, harbor superstition, half of them I buy from a carver upriver who doesn't even—"

"The knot here." I turned the cord over. "You told the buyer it would hold a fair wind for his crossing."

"A figure of speech."

"You told the widow on Tanner's Lane that the lavender would help her sleep without dreams."

"That's just—it's lavender, Investigator, anyone will tell you lavender helps you sleep—"

"And the stone." I held it up. It caught the light from the high window, and for a moment—just a moment, a trick of the angle, I told myself later, I tell myself many things later—the hole in its center seemed to darken rather than brighten, as if something behind it were drinking. "What did you tell the boy who bought this?"

He stopped talking.

That was always the moment. Not the accusations, not the evidence, not the grey coat. The moment when a man ran out of words was the moment he told you everything, because silence is where the truth lives in Corvane now, and everyone knows it, and no one can afford to fill it.

"There was a boy," he said finally. Very quietly. "Fisherman's son. Bad lungs. Coughing all winter, and his mother came to me crying, and I—" His hands opened on the table, empty. "I told her the stone would ease his breathing. And it did. That's what I can't explain to you, Investigator. It *did*. I don't know how. I bought it off the same carver, I swear it, but when I held it out to her it was warm, and I'd been carrying it in my pocket all day and it had never been warm once, and the boy breathed easier that very night, and I don't know *how*."

The room was quiet. Somewhere above us, boots crossed the garrison floor.

"Do you believe in magic?" I asked him.

"No," he said. "Gods, no. They hang people for it."

"I know," I said. "I'm one of the people who hangs them."

He looked at me then—really looked, past the coat—and whatever he saw in my face made him afraid in a different way than before. I didn't blame him. I was afraid of the same thing.

I marked his file: *unregistered peddler, charms of negligible potency, no demonstrated gift.* Negligible potency. I wrote those two words and felt them like stones in my mouth. The boy could breathe. That was not negligible. That was the whole world, to a mother watching her son sleep.

"Fine," I said. "Public whipping, three days' labor at the granary, and your stock confiscated. Sign here."

He signed. His hand was still shaking. At the door he paused, and I thought—he's going to thank me, gods help us both, he's going to thank me for not hanging him—

"Investigator," he said instead. "There's others. Better than me. Ones who actually know what they're doing. If you want names—"

"Don't," I said.

He went.

I sat alone in the room with eleven confiscated toys and the light crawling across the floor, and I did not write down the name he'd almost given me, because he hadn't said it, and because I hadn't asked, and because somewhere under my ribs there was a small cold voice asking what kind of investigator doesn't ask.

The answer, of course, was: the kind whose brother sells charms.

* * *

My grandmother used to say the Veil wasn't a wall. She said that was the first lie everyone learned, that the Veil War ended with mages walled off from power like water behind a dam. "It's a membrane, girl," she'd say, tapping her temple with one yellowed fingernail. "Thin as skin. And skin remembers what touched it."

Paloma believed her. That was the whole tragedy of our childhood, if you want it in one sentence: Paloma believed her, and I didn't.

I remember the night it broke between us. I was sixteen, he was fourteen, and Grandmother had spent the evening telling us about the king. Not stories—she was very clear about the difference, and offended when we confused them. Stories had dragons. This had arithmetic.

"He died," she said. "Years ago. Before either of you were born, or just after—you were babies, it was during the plague winter. Aldric died in his bed like any man, and something climbed into the shape he left behind, and it's been wearing him ever since. Feeding. The hunts aren't the crown protecting us from magic, children. The hunts are the crown *harvesting* it."

"That's treason," I said. I remember saying it precisely, because I remember being proud of how steady my voice was. Sixteen years old and already practicing for the grey coat, though I didn't know it yet. "That's a hanging offense to even say."

"It's an observation," she said. "Hang me for looking."

"Grandmother." I leaned forward. Our kitchen smelled of tallow and boiled cabbage and the lavender she hung in bundles that never seemed to dry out. "The king saved the city. Everyone knows the history. He bound the wild magic after the war so it wouldn't tear Corvane apart, and the Veil laws are his law, and—"

"—and everyone knows what everyone knows, and knowing it together is called *truth*, and questioning it is called *madness*." She smiled at me, and her smile was the worst thing about her, because it was patient. She was never angry with me. She just waited, like weather. "Your grandfather questioned it. Your mother questioned it. Now you'll get to decide, Serah. Decide or inherit. Those are the only two choices anyone gets."

"I choose what's real," I said.

Paloma said nothing. He was sitting by the fire with his knees drawn up, and he was looking at Grandmother with an expression I have spent eleven years trying to forget—not worship exactly, but recognition. The look of someone hearing a door open that they'd always suspected was there.

"She's right," he said.

And I laughed. I want to be fair to myself across that distance; I was sixteen, and I was frightened, and laughter is what fear wears when it wants to look like armor. "She's *old*, Paloma. She talks to her plants. She buried jars in the garden during the last drought and swore the rain came because of it."

"The rain came," he said.

"The rain came because it *rains*, you absolute—"

"Serah." He stood up. My little brother, all elbows and ink stains, standing between me and the fire so I had to look at him instead of her. "When did you last sleep without dreaming? When did any of us? She says the Veil thinned over Corvane and everyone's dreams leak through it, and you laugh, but you *know*. You wake up tired every morning same as I do. Same as the neighbors. Whole city waking up exhausted and nobody asks why."

"Because people are tired, Paloma. That's not magic, that's *Corvane*."

"Then explain the stone."

I blinked. "What stone?"

He went to the shelf and took down the river-stone with the hole in it—the same kind, I realized much later, the same exact kind, that I would one day catalogue as evidence—and set it on the table between us. "She found it. Said it was a door-key for something small. Watch."

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. I waited, arms crossed, victorious in advance, and then the candle flame bent toward the stone. Not guttered—bent. Drew toward the hole like breath being taken in, held there for three heartbeats, four, and released.

I picked up the stone. It was warm. I carried it in my pocket all day and it was never warm again, and I told myself the fire had heated it, except we'd been sitting well away from the fire, and I told myself a dozen other things over the following weeks, each explanation thinner than the last, until explanations wore out entirely and only the choice remained.

I chose the army. Then the constabulary. Then, when the witch-hunters were reorganized under the new edicts, the Office of Investigation, because I told myself—there's that word again, *told*, my whole career built on the passive grammar of self-deception—I told myself that someone reasonable should hold the leash. That if I didn't take the post, someone crueler would. That I could protect people from inside the machine better than outside it.

Eleven years later, I whip men for selling lavender, and the machine runs smoothly through my hands, and I am still telling myself.

* * *

That evening, Sergeant Vess found me at my desk with the day's files.

"Registry updates," he said, and dropped a folder thick enough to stop a door. "Bell Gate district. Forty-one new registrations this week since the proclamation went up."

Forty-one. People who had read the edict nailed to the Bell Gate itself—*all persons possessing the gift shall present themselves for registration within thirty days; unregistered practitioners will be subject to investigation and execution*—and had walked to the garrison and written down their names. Their *names*. In a book. For bread rations, mostly. Registration came with a grain allotment, and hunger does arithmetic faster than fear does.

"Any refusals?"

"Three households flagged. One's already fled—neighbors say south, toward the marshes." He hesitated. He was young, Vess, and honest in the way that made him bad at his job and good company, and I liked him more than was prudent. "There's... one more thing, Investigator. The Kettering boy. From Lantern Row."

"What about him?"

"He didn't register. His mother hid him when the collectors came yesterday. And this morning—" Vess's throat moved. "This morning the collectors went back. With the writ expanded. They took him. He's fourteen, ma'am. Fourteen years old, and they took him to the citadel for assessment, and the assessment order is signed under your office."

The room got very quiet, the way rooms do around me lately, as if they've learned what I do to silences.

"My signature," I said.

"Yours. Stamped. It's standard procedure, the batch orders go out under the chief investigator's seal automatically, you don't actually—"

"I know how my own seal works, Sergeant."

He shut up. I stared at the folder. Forty-one names who had trusted the book, three households who hadn't, and one boy on Lantern Row who was fourteen years old and coughing—no. Not coughing. That was the charm-seller's story bleeding into this one. The Kettering boy was healthy, as far as anyone knew. Healthy, and gifted, and now in the citadel, where assessments happened, and where the assessed did not come back, and where the paperwork describing what happened to them was filed in a wing I had clearance to enter and had never once entered, because entering it would mean reading it, and reading it would mean knowing, and I had built eleven years on the foundation of not-knowing, and the foundation was starting to sing under load like a bridge in wind.

"Pull the boy's file," I said. "Full intake record. I want to review the assessment order before it's executed."

Vess blinked. "Review it? Ma'am, the assessments are citadel jurisdiction, we just—"

"*Before it's executed,* Sergeant. That's not a request."

He went. I sat with the registry folder in front of me, forty-one names, and I read them. All of them. A laundress. Two dockworkers. A chandler's widow. A girl of nineteen who listed her gift, in the space provided, as *"making bread rise."*

Making bread rise. She'd written it down honestly, because she was honest, because she was hungry, because the edict promised registration meant safety, and the edict was nailed to the Bell Gate in the king's own name, and the king's own name was the oldest lie in Corvane, according to a mad old woman in a kitchen that smelled of lavender, according to a boy of fourteen who watched a candle bend toward a stone and chose to believe the truth of his own eyes over the comfort of his sister's laughter.

I pressed my palms flat against the folder until the edges bit into my skin.

My grandmother died alone. That's the part I come back to, the part that turns over in the dark hours like a stone in a pocket. Not mad, not friendless—alone *by design*, because her granddaughter had chosen the uniform and her grandson had chosen her, and the two choices could not live in the same house. Paloma went to her funeral. I sent a letter of condolence through proper channels, proper words, and I have it memorized still, every polished sentence of it, and not one of those sentences says what I actually felt, which was that I had attended the execution of the only person who ever told me the truth, and I had done it with my signature.

The lamp guttered. Outside, Corvane settled into its nightly exhaustion, ten thousand dreams leaking through a thin skin of the world.

Vess returned with the Kettering file and laid it on my desk, and I opened it, and the first page was the intake form, and the second page was the transfer writ, and the third page bore the citadel's assessment schedule, and beside the boy's name, in the column where the assessor's initials would go, there was already a mark.

The assessment was scheduled for tomorrow. But the mark was dated today.

They had assessed him before he arrived. Or the paperwork had been finished before the boy existed. Either way, the machinery of the hunt had recorded an outcome for a child who was, at this moment, alive in a cell beneath the citadel, and the outcome was not one I was prepared to read while anyone was watching.

I closed the file. I looked at the mark for a long time.

Then I reached for my coat, and told Vess I'd be reviewing records at the citadel archive in the morning, and walked out into the grey evening with one thought repeating under my ribs like a pulse:

If the Crown harvests the gifted—if Grandmother was right, if Paloma was right, if the truth I executed her for was true—then every name in this folder is already dead, and the only question left is which side of the door I'm standing on when I finally admit it.

Tomorrow I would pull the Kettering boy's records. Tomorrow I would enter the wing I'd avoided for eleven years.

Tomorrow, I was afraid, the not-knowing would end.
