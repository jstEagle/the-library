## 6. The First Key: Salt

The tide went out at midnight, and the drowned chapel showed its teeth.

I'd been watching it for three nights from the end of Gullwharf, wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin that smelled of tar and old regret, waiting for the water to drop low enough to reveal what my grandmother's journal called *the salt door*. The journal had a talent for poetry that I resented. What it actually meant was: when the moon is dark and the tide is mean, walk out past the pilings and look for the archway in the mud.

There it was now, black against black, crusted with barnacles like some leper's blessing. The chapel of Saint Verity—or what was left of it—had gone under during the Veil War, when the harbor masters scuttled half the district rather than let the enemy hold it. Corvane had always been good at drowning its own history. It was doing it again, just slower, with edicts instead of seawater.

"You're either very brave or very stupid," I told myself, "and knowing you, it's both."

Talking to myself was a habit of loneliness, not madness. I want that on the record. Though after everything, I suppose the distinction stopped mattering.

I climbed down the ladder bolted to the last piling, boots sinking into silt that sucked at me like it had opinions. The cold came up through the soles immediately—the harbor in autumn doesn't chill you so much as file a formal complaint with your bones. Above me, Corvane glittered on its hill, ten thousand windows, and not one of them lit for people like me anymore.

*Three keys,* Grandmother's journal said. *Salt, iron, bone. Salt for what was promised, iron for what was enforced, bone for what remains.* And beneath that, in her cramped hand: *The first is easy. That's how they get you.*

Comforting woman, my grandmother. I missed her every day and resented her about once an hour, which I'd come to understand was the standard rate for inheritance.

The archway stood maybe eight feet high, and inside it the mud gave way to old flagstones, slick as liar's promises. I felt for my charm-bag out of habit—luck-knots, fever-beads, the little braided things I sold dockworkers to make them feel less cursed by their lives—and then remembered I'd left it behind. Nothing on me tonight but the journal wrapped in waxcloth against my chest, a hooded lantern, and a knife I mostly used for cutting twine.

A charm-seller going key-hunting with no charms. If Maren Ostwick could see me, she'd have something dry to say about it. Something like: *The guild survived two hundred years of persecution, Paloma, and it will not survive your sentimentality.*

But I couldn't take the bag. If the hunters caught me with working charms, I wasn't a vagrant or a smuggler—I was gifted, and gifted meant the Bell Gate gallows. The unregistered ones didn't even get trials anymore. They got a ledger entry and a lime pit.

So I went in empty-handed, which is either courage or stupidity, and we've established which.

* * *

Inside, the chapel held its breath.

My lantern found walls of black stone furred with weed, and above them—impossibly, beautifully—a ceiling of mosaic that the water had preserved. Thousands of glass tiles, blues and greens and one streak of gold, arranged into a figure with arms spread wide. Not a saint. A woman holding up a sky. The Veil itself, maybe, from back when people painted it as protection instead of prison.

"Hello, gorgeous," I whispered, because even terrified, I'm incapable of not talking to beautiful things.

The nave stretched ahead of me, flooded ankle-deep with water so still it looked solid. My footsteps sent rings across it, and each ring made a sound—not a splash but a chime, faint, like the chapel was humming along. Bound magic. Old bound magic, soaked into the stones the way grease soaks into a cook's apron. The whole place was saturated with it, and every hair on my arms stood up and paid attention.

Here's what they don't tell you about having the gift in outlawed Corvane: you spend your whole life pretending you're deaf, and then you walk into a place like this and it's like someone struck a bell next to your ear. I could feel the chapel's magic the way you feel a stare. It knew I was there. It had been waiting.

The journal's map put the key beneath the altar, in a hollow sealed with salt-glass. I waded down the nave, water climbing my shins, my thighs, the lantern held high enough to catch the mosaic woman watching me with her thousand glass eyes.

The altar was a slab of grey stone, cracked down the middle, furred white with mineral bloom. I set the lantern on a fallen column, took out the knife, and started prying at the seam where the slab met its base.

It came easier than it should have. Remember that. Everything easy in Corvane comes easier than it should.

Underneath: a cavity lined with something that glittered even through the grime. Salt-glass, just as the journal said—crystallized brine grown over decades into a rough casket shape. Inside it, a dark shape the size of my forearm.

I reached in.

The moment my fingers touched the salt-glass, the chapel *woke*.

Not loudly. That's the horror of it. No crash, no roar. Just a change in the water's hum, a deepening, and then every chime my footsteps had made began repeating themselves all at once, overlapping, building, until the sound wasn't sound anymore but pressure—and the water around my legs went warm as bathwater, which is the single most frightening thing harbor water can do.

"It's fine," I told the chapel, the way you'd talk to a dog. "I'm Blackwood. I'm supposed to be here."

The warmth did not care about my pedigree.

I wrenched the salt-glass open—it crumbled like sugar left in rain—and there it was. The first key. Not gold, not jeweled. A rod of cloudy crystal shot through with threads of white, and when I lifted it, it was light as a bird's bone and humming against my palm in a frequency I felt in my teeth.

*Salt for what was promised.* One third of the way to unbinding a dead king from his own corpse. One third of the way to getting myself killed.

That's when the light swept across the ceiling.

Lantern-light, from outside. From the archway. And with it, voices—two of them, maybe three, clipped and official, the particular cadence of men who read edicts aloud for a living.

Witch-hunters.

I killed my own lantern and stood in the dark, heart slamming, mind doing the arithmetic of desperation. Exits: the archway I'd come through, now silhouetting three figures against the night. The water. The cracks in the walls too narrow for a cat. The arithmetic came out badly.

"—says the tide chart's wrong," one voice was saying. "Says there's a chamber under the harbor the old maps don't show."

"The old maps don't show it because the old maps were drawn by traitors," said a second voice, younger, harder. "Sweep it anyway."

They had a tide chart. They had orders. Which meant someone had talked, or someone had guessed, or the Hollow Crown's reach extended even into drowned chapels. I pressed myself flat against the altar's base, the key against my chest, and discovered that terror has a taste. Copper and salt. Like biting a coin while crying.

Their boots rang on the flagstones. Lantern-light crawled up the mosaic woman's face, gilding her glass eyes, and I thought, absurdly, *don't look at her, she's innocent.*

Then the youngest one stopped walking.

He was closest to me—ten feet, maybe twelve—with his lantern raised toward the altar, toward the disturbed mineral bloom, toward the crumbling salt-glass scattered on the stone like spilled sugar. I watched him see it. Watched him go still in the way hunters go still, the whole body becoming an instrument of attention.

"There's been someone here," he said.

"Don't be stupid, Tovan. Who'd come to a drowned church at slack tide?"

"Someone who knew the tide." He crouched, and I watched his gloved fingers hover over the salt-glass fragments. "This broke recently. Hours. Maybe less."

"Tovan—"

"Spread out."

And that was the moment I understood I was going to die in a flooded church, and my last thought would probably be something sarcastic, and Serah would never know I'd died trying to fix the world she was breaking. The unfairness of it hit me harder than the fear. I'd spent thirty years being invisible, and now, finally, finally I was doing something that mattered, and it was going to last four more minutes.

The young hunter—Tovan—rose and turned toward the altar. Toward me. His lantern came up.

I did the only thing left. I let the gift off its leash.

Just a thread of it, just enough—I pushed warmth into the water between us, a shimmer, a distortion, the oldest trick in the Blackwood book: make the light bend, make the eye slide off. Veiling. Grandmother's specialty. I'd hidden behind it my whole life without ever using it full-strength, and now I threw everything I had into it, and the strain hit me like a fist behind the eyes.

His lantern-light slid across me. Stuck. Slid again.

He frowned. Took a step closer. His hand went to the long knife at his belt—not a sword; hunters carried knives for close work, for work done quietly—and I watched him weigh something. The broken glass. The bent light. Me.

"He's here," Tovan said quietly. Not shouting. Quietly. To himself, almost. "Right here."

"Tovan!" The older hunter's voice cracked like a whip. "We sweep and report. Those are orders. Whatever you think you've found—"

"I know what I've found."

And then he did something no hunter has ever done in the history of hunting.

He turned his back on me.

"Lantern's guttering," he announced loudly. "Oil's sea-damp. We should surface before we're stranded by the turn." He was already walking away, toward the archway, his voice carrying back over his shoulder with terrible casualness: "Chapel's clear. Nothing here but fish and saints."

The older hunter muttered something about wasted nights, and their lights retreated, and their boots faded, and I stood in the dark shaking so hard the key chattered against my chest like a small animal.

I didn't move for a long time. Long enough for the water to start cooling. Long enough for my legs to remember they were legs.

Then, from the darkness near the archway, a voice:

"You can drop the veil now. It's giving you a nosebleed."

* * *

He was leaning against the archway when I limped out, arms crossed, lantern doused, face half-lit by the moon coming up over the breakwater. Young—younger than me—with the close-cropped hair and the heavy shoulders of a man built for doors he expected to kick in. Hunter's grey, but the collar was torn off, and where the order's sigil should have been stitched, there was only a rectangle of paler cloth.

A disgraced hunter. Or a hunter running a longer game than I could imagine.

"You," I managed. My voice came out in pieces. "You turned around."

"I did."

"Why?"

He considered the question with the seriousness other men gave to theology. "Because I've been reading the ledgers," he said. "The ones they burn. I processed forty-one arrests last year. Do you know how many of those people were dangerous?" He didn't wait for me to guess. "None. A woman who could sweeten milk. A boy who glowed in the dark when he slept. An old man whose roses bloomed out of season." His jaw worked. "Forty-one. And the order calls it cleansing, and I called it duty, right up until I couldn't."

"That's quite a confession to make to a stranger."

"You're not a stranger." He nodded at my chest, where the key's outline showed faintly through the wet shirt. "You're the one the elders wrote about. The Blackwood heir. Maren Ostwick sent word through the laundresses' line three days ago—she said if a fool went to the chapel alone, someone should be there to watch him drown."

"Maren sent you?"

"Maren blackmails everyone. It's her gift." He pushed off the wall and extended a hand—calloused, steady. "Tovan Reeve. Formerly of the Order of the Grey Lantern. Currently of nowhere."

I looked at that hand for a long moment. Trusting a hunter was like trusting a trap not to snap. But he'd turned his back on me. He'd lied to his own brothers-in-grey with a steadiness that couldn't be performed, and somewhere in the chapel he'd chosen me over his order without ever seeing my face.

I shook his hand. His grip was careful, like he knew exactly what he could break.

"The key," he said. "Did you get it?"

I drew it out. In the moonlight it caught the light strangely, drinking it, and Tovan went very still.

"That's what they're looking for," he said softly. "Not the chapel. The *key*. There's a standing order, sealed, top of the citadel. Find the salt key. Kill whoever holds it." He looked at me, and in the dark his eyes were very tired and very awake. "You didn't know that."

"No."

"And you went anyway."

"I went anyway."

Something that might have been respect crossed his face, or pity, or the place where those two live together in people who've seen too much. "Then you'd better come with me," he said. "Because the tide's turning, the hunt knows the chapel exists, and by morning every grey cloak in Corvane will be looking for a man who walks out of the water holding a piece of a dead king."

Behind us, the drowned chapel settled back into its silence, the mosaic woman holding up her glass sky over nothing, keeping her secrets the way Corvane kept everything—underwater, in the dark, waiting for someone brave enough or stupid enough to dive.

I tucked the key against my heart and followed the hunter into the city that wanted us both dead.

Two keys remained. And I had a feeling the second one would cost more than a soaking.
