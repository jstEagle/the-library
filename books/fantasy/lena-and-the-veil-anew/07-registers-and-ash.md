## 7. Registers and Ash

The registry office smelled of tallow, dust, and the particular staleness of paper that had been handled by too many frightened people. Serah stood in the doorway a moment longer than she needed to, letting her eyes adjust, letting the clerks notice her grey coat and straighten in their seats. Fear was useful. Fear kept order. She had told herself that so many times over the past months that the words had worn smooth as river stones, and she no longer felt their edges.

"Investigator Grey." The head clerk, a soft man named Pell with ink-stained fingers and a voice like wet wool, rose from his desk. "We weren't expecting you until the quarterly audit."

"Plans change." She crossed the room, boots ringing on flagstone. "I want to review the registry. All of it."

Pell's smile flickered. "All of it? That's—there are four hundred and eleven names, Investigator. Some of the volumes are quite old."

"Then I'll be here some time. Bring me candles."

He brought candles. He also brought tea he hadn't been asked for, and hovered, and asked twice whether she required anything else, and each time she said no with such flatness that even a registry clerk could read it. By the third hour he had found urgent business elsewhere, and Serah sat alone in the lamplight with four centuries of Corvane's guilty consciences stacked around her.

She opened the oldest volume first.

The handwriting changed from page to page, decade to decade, but the entries never did. *Name. District. Nature of gift. Status.* A girl in Salt Row who could warm water with her hands. A cooper whose barrels never leaked because he asked the wood politely. A midwife who could ease pain with a touch, registered in the year of Serah's grandmother's birth, marked *compliant* for thirty years until one winter when the mark simply stopped, replaced by a date and a small notation: *deceased — natural causes.*

Serah checked the cross-reference ledger. The woman had died in the cells beneath the Bell Gate. *Natural causes* meant she had not survived questioning.

She turned the page.

This was the part of the work no one talked about, the part that lived in ledgers instead of proclamations. The hunts got the bells and the crowds and the grey-coated procession through the streets. But this—the slow cataloguing of a city's gentlest people, entered in careful script by men like Pell who went home to their suppers—that was the true machinery. Every name here was a person who had come forward because the crown promised that registration meant protection. *Register, and you will be left in peace. Register, and your neighbors will know you are lawful.*

She had believed it once. She had repeated it at the Bell Gate with her hand on the edict, her voice carrying across the square, and she had watched people step out of the crowd to sign their names because an investigator in a grey coat had promised them safety. She remembered a dockworker's wife with flour on her apron, signing with a shaking hand while her husband gripped her shoulder. She remembered thinking: *this is mercy. This is the civilized alternative to fire.*

Four hundred and eleven names. She started counting the ones marked *deceased* somewhere past sixty and made herself stop.

* * *

"You've been down there all night."

Tovan leaned in the doorway of her office above the citadel armory, two cups in his hands. He set one on her desk without asking, which was either kindness or presumption, and with Tovan it was usually both.

"I'm working."

"You're reading." He dropped into the chair across from her, and his eyes went to the open volume, and something moved behind them—something old and tired. "Ah. The registry."

"You've read it?"

"Everyone in the order has, eventually. Most of us wish we hadn't." He wrapped his hands around his cup. "There's a clerk in the Hall of Records, Ferren, who used to weep every Friday. Copying the weekly status reports. He transferred to map-making last spring. Says he'd rather chart coastlines than count the dead."

"Then why does anyone stay?"

"The same reason you do." Tovan looked at her over the rim of his cup. "Because someone has to hold the leash, and better us than the ones who enjoy it. Because if good investigators resign, the ones left behind are Vessik's kind." He named the name deliberately, watching her face. "You've heard what he did in Tanner's Walk on Tuesday."

"I filed the report."

"You rewrote the report. I read both versions." He set the cup down. "In yours, the charm-seller resisted arrest. In Vessik's draft, the man was on his knees with his hands up when Vessik broke his arm, and then broke the other one for good measure, and then told the man's daughter that she'd be next unless she came in to register. Which she did. Yesterday. Her name is in the book you're sitting next to."

Serah said nothing. The candle guttered. Outside, the citadel bell rang the third hour after midnight, and neither of them moved until it finished.

"My brother used to say," she heard herself begin, and stopped.

"Used to say what?"

"That law is just a story powerful people tell so the rest of us don't ask who wrote it." She laughed, short and bitter. "He was twelve. Grandmother had just been taken to the asylum, and he said it at supper like he was reciting scripture. Mother threw him out of the house for a week."

"And you?"

"I told him he was being dramatic." She pressed her thumb against the edge of the ledger, hard enough to blanch the skin. "I told him Grandmother was ill, and the physicians knew best, and the guild was full of frauds and worse, and someone had to keep order or the Veil War would happen again. I gave the speech. The whole speech. Order, Tovan. Safety. The alternative to law isn't freedom—it's fire."

"Do you still believe that?"

She wanted to say yes. That was the terrible thing; some stubborn root of her still wanted to say yes, because if she said no, then everything rearranged—the edicts, the arrests, the eleven years since she'd spoken to Paloma, the whole architecture of her life—and buildings that tall did not fall gently.

"I believe," she said carefully, "that I have spent my career catching people who light fires. And lately I keep noticing how much of the burning is done by us."

Tovan nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd carried a long way. "Vessik's promotion comes through next month. You know that. And when it does, he'll have authority over intake at the Bell Gate. Every registered name, every new signature—he'll decide who gets 'questioned.'" He stood, drained his cup. "Think about what that means for your dockworker's wife with the flour on her apron."

He paused at the door. "For what it's worth, Investigator—I don't think your brother was being dramatic."

* * *

She should have gone home. She had a room in the investigators' quarters with a narrow bed and a window facing the exercise yard, and she had not slept properly in nine days, and exhaustion makes everything simpler because it makes everything distant.

Instead she went back down to the registry office and asked Pell for the key to the archive annex, telling him she needed to verify cross-references against the master rolls. Pell, who feared her and therefore obeyed her, handed it over without questions.

The annex was colder than the main office, windowless, lit by a single oil lamp. The master rolls were kept in an iron cabinet against the far wall—the original registry, the one from which all copies were made. Four hundred and eleven names in the current volume. Names going back further than that in the older ones, whole generations of the gentle and the harmless, catalogued like inventory.

She opened the cabinet and stood there for a long time with her hand flat against the cold iron.

Here was the thing she could not stop turning over: the registry did not protect anyone. She had known this for weeks now, in the place where knowledge waits before it becomes unbearable. The registered gifted were not safer than the unregistered—they were *found*. The registry was not a shield. It was a harvest list. She thought of Vessik's promotion, of the quiet orders that had begun arriving from the palace in sealed black wax, orders that bypassed the normal chain of command, orders that used words like *intake quotas* and *processing*, words that belonged to granaries and slaughterhouses and not to human beings. She thought of the midwife marked *natural causes*.

Every name in this cabinet was a person who had trusted the grey coat. Who had walked into the square because Serah's voice had promised them peace.

Her grandmother would have known what to do. That thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome—old Maren Blackwood, carted off raving about crowns and kings, the family shame, the reason her grandchildren had split like a dropped plate. Serah had built her whole life on the certainty that the old woman had been mad. Lately, in the dead hours, other thoughts crept in. *The king hasn't appeared in public in three years. The king's speeches are read by heralds. The king granted Vessik's order directly, in black wax.* Madness explained those things comfortably. But madness was starting to require more faith than the alternative.

She was not here to think about that. She was here for one thing only.

Serah took the lamp from its hook and held the flame to the corner of the oldest roll.

Paper catches reluctantly at first, then all at once. She watched the fire crawl up the column of names—the water-warming girl, the polite cooper, the midwife with the healing touch—and felt nothing that resembled triumph. It felt like amputation. It felt like finally, after years of carrying it, setting down something heavy, and discovering the weight had grown into her shoulder.

The smoke was thick and sweet and wrong. She fed the second roll to the flames, then the third, working quickly, methodically, feeding pages in so the fire wouldn't flare and singe her sleeve and leave evidence. Copies existed—of course copies existed, in the Hall of Records, in the citadel vaults—but the master rolls were the legal instrument, the document of record, and without them the registrations were unsigned, unenforceable, *arguable*. Lawyers could fight over copies. They could not fight over ash.

When it was done she stood in the smoke with watering eyes and thought, absurdly, of Paloma. He would have had a joke for this moment. He always had jokes for the moments that didn't allow them. *Burning the family Bible now, Serah? What's next, kicking beggars?* Eleven years since she'd heard his voice, and it still showed up uninvited at exactly the wrong times.

She took the lamp and left the annex, closing the door behind her on the smell of burnt paper and freed names.

* * *

The summons came at dawn, as she had known it would.

Not a summons—an invitation, which was worse. Black wax, no seal, delivered by a palace courier who did not wait for a reply. *The Lord Protector requests the presence of Investigator Grey at the Bell Gate, ninth hour, regarding the matter of the registry.*

The Lord Protector. Not the Marshal of Hunters, not the Master of Records. The palace itself had noticed overnight. Either they had eyes in the registry office—which meant Pell, poor trembling Pell—or they had simply been waiting for someone to do what she had done, the way a trap waits.

She dressed in her grey coat and pinned her investigator's badge and looked at herself in the mirror for a long moment. There was ash under her fingernails that no amount of scrubbing had fully removed. She turned her hands over, studying them, these hands that had signed edicts and broken arms and burned four hundred names to set them free.

*You're either a traitor or the first honest officer this city has had in a century,* she told her reflection. *Possibly both. Possibly the same thing.*

At the Bell Gate, the morning crowd parted around her the way it always did, and she climbed the steps to the proclamation platform where the Lord Protector's chamberlain waited with a folded paper and a face like a closed door. Behind him, two hunters she didn't recognize flanked a small figure—a boy, fourteen maybe, wrists bound, hair matted, wearing the look of someone who had already learned that crying made it worse.

"Investigator Grey." The chamberlain's voice carried pleasantly across the square. "The crown thanks you for your diligent oversight of the registry. It has come to our attention that certain records were destroyed last night. Arson. Sabotage." He unfolded the paper. "His Majesty's investigators are instructed to identify the perpetrator and bring them to justice. You, of all people, understand the gravity of tampering with the instruments of royal law."

"I do," Serah said.

"Good." The chamberlain smiled, and it was the smile of a man reading from a script written by something that was not a man. "Then you'll want to begin immediately. We've taken the liberty of providing your first lead." He gestured to the boy. "A registry clerk's apprentice. Found fleeing the archive annex shortly after midnight. He confesses to the destruction of the records. He will be processed accordingly."

The boy's eyes found hers. There was no hope in them—only a dull, practiced terror, the look of someone who had been coached on what confession to give. *Found fleeing.* Or dragged there. Or simply chosen, because a clerk's apprentice was expendable and an investigator's guilt was useful.

Serah looked at the boy, and at the chamberlain's smiling mouth, and at the crowd below holding its breath, and she understood with perfect clarity that this was not an investigation. It was a test. The Crown—whatever wore the Crown—had noticed the ash, and was offering her a stranger's corpse to stand on, and watching to see if she would take it.

"He confesses," the chamberlain prompted.

Serah descended one step, then another, until she stood level with the boy. Up close she could see flour dust still caught in the collar of his shirt. A baker's son, then. A dockworker's neighbor. One of the invisible.

"What is your name?" she asked him quietly.

"Osric, Investigator." His voice cracked. "Osric Fen."

"Well, Osric Fen." She turned back to the chamberlain, and heard her own voice go flat and cold and steady, the voice she'd used at eleven years old to tell her brother he was being dramatic, the voice of the law she had served and was now, step by step, leaving behind. "This investigation begins with me. And the first thing I'll be examining is how a registry clerk's apprentice came to be alone in a locked annex at midnight—under whose authority, and on whose orders. Because in my experience, confessions that arrive gift-wrapped at dawn usually have someone's fingerprints on them besides the confessed."

The chamberlain's smile did not waver, but something behind his eyes shifted, the way water shifts when something large passes beneath it.

"Careful, Investigator," he said softly. "The Crown sees everything."

"Then it saw who really burned the registry," Serah said, and turned, and walked down into the crowd, knowing that behind her the boy Osric was watching, and the chamberlain was watching, and whatever wore the king's face atop the palace hill had just added her name to a very different kind of list.
