## 8. The Guild That Wasn't

Maren Ostwick washed other people's linens for a living, and she did it the way she did everything—thoroughly, without complaint, and with a certain grim satisfaction in the scrubbing.

I'd been coming to Laundress Row for three days before I worked up the nerve to say anything beyond *two shirts, please*. There's a particular kind of cowardice that comes with age and responsibility. When I was nineteen I'd have marched up to her and announced myself like a festival banner. Now, at twenty-six, I stood at the edge of her washing yard for the better part of an hour, rehearsing opening lines like a suitor, while she wrung out sheets that were whiter than anything had a right to be in Corvane.

That was the tell, actually. The sheets. Nobody got linen that white with lye and river water alone. Somebody was persuading the stains to leave, politely but firmly, the way you'd persuade a drunk uncle out of a wedding. And the water in her basins never went grey. It just... gave up.

"You're going to wear a groove in my cobbles, boy," she said without looking up. "Either come in or go haunt somebody else's doorstep."

I came in. My legs decided before my pride did.

She was smaller than I remembered from Grandmother's stories—or rather, she was exactly as small as I should have expected, because Grandmother had described her as "a sparrow with a ledger for a soul," and sparrows are not imposing creatures. Maren Ostwick had iron-grey hair pinned back with what looked like a knitting needle, forearms like dock rope, and eyes the color of a winter sky deciding whether to snow. She pointed a dripping hand at a stool.

"Sit. You have her jaw."

"Her—" 

"The Blackwood jaw. Stubborn as a mule's opinion of fences." She wrung out another sheet, twisted it until the water screamed out of it. "You're Odile's grandson. The charm-seller. Paloma."

The name of my grandmother in a stranger's mouth hit me somewhere under the ribs. "You knew her."

"I trained her. Which means I've now outlived everyone I ever taught, which is either a great honor or a great failure of planning." She hung the sheet on a line with a snap. "You dug up the cache."

It wasn't a question. Word travels fast among people who have spent forty years pretending not to exist. "The journal," I said. "The Hollow Crown. The keys."

"Mm." She studied me the way a butcher studies a carcass, deciding which cuts are worth the knife. "And you came here because you want me to tell you it isn't true. That the old woman was mad, that the king is fine, that all this is a story you can put back in the ground and sleep soundly above."

"I came here because I want someone to tell me what to do," I admitted. "Those aren't quite the same thing."

Something moved across her face—not a smile, but the place where a smile might have lived once, before life evicted it. "Honest. Good. Liars wash up badly." She untied her apron, hung it on a nail, and sat down across from me on an overturned bucket, which somehow made her more formidable, not less. "Then here is what you do first. You listen. And when I'm finished, if you still want the keys, you'll wish you didn't. But you'll take them anyway, because that's the Blackwood curse—we do the necessary thing and then spend sixty years apologizing for it."

* * *

What she told me took the rest of the afternoon, and I will set it down here as straight as she told it to me, because a lie of omission is how guilds rot in the first place.

"You think the Veil War was mages against the crown," she began. "Everyone thinks that. It's a tidy story. Rebels with fire in their hands, a righteous king who bound them, magic outlawed forever after. Very clean. Very wrong.

"The Blackwood guild served the crown. Not reluctantly. Not under duress. We were the crown's mages—the court guild, chartered by Aldric's grandfather, sworn by blood-oath into the royal line itself. Every healing in the palace, every ward on the walls, every harvest blessing and storm-turning, that was us. Four hundred years of service. We weren't hiding in Corvane's shadows, boy. We built the shadows. We built half the city."

I thought of the guildhouse ruins in Saltrow, the ones I'd played in as a child, thinking them just old. "Then why—"

"Why does no one remember? Because we made sure they wouldn't. After the war, after the binding, the surviving elders wove a forgetting over our own name. Better to be nobody than to be *them*. And 'them' is what we became." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Here is the part that will keep you up nights.

"Aldric wasn't a tyrant. He was a good man. That's not sentiment; that's record. He ended a famine in his third year by opening the granaries against his council's screaming. He settled the border wars by going to the negotiating table himself, unarmed, twice, knowing both times he might not come back. He took gifted children into palace schooling when the provinces wanted them drowned. When the plague came through the low quarters, he carried the dead himself, because there weren't enough hands and he said a king who won't carry his people has no right to rule them."

Her voice had gone quiet in the way voices go quiet around graves.

"And then something found him. We never learned its name—it may not have had one before we gave it one. It came out of the deep places where the old bindings had worn thin, and it doesn't take bodies the way your grandmother's journal says demons do. Demons ride you. This thing *replaces* you. Slowly. It wears a person down to a shape and moves in, and it feeds on magic the way a lamprey feeds on blood—from the inside, patiently, without killing the host, because the host is the door.

"We saw it happening. The court mages saw it first—Aldric forgetting names, standing too long at windows, smiling at nothing. Then the smiles started lasting too long. Then the edicts changed. The man who opened the granaries began closing them. The man who sheltered gifted children signed the first proscription. We told ourselves it was illness. Grief—he'd lost his queen that same winter. Anything but the truth, because the truth had teeth.

"Aldric knew. That's the thing no one wants carved on any monument. In his last lucid hour—and we have testimony, elder to elder, unbroken down to me—he summoned the guild's inner circle to the throne room, and the thing wearing him let him surface long enough to speak. Do you know what he asked us?"

I shook my head. My mouth had gone dry somewhere around *the thing wearing him*.

"He begged," Maren said. "The King of Corvane, on his knees on his own dais, begging six mages to bind him—to bind the throne, the crown, the whole royal line's magic—before the thing finished eating him and wore him out into the world wearing his face. 'Don't save me,' he said. 'Save the kingdom *from* me.' And we did it. We were strong enough, then—all six circles together, the full inherited weight of four hundred years. We bound the king's soul beneath the Crown, sealed the entity inside the shell of him, and cut Corvane off from its own magic to starve the parasite. The Veil War, the outlawry, the hunts—all of it, every law and every pyre since, is the aftershock of one act of mercy performed so badly it became a prison."

The yard was very silent. Somewhere beyond the wall, a cart rattled past, and a woman called out a price for fish, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, oblivious, which suddenly seemed like the most obscene luxury imaginable.

"So the hunters," I said slowly, "the executions, Serah's entire career—they're all enforcing a dead man's emergency measure."

"Worse." Maren's voice hardened. "The binding was meant to hold for a lifetime. A human lifetime. Aldric should have died decades ago, soul freed, entity starved into nothing. But the binding needs tending, and the only ones who could tend it were us—and we ran. After the binding, the guild broke. Some fled, some were hunted by the very laws we'd inspired, some simply couldn't live with what they'd done and walked into the sea. The tending rites lapsed. And a starving parasite is a patient parasite. It learned to feed another way. Why wait for wild magic to trickle through the Veil when you can farm it?"

"Farm it."

"What do you think the registries are, boy? What do you think the hunts *harvest*?" She didn't wait for me to answer. "Every gifted person taken, every execution—I used to believe those were waste. Atrocity, yes, but senseless. It isn't senseless. The Crown takes them alive first. The questioning, the holding cells, the weeks between arrest and burning—that's the feeding. It drinks the gift out of them through the binding's channels, and what walks to the pyre is already hollow. Your grandmother figured it out too late to stop it, soon enough to write it down and bury it where a stubborn grandchild would dig."

My hands were shaking. I pressed them flat against my knees and thought about the neighbor boy, Tam, sixteen years old, who could make bread rise with a touch and who had been taken in the night three weeks ago. Sixteen. They'd held him for eleven days before the burning. Eleven days of—

"Don't," Maren said, not unkindly. "Grief is fuel, but it burns wild. Bank it. You'll need it steady."

"Why tell me all this?" I asked. "Why now, why me?"

"Because the binding is failing. Can you feel it? Of course you can't—you're barely trained. But the Veil thins a little more each year. The Crown grows stronger. Another decade and it won't need hosts at all; it'll drink the whole city, gifted and plain alike, and Corvane will be a beautiful hollow shell with a smiling corpse on the throne. The unbinding rite exists. Three keys, as your grandmother wrote. Salt, iron, bone. Gathered and spoken rightly, they release the king's soul and unmake the parasite's anchor. But—" and here she paused, and I would give a great deal to unhear what came next, "—there is a cost, and I will not soften it. The binding was laid with the full inherited power of the Blackwood line. Only that same power can undo it. Whoever speaks the unbinding spends the guild's gift entirely. All of it. Forever. Every scrap of inheritance our line carried for four hundred years, burned in one rite."

"That's—" I started to calculate, and stopped. "That's my gift. Whatever I actually have, buried under the sleight-of-hand and the luck-charms. Gone."

"Yes."

"And if I don't?"

"Then in ten years, no one in Corvane has anything, including their selves." She rose, went to the line, and took down a dry sheet, folding it with fierce precision. "Your grandmother refused the choice for thirty years. She told herself the journal was enough, that passing it on absolved her. It didn't. It won't absolve you either. Nothing absolves us, Paloma. We were complicit. The guild that built the light of this city also built its cage, and when the man in the cage begged us to end it properly, we flinched, and ran, and let our cowardice become law. That is your inheritance. Not power. Shame, and a debt."

* * *

I stayed until dark. She fed me barley soup that tasted like penance, and I asked the questions I'd been swallowing all afternoon.

"The other elders. Grandmother's journal named five. Are any left besides you?"

"No. Elder Caspian died in the second purge, refusing to burn quietly—he took three hunters with him, which I neither praise nor condemn. Elder Wren crossed the sea. Elder Thessaly..." Maren's jaw tightened. "Thessaly went to the palace, alone, twelve years ago, to try a partial unbinding. Her gift wasn't sufficient. The Crown kept her alive for a month afterward. What it sent back to us was not Thessaly. We burned what it sent back ourselves, and I have not slept well since."

I filed that away in the growing drawer of horrors, next to Tam's eleven days.

"The keys," I said. "Salt, iron, bone. Where—"

"Salt lies in the drowned chapel under the harbor—your grandmother marked it in the journal's margin, the tide-chapel of Saint Verity. Iron is in the hunter citadel's vault, under the heaviest ward the guild ever wove, because we wove the citadel's wards ourselves, once, in prouder days. Irony is free in Corvane; everything else costs blood." She set down her spoon. "Bone is the worst, and I'll not speak of it tonight. One horror per meal is my rule."

"And you? Will you come with me? Teach me? I can't even feel the Veil properly, you said so yourself—"

"I will teach you what I can," she said, "and I will come as far as an old woman's legs allow. But hear me, Paloma Blackwood." She took my wrist, and her grip was iron despite the sparrow frame, and for one moment I felt the weight of her—four hundred years of guild pressing down through one laundress's hand. "There is one more thing in your inheritance, and it is not shame. The Blackwoods were oath-sworn to the crown by blood. That oath never expired. It sleeps in you the way your gift sleeps. When you stand before the throne and speak the unbinding, you will not be a rebel breaking the king's law. You will be the last servant of a dead king, keeping a promise made to a dying man: *save the kingdom from me.*"

"Do you believe I can do it?"

"I believe you're stubborn as a mule's opinion of fences," she said, gathering my bowl, "and that your grandmother bet the family name on worse odds." At the door she paused, silhouetted against her yard of impossible-white sheets. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow we begin. And Paloma—if anyone asks, you came for your shirts."

I walked home through streets that had changed shape around me. Every wall I passed, I now knew, had been raised by guild hands. Every law I'd hidden from had grown from our seed. I had spent my whole life believing I was the descendant of victims, hiding from the descendants of villains, and the truth was uglier and stranger: the villains and the victims shared my blood, and the debt had come due in my generation, addressed to me by name.

At my window, I took out the journal and read Grandmother's final entry again. *If you are reading this, the choice is yours now. Forgive us if you can. Use us if you must.*

Below it, in fresher ink, someone had added a single line I had never noticed—a note in a different hand, cramped and hurried:

*The citadel vault key changes location monthly. Find Tovan Rell. Tell him Thessaly's ghost says the debt stands.*

I read it three times. Then I blew out the candle, lay down in the dark, and did not sleep at all.
