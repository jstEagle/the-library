## 9. The Second Key: Iron

There are three ways to betray an order you've served for nineteen years.

The first is loudly — a speech, a refusal, a thrown badge. Dramatic, memorable, and fatal within the hour. The second is quietly: you simply stop showing up, drift to some coastal town, and drink yourself into a new shape of man. I'd been drafting that version in my head for two years, ever since the Marlowe executions, and I'll admit it had a certain appeal.

The third way is the one nobody warns you about. You do it at midnight, with a stolen keyring, leading a charm-seller with soft hands down into the vaults beneath the citadel where you spent your whole life learning which screams meant confession and which meant something worse.

That's the way you can't take back. That's the way I chose.

* * *

"You're sweating," Paloma whispered.

"It's a cellar under a fortress full of witch-hunters. If I weren't sweating, you should worry."

We stood in the wine cellar of the Hunter's Citadel, which sounds grander than it is. The citadel had been a monastery once, back when Corvane still pretended its magic was holy instead of merely useful, and the monks had dug their cellars deep and cool and honest. The hunters kept the wine. They also kept other things below, things that didn't care about temperature.

I pressed my palm against the third barrel from the east wall and found the iron ring set flush into the stone behind it, exactly where it had been eleven years ago, when I was a raw recruit assigned to inventory duty and too bored to report the door I'd found. Boredom, I've since learned, is the most dangerous virtue a hunter can have. It makes you look at things.

The ring turned. The barrel rolled aside on rails someone had oiled within the year — which meant someone else knew about this door, and I filed that thought away in the drawer marked *later, if we live*.

"Eleven years," Paloma said, peering into the dark beyond. "You found this when you were what, twenty?"

"Nineteen. And I reported nothing, because reporting it would have meant inventorying whatever's down there, and I could hear it breathing."

"Breathing."

"A figure of speech." Mostly. "Stay close. Touch nothing with your bare hands. And if I say the word *vespers*, you run for the tunnel and you don't argue."

"What happens at vespers?"

"The wardens change shift, and the corridor above us fills with men whose entire personality is a grudge against people like you."

He nodded, and I watched him tuck his fear away the way he probably tucked his charms up his sleeve — practiced, economical, gone. I'd arrested forty-one gifted folk in my career. I'd never once watched one be brave in real time before. It rearranged something in my chest, and I decided not to look at it too closely until we were above ground.

* * *

The passage sloped down through stone that got older as we went — monastery blocks, then older masonry, then sections of wall that predated Corvane itself, fitted without mortar by hands that had believed very hard about something. Our lantern threw our shadows long and wrong.

Paloma kept up well for a man whose trade was standing in markets being underestimated. But I caught him touching the walls as we walked, fingertips light on the stone, and after the third time I said, "Stop that."

"Why?"

"Because the vaults are warded, and wards notice attention. You're gift-touched. Everything down here is designed to notice exactly you."

He pulled his hand back like he'd touched a stove. "You know a lot about this for a man who hunted us."

"I inventoried it. For three years. Do you know what's in the lower vaults? Neither did I. Nobody does. The manifest lists *reliquaries, assorted*. Eleven hundred crates of assorted reliquaries, and every warden who's asked to see them has been reassigned to the border forts." I lifted the lantern higher. "The crown doesn't collect evidence, Blackwood. It collects *material*. There's a difference, and the difference is that evidence gets looked at."

He was quiet for a stretch. Then: "Tovan. The salt key worked. In the chapel — when I held it, the water went still. Not calm. *Still*, like it was listening. What does the iron one do?"

"I don't know. I know where it is, not what it is. Third vault, second shelf, sealed case marked with the guild sigil." I glanced at him. "Your guild's sigil. Which has always struck me as either a kindness or a joke, depending on how much you trust dead elders."

"I don't trust anyone anymore," he said pleasantly. "It's very freeing."

* * *

The vault door was iron, naturally, six inches thick, with a lock that predated the lockmakers' guild. I had three keys for it. Two were legitimate, lifted from Warden-Captain Hess's belt while he lectured recruits about vigilance — a sentence I intend to treasure for the rest of my presumably short life. The third was mine, cut from wax impressions over four patient weeks.

The lock took all three and a quarter turn of force and opened with a sound like a sigh from a very large man.

Inside, the air changed. Colder. Drier. The kind of dry that means preservation, that means someone is spending money to keep things from rotting. Shelves ran back into the dark, and the lantern showed me dust-free glass cases, row upon row, each labeled in a clerk's careful hand.

Paloma stopped walking.

"Tovan," he said. His voice had gone strange. "These labels. Read them."

I read them. *Charm, luck, minor. Confiscated, Dock Ward.* *Knot-spell, household. Confiscated, Weavers' Row.* *Blessing-cord, funerary. Confiscated, Bell Gate parish.*

They weren't reliquaries. They were *charms*. Thousands of them. Harmless ones — the luck-pieces and knot-spells and stitched blessings that grandmothers made and dockworkers bought for a copper, the exact harmless trade Paloma plied at the harbor. Every case had a confiscation date. The oldest went back decades.

"They never melted them down," Paloma said. He'd gone pale. "All these years, they told everyone confiscated charms were destroyed. Burned. That's what they announced."

"Announcing isn't doing. Ask any government." But my mouth had gone dry too, because I'd written destruction certificates myself. I'd signed forms saying *items rendered inert by fire* while some clerk downstairs signed forms saying *items received, condition good*. Somewhere between our signatures, the truth had been living in a cold room, catalogued and kept.

"Why keep them?" Paloma whispered. He touched his own sleeve, where he carried his working charms, and I saw him do the arithmetic I was doing. Bound magic feeds something. The Crown fed on bound magic. And a confiscated charm is magic already caught, already collared, already—

"It's a larder," I said. "This whole floor is a larder."

He looked at me. "Then every arrest—"

"—was a harvest. Yes." I'd known the hunts were cruel. I'd told myself cruel wasn't corrupt, that order required cruelty sometimes, that the alternative was chaos. Standing in that cold room among eleven hundred crates of *assorted reliquaries*, I finally understood that I hadn't been keeping order. I'd been a farmhand.

"We should burn it," Paloma said suddenly. Fiercely. "All of it. Free it all."

"And tell the Crown exactly where we've been and what we value? No." I put my hand on his shoulder. It was shaking. So was mine; I chose not to mention it. "We take the key. We come back with an army. That's the only version of this where it matters."

He breathed. Nodded. And I understood then why Maren Ostwick had chosen him, out of everyone the Blackwood line had left. Not because he was powerful. Because he could want to burn everything and choose the harder patience instead.

* * *

Third vault. Second shelf. The case bore the Blackwood sigil — a veiled hand, open — scratched into the lead seal by someone in a hurry, or someone angry, or both.

Paloma broke the seal himself. I offered, but he said, "It's my inheritance. Let me at least be the one who touches it," and there was nothing to say to that.

Inside, on a bed of rotted velvet, lay a bar of black iron no longer than my forearm. Unremarkable. Ugly, even. The kind of thing you'd use to prop a door.

When Paloma reached for it, the lantern flame leaned toward him. All of it, every flame in the room, bending like grass in wind that wasn't there. And the iron — I swear this on whatever I still believe in — the iron *warmed*. I felt it from three feet away, a heat like a forge coming awake, and the dry preserved air filled with a smell of hot metal and old blood.

Paloma picked it up and didn't flinch, which is more than I can say for myself.

"Heavy?" I managed.

"No," he said, staring at it. "It's nothing. It weighs nothing at all." Then, quieter: "It's humming. Like it knows the salt key. Like it's been waiting so long it's forgotten how to stop."

"Then let's not make it wait longer. Vespers in ten minutes."

We wrapped the key in wool, wool in oilcloth, oilcloth in the satchel against Paloma's chest, and we started back the way we came, and for maybe ninety seconds I believed we might actually do this cleanly.

I want that noted for posterity: for ninety seconds, I believed.

* * *

The trouble was the barrel. Specifically, the fact that when we came back up through the hidden door, the wine cellar was not empty.

A junior warden named Colm sat on a cask not ten feet from our exit, eating bread and reading a letter by candlelight, and I will go to my grave grateful that he was a lazy, incurious boy, because he looked up, saw me, and said, "Oh — Sergeant Tovan. Thought you'd gone home."

"Inventory," I said, with the flat boredom of a man who has said *inventory* a thousand times. "Lower vaults. Don't write it up, it's all catalogued."

And here is where I made my error, and I want to be honest about it, because the whole point of betrayal is that you don't get to lie to yourself anymore afterward. I looked at Colm. Just a half-second too long. A man checking whether a loose thread will hold.

Colm was lazy, but he wasn't stupid. His eyes went past me. To the stranger in the dark coat. To the stranger's face, which even in shadow had the particular hungry watchfulness of a man who has spent years pretending to be less than he is.

Colm stood up. The letter fell. "That's—" he began, and reached for his whistle.

I hit him. Open hand, then closed fist, then the cask-edge, in that order, fast and ugly, and he went down heavy and stayed down, and I knelt beside him with two fingers at his throat while Paloma stood frozen in the doorway with a stolen god's fingerbone of iron clutched to his chest.

"He's breathing," I said. "Out cold, but breathing. Give it five minutes, he wakes with a headache and a story nobody believes."

"You didn't have to—" Paloma started.

"Yes, I did, and we can discuss my moral development later. Move."

But the whistle. Colm's whistle had fallen from his hand, and it had landed on stone, and it had made a sound. A small one. A chirp, really.

Down in those cellars, sound carries like guilt.

* * *

We ran.

Not the way back — the way back now had boots coming down it, torchlight swinging along the walls, voices calling the alarm in the clipped cadence of men who drill for this and enjoy the drilling. I hauled Paloma left instead, into the old monastery galleries, through a doorway I'd found during another bored afternoon years ago, up a stair that smelled of pigeon and centuries.

"They'll seal the gates," Paloma gasped.

"They'll try. Gates are for people who use gates." I shoved him through a gap in the gallery wall where the mortar had crumbled, out onto the citadel's inner roofline, three stories above the yard. Below us, the night was filling with hunters, torches blooming like terrible flowers. Someone was shouting my name. My actual name. *Sergeant Tovan. Sergeant Tovan, report.*

"There he is!" From the yard. From a window across the court. From everywhere.

"Roof," I said. "East slope, the shingles are slick, don't stop, don't look down, and whatever you hear behind us—"

"What will we hear behind us?"

"Me, apologizing to several architectural decisions."

We went over the edge together. The shingles were slick. I fell twice, caught the gutter once, and lost my hat forever, which stung more than the gash it left on my palm. Paloma moved like a market thief — quick, low, economical — and I remember thinking, absurdly, *his grandmother taught him that too, probably,* right before a crossbow bolt buried itself in the shingle where my head had been.

Below, the yard gate was closing. Beyond it, the street, the fog off the harbor, freedom of a sort.

"Hold the gate!" someone bellowed. "Nobody leaves! Seal it!"

And that's when Paloma did the thing I'll be thinking about for the rest of my life, however long the rest of my life turns out to be. He stopped on the roof's edge, pulled the wool-wrapped iron key halfway from his satchel — just enough to bare it to the night — and held it up.

Every torch in the yard guttered. Every one. Forty flames bending toward him like a congregation, and the iron singing — I heard it from thirty feet away, a low note like a bell struck underwater — and the gate mechanism, the great counterweighted iron portcullis the citadel was proud of, groaned and *shuddered* and jammed half-fallen, rusted solid in a single heartbeat, as if it had been aging fifty years in one breath.

Iron obeys iron, Maren Ostwick had apparently told him. I hadn't been there, but I intended to kiss that old woman's hands if I ever saw her again.

"GO," Paloma shouted, and we went, down the outside stair, through the gap in the half-jammed gate, into the fog and the streets of Corvane with the whole citadel howling behind us.

* * *

We didn't stop running until the Tanner's Reach, three districts south, where the alleys are so narrow the moon gives up on them. We leaned against a wall, side by side, wheezing like a pair of broken accordions.

Above the rooftops behind us, the beacon fire on the citadel tower caught and climbed — the signal that meant *hunters abroad, close the wards, find them*.

"They saw my face," I said. "Half the yard knows me by name. By morning every warden in Corvane will have my description and a bonus attached to it."

"Yes."

"I had a pension," I said. "Nineteen years. I had a modest cottage saved for, in the hill country. I had a reputation that was mostly lies but at least it was *my* lies."

Paloma slid down the wall until he was sitting in the filth, laughing — actually laughing, quiet and cracked and real. "You had a hat," he said. "You loved that hat."

"I did love that hat."

He fished the wrapped key from his satchel and held it between us. Even through the wool, I could feel it. Warm. Patient. Humming its one low note, like something that had waited a hundred years in the dark and could wait a little longer but wanted us to know it hadn't enjoyed it.

"Two keys," he said. "Salt and iron. One left."

"Bone," I said. "Maren said bone. And there's exactly one place in this city with bones important enough to bind a king." I looked at him, and he looked at me, and we both arrived at it together, the way you arrive at a cliff edge together.

"The palace," he said.

"The palace," I agreed. "Which we just informed, by beacon fire and jammed portcullis, that we exist."

Far off, across the sleeping city, the beacon burned, and somewhere in the dark between us and it, the hunt had already begun to spread out like spilled ink.
