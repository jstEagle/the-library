## 10. The Hunt Closes

The summons came at dawn, which in my experience is when bad news likes to arrive. Bad news has never once had the courtesy to wait until I'd finished my tea.

"Chief Investigator," said the courier, a boy of perhaps fifteen with the flat eyes of someone who'd learned early that delivering orders was safer than reading them. "Commander Vess requests your presence at the hunter citadel. Immediately."

"Immediately," I repeated, because repeating a word back at a courier makes you feel like you have some say in the matter.

"Yes, ma'am."

I looked down at my tea. It was still too hot to drink. I drank it anyway, because Serah Grey does not waste time and does not waste tea, and if those two principles ever come into conflict, the tea loses.

* * *

The citadel's vaults sat three levels below the harbor line, behind doors of black iron that were supposed to be impregnable. Someone had disagreed with them on this point.

I stood in the antechamber with Commander Vess—a man built like a door himself, all slab and hinge and no handle—and surveyed the damage. The vault door hung open at an angle that suggested it had been persuaded rather than forced. The wards that should have flayed an intruder's skin from his bones had been peeled back like orange rind. And in the center of the room, where the iron key had rested for longer than anyone living could remember, there was nothing but a square of dust-free stone and a single boot print.

"Well?" Vess said.

"Well," I said, "someone took your key."

"I can see that, Chief Investigator. What I cannot see is how."

That made two of us. I crouched by the ward anchors. They hadn't been broken. They'd been *unpicked*—each thread of binding drawn out in sequence, patient as a laundress teasing a knot from wet wool. And there was something else, something small and wrong about the air near the anchors, a faint sweetness like burnt sugar.

I knew that sweetness.

I had spent eleven years cataloguing charm-work for the hunt. Every registered practitioner left a signature in their craft the way a forger leaves a tremor in his hand. Dock-charms smelled of tar. Hearth-charms of ash and rosemary. And this particular style of unpicking—elegant, economical, each thread released rather than cut, because cutting a thread of binding leaves a scar and scars are evidence—

This was Blackwood work.

My stomach did something slow and cold, like a fish turning over in dark water.

"Chief Investigator." Vess's voice came from somewhere above me. "You've found something."

I stood up. I put my face into the shape it needed to be: competent, incurious, grey as my coat. "The thief was trained. Guild-trained, or close enough. The wards weren't broken; they were unwoven. That takes years of practice and a specific lineage of technique." I paused, letting him draw his own conclusions, because men like Vess trust conclusions they reach themselves far more than ones handed to them.

"The Blackwood guild is dust," he said. "Wiped out in the purges. You told me so yourself."

"The guild is dead. Its techniques aren't. Techniques get passed down. Techniques survive in attics and cellars and grandmothers' journals." My voice was steady. I was almost proud of how steady it was. "Whoever did this learned from someone who learned from the Blackwoods."

Vess grunted, satisfied. He wanted a dead guild to blame; dead guilds couldn't object. "Find them. The Crown wants the key recovered and the thieves hanged before the month turns. You have full authority. Full resources." He leaned closer, and his breath smelled of the citadel's boiled meat and something underneath it, faintly sweet, that I chose not to name. "And Chief Investigator—the Crown is watching this one closely. Fail here and your career ends. Succeed and—" he smiled, and the smile went on a half-second too long, the way smiles do in this building lately, "—there will be rewards."

I bowed. I walked out. In the corridor, my hands shook, and I watched them shake with the detached interest of a woman watching someone else's hands.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being good at your job: your job gets better at using you.

I joined the hunt at nineteen, after Grandmother died and after Paloma left—or after I left Paloma, depending on which night I'm honest about, and honesty has been arriving later and later these days, like a guest who knows he isn't welcome. I joined because the hunt promised order. Corvane after the Veil War was a city of burned houses and unmarked graves, and I had grown up watching my grandmother whisper over charms while neighbors disappeared, and I wanted, more than anything, for the world to make sense. Rules. Registers. A place for everything and everything in its place.

Magic outside the law was chaos. That was the whole argument. That was the entire architecture of my life, load-bearing wall and all.

And now I stood in a vault beneath the harbor, looking at the most beautiful piece of ward-work I had ever seen, thinking: *that's my brother's handwriting.*

Not literally. Paloma doesn't write wards; he weaves them, always has, since we were children stealing moments in grandmother's kitchen while she pretended not to see. But every craftsman has a signature, and his was always the same: he *never breaks anything*. Not locks, not promises, not threads. When other children pried things open, Paloma would sit with a stuck drawer for an hour until he understood why it was stuck, and then it would simply open, as if it had decided to. As if it trusted him.

The vault door had decided to open.

I told myself it could be coincidence. There must be dozens of practitioners descended from Blackwood training scattered through Corvane's slums—I'd arrested two of them myself last spring. I told myself that grief and guilt are both liars, that they dress coincidence up as fate because fate feels less lonely. I told myself all of this while requesting the scene reports from every unsolved charm-related incident in the past year, and spreading them across my desk like a fortune-teller's cards, and finding, in the corner of a report from the drowned chapel in the harbor, a sketch of thread-marks pulled loose in sequence, uncut, unscarred.

Trusted open.

I sat in my office until the candles burned down to puddles, and I let myself think the thought I'd been holding at arm's length since dawn:

*Paloma is alive. Paloma is gifted. Paloma stole the iron key.*

And beneath that thought, quieter, in the voice I use for truths I'm not ready to say aloud: *and if he did, then he knows something. About the Crown. About why the hunts changed last year. About why the registers stopped being lists and started being harvests.*

Because I had noticed. I want that on the record, wherever records go when no one reads them. I had noticed that the orders shifted eighteen months ago—from *find and register* to *find and hold*, from trials to quiet transport, from justice to logistics. I had noticed that the prisoners taken to the palace for "assessment" never sent letters home, and their families stopped asking, and the not-asking had a smooth, glazed quality to it, like everything else in this city lately. I had noticed Commander Vess smiling too long. I had noticed the king's portraits repainted every season because the face kept going *wrong*, somehow, though no painter could say how.

I had noticed, and I had filed it under *not my concern*, and the file had grown so heavy it was crushing me, and I had called the crushing weight *duty*.

* * *

On the third day, they brought me a suspect.

A girl, sixteen maybe, caught selling luck-charms off Salt Row. Small, sharp-faced, hands blistered from dock work. They put her in the interrogation room and left us alone, because that was my method—Vess preferred fire and irons, but fire produces screaming and screaming produces whatever the torturer wants to hear, whereas silence produces truth, eventually, if you can stand the waiting.

I could stand the waiting. I'd had practice.

She sat across the table from me with her chin up and her knees shaking, and I set down the charge sheet, and I looked at her, and I saw—God help me—I saw a girl who reminded me of no one, because the person she reminded me of was me at sixteen, sitting in a kitchen learning knots of binding from a woman the neighborhood called mad.

"Name," I said.

"Marta Quill."

"You were selling luck-charms. Threaded, not spoken. Low-power, low-signature. Good work, actually. Whoever taught you was careful."

Her eyes flickered. Fear and pride fighting for the same ground.

"Here's what happens now," I said. "You sign the confession, you register, you go on the list, and you go home. Or you don't sign, and you go to the palace for assessment, and—" I stopped. Because I had been about to say *and you'll be fine*, and the lie lodged in my throat like a fishbone.

Marta Quill watched me stop. Sixteen-year-olds are better at noticing lies than we are at telling them; they haven't yet learned to look away from the telling.

"What happens at assessment?" she asked.

"You're held. Questioned. Released."

"That's not what happened to Denny Farrow."

Denny Farrow. The neighbor boy taken the night the edicts went up, back when this all began. I remembered his mother's face at the registry office, weeks later, asking when her son would come home, and the clerk's smile going on half a second too long, and the mother's expression smoothing over into something placid and wrong, like a pond freezing from the edges inward.

"No," I heard myself say. "It isn't."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above us, the citadel ticked and settled, a building digesting.

"Then what do I do?" Marta whispered.

And here is the moment I should tell you about, because it is the moment everything turned, though it didn't look like turning. It looked like a woman sitting very still at a table, doing arithmetic. On one side of the ledger: my oath, my rank, my coat, eleven years of order, the whole grey architecture of my life. On the other side: a girl with blistered hands, a frozen pond, a vault door that had been trusted open, and a brother I had reported dead to myself years ago who was apparently alive and robbing the Crown.

The arithmetic did not take long. It had, I suspect, been done long ago, in a part of me that files things under *not my concern*.

"Sign the confession," I said. "Register. Go home. Then lose the charm-work for a season—no, don't argue, just *lose it*. And if a woman comes to find you, tall, grey coat, tired eyes—you never saw her before in your life."

"And if she asks about the charms?"

"Tell her they were your grandmother's design. Tell her your grandmother learned from the old guilds. Let her draw her own conclusions." I stood, took the confession, and blotted it dry. At the door I paused. "Marta. Do you know what happens to people who cross the Crown?"

"Everyone knows what happens."

"Do you know *why*?"

She shook her head. Neither did I, not fully, not yet. But I was closer than I'd been that morning, and the distance was closing, and it closed along a road paved with things I'd refused to look at.

I signed her release myself. Vess would call it efficiency. I called it the first theft of my career, and I found, to my surprise, that it fit my hand even better than the badge did.

* * *

That night I went back to the vault alone.

Unauthorized, off-record, lamp turned low. I don't know what I expected to find—confession scrawled on the walls, perhaps, or a note addressed to me personally, because guilt makes you egotistical that way. What I found was smaller. In the corner, near the ward anchor, half-hidden in dust: a charm.

A luck-charm. Threaded, not spoken. Woven from cheap harbor twine, the kind sold three-for-a-copper on Salt Row. But the weaving—

I knelt and picked it up and turned it in the lamplight, and my chest did something painful and complicated, because I knew this weave. I had seen it a hundred times in a hundred kitchens. A protective lattice folded around a core of simple luck, redundant strands laid so that if one failed the next would catch—grandmother's pattern, the Blackwood pattern, the one she used to tie into our hair before market days, saying *the world is hard on girls who notice things, so let the world be kind instead.*

Paloma still wove it. All these years. Eleven years of estrangement, of my choosing the hunt over him, of his choosing exile over surrender—and he still wove grandmother's luck-charm, and he had dropped it in a vault beneath the harbor while stealing from the Crown itself.

He'd left it behind the way you leave anything you can't afford to carry but can't bear to throw away.

I sat down on the cold stone floor of the citadel vault, in my grey coat with my grey authority, and I held a three-copper charm in both hands, and I laughed. It came out wrong, more like sobbing, but there was no one to hear the difference, and after a while I wasn't sure there was one.

Because here was the shape of it, finally, unavoidable: my brother was at war with the thing that wore the king. My brother had allies and a plan and three keys to gather—I'd read enough of grandmother's ravings before I ran from her to recognize the pattern; the journal, the keys, the unbinding, it was all there in the madness I'd dismissed at fourteen. And I was the chief investigator of the hunt that was trying to kill him, with full authority and full resources and a commander whose smile went on too long and whose breath smelled sweet as burnt sugar.

Loyalty torn in two? No. That was the comfortable version, the version where the pain is symmetrical and noble. The truth was uglier. The truth was that loyalty had only ever run one way, and I had spent eleven years pointing it at a corpse wearing a crown, and the man I'd actually owed it to had never once stopped leaving luck-charms where I might find them.

I stood. I tucked the charm inside my coat, against my ribs, where grandmother used to say the heart keeps its own accounts.

There was a knock at the vault door—one of Vess's runners, breathless. "Chief Investigator. Commander's compliments. We've had a sighting. Two men fleeing the harbor district toward the old quarter, one of them armed with hunter-issue steel. The Commander believes it's your thieves. He wants pursuit tonight."

Tonight. Of course. The Crown was watching closely, and the Crown did not like to wait, and somewhere in the old quarter, in a city full of laundresses and dockworkers and charm-sellers who had learned to be invisible, my brother was running out of places to hide.

"Tell the Commander I'll lead the pursuit myself," I said.

The runner blinked. "Ma'am?"

"You heard me." I pulled my coat straight. Against my ribs, the little charm pressed warm, absurd, alive. "Get me six riders and the district maps. And runner—if anyone asks which way we went first, tell them the river gate."

The river gate was the wrong direction. The runner wouldn't know that. By the time anyone did, I intended to have already decided which side of my own ledger I was standing on.

I blew out the lamp and climbed toward the surface, toward the horses and the orders and the sister-shaped ruin of my old life, and above me Corvane lay under a sky the color of unpolished iron, and somewhere in it, my brother was carrying a stolen key toward a throne room, and I was going to find him first—not to arrest him.

Whatever happened after that, I'd decide on the ride.
