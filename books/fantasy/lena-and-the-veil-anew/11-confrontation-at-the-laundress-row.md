## 11. Confrontation at the Laundress Row

### Serah

The charm-work told me before the witnesses did.

I stood in the citadel vault at dawn, holding a scrap of wax paper that had once wrapped a luck-charm, and I knew the hand that had folded it. Four creases, corner tucked first, then a small twist at the end—our grandmother's fold, taught to two children on a kitchen table in Saltrow while our father complained about wasted paper. Paloma kept the habit. Paloma kept everything.

"Inspector," said Vess, one of my hunters, breathless from the stairs. "The intruders went out through the cisterns. We've got blood—one of them's wounded—and a witness says the smaller man had river-tan hands. A dockworker, maybe."

A dockworker with clever fingers and a conscience he couldn't afford. I turned the wax paper over. On the back, invisible unless you knew to breathe steam on it, was a tiny drawn spiral—the Blackwood guild mark, the one Grandmother used to scratch into the margins of her ledgers when she thought no one saw.

My brother had been inside the hunter citadel. My brother had robbed us.

"Inspector?" Vess prompted.

"Seal the vault," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "And get me every report on unregistered gifted activity in the lower wards for the past month. Cross-reference with harbor workers."

"You think it's one man?"

I thought it was one boy who used to sleep curled against my back during thunderstorms because he said my heartbeat sounded like rain on a roof. I thought about how he'd looked at me across our grandmother's coffin—no, not a coffin; she'd been burned per guild custom, and we'd stood at the ash-pit arguing about whether she'd been a criminal or a saint—and how he'd said, *You'll understand someday, Serah,* and how I'd spent eleven years making sure I never did.

"I think it's someone specific," I said.

The orders from above were clear: find the thieves, recover the iron key, execute on capture. The orders had been clear since the first proclamation, and I had followed them, and I had told myself every night that law was the only thing standing between Corvane and the old chaos of the Veil War. Law was a wall, and walls keep out wolves.

But lately I'd begun to wonder what the wall was keeping *in*. The registry burnings I'd signed off on. The way the king's edicts arrived already sealed, already worded, as if they'd been written by something that had never once needed to breathe. The way Chancellor Veyne smiled at nothing, at length, like a portrait left too long in the sun.

I put those thoughts away the way I put away everything—in the drawer that didn't lock but that I pretended did.

The reports gave me what I needed by midday. Three unregistered charms recovered in the past month, all with the same fold pattern, all sold within a mile of Laundress Row. And one more thing, buried in an informant's note: an old woman doing wash at the communal basins who paid her stall fees in coins too old to be legal tender. Coins from before the Veil War.

Grandmother's generation. Guild generation.

"They're using the laundries," I told Veyne's aide that afternoon, and hated myself for the efficiency of it. "The basins. Steam hides scent-traces, wet wool kills tracking spells, and nobody looks twice at women hauling bundles. I want the Row watched tonight. Quietly. If the thief comes for his contact, I take him alive."

"And if he resists?"

"He won't." I said it with a certainty I didn't examine. "He's not a fighter. He's a folder of paper."

### Paloma

Maren had told me not to come back to the Row until moonset, and I had told her I'd come at moonrise, and this is what happens when you argue with the last elder of the Blackwood guild: you end up bleeding through a stolen shirt in a laundry yard while your sister's hunters close the streets.

In fairness, the plan had seemed sound. Tovan—wounded at the vault, thigh bound in linen I'd charmed to knit slow and clean—insisted he could walk. Maren had a cart, a coverlet, and forty years of practice looking boring. We'd be across the city and into the salt tunnels under the harbor chapel before anyone knew to look.

What we hadn't counted on was that Serah didn't need to look. She needed only to remember me well enough to predict me, and apparently eleven years of estrangement hadn't dulled that particular talent of hers. She'd always been the one who could find whatever I hid. When we were children she found my hoard of river-glass in an afternoon. It should have occurred to me that a woman who made finding things her profession would eventually point that gift backward, toward the one person who'd been hiding from her his whole life.

We were loading the cart when the lanterns came on.

Not one lantern. All of them—every lamp along Laundress Row flaring at once, which meant a coordinated ignition charm, which meant trained casters, which meant the citadel had sent its actual witches and not just its muscle. The light fell flat and white across the wet cobbles, and the shadows of the washing lines stretched long and thin like gallows poles.

"Maren," I whispered. "Back door."

"There is no back door, boy. There's the front, and there's dying tired." But she was already moving, putting her broad body between the cart and the street, hands still dripping suds like some domestic war goddess.

They came from both ends of the Row. Six hunters in grey, moving in pairs, and then—last, unhurried, her boots striking the cobbles like punctuation—a woman in the black-and-silver of the chief investigator.

Serah.

She'd cut her hair since I'd last seen her. That was the detail my mind seized on, absurdly, while everything else in me went cold: she used to wear it long, braided with ribbon, and now it was cropped short and practical, and somehow that hurt worse than the crossbow bolt I could see in the quiver at her hip.

"Paloma." She said my name the way you'd set down something heavy. "Don't run. Please don't make this harder than it has to be."

"Serah." My voice cracked on the second syllable, traitor that it was. "You're hunting children now. The Bell Gate boy was twelve."

"The Bell Gate boy was unregistered gifted in violation of crown law, and I didn't take him—I executed the warrant, that's all a warrant is—" She stopped herself. I watched her stop herself, watched the doubt flicker behind her eyes like a candle in draft, and I understood suddenly that she wasn't here because she believed. She was here because she was afraid of what happened to investigators who stopped believing.

"Give me the key," she said. "Whatever you took. Give it back, testify, and I can maybe—*maybe*—argue registration instead of execution. It's the best I can do. It's more than anyone else would do."

"It's nothing," I said. "You know it's nothing. You've seen the execution lists. Registered or not, they go to the same pit."

Something moved behind her face—agreement, maybe, or grief wearing agreement's clothes. "Then give me the key anyway," she said quietly, "and let me lie to my superiors for you. Let me try."

And gods help me, I almost did. Eleven years of anger, and she offers to commit treason badly, clumsily, out of love she doesn't know how to hold anymore, and I nearly handed over the iron key to buy three days of her forgiveness.

Then the nearest hunter raised his crossbow at Maren, and the moment died.

"No," I said. "I'm sorry, Serah. I'm sorry for all of it—for leaving, for hiding, for letting you believe Grandmother was mad. She wasn't mad. The king is dead, Serah. He's been dead for years, and the thing on the throne is eating this city, and I am going to prove it if it's the last—"

"*Enough.*" Her voice went hard as cell-stone. "Take him."

The world became very fast after that.

* * *

### Serah

Later, I would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail, because memory is a coward and mine flinched.

Vess fired first—at the old woman, of all people, because she'd stepped into the line of fire with her arms spread like a mother hen over a coop. I shouted *hold*, and half the pair held, and the other half didn't, and that was the moment I understood my own order had already rotted through: I commanded six hunters and controlled perhaps three.

Paloma moved. Not like a fighter—like a trickster. He threw a handful of something from his sleeve, and the lantern-light bent, actually *bent*, folding around us so that every hunter suddenly aimed at a different brother-shaped shadow. Illusion work. High guild craft, the kind that was supposed to have died with the Veil War. My brother, the charm-seller, the folder of paper, was casting like the elders of old.

And the old woman—

The old woman sang.

One note, low, pitched below hearing, and the wet cobblestones of Laundress Row answered. Water rose out of the gutters and the wash-basins and the air itself, coiling into ropes, and it took two hunters off their feet and slammed a third into a wall with a force that made my teeth ache. She wasn't a laundress. She had never been a laundress. She was something out of the stories Grandmother used to tell, and she was spending it all, freely, extravagantly, the way you spend coin when you already know it's your last purse.

"Maren Ostwick," I breathed, because I'd read the guild rolls, everyone in the hunt had, and that name sat at the top of the extinct list with a black line through it.

"Go!" the elder roared at my brother—not at me, at him, in a voice that brooked nothing. "The cart, the tunnel, *go*, boy, and don't you dare look back!"

He looked back. Of course he looked back. He was Paloma.

"Serah—" he started.

"Take them!" I ordered, and my voice broke in the middle of it, and Vess, bless her confused loyal heart, hesitated exactly one second too long.

The water-ropes parted for the cart like a curtain. Paloma hauled Tovan up onto it, and the horse—God knew what Maren had done to the horse—bolted, and the cart went rattling up the Row toward the harbor road with my brother clinging to it and looking at me over his shoulder with an expression I will see when I close my eyes for the rest of my life.

I could have shot him. I had the angle, the range, the training. My hand was on the crossbow and every law I had ever served was screaming *fire*.

I aimed wide. I told myself it was to disable the horse. I have never once managed to believe myself.

Then the world went white.

Maren Ostwick, cornered, wounded—Vess's first bolt had caught her shoulder—did the thing that elders of the Blackwood guild did when they chose to stop rationing themselves. Every drop of water on Laundress Row came up at once. Every gutter, every basin, every raindrop hanging in the night air, gathered into a single towering shape, a woman made of grey water ten feet tall, and it swept the street clean of hunters—including me—flinging us like dolls into doorways and drain-grates.

And then it came apart. All of it. Because there is a price, and the old ones know the price better than anyone, and she paid it without hesitation, without ceremony, collapsing onto the wet stones like a puppet whose strings had been cut.

I reached her first. I don't know why. Duty, maybe. Or the sudden, sick certainty that I was watching something irreplaceable die and that I had brought it to this street myself.

Her shoulder was wrong. Her breathing was worse. Blood diluted in the wash-water around her, pink spreading like ink in a rinse basin, and her eyes found me and sharpened with terrible clarity.

"Investigator," she rasped. Almost amused. "Come to finish the job?"

"Medic!" I screamed over my shoulder. "*Medic!*"

"Waste of breath." She coughed. "Listen, girl. You have your grandmother's ears. I can see it. You hear the truth under words. So hear this one." Her hand closed on my wrist with shocking strength. "The king is dead. Has been dead nine years. What wears him is older than the war—it was the *reason* for the war—and it feeds on bound magic, on harvested gifted folk, and your hunts are its harvest. You are its scythe. Do you hear? Its scythe."

"That's sedition," I said, and my voice was a child's voice, and I despised it.

"It's arithmetic," she said. "Your brother holds two keys. Salt and iron. The third—" Her grip tightened. "The third is bone. It's in the palace itself, girl. In the reliquary beneath the throne room, in a box of glass and gold, and it is the king's own fingerbone, and it is the only thing in this world the Crown cannot replace or counterfeit. Your brother knows the rite. Get him inside. Whatever it costs—get him *inside*."

"The palace," I repeated stupidly. "You want him to break into the palace."

"I want him to break the *king*," she said, and laughed, a wet broken sound. "There's a difference. There's always been a difference. Ask your grandmother. Oh—wait." Another laugh, weaker. "Ask me. Too late. Ha."

"Maren—" I heard myself use her name like we were kin. Maybe we were. Maybe everyone with the gift had been kin once, before my laws made them strangers. "Stay awake. Stay with me."

"Girl." She pulled me down closer, and her whisper was urgent now, all the humor gone. "It knows he's coming. It looked at him through the king's eyes—I felt it turn, like a lighthouse. It will offer him things. It offered all of us things, once. Tell him—" Her breath hitched. "Tell him the Crown lies best about love. That's its favorite bait. Tell him..."

She didn't finish. Her hand went slack on my wrist, and the great elder of the Blackwood guild, the woman who had held back a hunter squad with the water in the gutters, lay still in the wash-water of Laundress Row among the soaking linens, and the lanterns burned on, ordinary and white, as if nothing had happened.

The medics arrived. They worked on her for a long time. I stood in the doorway of a stranger's house and let them.

When Vess approached, pale, asking for orders—pursuit, the harbor road, the dogs—I heard myself say: "No pursuit. The trail's dead. Report the elder resisted arrest and expired. Report the thieves escaped by boat."

"By boat? Inspector, there's no—"

"*Report it,*" I said, and she went.

I knelt afterward and picked up the elder's fallen shawl, sodden and grey, and folded it. Four creases, corner tucked first, then a twist at the end. The Blackwood fold. I don't know why I did it. I don't know why my hands knew how.

I do know that somewhere between that fold and dawn, the drawer where I kept my doubts finally burst open, and everything I had stuffed into it came out at once, and I stopped being able to pretend the wall was keeping out wolves.

The wall was the wolf. It had been all along.

* * *

### Paloma

We ditched the cart at the salt-stairs and walked the last mile to the drowned chapel in silence, because Tovan was grey-lipped with blood loss and I was carrying something heavier than he was.

I'd looked back. I'd seen her aim. I'd seen her fire wide, and I'd seen Maren fall, and both images lived in me now side by side, refusing to sort themselves into sense. My sister had spared me. My sister's hunters had killed the last elder of my guild. Both true. Both hers.

At the chapel, in the blue dark smelling of brine and old incense, I laid the two keys on the altar slab beside Grandmother's journal. Salt, crusted white as frost. Iron, cold as a verdict. Two-thirds of a dead king's freedom, and one-third still waiting in a reliquary under the throne of a thing that wore his face.

Tovan slept finally, feverish, his hand near his knife even in dreams. I sat with the journal open on my knees and read Maren's marginal notes in her crabbed hand, and near midnight I found the passage she must have known I'd need, because she'd underlined it twice:

*The Crown cannot be fought with force. It owns force. It cannot be argued with, for it has eaten every argument ever sworn before the throne. It is defeated only by truth spoken freely, by a soul given back willingly, and by keys carried in open hands.*

Open hands. No weapon. No shield. Just truth, and a fingerbone, and whatever I had left after that.

Near dawn, footsteps on the chapel stair—soft, deliberate, boots I knew. I was on my feet with a charm half-formed before the door opened, and there she stood, out of uniform, hair damp with fog, my sister with a sodden grey shawl folded in the Blackwood style under her arm.

"Before you decide whether to fight me," Serah said, and her voice was wrecked and steady at once, "you should know I'm not here to arrest you. I'm here because a dying woman grabbed my wrist and gave me orders, and I find I'm done taking orders from anyone else." She held up the shawl. "She wanted you to have this. And she wanted me to tell you one thing."

The candles guttered. Somewhere far off, in the direction of the palace, a bell rang the hour, and I thought of the motionless figure on the throne, and the reliquary beneath it, and the price written in my grandmother's journal that I had not yet told anyone aloud.

"What thing?" I asked.

Serah's jaw tightened. "She said the Crown lies best about love. She said it's the Crown's favorite bait." Her eyes met mine, and for the first time in eleven years, my sister and I were looking in the same direction. "So. Tell me about this third key, little brother—and tell me fast, because I know a way into the palace, and I don't think we have much time before the thing on that throne sends the whole hunt after us both."
