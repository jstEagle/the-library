## 12. The Hollow Court

The trouble with disguises, Maren had told me once, is that people don't look at what you're wearing. They look at what you're afraid of. Wear the fear wrong and no amount of borrowed livery will save you.

So I spent three days learning to be a clerk.

Not pretending—learning. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole trick. A man pretending to be a clerk checks his sleeves for ink. A clerk has ink on his sleeves and doesn't check them because he stopped caring about that particular shame around his second year of service. I stained my cuffs deliberately. I practiced walking with the particular hunched patience of a man whose back has surrendered to a desk. I learned to carry paper the way clerks carry paper: not like it matters, but like losing it would be worse than death, which is a different thing entirely.

Tovan got me the uniform. He didn't ask how I knew the palace was hiring temporary scribes for the autumn census rolls, and I didn't ask how he knew which gate guard took bribes in copper instead of silver. We had developed a rhythm between us, Tovan and I—a language of not-asking that was, in its way, more honest than most conversations I'd ever had.

"You'll be searched at the second gate," he said, the night before. "They'll take your knife. Let them."

"I know."

"And if anyone asks about the census work—"

"Grain tallies from the river districts. The harvest numbers are late because the ferry tolls changed hands twice this summer and nobody upstream wants to admit which district lost the barges." I recited it flat. "I've been a bored government functionary before. It's not far from being a charm-seller. You spend all day telling people comforting lies about numbers."

He almost smiled. Tovan's smiles were rationed like wartime bread, but they existed. "You'll want to be invisible," he said. "Can you do that? Without—" he gestured vaguely at me, at all of me, at the thing under my skin I'd spent thirty years hiding.

"No magic," I said. "Just craft. There's an art to being overlooked. My grandmother called it the first lesson of the guild. *The world looks away from the ordinary, child. Be ordinary, and you can stand anywhere.*"

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have made a fine thief."

"She'd have been offended by the comparison. She thought thieves lacked ambition."

* * *

The palace of Corvane sat on its hill above the city like a judge who never blinks. I'd seen it my whole life from below—from the docks, from the market, from every street where people looked up at it the way you look up at weather, something vast and indifferent that could ruin your day without ever noticing you existed. White stone, grey roofs, the king's banner hanging limp in windless air. Everyone in Corvane grew up with that building in the corner of their eye. You forgot it was there, and then some tax collector or witch-hunter reminded you, and there it was again, watching.

Walking toward it through the outer court, I discovered something I hadn't expected: the palace looked smaller up close. Not humbler. Smaller, the way a face looks smaller when you see it slack with sleep. All that authority was architecture, I realized. Take away the distance and the hill and the banners, and what remained was stone and mortar and doors that needed oiling. Someone had scratched initials into the base of a pillar by the records annex. Someone else had let ivy win a fifteen-year war along the east wall. Kings lived here. Kings were just men with better real estate.

I clutched my census folios and repeated this to myself like a prayer.

The second gate search went exactly as Tovan promised. A guard with a face like a closed ledger patted me down, confiscated a knife I'd brought specifically to lose, waved a bored hand at my papers, and sent me into the heart of the most dangerous building in the kingdom carrying three keys sewn into the lining of my coat.

That's the joke I kept returning to all morning, the one that kept my hands steady: the salt key against my left ribs, cold as harbor water; the iron key warm from my body heat, humming faintly the way iron does when it remembers being forged for a purpose; and the bone key—the king's own fingerbone, wrapped in linen and tallow-cloth—in a pouch over my heart. Three pieces of a dead man's unbinding, carried past armed guards by a man dressed as a bean-counter. If the Crown had any sense of humor, it would have appreciated the irony. I suspected it did not.

* * *

The census office was everything I'd hoped: dim, dusty, staffed by two men so old they seemed to predate the building, both asleep by mid-morning. I signed the attendance roll with a false name in handwriting I'd practiced until it wasn't mine—*Corvin Hale, junior scribe, seconded from the Harbor Assessor's office*—and then I did the single bravest thing of my life up to that point.

I asked to use the privy.

And then, instead of going back, I walked.

This is the secret nobody tells you about great fortresses: they are full of corridors, and corridors are full of servants, and servants are invisible. I had spent my whole life being invisible out of necessity; now I was invisible out of profession, and the sensation was almost nostalgic. Nobody looked at me. A chambermaid nodded. Two guards argued about dice debts and didn't break stride. A steward barked at me to mind the wet floor and moved on before I could apologize. I was Corvin Hale, junior scribe, a man whose entire personality fit inside a grain tally, and the palace parted around me like water around a stone.

I told myself I was scouting. Mapping the route to the reliquary, noting guard rotations, learning the rhythms of the place for the night I'd come back for the bone key's siblings—for whatever the third key turned out to be, though Maren's journal had gone silent on that point, maddeningly, right where it mattered. That was the official reason. That was the reason I gave Tovan, and Maren's memory, and myself.

But some part of me—and I think it was the part that had grown up poor and gifted in a city that hated both—some part of me wanted to *see*. I had imagined the Hollow Crown for weeks now. I had built it out of my grandmother's frantic journal entries and Maren's silences and my own nightmares, and nightmares are always vaguer than the truth. I wanted to look at it. I wanted the horror to have a shape, because shaped things can be fought, and vague things only gnaw.

I found the throne room by following the silence.

Every corridor in the palace had noise in it—footsteps, voices, the clatter of kitchen life bleeding through service doors. But the central hall drew sound into itself and swallowed it. As I approached the great double doors, standing open, flanked by guards who stared straight ahead with expressions I couldn't yet read, the noise of the palace fell away behind me like a tide going out.

I picked up a folio of nothing-papers, tucked them under my arm, and walked in behind a knot of actual clerks delivering documents. Invisibility is easier in company. Wolves ignore the herd.

* * *

The throne room of Corvane was built to make you feel small, and it worked even on a man dressed as dust. Vaulted ceiling painted with the Veil War—heroes in gold, monsters in shadow, the whole glorious lie rendered forty feet overhead. Banners of every guild and province. And at the far end, on a dais of black marble, the throne itself: iron and antler and old dark wood, ugly as truth, beautiful the way a wound is beautiful.

King Aldric sat upon it.

My first thought—my shameful, human, immediate first thought—was *he's older than the coins*. The face on Corvane's currency was a man of maybe fifty, stern and handsome. The man on the throne was older than that, or seemed to be, in a way that had nothing to do with years. His skin was the color of candle wax left too near the flame. His hands rested on the arms of the throne with perfect, terrible stillness—not relaxed, not gripping. *Placed*, the way you'd arrange a mannequin.

And he did not move.

I have seen statues move more. Statues at least shift when the ground settles. This man breathed—I watched for it, counted, found it, shallow and slow as a sleeping child—but nothing else about him moved at all. Not his eyes. Not the small muscles of his face. He sat in a room full of people and he was *elsewhere*, or *elsewhen*, or simply absent, the way a house is absent after everyone who loved it dies.

The court moved around him.

That was the part I hadn't prepared for. I'd expected guards, maybe a few officials. What I found was a full court in session—forty, fifty nobles in silk and brocade, petitioners kneeling on the marble, clerks (my colleagues, my tribe) scurrying with documents. A whole apparatus of governance, running smoothly, efficiently, around a king who was not governing anything.

A duke—I learned later his name was Vessic, Master of the Exchequer—stood presenting the autumn revenues. He spoke well. Measured, confident, the polished cadence of a man who had given this exact speech many times. And while he spoke, he smiled. He smiled when he reported the grain surplus. He smiled when he reported the ferry tolls. He smiled when he mentioned, in passing, the "regrettable attrition" in the registered-gifted population—a phrase that meant, translated out of court-language, *we are executing them on schedule*.

He smiled, and he kept smiling, and the smile went on perhaps four seconds too long after the sentence ended, hanging on his face like a hat hung on a hook, and then it came down all at once, replaced by nothing at all.

I looked around the room and saw it everywhere.

The nobles of Corvane smiled too long. All of them. A countess laughed at something her neighbor said, and the laugh ended and the smile stayed, fixed, a portrait of amusement rather than amusement itself. A young lord received a document, bowed, thanked the courier with warmth—and his eyes never changed, never flickered, stayed flat and distant as coins at the bottom of a well. They moved through their courtesies like dancers following steps rehearsed so thoroughly the dancing had died out of them. Graceful. Precise. Empty.

Hollow-eyed. That was the word from my grandmother's journal, and I'd read it a dozen times and thought I understood it. I hadn't. *Hollow-eyed* isn't a description of how someone looks. It's a description of what's missing when you look at them. The light that answers you from another person's eyes—that little spark that says *someone is home*—was simply gone. These people were inhabited by etiquette.

And the terrible thing, the thing that made my skin crawl in a way the king himself hadn't yet managed, was that they seemed *happy*. Content. A woman near me sighed with genuine pleasure as she adjusted her fan. A man murmured to his companion, "A fine session, a very fine session," with the deep satisfaction of a child fed and bathed. Whatever wore them had given them exactly enough feeling to keep the machinery running and taken the rest. They were puppets who had been permitted to believe they were puppeteers.

I thought of Serah. The thought arrived uninvited, the way thoughts of her always did, and it arrived wearing armor: *she works among these people. She serves this court. Does she see it? Has she stood in this room? Is this what waits for her if she rises high enough—a smile that lasts too long, eyes like wells?*

I made myself stop. Grief and fear are luxuries in enemy territory. I filed the thought away where I filed all my thoughts of Serah, in the crowded locked room at the back of my skull, and I turned my attention to the throne.

Because the king had moved.

It was nothing. Less than nothing. His head had tilted—one degree, perhaps two, the smallest correction, the kind of motion a hunting owl makes when it hears a mouse beneath snow. His eyes had not shifted from their middle distance. But something in the room had *noticed*, and I felt it the way you feel a draft from a door opened somewhere far off in a large house.

The presentation ended. Duke Vessic bowed low, held it, rose, retreated backward with the smooth deference of long practice. The king said nothing. The king said nothing to any of it—no decree, no assent, no dismissal. And yet the court treated his silence as speech. Every petition, every report, every request was offered up to that motionless figure and then withdrawn *resolved*, settled, done, as though the silence itself had ruled. Governance by absence. Law pronounced by a corpse.

An old woman knelt at the foot of the dais then, one of the last petitioners, pleading something about her son—tax debt, I caught, or conscription, the details drowned in her trembling voice. She wept as she spoke. Real tears, real desperation, the only genuinely alive emotion in that entire glittering room. She begged the king to look at her.

And the king looked at her.

Slowly—so slowly it took me a moment to register it as movement at all—his head turned, those waxen eyes descending from their middle distance to fix on the kneeling woman. The court went quiet. Even the hollow ones sensed something. The woman lifted her tear-streaked face, hope cracking across it like ice on a pond, and opened her mouth to speak—

—and the king smiled.

I will carry that smile to my grave. It was a good smile. That's what I can't explain properly, what no account will ever render correctly: it was a *kind* smile, warm, gentle, the smile of a father, a benefactor, a saint. It reached his eyes. It crinkled them. Every muscle did precisely what a loving smile should do, in precisely the right order, with a fluency no hollow noble could manage.

And underneath it, behind it, through it, there was *nothing looking out*. Worse than nothing. Nothing doesn't watch. This watched. Something enormous and patient and hungry had arranged that face the way I arranged charms in my market stall—carefully, attractively, to draw the customer close—and the woman on her knees before it leaned into that smile like a moth leaning into glass.

Her petition was granted. I heard the herald announce it. She wept with gratitude, kissed the marble, was helped away glowing with joy, and I stood there in my clerk's coat with my folios of nothing and understood, finally, completely, what my grandmother had died trying to warn us about.

The Crown didn't rule through terror. Terror was for the streets, for the hunters, for people like me. Up here, in the seat of power, it ruled through *giving*. It gave the nobles their contentment. It gave the petitioners their mercies. It gave and gave and gave, generously, warmly, smiling all the while—and everything it gave came from somewhere, and everything it gave bound the giver tighter. The whole court was a web of gifts, and at the center sat the spider, motionless, patient, feeding.

On what? On the bound magic of the city, Maren had said. On the gift itself, harvested from the registered and the hunted. But standing there, I understood it was more precise than that. It fed on *surrender*. Every person in that room had handed over some piece of their will—their doubt, their grief, their refusal—and the Crown ate it, and smiled, and gave them comfort in exchange.

The audience ended. The court began to disperse in slow, murmuring, satisfied clusters. Clerks gathered their documents. Guards shifted. The ordinary machinery of the extraordinary room resumed.

And the king looked at me.

Across the length of that hall, past dozens of departing backs, the thing wearing Aldric's face turned its head—smoothly now, no pretense of slowness—and its eyes found mine with the unerring ease of a hook finding a fish. The smile was gone. The face was calm, empty, attentive. It looked at me the way I imagine a reader looks at a page, taking in the whole of me at a glance: the borrowed coat, the false name, the three keys sewn against my ribs, the gift coiled and hidden under thirty years of practice.

It knew. Of course it knew. It had probably known since I passed the second gate. Perhaps since I was born.

I did not run. Running is what prey does, and whatever else I am, I am my grandmother's grandson. I bowed—the deep, correct bow of a junior scribe before his king, the bow I'd practiced in Tovan's rented room until my back ached—and I turned and walked toward the doors at the pace of a man with grain tallies on his mind and nowhere in particular to be.

Behind me, in a voice like dry leaves settling, soft enough that I might have imagined it, the King of Corvane spoke for the first time all day.

"Come again soon, Corvin Hale."

I did not look back. I walked out through the doors, down the corridor, past the guards, through the courts, out the gates, into the city, one measured step at a time, while my heart hammered a message against my ribs in time with the keys hidden there:

*It knows my name. It knows my name. It knows my name.*

And beneath the fear, in the quiet cellar of myself where I keep the things I'm not ready to feel, another thought was already forming—cold, clear, and absolutely certain:

Tomorrow night, I was coming back for the reliquary. And this time, I intended to make sure the thing on the throne watched me do it.
