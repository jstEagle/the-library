## 13. The Third Key: Bone

There is a particular kind of terror that comes from standing in a room where everyone is smiling too much.

I'd felt it once before, years ago, at my grandmother's funeral, when a distant cousin kept grinning through the entire eulogy like a man who'd heard a joke the rest of us were missing. I'd thought then that grief made people strange. Now I knew better. Grief makes people honest. It was something else entirely that made them smile like that—something with teeth behind the lips and nothing at all behind the eyes.

The banquet hall of Corvane Palace was full of such smiles.

I stood among the clerks, third from the end, holding a ledger I had no business understanding and a candle I had no business carrying. My disguise was Maren's final gift to me before Laundress Row—a grey clerk's coat, a forged writ of attendance stamped with a seal she'd kept hidden in her wash basin for thirty years, waiting for a night just like this one. *The reliquary opens during state functions,* she'd told me, her blood drying on my hands as I pressed them against her wound. *Ceremony is the Crown's religion, Paloma. It cannot resist its own rituals.*

She'd been right about everything else. She would be right about this.

The hall itself was a monument to excess—the Veil War won and then milked for two generations of marble. Banners of the nine provinces hung from rafters tall enough to have their own weather. A hundred candles burned in sconces of black iron, and their light did something strange down at the far end of the room, where the dais rose and the throne sat and the king of Corvane received his guests.

Strange how? you might ask. Well. Candle flames lean toward things that draw them. Everyone knows a flame bends toward an open door, toward a draft, toward breath. Every flame in that hall leaned toward the throne. Not flickering. Leaning. A hundred small golden tongues all straining in the same direction, like flowers following a sun that gave no light back.

And on the throne sat King Aldric.

Or the thing that wore him.

* * *

Let me tell you what I saw, because I spent a long time making myself look, and looking cost me something each time.

From thirty feet away, he was only a king: old, silver-bearded, robed in white and gold, hands folded on the arms of his throne with the patience of a portrait. Up close—and I would get close, God help me—he was wrong in ways the eye caught one at a time, each detail a small knife.

He did not blink enough. A man blinks; it's involuntary, a little tide the body turns. Aldric blinked perhaps once a minute, and when he did, it was slow and deliberate, like a door being closed by someone who wanted you to know they could open it again.

He did not breathe. I watched his chest. The robes didn't move. There was a stillness to him that wasn't calm—it was the stillness of a thing that had forgotten bodies needed motion and only remembered to imitate it when someone approached.

And his face—Aldric's face, if the portraits told the truth—was handsome in the way of old coins, worn smooth, dignified. But the expression never changed. Not between guests, not across the hours. The same faint smile, the same half-lidded regard, held so long and so perfectly that your mind began to scream quietly that faces don't do that, faces are weather, faces move—

He's dead, I reminded myself. He has been dead for years. What sits there is a parasite wearing a dead man like a coat, feeding on every scrap of bound magic in this city, harvesting my people one registry at a time. That is what you came to rob. That is the thing whose fingerbone you're going to steal out of its own house.

It helped to think of it as a burglary. Burglary I understood. I'd been a small-time criminal since I was twelve years old, selling luck-charms to dockworkers and passing off sleight of hand as sorcery. Tonight was simply the largest job of my career, with slightly higher stakes than a dockworker's bad knee.

The reliquary stood against the eastern wall, beneath a window of stained glass depicting the Veil War's triumphant conclusion—which, given what I now knew, was roughly as accurate as a painting of a fish riding a horse. It was a case of glass and gilt, tall as a man, and inside it, on a cushion of crimson velvet, lay the bones of Corvane's saints and heroes. And among them, according to Maren's journal, mounted in a setting of gold and displayed as the kingdom's most sacred relic:

*The Finger of Saint Aldric the Unyielding.*

They'd made it a saint's bone. They'd put the king's own severed finger under glass and prayed to it, generation after generation, and not one priest or scholar or thief had ever wondered why a living king's finger was a relic. Because the answer was in the question, wasn't it? Kings don't give up fingers while alive. Somewhere in the early years—before the Crown learned to wear him seamlessly—someone had taken that finger from Aldric's body, and the body had been dead when they took it.

Three keys. Salt, iron, bone. Two already burned holes in the lining of my coat, against my ribs, cold as debts. The salt key from the drowned chapel, which had nearly drowned me along with it. The iron key from the citadel vaults, which had cost Tovan everything and very nearly gotten us both hanged. One key left, and it was lying in a box twenty feet from the thing that knew I was coming.

Because it knew. I was sure of it. Had been sure since the night in the reliquary's direction, weeks ago now, when I'd first read Maren's journal by candlelight and felt, distinctly, the sensation of being read back.

* * *

The banquet proceeded with the grinding pomp of a millstone.

Ambassadors from Vell and the Salt Coast presented gifts. A choir of children sang a hymn to the Unconquered Crown—I use the word "hymn" loosely; it sounded like cats being lowered into a well. Wine circulated. The nobles circulated faster. And everywhere I moved with my ledger and my candle, taking up a clerk's post near the eastern wall, counting steps to the reliquary, counting guards, counting the seconds between the chamberlain's announcements.

Four guards on the reliquary. That was more than Maren's notes promised, but tonight was no ordinary function. Tonight marked the fortieth anniversary of the Veil War's end, and the palace had pulled in soldiers from every garrison. The hall held perhaps three hundred guests and twice that many armed men, and every one of the armed men answered, ultimately, to the thing on the throne.

But here was the flaw in its perfection, the crack Maren had found and left me: ceremony required the reliquary to be opened. Once per banquet, at the toast to the founders, the Master of Relics would unlock the case with two keys carried on a chain around his neck, reach in with white gloves, and lift the Finger of Saint Aldric high so the court could bow to it. Forty years of tradition. The Crown loved tradition the way a leech loves a vein.

During the toast, everyone looked at the relic. Everyone bowed. Everyone's eyes went where they were told.

Everyone's except a clerk's. Clerks, in the hierarchy of the court's attention, ranked somewhere below the furniture. Nobody had looked at me all evening except to hand me things to carry, and that was exactly as planned. Invisibility, my grandmother used to say, is a magic anyone can learn. She said it as a joke. Like most of her jokes, it turned out to be survival advice.

The chamberlain's voice rang out: "The Toast of Founding!"

The hall stilled. The Master of Relics—a soft, sweating man named Corvel, whom I'd watched all evening the way a cat watches a mouse hole—ascended to the reliquary with his chain of keys jingling like tiny bells. The four guards straightened. Three hundred nobles turned toward the eastern wall and sank into their bows, a field of silk bending in unison.

I bent with them, ledger clutched to my chest, eyes down—and watched through my lashes as Corvel unlocked the case, drew out the golden setting, and raised it high.

Light caught the relic and threw it across the bowed heads: a fingerbone, browned with age, mounted upright in gold, unmistakable even at distance because of the ring still circling it. A signet ring. The royal seal of Corvane pressed into dark metal, worn by a dead man's knuckle for forty years while the thing upstairs wore the rest of him.

"To the Founders," the chamberlain intoned, "and to the King, who endures."

*Who endures.* I nearly laughed, there in my bow, and the laugh curdled halfway up my throat and became something colder. Endures. Yes. Like mold endures. Like debt endures.

Corvel returned the relic to its cushion. The case locked. The court rose. The choir struck up again, and the banquet resumed its slow digestion of the evening.

Now. While the hall was still settling, while the guards relaxed from their ceremonial stiffness, while Corvel himself stepped away to mop his face with a napkin and take a long drink of wine—now, in the ten minutes before the next scheduled ceremony, when attention would swing to the west windows for the lantern release.

Ten minutes. Four guards. One clerk nobody saw.

I moved.

* * *

Walking across that floor was the longest walk of my life, and I have walked to my own near-execution, so I speak with some authority.

I kept my eyes on my ledger. I murmured numbers under my breath, the way clerks do, the way bored men do, giving the world nothing to look at. A guard glanced at me and dismissed me in the same heartbeat—I was grey, I was stooped, I was carrying papers, I was part of the machinery of the evening. My grandmother's charm-work hummed faintly against my skin, the concealment weave Maren had stitched into the coat's lining, thin as breath. It wouldn't stop a determined search. It only had to stop a glance.

The reliquary's lock was the problem. I couldn't pick it—not in the open, not in time. But I didn't need to pick it. I needed Corvel's keys, and Corvel was currently at the wine table, and Corvel's chain was hooked over his chair like a man who had done this a hundred times and never once imagined a world in which a clerk might want it.

I drifted past the wine table. I let my sleeve catch the chain—sleight of hand, oldest trick in the Blackwood book, the same lift I'd used on purses at the Bell Gate market when I was fourteen. The chain came away silent. My heart hammered so loudly I was certain the whole hall could hear it, certain any moment a hand would fall on my shoulder—

Nothing. The choir sang. The nobles nibbled. I walked on with two small gold keys warming in my palm like stolen suns.

The reliquary. The lock. The click, soft as a swallowed word. The glass door swinging open on hinges that had been oiled that morning, because ceremony tolerated no squeaks.

Inside, the smell was dust and incense and something older, something like the inside of a tomb, which I suppose it was. Bones of saints in rows. Banners folded. And there, center cushion, the Finger of Saint Aldric the Unyielding, gold setting gleaming, signet ring dark.

I reached in.

My hands were shaking. I'll admit it freely; there's no one left to impress. But they did what I asked them to do. I lifted the setting free of the velvet, and the moment my skin touched the bone through the gold, the two keys against my ribs woke up.

Salt flared cold. Iron flared hot. And the bone—God, the bone *sang*, a note below hearing, a vibration in my teeth and the roots of my hair, and for one vertiginous second I felt all three keys recognize each other like siblings separated at birth. The rite assembled itself in my mind unbidden, a shape of words and will that Maren's journal had only sketched. Complete. Waiting. Needing only a fourth element: me.

"Paloma."

I froze.

No—that wasn't right. No voice had spoken. The hall's noise continued uninterrupted, the choir, the chatter, the clink of glasses. But my name had been said, clearly, gently, in a voice like dry paper folding, and it had been said *inside my skull*, in the space where my own thoughts live.

I looked up.

Across the hall, past three hundred bowed and bobbing heads, the King of Corvane was looking directly at me.

Not at the reliquary. Not at the disturbance. At *me*. Those half-lidded eyes, that unchanging smile, fixed on the grey little clerk by the open case with the saint's finger in his hand, and the distance between us meant nothing, meant less than nothing, because I felt his attention land on me like a physical weight, like a hand pressing flat against my chest.

*Paloma Blackwood.* The voice again, patient, almost fond. *You have your grandmother's stubbornness. And her hands. I remember her hands.*

The hall tilted. I gripped the reliquary's frame to stay upright. Around me, nothing had changed—no guard had turned, no noble had noticed, the choir was mangling its second hymn—but everything had changed, because the thing on the throne had stopped pretending not to see me.

*I could show you her,* it offered, mild as a merchant discussing weather. *The last hour of her. She spoke of you, at the end. Would you like—*

I slammed the reliquary door.

The glass shut on the sound of its own voice, or I told myself it did, and I turned and walked—did not run, *walked*, though every nerve screamed to sprint—back into the crowd with the bone key palmed against my wrist, its song still shivering through my arm like a struck bell refusing to finish.

Behind me, unhurried, the chamberlain announced the lantern release at the west windows, and three hundred nobles turned as one to watch the lights rise, and the guards' eyes went with them, and I slipped sideways through a servant's door into the dark of the service corridors with my heart trying to claw its way out of my mouth.

* * *

The corridors behind the banquet hall were all brick and shadow and the smell of roast meat, and I ran down them anyway, walk be damned, because running was the only thing keeping the voice from settling back into my head.

At the second turning I nearly put a knife into Tovan, who caught my wrist before the blade cleared my belt.

"It's me." His grip was iron and his eyes were wide. "Paloma. *Look at me.* Did you get it?"

I held up my hand. The bone key lay in my palm, singing its low note, and beside it the other two, and the three together threw a light that wasn't light—a shimmer, a pressure, a sense of something vast leaning close to the world's skin.

Tovan stared at it. Then at me. "You're bleeding."

"I'm what?" I touched my nose. My fingers came away red. Behind my eyes, a headache was building like a storm front. "Fine. It's fine. We need to move. Now. Before—"

Before it finished whatever it had begun when it said my name. Before it decided that a clerk in a hallway was worth sending four hundred soldiers after. Before—

The bells began.

All of them. Every bell in the palace district, and then, rolling outward across the city like a wave, every bell in Corvane, the alarm peal I'd heard only once before in my life, the night they came for the neighbor boy. The night invisibility ended for all of us.

Tovan went pale. "That's a full hunt call. That's not for a theft, that's—" He looked at me, and I watched him understand it at the same moment I did. "It's not ringing for the bone. It's ringing for *you*. It knows your name."

"Yes," I said, and closed my fist around the three keys, and felt them burn cold and hot and singing against my palm, one heart shy of whole. "It knows my name. And Serah's going to hear those bells too, Tovan. And when she does—"

Somewhere above us, boots on stone, many boots, descending.

"—she's going to know exactly where to come."

I stuffed the keys inside my shirt, where they lay against my chest like three pieces of a promise, and we ran.
