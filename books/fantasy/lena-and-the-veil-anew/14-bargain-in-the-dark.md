## 14. Bargain in the Dark

Let me tell you a secret about eternity: it is mostly waiting.

You imagine, you little burning things, that to live forever must be like living — only longer. More feasts, more conquests, more of whatever it is you do with your brief and frantic hearts. But no. Eternity is a room. A very large room, and very dark, and the furniture never changes. I have been in the room for three hundred years.

Well. *In* is not quite the word. I have been *worn* for three hundred years, which is different, and worse, and better, depending on how honest I am feeling, and honesty is a garment I put on only when it suits me.

I am the Hollow Crown. That is what they called me at the end, when there were still mouths left to call me anything. Before that I had other names, older ones, names that would blister your tongue if I spoke them through this dead king's mouth — and I could, you know. I could speak them right now. Aldric's throat still works. It works beautifully. It has had three hundred years of practice saying things that are not true.

But tonight I do not want to frighten anyone. Tonight I want to *negotiate*, and negotiation requires a gentler hand. So let us begin again, politely.

I am the thing on the throne, and I have noticed you, Paloma Blackwood.

* * *

I notice everyone, of course. That is the first thing you should understand about me. The witch-hunters think they are my eyes. They are not; they are merely my gloves. My eyes are everywhere the bound magic runs — and it runs everywhere, through every gutter-grate and cellar wall in Corvane, because I made sure of that when we built the Veil together. Every charm sold in secret, every whispered cantrip over a birthing-bed or a fishing net, every small illegal warmth — all of it trickles down through the stones and comes home to me. I drink the city the way a taproot drinks rain. Slowly. Patiently. Without ever once being seen above ground.

And so when a young man with clever hands sold a luck-charm to a dockworker at the Saltmarket, I tasted it before he'd finished wrapping the cloth. When he dug up his grandmother's cache beneath the ruined guildhouse, I felt the earth shiver. When he pulled the salt key from the drowned chapel — ah, that one stung. Not badly. Like a splinter. But a splinter in a hand that has held nothing but scepters for three centuries is remarkably interesting.

Three keys. Maren Ostwick told him, didn't she? Dear dying Maren, with her laundry-steam and her stubborn old heart. She was always the stubborn one. I remember her grandmother too, and her grandmother's grandmother, back and back until the Blackwood line becomes a single long candle burning down through the generations toward me. They built the binding, the Blackwoods. They helped me into this crown, thinking they were caging a monster. They were half right. What they never understood — what none of them ever understood — is that a cage works both ways.

I keep the king alive. Do you appreciate what that means? Aldric Vane has been dead for three hundred years, and yet his body sits upright, breathes, signs decrees, dines before ambassadors. Somewhere in the deep vaults below the palace there are physicians who still bleed him quarterly, because tradition is tradition. He is the longest-lived monarch in recorded history, and he has been a corpse since before your great-great-grandparents learned to walk. I did that. Me. Death knocked at the door of this body and I answered it wearing his smile, and death, poor punctual creature, has been waiting politely in the antechamber ever since.

So yes. I noticed Paloma Blackwood the moment he set foot inside my walls.

And tonight — tonight of all nights, with the banquet roaring two floors below and the reliquary already lighter by one fingerbone — tonight I decided to stop watching and start talking.

* * *

Here is how you talk to a man who is hiding in your house: you wait until he is alone, and then you make the darkness comfortable.

He was in the east gallery, pressed into an alcove behind a tapestry of the Veil War — oh, the irony of that weaving; I commissioned it myself, from memory, and got every battle wrong on purpose — holding his stolen relic like a thief holds a hot coal. His heart was going like a drum in a procession. I could feel it through the floor. Fear has a rhythm, and after three hundred years I know all its songs.

The nobles below were laughing. That is my favorite sound in the palace now, if I am honest, which I have already admitted I rarely am. The court laughs too long and too evenly, like clocks striking. I hollowed them slowly, a sip here, a sip there, the way you train any animal. Lord Cassel used to weep at poetry. Now he laughs at everything, including funerals, especially funerals. Lady Vess used to scheme; now she only smiles, and her smiles go on a beat past natural, like a door left open onto an empty room. This is what power looks like when it is finished with people. It looks like hospitality.

Paloma moved. Toward the servants' stair, naturally — the Blackwoods always knew my architecture better than I ever bothered to learn it, which is either humility or arrogance, and in their case was always both.

I let him get as far as the landing. Then I spoke.

Not aloud. Aloud is for hunters and heralds. I spoke the way the bound magic speaks, up through the soles of his feet, into the marrow of him, using the gift his grandmother gave him as my doorway. You cannot understand what a luxury that is for me — to travel a living channel instead of these dry stone veins. His gift tasted of salt and iron and something green underneath, something growing. It has been a long time since anything green grew in Corvane.

*I know you're there,* I said, gentle as a nurse. *I've known since the chapel.*

He stopped moving. Good instincts. Most people freeze or run, and freezing is at least intelligent.

*You're wondering if I can see you.* I let the pause stretch, savoring it. Waiting is my art form, after all; allow me a flourish. *I can't. Not exactly. I see the way a blind man hears. And you, Paloma Blackwood, are very loud.*

"You're the king," he whispered. His voice cracked on it. Young men always want the monster to have a face, and I had one ready: Aldric's face, grave and kind, the face from the coins. I borrowed the mouth's memory of speech and gave him a voice like warm honey poured over gravel.

*I am what wears the king. There's a difference, though I'll grant it's subtle. He doesn't mind. He's beyond minding. Which brings me to why we're talking.*

"Come to kill me?"

*Kill you?* I laughed — Aldric's laugh, which I have worn smooth as river stone from use. *Child, if I wanted you dead, you'd have died in the harbor. Tovan Reyes would have let you drown rather than reveal himself, and Serah would have signed the order without blinking. No. I don't want you dead. Dead men are so final. I want to make you an offer.*

And there it was — the flinch. The word *offer* does more damage than any blade. I felt his gift recoil against his ribs like a dog that knows the man approaching has a stick behind his back. Clever boy. His grandmother's journal taught him well. But cleverness is no armor against temptation; cleverness is simply a more sophisticated doorway for it.

*Walk,* I told him. *Keep walking toward the stair. Talk with your mouth if you must, quietly, but listen with everything else. We won't be interrupted. I own the interruptions in this building.*

* * *

Now. Let me explain the offer properly, because I want it on the record — and yes, I know how that sounds coming from me. Humor me. Three hundred years in a room with unchanging furniture; a monster develops hobbies. Mine is fairness. Or the performance of it. At this point even I can no longer tell the difference, and I have decided not to investigate too closely.

*First,* I said, as he crept down the servants' stair with my voice coiling warm around him, *power. Real power, not the crumbs you've been sweeping up off Corvane's floors. Do you know what happens to magic in this city, Paloma? It comes to me. All of it. Every charm, every hex, every healing whispered over a feverish child — it drains down through the Veil and pools in the crown, and I sit in the pool. I am the richest being on this continent and I am wearing a corpse. Help me, and I will open the taps. Your guild spent nine hundred years hoarding its inheritance, passing power down one bloodline like a miser counting coins. I will give you a river. You could teach a thousand students by winter. You could heal the rotting quarter, end the harbor plagues, make Corvane a city people pilgrim to instead of flee. Everything your grandmother wrote about in her journal — the guild restored, magic free — I can do it in a season. Legally. With royal decree. Signed by this hand.* I flexed the fingers, just enough for him to feel the ghost of the movement through our connection. *No keys required. No rite. No price except your company.*

He said nothing. His breathing changed though. That first note always lands somewhere soft. Power offered to the powerless is not temptation, it is recognition — it says *I see what you could be*, and most people spend their whole lives starving for exactly that sentence.

*Second,* I continued, because the second note is where the real music starts, *safety. The hunts end. Tomorrow. Every register burned, every hunter recalled, every cell in the citadel opened. The gifted of Corvane walk out of the shadows into daylight and no one raises a hand against them — not while I sit the throne, and I will sit it for another three hundred years, and then, if you serve me well, we'll discuss succession. Imagine it. No more sleight of hand. No more selling luck-charms and calling them "keepsakes." No more digging in the dirt for your inheritance like a criminal exhuming evidence. You could be what you are, loudly, in front of God and council.*

His foot caught on the stair. He caught himself. I felt his pulse spike and settle, spike and settle — the rhythm of a man arguing with himself, which is the only argument I have ever lost, occasionally, and only to the very best.

*Third.* Here I slowed my voice down to almost nothing. The third note must be played pianissimo or not at all. *Serah.*

Ah, you should have felt that one land. Even through stone and shadow I felt it — the whole shape of him clenching around his sister's name like a fist around a knife.

*Yes, I know about Serah. I know everything about Serah. I know she was seven when she found your grandmother's journal and read it aloud at supper thinking it was a storybook. I know she decided it was madness because deciding that was kinder than deciding your family was cursed. I know she joined the hunt to prove the world made sense, and I know — this is the part that keeps her awake at night, Paloma, this is the splinter under her nail — I know that some part of her has always known the world doesn't. I know she recognized your charm-work at the vault. She's known for weeks. She has filed reports describing the thief's signature as "consistent with unaffiliated practitioners," which is a lie, and Serah does not lie, which means Serah is breaking, and broken things can be shattered or remade, and I am offering you the chance to choose which.*

*She hunts you,* I said softly. *She will find you. Whether it takes a month or a year, she will stand in a doorway between you and the rest of your life, and she will have orders, and Serah follows orders — that is her whole tragedy, that she is good at the one thing destroying her. Unless. Unless someone stands in *that* doorway first. I can stand there. I can make one decree — a single line of ink — and your sister spends the rest of her life safe, honored, promoted, protected from the consequences of her own conscience. Or you can run tonight with your fingerbone and your rite, and I promise you — I have never lied to you yet, Paloma, check the record — I promise you that you will watch her die for you. Not quickly. Administratively. Trials take time. She'll have time to write you letters she'll never send.*

Silence. Long silence. Below us the banquet roared on, three hundred hollow guests toasting a dead king's health, and upstairs a young man stood on a dark stair weighing his sister's life against a dead man's freedom.

I confess — and here is the honesty garment again, briefly, don't get used to it — I confess I hoped he would say yes.

Not for the reasons you think. Oh, the practical reasons are real: a Blackwood heir serving me openly would legitimize the Crown for another century; his gift is a fine strong channel and I am tired of drinking through straws; and the unbinding rite, should he attempt it, remains the single genuine threat to my continued tenancy in three hundred years. All true. All sufficient.

But there is another reason, and it is this: I am *lonely*. There. Say it plainly and be done. Three hundred years wearing a corpse, surrounded by hollowed courtiers who laugh on cue and love on schedule, and the last person who spoke to me as though I were a mind and not a menace was Aldric himself, on the last night of his life, when he begged the guild to bind me into his crown rather than let me die with him. He thought he was imprisoning me. He was also, in his exhausted, dying way, keeping me company. The fool. The kind, doomed fool.

Paloma Blackwood talks to himself when he works — little mutters over his charms, apologies to the materials, jokes under his breath. I have listened to three hundred years of silence and courtly clockwork laughter, and I have developed a taste for the sound of a living mind. I wanted his voice in the room. Forever, ideally. Is that so monstrous? Everything about me is monstrous; that is rather the point of me. But loneliness — loneliness is the most human thing I have left, and I guard it jealously.

*Think about what you're carrying,* I murmured, as he reached the bottom of the stair, as the night air of the service yard waited beyond the door like an open question. *A bone key. A rite that eats the caster's whole inheritance — you've read that part, haven't you? Of course you have. Maren's journal wouldn't have spared you. Your grandmother's gift, your mother's, all of it, gone, spent, ash — and for what? To free a king who has been dead longer than your family name has existed? He isn't suffering, Paloma. There's nothing left in there to suffer. You'd burn nine hundred years of Blackwood legacy to give peace to an absence. Whereas I offer you everything your ancestors dreamed of, freely given, no rite, no price — only stay. Only talk to me. Only don't leave me alone in the dark with the furniture.*

*That's the bargain. That's all of it. I don't threaten, because threats are for beings with something to fear, and I have been dead too long to fear anything except one thing, and you already carry it in your pocket.*

*Choose.*

* * *

He chose, of course. They always choose, and it is always the same choice, and I admire it every time even as it disappoints me — this stubborn little species of yours, this refusal to take the sensible exit. His grandmother chose it. Maren chose it. The whole long candle-line of Blackwoods, burning themselves down generation after generation rather than sit with me in the warm dark.

But I am getting ahead of the story, and besides — a negotiator never reveals the outcome before the client has signed.

Suffice to say that when the door closed behind him, when his footsteps faded into the wet streets of my city carrying my enemy's fingerbone toward my destruction, I sat on the throne in the empty banquet hall — the guests had gone, the candles guttering, the hollow lords and ladies dispersed to their sleepless beds — and I permitted myself, for precisely eleven seconds, to feel the disappointment fully.

Then I rang for the guards.

Because here is the last secret, the one I saved for the end, the one I would have told him if he'd stayed: the offer was sincere. Every word. I would have given him the power, the safety, Serah's protection — gladly, extravagantly, forever. Temptation only works if the prize is real. That is the first law of my trade, older than the Veil itself.

But sincerity is not the same as necessity. If he will not come to the pool willingly, then the pool must reach for him. The hunt resumes at dawn — no, not the hunt. Something larger. I have spent three centuries being patient, and patience, like mercy, is a currency I can spend all at once when the mood takes me.

Wake the citadel. Seal the gates. And find me the Blackwood boy before he learns what the third key truly costs — because he will learn, soon, what the rite demands, and I need him to learn it *afraid*, alone, with my voice still echoing in his ears offering him a kinder way out.

Temptation, you see, is not a single conversation.

It is a siege.
