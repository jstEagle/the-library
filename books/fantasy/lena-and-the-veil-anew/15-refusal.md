## 15. Refusal

The voice in the dark had a way of making silence feel crowded.

*Think of it as an inheritance,* it had said, in the throne room's shadow, in the banquet hall's candlelight, and now—apparently—in the abandoned tannery where Tovan and I crouched among vats of old lime, listening to hunters pound on shutters three streets away. *You were always meant for more than charms.*

"No," I said out loud, because apparently I'd become the sort of man who argued with parasitic headwear.

Tovan looked up from bandaging his forearm. "It talking to you again?"

"It never stops. It's like a grandmother who learned persuasion from a debt collector."

"Mine just left me money and disappointment." He tied off the knot with his teeth. "Can you make it stop?"

"I could stop listening. That requires not having ears, or possibly not having a mind, and I'm attached to both."

The pounding faded. We waited anyway, because waiting is free and dying is not. Outside, Corvane was burning its own night sky—the witch-hunters had taken to lighting signal fires on the rooftops, orange blooms against the smoke-colored clouds, so that the whole city looked like a hearth that had decided to eat its house.

Here is what happened after the reliquary, compressed for your convenience: I ran. The thing wearing King Aldric's face had looked at me across a room full of dancing nobles, and its look had been patient in the way a trap is patient, and I had done what any sensible thief does when the owner wakes. I took the bone key, I broke a window that had survived two sieges and would not survive me, and I went over the palace wall into the dark with the Crown's whisper following me down like a cat following a mouse that it has already decided belongs to it.

Tovan found me in the tanners' quarter because disgraced hunters know all the places disgrace can hide. He was bleeding from a spear graze along his arm—he'd held a door closed against three of his former colleagues while I limped through it—and he hadn't asked a single question, which from Tovan was practically a love letter.

Now he sat back against a lime vat and exhaled. "They'll sweep this district by dawn. We need walls between us and them. Real ones. City ones are no good—they own the gates."

"The tunnels under the guildhouse go past the wall," I said. "Grandmother's maps showed—"

"Your grandmother's maps have gotten us robbed, drowned, and shot at. I'm starting to think she liked us suffering."

"She liked *me* suffering. You're collateral."

He grinned, which cost him something, because the grin pulled at the exhaustion underneath it. Tovan had been grinning less lately. When a man betrays everything he swore to, the first few days are adrenaline, and then the bill arrives.

We moved at the hour when even cats file complaints about their working conditions. Through the tannery yard, along the drainage channel, past the shrine of Saint Vess where an old woman sold candle stubs and pretended not to see us—though her eyes followed us, and I felt the faint warm prickle of a blessing laid on our backs like a coat. Even now, even hunted, the small magics persisted. People like her were why I kept going. Not glory. Candle stubs.

* * *

The guildhouse tunnels smelled of my childhood, which is to say dust, mildew, and the ghost of my grandmother's pipe tobacco, though she'd been dead six years and the pipes longer. We came up through the false floor behind the cold hearth into a room where the wallpaper hung in ribbons and moonlight came through a hole in the roof shaped, if you squinted, like a keyhole. The city had been trying to tell me something for years. I'd been declining to listen.

Tovan collapsed onto a bench and immediately began the process of becoming unconscious. I built a fire small enough to deny and set water on to boil, and then I did the thing I had been avoiding since the reliquary: I opened Grandmother's journal to the pages about the binding rite.

Her handwriting changed there. Earlier entries were quick, slanted, impatient—a woman writing faster than thought. The rite pages were slow. Careful. Each word placed like a stone in a wall she hoped would hold.

*The unbinding requires the three keys united: salt, iron, bone. Salt for what the sea remembers. Iron for what the law forged. Bone for what the king surrendered. Speak the names of the bound, name the binder, and break the pattern.*

Simple. Almost gentle. And then, beneath it, in letters pressed so hard they'd torn the page:

*Understand the price before you understand anything else. The rite consumes the caster's inherited gift. Entirely. Not borrowed. Not dimmed. Gone. The Blackwood gift dies with the Crown or not at all. I could not pay it. I was a coward, and I buried the keys instead, and I am sorry, child, whoever you are, that you are reading this.*

I read it four times.

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, a patrol passed, boots ringing on cobbles, and Tovan slept through it with the deep shamelessness of the genuinely exhausted.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about an inheritance: you don't get to choose which parts arrive. Grandmother left me a gift, a guild, a guilt, and a confession, all wrapped in the same paper. The gift was the reason Serah and I had shattered—the night our grandmother died, Serah had called the magic madness, had called me a fool for grieving a delusion, had walked out and eventually walked into grey armor and a crown warrant. The gift was also the only reason I knew how to hide, how to charm, how to survive fifteen years of outlawed existence without once being caught.

And the rite wanted to take it. All of it. Permanently.

I am not going to pretend I took this nobly. I sat there by my denial-sized fire and I grieved in advance, which is a stupid exercise, like eating a meal you haven't cooked yet. I thought: *I'll be ordinary.* I thought: *I won't feel the tide of the harbor anymore, or know when rain is coming an hour early, or warm a sick child's fever down with a touch and a rhyme.* I thought: *Serah will have been right all along. There will be nothing left of me but the part she could love, and she doesn't love that part either.*

Then I thought about the boy from Bell Gate. Tam. Fourteen. Taken in the night for a talent so small it was mostly a party trick—he could make bread rise twice as fast. They'd registered him because registering seemed safer than hiding, and the registry had fed him to the harvest. I didn't know for certain yet—I wouldn't know until Serah brought the truth—but I knew the shape of it. The Crown wasn't hunting mages. It was *farming* them. Every gifted person in Corvane was a row in a field, and the thing on the throne was the reaper.

My gift, spent entirely, might buy every one of those rows back.

Or it might buy nothing, and I'd be empty and dead and the Crown would laugh in whatever language parasites laugh in. Grandmother's journal was honest about the odds too:

*I do not know if it will work. The elders disagreed. Ostwick believed truth spoken aloud could crack the binding; Halvard believed only force could. I believe both, and neither, and I believe someone must try, and I hate that it must be someone, and I am glad it is not me, and I am ashamed of being glad.*

That was the passage that undid me, more than the price. Because it was honest. Because she'd been afraid exactly the way I was afraid, and she'd written the fear down instead of pretending, and somehow that made the fear shareable, and shared fear is half-sized.

"Paloma." Tovan's voice, rough with sleep. "You've read that page so many times it's going to file a complaint."

"How long have you been awake?"

"Since you started arguing with the journal under your breath. You do that. Argue with books. It's very charming and slightly alarming." He sat up, winced at his arm. "What's the price?"

I told him. All of it. He listened the way he did everything—without flinching, without comforting, which is its own kind of comfort.

"So you do the rite," he said slowly, "and you lose the thing that makes you *you*."

"That's the theory. Though I'd argue the thing that makes me me is mostly sarcasm, and I'm told the rite leaves sarcasm intact."

"And if it kills you?"

"Then the sarcasm goes too, tragically."

He was quiet for a while. The fire had burned down to a red eye. "When I took the hunter oath," he said, "they told us we were the wall between Corvane and the wild magic. Keep the Veil intact, keep the people safe. I believed it. Eleven years." He turned his bandaged arm over, examining it like a document. "Turns out I was the wall around a farm. Keeping the livestock in." He looked up. "Whatever you decide—I'm in. But decide honestly. Not the version of honest where you pretend the sacrifice doesn't scare you because heroes aren't scared. The real kind."

"The real kind is worse."

"The real kind is the only kind that holds a door shut."

So I decided honestly. I sat with the journal open on my knees and I let myself want my gift—really want it, greedily, the way you're allowed to want things at three in the morning when no one virtuous is watching. I let myself imagine growing old with it, teaching it someday to students of a reopened guild, feeling the harbor tide in my chest until my chest gave out. I let the loss be enormous.

And then I noticed something, sitting there in the wreckage of that wanting: the loss was enormous, and I was still going to do it. Both things were true, and neither canceled the other, and that—that was the acceptance. Not the absence of grief. Grief riding alongside purpose like a passenger who never stops talking.

Grandmother, I thought, you cowardly, brave, dishonest, honest woman. You buried the keys and wrote the map and hated yourself for both. I forgive you. Mostly. Ask me again after the rite.

*The caster's inherited gift will be consumed entirely,* the journal said.

"Fine," I said to it. "Take it. But I'm spending it loudly."

Tovan raised an eyebrow. "Talking to the book again."

"Negotiating."

"With a dead woman's diary."

"She started it."

He laughed—short, pained, real—and lay back down, and within minutes his breathing went slow and even, because soldiers sleep when they can and worry on schedule.

I stayed up. I copied the rite onto a scrap of paper in my smallest hand and burned the copy-practice in the fire, and then I read the original once more, and then I did the last thing: I spoke aloud to the dark, to the city beyond the broken roof, to the thing wearing a dead king.

"You offered me power," I said quietly. "Safety. My sister's protection. Here's my answer: no. Not because I'm brave. Because everything you offer, you offer the way a farmer offers grain to a mill. I've seen your court. I've seen the smiling. I decline to smile forever."

Somewhere far off, a bell rang the hour, and I chose to believe it was laughing.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and thin through the keyhole in the roof. I had slept maybe two hours, folded against the cold hearthstone, the journal under my cheek like a pillow with opinions. Tovan was already up, moving around the ruined kitchen with the economy of a man who'd lived rough before and expected to again. He'd found a kettle somewhere. Disgraced hunters are resourceful.

"We can't stay past midday," he said. "This district gets swept on a rotation. I know the rotation. It changes tomorrow."

"Where do we go?"

"Nowhere yet. Because—" He stopped. His face did something complicated. "There's a rider at the end of the lane. Grey cloak. Been there since before light. She's not hunting. She's waiting."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the tunnel, through the bedrock of Corvane itself. "Describe her."

"Tall. Dark hair under the hood. Stands like she's holding herself together with discipline." He met my eyes. "Paloma. She's standing like *you* stand."

I went to the gap in the shutters and looked down the lane, and there she was. Serah. Out of uniform, out of citadel, out of everything, standing in the mud of Laundress Row with a satchel at her hip and ash on her gloves, looking up at our broken roofline with an expression I recognized from mirrors: someone deciding, honestly, at last.

She saw me at the shutter. Neither of us waved. Some recognitions are too large for gestures.

"She's alone," Tovan said beside me. "No escort. And Paloma—look at her hands."

Her hands were empty. Open. A hunter carries warrants in those hands, or irons, or a blade. Hers carried nothing at all.

"She's coming to the door," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, scraped thin by the night's arithmetic. "Open it, or shall we make her knock? Because I have opinions about people who arrest their family, and most of them involve making them wait in the cold."

Tovan put his hand on the latch. "Whatever she's carrying in that satchel," he said, "I hope it's worth what it cost her to get here."

Below, Serah lifted her chin toward the door—the old gesture, the one from childhood, *well? are you going to make me stand here all day?*—and the satchel at her side bulged with the shape of papers, and papers, in Corvane, in this season of ash, could mean anything.

Could mean everything.

Tovan opened the door.
