## 16. Serah's Choice

The ledgers were kept in the sub-vault beneath the citadel's records hall, behind a door that required two keys and a lie. I had both. The keys came with my rank; the lie was the one I'd been telling myself since the night I burned the registry—that I was still a loyal officer who had merely misplaced a document.

I told myself that again as I descended the stairs, lantern held high, my boots loud in a silence that felt deliberate, like a held breath.

*You're just checking inventory discrepancies,* I told myself. *Thieves hit the vaults three weeks ago. An investigator of your standing would naturally want to review acquisition records.*

Acquisition records. That was the phrase that had started this. Not *confiscation*. Not *seizure*. Every other department in the citadel used those words for what we took from gifted folk—the charms, the talismans, the grimoires, the people. But the requisitions from the palace used *acquisition*, and they used it with a specificity that had nagged at me the way a splinter nags: not painfully, at first, but always.

Because you don't *acquire* a person unless you intend to keep them.

I reached the bottom of the stairs and set my lantern on the iron table. The sub-vault was long and narrow, lined with shelving that went back further than the lamplight did. Dust hung in the air like a curtain that hadn't been drawn in years—which was strange, because the requisition slips said material arrived here monthly. Things that arrive monthly do not gather dust.

Unless they don't stay.

I found the ledger where the index said it would be: *Crown Requisitions, Gifted Persons, Years One Through Nine.* Nine years. The hunts had been running nine years. I had been hunting for six of them, and in all that time it had never once occurred to me to ask where the people went after we delivered them. There was a transfer point at the Bell Gate. There was a palace escort. And then there was nothing—no trial records, no execution certificates, no burial registries. I had signed delivery manifests for one hundred and forty-two human beings, and I had never followed a single one past the gate.

I opened the ledger.

The early entries were almost mundane. Names, ages, districts, the nature of the gift noted in a clerk's cramped hand. A weaver from Tanner's Row whose thread never frayed. A midwife whose hands could ease a fever. Children—I stopped at the first child's entry and made myself keep reading, because if I stopped I would close the book, and if I closed the book I would remain the woman I had been an hour ago, and I could not be her anymore. Not after tonight.

Beside each name, in a different ink—a brownish ink I chose not to think too hard about—was a notation. Not a sentence. Just figures. Numbers with units I didn't recognize at first, until I found a key at the front of the ledger, written in the same brown hand:

*Yield, in drams of bound essence, per subject.*

Yield. Per subject. As though the people were grain fields. As though my sister's neighbors, my city's midwives and dockworkers and charm-sellers, were livestock to be measured at harvest.

I turned pages faster now, and the numbers grew. Year one: modest yields, crude methods, losses—"losses," the word used without shame—of nearly half the subjects. By year four the methods had improved. By year seven there were annotations about *sustained extraction*, about subjects kept alive for months because a living gift yielded more slowly but far more fully than a dead one. There was a note in the margin, initialed by someone whose seal I recognized—the Lord Chamberlain's—in a tone so briskly administrative it made my skin crawl:

*Crown prefers continuous yield. Terminate only on depletion.*

Terminate only on depletion.

I sat down on the iron stool because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me. The lantern flame didn't waver. Nothing in the room moved except my hands, which shook the ledger, which shook the light, which shook the shadows along the shelves where all those names lived in cramped brown ink.

One hundred and forty-two. I had thought of myself as a hunter who perhaps, occasionally, regretted the necessity of her work. I had built a whole architecture of justification over six years—the law is the law, the gifted are dangerous, order must be maintained—and every beam of that architecture had been load-bearing, and every beam had just been pulled out at once, and I was sitting in the rubble of it holding a book.

They weren't hunting mages. They had never been hunting mages. They were *farming* them.

And then I turned to the last page, and found the thing that finished the job.

It was a summary sheet, updated quarterly, comparing total yield against something called *the Crown's requirement*. For eight years the columns ran roughly parallel—yield rising to meet need. But in the final quarter, the requirement column jumped. Nearly tripled. And beside it, in fresh brown ink, a projection: at current harvest rates, the city's remaining gifted population would be depleted within eighteen months, and the Crown's requirement would go unmet.

There was a proposed solution appended. Three words. I read them four times before I understood them, and understanding them was like swallowing cold iron.

*Expand the criteria.*

Not find more mages. Widen the net. If the yield ran short, they would simply redefine who counted as gifted. A grandmother's luck-charm. A sailor who could read weather in his bones. Anyone. Everyone. The criteria were already being drafted—I could see the draft clipped to the inside back cover, waiting for the right moment, waiting for some new edict, some new proclamation at the Bell Gate.

My brother had been right.

That thought arrived without permission and stayed without apology. Paloma, standing in our grandmother's kitchen nine years ago, his voice cracking: *She's not mad, Serah. She sees things. Real things. Why can't you just look?* And me, seventeen and terrified and desperate to be respectable, saying the unforgivable thing: *Because she's sick, Paloma. Because someone has to be normal in this family.* Because I had chosen the law over my own blood, and told myself it was principle, and been promoted for it, and decorated for it, and here was the truth of what I had served, written in brown ink, measured in drams.

Grandmother had known. Of course she had known. She used to say the king's shadow fell wrong, that the court smiled like drowned men, that something wore the crown that wasn't Aldric. We'd had her committed partly for that. Partly. I had signed nothing, but I had said nothing, and silence was its own signature.

I closed the ledger. Then, because closing it felt too much like letting it happen again, I opened it and began, methodically, to take it apart.

* * *

I worked until the third bell of the morning. I am, whatever else is true of me, thorough. I took the ledger itself—too large to hide on my person, so I took the pages that mattered, folded flat against my ribs beneath my coat. I took the draft edict. I took the quarterly summaries. I took the delivery manifests with my own signature on them, because if I was going to burn my life down I intended to do it with complete documentation, like any good investigator building a case.

The case, I realized somewhere around the fourth bell, was against me too. I had delivered these people. My signature was on their deaths as surely as the brown ink was. There was no version of justice that spared me, and I found—to my genuine surprise—that I didn't want one. I wanted the opposite. I wanted to stand in whatever square this ended in and say *I did this, I was the instrument, judge me accordingly.* Vengeance wasn't the point. Accountability was. I had spent six years confusing the two, and the confusion had cost one hundred and forty-two lives.

But first, the tunnels.

Every hunter knows the citadel has ways out that aren't the front gate. We're taught them in our second year—not as escape routes, but as *approach vectors*, ways to slip into the city unobserved when following someone requires it. What we're not taught, what only the oldest officers remember and only ever whisper, is that the tunnel system predates the citadel entirely. It belongs to the old guildhouses. The hunters' fortress was built on top of the Blackwood guild's foundations, the way a conqueror builds a fort on a temple—out of contempt, and out of fear.

Maren Ostwick had told me that once, years ago, before I knew what she was. She'd been mending a sheet in the citadel laundry, and I'd been inspecting the linens, and she'd said, conversationally, wringing water out of cloth with hands that were stronger than they looked: "Old buildings sit on older ones, Investigator. Dig down anywhere in Corvane and you'll find somebody else's floor." I'd thought it laundry-woman philosophy. I'd filed it under nothing.

Now I went down to the lowest level of the citadel, past the cells, past the interrogation rooms with their drains, to a wall that the official plans marked as solid stone. The plans were wrong. There was a seam in the masonry, hairline, running floor to ceiling, and set into it at shoulder height a stone that didn't match its neighbors—smoother, darker, worn at its edges as though many hands had pressed it over many years.

I pressed it. Nothing happened, because I was doing it wrong. I stood there feeling foolish, and then I remembered Maren's hands again, the way she worked the wash, and I understood: it wasn't a button. It was a rhythm. You didn't press it; you knocked. Three and two, the way washerwomen beat heavy cloth. I had heard that knock echoing up through the citadel floors for six years and assumed it was laundry.

Three and two. The seam split. The wall swung inward on hinges that had been oiled within the decade, which meant someone was still using this door, which meant—

Which meant I was probably expected. Fine. I had stopped caring about probable around the time I started stealing state documents.

The tunnel beyond was narrow, brick-lined, dry, lit at intervals by lamps that were also recently filled. I walked it for what felt like a quarter mile, my stolen papers crackling faintly against my ribs with every step, my heart keeping time. The tunnel branched twice, and at each branch someone had chalked small marks—guild marks, I realized, the same symbols I'd seen on confiscated charms, the ones our training manuals called *harmless superstition*. Harmless superstition that mapped a secret road beneath the hunters' own fortress.

The tunnel ended at another door, wooden this time, and through it I heard voices. Low, tired, arguing quietly the way people argue when they've been arguing for days and have run out of heat for it.

I knocked. Three and two.

The voices stopped. Long silence. Then footsteps, and the door opened a hand's width, and a lamplit eye regarded me through the gap—an eye I knew. Grey-green, wary, set in a face that had gotten harder since the last time I'd seen it, which had been across an interrogation room, him in irons, me reading charges.

Tovan. The disgraced hunter. The traitor, according to my own warrants.

"Serah Blackwood," he said, and his voice was flat as slate. "Chief Investigator. Come to arrest us at last? Or did you just get lost in the laundry?"

Behind him, deeper in the room, I heard a chair scrape, and a voice I would have known blindfolded, in a crowd, at the bottom of the sea, saying my name like a question he couldn't afford to ask.

"Tovan," I said. "Step aside. I'm not here for you."

"And I'm supposed to believe that because—"

"Because I brought the proof." I pulled the folded pages from inside my coat, and my hands, which had been steady all night, finally shook. "The requisitions. The yields. The draft edict that widens the criteria to whoever they decide counts. They're not hunting the gifted, Tovan. They never were. They're harvesting them, and when we run out, they'll take everyone, and I have it all here in writing, and I need—" My voice cracked, and I hated it, and I let it. "I need to see my brother."

The door opened the rest of the way.

Paloma stood in a lamplit cellar that smelled of soap and candle wax, and he looked thinner than I remembered, and watchful, and afraid—but underneath the fear, when his eyes found mine, there was something that hadn't been there nine years ago in Grandmother's kitchen. Something like a door left unlocked.

He crossed the room slowly, as if I might be an illusion, or a trap. Maybe I was both. He stopped an arm's length away—the exact distance protocol requires, the distance I'd taught him once, joking, when we were children playing at soldiers—and looked at the papers in my hands, and then at my face.

"You burned the registry," he said quietly. "We heard. Maren said—" He stopped. His jaw tightened. Maren, who was dead because of me, because I had cornered them on Laundress Row, because I had been so very good at my job. "Serah, Maren—"

"I know what I cost you." I held out the papers. "I can't give her back. I can't give any of them back—one hundred and forty-two, Paloma, I signed for every one. All I can give you is this, and the tunnels, and everything the citadel knows about its own defenses, because I know them better than anyone alive. And tomorrow, in front of the council, I'm going to testify to what the Crown really is, and what I did for it, and they can do what they like with me afterward."

He took the papers. Our fingers touched on the fold, and neither of us pulled away.

"The council won't listen," Tovan said from the doorway, but he said it like a man hoping to be wrong. "Half of them are hollow-eyed already. You've seen the court."

"Then I'll say it anyway," I said. "Loudly. Publicly. Where it can't be unsaid. That's how this works, isn't it?" I looked around the cellar—at the chalked maps on the walls, the lists of names, the quiet army of laundresses and dockworkers and charm-sellers that my own hunts had driven underground and, apparently, organized. "You don't need permission to tell the truth. You just need witnesses."

Paloma was reading the requisitions page. I watched his face change as he reached the yield tables, watched him arrive where I had arrived hours ago, and when he looked up his eyes were wet and furious and certain.

"They tripled the requirement," he said. "Why now? What does it need that much for?"

"That," I said, "is the question I've been asking since midnight. And I think—" I steadied myself against the table, against the enormity of it. "I think whatever wears the king is getting ready to stop being subtle. Eighteen months of harvest left in this city, and it doesn't want to wait. I think it's going to feed soon, all at once, on everything it can reach."

Silence in the cellar. Somewhere above us, the citadel bells rang the fifth hour, and beneath our feet, faint as memory, water moved through channels older than the walls.

Paloma folded the papers and tucked them inside his shirt, over his heart, the way Grandmother used to carry letters. Then he looked at me—really looked, the way he used to when we were children and he was deciding whether to share a secret—and nodded once.

"Then we'd better move fast," he said. "Because I know how to kill it. And I'm going to need my sister to get me into the throne room."
