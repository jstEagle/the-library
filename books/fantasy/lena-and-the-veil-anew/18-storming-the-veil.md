## 18. Storming the Veil

Dawn came to Corvane grey as dishwater, and I stood on a rooftop in the Tanner's Quarter watching it arrive, thinking that if this was my last morning, the city could at least have dressed for the occasion.

Below me, the quiet army was assembling. That's what Serah called them—the people's quiet army—and she'd said it with a straight face, which was more than Tovan managed. "An army of laundresses," he'd snorted. "The Crown will surrender by noon out of sheer embarrassment."

But standing here now, watching them gather in ones and twos and small determined knots, I didn't feel like laughing. I felt like crying, which is worse, because crying takes time and we were short on everything except resolve.

There was Bess from the fish market, who'd bought luck-charms off me for six years without either of us ever saying aloud what they really were. There was old Hobb the cooper, whose hands could coax a barrel stave into bending with nothing but a hummed note. The laundresses of Laundress Row came together, moving like a flock, and at their center walked a woman I didn't recognize until she turned her head—Maren's sister-in-law, wearing Maren's old blue scarf. She caught me looking and lifted her chin. *We're still here,* the gesture said. *You didn't burn us all out.*

I pressed my hand flat against the rooftop tiles and let myself feel it one last time: the deep, tidal pull of Corvane's bound magic underneath everything, the way I'd felt it since I was a child—the hum beneath the cobblestones, the taste of iron in rainwater, the sense that the whole city was a held breath. The Hollow Crown had been holding that breath for years now. Tonight, one way or another, it would exhale.

"You're brooding," Tovan said, dropping onto the roof beside me with the gracelessness of a man who'd been a hunter too long to sit anywhere quietly. He handed me half a loaf of bread. "Eat. You can't unbind a king on an empty stomach."

"I can't unbind a king at all, probably. But I'll die trying, so the bread seems moot."

"Eat anyway." He tore his own portion with his teeth and chewed, staring out over the rooftops toward the palace, where the Veil-towers caught the first light and threw it back wrong—too white, too clean, like bone bleached by something worse than sun. "Serah's in position?"

"East postern. She has the tunnel keys and forty people who used to be hunters' informants, which means they know every secret passage in Corvane and hold grudges against the citadel that predate the war."

"Good. I always liked her spite." He said it lightly, but I heard what sat under it. We both did the arithmetic of the morning: gates, guards, wards, and two people carrying three keys between them into the teeth of a god-shaped parasite.

"Tovan." I turned the bread over in my hands without eating it. "If this goes badly—"

"It won't."

"If it goes badly, you take the keys and run. Not fight. Run. Find Serah, get out through the harbor, get the journal to someone who can finish the rite. Promise me."

He looked at me then, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—not the sardonic mask, not the disgraced hunter's careful blankness. Something older and simpler. "Paloma. When I pulled you out of that drowned chapel, you weighed about as much as a wet cat and you thanked me by vomiting seawater on my boots. I have carried you before. I will carry you again if I must. Nobody's running anywhere." He stood, brushed crumbs from his coat, and offered me his hand. "Besides. Someone has to hold the gate."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't meant to be. It's meant to be a job assignment." His grin flashed, quick and bright as a struck flint. "Come on, charm-seller. History's waiting, and history hates tardiness."

* * *

The plan was simple, which is how I knew it would go wrong. Simple plans leave room for the world to improvise.

The palace of Corvane sat on its hill above the river like a fat spider in a web of walls and terraces, and the only ways in were the great gate—which might as well have been the moon, for all our chances there—the east postern, and the sky. Serah took the postern. The sky was beyond all of us. Which left the gate.

But not the way you'd think. We weren't going to storm it. We were going to *be* stormed.

At midmorning, the market bell rang, and the people of Corvane did something they hadn't done in living memory: they walked toward the palace instead of away from it. Dockworkers down from the harbor with gaff hooks and chain. Fishwives with knives and voices like gulls. Charm-sellers who'd spent lifetimes pretending to be nothing, walking openly now with charms braided into their hair and sewn into their hems, glowing faintly through the cloth like coals under ash. They came up the Processional Way in a slow, unstoppable tide, ten thousand strong, and they weren't shouting. That was the eerie part. They were singing—a work song, an old hauling chantey from the docks, the kind of thing sung to keep rhythm while pulling something heavy. Something heavy indeed.

And the crown's response was exactly what Serah had promised it would be. The gate opened. Hunters poured out—not to parley, but to break the crowd, because the Hollow Crown had never once in its long parasitic life mistaken patience for strength. It fed on fear. Fear made the magic run sweeter.

Which meant the first move of the battle belonged to the oldest magic in Corvane: the refusal to be afraid.

I watched from the flank, behind a barricade of overturned fish carts, as the first volley of binding-spells arced out over the heads of the crowd. And I watched the crowd do the thing we'd drilled for three nights in the tannery yard: they knelt. All at once, thousands of people, kneeling and covering their heads, and the spells passed overhead and shattered harmlessly against the ward-lanterns Bess and her sisters had hung from every eave along the Processional Way. Harmless charms, everyone had called them, for years. Luck-knots and hearth-blessings and little woven protections against bad weather. Ten thousand tiny magics, strung across a street like festival lights.

They held. Gods help me, they held.

Then the crowd rose, and surged, and the battle of Corvane began in earnest—not with a charge but with a *push*, ten thousand ordinary bodies moving with the patient terrible momentum of a river deciding where it wants to go.

"Now," Tovan said, and we ran.

* * *

Our route to the gate went through the old customs house, which backed onto the gatehouse itself via a corridor built for smugglers by customs officers who'd been smugglers first. Tovan knew it from his hunting days. I knew it from selling contraband charms in it. Between us, we could have written a guidebook.

We came up inside the gatehouse just as the pressure outside peaked—the crowd hitting the outer barricades, hunters falling back step by step, the air thick with spell-smoke and the copper smell of overworked wards. Through the murder-holes above the gate arch I could see the front line: dockworkers linking arms, laundresses throwing boiling wash-water over the barrier runes to short them out, old Hobb the cooper bellowing the hauling chantey louder than anyone, his voice cracking with age and fury.

And at the center of the line, holding the narrowest point of the outer gate where the crowd funneled through, stood a single figure in hunter's grey with the sigils torn off his shoulders.

Tovan looked at me. "Gate," he said.

"Tovan—"

"Paloma." He gripped my shoulder once, hard enough to bruise, and shoved the iron key into my hand along with the salt key I already carried. All three keys now rode against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth, cold as three small graves. "The rite needs you alive and upstairs. The crowd needs someone here. I'm better at the second job than the first, and you're the only one who can do the third." He smiled, and this time it reached his eyes, and that was how I knew. "Go make your grandmother proud. Go make *me* proud. I've gotten used to being proud; don't waste the investment."

"Tovan, please—"

"Charm-seller." He said it gently, the way he'd said it the night we met, when I was a wet cat full of seawater. "Hold the door."

Then he stepped out into the smoke and the noise, and I lost him.

I want to tell you I saw him fall. I want to tell you it was quick, or noble, or that he died with some perfect last word hanging in the air. But war doesn't give you those moments cleanly; it gives you glimpses through smoke, and then it gives you a job to do. What I saw, looking back from the top of the gate stairs, was Tovan planting himself in the breach like a fencepost in a flood, hunter's blade drawn, turning back the first rush of hunters trying to seal the gate against the crowd—and then the smoke rolled in, and the chanting swelled, and I had a rite to perform and a king to unbind, and I ran.

I ran because he told me to. I ran because staying would have wasted him. I ran because grief is a luxury and I'd already spent mine on Maren Ostwick, and the account was overdrawn.

But I heard it, even from the inner wall. When the hunters finally broke past him—when the gate finally groaned open wide enough for the crowd to pour through—I heard the roar that went up from ten thousand throats, and underneath it, faint and clear, old Hobb's cracked voice changing the words of the chantey. Changing them to a name.

They sang Tovan's name into the stones of the gate he held, and the stones remembered it, and I felt it happen—a small warm pulse in the city's bound magic, like a coal refusing to go out.

Cost already paid. The ledger was open. I climbed.

* * *

The palace interior was wrong in a way I hadn't been able to imagine until I stood inside it. I'd been here once before, disguised as a clerk, and I'd thought then that the wrongness was decoration—hollow-eyed courtiers, smiles held too long, the king motionless on his throne. Now, with the keys humming against my chest and my gift fully awake for whatever remained of its life, I understood: the wrongness wasn't decoration. It was digestion.

The harvested magic of Corvane's gifted dead and bound living filled these halls like water fills a drowned ship. Every portrait on the walls had been drained of its pigment. Every tapestry was grey. The candles burned without flickering, flames standing perfectly still, as if even fire here had been taught obedience. And everywhere, everywhere, that slow tidal pull, stronger than I'd ever felt it, drawing all the loose magic of the city upward, inward, into the throne room at the heart of the web.

I moved through corridors that should have been full of guards and weren't. The Crown had pulled everything it had to the walls. It knew I was coming. Of course it knew—it had looked directly at me across a banquet hall, once, with Aldric's dead eyes, and it had known then. This was the part of the day it had been waiting for. The spider doesn't chase the fly. The spider lets the web do the work.

The doors of the throne room stood open.

Of course they did.

Inside, the light was the light of a place underwater. Grey-gold, sourceless, pressing down. The courtiers were gone—all of them, swept to the walls or the windows or wherever parasites send their furniture when company comes. Only the throne, and the thing upon it.

King Aldric sat exactly as I'd seen him sit at the banquet: motionless, hands on the armrests, face composed into the gentle vacant smile of a portrait. The Hollow Crown sat upon his brow, and it was not gold, though it looked gold the way a lure looks like a fly. Up close, with my gift screaming against my skin, I could see what it actually was: a ring of thin black filaments, fine as hair, sunk into the skull beneath, drinking.

It spoke without the mouth moving. The voice came from everywhere—from the walls, from the floor, from the space behind my eyes.

"You brought them," it said. "All three. How considerate. I was going to have to fetch them eventually, and fetching is such vulgar work."

I stopped at the edge of the dais steps, and I unwrapped the oilcloth, and I held the three keys up between us: salt, iron, bone. My hands were steady. I remember being surprised by that.

"Below us," the Crown continued, conversationally, horribly, "your ten thousand friends are learning what harvest means. Did you think I gathered all that power to defend walls? Walls are for things that can die." The king's head turned—only the head, the body still as carved stone, the neck rotating with a sound like wet rope. Dead eyes found mine. "Watch."

Through the high windows behind the throne, the sound of the city changed. The singing stopped. Then came a sound I will hear until I die: a low, vast thrumming, like a plucked string the size of a street, and the light in the windows went from grey-gold to a sick, hungry violet, and I felt it through the soles of my boots—the Crown pulling, *drinking*, dragging the harvested power of decades up through the palace foundations and hurling it down onto the Processional Way.

Screaming, faint and far. Ward-lanterns shattering, ten thousand harmless charms burning out at once, a whole street's worth of small protections dying in a single breath.

"No—" I was moving before I decided to, up the dais steps, keys in my fist, and the Crown laughed with the king's dead mouth, a sound like a hinge that had never once been oiled.

"Yes," it said. "Run to them. Or stay, and finish what you came for. You cannot do both, Paloma Blackwood. That has always been the shape of this. That will always be the shape of this."

And below, in the streets, the violet light pulsed again—closer now, hungry, climbing—and somewhere in the smoke of the Processional Way, I heard a voice I knew, hoarse from shouting, cutting through the panic like a blade through wash-water:

*"HOLD THE LINE!"*

My sister. Holding the line I should have been holding, buying with her own body the seconds I needed to climb these steps.

Three keys in my fist. A rite in my grandmother's journal, folded against my heart. A dead king on a throne, wearing a parasite like a wedding band. And below, everyone I loved in the world, drowning in harvested light.

I set my foot on the first step of the dais.

The Crown smiled with Aldric's face, and the filaments in the skull flexed like roots finding purchase, and it said, almost tenderly:

"Let's begin."
