## 19. Unbinding

The throne room doors were taller than grief and twice as heavy, and Serah pushed them open like she was shoving aside a curtain in a room she owned.

"Stay behind me until the circle's drawn," she said. Her voice had gone flat the way it did when she was frightened—she'd never learned any other way to be frightened, even as a girl. When our mother's boat went down, Serah hadn't cried at the funeral. She'd reorganized the entire pantry by color.

"I remember how to draw a circle," I said. "I've been drawing them since I was six."

"You've been drawing *charms* since you were six. This is different." She turned, and for one unguarded moment I saw what the last few days had carved out of her—the registry ash still under her fingernails, Maren's blood on her sleeve where she'd carried her, the look of a woman who had spent her whole life holding a sword pointed the wrong direction and only just now turned it around. "Paloma. If this goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong," I said. "It'll go terribly, catastrophically right, which is worse."

That got half a breath of a laugh out of her, which was all either of us could afford.

The throne room of Corvane was built to make you feel small, and it worked on me even now, even knowing what I knew. Vaulted ceiling lost in shadow. Banners of the Aldric line hanging dead-still in air that should have been moving. And at the far end, on a throne that might once have been oak but now looked more like something grown than carved, sat the king.

King Aldric. Or the shape of him. He didn't move. He didn't blink. His eyes were open and fixed on the middle distance, and his face wore the expression of a man listening to a conversation happening somewhere very far away. Up close—and I had been close, at the banquet, close enough to steal a piece of his hand—I could see the wrongness underneath. The skin too smooth over the jaw. The chest that rose maybe once a minute, out of politeness rather than need.

The Hollow Crown sat on his head like a spider on an egg.

I couldn't see it directly. Nobody could, not really—it wasn't a thing so much as a hunger wearing the idea of a thing. But I could feel it, the way you feel someone watching you from a window above the street. It had felt me at the banquet. It had looked at me with the king's borrowed eyes and smiled with the king's borrowed mouth, and it had let me walk out with the bone key because it thought I was coming back anyway.

It was almost right.

Serah took up position between me and the dais, sword drawn, and I knelt at the center of the floor where the old guildhouse mosaic used to sit before they paved over it—Maren had told me, in her last hour, that the throne room was built on the guild's first meeting hall, that the stones beneath my knees still remembered the shape of every rite ever performed here. The city's bound magic pooled down here like water in a cellar. I could feel it pressing against the soles of my boots, all the stolen years of it, all the harvested mages, waiting.

Three keys. Salt, iron, bone.

The salt key was a lump of gray crystal that smelled of low tide and drowned prayers. The iron key was a bar of cold black metal that hummed when I held it too long, like a struck bell nobody else could hear. The bone key was a fingerbone, yellowed, small—a king's finger, snapped from his living hand by his own request, by men who loved him enough to do a terrible thing and then spend three generations pretending it had been necessary.

I set them in a triangle around me and began the rite.

* * *

The words were in the journal. Grandmother's journal, water-stained and furious, written in a hand that started neat and got progressively less neat as the decades went on, which told you everything about being a Blackwood. The binding rite ran backward through the same channels it had been cast through: salt to open the wound, iron to hold it open, bone to call the soul home. Simple, Maren had said. Terrible, she had also said. And then, because she was Maren: *the price is written on the last page, boy, and don't you dare skip to it.*

I hadn't skipped to it. Then. I had, eventually, the night Tovan died holding the gate, and I'd read it twice, and closed the book, and said nothing to anyone, and Serah had looked at me across the fire and known anyway, because she always knew, because we were siblings and silence between us had always been louder than speech.

Now I spoke the opening words, and the salt key flared white, and the world tilted.

Not the room. The *world*. The stones under my knees remembered, just like Maren promised, and they remembered out loud.

I was standing in this hall a hundred years ago, in a body not mine, watching a young king kneel where I knelt. Aldric. Alive, whole, terrified. Around him stood the elders of the Blackwood guild in their grey robes, and their faces were faces I knew—Maren's face, younger; my grandmother's face, younger than I'd ever seen it. They were casting the binding, and he was begging them for it.

"*Please,*" the young king said, in a voice cracked down the middle. "It speaks to me at night. It wears my dreams like gloves. I can feel it eating, Elders. I can feel it *eating*, and no one will tell me what." He grabbed my grandmother's hands—my grandmother's hands, the ones that taught me knots and card tricks and how to gut a fish. "If death is the only door out, then open it. I am not afraid. I have been brave for twenty years and I am so tired."

And my grandmother—her eyes wet, her voice steady, her hands shaking where he couldn't see—wept and bound him anyway. Not killed him. Bound him. Sealed the Crown inside its own cage with the king's soul as the lock, because killing him would free it, and binding him would only mostly damn him, and the guild chose *mostly* and called it mercy and then outlawed magic so no one would ever be strong enough to ask hard questions about it.

The vision released me. I was on my knees in the present, gasping, and the salt key was burning cold in its place, and the Crown had noticed.

* * *

It came for me the way a flood comes—not fast, but total.

One moment I was chanting the second verse with the iron key raised. The next, I was eight years old, standing in the kitchen of our house on Tanner's Row, and Serah was slamming a door, and my grandmother was saying *she'll come around, Paloma, give her time,* and I was crying into bread dough because my sister had called our grandmother a madwoman and meant it.

No. Not memory. *Lie.* Because in the real memory Serah had come back that same night, and sat on my bed, and said nothing, which was her way of apologizing, and I had understood. In this version she never came back. In this version the door stayed slammed for thirty years, and the loneliness of it was so precise, so tailored, that I nearly believed it—nearly believed I had lived my whole life unloved by the one person whose love I'd fought hardest for.

"That's not how it happened," I said aloud, to the dark behind my eyes.

The dark answered with my own voice: *Isn't it? You hid from her for fifteen years. You watched her put on the grey coat and say nothing. You let her become this.*

"I grieved her," I said. "Grieving isn't agreeing."

Another lie, then, faster: Tovan at the gate, dying—but in this telling he looked at me with hatred, mouthed *you did this*, blamed me with his last breath. Another: Maren, in the laundry row, bleeding out, whispering not the third key's location but *it was a mistake, boy, run, save yourself.* Another: the dockworker from chapter one of my miserable career, the luck-charm, except now he spat on it and reported me and I burned for it and it was all pointless, all of it, every charm, every risk—

"Lies," I said, and my voice cracked, and I kept going anyway, because that was the whole trick of it, wasn't it? The Crown couldn't create. It could only wear things. It was a parasite dressed in borrowed memories, and borrowed clothes tear at the seams if you look closely enough. "Tovan died proud. Maren died pointing me forward. And that dockworker—" I laughed, actually laughed, kneeling in a burning circle while a dead king's ghost-tyrant tried to unmake me, "—that dockworker bought three more charms off me over the years. Luck-charms. From a man who, statistically, should have had none."

The lies thinned. Through the gaps I heard Serah shouting—real sound, real world—steel ringing against something that shouldn't have been steel-able. The Crown was pulling harvested magic out of the floor and throwing it at her in shapes: hounds made of grey light, chains of whispered law, the edicts themselves given teeth. She was holding. Barely. Holding because I'd asked her to hold, and Serah Villalobos had never once failed to do a thing she'd decided to do, including, apparently, believing in me again.

Third verse. Bone key.

I lifted it, and the Crown stopped lying and started screaming.

* * *

It screamed in the king's voice.

That was the cruelest thing it could have done, and it knew it. Aldric's voice—young Aldric, the drowning man from the vision—filled the throne room, pleading, bargaining, threatening, all at once, overlapping itself like a crowd:

*Stop. Stop, you're killing him, you're killing ME, I'll give you anything—*

*I gave your grandmother everything she wanted and she thanked me—*

*Boy. Boy, listen. Your sister dies tonight if you finish that verse. Look at her. LOOK AT HER—*

I looked. That was the trap and I walked into it with my eyes open, because refusing to look at Serah would have been its own kind of surrender. She was on one knee, sword braced against a wall of crawling shadow, blood on her temple, and she caught me looking and shouted, "Don't you DARE stop on my account, I will haunt you, I know people who can arrange it—"

"I'm not stopping," I said. "I'm reading."

And I opened the journal.

Grandmother's journal, to the page I'd read a hundred times, the page where the handwriting went wildest, where she'd stopped writing *to herself* and started writing *to whoever came after.* I read it aloud over the Crown's screaming, loud, clumsy, my voice nothing special, a charm-seller's voice, a little brother's voice:

"*To the one who finds this. If you are reading this in the throne room, then you are the fool I hoped you'd be. Listen. The thing in the crown wants you to believe it is ancient and inevitable. It is neither. It is hungry, and hunger is small. It wants you to believe the king consented forever. He begged us to let him die, and we were cowards, and I have repented of it every day of my life, and I am repenting now, in ink, so that you won't have to. It wants you to believe magic must hide. Magic hid because WE told it to, to bury our shame. Unbind him. Free the king. Break the Veil. And when it offers you bargains—*" my voice broke, and I steadied it, "*—when it offers you bargains, remember that it has never once kept one. It ate the ones who trusted it. Every name in the back of this book. Every single name. Read them, if you doubt me. I wrote them all down so someone would.*"

The screaming changed pitch.

Because here was the truth of parasites: they cannot abide being *known*. A lie needs darkness. Grandmother had spent forty years dragging this one into the light, word by word, name by name, and I was her grandson and I had inherited exactly one thing worth having, and it wasn't power. It was the habit of writing things down.

I read the names. All of them. Dockworkers and laundresses and midwives and a hunter named Tovan added in fresher ink, and each name landed in the throne room like a stone dropped into a well, and the well got brighter, and the Crown got smaller, and King Aldric—the real one, the drowned man under the flood—began, very slowly, to surface.

His eyes moved. For the first time. Found me.

"*Thank you,*" the king's mouth said, in the king's own voice, quiet under all the shrieking, and it was not the Crown speaking, and everyone in the room knew it, and Serah made a sound I had never heard her make in her life.

Final verse. The price, written on the last page, come due.

I had expected fire. What came instead was quieter and much worse: it felt like being *read*. Like every spell I'd ever cast, every charm, every sleight of hand that wasn't sleight at all—all of it was being lifted out of me gently, page by page, the way you'd take books from a house you were leaving forever. My grandmother's gift, and hers before her, the long river of Blackwood magic running back a hundred years, flowing out through my hands into the triangle of keys, into the rite, into the breaking.

I held on to two things. One: Serah's face, furious and alive and *mine*, my sister, come home. Two: the last line of the journal, which I recited as the river left me, because it seemed important that someone say it in this room, out loud, where the stones could remember it:

"*What we pass on is not what we possess.*"

The salt key shattered. The iron key rang once, a true bell-note, and split. And the bone key—the king's own finger, his chosen key, his exit door—dissolved into light, and the Hollow Crown *screamed its last*, a sound like a city exhaling after a hundred held breaths, and came apart into nothing at all.

* * *

Silence.

Real silence, the kind a room makes when it hasn't been allowed to speak in a century.

King Aldric slumped forward on his throne—an old, old man suddenly, worn thin as winter paper—and looked at his own hands as though greeting them after a long voyage. Then he looked at me, kneeling in a circle of broken keys with empty hands and, I suspected, empty eyes where my gift used to live.

"You're the Blackwood boy," he rasped. "The one from the banquet."

"The very same," I said. My voice sounded strange. Thin. Ordinary. I found I didn't mind. "Your Majesty, I regret to inform you that you've been dead some time, and the management has been unacceptable."

He laughed. It cost him, but he laughed—a real laugh, rusty, human—and then his gaze went past me to Serah, standing in the wreckage of shadow-hounds with her sword point-down and her shoulders shaking.

"Your sister?" he asked.

"My sister," I said.

"She held the door," he said. "All hundred years, someone had to hold the door. None of them ever did." He closed his eyes. "Tell the city... tell them the king is sorry. Tell them the king is *free*." And King Aldric, last of his line, first honest man to sit that throne in a century, bowed his head, and breathed out, and did not breathe in again—at peace, at last, in a room full of witnesses.

Serah crossed the floor and knelt beside me, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because there are moments language was never built for.

Then, distantly, from below the windows—from the streets where the quiet army was holding the gates, where the people of Corvane were looking up at the palace and seeing the shadows lift off it like fog burning away in sun—a sound began. Faint at first. Then not faint.

Cheering.

Serah pulled me to my feet. Her hand came away red from my sleeve and she frowned at it, and then at me, doing the arithmetic of everything I hadn't told her about the rite's price.

"Paloma," she said carefully. "Try to light a charm."

I reached for the gift the way I'd reached for it ten thousand times, the way I'd reached for it in the dark of every hiding place of my life.

And found nothing there at all.
