## 20. The Gift Spent

The Crown fought the way a drowning man fights—ugly, without dignity, with everything it had.

I stood in the center of the binding circle, salt at my feet, iron in my left hand, bone in my right, and I read. That was the whole of my grand strategy, the thing that would save or damn Corvane: I read aloud from my grandmother's journal in a voice that shook like a drunkard's.

*"The king came to us in the eleventh month, grey as ash,"* I read, and the words tore through the throne room like knives through silk. *"He said the Crown whispers. He said he cannot sleep for the whispering. He asked us to bind him before it takes him wholly."*

The Hollow Crown screamed.

Not with sound—with memory. The air around me filled with my grandmother's kitchen, bread rising on the sill, her hands dusted with flour, her voice calling me in from the rain. Then it curdled: the same kitchen burning, her screaming that it was my fault, that I had let the gift die with me, that I was nothing, no one, a charm-seller of trinkets who had never once been brave.

"Lies," I said, and kept reading.

*"We bound him with three keys,"* I went on, louder, over the shrieking dark that poured off the throne like smoke given intent. *"Salt to hold what flows. Iron to hold what endures. Bone to hold what remains. And we were fools, because we did not ask what would remain when the binding held too well. We did not ask what would feed."*

The Crown changed tactics. It stopped attacking me and started *offering*. The throne room bloomed with visions: Serah alive and safe, Tovan standing up from where he'd fallen at the gate, bloodless and grinning, Maren Ostwick laughing over her washboard as though Laundress Row had never run red. All I had to do—the vision whispered directly into the marrow of my bones—was set down the keys. Set them down, and everyone lives. Keep reading, and watch them all die again, one by one, and know it was your choice.

"It already happened," I told it, and my voice cracked, and I let it crack, because grief is not a weakness you can hide from something that eats souls. "They're dead or they're not. You don't get to sell me their ghosts."

Below the throne room windows, through the shattered glass, I could hear the city. The people's quiet army holding the streets—dockworkers singing work songs as wards, laundresses wringing out rivers of light, charm-sellers whose harmless little magics had suddenly become load-bearing walls against the harvested storm the Crown hurled down at them. They were holding. Barely. Loudly. Beautifully.

And behind me, between me and the throne, Serah stood with her hunter's blade drawn and her hunter's training wrapped around me like a second cloak, deflecting what she could, absorbing what she couldn't, bleeding from a dozen small cuts and refusing to move.

"Read, Paloma," she said through her teeth. "Just keep reading."

So I read.

*"If any soul finds this journal and these keys,"* I read, and here my grandmother's handwriting had grown shaky, urgent, pressed so hard into the page it nearly tore, *"know that unbinding is not destruction. The Crown is a parasite, but the king is still under it. Still in there. What you free, you free. What you kill—"*

The last word was smudged. Water damage, maybe. Or tears.

I finished it anyway. "*What you kill, you murder.*"

The bone key grew hot in my right hand. The iron key rang like a struck bell no one else could hear. The salt at my feet began to glow, each grain a tiny white star, and the circle rose around me in a column of light that smelled of the sea and of old iron and of my grandmother's flour-dusted hands.

The Hollow Crown made its last mistake then. It stopped fighting me and reached for the king instead—to drag Aldric deeper, to bury whatever remained of him beneath its hunger one final time, to make sure that when the keys turned, there would be nothing left worth freeing.

And the king—whatever was left of King Aldric after thirty years inside that thing—*pushed back.*

I felt it through the keys. A hand under a heavy lid, pushing up. Faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall. But pushing.

"NOW!" I shouted, though I didn't know if I was shouting at Serah, at the king, at the keys, at the dead, or at myself. Probably all of us. "NOW NOW NOW—"

I brought the three keys together above my head.

Salt met iron met bone.

The world went white.

* * *

I have tried, since, to describe what breaking the Hollow Crown sounded like. The best I've managed is this: imagine every bell in Corvane ringing at once, except the bells are made of glass, and they are all shattering rather than ringing, and underneath the shattering there is a voice saying *thank you* in a language older than the city itself.

The Crown didn't explode. It *came apart*—unraveled, thread by black thread, like a garment being unpicked by patient invisible hands. And as it unraveled, everything it had stolen came pouring back out. Thirty years of bound magic, thirty years of harvested gifts, thirty years of the city's stolen breath—all of it released at once into the throne room, into the palace, into the streets below.

It should have destroyed us. A flood that size, released that fast, in an enclosed space—

But it didn't come as a flood. It came as a *returning*. Magic going home. I watched it stream past me in ribbons of every color I had names for and several I didn't, seeking out the descendants of those it had been taken from, settling into cradles and kitchens and workshops across the city like birds coming back to roost. Somewhere out there, a dockworker's luck-charm just became genuinely lucky. Somewhere, a laundress's wash water just started healing chapped hands. The invisible gifted of Corvane woke up richer than they'd ever been, and most of them wouldn't understand why until morning.

And in the middle of it all, on the throne, the thing wearing Aldric's face collapsed inward like a puppet with cut strings.

What remained was small. That's what I wasn't prepared for—that what remained would be *small*. A man shrunk inside his own royal robes, ancient beyond his years, his skin translucent as parchment, his eyes—

His eyes were his own. For the first time since I'd known of him, King Aldric's eyes were his own.

He looked at me. He looked at Serah. His gaze drifted to the broken window, to the city beyond, lights coming on in windows, people spilling into the streets, and something moved across his ruined face that might have been wonder.

"How long?" he asked. His voice was dry leaves. "How long was I..."

"Thirty years," Serah said. She had lowered her blade. She was crying without seeming to notice. "Your Majesty. Thirty years."

"Thirty years." He said it the way a man confirms a debt. Then, quieter: "The guild. The Blackwood guild. Did they...?"

"They're gone," I said. My voice came out strange, flat, distant. "All but one. She died getting me the way in. Her name was Maren Ostwick. She wanted you to know the guild was sorry."

Something like relief crossed his face—not at the death, but at the apology. At being *seen*, even secondhand, even now.

"The keys," he said. "You used the keys. It's really..." He lifted a trembling hand and touched his own chest, as though checking that he was still in there. "It's quiet. It's finally quiet."

"Yes," I said.

"I had daughters," he said, to no one, to the ceiling, to thirty lost years. "Did I? I had—I can't—there were daughters—"

"There were," Serah said gently. "Two. Their houses still stand. Their grandchildren sit on the council."

"Good," he whispered. "Good. That's good."

He was dying. We both knew it. Whatever had sustained him for thirty years hadn't been life—it had been the Crown, wearing him the way a boot wears a foot, and now that the boot was off there was nothing left to stand on. He had minutes. Maybe less.

He used them strangely. He asked about the weather. He asked whether the harbor bell still rang at dawn—he'd always hated it, he said, and then, reconsidering, decided he'd missed it terribly. He asked Serah if she'd been happy in her service, and she lied and said yes, and he smiled like he knew it was a lie and appreciated the kindness of it.

Then he looked at me.

"You read the journal," he said.

"All of it. Out loud."

"Good boy." A ghost of humor. "My mother read to me. At the end there, it was almost like—" He stopped. Started again. "The binding rite. What did it cost you?"

I opened my mouth to lie, the way Serah had lied, kindly.

And found I couldn't feel the answer.

That's how I knew. Before I checked, before I reached inward for the warm current that had run through me since childhood—the hum under my skin, the sense of the city's magic like a second heartbeat, the gift my grandmother had passed down through four generations of Blackwoods—I knew, because when I reached for it, there was no reaching. There was no inward. There was just me, Paloma Blackwood, standing in a wrecked throne room, feeling exactly as much and exactly as little as any man on the street.

"It took it," I said. "All of it. The inheritance. The whole line, back to the beginning. It's gone."

Aldric studied me with those newly-freed eyes, and what I saw in them wasn't pity. It was recognition. One freed thing looking at another.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Don't be." And I meant it, mostly. "You were in there thirty years. I only lost what I never earned."

"That's not how inheritance works," he said, with the faintest smile. "None of us earn it. We just carry it." His breath rattled. Shorter now. "Will you—"

"What?"

"Tell them. The council, the people, whoever comes after. Tell them I begged. Tell them the guild bound me at my own request, and that I forgive them, and that the crime was the Crown's alone." His hand found mine—papery, weightless, gripping with the last strength of a dying king. "History will want someone to blame. Give them the right name."

"I will," I said. "I swear it."

"Good." He closed his eyes. Opened them one more time. "The harbor bell," he murmured. "Let it ring however it likes."

And King Aldric, last of his name, first victim and longest prisoner of the Hollow Crown, died in his own throne room at dawn, with his soul his own, his debts confessed, and two strangers—a disgraced witch-hunter's sister and a giftless charm-seller—holding his hands.

It was, I thought, more than most kings get. More than most *people* get.

Serah bowed her head. I didn't know the words, so I just stood there, ordinary as bread, and kept him company while he went.

* * *

Dawn found us on the palace steps.

I don't remember walking down. I remember the light—grey-gold, the color of the moment before the harbor bell—and I remember the crowd. Thousands of them, filling the plaza and spilling into every street, the people's quiet army no longer quiet, no longer an army, just... people. Charm-sellers and dockworkers and laundresses and children, covered in the ash and glitter of a night's worth of battle-magic, staring up at the palace doors as though expecting the building itself to bite.

They saw us come out. They saw the sword at Serah's hip and the blood on us both and the empty look on my face, and a ripple went through the crowd—fear first, old fear, the fear of hunters and thrones—and then someone, I never learned who, began to cheer.

The sound hit me like a wave. I stood there and let it break over me and felt... nothing. That's the honest truth, the ugly truth: the crowd roared my name—*Blackwood! Blackwood!*—and I felt nothing, because the part of me that would have felt it, the part that could taste the joy in the air like sugar, the part that thrummed in harmony with a thousand small magics waking up all over the city—that part was spent. Burned away. Ash in the grate.

I was ordinary. Completely, irrevocably ordinary. I couldn't feel a single charm in the crowd. Couldn't sense the returning magic settling into its new homes. Couldn't do so much as light a candle with anything but a match.

Paloma Blackwood, heir of the Blackwood guild, breaker of the Hollow Crown, savior of Corvane: a man who could now sell luck-charms that worked exactly as well as anyone else's, which is to say not at all.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Right there on the palace steps, with the crowd roaring and Serah staring at me like she was afraid I'd broken along with the Crown, I laughed until my ribs hurt, and then I laughed some more because I was crying and the two sounds got tangled together and came out as something that was probably hysterical and definitely necessary.

"Paloma." Serah gripped my shoulder. "Paloma, what's wrong?"

"Nothing." I wiped my face. "Everything. I don't know. I'm—" I gestured at myself, at all of me. "Empty. She warned me, you know. Grandmother. The rite consumes the caster's inherited gift entirely, she wrote. Entirely. And I thought, fine, yes, I'll pay that, obviously, how hard can it be?" I laughed again, wetter this time. "Turns out it's like losing a limb you never saw. Like finding out you've been hearing music your whole life and now the music's stopped and the silence has a *shape*."

Serah was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone of an older sister delivering a verdict: "You look terrible."

"I feel terrible."

"Good. That means you're alive." She pulled me into a hug before I could protest, which is very much a hunter's technique—strike before the target can react—and held on. "You're alive, Paloma. Tovan's dead, Maren's dead, half the Row is rubble, and you're *alive*, and the king died free, and the Crown is *broken*, and I am never arresting you, and if you tell anyone I hugged you on the palace steps I'll deny it to my grave."

"Noted," I said into her shoulder.

She pulled back, and we stood there, brother and sister, estranged for a decade and reconciled for approximately six hours, watching the sun come up over a city that would never be the same.

The crowd was still cheering. Someone had started a song—one of the old work songs, the ones with hidden wards woven through the verses, sung openly now for the first time in a generation. It spread through the plaza like fire through dry grass, hundreds of voices, thousands, and the hidden magic in the song rose up with it, visible now as faint gold threads weaving through the morning air, and nobody screamed, and nobody ran, and nobody called the hunters, because there were no hunters anymore.

Magic, out loud. In daylight. In Corvane.

I watched it and felt the silence where my gift used to be, and do you know what I found there, in that silence, underneath the grief?

Relief.

Not joy—relief. The relief of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy. All my life I'd hidden, hunched, flinched, lied, sold fake charms to hide real ones, loved people at arm's length because closeness meant exposure. And now there was nothing to hide. Nothing to lose. No secret left that anyone would burn me for.

For the first time since I was a child, Paloma Blackwood had nothing to conceal.

It was the strangest freedom I've ever worn, and it fit terribly, and I think I'll grow into it.

* * *

They buried King Aldric three days later, in the old royal crypt beneath the Bell Gate, and the harbor bell rang at dawn for it, long and low, and I stood in the crowd and listened and thought: *he'd have hated this, and then decided he'd missed it terribly.*

The council convened in emergency session the same week. Serah was summoned to testify—summoned, notably, not arrested, which tells you something about how fast the ground was shifting. The old laws against magic were collapsing under the weight of what she'd seen in the citadel records: the hunts, the registries, the harvests. Thirty years of lawful atrocity, ordered by a dead king's parasite and executed by frightened people following frightened orders.

There would be trials. There would be reckonings. There would be years of hard, ugly work, sorting complicity from coercion, justice from vengeance. Serah walked into that session carrying evidence and a resignation letter, and I walked her to the door, and neither of us said anything, because we'd said it already, on the steps, at dawn, in the space between one heartbeat and the next.

Me, I went home. Such as home was—the old guildhouse, gutted, half-collapsed, smelling of ash and salt water. I sat in the ruins of my grandmother's parlor with her journal open on my knees, rereading it for the hundredth time, and I noticed something I'd missed before, tucked into the back cover, in handwriting I recognized as hers but younger, quicker:

*A note for whoever comes after:*

*If you are reading this, the rite was performed, and the price was paid, and you are sitting there feeling hollow as a drum and wondering what you are now.*

*Here is what you are: you are the vessel the gift traveled in, not the gift itself. Gifts pass through families like water through a channel. The channel remembers the water. That is enough.*

*Rebuild. Not the vaults, not the secrets, not the hoarded power that made us complicit in the first place. Rebuild the teaching. Put the doors open. Let them all in.*

*The guild was never the power. The guild was the passing-on.*

*I love you, whoever you are. Now go be ordinary somewhere useful.*

I sat with that a long time. Outside, through the broken roof, the sky went from grey to gold to blue, and somewhere down the street a neighbor was singing one of the warded work songs, badly, happily, and the ward in it glowed soft and shameless in the open air.

I am, I realized, the only person alive who knows everything the Blackwood guild knew—the rites, the histories, the warnings, the sins—and possesses none of the power that made any of it dangerous. Which makes me either the safest keeper of dangerous knowledge in Corvane, or the most qualified teacher the city has ever accidentally produced.

Grandmother would have laughed herself sick.

There's a knock at the door. Persistent. Familiar. When I open it, there stands a girl of perhaps twelve, gap-toothed, scowling, holding out a cracked teacup with a faint, flickering light trapped inside it like a firefly.

"Mum says you're the one who broke the Crown," she says accusingly. "Mum says you know things. Can you fix this? It won't stop glowing and Da thinks it's cursed and Nana thinks it's a blessing and they won't stop arguing."

I look at the cup. I look at the girl. I feel, in the hollow place where my gift used to live, something that isn't magic at all—but might be its close cousin.

"Yeah," I say, stepping aside to let her in. "Come in. Let's see what we've got."

Because the channel remembers the water. And it turns out there's more than one way to flow.
