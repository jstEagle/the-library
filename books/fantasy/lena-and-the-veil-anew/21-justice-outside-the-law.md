## 21. Justice Outside the Law

The order came at dawn, written in my own hand.

That was the trick of it, of course. Commander Vess had taken my draft of the arrest warrant—*Paloma Blackwood, wanted for sedition, theft of crown property, and the murder of Hunter Tovan Rell*—and copied it out in fair script, then set it before me for signature. My words, wearing someone else's handwriting. I'd spent three years learning how the Crown did that: took a thing you made and gave it back to you changed, until you couldn't remember which version was yours.

I signed nothing.

"You wrote it," Vess said. He was not a cruel man, which made him worse than a cruel one, because cruelty you can hate. He was tired, and loyal, and he believed that law was a wall that kept the flood out, and that anyone who questioned the wall had never seen the flood.

"I wrote it when I believed Tovan's death was Paloma's doing," I said. "I've since read the citadel's harvest ledgers. I'd like to amend the document."

"Amend it."

"Strike *sedition*. Strike *murder*. Add: *wanted for questioning regarding the death of King Aldric, deceased approximately nine years, cause of death: regicide by the office of the Crown itself.*"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below us, in the wrecked lower floors of the citadel, hunters who had chosen not to defect were barricading doors against hunters who had. The smell of smoke from the palace still hung over everything, sweet and wrong, like flowers burning.

Vess looked at me for a long moment. "Serah. You are the finest investigator this order has produced in twenty years. That is not flattery; that is arithmetic. Don't do this."

"Do what? Tell the truth?"

"Tell it *there*." He nodded toward the window, toward the Council Hall across the square, where the surviving councilors had convened an emergency session and summoned me—the chief investigator, the hero of the hour, the woman who could explain why the king's face had been seen moving behind the throne room windows while the throne room itself stood shattered open like an egg. "They want reassurance. Give them the reassurance. Then we handle the rest quietly, internally, as institutions do."

"Institutions," I said, "are how a dead man sat on a living throne for nine years."

He flinched. Good. Someone should.

"The mage is downstairs," Vess said finally. "Wounded. Under guard, per your own standing orders, which I have not countermanded because I keep hoping you'll countermand them yourself." He set the unsigned warrant on the table between us, squared its edges with two fingers, precise as a man laying out a corpse. "Sign it or don't. But if you walk into that hall and say what you're planning to say, understand what happens. The order falls. And when the order falls, everything it held up falls with it. The registries. The trials. The executions we told ourselves were justice. There will be no one left to blame but all of us, and no procedure left to hide inside."

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They let me see him first. I think Vess arranged it, though he'd deny it to his grave, which given recent events might be soon.

Paloma sat on a cot in a cell that smelled of wet stone, his hands bandaged to the wrists. When the gift burns out of a person, I learned afterward, it doesn't leave gently. It takes the nerves with it. His hands would never again do the small quick work they'd been made for—the palming of charms, the folding of paper birds, the sleight that had hidden a lifetime of magic inside a lifetime of nothing.

He looked up when I came in, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because what do you say? *Sorry I hunted you. Sorry you burned your whole inheritance to save a city that would have hanged you. Sorry about Maren Ostwick, whose blood was on both our hands, hers given, mine compelled.*

"You look terrible," he said.

"You should see the other guy," I said. "Oh wait. You shattered him into a thousand pieces on the palace floor."

His laugh cracked halfway through and became something else, and I crossed the cell and sat beside him on the cot, shoulder to shoulder, the way we used to sit on the roof of the guildhouse before Grandmother's madness—or her truth—broke us apart. We watched the light move through the barred window. It seemed important, somehow, not to look at each other for this part.

"They're going to ask me to testify," I said. "About you. About all of it."

"And?"

"And I'm going to tell them everything. The Crown. The harvests. The hunts ordered not to find mages but to *farm* them." I turned my head, finally, and looked at him. His eyes were the same grey as mine. Our grandmother's eyes. "Which means telling them about the guild. What the Blackwoods did. What we helped bind into that throne."

He was quiet a long moment. "You don't have to carry that part."

"It's mine to carry. That's what accountability means, Paloma. It isn't punishment. It's just... refusing to put the weight down." I stood, because if I stayed on that cot much longer I was going to cry in front of my brother for the second time in one week, and a chief investigator has a reputation. "There will be a warrant for your arrest within the hour. I'm supposed to deliver it myself."

"Are you going to?"

"No." I paused at the door. "I'm going to resign first. You can't arrest someone properly if you've made yourself unemployed. It's in the regulations. Look it up."

* * *

The Council Hall had been built to intimidate, and like most things built to intimidate, it worked best on people who already felt small. Seven councilors on the raised dais beneath the old banners of Corvane. Clerks with quills poised. A gallery packed with citizens who had come to watch the mage be condemned—and found instead, in the front row, a contingent of charm-sellers and laundresses and dockworkers sitting with their hands folded, quiet as a jury.

Commander Vess stood beside me as I entered. He had chosen, in the end, to attend. I never learned whether it was to stop me or to witness me. Perhaps those were the same thing.

"Investigator Blackwood," said Lord Chancellor Ives, a dry stick of a man with ink-stained fingers and the expression of someone perpetually auditing the universe. "The council thanks you for your service. You may present the prisoner."

"There is no prisoner," I said. "I resigned this morning. Effective an hour ago. You'll find my letter with the clerk."

A stir. Ives's quill stopped mid-word.

"Then by what authority do you address this council?"

"None whatsoever. Which is precisely why I'm going to be believed." I walked to the center of the floor, where the accused usually stood, and turned to face the dais. "For nine years, this city has been governed by a corpse. King Aldric died in the ninth year of his reign—I have the physician's sealed report, the mortuary ledger, and the testimony of the last elder of the Blackwood guild, recorded before her death. What has worn his face since is a parasitic entity bound into the crown of Corvane. It fed on harvested magic. The witch-hunts were not law enforcement. They were farming."

I let the silence sit. Silence, in a courtroom, is a tool. Use it wrong and it buries you. Use it right and it makes the room listen for you.

"The evidence is in three crates outside this hall," I continued. "Harvest ledgers. Transfer manifests. Orders bearing the privy seal, including one instructing the execution of registered gifted persons *whose gifts had ripened*, dated four years ago—signed under seal by officials in this building. Some of them are in this room."

Another stir, uglier this time. In the gallery, a woman in laundress grey folded her hands more tightly and did not look away.

"You'll be arrested for treason before you finish the day," said Councilor Dane, red-faced, rising.

"Probably," I agreed. "But you'll want to hear the rest first, because the crates also contain the name of every official who took payment from the harvest transfers, and I've given copies to three printers, two harbor captains, and a very persuasive grandmother of six who owes me a favor. Arresting me now would be an act of public relations, not justice. Choose carefully."

Dane sat down. Slowly. Like a man lowering himself into cold water.

Lord Chancellor Ives steepled his fingers. "You expect this council to simply accept that the laws of Corvane—laws that have held for a generation—are founded on a lie?"

"No," I said. "I expect you to accept something worse. That the laws were true once, and honest men built them, after a war that frightened everyone senseless. And then something hollow moved into the house those laws built, and used the locks against the people inside." I looked around the hall—at the clerks, the guards, the gallery full of ordinary faces. "Magic didn't fail Corvane. Fear did. Fear is the only thing the Hollow Crown ever needed to survive. Starve it of fear and it starves."

"And the mage?" Ives asked. "Paloma Blackwood. Your brother. He broke the Crown. He destroyed the binding. By your own account he wielded forbidden power at a scale this city hasn't seen since the Veil War. What does your justice require of *him*?"

And there it was. The question I'd been carrying since the throne room, since I'd stood shielding my brother with my body while the world came apart above us.

I thought about the law. The law was clear. The law said he should hang, and the law had been clear right up until the moment it became a harvesting tool, and I had enforced it faithfully through every step of that descent, signing warrants, burning registers only when secrecy demanded it rather than conscience, telling myself each compromise was the last.

I thought about Maren Ostwick dying on Laundress Row so that a boy she'd tested with fire could run free.

"He pays," I said. "Not to the gallows. To the people. His gift is gone—it burned out entirely in the unbinding; the healers can confirm it. What he has left is knowledge, and knowledge is what this city actually lost when we outlawed half of ourselves. Let him teach. Let him stand in the rebuilt guildhouse and give back everything the Blackwood guild hoarded for a hundred years. That is his sentence: service, in the open, under the eyes of everyone he serves. If he ever uses coercion—if any gifted person does—that's a crime, tried openly, by a court that includes gifted jurors. Not because magic is special. Because *people* are."

"That's not law," said Ives. "That's philosophy."

"My lord chancellor," I said, "the law just spent nine years eating its own citizens. I think it could stand to take some philosophy."

* * *

It took eleven days.

Eleven days of sessions, of crates opened before the full council, of officials resigning in the night and one—Councilor Dane himself, as it happened—taken away in irons when the transfer manifests caught up with him. Eleven days of Serah Blackwood, formerly chief investigator, now merely a citizen with excellent documentation, standing in the same spot on the floor and answering questions until her voice went raw.

On the eleventh day, the council voted. The Registration Act: repealed. The Proscription of Gifted Practice: repealed. The hunter order: dissolved pending full inquiry, its records seized, its serving members offered amnesty conditional on testimony. The old Veil War statutes—every one of them—struck from the books in a single afternoon, and the bells of Corvane rang at dusk for the first time in a generation without announcing an execution.

I stood in the square afterward and listened, and found I was holding someone's hand—a dockworker I'd once nearly arrested, who had simply reached over and taken it, the way you comfort a stranger at a funeral.

Vess found me there. He'd surrendered his commission the day after my testimony; he said someone had to audit the collapse from the inside, and he was, annoyingly, the most qualified.

"You know what the histories will say about you," he said.

"That I betrayed my order."

"That you fulfilled it," he said. "Late, and at cost, but actually. There's a difference, and the historians will argue about it forever, which is how you'll know it mattered." He handed me a folded paper. My resignation letter, returned, stamped *received and filed*. At the bottom, in his own hand, he'd added a line: *The wall fell. The flood did not come.*

I laughed, which surprised us both.

Behind us, the crowd was thinning, drifting home through streets that smelled—for the first time in years—of bread and rain instead of ash. And at the edge of the square, leaning against a lamppost with his bandaged hands tucked in his pockets, stood my brother, watching the bells swing, waiting for me with the particular patience of a man who has already survived the worst day of his life and intends to enjoy the second-worst.

"Well?" he called. "Unemployed and infamous. How does it feel?"

I started toward him, and somewhere past the lamppost, past the square, past the ruined gates of the old guildhouse where tomorrow a new kind of teaching would begin, I heard myself answer—

"Like the first honest day's work I've done in years. Come on. We've got a guild to rebuild, and you're going to need someone to handle the paperwork."

He grinned, and the bells rang on, and Corvane—wounded, grieving, unbowed—began, at last, to live.
