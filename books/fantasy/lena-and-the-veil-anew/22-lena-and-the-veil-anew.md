## 22. Lena and the Veil Anew

The bell over the guildhouse door still rings wrong. I've been meaning to fix it for a month, and I keep not fixing it, because it's the same bell that hung there when my grandmother was alive, and a wrong-ringing bell is at least a familiar wrongness. There's a kind of comfort in that, I've found. Most of what I loved about the old world is gone or changed beyond recognition, but the bell still sounds like a coin dropped in an empty cup, and so I let it be.

One year.

I stood on the guildhouse steps this morning with a broom, sweeping ash from last night's festival off the stones—paper lanterns, spilled wine, someone's lost shoe—and I thought: one year ago I was hiding charms inside hollow buttons and lying to every customer who walked through my stall. One year ago, saying the word *magic* out loud in Corvane could get you a cell and then a rope. And now there's chalk on my doorstep. A child drew it—a spiral, wobbly, done by someone six years old at most—with little stars around it, because children have decided that spirals mean magic the way they once decided dragons meant adventure, and no one has told them otherwise, because no one needs to anymore.

I swept around the spiral. Some things you don't clean up.

* * *

The morning class arrives at nine, which means they start arriving at half past eight, because children have no respect for scheduled beginnings. Lena comes first, as always. She's eleven, gap-toothed, ferocious, the daughter of a dockworker named Brannic whom I once sold a luck-charm back when luck was contraband. Lena was born eight months after the Veil War's end, into a city where her mother taught her to whisper her small gifts into pillows so the walls wouldn't hear. Now she walks through the Bell Gate with her chin up and sparks literally coming off her fingertips when she's excited, which is always, and the guards at the gate nod to her like she's a person, which took me a moment, even now, to stop being astonished by.

"Master Paloma." She skids to a halt in the courtyard, breathless. "Master Paloma, the well-water did the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The *thing*. The swirly thing. Tam says it's because of the ley-line under the street but Tam is wrong because ley-lines don't run under streets, they run under *churches*, you said so yourself."

"I said ley-lines are like rivers," I said, setting down the broom. "And rivers go wherever they please, including under streets. What did the water do?"

"It went up. Out of the bucket. Just a little. Like it was standing on its toes."

I looked at her for a long moment. A year ago, water moving against its nature would have meant hunters, registers, ash. Now it meant a Tuesday.

"Well," I said. "Did anyone tell it to stop?"

"No. We watched. It was beautiful."

"Then next time, watch longer. And write down how long it lasted. Magic likes being taken seriously. It misbehaves when it's ignored, like most living things."

She grinned and ran inside to tell Tam he was wrong, loudly, in front of everyone, which is the traditional way.

Here is the thing I could never have explained to myself a year ago, standing in the drowned chapel with salt water in my lungs and a key in my fist: I thought we were fighting to survive. I thought the whole war—the keys, the rite, the Crown, all of it—was a fight for permission to keep existing in the cracks. I did not understand that we were fighting for something larger and stranger: the right to teach. Hoarding is what fear does with a gift. My grandmother hoarded. The Blackwood guild hoarded, for generations, keeping its knowledge behind initiations and bloodlines and oaths, and look where that got us—one paranoid old woman burying a journal in the dirt, one dying elder doing laundry until her hands gave out, and a king eaten alive by the thing his own protectors were too proud to understand.

Maren told me once, near the end, her side pressed against my shoulder where the wound would soon be: "We kept the flame in a box because we were afraid of the wind. But a flame in a box goes out, child. It only looks safe."

So the doors are open now. That was the first law of the new guild, written before anything else, before the charter, before the curriculum, before we'd even swept the floors: *Anyone may enter. Anyone may learn.* Not because every person has the gift—most don't, and that's fine—but because fear grows fastest in the dark, and we had all seen what Corvane became when magic was a rumor instead of a fact.

* * *

At midday I teach the class nobody expected me to teach: the non-gifted students. Yes, they exist. More of them than anyone predicted, actually. When the council lifted the ban and the guild reopened, we braced for a flood of gifted children and got something else entirely—a crowd. Fishwives and clerks and retired soldiers and one extremely serious baker who wanted to know why his sourdough behaved strangely near the harbor. People who couldn't light a candle with a thought if their lives depended on it, and knew it, and came anyway.

"Why?" Serah asked me once, watching them file in. She still does that sometimes—appears at the edge of my classroom unannounced, arms crossed, wearing grey out of habit though the hunter's order is dissolved, watching me work like she's checking my arithmetic. "They can't do any of it. Why sit through hours of theory?"

"Because they live here," I said. "In a city full of it. You can either understand the river you're standing in or get surprised by it. Besides—" I nodded toward old Hesta, the fishwife, who was taking notes with the grim diligence of a woman who had survived three plagues and intended to survive metaphysics too. "Hesta will never cast a spell in her life. But she'll know when someone else is casting one badly. That's worth more than half the charms I ever sold."

Serah didn't answer right away. Then she said, quietly, "Grandmother would have hated this."

"She would have hated it enormously," I agreed. "All those strangers touching her books. And then she would have stayed anyway, and corrected everyone's pronunciation, and secretly been pleased."

That got the closest thing to a laugh I ever get out of Serah these days. Progress. We're rebuilding that too, brick by brick, conversation by stilted conversation. Some nights we eat together at her rooms above the old registry office—she kept the office, of all things, turned it into an archive where the burned registry's survivors can come reclaim their names. She's good at it. Order from ash. It turns out my sister was never made for hunting; she was made for guarding, and the Crown just pointed her guard-dog instincts at the wrong door for twenty years.

* * *

Afternoon. Advanced theory, which today means six teenagers, two adults, and a philosophical argument about whether the well-water incident constitutes "levitation" or merely "enthusiasm."

Tam raises his hand. Tam is fourteen, solemn, gifted with the particular talent of making things heavier, which he considers a tragedy and which I consider one of the most useful gifts in the building. "Master Paloma. If the gift isn't inherited—like, strictly—if it shows up anywhere, in anyone—then what makes a family? A magical family, I mean. Like yours was."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when a question lands harder than the asker knows.

I sat down on the edge of my desk. Old habit; new desk; the old one burned with everything else.

"When I was your age," I said, "I thought inheritance was a thing you *had*. Grandmother's gift lived in our blood, passed down like eye color, and my job was to not lose it. Keep it hidden, keep it whole, hand it on intact. That was the whole religion. Possession and preservation." I turned my left hand over, palm up. Empty. It's been empty for a year, and some mornings I still wake up reaching for a warmth that isn't there, the way you reach for a railing on stairs that aren't there anymore. "Then I spent it. All of it. Every scrap of the Blackwood power went into breaking the Crown, and when the dust settled I was the least magical person in Corvane. Truly. Hesta can sense a warding at fifty paces and I can't find my own boots without looking."

Someone giggled. I let them.

"And for a while I grieved it like a death, because it was one. But here's what I learned, and it's the only exam question that matters, so write it down." I waited until pencils actually moved, because I've earned that much. "A gift is not a possession. It's a *practice*. It's what you do with what you know, passed hand to hand. Grandmother's real inheritance wasn't her power—it was her journal, her stubbornness, her terrible handwriting, her conviction that somebody should write the truth down even if nobody reads it for thirty years. Maren's inheritance wasn't her gift either. It was the lesson plan she carried in her head for forty years, waiting for a world that would let her use it. I'm just the delivery mechanism. So am all of you, eventually."

Lena's hand shot up. "So you're still magical. Even without the—you know. Even empty."

"I'm the most magical person I know," I said. "I just perform my magic entirely through other people. It's very efficient. No fatigue, no backlash, and the effects continue whether I'm awake or not."

"That's just teaching," said Tam.

"Yes," I said. "Exactly. Well spotted. Full marks."

* * *

Evening. The students gone, the lamps lit, the bell silent at last. I was cataloguing donations—we receive strange offerings now, since word spread that the guild accepts anything with history: a cracked scrying bowl from a widow in Salt Row, three volumes of hedge-healing lore from a farmer who'd kept them under his floorboards through the whole interregnum, and today, wrapped in oilcloth, a bundle of letters that made me sit down hard on my chair.

Letters from my grandmother. To Maren, apparently, from the years before the burying—before the journal, before the silence. Young grandmother. Frightened grandmother. In the second letter, in handwriting steadier than I ever knew it, she wrote:

*Maren — if the guild must die to save the city, then let it die, but hide the seeds. Not the power. The power is nothing. Hide the knowing. Someone will come asking questions in thirty years and they will need more than answers; they will need to know the questions were worth asking.*

I read it four times. Then I put it in the archive copy-book, in Serah's careful hand-to-be, and I blew out the lamps except one, and I sat in the dark of my grandmother's guildhouse—which is my guildhouse, which is nobody's guildhouse, which belongs to whoever walks through the door tomorrow wanting to learn—and I thought about seeds.

A year ago I broke the Hollow Crown and killed my own inheritance doing it, and I would do it again before breakfast, and the strange grace of it is this: nothing was actually lost. The gift I spent was a hoard. Hoards burn. Seeds don't. Seeds do exactly one thing, which is become something other than themselves, and that is the entire secret, and it took me a war and a dead king and an empty palm to understand it.

Tomorrow Lena will ask me about the well again, and Tam will argue about ley-lines, and Hesta will take notes like the world depends on it, and maybe it does, a little. Tomorrow the bell will ring wrong and I will still not fix it.

Tonight, though, walking home through Corvane—through a city where lanterns glow without oil in shop windows and nobody flinches, where a guard hums a charm-lullaby off-key, where the Veil lies torn open and gentle and full of ordinary wonders—I passed the Bell Gate and saw fresh chalk on the stones. A new spiral. Bigger than the one on my doorstep. And beneath it, in a child's uneven letters, someone had written a word I hadn't seen written in public since before the War.

Not *magic*. We say that word freely now; it's lost its teeth.

The word was *school*.

With an arrow pointing toward the guildhouse, and underneath the arrow, smaller, in different handwriting—someone else, some other kid, adding their own mark to a stranger's sign, the way marks accumulate on old doors:

*everyone welcome.*

I stood there a long time, an empty-handed man in a full-handed city, grinning at chalk like a fool.

Then I went home to prepare tomorrow's lesson, because the seeds were already planted, and they were growing faster than any of us knew how to prune.
