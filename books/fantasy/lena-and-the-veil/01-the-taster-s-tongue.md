## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The quail arrived at the king's table on a silver platter, glazed in honey and rowan berries, arranged so that its little roasted body seemed mid-flight. Oscar Sandoval looked at it the way a priest looks at a sinner: with professional affection and no illusions whatsoever.

"Master Sandoval." The steward's voice carried the particular hush reserved for moments when a man might die. "If you please."

Oscar stepped forward from his station along the wall, where he had spent the last two hours being furniture — one of those necessary furnishings of power that everyone forgets until it saves their life. He was a narrow man, thirty-one years of age, with ink-stained fingers he kept carefully hidden beneath white serving gloves, because a food-taster with ink-stained fingers invites questions about what else he does after dark.

He lifted the quail from the platter. It was warm. It smelled of honey, rowan, thyme, and — beneath all that, faint as a whisper through a wall — something bitter and green.

*There you are,* he thought.

He carved away a portion of breast meat, chewed slowly, and let his tongue do the work it had been trained to do since he was nine years old, when a grim old taster named Hobb had put a drop of larkspur tea on his tongue and told him, *Learn this one well, boy, because it's the one most likely to kill you.*

Bitterness first, at the back of the throat. Then a numbness beginning at the root of the tongue, spreading forward like frost across a windowpane. A slight metallic edge underneath, like biting a coin. And a sweetness that didn't belong — the cloying, wrong sweetness of foxglove syrup masking its own crime.

Foxglove, cut with something to blunt the bitterness. Larkspur, possibly, or monkshood if the cook was ambitious. Dosed not to kill outright but to sicken — to make the king ill over days, weak and confused, while whoever held the vial waited.

Amateur work, honestly. Oscar had tasted worse from guild apprentices.

He swallowed.

Around him, the banquet continued: forty nobles pretending they weren't watching, the queen laughing at something too loudly, the king — old King Aldous, whose hands trembled these days even when lifting a goblet — waiting with the terrible patience of a man who has outlived three tasters and knows exactly what the fourth is doing.

Oscar set down the carved portion, bowed, and said clearly: "The quail is excellent, Your Majesty. The kitchen has outdone itself."

A ripple of relief went around the table. The king reached for the bird.

And Oscar walked back to his place against the wall, folded his hands, and began counting.

One hundred heartbeats before the numbness would reach his lips. Two hundred before it climbed toward his throat. He had taken perhaps three times the dose that would lay a strong man flat by morning, and he had done it deliberately, because a taster who only ever took tiny sips grew careless, and carelessness in his profession was measured in funerals.

Three hundred heartbeats. His fingertips had gone pleasantly distant, as if they belonged to someone standing slightly behind him. Four hundred. The taste of coins filled his mouth. Five hundred, and sweat prickled at his hairline, and somewhere in the room a violin sawed away at a dance tune as though the world were a reasonable place.

Six hundred. The numbness crested, hovered — and then, as it always did, as it had done since he was a child who should have died a dozen times over, it *receded*. His blood did whatever his blood did, the thing no physician had ever satisfactorily explained, and the poison broke apart like sugar in warm water.

By the time the king had finished his second helping, Oscar Sandoval felt nothing worse than he might after a long walk in cold air.

He was, he reflected, very good at his job. This was both his pride and, if he was honest with himself in the small hours, rather a sad thing to be proud of. Most men built themselves out of ambitions or loves or grievances. Oscar had built himself out of precautions. He knew which of the palace's four hundred and twelve doors stuck. He knew which wells fed which fountains. He kept antidotes sewn into the lining of every coat he owned, including the summer ones, because assassins were not seasonal workers. He chewed a pinch of fireleaf every morning the way other men took snuff, and he had not eaten an untested meal in twenty-two years.

*A man made of precautions,* his mother would have said, if he'd had a mother he could remember, which was a wound he'd long since stopped poking. *A man made of locks, guarding doors that open onto nothing.*

He smiled at the second violinist, who smiled back uncertainly, and waited for the fish course, which smelled honest, and tasted it anyway.

* * *

Midnight found him three levels below the streets, in a cellar that officially did not exist, lit by lamps turned down to a glow the color of weak tea.

The Guild of Quiet Commerce kept its offices above ground in a respectable counting-house on Candlemaker's Row, all brass fittings and sober clerks, dealing in the sorts of goods that made respectable profits: silks, salt, insurance against weather. But the real business happened here, forty feet under the fish market, in a cellar lined with shelves, and the shelves were lined with things that had no business existing.

Oscar hung his coat on a peg, removed his gloves, and sat down at his desk — a broad oak thing scarred by a century of elbows — where the ledger lay open, waiting for him like a dog that knows the sound of its master's boots.

He dipped his pen. He was, before anything else, a bookkeeper. The tasting paid the rent; the ledgers were the life.

*Received into stock, this 14th day of Embermonth:* he wrote, *one jar, sealed wax, contents: courage, sourced from a soldier of the border wars, quantity sufficient for one coward to face one wedding, one funeral, or one creditor. Condition of donor: willing. Payment rendered: the memory of his worst day, which he reports he does not miss.*

He blotted the entry and considered it. Somewhere in the shelves behind him, between a bottle of last winter's thunderstorm and a box of borrowed luck (three uses remaining), sat that jar of courage. He had inspected it personally. Courage, properly bottled, looked like pale gold smoke that leaned toward you when you opened the lid, the way a plant leans toward light. It wanted to be used. That was the trouble with everything in this cellar: it all *wanted* something.

*Also received:* he continued, *one Tuesday, belonging to a clerk of the customs house, who found himself in possession of a spare and could not account for its origin. Sold onward same evening to a widow, who reports she will use it to attend her daughter's recital twice.*

That one had given Oscar a headache for a week. Not the accounting of it — the accounting was simple enough, one Tuesday in, one Tuesday out, and the widow's coin was good. What troubled him was the metaphysics. Somewhere there was now a version of events in which the customs clerk had never lived that Tuesday at all, and somewhere else a recital hall in which a girl performed twice to the same scattered applause, and Oscar's neat columns of figures sat underneath it all like the foundations of a house built on weather.

He had raised the question once, years ago, with old Master Ferrow, Corvane's predecessor. Ferrow had stroked his beard and said, "Bookkeeping is not philosophy, Sandoval. Our task is to record what is traded, not to ask why the gods permit it."

"And if the gods don't permit it?" Oscar had asked.

"Then we are in a great deal of trouble," Ferrow had said pleasantly, "and it will be someone else's."

Ferrow was dead now, three years gone, and Master Corvane ruled the guild in his place — Corvane, who wore grey like other men wore black, who spoke softly and paid promptly and had doubled the guild's profits in three years through channels he did not explain. Corvane, who had taken one look at Oscar's immaculate ledgers and said, "You count like a man who wants the world to make sense. I value that."

Oscar had taken it as a compliment. He still wasn't entirely sure it had been one.

He worked through the night's entries. A name — an actual name, spoken once into a locket by a dying woman, good for opening exactly one door anywhere in the city, buyer unknown. A vial of sleep drawn from a hibernating bear, sold to an insomniac duchess. Six inches of ribbon that had been tied around a promise, now empty of the promise but still faintly warm.

Ordinary impossibilities. Comfortable ones, almost.

It was near the third bell when he came to the entries that weren't his.

They appeared in the intake ledger every few weeks now: *Veil-thread, received, quantity: X spools. Source: private. Buyer: private.* No condition of donor. No description beyond the word itself. And the quantities — Oscar ran his finger down the column, checking his own arithmetic twice, then a third time, because numbers were the only people he trusted completely — the quantities were growing. Half a spool, months ago. Then one. Then two. Last week, five.

Five spools of Veil-thread. Oscar had been keeping the guild's books for seven years and he had never once seen more than a decorative inch of the stuff, sold at prices that made the bottled courage look like turnips. It was the single item in the entire inventory that old Ferrow had refused to stock, and when a junior clerk had once asked why, Ferrow had looked at the boy for a long moment and said only: "Because some goods cost more than money, and the difference comes due all at once."

Now Corvane was buying it by the spool. Buying it steadily, privately, in quantities that had no corresponding sales entry anywhere in Oscar's meticulous records — and Oscar's records were *complete*, that was the whole point of them, that was his one article of faith. Every impossible thing that passed through the Guild of Quiet Commerce passed through his columns. Except, apparently, this.

Which meant either that Corvane was keeping a second ledger —

— and here Oscar's pen stopped, hovering, leaving a small blot on the page —

— or that the Veil-thread was not passing through the guild at all. That it was going *out*, through some door he couldn't see, to some buyer he couldn't name, in quantities that were growing like a fever chart.

Oscar sat back in the lamplight and listened to the cellar. The bottles murmured to each other in their glass voices, the way they did when the lamps burned low — the thunderstorm grumbling, the borrowed luck rattling hopefully, the jar of courage leaning toward the door. Below the floor, faint and rhythmic, came the sound of the fish market's ice melting, drip by drip, into drains that had no business being this deep.

He looked down at the ledger, at his own tidy hand, at the column of growing numbers that added up to nothing he could name.

Oscar Sandoval trusted numbers above all people, because numbers had never once lied to him. And the numbers were telling him, in their patient, silent way, that something in his careful world had stopped balancing.

He wrote one final entry — not in the official ledger but in the small private notebook he kept sewn into his sleeve, the one no one knew about, because a man made of precautions keeps precautions about his precautions:

*Embermonth 14. Veil-thread intake again exceeds recorded sales. Discrepancy now totals eleven spools. Query for tomorrow: where does it go?*

He blotted it, closed the book, and blew out the lamps.

Above him, the city slept on, unaware that its foundations were being counted — and found, for the first time in seven years, short.
