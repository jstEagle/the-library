## 2. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The cellar beneath the Chandlery Row chandler's shop smelled, as always, of tallow and old paper and the particular cold that lives in places where money changes hands without ever being seen. Oscar Sandoval came down the stairs the way he came down all stairs: carefully, one hand on the rail, counting the steps out of habit rather than need. Fourteen steps. There had been fourteen steps for as long as he had kept this ledger, and he liked that about them.

He lit the lamp on his desk — a real desk, oak, with a drawer that locked three ways — and set his satchel beside it. Then he did what he did every night after the palace kitchens released him: he took off his food-taster's coat, hung it on the peg with the reverence other men reserved for vestments, and became, quietly and completely, himself.

Oscar Sandoval was, by daylight, a servant. A good one. He had a palate the royal physicians called a gift and the royal steward called an expense, and he tasted everything the king ate so that the king could go on living long enough to eat more of it. It was honest work, in its way. It paid for his lodging above the cobbler's on Tanners' Lane, for his tea, for the small hoard of copper coins he kept in a jar labeled RAINY DAY in his own neat hand.

But by night — or rather, by the hours between midnight and the third bell, which were not properly night but something adjacent to it — Oscar Sandoval was the bookkeeper of the Meridian Guild, which traded in impossible things.

He unlocked the drawer, took out the ledger, and opened it to tonight's page. The ledger itself was unremarkable to look at: leather-bound, brass-cornered, the kind of book any merchant might own. The remarkable part was what it recorded, and the fact that it balanced. It always balanced. That was the whole point of Oscar. Anyone could sell a bottled memory. Selling it *accountably* — that took a man who trusted numbers above all people, including, on most days, himself.

* * *

The first entry arrived at half past midnight, carried down the stairs by a nervous apprentice named Fennick who held the parcel at arm's length as though it might bite him. In fairness, it might have. Guild policy discouraged biting parcels, but policy and possibility were different ledgers entirely.

"From the Vellum Street consignment," Fennick said. "Master says log it quick."

"Master can wait for me to do it right," Oscar said, which was as close to rebellion as he ever got, and even that only because Corvane wasn't present to hear it.

He unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside sat a squat glass jar, stoppered with wax, filled with nothing visible at all — just a faint amber shimmer, like sunlight remembered rather than seen. The label, in the seller's cramped script, read: *Courage, one measure. Not mine. Never was.*

"Whose is it?" Fennick whispered.

"Seller's discretion," Oscar said. "That's what the discretion column is for." He turned to the appropriate page. "Jarred courage, one measure, provenance withheld, condition excellent. Purchased from—" he squinted at the wax seal— "the Widow Asque, Vellum Street. Payment rendered: forty crowns and one favor, favor valued at fifteen. Total fifty-five."

Fennick watched him write with the expression of a boy watching a priest perform rites. "Does it work? The courage?"

"It works," Oscar said. "Whether it works *well* depends on whose courage you're borrowing. The Widow Asque's late husband was a fire-brigade man. Very sound courage. Holds its shape under pressure." He stoppered the jar again and set it on the shelf marked LIQUID VIRTUES, between a bottle of borrowed patience and a vial of somebody's grandmother's luck, which was nearly empty because everybody wanted grandmother's luck and nobody wanted to give theirs back.

"And if someone drinks it and it doesn't suit them?"

"Then they bring it back within nine days and we deduct a restocking fee." Oscar said this without irony, because there was no irony in it. He had once spent an entire evening drafting the refund schedule for returned courage, and he stood behind it. "Impossible things are still commerce, Fennick. Commerce has rules. That's what makes it possible."

The boy nodded solemnly, as though this were wisdom, and went back up the stairs. Oscar allowed himself half a smile. It was, he supposed, wisdom. It was certainly the only kind he had.

* * *

The second entry came to him rather than through him, which was rarer and always made him suspicious. At the first bell past midnight, the cellar door opened and Master Corvane descended — not often, not lately, and never without purpose.

Corvane was a tall man built like a question mark that had decided to stand up straight out of spite. He wore grey the way other men wore armor, and he smiled the way accountants frowned: precisely, and only when the numbers justified it.

"Sandoval." He said the name pleasantly. He said everything pleasantly; it was one of the many reasons Oscar trusted him exactly as far as he could audit him. "Working late."

"I always work late, Master."

"Yes. It's one of your virtues. I keep a list." He drifted along the shelves, trailing one finger across the labels without touching anything — a habit Oscar approved of deeply. People who touched inventory were people who would eventually steal inventory. "Anything unusual tonight?"

"A jar of courage. Sound provenance. And I'm expecting a delivery of Tuesdays before dawn."

Corvane paused. "Tuesdays."

"Stolen ones," Oscar clarified, turning to the correct page. "There's a man in Lowmarsh who extracts them. Whole Tuesdays, removed from a person's life — the dull ones, mostly, the Tuesdays where nothing happened and no one would miss them. He sells them to people who've run out of week. Students, mostly. Grieving widows around quarter-days." He wrote as he spoke: *One Tuesday, gently used, donor unaware, condition fair — slight rain damage.* "They keep poorly. Tuesdays. Too much weather in them."

Something flickered across Corvane's face — amusement, or the memory of it. "And people buy these."

"People buy everything, Master. That's the finding of my entire career." Oscar blotted the entry. "Though I'll note we're overstocked. Fourteen Tuesdays in inventory against a moving average of two per month. If we don't move them by autumn they'll spoil into Mondays, and nobody wants those."

"I'll leave the inventory questions to you," Corvane said, which was either trust or abdication, and Oscar had spent three years trying to determine which. "You have a gift, Sandoval. You make the impossible look tidy."

"Thank you, Master."

"It wasn't entirely a compliment." But he said it pleasantly, and then he left, taking his pleasantness up the stairs with him, and Oscar sat for a moment in the lamplight feeling the way he always felt after speaking with Corvane: as though he'd been weighed, and the figure entered somewhere he couldn't see.

He shook it off. He had work to do.

* * *

It was while reconciling the quarterly columns — the great pleasure of his week, the hour when the guild's chaos resolved into arithmetic like sediment settling out of river water — that Oscar found the thing that didn't fit.

He found it the way he found everything: by trusting the numbers.

Column seven was acquisitions. Ordinary enough, lately. Courage, Tuesdays, a consignment of secondhand lullabies (sold by weight), six months of someone's forgotten childhood purchased from a man who claimed he'd simply grown tired of carrying it. All logged. All priced. All balancing against payments rendered in coin, favors, or trade.

Except.

Every ninth day or so, stretching back four months now, there was a purchase entry with no corresponding acquisition. Payment out — and substantial payment, sums that made Oscar's eyebrows climb toward his hairline — credited to sellers listed only by initial. *M.K.* *R.V.* *A single asterisk, which was not a name at all and should not have been permitted in his ledger.*

And against each payment, in the goods-received column, the same notation, over and over, in handwriting that was almost his own but slightly wrong, like a portrait painted by someone who'd only heard him described:

*Veil-thread. Qty as agreed.*

Oscar set down his pen.

He read the entries again. Then again, slower, the way he tasted a suspect dish — first the surface, then the structure, then the thing underneath the structure. Fourteen purchases in four months. No vendor addresses. No condition notes. No storage location, which meant the goods weren't in the cellar, which meant they were somewhere else, which meant someone else knew where, which meant—

Which meant Oscar Sandoval, keeper of the tidy books, had been signing off on a blind column for a hundred and twenty-six days.

He did the arithmetic again because the arithmetic was the only witness he'd ever fully believed. Fourteen lots. Average lot value, conservatively estimated: three hundred crowns. Total: four thousand two hundred crowns, plus or minus the favors, flowing out of the guild's accounts and into the dark, in exchange for a commodity that appeared nowhere in the intake registers, occupied no shelf, and was described in not one single entry by so much as a color.

Veil-thread.

He knew the phrase the way he knew half the guild's vocabulary — from the margins, from the gossip of couriers, from a joke Fennick had once made about a customer who'd asked for it and been laughed out of the shop. Everyone knew the words. Nobody, as far as Oscar's records showed, had ever actually sold any. It was the guild's unicorn. Its philosopher's stone. The thing mentioned to make the other impossibilities seem reasonable by comparison.

And now it was in his ledger fourteen times, and he hadn't noticed, and worse — far worse, and he made himself look at this squarely, because looking squarely was another of his virtues — worse, the entries were initialed. *O.S.* His initials, in ink that matched the period, beside figures he had never written.

He checked the ink. He always checked the ink. Iron-gall darkens with age; fresh entries lie brighter than old ones. These lay correctly. Whoever had written them had known how old they ought to look.

Oscar sat very still in the lamplight and felt the particular cold of the cellar change character. It was no longer the cold of tallow and paper. It was the cold of a room where a door stands open somewhere you can't see, letting in air from a place you don't want to think about.

Numbers don't lie. That was the creed he'd built his careful life on, the load-bearing wall of the man made of precautions. Numbers don't lie — but people use numbers to lie, and the only defense was vigilance, and he had been vigilant, he had been *relentlessly* vigilant, and still something had walked through his books wearing his initials like a stolen coat.

He thought about Corvane drifting along the shelves an hour ago, finger trailing, asking *anything unusual tonight?* in that pleasant voice.

He thought about four thousand two hundred crowns.

He thought — and here he surprised himself, because the thought arrived uninvited and refused to leave — about the fish gate. About the fog that had been sitting on the docks all week, thicker than any fog he could remember, thick enough that the harbor bells sounded wrong coming through it, muffled, as though the sound had to travel farther than it used to. The dockhands complained of it. The fishermen said the tide had been coming in strange, running hard when it should have slackened, as if pulled by some moon that wasn't the moon.

Coincidence, said the sensible part of Oscar, the part that kept a rainy-day jar and counted stairs. Fog is fog. Tides are tides. You are a bookkeeper, and bookkeepers do not chase phantoms through their own columns; they flag the discrepancy, they raise the query, they follow procedure.

Very well. Procedure. Tomorrow he would draft a formal query regarding the Veil-thread acquisitions and submit it through channels, and channels would either answer him or reveal by their silence exactly how deep the blind column went, and either outcome was information, and information was the one impossible thing Oscar Sandoval had never once failed to acquire.

Satisfied, or as near to satisfied as his nature permitted, he closed the ledger, locked the drawer — all three locks — and trimmed the lamp.

And that was when he heard the bells above change their pattern. Not the chandler's bell, not the watch. The harbor bells, out through the floorboards and the fog, ringing the alarm that meant a body had come ashore at the fish gate.

Oscar stood in the cold cellar with his coat half-on and told himself, firmly, in the voice he used for apprentices and for his own worst impulses, that drowned sailors were a harbormaster's problem, and coroners' problems, and none of them appeared anywhere in column seven.

He put on the coat anyway. He told himself it was because he needed air.

He was, as it happened, lying to himself for the very first time, though he wouldn't recognize that until later, standing over a dying stranger with silvered veins crawling up her throat and a map case pressed into his hands — and by then, of course, the ledger of his old life would already be closed, and the new one open, and neither of them would balance ever again.
