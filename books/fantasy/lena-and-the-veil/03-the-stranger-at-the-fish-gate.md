## 3. The Stranger at the Fish Gate

The fog on the docks that night had the particular quality of a lie told by a friend — well-intentioned, enveloping, and designed to keep you from seeing what was actually in front of you.

Oscar Sandoval knew fog the way he knew most things: professionally. He had spent eleven years learning to distinguish between what things appeared to be and what they were. The quail at last night's banquet had appeared to be merely over-salted; it had in fact been laced with bitterleaf extract, which he had identified by a faint almond ghost beneath the salt and neutralized through the calloused trick of a stomach trained like a duelist's wrist. The king's chamberlain appeared to be an honest man; he was, per the guild's ledgers, three payments deep into selling palace floor plans. And this fog appeared to be ordinary river weather.

It was not. It smelled faintly of copper and old coins, and it moved against the wind.

But then, Oscar thought, tugging his coat tighter as he picked his way along the Fish Gate quay, everything in this city had been moving against the wind lately. Including, apparently, him.

He had no business being out here. That was precisely why he was here. The guild's night ledger required updating — two entries from the afternoon's trades needed recording before Master Corvane's morning audit, and Corvane's audits had developed teeth of late. Oscar had learned to do his bookkeeping the way smugglers ran contraband: quietly, at odd hours, along routes nobody watched. The docks at midnight were a route nobody watched, mostly because anyone with sense was elsewhere.

"Mostly because anyone with sense is elsewhere," he murmured to himself, stepping around a coil of rope that might have been an eel or might have been a rope. "Well done, Sandoval. A man made entirely of precautions, taking none."

It was a habit he'd developed — talking to himself on these walks — and he'd long ago stopped examining why. A man who examined all his own reasons might find some of them wanting, and Oscar preferred his accounts balanced. Unexamined motives were like uncounted coin: fine right up until someone asked you to total them.

The Fish Gate itself loomed ahead, a great arched throat of black stone through which the fishing fleet passed at dawn and, at midnight, through which nothing should have been passing at all. The lamps along the quay burned in halos, each one an island of amber in the grey. Somewhere behind him, a gull complained with the tone of a creditor.

That was when the woman came out of the fog.

She did not walk so much as fail forward, one lurching step at a time, her hand trailing along the quay wall as though the wall were the only thing in the world still holding its shape. She was tall, dressed in traveling leathers gone dark with spray, and her hair — he would remember this later, in the terrible inventory-taking way his mind worked — her hair was coming loose from its braid in silver-streaked ropes, though she could not have been older than forty.

She saw him. He saw her see him. And then her knees simply resigned their post.

Oscar caught her more by ledger-keeper's reflex than heroism — she weighed about as much as a strongbox of wet paper, and she collapsed into his arms with the finality of a debt called due.

"Steady," he said, which was a foolish thing to say to a dying woman but is, apparently, what human mouths produce under pressure. "Steady, I've got you."

Her head lolled back against his shoulder, and in the halo-light of the nearest lamp he saw her throat.

He had seen wounds before. Palace life taught you wounds the way a fishmonger teaches you fish — casually, repeatedly, until you stopped flinching. But this was not a wound. This was her veins. They stood up beneath the skin of her neck like rivers on a map, and they were *silver* — actual silver, luminous, the light moving through them in slow pulses, as if something bright were flowing backward through her body toward her heart.

"That's not possible," Oscar said, with the calm authority of a man whose entire profession rested on knowing what was possible. Poison was possible. Poison was, in fact, his oldest acquaintance. Silvered veins crawling up a stranger's throat like frost climbing a windowpane was not on any list he kept.

The woman's eyes found his. They were grey-green, sharp even now, even drowning in whatever was drowning her, and there was a ferocious intelligence in them that made Oscar feel suddenly, absurdly, like a junior clerk caught fudging a column.

"You're—" she began, and coughed. The cough produced not blood but a thin thread of silver mist that dissolved in the air between them. "You're the taster."

"I'm a food-taster," Oscar corrected automatically, because apparently his survival instinct included pedantry. "Royal household. If you've been poisoned, I can—"

"I haven't been *poisoned*." She almost laughed. It came out as a rattle. "I've been *unraveled*, you precise little man. There's a difference, and you're going to learn it whether you want to or not."

"I really don't think I am."

"You are." Her hand shot up and seized his collar with a grip that had no business existing in a body this far gone. "Listen to me. I don't have — the words go out faster than they come in now. So listen. Twice."

"Once is my usual rate, but for you—"

"*Twice.*" The silver in her throat flared, and for a moment her voice came out doubled, harmonized with itself, one tone human and one tone like a bell rung underwater. Oscar's skin prickled from collar to scalp. "There is a covenant. Old. Older than the crown, older than your guild, older than the stones under this city. It holds a door shut. The door is opening. The man who holds the key is selling the door."

Oscar opened his mouth to say something sensible — *madam, you've the look of a woman who's eaten something, let me fetch a physician, I know several discreet ones* — and then stopped, because her free hand had gone to her belt, to a leather case strapped there, and the sight of it short-circuited every sensible thing he owned.

He knew that case. Not that specific case — he'd never seen it before in his life — but he knew its *kind*. The guild dealt in leather cases. Documents, seals, maps. In eleven years of keeping books for people who traded in impossible things, Oscar had catalogued enough of these to know that a dying courier gripping one meant the contents mattered more than the courier.

And she was pressing it into his hands.

"No," he said. "No, no. You've mistaken me for someone important. I'm a servant. I taste soup for a living. Whatever this is, it wants someone who—"

"Reforge it."

The words came out quiet, which somehow made them worse. All that doubled-bell urgency, and then this: two plain words, spoken almost gently, the way you'd ask someone to carry something home for you from market.

"What?" said Oscar. "Reforge what? Reforge *what?*"

But her eyes had already begun to change. The sharpness was draining out of them, and what remained was worse — not emptiness, exactly, but a kind of receding, like a tide going out and leaving him standing on exposed sand with no idea what the water had been hiding. Her grip on his collar loosened. Her other hand slid from the case, and Oscar held it without meaning to, the way you hold something hot that you can't put down.

"Wait," he said. "Wait, wait — who are you? What door? What man? Who's selling the—"

Her lips moved. No sound came. Then, at the very last, a breath that might have been a word — it sounded like *wrong*, or possibly *wren*, or possibly nothing at all, just air spending its final coin — and then she was still.

Not asleep-still. Not fainting-still. Oscar had seen death often enough to know its particular grammar, the way the whole sentence of a person simply stops mid-clause. She was dead in his arms on the Fish Gate quay, in the fog that smelled of copper, and the silver in her veins was fading like a lamp running dry, dimming down her throat in slow inches until only the faintest shimmer remained, and then not even that.

For a long moment, Oscar did nothing at all.

This was unusual for him. Oscar's entire existence was built on doing the next correct thing immediately — taste, assess, swallow or spit; record, seal, file; observe, deduce, survive. His mind was a machine for producing next steps. And now the machine sat silent, presenting him with nothing, while a dead woman's weight slowly went from warm to cool in his arms.

Then the gull screamed again, closer, insolent, and the world resumed.

"Right," Oscar said aloud, because saying things aloud was how he kept the walls of himself standing. "Right. Options."

He looked left, toward the city. Watch patrol came by the Fish Gate twice a night; the second round was due within the hour. He looked right, toward the water, black and patient. He looked down at the woman, and the woman looked back.

That was the worst part. Her eyes were open.

Dead eyes closed themselves, usually. Some muscle, some final courtesy of the body, drew the curtains. Hers hadn't. She stared up past his shoulder at something in the fog — or at nothing, or at him — and the expression frozen there was not peace. It was expectation. It was the look of a supervisor who has handed you a task and stepped out of the room, fully confident you'll complete it, and Oscar felt the full weight of that confidence land on him like a second corpse.

"Don't look at me like that," he whispered. "I didn't agree to anything. You didn't give me time to agree to anything. There are procedures for this. There are forms. Someone should be notified. Someone should be — I don't know — *informed.*"

Her eyes continued to inform him of nothing except their own accusation.

He laid her down as gently as he could on the quay stones, arranging her limbs with the awkward tenderness of a man who had never touched a dead body that wasn't scheduled for it. He took off his coat and put it over her, which was stupid — the coat was good wool, and she was beyond cold — but it seemed like the kind of thing a person ought to do, and Oscar was rapidly discovering that he contained a person underneath all the procedure, and that this person was shaking.

The leather case sat in his hands. It was heavier than it looked. Of course it was. Everything tonight was heavier than it looked.

He should leave it. That was the correct entry in the ledger of self-preservation: unknown contents, dying stranger, silver veins, words like *covenant* and *door* — this was guild business at best and treason at worst, and Oscar Sandoval had survived eleven years in two dangerous professions by never touching anything that wasn't already his.

He turned the case over. There was a stamp in the leather, worn nearly smooth: a circle bisected by a wavy line. A door, perhaps. Or a veil. Or an eye half-shut.

His fingers, entirely without authorization from the rest of him, checked the clasp. Fastened. Good leather, oiled, maintained — someone had cared for this case the way Oscar cared for his ledgers, which is to say the way misers care for gold and the devout care for relics.

Somewhere across the water, a bell rang the half-hour. Fifty minutes, perhaps less, until the watch.

"Put it down," Oscar told himself firmly. "Put it down, walk away, be a man with one profession tomorrow instead of a man with none."

He put it inside his shirt, against his chest, where it lay like a second heart — a colder one, beating slower.

"There," he muttered, hating himself with real craftsmanship. "That's done, then. That's absolutely done."

He stood, pulled the coat higher over the dead woman's face — though it didn't help, because her eyes were closed now, finally, as if she'd only been waiting to see what he'd choose — and turned toward the city.

He got four steps.

On the fifth, his foot struck something soft, and he looked down, and the fog chose that moment to thin, and he saw what lay half-hidden against the quay wall: a trail. Faint, silvered, like snail-track written in moonlight — the same substance that had crawled her veins — leading away from the spot where she'd fallen, back toward the black arch of the Fish Gate.

Leading, in other words, from wherever she had come.

Oscar stood at the edge of that shining line for a long moment, a bookkeeper weighing an entry that refused to balance. Behind him, the watch bells counted the city's dwindling patience. Ahead, the trail gleamed and vanished into the arch, into the fog, into whatever country lay on the far side of a door that a dying woman swore was opening.

Every precaution he had ever taken, every rule that had kept him alive through banquets and back rooms and eleven years of double bookkeeping, said the same thing: *go home, lock the door, count your spoons.*

Instead, Oscar Sandoval — taster of poisons, keeper of impossible accounts, a man made entirely of precautions — followed the silver line toward the Fish Gate, telling himself he was only going to look.

He was already lying to himself, then. He simply didn't yet know how good he'd get at it.

* * *

The trail ended ten paces inside the arch, at a drain grate, as if whatever she'd fled from had simply poured itself away into the city's plumbing. Which was either reassuring or profoundly not, depending on one's philosophy regarding drains.

Oscar crouched beside it, coatless in the fog, and allowed himself thirty seconds of honest terror. He timed it. Thirty seconds was his allowance for fear; longer and it started making decisions for him.

At second twenty-nine, he heard boots on the quay behind him.

"—swear to the Twins, Hask, if it's another drunk fisherman I'm writing us both up for wasted rounds—"

Watch lanterns. Two of them, bobbing through the fog toward the arch, toward the coat-covered shape on the stones, toward the man crouching in the shadows ten feet from a corpse.

Oscar's mind, so recently empty, snapped back to full function with an almost audible click. Exits: the arch mouth, blocked by incoming lanterns. The drain, too narrow, probably haunted, no. The quay wall — there. To his left, a ladder of iron rungs set into the stone, descending to a maintenance ledge below the waterline, used by the gate-keepers for clearing debris from the arch. He'd catalogued it months ago, idly, the way he catalogued everything, because a man made of precautions never met a route he didn't note.

He was over the wall and three rungs down before the first lantern swung into the arch, and he pressed himself flat against the cold stone below the sightline and listened to the discovery happen above him.

"Lantern down here! Over by the wall — saints. Saints above. Hask. *Hask.*"

"It's a woman?"

"It's a dead woman."

"Cover her up, cover her — whose coat is that? Whose coat—"

Oscar climbed down the rest of the rungs as quietly as a man has ever climbed anything, and moved along the maintenance ledge with the fog swallowing him whole, and the last thing he heard from above, faint and strange and chasing him all the way home, was the younger watchman's voice, puzzled and rising:

"Hask — come look at her neck. Come *look* at her neck."

Oscar walked fast through streets that were only streets, past doors that were only doors, with a stolen case pressed against his chest and a dead woman's last two words lodged in him like a splinter working deeper with every heartbeat.

*Reforge it.*

He didn't know what it was. He didn't know who she was. He knew only that somewhere in the city behind him, men were lifting a lantern over silvered veins, and somewhere ahead of him, a leather case waited to be opened, and that whichever way he moved now, the ledger of his careful, balanced, survivable life had just received its first entry he couldn't total.

He got the case home. He locked his door. He counted his spoons — all nine present, which surprised him; he'd have bet on eight.

Then he set the case on his table, lit his lamp, and reached for the clasp.

His hands, for the first time in eleven years, were shaking too hard to work the catch.
