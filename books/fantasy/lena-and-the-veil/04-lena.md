## 4. Lena

The dead do not blink. This was the first thing Oscar Sandoval learned about corpses, crouched in fish-gut fog with a stranger's head cradled in his hands, waiting for her eyelids to fall the way eyelids were supposed to fall after the person behind them had stopped.

They did not fall. Her eyes stayed open, grey as dockwater, fixed on some point slightly above and to the left of his ear, as though she had died mid-calculation and the answer was still coming.

"You could at least have the courtesy," Oscar said quietly, "to look at me."

She did not. The fog rolled in off the harbor in long grey tongues, and somewhere behind him the Fish Gate market was closing for the night — shutters banging, a fishwife swearing with real artistry at her son, the ordinary machinery of an ordinary evening grinding on without any awareness that a woman had just died against a royal food-taster's chest and handed him a leather tube like a baton in a race he hadn't agreed to run.

*Reforge it,* she had said. Then she had stopped saying anything at all.

Oscar knelt there for what his internal ledger later recorded as four minutes and eleven seconds, doing nothing, which for Oscar was itself an extraordinary entry. He was not a man who did nothing. He was a man who counted things: the drops of almond essence in the king's wine (three; safe), the seconds between lightning and thunder, the number of lies Master Corvane told before breakfast (he'd stopped counting that one; it depressed the totals). But now he knelt in herring brine holding a dead woman and simply breathed, because some part of him understood — the part that kept the ledgers — that whatever happened next would be the first line of a new book, and once ink touched the page it did not come out.

Then the practical part of him won, as it always did, and he began searching her body for identification.

He was gentle about it. He had handled enough things in his life that were delicate and dangerous — vials, documents, the king's digestion — to have a touch that was careful without being squeamish. He went through her pockets the way he went through a spice merchant's ledgers: methodically, respectfully, noting everything.

Her coat was good wool gone grey with travel, mended at both elbows by someone who loved her or by herself, which amounted to nearly the same thing. In the left pocket: a stub of red sealing wax, worn round. In the right: three copper coins from no mint he recognized, each stamped with a door standing open. Around her neck, on a cord, a seal — heavy, dark metal, warm as if it had been held rather than worn. He turned it toward the lantern light swinging from the gatepost.

It showed a veil. Not a woman's veil — a *veil*, the thing itself: a curtain rendered in such fine detail that Oscar could almost see it move, threads woven so tightly they seemed to breathe. Beneath it, in script older than any alphabet he'd been paid to read, ran a single word he somehow knew without knowing how:

*Warden.*

"Well," Oscar said to the corpse, "that explains absolutely nothing."

*—the seal is warm because I never let it cool, little taster, forty years it has lived against my skin and it drinks me the way the Well drinks names—*

Oscar froze.

The voice had come from nowhere. Or rather — he pressed two fingers to his temple — from *her*. It had the same texture as her last words, the same low urgency, but it arrived the way a smell arrives: unbidden, sideways, already inside before you noticed the door open. He looked around the emptying dock. Fog. Lanterns. A cat regarding him with professional indifference.

Grief does strange things, he told himself. Shock does stranger ones. He had watched men die at banquet tables with smiles still buttered on their faces; he knew how the mind scrambled when death came too close and sat down uninvited.

But the voice had sounded *amused*. And it had called him *little taster*, which no one alive knew to call him.

He shook his head and kept searching, because the alternative was to sit down in the fish guts and think about it, and thinking about it was a luxury for people who were not currently kneeling over a body near a city gate with a watch patrol due within the quarter hour. He knew the watch schedules the way he knew everything else — by heart, in columns. Gate watch changed at the ninth bell. It was now half past the eighth.

In an inner pocket, buttoned twice, he found the letters.

There were seven of them, tied with string, carried soft at the folds from much handling. He hesitated. Reading a dead woman's correspondence was theft, probably, or something adjacent to theft — but she had put this tube in his hands with her final breath, and final breaths, in Oscar's experience, constituted a binding contract. He untied the string.

The first letter was official, on guild paper, the watermark a set of scales. He recognized the hand immediately. He had spent six years copying that hand into fair copies, admiring its economy, its confidence, the way it made even the word *regret* look profitable.

Master Corvane's hand.

*Dear Warden,* it began, in a tone Oscar knew intimately — the pleasant one, the one Corvane used on people he was about to ruin. *Your inquiries are noted. Your persistence is admired. I remind you that the Guild trades in all things, and that loyalty, too, has a price, and that mine is generous. Come in from the cold. Bring the seal. Bring the list. You will want for nothing, least of all time.*

Beneath it, in different ink, someone had written a reply that was never sent: *I want for exactly one thing, Corvane, and it is your arrest.*

Oscar read it twice. Something in his chest performed a maneuver he had no column for.

The second letter was older, softer paper, a child's careful hand grown up into a scholar's. *Mother says the Wardens swore to hold the door and never walk through it. Is that true? If the door must be held forever, who holds the holder? Write soon. — L.* And beneath, in a firmer hand: *The covenant holds us, little Lena. That is the whole secret. We do not hold it. — your father.*

*Lena.* So that was her name. He said it aloud, quietly, testing it, and the fog seemed to accept it, folding the syllables away like a clerk filing correspondence.

*—Lena, Lena, say it until it means nothing and then keep saying it until it means everything again, that is how you carry a dead man, that is how I have carried Papa for thirty years—*

The voice again. Closer now, or louder, or perhaps just more patient. Oscar sat back on his heels and let it wash through him, because fighting it felt rude, the way interrupting a eulogy feels rude.

"I'm sorry," he said to the air, feeling foolish. "I don't know what you are."

*I know what you are,* the voice said, with that same dry amusement. *That's rather the point.*

The third letter was a warrant — unsigned, undated, but bearing the crown's cipher, naming *the Warden of the Veil* as an enemy of the realm, description enclosed: female, fifty years or more, silver-marked. Silver-marked. Oscar looked at the dead woman's throat, where the veins he'd taken for frostbite or poison crawled up beneath the skin like rivers of mercury under ice. They reached her jaw now. As he watched, one thread of silver crept a fraction further, and he realized with a lurch that she was not finished changing. Death had interrupted something, not ended it.

The fourth, fifth, and sixth letters were evidence, and they were damning, and they were about *him* — or near enough. Entries copied in Corvane's hand: *Veil-thread, 40 lengths, acquired. Veil-thread, 120 lengths. Payment received: His Majesty's household, per arrangement.* Quantities climbing month over month like a fever chart. Oscar's stomach performed the specific cold drop it reserved for numbers that refused to balance. He had seen those entries. He had *filed* those entries, in the cellar, in his neat columns, telling himself the source column's blankness was an oversight, a clerical gap, a thing to be queried politely at month's end.

A woman had died with silver in her veins chasing the answer to a question Oscar had been too well-paid to ask.

The seventh letter was different. No addressee. Just her own hand, hurried, the pen pressed hard enough to tear:

*If you are reading this, I am dead, and you are whoever caught me falling, and I am sorry for that, truly, because I chose you the way one chooses a beam in a collapsing house — not because it is strong but because it is nearest. The map will explain what I cannot. Do not trust the Guild. Do not trust the crown. Trust the numbers; numbers cannot be bought, only misread. Reforge it. Forgive me. — L.*

"Numbers cannot be bought," Oscar repeated. His voice cracked on the second word, which annoyed him. "Only misread."

He had spent his whole life being the man who noticed. It was the entire architecture of him: notice, record, verify, survive. He had tasted ten thousand dishes and swallowed nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine of them safely because he noticed things — the wrong shimmer on a sauce, the sweetness sitting a hair too high. And here, in his own tidy ledgers, in his own careful columns, a hole the size of the end of the world had been sitting for months, and he had planned to raise it *at month's end*.

The grief arrived then, all at once, for a woman whose face he had known for eleven minutes. It arrived the way the tide arrives — not dramatically, just everywhere at once, lifting everything he'd carefully stacked. She had mended her own elbows. She had carried her father thirty years in the repetition of her own name. She had chosen him because he was *nearest*, and she had apologized for it while dying, and her eyes were still open, still fixed past his shoulder, still waiting for an answer to a calculation he couldn't see.

"I'll reforge it," he heard himself say. "Whatever *it* is. I — yes. All right."

*Good lad,* said the voice, fainter now, like a lamp being carried away down a long corridor. *Mind the third bell. And little taster — check the lining.*

"The lining?"

Silence. The fog closed like a ledger being shut.

Oscar checked the lining. Of course he checked the lining; he was, whatever else was happening to him, still himself. He turned the coat inside out, running his fingers along the seams, and found them — stitched flat into the left breast, where a heart's pocket might be — three small squares of oilcloth, each holding a sliver of something that was not quite cloth and not quite light. Grey-silver, fine as thread, and when his fingertip touched one, the whole dock *leaned*. Just slightly. Just the way a room leans when you stand up too fast. The lantern flame bent toward the slivers like a plant toward sun.

Veil-thread. He didn't know how he knew the name, but he knew it the way he knew the taste of bitter almond: completely, and with dread.

He wrapped them back into the lining without letting them touch anything, including, as much as possible, himself.

And then the ninth bell rang early.

It always rang early — that was the trick of it, the watch ringing the change fifteen minutes ahead of schedule to catch exactly the sort of person who knew the schedule. Oscar had filed that fact years ago in a column labeled *useful, others' dishonesty* and had never once needed it until this moment, when boots came splashing down the quay road and a lantern beam swung across the water and a voice called out, "Who's there? Gate's closed! Show yourself!"

Oscar did the arithmetic in half a heartbeat: forty yards to the fish-gutters' stair, ninety seconds of shouting between the challenge and the arrival, one dead Warden, one seal, seven letters, one map case, three impossible threads, and one food-taster whose entire life had been preparation for moments exactly like this one, except that every previous moment had involved someone else's danger, which was infinitely easier.

He took the seal. He took the letters. He took the map case, which was heavier than a map case should be, and warm, and humming very faintly, like a struck glass deciding whether to ring.

He could not take her. There was no time, and no way, and the tide was coming in, and the watch was coming faster. He arranged her hands instead — folded them neatly over the seal's absence, the way one straightens a bed for a guest who will not return — and closed, with two fingers, one of her eyes. The other stayed open. Accusing. Or perhaps, he thought desperately as he ran, *wry*. Perhaps that was just how her face had settled over fifty hard years, and she wasn't accusing anyone, and the guilt curdling in his chest was entirely his own invention, the first entry in a brand-new ledger.

*Sorry,* he thought at her, sprinting for the stair, the case clutched to his ribs like a second heart. *I'm sorry. I only met you and I'm already lying to myself on your behalf. You'd have hated my handwriting. You'd have liked my numbers.*

Behind him, lanterns. Shouting. The word *body* passing from mouth to mouth like contraband.

Ahead of him, home, and the leather tube against his chest, ticking — no, not ticking, *humming* — and inside it, though he could not yet have said how he knew, a map of a city he had lived in all his life, drawn by someone who had clearly believed it was a different city altogether.

The fog closed behind him like a page turning.
