## 6. Corvane Summons His Bookkeeper

The summons arrived the way all of Corvane's summons did: too politely.

Oscar found it tucked inside the morning's ledger delivery, a slip of cream-colored paper folded once and sealed with a drop of grey wax that smelled faintly of cold iron. *Master Sandoval will attend the guild house at the ninth bell. The Master wishes to discuss the quarter's accounts.* No threat in it. No urgency. Just the word "wishes," which from Corvane was more alarming than any command could have been.

Corvane never wished. Corvane arranged.

Oscar read the note three times over breakfast — bread, hard cheese, and a cup of water he tasted out of habit before drinking, though nobody had poisoned his lodging in six years and he knew it. Precautions were not about likelihood. They were about being a man who would know, instantly, if the world changed on him.

The world had changed on him two nights ago at the Fish Gate, and he had been walking around inside the change ever since like a man wearing someone else's coat.

He set down the cup. The map case sat where it always sat now, wrapped in his spare shirt at the bottom of the flour barrel, because even a man made of precautions could not think of a better place on short notice, and everything since the docks had been short notice. He had not opened it again. He had wanted to — his fingers had actually twitched toward it at odd hours, the way they used to twitch toward the ledgers when the numbers were going to be interesting — but some instinct older than bookkeeping told him that whatever lived in that leather was safer unexamined until he understood what examining it might cost.

Besides, he had work. Work was ballast. He spent the morning doing what he always did after a strange night: he balanced things. The palace kitchens' spice accounts. The wine cellar discrepancies that were not discrepancies at all but the under-steward's private economy, which Oscar recorded nowhere and remembered everywhere. He checked the seals on the taster's kit. He sharpened the little knife he used for portioning, though a food-taster's knife was mostly ceremonial; his real instruments were his tongue, his memory, and his patience.

By the eighth bell he had almost convinced himself that the summons was ordinary. Masters did occasionally wish to discuss accounts. It was, when you thought about it, the single most plausible reason anyone had ever summoned him.

Then he caught himself noticing that he was trying to convince himself of something, and stopped, because Oscar Sandoval had built his whole life on the principle that a man who has to argue himself into calm is a man who already knows better.

* * *

The guild house stood on Candlemaker's Row behind a façade so deliberately dull that most people walked past it their entire lives without once wondering what was inside. That was the point. Oscar had helped choose the paint. It was a grey-brown called, in the chandler's catalogue, "Respectable," and it cost four coppers more per pot than the other greys precisely because it was the grey least likely to be remembered.

Inside, the corridors were clean and quiet and smelled of lamp oil and paper. Two clerks nodded to him. A courier he half-knew — Marisol, sharp-eyed, always moving as though late for something she'd never admit to — glanced at him a beat too long as she passed, and Oscar filed the glance away in the drawer of his mind marked *unexplained, keep.*

Corvane's office was on the second floor, at the back, with a window that looked onto an airshaft rather than the street. Oscar had never asked why. He suspected the answer was that Corvane liked to be able to hear everything and see nothing, or possibly the reverse; with Corvane it was difficult to tell which way the trick ran.

"Master Sandoval." Corvane rose from behind his desk, and — this was the thing people never believed until they met him — he smiled. Warmly. He was a broad, comfortable-looking man with silver at his temples and the hands of someone who had done honest labor sometime in the distant past and had since discovered that other people's labor paid better. He looked like a prosperous merchant. He looked like your uncle. He looked, Oscar had often thought, exactly like the kind of man you would trust with your savings and your sister and your secrets, which was how he got all three.

"Master Corvane." Oscar bowed the precise amount owed to a superior. Not a hair more. Precision was safety.

"Sit, sit." Corvane waved him into the chair across the desk and poured wine himself — two cups, from the same bottle, which was its own small message: *I am not poisoning you, and we both know you'd know if I tried.* Oscar took his cup and tasted it anyway, openly, the way he tasted everything, and Corvane laughed with genuine delight.

"There he is. The famous tongue. Do you know, I have offered that tongue of yours three separate positions over the years, and every time you turn me down with that same polite little bow."

"The ledgers suit me, Master."

"The ledgers suit you." Corvane settled back, cradling his own untouched cup. "Yes. I believe they do. That is rather what I want to talk about."

Here it comes, said the part of Oscar's mind that had been saying *here it comes* since the ninth bell invitation, and possibly since the Fish Gate, and possibly, if he was honest, since the moment a dying stranger had pressed a leather case into his hands and made him an heir without asking permission.

"The quarter's accounts are in order," Oscar said. "I brought the summary." He produced it — he had prepared it that morning, twice as carefully as usual, because a man walking into a room he feared should carry something to do with his hands.

"I'm sure they are. Your accounts are always in order. It's one of the great comforts of my life, Oscar — may I call you Oscar? — knowing that somewhere in this city is a set of numbers that behaves itself." Corvane smiled again, and then, gently, the way a man sets down something fragile: "Tell me about your week."

"My week."

"Your week. The banquet Tuesday — I heard the quail story, by the way, magnificent work, the cook will be flogged and the ambassador humiliated and the crown owes you its collective life, again. And then?" A small shrug, expansive, innocent. "What does a careful man do with his evenings?"

*Careful* landed on the word like a stamp on wax.

Oscar had rehearsed nothing, because he had told himself there was nothing to rehearse for. Now, with no script, he watched his own mind move with the speed it reserved for emergencies: cataloguing what Corvane could know, what he could only suspect, and what he was pretending not to know in order to see what Oscar would volunteer.

If Corvane knew about Lena, Oscar would already be in a cellar with men who owned rope. If Corvane suspected, this conversation was the net being drawn slowly. If Corvane knew nothing — if this truly was a master checking on his bookkeeper — then the correct answer was the truth, minus the parts that would kill him.

The trouble was that Oscar had spent thirty-one years giving answers that were true all the way down. It was the foundation of his entire architecture. Numbers didn't lie because lying required a self that wobbled, and Oscar had sanded the wobble out of himself dish by dish, entry by entry, precaution by precaution. He was honest the way other men were sober. It wasn't virtue, exactly. It was engineering.

"Evenings," Oscar said, and heard his own voice come out level, "are much like mornings. Accounts. Sleep. I am not a man of wide interests, Master."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "You are a man of narrow interests, pursued very deeply. It's why I trust you." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the desk. "Which brings me to the actual matter. There has been some… irregularity. In the upper trade." The upper trade. The impossible inventory. Bottled memories, borrowed years, names. "Nothing you've handled — your books are clean, I want to be clear about that, this is not an audit of you. But certain goods have been moving through channels that concern me, and certain parties have become curious whether anything unusual has… reached your hands."

The sentence hung in the lamplight.

*Anything unusual.* Oscar thought of silvered veins crawling up a stranger's throat. Of a leather case against his ribs through two layers of shirt. Of her eyes staying open.

He became aware, with terrible clarity, that Corvane was watching him taste this question the way he tasted everything — and that for the first time in his life, the tasting had to come back *clean* regardless of what was actually in the dish.

There was a silence. It lasted perhaps two seconds. Oscar would later remember it as a year.

"No," Oscar said.

Just that. One syllable, and the world tilted.

Because here was the thing he discovered in that instant, the thing no one had warned him about, the thing the moralists and the priests and his grandmother all failed to mention: lying was *easy*. It went down smooth as good butter. All those years he'd assumed honesty was the natural state and falsehood the effortful one, and it was exactly backwards — truth required you to haul the whole weight of yourself into the room, while a lie weighed nothing at all. It simply floated there between them, small and neat and perfect, and Corvane received it with a nod, and the floor did not open, and the lamps did not gutter, and God or whatever kept the great ledger above did not strike him down.

And underneath the terror — because the terror was there, swimming just beneath his breastbone, cold and bright — underneath it was something else. Something warm and shocking and shameful.

It felt *wonderful*.

That was the part that frightened him most. Not that he had lied — a man lies or dies, sometimes both — but that some locked room inside him had swung open at the first taste of it and revealed shelves and shelves of appetite he hadn't known he possessed. The liar's first lie, he thought dizzily, is always the sweetest, and whoever coined that had been speaking from experience, and had probably also gone back for seconds.

"You're certain?" Corvane asked, mild as milk.

"Certain, Master. Nothing unusual reaches me. That is rather the design of my position." And there — oh, there it was — a second lie, built on the first, arriving without being summoned, fluent as a native language learned in an afternoon. *Rather the design of my position.* He had made it sound like humility. It was armor dressed as humility.

Corvane studied him for a long moment, and Oscar held the look the way he held poison on his tongue: still, breathing shallowly, letting the dangerous thing sit there without swallowing and without spitting.

Then Corvane laughed, warm and easy, and the tension in the room broke like weather passing.

"Good. Good! I confess I hoped you'd say that. There are men in this city, Oscar, who would invent an unusual thing just to seem important. You are not one of them. It is why—" he spread his hands, generous, "—why you may be the only man in this building I trust completely."

*I lied to your face forty seconds ago,* Oscar thought, and bowed.

"One more matter." Corvane drew a slim folio from a drawer and slid it across. "The quarter's special entries. Veil-thread acquisitions — you'll find the figures larger than last season. I want them recorded under the standing heading, same as always, and I want your discretion as ever. There are buyers whose names must not appear even in cipher. Use the red notation."

Oscar opened the folio. Numbers marched down the page in a clerk's tidy hand, and they were wrong — not arithmetically, arithmetically they were flawless, but wrong the way a recipe is wrong when the quantities have quietly tripled. Last quarter the guild had bought eleven lengths of Veil-thread. This page showed sixty. Sixty lengths of a substance Oscar had recorded for years without once asking what it *was*, because a bookkeeper's job was the what and not the why, and he had told himself that for so long it had worn smooth as a river stone.

Sixty. Enough thread to hem a boundary, if boundaries could be hemmed. Enough to—

"Trouble with the figures?" Corvane asked pleasantly.

"No, Master. Only admiring the growth of the trade."

"Mm." Corvane took the folio back, and for just a moment — less than a moment, a flicker, gone before it finished existing — his eyes rested on Oscar with an expression that had nothing uncle-like in it at all. It was the expression of a man checking a lock he had personally oiled. Then it was gone, buried under the smile. "Ninth bell next week, as usual. Oh — and Oscar?"

Oscar paused at the door, hand on the frame, heart performing its duties with suspicious steadiness.

"If anything unusual *should* reach your hands someday — anything at all — you'd bring it to me. Wouldn't you?"

The question was shaped like kindness. It was weighted like a trap.

"With my life, Master," Oscar said, which had the advantage of being, in every direction he cared to measure, true.

* * *

He walked home the long way, through streets going gold with evening, and bought a meat pie from the woman at the corner of Tallow Lane because his body demanded evidence that the world still functioned normally, and ate it standing up, and tasted it automatically — mutton, onion, pepper, no surprises — and laughed, alone, a short startled laugh that made the pie woman frown at him.

Because he had done it. He had stood in front of the most dangerous man he knew, with a dead Warden's map hidden in a flour barrel, and he had lied — twice, fluently, *well* — and walked out with the folio under his arm and his pulse barely above conversation pace.

Terror and exhilaration, braided together so tightly he couldn't tell which strand was which. His hands shook slightly, and he examined the shaking with professional interest, the way he'd examine an unfamiliar spice. Was it fear? It was partly fear. But fear alone didn't feel like this. Fear alone didn't feel like a door swinging open in a house he'd thought fully furnished.

At his lodging he went straight to the flour barrel, because of course he did, because the lie had not freed him from the map — it had shackled him to it. Every hour he kept it secret deepened the lie. Every day deepened the danger. Corvane had asked him directly. Which meant Corvane had reason to ask. Which meant somewhere in the city, threads were being pulled, and Oscar Sandoval was now a man standing on a thread, pretending it was floor.

He unwrapped the case and carried it to the table and set the lamp close, and for a long moment he simply looked at it, the way he'd once looked at a cup of wine at a banquet that smelled, very faintly, of bitter almonds.

The numbers didn't add up. Sixty lengths of Veil-thread. A dying stranger who knew his face. A master who trusted him *completely*, which in Corvane's vocabulary meant *watched him constantly*.

A careful man, Oscar thought, would burn the map and forget the docks and go on tasting quail until he died comfortably of old age surrounded by balanced books.

He picked up the case. The buckles were cold.

"A careful man," he said aloud to the empty room, "would also open it first."

He worked the buckles loose, and unrolled the leather, and the lamp flame leaned sideways though the window was shut — and three streets away, behind a door he had passed a hundred times without seeing, something that was not quite a market began, very quietly, to wake up.
