## 7. The Door That Wasn't There Yesterday

Oscar Sandoval had spent thirty-one years learning that the world could be trusted in exactly one respect: it was consistent. Poison was poison. Numbers were numbers. A door that led to a cellar led to a cellar every time you opened it, and the cellar smelled of the same three things—damp stone, old tallow, and Master Corvane's imported Vessian ink.

So when he stood before the door on Candlemaker's Lane—the door the map had marked with a small, patient circle of ink—and found that the door was, in fact, *there*, he felt a brief and foolish relief. He had half expected to arrive and find nothing but brick, and then he could have gone home, made himself a cup of chicory, and told himself that dying women on docks and maps of impossible cities were the sort of thing that happened to other men. Men with better boots. Men who had never once, in their whole lives, tasted something and thought, *hmm, foxglove, but the cheap kind.*

The door was there. It was a perfectly ordinary door: grey wood, iron hinges gone orange with rust, a knocker shaped like an acorn that some previous tenant had clearly considered whimsical. The shop above it—Oscar craned his neck—sold hats, or had once. The window was papered over. A cat sat on the sill with the profound indifference of a creature that has seen everything and been impressed by none of it.

"Right," Oscar said to the cat. "You're the witness, then. If I don't come back, tell the watch it was the hats."

The cat yawned.

He had prepared, because Oscar prepared the way other men breathed. In one pocket: chalk, a stub of candle, and a strip of dried beef in case the crossing took longer than an afternoon. In the other: a knife, the map folded to the size of a playing card, and the receipt from the ferryman, which he had not yet needed but which had begun to feel like a talisman. On his person, worn against his skin where the palace physicians never thought to look: a locket of Lena's—the Warden's seal, small and cold as a coin left out all winter.

He had also, before leaving his lodging, eaten a breakfast designed by a man who expected to be poisoned. Bitter almonds, a spoonful of charcoal, and a rind of cheese so old it had achieved a kind of moral authority. If the Near Faded had food—and the map's margins suggested it had *everything*—Oscar intended to survive it the way he survived everything: one careful mouthful at a time.

He put his hand on the acorn knocker. Knocked twice, because the map's margin note said *knock twice and mean it*, which was the single most unsettling set of instructions he had ever followed, and he had once followed a recipe that began *first, convince the eel it is already cooked.*

The door opened inward without a sound.

* * *

The first thing Oscar noticed was the light. Not darkness—twilight, but twilight with an opinion about itself. It came from everywhere and nowhere, the color of a candle seen through honey, and it did not flicker. It simply *was*, steady as a held breath, as though the sky here had been lit once, long ago, and no one had ever gotten around to blowing it out.

The second thing he noticed was the market.

It spread out before him in a long crooked street that should not have fit inside Candlemaker's Lane, or inside the city, or possibly inside the world. Stalls leaned against stalls. Awnings of faded silk sagged under the weight of goods Oscar's eyes kept trying to file and kept failing to file: jars of laughter sealed with wax and labeled in a hand that slanted upward, as if the writer had been giggling. Bundles of sleep tied with string. A tray of *last words*, each one folded into a paper boat, drifting in a shallow trough of water that had no business staying afloat and no business being there at all.

And people. Or—the word *people* fit loosely, like a coat borrowed from a larger man. Some were human enough. Some were human the way a memory of a face is human: right in outline, wrong in the details, an eye too far apart, a smile that arrived a half-second before the reason for it. They moved through the twilight market with the unhurried air of folk who had all the time that had ever been traded away, and not one of them looked at Oscar twice.

He stood in the doorway for a long moment, one foot in Candlemaker's Lane and one foot in nowhere, and did the thing he always did when the world misbehaved: he took out his mental ledger and began to write.

*Entry the first,* he thought. *One door, ordinary. One street, impossible. One Oscar, deeply regretting the cheese rind, which now sits in his stomach like a grudge.*

He stepped through. The door swung shut behind him without a sound, and when he turned to check—because of course he turned to check—there was no door. There was a wall of twilight, soft as felt, and the acorn knocker hanging on it alone, like a joke whose punchline had wandered off.

"Well," said Oscar, to no one. "That's that, then."

* * *

He walked. It was the only strategy he had, and he had learned long ago that when you have no strategy, the next best thing is to look like you do. He kept his hands visible, his chin level, and his eyes busy. He counted exits. There were none, which was a number he did not enjoy. He counted the stalls instead, because counting things calmed him, and by the fortieth stall he had noticed something that turned his stomach in a way no poison ever had.

The guild's goods were *everywhere*.

He knew them the way a mother knows her children's handwriting. The little stoppered vials of borrowed years, each one faintly warm. The jars of courage—*someone else's*, the ledger said, always *someone else's*, because courage, like everything in this place, had to come from a person. The bottled memories with their wax seals stamped with Corvane's private mark: a crow holding a key. Oscar had written that mark into the ledgers four hundred and eleven times over six years. He had assumed it stood for the guild. He had assumed it was a symbol.

It was not a symbol. It was a *signature*, and it was signed on everything, and everything had been *taken* from somewhere.

"First time through?" said a voice at his elbow.

Oscar did not jump. Oscar had been trained by twenty years of tasting for kings never to jump, because a man who jumps is a man who has already admitted something is wrong. He turned, slowly, and found himself facing a woman made mostly of shawl. Her face was kind and creased, and her eyes were the color of tea that has been brewed twice.

"Is it that obvious?"

"You've got the look." She nodded at his pockets, his careful boots, the locket's chain just visible at his collar. "Counting. Everyone counts, their first day. Then they stop counting, and that's when you know they've been here too long." She held out her hand. In her palm sat a small tin. "Mint? It's real mint. Grew it myself on the other side. Only thing I've got that's still entirely honest."

Oscar considered the mint. He considered the woman. He took one leaf, tasted it—cool, green, clean, no bitterness of any kind—and felt something in his chest unknot by a single notch.

"Thank you," he said. "I'm looking for the source of a particular good. Veil-thread."

The tea-colored eyes went still.

"Are you," she said.

"I'm a bookkeeper," Oscar said, which was true, and which had the pleasant property of being the sort of profession no one fears. "I've been recording entries for six years. Quantities, prices, dates. And the quantities have been going up." He heard his own voice go flat and precise, the voice he used for reading figures aloud to men who did not want to hear them. "A great deal up. And I have never once been told what the thread *is*. Only that it sells."

The woman looked at him for a long moment, the way one looks at a man who has asked a question he genuinely does not know the answer to, and pities him for it.

"Come on, then," she said. "You'd best see it before you buy any more of it. Or sell it. Whichever side of the knife you're on."

"I keep the books," Oscar said. "I'm not on either side of the knife."

"Everyone's on one side of the knife, love. The only question is whether you knew it at the time." She tucked her tin away. "This way. And walk like you belong here. Nothing in this market respects a man who looks like he's apologizing for existing."

* * *

She led him deeper, past stalls he would have paid good money never to see again: a merchant selling *directions*, each one a small brass compass that pointed not north but toward whatever you had lost. A stall of unspent apologies, stacked like kindling. A child—Faded, or half so, with hair like smoke deciding to be hair—turning somersaults in the honeyed light, and every time she landed, the ground under her remembered, briefly, how to be grass.

And then the stalls thinned, and the twilight deepened to the color of a bruise healing, and Oscar saw the Veil.

He had always imagined it, in the vague way busy men imagine large abstractions, as a wall. A curtain. Something with edges.

It was not a wall. It was a *weaving*—an enormous, slow, shimmering fabric stretched across the sky of that place and, he understood with a lurch, across the sky of every place, the boundary between his world and this one, between the possible and the Faded. It was beautiful. That was the first horror of it. It was beautiful the way a frost pattern on a window is beautiful, all silver filaments and patient geometry, and it was *unraveling*.

Great loose strands hung from its lower edge like yarn from a moth-eaten sweater. And below, on scaffolds of dark wood, men were *harvesting* it. They climbed on ladders with long slender hooks and drew down the loose strands, and the strands came away with a sound Oscar felt in his teeth rather than heard—a sound like a harp string being tuned by someone with no ear and no mercy. The harvested thread was wound onto spools. The spools went into crates. The crates were stamped with a crow holding a key.

"Veil-thread," the shawled woman said quietly. "That's what your ledgers call it. Pretty word. We call it what it is." She nodded upward. "That's the hem of everything, bookkeeper. That's the stitching that keeps your side and my side from bleeding into each other. Every strand they pull down is a strand that isn't holding anymore. Every vial of years your master sells to your king—" she tapped one of the crates with her foot, and inside, the spools seemed to shiver, "—that's a piece of the hem, cut and corked and drunk. And a hem only holds so long as it's whole."

Oscar stood very still.

He was, by profession and by nature, a man who believed that everything could be understood through its ledger. Show him the entries and he would show you the truth. And now the entries assembled themselves in his head with terrible, perfect clarity: *Veil-thread, 12 spools. Veil-thread, 40 spools. Veil-thread, quantities unlisted, buyer's discretion.* Six years of neat columns in his own careful hand. He had thought he was recording a commodity. He had assumed—God help him, he had *assumed*, the one sin he had never forgiven in anyone else—that because the numbers balanced, the thing they described was ordinary.

He had been itemizing the end of the world. Line by line. In brown ink. With a steady hand he had been quietly proud of.

"How much is left?" he asked. His voice came out smaller than he intended.

"Enough," the woman said. "If it stopped today. It won't stop today. It won't stop any day, because stopping doesn't make money, and the man who buys from you has never once in his life let a number go down." She looked at him sideways. "You really didn't know."

"No."

"Then you're either the last honest man in the trade or the best liar I've met this century, and I've met liars who could convince a mirror." She pulled her shawl tighter. "There's a woman came through here, months back. Silver in her veins, sharp as a tack, asking the same questions you're asking but louder. She went deeper. Past the market. Looking for the Well, and the ones who signed the old bargain." Her tea-colored eyes fixed on his. "You knew her."

It was not a question. Oscar's hand went, without his permission, to the locket under his shirt.

"She died," he said. "Three nights ago. In my arms, on a dock, with her eyes open. She gave me a map and one word." He swallowed. "*Reforge.*"

The woman was quiet for a moment. Around them the twilight hummed its held-breath hum, and somewhere above, a harp string was tuned by someone with no mercy, and a strand that had held the world together for longer than the world had words came loose and drifted down like a grey feather.

"Then you'd best not stand here counting stalls," she said at last. "Deeper in, there's a ferryman called Silt. He'll take you past the market, but he doesn't row for coin. He rows for *weight*—and he'll want yours." She pressed something into his hand: a sprig of the honest mint, tied with thread. "For the crossing. He can't take what's freely given, and he can't refuse it. Old rule. Older than the hem."

"Thank you," Oscar said. "You've been—"

"Don't thank me yet." She was already turning away, shawl and tea-eyes folding back into the market's twilight. "Thank me when you've seen what he charges. And bookkeeper—" she paused, not looking back, "—when you find whoever's pulling those threads down? Check your ledgers again. Check them *very* carefully. Men who trade in years are always careful about their own, and careless about everyone else's."

She was gone between two stalls, the way people are gone in that place: not leaving, simply ceasing to be where you were looking.

Oscar stood alone under the unraveling hem of the world, holding a sprig of mint, and did the only thing he knew how to do with horror. He opened the mental ledger, and he wrote:

*Entry the last. Six years of accounts. All of them accurate. None of them true.*

Then he turned toward the dark water at the market's edge, where something long and low and patient was waiting, and began to walk.
