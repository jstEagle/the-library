## 8. A Bargain with the Ferryman

The market at twilight had a smell to it, and the smell was wrong in a way Oscar's tongue could not name. He was, by profession and by temperament, a man who could name things. He could identify eleven poisons blindfolded, six of them by scent alone, and he had once won a private wager with himself by guessing the exact vintage of a wine the king's steward had watered down twice. But this smell—wet stone and old paper and something sweet underneath, like fruit going soft in a cellar no one opened—resisted every category he owned.

He stood at the edge of the Near Faded market with his collar up and his ledger-sense humming, watching vendors sell things that should not have been for sale.

A woman behind a stall of glass jars offered bottled afternoons, each one labeled in a careful hand: *June, 3 o'clock, rain beginning.* A boy no older than ten sold shadows off a string like dried fish, and the shadows wriggled. Somewhere deeper in the lanes, someone was auctioning a staircase that went nowhere, and the bidding was spirited.

Oscar kept his hands in his pockets and his face arranged into the expression he used at royal banquets: attentive, unremarkable, forgettable on purpose. It had taken him years to perfect that face. He thought of it as his plainest dish.

*You are a bookkeeper,* he reminded himself. *Bookkeepers observe. Bookkeepers do not gawk.*

He gawked anyway, briefly, at a jar whose contents appeared to be somebody else's courage—a golden, restless light that pressed against the glass like a moth—and then made himself stop, because looking too long at the merchandise here felt like standing too close to an open till.

What he needed was passage. The map Lena had died pressing into his hands showed a second city beneath the first, and at its center, drawn in ink so old it had gone brown, the Well of Names. Between him and any road toward it lay a river. On the map, the river ran uphill. Of course it did.

And rivers meant ferrymen.

* * *

He found the ferry landing by following the sound of water moving the wrong way. The river came down out of the twilight hills—or rather, it went up into them, sliding past the landing with the patient confidence of something that had never once been asked to explain itself. The water was dark and slightly silvered, like tea with too much milk, and where it touched the stone steps of the landing, the stone looked younger than the stone above it.

The boat was long and low and black, and the ferryman sat in it as though he had been sitting there since before boats were invented, which, Oscar suspected, might be literally true.

Silt. He learned the name later; at first meeting, the ferryman was simply a shape in a hood, gray as river fog, with hands folded in his lap. Those hands were the only part of him that seemed fully present. They were large, patient, and stained at the fingertips with ink—not guild ink, not any ink Oscar knew, but something darker, the color of deep water.

"Passage," Oscar said.

The hood tilted. "Where to?"

"The far bank."

"There is no far bank." The voice was dry and unhurried, like pages turning. "There is only further in. You want further in?"

"I want the road toward the Well of Names."

Something moved under the hood that might have been a smile, or might have been the hood settling. "Everyone wants the Well. Nobody wants the fare." The ink-stained fingers unfolded, one by one, like a flower deciding against it. "I don't take coin. Coin is just metal that agreed to mean something. I take meaning itself. Memory, mostly. It travels well. Keeps its shape in the crossing."

Oscar had known this was coming. He had spent the walk from the market rehearsing negotiations the way he rehearsed everything: three openings, two fallbacks, one graceful retreat. He was, he reflected, about to haggle with a myth, and some traitorous corner of his mind found this *interesting*, which was the most dangerous thing a mind could find anything.

"What are your rates?" he asked.

"For you? Curious question." The hood leaned forward. "Fares aren't fixed. They're weighed. I take what the crossing costs *you*. For a king, a kingdom. For a beggar, a crust. For a man built entirely out of precautions—" the fingers tapped together softly, "—a precaution."

"That's vague."

"It's honest. Vague would be quoting you a number."

Oscar considered his inventory. He had, in his pockets: a stub of pencil, a folded map worth more than the palace, a Warden's seal wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs, and forty-one years of trained immunity sitting in his blood like a garrison nobody thanked. In his head: ledgers upon ledgers. Every poison he'd survived, catalogued. Every entry in the guild's books, memorized. His mother's—

He stopped. He had been walking down the inventory the way he walked down all his inventories, briskly, and his feet had arrived at her without asking permission.

His mother's face.

Here was the embarrassing truth, the kind of truth Oscar usually locked in a drawer: he could not actually remember when he had last looked at it deliberately. He knew she had laughed with her whole chest. He knew her hands smelled of bread. He knew she had hummed while she worked, a tune he could still half-hear. But her face—the specific architecture of it, the nose, the set of the eyes—had gone soft-edged over the years, worn smooth like a coin passed through too many hands. He kept it the way one keeps a pressed flower: carefully, and mostly by not touching it.

It was, he realized with a lurch, exactly the sort of item the guild traded. Bottled. Shelved. Sold to strangers who wanted to feel they'd once been loved.

"No," he said aloud.

"I haven't named a price yet," said Silt.

"You were going to."

"I was going to let *you* name it. That's the cruelty of the trade, if you want honesty. People always reach for what they can least spare. They think they're being clever, offering the worthless thing, and their hand goes straight past it." The hood gestured gently at Oscar's pockets, at his head, at whatever lived behind his eyes. "Go on. Offer me something cheap. Watch your own hand."

Oscar set his jaw. "My memory of the taste of almonds."

"Almonds?"

"Cyanide smells of almonds. It's professionally useful."

"And when did you last eat an almond for pleasure?"

Oscar opened his mouth. Closed it. He genuinely could not recall. Somewhere along the way, almonds had stopped being a nut and become a warning, and the warning had eaten the nut entire.

"You see," said Silt, not unkindly. "Already sold, years ago, to the work. I deal in what remains. Try again."

This was, Oscar decided, the worst negotiation of his life, and he had once negotiated with a duchess who wanted a refund on a Tuesday she'd already spent. He cast about. His father—he had a father somewhere in the fog, a big laugh, tobacco, gone early. Too thin. The palace kitchens, the smell of the great hearth at midnight, the cook Marta who slipped him heels of loaves—warm, but common; he could lose those and remain himself. The first time he'd balanced a ledger perfectly and felt the small clean click of it—

"Don't," said Silt sharply.

Oscar blinked. "Don't what?"

"Offer me the ledgers. I watched your hand drift toward them. Boy, if you give me the books in your head, you'll step off my boat knowing how to count and nothing worth counting. You'll stand on the far shore holding a receipt for yourself." The ink-stained fingers stilled. "Some fares I refuse. Not from mercy. From professional distaste. I am a ferryman, not a butcher."

The river went on climbing its impossible hill beside them, patient as arithmetic.

And because Oscar was, beneath everything, a man who trusted numbers above people and precision above comfort, he did the accounting. What did he actually possess? A life assembled entirely of precautions: immunity layered on immunity, lie stacked on lie, exit routes memorized from every room he'd ever stood in. A man made of precautions, Lena's ghost-voice seemed to say in his memory, and the price of crossing is a precaution.

But there was one thing he carried that was not a precaution. One thing he kept not because it protected him but because it was *his*.

"Her face," he said quietly. "My mother's face. That's the fare."

The hood was silent for a long moment. The river murmured upward. Somewhere behind them in the market, the auction of the staircase reached a fever pitch and collapsed into applause.

"You're sure," Silt said. It was not quite a question. It was the sound a door makes before it closes.

"No," Oscar said honestly. "But I'm certain. There's a difference. I've built my whole life on the second one."

"Hm." The ferryman extended one vast ink-dark hand, palm up. "Then we do it properly. Sit. This is a transaction, and transactions deserve ceremony, or they curdle."

Oscar stepped down into the boat. It rocked once, gently, like a cradle, and then went still as held breath. Up close, Silt smelled of the same wrong sweetness as the market, but older, concentrated, the cellar-no-one-opened distilled to its essence.

"Rules," said Silt, producing from his robes a small glass bottle, no bigger than a thumb, stoppered with wax. "One: I take what is given, wholly. Half-memories sour. Two: you keep the receipt." He produced a slip of paper, gray and soft as moth wing, and a pen cut from something that was definitely not a feather. "Three: the receipt is binding. If ever you wish to buy back what you sold—if it ever comes up for resale, anywhere in either realm—the receipt entitles you to first claim. Keep it safe. People lose receipts. People losing receipts is half my business."

"And the other half?"

"Selling them back at interest." The hood managed, somehow, to convey a shrug. "I never said I was good."

Oscar almost smiled. It was the first thing in this twilight country that had sounded like anyone he knew.

"How does it work?" he asked. "The taking."

"You tell me her face. Truly tell it, not the pressed-flower version—the real one, edges and all. Speaking it aloud gives it shape, and shaped things can be poured. Then I bottle it, and you'll find the space where it lived swept clean. You'll know you had a mother. You'll know you loved her. The love stays—it's too heavy to pour. But the face goes." The fingers curled around the little bottle. "Most folk, at this point, ask me if it hurts."

"Does it?"

"Worse. It doesn't hurt at all. That's what they can't forgive, afterward. You'd expect a wound. You get a tidiness." Silt's voice dropped, and for a moment the dryness fell away and something ancient and tired looked out through it. "They come back to my landing years on, customers do, angry, wanting to know why they feel *fine*. Why the hole doesn't ache. And I tell them: because grief needs a face too, and I took hers along with yours. You'll mourn her less. That's the theft inside the theft. Now you understand my customers better than you want to."

Oscar sat with that. The boat rocked in no water he could see moving.

"Do it," he said.

* * *

Telling her face was harder and easier than he expected.

Harder, because the words kept snagging. *She had brown hair*—no, brown was nothing, everyone had brown, and besides, at the temples it had gone early silver, silver like frost on a windowpane in the shape of ferns, and she'd complained about it and secretly been pleased. *Her eyes*—dark, yes, but dark the way beer is dark, warm through, and one of them had a fleck of gold in it, just one, off-center, like a coin dropped in a well, and when she caught him staring at it as a boy she'd say, "That's where I keep the sun on rainy days," and he had believed her absolutely until the precise, humiliating age of nine.

Easier, because once he started, the face came up out of him like water rising in a well of its own accord, detail after detail he hadn't known he'd kept: the small scar over her eyebrow from a kettle, the way her left eyebrow arched higher than the right when she didn't believe him (which was often; he had been a lying sort of child, apparently, which explained several things about his adulthood), the particular way she pressed her lips together before laughing so as not to laugh at whatever foolish thing he'd said, and always failing.

He told it all. He told it thoroughly, the way he entered figures in a ledger, complete and exact, because he could not bear to hand over anything sloppy.

When he finished, the twilight was quieter. Silt uncorked the little bottle and held it up, and Oscar saw—there was no other way to describe it—that the bottle filled with warmth. A glow the color of lamplit kitchen evenings, of bread and humming and one gold fleck in one dark eye.

"Beautiful stock," Silt said softly, and stoppered it, and sealed it with a thumbprint of dark ink. "Whoever buys this will think they're buying a stranger's memory. They'll weep at some stranger's kitchen table and never know why." He tucked it away in his robes. "Receipt."

The moth-wing paper wrote itself as the pen touched it, letters surfacing like fish:

*Received of Oscar Sandoval, one (1) memory: the face of his mother, complete. Payable back to bearer upon presentation, per terms. — S.*

Below it, in smaller script: *No refunds on regret.*

Oscar took the receipt. His hands were steady, which offended him.

"Well," he said. "Row."

Silt did something under the hood that was unmistakably, this time, a chuckle. "Oh, I like you. Terrible judgment. Excellent manners." The boat moved. There was no pole, no oar; the river simply agreed to carry them, and the silvered banks began to slide past, and the twilight market shrank behind them into a smudge of lantern-glow and wrong sweetness.

For a while neither spoke. Oscar tested the hole where her face had been, the way a tongue tests a missing tooth. Silt had been right. It didn't hurt. That was the unbearable part. He knew—he *knew*, the knowledge sat in him solid as a stone—that he had loved her with the whole of his small fierce heart. The love remained, vast and directionless now, like light with nothing left to fall on. He searched for her face and found instead a tidy swept room, and in the middle of the tidy swept room, a fact: *she loved you.* No face attached. Just the fact, glowing faintly, orphaned.

So this was what the guild's customers bought, he thought. This was what the vials and jars held. Not memories—amputations, healed too cleanly. Some duchess was walking around the mortal city right now having spent a Tuesday, feeling fine about it, unable to say why the week had a limp.

He understood them now, the buyers. That was the worst of it. He had always imagined them monsters, gourmets of other people's lives. They weren't. They were just people with holes, buying back pieces of themselves at terrible interest, and telling themselves the tidiness was peace.

"Question," Oscar said, to stop thinking.

"Questions are free. Answers cost extra."

"Why does a ferryman need a receipt system? Who's going to enforce a contract in a place like this?"

Silt was quiet long enough that the river answered first, chuckling over stones that were probably not stones.

"Everything enforces contracts here, bookkeeper. That's the one law both realms share. Debts outlive flesh. Names outlive bodies. Why do you think the old covenant was written in names?" The hood turned toward him. "You're carrying a dead woman's map toward a Well full of them. You of all people should know: nothing given is ever truly gone. It's only... filed."

"Filed where?"

"Where all filings go." The ink-stained hand rose and pointed—not at the banks sliding past, not at the hills the river climbed, but straight up, at the sky of the Faded realm, where the stars hung wrong, too close, arranged in patterns that looked less like constellations than like columns of figures.

Like ledgers, Oscar thought. The sky looked like ledgers.

The boat rounded a bend of silvered water, and ahead the banks narrowed toward a stone bridge, old and moss-eaten, and beneath the bridge, huddled in the dark like something ashamed of itself, a chapel. Small, forgotten, its spire broken, its windows dark. But as the boat drew closer, Oscar felt the hairs rise on his arms, and the Warden's seal shifted against his ribs as though it, too, had noticed.

"What's that?" he asked.

"Ferryman's advice comes free, since you tipped fair." Silt brought the boat alongside a rotting jetty below the bridge and steadied it with one hand. "That chapel has a resident. Old tenant. Been sitting in the dark longer than the dark's been dark. The covenant-folk put him there ages back, and the covenant-folk are all dead or worse, so nobody visits, and he's developed *opinions*." The hood tilted toward Oscar. "If you're hunting signatories—and you've got the look of a man reading a map he wishes he wasn't—you'll want to speak to him. Politely. In the old tongue. And whatever he says about betrayal—"

"Yes?"

"—believe every word. He earned them." Silt withdrew the hand, and the boat began, gently, to drift back from the jetty. "Mind the receipt, bookkeeper. And mind the hole. Tidiness is the most dangerous thing you'll carry today."

The gray shape folded back into the fog between one blink and the next, and the boat was gone, and Oscar stood alone on the jetty with a slip of moth-wing paper in his pocket and a swept-clean room where his mother's face used to live.

Above him, the broken chapel waited. And from somewhere inside its dark—faint, dry, unmistakably impatient—came the sound of stone clearing its throat.
