## 9. The First Signatory

The map called it the Chapel of Saint Verene the Unnumbered, and it took Oscar two days and three wrong turns to find it, which struck him as fitting. A saint who had been subtracted from every calendar and every hymnal would hardly want to be easy to visit.

It stood wedged between a tannery and a cooper's yard in the oldest quarter of the city, a place where the streets had stopped pretending to be planned and had simply settled into their arguments centuries ago. The chapel's roof had fallen in so long ago that a birch tree now grew through the nave, polite as a houseplant, its roots cradling broken pews. Pigeons had opinions about him from the rafters. The air smelled of tallow, wet stone, and the particular sweetness of rotting leather that Oscar associated with old account books — which was to say, with home.

He had come at dusk, because the map marked this door with a symbol he'd learned to read as *between*, and *between* was a time of day as much as a direction. He had also come armed, insofar as a food-taster could be armed, with a knife he mostly used for cheese, a stub of chalk, and the deep professional calm of a man who had swallowed things that would kill a horse and kept his complexion.

"Right," he said to the empty chapel, because talking to himself had become a habit somewhere around the third impossible ledger entry, and there was no one left to judge him for it except pigeons. "Signatory One."

The map showed a figure at the altar end of the ruined nave, drawn in that second geography's confident ink: a kneeling shape, small, patient. Oscar picked his way down the aisle over a carpet of birch leaves and found it at the foot of what remained of the altar steps.

A statue. Kneeling, hands folded, head bowed. About four feet of weathered grey limestone, worn smooth by rain that must have gotten in through the collapsed roof, moss colonizing its shoulders like a green mantle someone had draped on it out of respect. Its face had eroded into suggestion — a nose, a brow, the ghost of a mouth.

Oscar looked at it for a long moment.

"A statue," he said. "Of course. Why would anything in my life be simple anymore? I used to have a simple life. Banquets, ledgers, bed. Now I'm having a conversation with landscaping."

The statue said nothing, being a statue. But the map's instructions had been specific, and Oscar had copied them out twice, once in cipher, because he was still, at core, a man made of precautions.

*Speak the words of the covenant,* Lena's map read, in her sharp, slanted hand. *The first will hear you even sleeping.*

There followed nine syllables in an alphabet Oscar didn't recognize, beneath which Lena had written a phonetic rendering, and beneath *that*, in smaller letters, almost apologetic: *It sounds like clearing your throat. That is not a mistake. It is supposed to sound like that.*

Oscar cleared his throat, practiced the syllables twice under his breath — they did indeed sound like a very formal cough, the kind you'd use to interrupt a king — and then spoke them aloud to the mossy kneeling figure.

"*Vesh-ka-lumen. Torr-en-vash. Ilume.*"

The effect was not dramatic, which somehow made it worse. There was no flash, no rumble. What happened was that the quality of the silence changed. It went from the silence of an abandoned room to the silence of a room where someone has just decided whether to bother answering you.

Then the statue sighed.

Oscar would spend the rest of his life insisting he heard it sigh — a dry, mineral sound, like wind moving through a canyon, or like his old arithmetic master disappointed in a column of sums.

"Oh," said the statue, in a voice like gravel poured slowly from one sack into another. "Oh, wonderful. A living voice. Do you know how long it has been since a living voice said those words in this room?"

"I—"

"Two hundred and eleven years," said the statue. "I counted. What else was I going to do? I can't move. Counting was the whole entertainment budget." The eroded face somehow managed to convey that it was looking Oscar up and down. "You're not a Warden. You smell of ink and kitchens. Who are you?"

"Oscar Sandoval," Oscar said, and then, because apparently he was the sort of man who introduced himself honestly to masonry now, "royal food-taster. And, formerly, bookkeeper to a guild that trades in things that shouldn't exist."

"Formerly?"

"The guild's master is selling pieces of the Veil to the king, the covenant is failing, and a woman died in my arms on the fish docks handing me a map that led me here. So yes. Formerly. I've resigned by way of everything I own being either confiscated or cursed."

The statue was quiet for a moment. When it spoke again, its gravel voice had shifted into something drier still, if such a thing were possible.

"You're the new bearer, then. Lena's choice." Something moved in the tone — not warmth exactly, but the memory of warmth, the way a hearthstone holds heat after the fire dies. "How is she?"

Oscar looked down at the birch leaves. "She's dead. She died giving me the map. Silvered veins up her throat. She said one word. *Reforge.*"

The silence that followed lasted long enough that Oscar checked, absurdly, whether the statue had gone back to sleep.

"Ah," it said finally. "Ah, Lena." The gravel softened. "She was the only one who ever came. Did you know that? Two hundred and eleven years, and she came every year on the solstice. Sat right where you're standing and told me the news. Which kingdoms had fallen. Which had been invented. Whether the cooper's yard next door had burned down again — it burns down every forty years or so, it's practically liturgical." The statue's folded stone hands creaked, very slightly, the way old houses do at night. "And now she's dead, and the Veil is failing, and they've sent me a kitchen clerk."

"Taster."

"A kitchen clerk with a title. Wonderful. The covenant is saved." The sarcasm came off the statue in flakes, like its own limestone. "Tell me, kitchen clerk. What do you know of the covenant?"

"What the map told me. Nine signatories. Sealed at the Well of Names. It keeps the Faded realm and ours from... mixing."

"Mm. And what does your ledger-keeper's mind make of a covenant with nine signatories?"

Oscar considered. This, at least, was familiar ground; he had spent ten years reading contracts for a guild of thieves and finding the traps in them. "That it was designed to fail," he said slowly. "Nine points of failure. Nine people who can die, defect, forget, or be bought. Any competent accountant would have insisted on redundancy."

"*Any competent accountant,*" the statue repeated, with relish. "Oh, I like you. Don't tell anyone I said that; I have a reputation among the pigeons. Yes. Nine signatories, because nine was holy once, and holiness is always terrible engineering. And do you know what actually killed the covenant, kitchen clerk? Not the Faded. Not Corvane — though give that vulture time. Not any enemy at all."

"Betrayal," Oscar said. "You mentioned—" He stopped. Actually, the statue hadn't mentioned it yet. But the word had arrived in his head with the certainty of a sum that balances.

"Betrayal," the statue agreed. "From inside. Signatory Four sold a thread of the Veil within fifty years of the sealing. Just one, mind you, just to see if anyone would notice — and no one noticed, and that was the true poison. Once we learned no one was watching the watchers..." The gravel voice trailed off, and when it resumed it had gone quieter, and older. "Signatory Seven fell in love with something on the other side. Perfectly understandable. I've seen what lives over there. Some of it is very charming when it wants to be. Signatory Two decided the covenant was unjust — and here's the joke, kitchen clerk, here's the joke that has kept me company for two centuries — *she was right*. It *was* unjust. It sealed a door on a whole realm of people, some of whom never did anything worse than exist in the wrong shape. She was right, and her being right broke us faster than any villain could have."

Oscar lowered himself carefully onto a fallen pew, brushing away leaves. His legs felt odd. "What happened to them? The others?"

"What happens to everyone. Death, mostly. Two are statues now — I'll let you guess who the other one is, it's a famous one, you've probably walked past it and complained about the pigeons. One walked into the Veil and became something else; the map will show you, and I recommend a strong drink beforehand. One is alive and hates us all and will say no, but take her seal anyway, seals matter more than hearts in covenant-work, which should tell you everything about why covenants keep dying." The statue paused. "One of them, kitchen clerk, is a name you already know. The map knows it too. Look closely at the ninth mark sometime, when you're feeling brave."

Oscar's mouth had gone dry. "And you? What happened to you?"

"I?" The statue managed, in its eroded way, to convey a shrug. "I argued. That was my role. First Signatory, the Voice of Objection. Every clause, every seal, every sacrifice — I objected, and they wrote my objections into the margins, and the margins are why the covenant held as long as it did. And when the others began to fall, I objected loudest of all, and the Faded — or perhaps our own side, history is vague, history is always vague when it's embarrassing — turned me into a very effective paperweight." A dry rattle that might have been a laugh. "Two hundred and eleven years of objecting, and no one to write it down. Do you know what that's like?"

"No," Oscar said honestly. "But I know what it's like to write things down that no one reads. We may be opposite halves of the same misery."

"There it is," the statue said, with satisfaction. "There's the bookkeeper. All right. Kitchen clerk. Bookkeeper. Opposite-halves-of-misery. Listen to me now, because I am going to say the thing I have spent two hundred and eleven years composing, and I will only have the breath to say it once, and then we must talk business, because dawn is coming and the tannery men arrive early and they mustn't hear me, they think this place is haunted enough as it is."

Oscar leaned forward. "I'm listening."

"The last covenant did not die of enemies," the statue said. "It died of its own signatories. Of pride, and love, and righteousness, and fear, and one perfectly ordinary act of greed that no one bothered to punish. Whatever you rebuild at the Well — build it expecting betrayal. Build it so that betrayal is survivable. Trust no seal you haven't tested, no heart you haven't audited, and above all—" the gravel dropped to nearly nothing, and the pigeons went silent, as if they too were listening, "—above all, do not trust the covenant to make anyone good. It couldn't before. It won't now. It can only make them *bound*. The goodness has to come from somewhere else."

The chapel held the words the way a cup holds water. Somewhere outside, the tannery dogs began their evening argument.

"That's a terrible speech," Oscar said at last. "No offense. It's true and it's terrible and I wish to God I'd heard it before I started liking any of this."

"Good," said the statue. "Hate it properly. Remember it properly. Now — business." The stone hands creaked again, straining against two centuries of stillness, and got nowhere, and gave up with what sounded like dignity. "I cannot move. I cannot leave. But I am First Signatory, and my mark is not on the map, because maps can be stolen — look at my hands."

Oscar rose and knelt by the folded stone fingers. In the fading light he saw them: lines cut into the limestone, fine as script, filled with something dark that time had not eaten. Not a symbol. Writing. Rows and rows of it, tiny, covering every finger, both hands, running up under the mossy sleeves.

"What is it?"

"My objection," said the statue. "All of it. Every flaw in the old covenant, every clause that could be twisted, every name that could be forged, every door that could be bribed — carved into the only skin I have left, because paper burns and I do not. If you mean to reforge the covenant, you will copy every word before you go. It will take you three nights. Your fingers will cramp. You will curse me. Good. Cursing me means you're reading it."

Oscar stared at the tiny script, and the bookkeeper in him — the part that had survived banquets and guilds and a dying stranger's eyes — woke up and stretched with something horribly like joy. Three nights of transcription. A primary document. Marginalia from the founding itself.

"You're enjoying this," the statue accused.

"A little," Oscar admitted. "Is that wrong?"

"It's *necessary*," said the statue. "Enjoy the work, kitchen clerk. Everyone who built the last covenant hated the work, and look how that ended." The eroded face tilted, and for a moment the gravel voice lost its armor entirely. "Lena sat where you sit and asked me the same question you're about to ask. So I'll answer it early. Can it be done? Yes. Will it cost what the Well demands? Also yes. Those are the only honest answers in this chapel, and I've had two centuries to check them for errors."

Oscar pulled out his notebook, his good pen, and the little pot of ink he'd begun carrying everywhere the way other men carried knives. "Then let's find your errors," he said. "First line. Read it to me."

The statue chuckled, a sound like a rockslide deciding to be friendly, and began.

They worked until the moon rose through the broken roof and laid a silver stripe across the nave, and it was somewhere in the third hour, copying a clause about the binding of names that made his blood run cold in a way he couldn't yet explain, that Oscar heard footsteps in the lane outside — too many of them, too coordinated for tannery men, and one voice saying, low and certain:

"He came this way. The bookkeeper. Find the chapel."

The statue's whisper was barely a stirring of dust. "*Kitchen clerk. I believe your resignation has been declined.*"
