## 10. Hunted by the Crown

Oscar had a theory about trouble. He'd developed it over years of tasting other people's dinners, and it went like this: trouble, like poison, announces itself if you know how to listen. Bitterness at the back of the tongue. A faint almond sweetness where no sweetness belongs. A kitchen boy who suddenly won't meet your eye.

The trouble that came for him on the third night after Lena's death announced itself as a smell.

He was in his rooms above the chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, mending a shirt he didn't particularly like, when the air changed. Not the fog — the fog was constant, a damp gray companion he'd long since stopped noticing. This was something under the fog. Tallow smoke and wet wool and, beneath both, the particular oiled-metal scent of men who carried weapons they were proud of.

Soldiers didn't smell like that. Soldiers smelled of leather and onions and complaint. This was different. This was *institutional*.

Inquisitors, then.

Oscar set down his needle, threaded it through the fabric so it wouldn't be lost, and gave himself exactly ten seconds to feel afraid. He was generous with himself that way. Fear, properly rationed, was useful; fear left to run free made men stupid, and stupid men died in doorways.

Ten seconds. His heart hammered, his palms went slick, and somewhere behind his ribs a small voice pointed out that he was a food-taster, not a fugitive, and that this whole situation was a clerical error someone would surely sort out.

Then the ten seconds ended, and Oscar got to work.

* * *

Here is what nobody understood about royal food-tasters, Oscar reflected as he moved through his own rooms with quiet efficiency: you could not do his job and remain an ordinary man. The crown did not pay him to eat. The crown paid him to *notice*. Every dish that crossed his tongue was a small interrogation, and after eleven years of interrogating dinner, Oscar had become a man who read rooms the way priests read scripture.

His room, at present, told him several things.

First: the chandler below, old Brammel, had lit his sign early and let it gutter — which meant Brammel had been paid to be elsewhere tonight, or to be deaf. Oscar noted this without bitterness. Brammel had a granddaughter with bad lungs and good doctors who cost money. Everyone's ledger had entries they weren't proud of.

Second: the ladder to his loft had been repositioned two inches to the left. Small thing. But servants noticed things like that the way sailors noticed weather, and someone had climbed up here already, or intended to.

Third — and this was the one that made him smile despite everything — whoever was coming had come at the change of watch, when the lane lamps went unlit for six minutes while the lamplighter ate his supper. Corvane, or whoever had sold him, knew the city. They just didn't know it the way Oscar did.

Nobody knew it the way Oscar did.

Because here was the second thing nobody understood about food-tasters: a man whose entire profession consisted of surviving what other people prepared for him develops a certain relationship with architecture. For eleven years, Oscar had walked the palace's spine. He knew which walls were thick because they hid pipes and which were thin because some duke had wanted a bigger dressing room. He knew the service corridors — the servants' vein, the kitchen staff called it — that ran behind every great hall like arteries behind skin. He knew the door in the pantry that opened onto nothing useful unless you were carrying a tray, and the stair behind the tapestry of the Battle of Redford that the weavers had hung specifically to hide the crack in the plaster, and the coal chute that smelled of the river because it had once, before anyone living remembered, been a smuggler's entrance.

A palace, Oscar had long ago concluded, was simply a building pretending its servants didn't exist. Which meant the building's true map was drawn in servant-steps, and Oscar had spent a decade memorizing it without ever quite knowing why.

Now he knew why. It was the sort of knowledge you only understand retroactively, like most of the important entries in any ledger.

He packed in ninety seconds. Not the map case — that never left his body now, strapped flat against his ribs beneath his shirt, warm as a second heartbeat. He took coin, the small knife he used for cheese and emergencies, a change of shirt, and — after a moment's hesitation — the receipt from Silt the ferryman, because a man who kept ledgers kept all of them, even the ones that shamed him.

Down the loft ladder, past Brammel's shuttered window — "Sorry, old friend," Oscar murmured, meaning it — and out through the chandler's storeroom, where the smell of beeswax covered everything including, hopefully, him.

The knock on his street door came as he was easing open the yard gate. Three blows, measured, official. Then a voice, pleasant in the way that well-fed things are pleasant:

"Master Sandoval? Royal inquiry. We only want a word."

They always only wanted a word. The word was usually *confess*, and the sentence usually followed.

Oscar slipped into the alley behind the tannery, where the stench was so committed that even bloodhounds filed complaints, and began to run — not fast, not panicked, but at the pace of a man late for a delivery. Panicked men drew eyes. Busy men were invisible. This was lesson one of the servants' vein, taught to him years ago by a scullery maid named Pimm who had smuggled an entire roast goose through three checkpoints balanced on her hip like a fussy toddler.

Bless you, Pimm, wherever you'd gone. Your goose technique was saving a life tonight.

* * *

The first checkpoint was at Tanner's Bridge, and Oscar saw it before he saw it — that is, he registered the wrongness first: the beggar who usually haunted the bridge's near end was absent, and in his place stood a man too well-shod to be begging. Observation, caution, tolerance for poison. The taster's trinity. He turned down Coal Lane without breaking stride, mind already redrawing the city around him.

There were four ways off the bridge district. Two were watched now, almost certainly. One involved swimming, and the river in autumn was less a body of water than a slow-moving grudge. That left the way nobody took, because nobody knew it existed: the ash-chute of the old glassworks, which the apprentices used for smuggling ale and which emerged, via a series of drops Oscar's knees still resented remembering, into the cellar of the Fishwife's Rest tavern.

He was halfway down the chute, coated in gray filth and quietly grieving his shirt, when he heard boots above him and froze against the cold brick.

"—went to ground somewhere," a voice said, echoing down the shaft. "Corvane wants him found before he talks to anyone."

"And what exactly does he think the bookkeeper knows?" A second voice, younger, skeptical.

"Doesn't matter what he knows. Matters what he *has*. There was a woman dead on the docks with something missing from her hands. Corvane's been chewing his own lip since."

Oscar closed his eyes in the dark. So. They knew about Lena. They knew something had passed between her hands and his. Which meant Corvane hadn't merely tipped the inquisitors — Corvane was steering them, feeding them just enough to make Oscar hunted but not enough to make anyone ask why a guild master cared about a dead stranger's luggage.

Clever. Oscar respected cleverness the way he respected poison: professionally, and with a firm intention of survival.

The boots moved on. Oscar finished his descent, emerged into the Fishwife's cellar between a cask of white wine and a cask of something that smelled like it had achieved sentience, and stood for a moment in the dark, breathing, letting his heart settle.

That was when the memory arrived — unbidden, the way the worst ones did — of Lena's eyes staying open, accusing him, and the words she'd spent her last breath on: *Reforge it.*

"I'm working on it," he whispered to the dark. "Whoever 'it' turns out to be."

It was the first time he'd said anything aloud about the map since the docks, and saying it made it heavier. Ledgers weighed nothing until you spoke their totals aloud. Then they weighed everything.

* * *

The Fishwife's Rest at half past nine was loud enough to hide a regiment, which was fortunate, because Oscar looked like a man who had lost a fight with a chimney. He bought a bowl of stew with coins he dried on his shirt hem first — a taster's habit; damp coins passed disease, and disease was just poison that traveled slowly — and sat in the corner with his back to the wall, eating slowly and watching the room the way he watched a banquet table.

Two more of them came in while he ate. He knew them instantly, though they wore plain clothes: the way they scanned rather than looked, the way their eyes snagged on exits the way honest men's eyes snagged on pretty women. Inquisitors off duty were still inquisitors. You could put a hound in a ballroom, but it would still sniff the guests.

Oscar finished his stew, complimented the pot-girl on the seasoning — genuine; there was real marjoram in it, a small mercy in an unforgiving week — and left through the kitchen, because the kitchen of the Fishwife's Rest connected, via a passage built during some forgotten smuggling heyday, to the fish market's cold stores.

From the cold stores, the options bloomed. This was the part of the city Oscar loved without ever having admitted it: the underside, the servant-city, the network of passages and yards and cellar-doors that the great buildings pretended not to have. Most people thought the city was its streets. Streets were just the city's face. Oscar had lived his whole life in its pores.

He moved through them now with something that surprised him by feeling close to joy.

Not happiness — grief was still sitting in him like a swallowed stone, and fear hadn't gone anywhere either. But underneath both, in the place where competence lived, something was singing. Because this — this exact thing — was what he was *for*. Eleven years of noticing. Eleven years of small precautions stacked like coins in a jar. The shirt he'd mended instead of replacing, because mended clothes drew no eye. The habit of never using the same door twice in a week. The mental map maintained like a garden, weeded nightly before sleep. Every dull, unglamorous discipline of his careful, precautionary, much-mocked life had been, it turned out, rehearsal.

The world had finally asked him the question he'd spent his whole life studying for.

* * *

The palace itself was the risk, and therefore, logically, the refuge.

This was the sort of reasoning that would have horrified any sensible person, and Oscar embraced it with both arms. No one hunting him would look for him inside the palace on his night off — not yet. And he needed things that only the palace held: clean clothes from the staff quarters, his emergency purse hidden behind a loose brick in the linen room, and, most importantly, information. Servants heard everything. If inquisitors had been asking questions, the scullery would know within hours, and the scullery would tell him, because Oscar had spent eleven years being kind to scullery maids in ways that cost him nothing and meant everything.

He entered through the coal chute — the old smuggler's door, still stuck in the same place, still needing the same shoulder-lift-and-twist that he could have performed blindfolded, and had, once, on a dare from Pimm.

Inside, the palace breathed its nighttime breath: banked fires, wax and stone, the distant music of whatever the guards' mess was cooking. Oscar moved through it in stockinged feet, a ghost in his own workplace, and felt the strange doubled sensation of a man sneaking into the only building he had ever truly known.

The linen room first. Purse retrieved. Then the staff corridor, where he collected a fresh shirt from his own locker — pausing, absurdly, to straighten the spare collar — and then to the scullery, where he found exactly what he'd hoped for: Meggin, the youngest of the pot-scrubbers, still awake, still scrubbing, still willing to gossip with anyone who brought her honey cakes.

He had no honey cakes. He had honesty, which was rarer.

"Meggin." He crouched by her tub, keeping his voice under the slosh of water. "Men have been asking about me. I need to know how many, and what they asked."

Her eyes went wide, then — bless her, bless her forever — sharp. "Four of them. Yesterday and today. Wearing merchant clothes but standing like soldiers." She wrung out a rag with the violence of youth. "They asked when you came and went. Whether you had visitors. Whether you kept papers." Her voice dropped. "Mr. Sandoval, they asked if you'd ever seemed *afraid* of anything. Cook said that was a funny question to ask about you."

"Was it?"

"Well, yes." She frowned at him, scrubber's hands dripping. "You taste poison for a living. What's left to scare you?"

Oscar smiled, and it cost him more than she'd ever know. "Nothing, Meggin. Nothing at all. Now — the east service stair. Is it watched?"

"Every stair's watched except the one behind the stillroom. The one that smells of juniper. Nobody uses it since the mice."

"The mice and I have an understanding." He pressed a coin into her hand — not a bribe; a thank-you, and they both knew the difference, which was why she took it. "If anyone asks, I was never here. If anyone asks *nicely*, I was here yesterday, complaining about my wages like always."

"That last part's easy. You're always complaining about your wages."

"I contain multitudes, Meggin."

He left her laughing softly into her suds, and if laughter was a gift he could leave behind in that place, then let it stand in the ledger as payment rendered.

* * *

The stillroom stair smelled of juniper and mouse, exactly as advertised, and it let him out through the herb garden wall into the palace's outer yard, and from there the city took him back like a tide taking a swimmer.

But the city had changed while he was inside. He felt it at once — the same wrongness-smell as before, but thicker now, spread wider. Lanterns where lanterns shouldn't be. Men on corners who stood like soldiers dressed as merchants. They were tightening a net, patient and professional, and the center of the net was Candlewick Lane, and the edges were everywhere else.

Which meant the lodging houses were out. The guild cellar was out — going there now would be like a salmon delivering itself to the smokehouse. Even Marisol's name, which he'd found stitched inside the map case along with a courier's mark, was a door he couldn't knock on yet, not with the streets this full of ears.

So Oscar did the last thing anyone sensible would do.

He went to the chapel.

Not a proper chapel — nothing with a congregation or a roof in good repair. The ruined one behind the ropewalk, the one the city had forgotten so thoroughly that even the pigeons had renegotiated their lease. He'd found it years ago during a walk he'd taken specifically to learn the city's forgotten places, because forgotten places were where a careful man kept his reserves.

And it was there, wedged into the dark behind a fallen saint, wrapping his arms around a leather map case full of impossible geography, that Oscar finally let the fear finish its ten seconds — extended edition.

His hands shook. His breath came ragged. Somewhere out in the dark, men with institutional smells were hunting a bookkeeper who had lied for the first time in his life three days ago and was now, apparently, a fugitive of the crown.

"They only want a word," he whispered to the fallen saint. The saint, being stone, declined to comment, though its eroded face managed an expression of profound skepticism that Oscar found unfairly judgmental coming from a man who'd presumably also made mistakes.

He laughed — a small, cracked sound — and then, because laughing loosened something, he did what he always did with unbearable things. He itemized.

One: Corvane knew about Lena's death and suspected the transfer. Two: the inquisitors were looking for papers, which meant Corvane feared the map more than he feared Oscar's testimony. Three: they hadn't found him yet, which meant his disciplines held. Four —

Four was the entry he'd been avoiding. Four was the fact that the map showed a door three streets from his lodging, and that doors, according to everything he'd read in Lena's letters before burning them, opened both ways, and that if the crown was hunting him through the mortal city, then perhaps the smart move — the only move — was to stop being entirely in the mortal city at all.

Oscar looked down at the map case against his ribs. Warm. Patient. Waiting.

"You realize," he told it, "that tomorrow I'm going to open a door that wasn't there yesterday, and walk through it, and that this is objectively the worst decision of my carefully decision-managed life."

The map said nothing. Maps never did. But underneath his fear, in the ledger-room of his heart where he kept accounts no one audited, Oscar wrote a new entry in a steady hand:

*Item: one door, unmarked, three streets hence.*
*Item: one bookkeeper, compromised, armed with nothing but observation, caution, and a stomach that forgives anything.*
*Balance outstanding: everything.*

He pulled his coat tighter, settled deeper behind the fallen saint, and closed his eyes — not to sleep, but to rehearse. Tomorrow, the door. Tonight, the taster's trinity: observe, endure, survive.

Somewhere across the sleeping city, Corvane was chewing his lip. Let him chew. Oscar had survived worse things than being hunted.

He'd survived dinner.
