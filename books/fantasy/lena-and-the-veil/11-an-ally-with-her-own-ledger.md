## 11. An Ally with Her Own Ledger

Marisol Vane had three rules, and she had never once broken any of them.

One: never carry anything you can't eat, burn, or swallow.

Two: never deliver a package without knowing what's inside — or at least knowing how much it weighs, because weight tells you what the sender is afraid of.

Three: never trust a man who keeps his boots too clean.

She was thinking about rule three as she watched Oscar Sandoval pick his way through the fish market at dawn, moving like a man who had memorized every puddle in the city and resented each one personally. His boots were clean. Immaculate. Which meant either he was rich, or he was careful, and royal food-tasters were not rich.

So: careful. And careful men, in Marisol's experience, were either very safe to be around or extremely dangerous, with no middle ground whatsoever.

She had been looking for him for six days. Not because she wanted to. Because she owed Lena a debt, and Lena was dead, and dead creditors were the worst kind — they couldn't be haggled with, and they haunted you on principle.

The debt itself was simple, which is why it had festered. Two years ago, Marisol had carried a sealed letter from the Salt Quarter to a house that turned out to be full of crown inquisitors. She hadn't known. That was the point of sealed letters. The woman inside the house had been dragged out by her hair while Marisol stood in the doorway holding the receipt, and later — much later, after Marisol had spent a miserable winter convincing herself it wasn't her fault — Lena had found her drinking cheap wine on the toll bridge and said, "You didn't know. But now you do know what you don't know. That's worth more than innocence."

Then Lena had taught her to read the watermarks on guild paper, which had saved Marisol's life at least twice, and had never asked for anything back.

Until now. Until a fishwife told her about a taster who'd run from the docks carrying a dead woman's satchel, and Marisol had done the arithmetic, and the arithmetic only came out one way.

* * *

Oscar bought smoked herring from a stall near the Fish Gate, which struck Marisol as either sentimental or stupid, given that a woman had died there less than a week ago and the fishwives were still telling the story with relish. He counted out his coins precisely. He checked the herring for freshness with a sniff so expert it was nearly rude.

Marisol fell into step beside him.

"Don't," she said.

He didn't scream, which she noted approvingly. He did go very still, the way prey animals do, and then he said, "Don't what?"

"Run. Scream. Reach for that little knife in your left sleeve — yes, I know it's there, everyone who's ever been frisked by a nervous guard knows it's there. Just walk with me and listen."

"I'm listening."

"You're walking and pretending to listen. There's a difference. Walk faster."

They walked. The market woke up around them — awnings snapping open, a boy arguing with a mule, someone frying onions at a volume that suggested a personal grievance against onions.

"My name is Marisol Vane," she said. "I'm a courier. Independent. You'll have heard of the guild; you keep their books."

Oscar's stride hitched, half a beat, then resumed. "I don't know what you mean."

"Mm." Marisol smiled at an apple seller and stole two apples while the man wasn't looking, handing one to Oscar, who stared at it like it might be evidence. "Here's what I know, bookkeeper. Six days ago a woman died at the Fish Gate. Silvered veins up the throat. Before she died she gave something away. The watch found no body, no satchel, and one very confused fishwife. Meanwhile, Master Corvane has tripled his courier rates for anything going east of the river, put a price on information instead of packages, and asked me — me, personally, over tea he paid for, which should have been my first warning — whether I knew anyone who'd been near the docks that night." She took a bite of her apple. "And yesterday, a friend of mine saw a thin man with ink-stained fingers buying herring at the Fish Gate, which is either grief, stupidity, or a message. So. Which is it?"

Oscar said nothing for eleven steps. She counted. Then he stopped under an awning, out of the onion smoke, and looked at her properly for the first time. His eyes were tired in a way that went all the way down.

"You said Corvane asked about the docks," he said. "What exactly did he ask?"

"Whether anyone unusual had come into my hands lately." She watched him flinch at the phrasing. "Ah. So he used those words with you too. Interesting. He must really like them. Probably practices them in a mirror."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I owed Lena a debt, and I've just decided the payment is keeping you alive long enough to be useful." She finished the apple and pocketed the core — waste nothing, warn everyone. "Also because if Corvane finds whatever you're carrying before I understand what it is, I'll spend the rest of my life wondering, and wondering is undignified."

"That's your reason? Dignity?"

"It's a better reason than most. Most people's reasons are fear wearing a nicer coat."

Something shifted in his face — not warmth exactly, but recognition. The look of one professional meeting another across a crowded market and realizing they use the same knots.

"The truth, then," Oscar said quietly. "Since you're trading in it. Yes, a woman died in my arms at the Fish Gate. Her name was Lena. She gave me a map case and two words. And since then, three people have tried to follow me home, my rooms will be raided within days — I'd bet my ledgers on it — and the master of the guild I've served for nine years asked me, pleasantly, whether anything unusual had reached my hands." He paused. "I lied to him. It was the first lie of my life, and I want you to know it tasted better than I expected, and I find that alarming."

Marisol blinked. Of all the confessions she'd braced for, *I enjoyed lying* was not among them, and it disarmed her more thoroughly than any weapon could have.

"Well," she said. "You're either the most honest liar in the city or the most dishonest honest man. Either way, we should talk somewhere the walls aren't fed by Corvane."

* * *

The somewhere was a room above a dye-house in Tanner's Row, rented under the name of a woman who had died four years ago and whose rent had been quietly paid ever since by Marisol, because dead tenants attracted no questions and the dye-fumes discouraged visitors with noses. The room contained a cot, a table, a chair, and a locked strongbox that Marisol opened to reveal paper, ink, seals, and forty years' worth of other people's identities, sorted by season.

Oscar looked at the forged papers the way Marisol imagined other men looked at altars.

"These are good," he said, lifting a travel writ toward the window. "The watermark's right. The border stamp is — wait." He tilted it. "This is the old provost's seal. He changed the ribbon color in spring. Everyone knows this."

"Everyone who reads stamps for a living knows this," Marisol said. "Which is why I sell these to merchants, who don't. Merchants are grateful. Grateful customers tip."

"You're forging documents with deliberate errors."

"I'm forging documents with *affordable* errors. There's a difference, and the difference is dinner." She sat backward on the chair, arms folded over its back, and studied him. "Your turn. Audit time. I show you mine, you show me yours. What's in the map case?"

For a long moment she thought he wouldn't. She watched him weigh it — she could practically see the ledger behind his eyes, columns adding themselves, and she realized with a small cold shock that this was how he thought. Not like a spy. Like an accountant. Every person a line item. Every risk a sum.

Then he unrolled the map on the dye-stained table, weighted the corners with two apples and a spool of thread, and stood back.

Marisol had carried impossible things before. A jar of someone's courage. A stolen Tuesday, still faintly smelling of rain. She had once delivered a box that whispered her mother's voice, and she had charged double and never slept quite right again. But she had never seen anything like this.

The map showed the city — her city, her crooked streets, her bridges — and underneath it, in silver ink that moved when she wasn't looking directly at it, a second city. Doors marked on walls where she knew there were no doors. A river drawn running uphill. And at the center, where the palace should have been, a circle of small careful script around a single word:

*Well.*

"This is the Near Faded," Oscar said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man reading numbers he doesn't like. "Or a map of it. I've been through one of these doors. I saw what Veil-thread is. Where it comes from." He swallowed. "What the guild sells. What I've been *recording*, entry by entry, for nine years."

Marisol didn't touch the map. Rule one extended to maps that moved.

"Lena sent you this?"

"She died giving it to me. She said—" His jaw worked. "She said *reforge it*. I didn't know what *it* was until I read her letters. The covenant. The Veil. The thing that keeps us and... them... separate. And Corvane is cutting it up and selling it to the king in bottles."

"I know," Marisol said.

Oscar looked up sharply. "You know?"

"I know the guild's been paying couriers double to avoid the eastern districts, and I know why, even if I wish I didn't." Her voice came out harder than she intended. "My brother Tomás worked a warehouse job off Rope Lane last autumn. Good boy. Bad at cards, wonderful at singing, terrible taste in friends. One morning in the tenth month, I went to collect him for our mother's name-day dinner, and nobody at the warehouse had ever heard of him. Not the foreman. Not his lodgemates. His landlady swore the room had been empty for years." Marisol kept her eyes on the map, on the silver doors, because looking at Oscar while saying this was not survivable. "His name was gone out of the parish rolls. Out of my mother's mouth — she set three places at the table and cried without knowing why. Everything except me. Whatever they bottled out of him, they took the remembering with it, and I'm the only ledger he has left."

The dye-house fumes drifted through the floorboards, violet and sharp. Somewhere below, a worker began singing, tunelessly, about a girl from the coast.

When Marisol finally looked up, Oscar was watching her with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the look of a person recalculating everything.

"That's why you're here," he said. "Not the debt."

"The debt got me to your street." She shrugged, uncomfortable. "Tomás gets me the rest of the way. Debts end, bookkeeper. Brothers don't."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and it was the strangest partnership audit Marisol had ever conducted, because neither of them trusted the other and both of them kept being right.

She taught him the courier's grammar: how to read a road for watchers, how to split a journey so no single leg revealed the whole, how to make a checkpoint guard feel clever so he'd stop looking. He taught her things that made her skin prickle — how to test food for eleven common poisons with a spoon and a candle, how to tell a lie with your pulse steady (he demonstrated; her own pulse jumped just watching), how to notice which servant in a great house was afraid, because the frightened one always knew the way out.

"Where did you learn all this?" she asked, as he showed her how a bitter almond smell in wine could be masked by oil of roses.

"Nine years of tasting for people who want each other dead. You learn the kitchen the way a soldier learns a battlefield." He capped the vial. "Every dish is a question. Every guest is a suspect. Every meal you survive is a small exam you didn't know you were sitting."

"And the immunity? The fishwives say you ate poisoned quail in front of the whole court and didn't so much as sweat."

Oscar was quiet for a moment. "Trained tolerance," he said finally. "Small doses, over years. That's the official explanation."

"And the unofficial one?"

"I don't have one yet." He said it lightly, but his hand drifted, briefly, to his own forearm — an unconscious gesture, Marisol noted, the kind of gesture a man makes toward a scar he hasn't admitted to. "Ask me again when I've had time to be frightened properly."

"Oh, you'll have time," Marisol said. "Corvane will see to it."

By dusk they had a plan shaped like a list, because of course they did; she caught him drafting it in his head, lips barely moving, and decided she could grow accustomed to a partner who itemized. Two sets of travel papers — pilgrim and merchant, routes diverging at the river so neither could betray both. A rendezvous protocol with three fallbacks. And a first destination, argued over twice and settled once: the marked door three streets from his lodging, and beyond it, whoever or whatever waited at the far end of Lena's silver roads.

Marisol wrote out the papers herself, tongue between her teeth, using the good ink. When she slid them across the table, Oscar examined both — front, back, watermark, seal — for a long, insulting minute.

"The provost's ribbon," he said at last, tapping the border stamp. "It's wrong."

"It's *affordably* wrong."

"We're not selling these to a grateful merchant. We're running for our lives on them."

"Yes," Marisol said sweetly, "and if we're caught, the guard will feel clever spotting the error, and clever guards stop searching. It's called a flaw on purpose, bookkeeper. Try to keep up."

He stared at her. Then — and this was the moment Marisol would remember afterward, the moment she'd replay on bad nights and worse mornings — he laughed. A short, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house where no one had lived for years.

"All right," he said. "All right. Your flaws are on purpose. Mine apparently are too, though I haven't found the purpose yet." He held out his hand. "Partners. For the debt, and for Tomás, and for whatever Lena saw in me that I haven't found myself."

Marisol shook it. His grip was careful, precise, and warm.

"One condition," she said. "No secrets that get us killed. Secrets that merely embarrass us are fine. I collect those."

"No secrets that get us killed," he agreed. And then, because he was who he was, he added, "Though I should note that clause is difficult to enforce retroactively."

"*There* he is," Marisol said, and blew out the candle. "Come on. The door's three streets away, and I want to see the look on your face when—"

The knock came at the downstairs door. Three slow raps. Then three more.

Marisol froze, hand on the strongbox. She knew every knock in Tanner's Row — the dyers' knock, the rent-collector's knock, the knock of the widow upstairs asking to borrow salt she never returned.

That was none of them. That was a *counted* knock. Counted knocks meant professionals.

Oscar was already at the window, silent as smoke, looking down into the alley. In the last violet light of evening she watched his face change — watched the careful bookkeeper go somewhere colder and older.

"Inquisitors?" she breathed.

"Worse." He swung back from the window, already stuffing the map into his shirt. "Guild colors. Corvane's enforcers. They're checking the dye-house." He met her eyes across the darkening room. "How did they find us? Nobody knew about this place. Nobody *could* know."

Marisol's stomach dropped as the answer assembled itself, ugly and obvious, in the space between them. Someone had known. Someone always knew. And the only two people who'd known where a fugitive bookkeeper might run were standing in this room, looking at each other, each privately doing the same terrible arithmetic.

"Later," she said. "Blame each other later. Stairs or roof?"

Oscar glanced at the window, at the door, at the strongbox of forty years' worth of dead people's names.

"Roof," he said. "I've never once survived by using the front door."
