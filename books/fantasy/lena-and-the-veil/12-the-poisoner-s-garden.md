## 12. The Poisoner's Garden

The house at the end of Vetch Lane had no door knocker, which Oscar took as a fair warning. Houses that wanted visitors had knockers. Houses that wanted to be left alone had thorn hedges grown chest-high and trained into shapes that were almost certainly deliberate: a coiled serpent here, a reaching hand there, and one topiary near the gate that looked disconcertingly like a man mid-scream.

"Charming," Marisol said, pushing through the hedge gap with her satchel held high. "She's got a sense of humor."

"She's got a garden full of things that can kill you in eleven different ways," Oscar said. "I counted the ways on the walk up. The purple ones are aconite. The white star-shaped ones are probably datura. That little shrub by the wall is oleander, and if she burns it for warmth, the smoke alone will stop your heart."

"You know this how?"

"I'm a food taster. Knowing what's trying to kill you is the whole profession." He ducked under a low branch of something glossy-leaved and lovely. "The rest of the job is pretending to enjoy dinner afterward."

The garden opened into a courtyard gone gloriously wild. Roses climbed over a dry fountain. Bees—actual bees, fat and unbothered—drifted among foxglove spires taller than Marisol. And everywhere, tucked between the beautiful things, grew the other things: hemlock standing casual as parsley, monkshood nodding like an old friend, a trellis of moonflower that Oscar would have sworn was harmless until he noticed the way its vines had strangled a neighboring rosebush entirely.

A woman stood at the far end with her back to them, snipping cuttings into a wicker basket.

She did not turn around. "If you've come about the roses, they're not for sale. If you've come from the crown, I've been dead for nine years and I'd thank you to respect that. If you've come from Corvane—" the shears snipped, crisp as a verdict "—tell him his money still smells of the same rot it always did."

"We've come about Lena," Oscar said.

The shears stopped.

For a long moment the only sound was the bees, going about their honest work among the dishonest flowers. Then the woman turned.

She was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, with the kind of face that had once been beautiful in the way a drawn bow is beautiful—all tension and promise. Her eyes went over Oscar the way he'd seen customs officers go over smugglers' carts: unhurried, thorough, missing nothing. They lingered on his hands.

"Taster's calluses," she said. "You handle hot dishes bare-handed so you can feel temperature before you taste. Clever. Sloppy, but clever." Her gaze flicked to Marisol. "Guild courier. Third button wrong. You dressed in a hurry or you wanted someone to know you weren't who your coat says you are."

"I wanted *you* to know," Marisol said.

Something moved at the corner of the woman's mouth—not quite a smile, but a relative of one. "And you claim Lena sent you. Lena is dead."

"How do you know that?" Oscar asked.

"Because if she weren't dead, she'd have come herself, and she'd have brought wine, because she knew I refuse to discuss covenant business sober." The woman set down her basket. "I am Signatory Two. Or I was, when such things meant anything. My name is Odile Vasseur. I was royal poisoner to two kings, exiled by the second, and I have spent nine years growing flowers that cannot lie to me. What did she send you to say?"

Oscar reached into his coat—and stopped, because Odile's hand had drifted, casually, terribly, toward a cluster of dried seed pods hanging from the eaves.

"Slowly," she advised.

He withdrew the map case instead of the map itself, holding it out flat on both palms like an offering at an altar. "She didn't send me to say anything. She died putting this in my hands. Fish Gate, four nights ago. She said two words. *Reforge it.*"

Odile looked at the case for a long time without touching it.

Then she turned away and walked toward the house, calling over her shoulder: "Well, don't stand in my garden gawking. Half of what's blooming right now will put you in a grave with a smile on your face, and I haven't the space to bury you properly."

* * *

The inside of the house was a surprise. Oscar had expected skulls, or at least an atmosphere. Instead there were quilts on the chairs, a kettle on the stove, and shelves of books arranged with the loving precision he recognized from his own ledgers. A cat—a vast orange creature with the expression of a retired magistrate—occupied the best chair and declined to acknowledge them.

Odile set out three cups of tea without asking whether they wanted any. It was excellent tea. Oscar noted this professionally and then noted, also professionally, that he could detect nothing else in it, which either meant it was clean or meant Odile Vasseur was very good indeed.

"Lena hunted Corvane for six years," Odile said, settling across from them. "I told her she'd fail. I told her the covenant was dead, the signatories scattered or worse, and that chasing Veil-thread thieves was a young woman's game. She said—" and here the old woman's voice did something complicated "—she said, *'Someone has to be the last one who cares, Odile. After me, nobody.'*"

Marisol leaned forward. "You could have helped her."

"I could have gotten us both killed slightly faster, yes." Odile's fingers tightened around her cup. "I gave her my seal years ago. It's in that case you're carrying, unless I miss my guess, along with everything else she thought mattered. So don't lecture me about helping. I helped the only way I could bear to—from a distance, alive, useless."

"The seal isn't enough," Oscar said quietly. "We found the first signatory. A statue, in a chapel off Cooper's Row. He spoke to us. He said the covenant needs living signatories. Seals are... he called them receipts."

"Ambrose always did have a gift for making sacred things sound like accounting," Odile murmured. There was real fondness in it, and real grief underneath. She looked at Oscar for a long moment. "You're not what I expected. Lena's bearer—I imagined someone harder. Someone who'd already lost everything and had nothing left to lose."

"I have plenty left to lose," Oscar said. "That's rather the problem."

"Hm." She set down her cup. "Yes. All right. Here is my position, taster. Nine years ago I swore an oath of silence and exile, and I have kept it beautifully. Rejoining the covenant means breaking it. Breaking it means the old binding comes due—the price every signatory pays for having touched the covenant tongue. For Ambrose, it was his flesh. For me—" she spread her hands, elegant, scarred at the fingertips in ways Oscar's professional eye catalogued and disliked "—it will be something else. I know this. I agreed anyway, once, and I have no intention of agreeing twice out of sentiment for a dead friend."

"So you won't help us," Marisol said flatly.

"I didn't say that." Odile rose and went to a cabinet, from which she produced a tray. On the tray sat twelve cups.

They were identical: plain ceramic, unglazed, each filled to the same depth with the same amber liquid. Twelve cups, twelve swallows, indistinguishable even to Oscar's eye—and Oscar's eye was the best he knew of.

"One of these," Odile said, "holds a slow death. Not fast—the fast poisons are for amateurs and emergencies. This one works over days. First your hands go numb. Then your heart forgets its rhythm. Then, somewhere around the fourth day, it forgets entirely. The other eleven hold tea. Chamomile, mostly. Quite pleasant."

"And if I drink the wrong one?" Marisol asked.

"Then you die slowly and I feel briefly bad about it." Odile's eyes never left Oscar. "But it isn't your trial, courier. It's his. You want my help—my knowledge, my seal's authority, whatever scraps of the old covenant I still carry in my bones? Then prove to me that the man carrying Lena's map can do the one thing no one else in this company can. Identify the cup. Not by luck. By craft."

Oscar looked at the twelve cups. Identical. Truly identical—he made himself check twice, because hope was a poison too, and the antidote was rigor.

"Why?" he asked. "Not the riddle. Why does this prove anything?"

"Because Lena chose you," Odile said simply. "Lena, who trusted no one, who audited everyone, who once spent a month verifying that a baker in Lowbridge was exactly what he appeared to be before she'd buy bread from him. She pressed the fate of two worlds into the hands of a stranger on a dock. Either she was dying and desperate—or she saw something in you worth the risk. I intend to find out which. If it was desperation, I'll keep my silence and my garden, and you can all die at the solstice without inconveniencing me."

The cat opened one eye, considered the proceedings, and closed it again.

* * *

Oscar walked the length of the tray. Slowly. He put his hands behind his back, because touching the cups would be cheating and also because his fingers wanted to touch them, and wanting was how tasters died.

Twelve cups. Same liquid. Same level—no, wait. He crouched. Eleven cups sat at precisely the same fill line; the twelfth was lower by a hair's breadth, less than a drop. Evaporation? No—the tray had been poured recently; the surface of every cup still trembled faintly. But poisoners were careful people, and careful people measured. A slow death required a precise dose. Precision meant measuring. Measuring meant one cup had been filled separately from the others.

"That one," he said, pointing to the seventh cup. "Lower by a fraction. Poured apart from its sisters."

Odile's expression gave away nothing. "Guesswork dressed as observation. Anyone could notice a fill line."

"It's not the fill line." He crouched lower, nose nearly to the tray. "It's the smell. Eleven cups smell of chamomile and honey. The seventh smells of chamomile, honey, and underneath it—very faintly—of bitter almond. But here's the thing." He straightened. "Cyanide smells of bitter almond. Everyone knows that. Which is why no competent poisoner uses cyanide anymore—it's the first thing any trained nose hunts. So either you're incompetent, which I don't believe, or the almond note is a decoy and the true poison is masked beneath it. Something without a scent of its own. Something that hides behind a smell everyone's been taught to dismiss." He paused. "Colchicum, possibly. Or oleander extract—you grow enough of it out front to supply a small war."

Silence. The kettle ticked as it cooled. The magistrate-cat yawned enormously.

"You're wrong," Odile said.

Oscar's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the garden, through the bedrock of the world.

"The seventh cup is clean," she continued. "I lowered its fill deliberately, and laced it with almond, to catch clever boys who pattern-match instead of observing. The slow death is in the third cup. There is nothing whatsoever wrong with the smell of the third cup, because the poison I used has no smell, no color, and no taste—which is rather the point of it. You found the trap I built and walked straight past the truth." She picked up the third cup. "So. You failed."

The word landed like a stone in still water.

"But," Odile said, and now the relative-of-a-smile returned, fully realized this time, "you failed *beautifully*. Do you understand what I watched just now? Most men, given twelve cups, pray. Clever men look for the trick and stop looking when they find one. You found my trick, named it, dismissed it, and then kept looking—kept smelling, kept measuring, kept doubting yourself right up to the moment I stopped you. You didn't trust your own certainty. In forty years of poisoning kings, I have learned that certainty kills more often than arsenic." She held out the third cup to him. "Drink it."

Marisol made a small alarmed sound.

"It's the poisoned one, girl, yes. Drink it, taster. All of it."

Oscar took the cup. His hands were steady—they were always steady; that was half his training—but his pulse was doing something undignified. He looked into the amber liquid. Somewhere in it, invisible, patient, was a death with a four-day schedule.

*My curse,* he thought. *My body that refuses to die. The thing that makes me useful and strange and slightly wrong at every feast I've ever worked.*

He drank.

It really was pleasant tea. Chamomile, honey, a whisper of lemon balm. He drained it and set the cup down upside down, the old guild signal for *finished, satisfied, no complaint.*

Odile watched him with an intensity that would have flattered a lover. "Four days," she said. "Numbness in the hands by tomorrow evening. Irregular heart by the second day. By the fourth, if my craft still holds and your body is merely mortal, you'll be dead in my guest room." She folded her arms. "Unless."

"Unless," Oscar said, "it doesn't take."

"Show me your forearm."

He rolled up his sleeve. Odile took his wrist—her grip surprisingly strong—and turned his arm over, examining the veins, pressing here and there with a thumbnail. Her frown deepened by degrees.

"When did you last eat nightshade?" she demanded.

"At the chancellor's banquet, in spring. In the sauce. Accidentally on purpose—it was meant for the treasurer."

"And foxglove?"

"Tea at Lady Merrow's, two months ago. She waters her plants with the runoff. Everyone knows not to drink at Merrow House, but the alternative was insulting her, and insulting her is worse."

"And you're still standing."

"I'm always still standing. It's the one thing I do reliably."

Odile released his arm and stepped back, and the look on her face was one Oscar had never received before and couldn't immediately classify. It contained wonder. It contained pity. It contained, most strangely of all, a kind of recognition—as though she were looking at a plant she'd read about in old books and never expected to see growing wild.

"Faded blood," she said softly.

"What?"

"Nothing. An old wives' term. Forget I said it." But she said it the way people say *nothing* when they mean *everything*, and she turned away quickly, busying herself with the teapot. "The dose won't kill you. I doubt it will even numb your hands. Your body will metabolize it like dessert." She glanced back over her shoulder. "Which means, taster, that you've just passed a trial designed to be unpassable. The poison was never the test. The test was whether you'd drink knowing it might kill you—for strangers, for a dead woman's errand, for a world you owe nothing to." She set down the pot. "You did. So. I'll come."

Marisol let out a breath she'd apparently been holding since the tray arrived. "Just like that?"

"Not remotely like that. I'll come because the alternative is watching the Veil tear open over my garden, and I've grown rather attached to the garden." Odile began pulling books from the shelves with brisk efficiency, stacking them into a leather satchel. "But hear my terms. I am not a hero. I am a specialist. I will identify, advise, and where strictly necessary, poison. I will not charge barricades. I will not give stirring speeches. And if we fail, I intend to die last, comfortably, ideally mid-sip of something excellent."

"Those are the worst heroic terms I've ever heard," Marisol said.

"They're the only terms on offer."

Oscar looked at the overturned cup on the table—at the slow death he'd swallowed, already dissolving harmlessly somewhere in his blood, his strange inherited blood, doing whatever strange inherited things it did. *Faded blood,* she'd said. He turned the words over like a coin of unknown currency.

"Signatory Two," he said formally. "Welcome to the covenant."

"Don't sanctify it yet, boy. We've a long road and a short calendar." Odile shrugged into a traveling cloak the color of dried foxglove, then paused at the door, hand on the frame, and looked back at him with an expression that had shed all its armor. "Lena wrote to me, you know. Last winter. One letter, unsigned, in cipher. She said she'd finally found the answer to why the covenant broke in the first place—and that the answer was a person. She said when the time came, I'd know him by what he could survive." Her eyes dropped, briefly, to the empty cup. "I thought she was being poetic. She was never poetic. She was being precise."

The cat, sensing departure, rose at last from its chair and wound once around Odile's ankles with the air of one granting a reluctant blessing.

"Come along, then," Odile said. "We've eight days, a child to find, and a king's worth of stolen years to account for. And taster—" the ghost of the smile, one last time "—when we reach the next trial, try to fail beautifully again. It seems to be your particular genius."

They stepped out through the lethal garden, past the screaming topiary, into the long gold light of evening—and behind them, in the empty house, the twelve cups sat in their row, eleven clean and one deadly, waiting for no one, which was, Oscar reflected, the happiest ending any poison ever gets.

He did not yet know what Odile had seen in his veins. He would learn. But that was tomorrow's ledger.
