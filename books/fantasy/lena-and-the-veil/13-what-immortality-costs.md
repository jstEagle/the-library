## 13. What Immortality Costs

Marisol had a rule about secrets: never carry more than three at once, because four was the weight at which a courier started walking like a woman with a secret, and women who walked like that ended up in rivers.

She was currently carrying five.

One: Oscar Sandoval was not merely a guild bookkeeper but something rarer—a man who had looked at the Near Faded market and come out the other side still wanting to do arithmetic about it. Two: she had forged travel papers for him under the name *Master Ambrose Quill, purveyor of medicinal vinegars*, and he had thanked her so sincerely for the vinegar that she'd nearly laughed herself sick. Three: Lena Vey was dead, and Marisol owed her. Four: the poisoner's garden lay two days east, and Signatory Two did not, by all accounts, enjoy visitors. Five—

Five was the one she hadn't said out loud in six years.

They were camped in the lee of a fallen waystone on the second night of travel, and Oscar was doing what he always did when the fire burned low: balancing figures in a little pocket ledger, muttering numbers like prayers. The boy—no, the man; there was nothing boyish about those careful hands—had an air of a person who believed the universe could be reconciled if only someone kept decent records.

"Your ink is freezing," Marisol observed.

"My ink is fine."

"Your ink has icicles."

"They're decorative." He blew on the page anyway and glanced up. "You've been quiet since the bridge toll. Quieter than usual, I mean. You have a thinking face. It's very similar to your 'I'm about to overcharge someone' face, which is making me nervous."

"That's fair," Marisol said. She fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. Somewhere beyond the dark, the Veil ran thin as old parchment, and things on the other side could probably smell them cooking. "I'm going to tell you why I'm really here. And after I tell you, you're going to want to say something kind, and I'd take it as a personal favor if you didn't."

Oscar closed his ledger. That, more than anything, told her he understood. A bookkeeper knew when the accounts were about to get ugly.

* * *

"I joined the guild at fourteen," she began. "Not because I wanted to smuggle bottled courage and stolen Tuesdays. Because couriers eat. My mother had died of the gray fever, my father before her of a fall that was nobody's fault except gravity's, and there was me and Tomás, and Tomás was nine, and nine-year-olds require an alarming quantity of bread."

"Speaking as a former hungry child," Oscar said quietly, "accurate."

"So I ran packages. Little ones at first. Love letters that had been purchased rather than written—that was a whole trade, did you know? Grief-stricken widowers buying secondhand devotion by the spoonful. Then bigger things. Years, eventually. Do you know how years are sold?"

"I've seen the entries," Oscar said. "'Bottled years, vintage unspecified.' I assumed it was metaphor. Guild inventory is full of poetry pretending to be accounting."

"It's the other way around. Accounting pretending to be innocent." Marisol pulled her cloak tighter. "Here is what Corvane never writes down, because writing it down would make it real: every vial of bottled years is cut from the Veil itself. The boundary between our world and the Faded one—it isn't stone, it isn't law, it's more like... cloth. Woven. And it can be sheared. You shear a thread, you bottle it, and some gray-faced lord drinks it and wakes up feeling twenty again. One thread, one year. Clean transaction."

"And the cloth gets thinner."

"The cloth gets thinner." She let that sit in the firelight. "You saw the market. You saw what Veil-thread looks like raw. Now imagine the king's cellars. Imagine forty years of the king's birthdays, each one bought back, each one paid for out of the same bolt of fabric. The crown thinks Corvane is selling them immortality. He's selling them the wall between us and everything that wants us eaten, one goblet at a time."

Oscar had gone very still, in the way of a man recalculating everything. "The quantities," he murmured. "The Veil-thread purchases in the ledgers. They weren't inventory. They were withdrawals."

"Now you see why I needed you alive and not, say, sold to the inquisitors, which would have paid better."

"You considered it?"

"For a full hour. You snore like a man being robbed, which made the decision easier." But the joke came out flat, because they both knew where this road went, and Marisol found she couldn't put off the last mile any longer. She stared into the fire until her eyes watered, which gave her a respectable excuse.

"Tomás," she said. "My brother. When he was seventeen he took work in the guild house. Bottling room. Good pay, terrible hours, and the bottling room boys weren't told what they were handling—they just knew the vials had to be sealed singing, don't ask me why, some property of the thread. One night a batch came through badly cut. Not sheared clean—torn. Torn thread is unstable. It wants to go back to the weave, and if it can't go back, it takes something with it when it goes."

"What does it take?"

"A name." Her voice held. She'd practiced holding it for six years; it would hold now. "Tomás came home from his shift and I looked at him and I knew his face—I'll always know his face—but I couldn't remember his name. Not forgotten. *Erased.* Like a line ruled out in a ledger. And then I went to the parish rolls, and there was no record of anyone by that name ever born in our district. His landlord had no memory of him. The priest who'd baptized him remembered baptizing 'some child or other.' Our neighbors asked me why I kept setting a second plate at supper."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, something with too many joints clicked past and thought better of investigating their camp, because even the Faded respected a woman mid-story.

"He's alive," Marisol said. "That's the worst part. Whatever the torn thread took, it took the *name*, not the man. He's somewhere in this city breathing and eating and being nobody to everybody. I've spent six years looking for a person whose name I can't remember, in a city that's forgotten him harder than I have. All I have is—" she touched the inside of her wrist, where a faded scar sat in the shape of a crooked M, "—this. We cut our initials matching, when he was ten, because he read too many ballads. The scar remembers what the world won't."

Oscar set down his pen. He did not say anything kind. He did something better: he opened his ledger to a fresh page, wrote *T——, brother of Marisol, erased by torn thread, guild batch, to be found*, and turned it so she could read it.

"You can't write his name," he said. "So we write the debt instead. Debts survive erasure. It's practically their whole nature."

Marisol discovered that her eyes were doing something embarrassing, and that the darkness was sufficient cover, and that she was grateful to him in a way she intended never to mention.

* * *

They walked the next day through country where the Veil wore visibly thin—the light came down slightly wrong, buttery at the wrong hours, and once they passed a pond where the reflection showed clouds from a different sky entirely. Oscar stopped and stared at it for a long moment.

"Is that normal?"

"No."

"Should I write it down?"

"Everyone always writes it down," Marisol said. "That's half the problem. The guild turned the end of the world into inventory, and inventory feels safe, and safe things get ignored. You want to help? Start noticing things without reaching for your pen."

He looked genuinely wounded. "Noticing without recording is just gossip."

Despite everything, she laughed—a short, cracked sound, but real. "You're going to be insufferable right up until the moment this kills you, aren't you?"

"It's the plan, yes."

By late afternoon the land changed again, softening into a valley that smelled overwhelmingly of flowers, which in Marisol's professional experience meant one of two things: perfume merchants, or poison. Given that they were looking for an exiled royal poisoner, she put her money on poison.

The garden announced itself before the house did. Roses climbed what might once have been a fence, blooms deep as wine and sweet enough to make the teeth ache. Foxglove stood in ranks like soldiers. There were nightshades trained along trellises with the loving discipline of prize vines, and a bed of small white flowers Marisol didn't recognize and decided firmly not to touch, smell, insult, or make eye contact with.

"She grows all this in the open," Oscar whispered. "No walls?"

"Walls are for people who fear thieves," Marisol said. "She fears nothing that steals. Only things that touch."

The house, when they reached it, was modest, whitewashed, and had a sign nailed above the door that read, in neat letters: **KNOCK IF YOU MUST. THE GARDEN IS FASTER.**

"She's got a sense of humor," Oscar said weakly.

"She's got a sense of *warning*. Different thing." Marisol squared her shoulders, checked that the poisoner's old guild seal was secure in her inner pocket—Lena's letter of introduction, pressed wax and all—and knocked.

The door opened on a woman perhaps sixty, silver-haired, with forearms bare to the elbow and stained green to the wrist, as though she'd been caught in a rain that fell only inside her own house. Her eyes went from Marisol to Oscar and back with the unhurried assessment of someone deciding whether a specimen was worth potting.

"You're the courier Lena wrote about," the woman said. "Before she died." Something moved behind her face, quickly filed away. "And you're the taster. Sandoval. Your reputation precedes you—Corvane speaks of you often, in the tone men use for tools they intend to break."

"Madam Odile," Marisol said, with a courier's best bow. "We've come about the covenant. About the Well. About—"

"Yes, yes, the end of the world, I read the letter twice before I burned it." Odile stepped aside. "Come in before the angel's trumpet opens. It likes visitors. That's the problem with it."

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of drying herbs and lamp oil, and every surface bore either a jar, a blade, or a book, occasionally all three. Odile poured tea without asking whether they wanted it—an act Marisol recognized as either hospitality or a test, and with this particular hostess, almost certainly both.

Oscar looked at his cup. Looked at Odile. Looked at the cup again.

"You may drink," Odile said, amused. "Or you may prove yourself first. As it happens, I have a use for a famous tongue." She swept jars and papers from the table and set down twelve cups, identical clay, arranged in a row with the ceremony of a card sharp. "An old habit. Every guest who comes asking favors drinks. Twelve cups. Eleven contain nothing worse than chamomile. One contains a slow death—slow enough that you'd walk out of my garden, feel unwell by morning, and be comfortably dead by week's end. Choose correctly, and we talk business. Choose wrongly..." She shrugged elegantly. "Well. The garden is faster, but not by much."

Marisol's stomach dropped. "Odile, he's not—"

"He's a royal food-taster," Odile said, watching Oscar with bright, clinical interest. "Which means he has spent years training his body to tolerate doses that would fell an ox, and his mind to notice what doesn't belong. This is not cruelty, courier. This is an interview. The last covenant died because its signatories trusted people who hadn't earned it. I don't repeat mistakes."

Silence stretched. Oscar studied the cups.

And Marisol watched him work, and it was like watching a lockpick at a difficult door. He didn't touch anything at first. He leaned close—not drinking, *reading*. The surface tension of one cup, third from the left, sat a hair too smooth, as though something oily clung beneath. Two cups had faint rings of dried splash on their rims, careless pours; the deadly one would have been poured with a surgeon's care. The eleventh cup sat a finger's width further from its neighbor than the rest—set apart, the way one sets apart the thing one doesn't want to grab by accident in a hurry.

But it was the smell that settled it. Marisol saw his nostrils flare, barely, and saw him glance at the window where Odile's foxglove grew in ranks, and saw the tiny pause of a man matching a scent to its source.

"The third cup," Oscar said. "There's a film on the surface—something resinous, maybe dissolved from a root. And it smells faintly of the purple flowers outside. Foxglove-adjacent. Slowed heart, cold sweat, death in days. Slow, like you said." He straightened. "Also, you placed it apart from the others by a thumb's width. People do that with dangerous things. Even poisoners. Especially poisoners."

For a long moment Odile said nothing. Then she picked up the third cup, swirled it, and drank it herself, all of it, meeting his eyes over the rim.

"Antidote in the glaze," she said, setting it down. "But your reasoning was sound, and the observation about the spacing—" she smiled, and it transformed her face into something almost warm, "—that was the answer of someone who has watched poisoners work. Very good, taster. Better than good. The court wasted you on quail."

"Thank you," Oscar said, and then, because he was apparently constitutionally incapable of letting a kindness stand unexamined: "Was the film real, or did you paint it on for the test?"

"Real. I respect you too much to fake the evidence." She refilled his cup with plain tea and pushed it across. "Now. You want my seal for the reforging. Lena asked the same thing in her letter, and I'll give you the same answer I gave her, may she forgive me: no."

Marisol leaned forward. "Madam Odile—"

"You haven't heard my price yet, so stop rehearsing your arguments." Odile rose and went to a cabinet, and from among its bottles she took out a small leaden case, worn smooth by handling. She set it on the table between them but did not open it.

"One hundred and seven years ago," she said, "I signed the covenant. I was young, I was brilliant, and I was promised that my silence would cost me nothing but discretion. The covenant's binding runs deeper than promises. For a century I could not speak of the Veil—to anyone, in any form, written, sung, or screamed into a pillow. I watched Corvane begin his shearing and I could not warn a single soul. I wrote letters I couldn't send. I taught apprentices poisons and never once told them why precision mattered beyond craft. Do you understand what it is, to watch a catastrophe assemble itself piece by piece and be forbidden to say *stop*?"

Neither of them answered. Outside, the garden rustled in a wind that smelled of honey and harm.

"When the covenant broke—when Lena's generation let it lapse—I wept. Not from grief. From relief. The silence lifted, and the first thing I did with my voice was scream at a magistrate, and I have never regretted anything less." Odile's hand rested on the leaden case. "I will not bind myself again. Not to a covenant, not to a cause, not to a pair of earnest children with a map. I am done paying for other people's wars with the only currency I have left, which is myself."

"But you kept the seal," Marisol said quietly.

"I keep everything. It's a flaw." Odile slid the case across the table. Inside, on a bed of rotted velvet, lay a ring of dull green metal stamped with the old sigil of the Wardens—one of the signatory seals, intact, waiting. "Take it. It will burn at the Well when the time comes, and my part of the old bargain burns with it. Consider it a payment, not a pledge. Sixty years ago I owed Lena's mistress a life; today the debt transfers. That's all this is. Debt settlement." Her voice roughened, just slightly. "Don't make it more than that. I couldn't bear it if it were more than that."

Oscar closed his hand around the case and bowed his head. "It's enough, madam. More than."

"Flatterer. Drink your tea." But she said it gently, and she refilled Marisol's cup too, and for a while the three of them sat in the herb-smelling quiet of a house built by a woman who had made peace with exactly as much of the world as she could afford.

It was Marisol who broke it, because the fire in her chest had been building since the waystone, since the ledger entry, since six years of second plates at supper, and it needed somewhere to go.

"Odile. The vials. The batches. Is there any way a torn thread could be reversed? Could a name be given back?"

The old poisoner was silent long enough that Marisol's hope began its familiar work of dying politely. Then Odile said, "Reversed, no. Restored—" and her eyes flicked to Oscar, to the map case at his belt, to the whole improbable shape of the errand they carried, "—the Well of Names keeps every name ever surrendered to it. Nothing given to the Well is truly lost. Whether it gives anything *back*..." She spread her stained hands. "That depends entirely on what walks away from the reforging with the authority to ask."

Marisol felt the words land somewhere below her ribs and catch fire. Tomás. Six years of hunting a nameless man through a forgetting city—and the answer had been sitting at the bottom of the same well they were already dying to reach.

Oscar was watching her with that careful, adding look of his. "Marisol," he said slowly, "you knew that. Didn't you. That's why you've been coming with us. Not for Lena. Not only for Lena."

She met his eyes and didn't bother lying, because lying to a bookkeeper was a waste of good material. "I've been coming with us," she said, "because the ledger of my life has exactly one unpaid line in it, and I mean to see it settled if I have to drag the whole guild, the crown, and both realms to the Well to do it."

Outside, in the gathering dusk, the garden rustled—and beneath the rustle, faint and wrong, came a sound none of them liked: a bell, far off, ringing the pattern the guild used for *retrieval*. Someone had reported two travelers on the poisoner's road.

Odile stood, unhurried, and began blowing out lamps. "Back door," she said. "Through the nightshade. And taster—if Corvane's hounds are already sniffing this valley, you should know they're not following you."

"Then who?" Oscar asked.

Odile's face, in the last lamplight, was grim as a headstone. "They're following the seal. Which means someone told them a dead Warden's ring is on the road to the Well—and there are only three people alive who knew I still had it."
