## 14. Signatory Two Refuses

The poisoner's name was Odile Vane, and she made tea the way other people made threats.

"Drink," she said, setting three cups on the table in her garden, where every flower was beautiful and at least half of them could kill you before you finished admiring them. "It's only chamomile."

"That's what worries me," said Marisol.

Oscar picked up his cup and drank without hesitation. It was, in fact, chamomile — good chamomile, with a whisper of honey and something underneath it, green and bitter, that he recognized the way one recognizes an old enemy across a crowded room: moonwort extract, a mild paralytic, harmless in this dose to anyone but a fool who gulped. He let it sit on his tongue a moment longer than politeness required, then swallowed.

"You put moonwort in it," he said.

Odile Vane's eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch. She was a small woman, silver-haired, with hands that looked soft and were not. Her garden climbed the walls of a ruined conservatory on the city's forgotten western edge, and it was the most dangerous lovely thing Oscar had ever stood inside, which was saying something, given his profession.

"I put moonwort in everything," she said. "Habit. You'd be astonished how many visitors it weeds out." She turned to Marisol. "Your friend has a remarkable tongue."

"He has a remarkable everything," Marisol said. "Most of it I don't want to know about."

They had come at dawn, through streets still blue with fog, following the map's second geography to a door that existed on both sides of the Veil at once. The chapel behind the conservatory was roofless and patient, its stones wearing moss like old velvet. Somewhere in that chapel, according to the stone signatory's grudging directions, slept the second seal of the old covenant.

Oscar set down his cup. "Madam Vane. We've come about the covenant."

"I know why you've come." She poured herself tea from a pot that steamed faintly violet. "The stone sent you. It always did have a talent for delegating." Her voice was light, almost pleasant, and beneath the pleasantness ran something worn smooth as a river stone — something that had been angry for so long it had stopped being sharp. "Sit. Both of you. If we're going to have this conversation, we'll have it properly, with tea, and you will tell me first how Lena died."

Oscar blinked. "You knew her?"

"I knew *of* her. The last Warden. The last one who kept writing to me even after I stopped writing back." Odile's hands were very still around her cup. "So. Tell me."

He told her. The fish gate, the fog, the silvered veins crawling up the stranger's throat like frost climbing a window. The map case pressed into his hands. *Reforge it.* The eyes that stayed open.

Marisol looked away at the part about the eyes. Odile did not look away at all. She listened the way she had presumably listened to dying men describe symptoms — completely, without flinching, cataloguing.

"The silvering took her," she said at last. "That's Veil-thread poisoning. Prolonged exposure. She'd been handling the stuff for years, tracking where Corvane cut it from." A pause. "I told her once, in my last letter, that if she kept going she would die of it. She wrote back that everyone dies of something, and most people die of nothing worth naming."

The garden rustled around them. One of the flowers — a bell-shaped thing the color of bruised plums — turned slightly on its stem to face Oscar, which he decided firmly not to think about.

"You were a signatory," he said. "Signatory Two."

"I was." Odile set down her cup with a small, precise click. "A hundred and twelve years ago, give or take a decade I'd rather forget. And now you're going to ask me to sign again. So before you do, boy, let me show you what signing costs."

* * *

She led them into the chapel.

It was cooler inside, and quieter, the way ruined places are quiet — not silent but *attentive*, as though the stones were listening for footsteps they remembered. Light fell through the missing roof in pale columns. Along the walls, half-swallowed by ivy, ran a frieze of carved figures: seven of them, hand in hand, encircling something Oscar couldn't quite make out because the center of the frieze had been chiseled away.

"We were seven," Odile said. Her voice changed in here. It lost its polish. "Seven names, freely given, woven into the covenant when it was forged. Not signatures on a document — understand that. The old books make it sound like a contract. It wasn't. It was a *surgery*. Each of us gave something to the Veil, and the Veil grew around the giving, the way bone grows around a pin. My contribution was my silence."

She stopped before the second figure in the frieze. It was a woman holding a cup, and someone — long ago, deliberately — had scratched out her mouth.

"For a century," Odile said, "I could not speak of the covenant. Not to anyone. Not to explain, not to warn, not to say goodbye. The words simply would not come. I would open my mouth and it was like pushing against a locked door from the inside. Do you know what that does to a person? To watch your daughter marry a man you knew would break her heart, and be unable to say one word of warning? To stand at a friend's deathbed while she begged you to tell her what you'd been doing all those vanished years, and have your throat close like a fist?"

Marisol had gone very still. "That's monstrous."

"That's *binding*," Odile corrected. "There is no such thing as a free oath. Every promise takes collateral. The covenant needed silence from its keepers so that no signatory could be tortured or tricked into unraveling it — sensible, from the Veil's point of view. From mine—" she touched the scratched-out mouth of the carved woman, gently, the way you'd touch a scar, "—from mine it was a hundred years of swallowing my own truth until I forgot what it tasted like."

"And when the century ended?" Oscar asked quietly.

"When the century ended, the covenant broke anyway. Corvane saw to that, or time did, or the others died or scattered or — well. Signatory Three is a story for another day, and Signatory Four is a *tree* now, if you can believe it, and I am not going to explain that before breakfast." She turned to face them, and in the pale column of light her eyes were dry and terribly steady. "The point, bookkeeper, is this. You are asking me to bind myself again. To give again. And I am telling you: I already paid. I paid a hundred years of silence for a covenant that failed regardless. Justice outside the law still demands payment, child, and some debts outlive willingness. I am *out*. I have been out since before your grandmother learned to walk."

The plum-colored flower had followed them into the chapel. It hovered near the doorway, turning slowly, like a dog that hadn't been invited in but intended to supervise.

Oscar thought about arguing. He was, by nature and profession, an arguer — a man who negotiated quantities, disputed entries, haggled with ferrymen over memories. But standing in front of the scratched-out mouth, he found he had nothing clever to offer. Only the truth, which was not a currency he spent lightly.

"Lena believed it could be reforged," he said. "She died carrying the map. Whatever it cost her — whatever it cost all of you the first time — she thought it was worth paying twice."

"Lena was young," said Odile. "Lena was *brave*, which is worse. Brave people always believe the bill will land on someone else's table." Something moved behind her steadiness then, brief as a fish under ice. "I liked her letters. She wrote like someone who expected to be argued with. I haven't enjoyed being wrong about anything that much in decades."

"Then help us another way," Marisol said. She stepped forward, and Oscar heard the courier in her voice — the flat, practical tone of a woman negotiating terms. "You won't sign. Fine. But you were there. You know things the stone won't say and the map doesn't show. Sell us that instead. Name your price."

Odile studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled — a real smile, crooked and tired and briefly young.

"Oh, I like you. You haggle. The last courier who came sniffing around here just groveled." She walked to the frieze's far end, where the seventh figure stood apart from the others, its arms raised, its face smooth and unfinished. "Here's what I'll sell you, and cheap, because Lena paid for it already with her veins. Listen carefully, because I will only say this once, and then we will never speak of the covenant again."

* * *

What she told them took less than an hour and rearranged everything Oscar thought he understood.

"The covenant doesn't need seven signatures," Odile said, pacing between the columns of light with her hands folded behind her back, a lecturer's habit worn into her bones. "It needs *one name given wholly* — but a name alone isn't enough. A name is a key. It must be turned in a lock, and the lock is the Well, and the Well requires... witnesses. Anchors. The old forge used six seals plus the Warden's own name. Six hands on the wheel, so to speak, so that no single keeper held the whole weight."

"But the others are gone," Oscar said. "Dead, transformed, scattered."

"Gone, yes. Their *seals* are not. A seal outlives its signer — that's the entire cruelty of the design. Mine is in my keeping. The stone holds his own, naturally; he's smug about it. The fourth's is buried in her roots somewhere in the Faded, and good luck negotiating with an oak. The fifth's was lost at the Toll Bridge forty years ago, which is a tragedy I'm not paid enough grief to discuss. The sixth—" she paused, and for the first time her composure flickered, "—the sixth seal belongs to a man who sold it. Knowingly. To Corvane. Which means the master of your guild is sitting on two pieces of the lock already, and if he learns what they're for before you gather the rest—"

"He becomes the sole gatekeeper," Oscar finished. The ledger-mind did the arithmetic instantly, coldly: two seals in hostile hands, one lost, one in roots, one in a chapel, one on a child not yet found. The numbers didn't add up. The numbers never added up anymore, and he missed the days when they had.

"So you see," Odile said, "your task is not to find six willing souls. Your task is to find six *seals* and one fool willing to pay the final price. The seals can be carried by anyone. They'll burn themselves into whoever holds them at the Well." Her gaze settled on Oscar, and it was not unkind, exactly, but it weighed him like a scale weighs coin. "And the name. The name must be given freely, wholly, and never returned. Do you understand what 'never returned' means, taster? Not hidden. Not sleeping. *Spent*. The Well drinks it, and the drinker forgets the taste, and the world forgets the man, and the Veil remembers instead. Forever."

The chapel was very quiet. Somewhere above, a bird crossed the open roof and was gone.

"Cheerful place you keep," Marisol muttered.

"I keep a *honest* place," Odile said. "There's a difference, and it smells like nightshade." She crossed to a loose flagstone near the altar, pried it up with a foot that knew the move by heart, and drew out a small object wrapped in oilcloth. She unwrapped it with the care of a surgeon and a thief simultaneously.

The seal was a disc of gray metal, no larger than a coin, stamped with the figure of a woman holding a cup. It should have looked ordinary. It did not. Looking at it felt like looking at a lit window in a dark house — warmth, and the certainty of someone home.

"This is mine," Odile said. "A hundred and twelve years of silence, pressed into an ounce of metal. Take it."

Oscar reached for it and stopped. "Just like that? After everything you said?"

"After everything I said, precisely *because* of everything I said." She pressed it into his palm and closed his fingers over it, and her small soft-not-soft hands were stronger than they had any right to be. "I told you the covenant failed because its signatories were human — proud, frightened, and tired. I refuse to be the reason it fails twice. I will not sign. I will not speak its tongue again, I will not walk to any Well, and if either of you ever asks me a third time I will feed you to the wisteria, which is not decorative. But the seal goes with you, because a debt unpaid is a debt that collects interest, and I have paid interest on this particular principal for a century." Her voice roughened, just barely, at the edges. "Lena wrote me eleven letters. I answered none of them. Consider this my reply."

Oscar looked down at the seal in his hand. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything in this impossible business was warm, as if impossibility ran a few degrees above blood.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me. Thanking implies I did you a kindness. I balanced a ledger." She almost smiled. "You'd know the difference better than most, I suspect."

* * *

They left the conservatory as the morning fog burned off, the seal wrapped in oilcloth against Oscar's chest, heavy as a heartbeat that wasn't his.

Marisol didn't speak until they were three streets gone, weaving through the fish-market crowds where no one listened and everyone smelled the same. Then: "She lied about one thing."

"She lied about several things," Oscar said. "The wisteria is absolutely decorative. I checked."

"Not that." Marisol kept her eyes forward, her courier's stride never breaking. "When she talked about the fifth seal, the one lost at the Toll Bridge. She flinched. People don't flinch over strangers' tragedies. That bridge meant something to her. Maybe the fifth signatory was someone she—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "Doesn't matter. What matters is the Toll Bridge. If the seal's really still there, after forty years—"

"It'll be guarded, or gone, or both," Oscar said. "Nothing in this business is ever simply lying where it fell."

"No. But bridges have tolls, and tolls have keepers, and keepers can be bribed." Marisol finally glanced at him, and there was something new in her expression — the look of a professional who has just spotted a route on a map she'd assumed was closed. "Which reminds me. There's someone I need to see. Someone who knows every crossing on the river better than the river does."

Oscar waited. He had learned, in these few strange days, that Marisol revealed information the way Odile dispensed tea: in doses, with something bitter hidden at the bottom.

"My brother," she said at last, and her voice did something he had never heard it do — it went careful, the way voices go around broken glass. "Tomas. He worked guild runs, same as me, until eighteen months ago. Then a batch of bottled years came through corrupted — cut too deep from the Veil, the way Corvane's cutters do it now — and Tomas handled the crate." She was walking faster now, not looking at Oscar at all. "The batch didn't kill him. It did something worse. It *unmade* him. Not his body — his place in the world. His name off every record. His face out of every memory that wasn't mine. Our mother looks straight at him and sees a lodger. The guild rolls show his room as empty. He walks through the city like a draft through a door, and the only person alive who remembers he exists is standing right in front of you getting angrier by the step."

Oscar's hand rose unconsciously to the seal against his chest. *Unremembered.* He thought of the guild ledgers, the neat columns, the entries he had recorded without question — *one jar courage, one stolen Tuesday, quantity: Veil-thread, purpose unlisted* — and felt the floor of his tidy world tilt another degree.

"Where is he?" he asked.

"A workhouse by the tanneries. Saint Verity's. He's been there eighteen months, and nobody knows his name, because he hasn't got one anymore." Marisol stopped walking. She turned to face Oscar fully, and her eyes were bright and hard and furious, and underneath the fury, for one unguarded instant, heartbroken. "You want to know what Corvane's trade does to people, bookkeeper? Don't read it in a ledger. Come meet my brother. He'll serve you dinner and not know why you're crying."

The fog had lifted entirely now, and the city lay open before them, ordinary and sunlit and full of doors. Somewhere in it, a man without a name was waiting to be remembered.

Oscar adjusted the strap of his satchel and followed her toward Saint Verity's.
