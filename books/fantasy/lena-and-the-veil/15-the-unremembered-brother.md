## 15. The Unremembered Brother

Marisol had carried a great many things in her years as a courier. Sealed letters that whispered when you shook them. A jar of someone's courage—she'd checked the seal twice on that one, because courage sloshing about in a satchel seemed like a bad idea. Once, memorably, a live rooster named General who had opinions about the route and expressed them for six miles.

But she had never carried a brother.

Not in a satchel, anyway. In her head. In the one place no one could audit.

Tomas was two years younger than she was, which meant he'd been fourteen the year he vanished, and she had been sixteen and furious at him for something so trivial now—a borrowed knife, a chipped handle, an apology never offered—that she couldn't even remember what it was. That was the cruelty of it. She remembered everything about him except the last thing she'd said to him. The ledger of her guilt had one entry with the amount smudged beyond reading.

He'd gone out for bread. He'd come back wrong—or rather, he hadn't come back at all, and then, three days later, he *had* come back, walking through the door like nothing happened, and their mother had looked at him and screamed.

Not because he was hurt. Because she didn't know him.

"Who is this boy?" she'd said, and Marisol, standing in the doorway of their little house on Candle Lane, had watched her mother look at her own son the way you look at a stranger who has knocked at the wrong door. And Marisol had known—known the way couriers know things, in the gut, before the mind catches up—that this was not madness. This was a delivery gone wrong. Someone had taken something out of the world, and the world had closed over the hole like water over a dropped stone, and only Marisol still held the shape of it.

Their mother had died eight years later without ever once saying Tomas's name. Not from malice. From impossibility. You cannot miss what you cannot remember.

Marisol had never forgiven the world for that. Now she knew who to blame instead, and blame, properly directed, is a fuel.

* * *

The workhouse of Saint Ferro's stood on the east bank of the Mire, where the city kept the things it didn't want to look at: the tanneries, the plague pits, the poor. It was a long grey building with windows like closed eyes, and the smell arrived a street early—lye and boiled cabbage and something underneath both, something institutional, the smell of a place that processes people.

"You're sure it's this one?" Oscar asked. He had his hood up and his shoulders drawn in, doing his best impression of a man who is nobody, which was impressive, because Oscar was very good at being nobody. It was practically a profession for him.

"The record said Saint Ferro's." Marisol patted the folded paper in her coat—the poisoner's parting gift, a list of names and places extracted from the old covenant's margins. "The record also said the man listed there doesn't exist, which I'm choosing to interpret as encouraging."

"That's very optimistic."

"I contain multitudes, bookkeeper. Most of them annoyed."

They signed the visitor book at the gate. Marisol wrote *M. Reyes, sister of a resident*, and the gatekeeper—a soft-bodied man with kind eyes and the defeated posture of someone who had once wanted to be a fisherman—read the line twice.

"Sister of which resident?" he asked. "We've got forty-one men."

"Tomas Reyes."

The gatekeeper frowned. He went down his list with one finger, slowly, the way a man reads when he already knows the answer will be no. "No Reyes," he said at last, almost apologetically. "There's a Rivas. Tomás Rivas?"

Marisol felt her heart do something complicated. "That's him," she said. "That's my brother."

"Well." The gatekeeper smiled, relieved to have been helpful. "Funny thing, names. We get them wrong all the time. Half our records are a mess, if I'm honest. Ink's expensive; memory's free."

*Memory's free.* Marisol wanted to laugh and throw up in roughly equal measure. She managed neither, which left her with a facial expression the gatekeeper politely ignored.

Oscar, meanwhile, had gone quiet in the particular way he went quiet when numbers were misbehaving. Later, walking across the yard, he murmured, "Rivas isn't a clerical error. Rivas is *Reyes* with the meaning scooped out. Someone edited him, Mari. Not the record. *Him.*"

"I know what someone did to him," Marisol said. "I've had eleven years to know it. What I don't know is whether there's anything left to find."

* * *

They found him in the weaving shed, at the third loom from the window, because of course he was at the third loom from the window. Tomas had always liked thirds. Third slice, third turn on the rope swing, third row back at the puppet theater. Some habits were apparently load-bearing enough to survive being erased.

He was thirty now. He should have been a carpenter with a wife and a crooked smile and sawdust permanently in his hair. Instead he was thin and grey-faced, with the loom-calloused hands of a man who had been making cloth for other people for a decade, and when Marisol walked toward him he looked straight through her.

She had prepared for that. She'd told herself on the walk over: he won't know me. Don't let it break you in front of strangers. Save the breaking for later, somewhere private, somewhere with a lockable door.

Preparation, as it turned out, was useless. Nothing prepares you for your own brother looking at you the way a man looks at weather.

"Hello," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I'm Marisol."

He blinked. "Should I know you?" he asked—not rudely, but with the gentle bewilderment of a man being handed a package with no address. "You seem familiar. Like a word I used to know and lost."

And then—and this was the moment, this was the moment Marisol would think about for the rest of her life—he tilted his head slightly, the way he used to tilt his head when he was trying to remember where he'd left something, and he said:

"You smell like the blue door."

* * *

Their childhood house on Candle Lane had a blue door. Not a nice blue—an aggressive, peeling blue, the blue of a family that had bought paint on sale and regretted nothing. Every courier in the district knew the house by that door. And every person who had ever lived behind it carried the smell of it somewhere deep: woodsmoke and linseed oil and the rosemary bush that grew against the wall and refused, with botanical stubbornness, to die.

Tomas didn't remember her face. He didn't remember her name, or their mother, or the knife he'd borrowed, or the apology. But some animal stratum of him, beneath whatever Corvane's people had scraped away, still held the smell of home, and it had attached itself to Marisol the way a drowning sailor attaches himself to driftwood.

She sat down beside the loom. She didn't explain herself. Explanations were for people who existed in his world.

"Do you dream?" she asked him.

"Sometimes," he said. "Mostly I dream about a kitchen. There's a woman burning something. Bread, I think. She swears in a way that would make a dockworker blush, and then she laughs, and everyone laughs, and I can't see anyone's faces. Just the smoke and the laughing."

Their mother burned the bread every single Sunday of their lives. It was practically liturgy. Marisol felt her throat close and managed, "What does she swear?"

"I can't—" He frowned, reaching. "It's like the words are there but they've been... rinsed. You know how a shirt fades? The shape stays but the color goes. Everything in my dreams is the shape of things with the color washed out."

Oscar made a small sound beside her. When Marisol glanced at him, he was staring at Tomas with an expression she'd learned to read over the past weeks: the look of a bookkeeper finding an entry that shouldn't exist. "The color," Oscar said softly. "The *color*. Mari, that's what Veil-thread looks like when it's cut. Strips of boundary. The Faded realm isn't grey—it's just... uninked. Things keep their outlines and lose their saturation. Whoever took your brother didn't take his memories whole. They took the *pigment* out of them and sold it."

"Sold it," Marisol repeated flatly.

"Bottled it. Somewhere in the guild cellar there is a shelf with your brother's Sundays on it. His birthdays. Probably his name, in the beginning, before they realized the name was the anchor and started cutting around it." Oscar's voice stayed level, because Oscar's voice always stayed level, but his hands had gone white on the edge of the loom's bench. "I entered those vials, Mari. Or entries in my handwriting did. I logged them as 'assorted domestic recollections, mixed lot.' I priced them."

Marisol turned that over in her chest for a long moment. She found, to her surprise, that she wasn't angry at Oscar. Anger needs a target that chose, and Oscar hadn't chosen; he'd counted. Counting was worse than nothing, maybe, but it wasn't malice. Malice had a different address.

Malice lived in a fine house and wore good boots and called himself Master.

* * *

They stayed through the afternoon. Marisol talked; Tomas wove; the shuttle clacked its patient rhythm like a metronome for a conversation only half of which was happening aloud.

She told him stories. Not the big ones—she was saving the big ones—but small ones, safe ones, the kind you tell children: the time he fell in the Mire chasing a paper boat and came up with a frog in his collar; the time he'd tried to train the neighbor's goat to fetch and the goat had trained *him* instead; the summer he'd decided he was going to be a pirate and made a flag out of their mother's good tablecloth, and the ensuing household tribunal.

He laughed at the frog story. Actually laughed, from the belly, and for three seconds he was entirely, unmistakably *there*—a boy of thirty with sawdust-colored eyes and a gap between his front teeth that she had missed so badly it was a physical ache.

Then the laugh faded, and he looked puzzled at his own laughter, the way a man puzzles at a cough in a room he thought was empty. "Why do I know that story?" he asked. "I don't know you. But I know the frog. I can *see* the frog. Green, with a yellow stripe."

"Because they couldn't cut everything," Marisol said. "Because you're more than a list of memories, Tomás. You're the shape they were poured into. They took the wine and left the bottle, and the bottle remembers."

He went back to his loom after that, quieter. But near the end, as the light went amber in the high windows, he stopped and looked at her again, and this time he didn't look through her.

"You'll come back?" he asked.

"Every week," Marisol said. "If they let me."

"They let everyone," he said, and shrugged, and there was something heartbreaking in the shrug, something that said: *they let everyone because no one comes.*

* * *

Outside the gates, in the last light, Marisol stopped walking and stood very still, and Oscar, who was learning her rhythms, stopped too and waited.

"I spent eleven years thinking he was dead," she said. "Dead was better. Dead is clean. Dead means grief, and grief ends, eventually, or at least it stops getting worse. This—" She pressed her knuckles against her sternum, hard, as if trying to hold something in place. "This is worse than death, Oscar. Corvane didn't kill my brother. He *unmade* him and left the remainder working a loom. And he did it for inventory. For stock. For a column in a ledger marked *mixed lot*."

Oscar said nothing. He was good at that—saying nothing at exactly the right volume.

"And here's the thing that's going to keep me awake tonight," Marisol went on, "which is saying something, because I sleep like a cat and wake at shadows. If they can do it to him—if they can reach into a fourteen-year-old boy and rinse him out—then nobody in this city is real to them. Nobody is a person. Everyone is stock. The king buying his bottled years isn't buying time. He's buying *people*. He's drinking somebody's mother's Sundays, and he doesn't know, and he doesn't ask, because why would you ask where the wine comes from when the merchant is reputable?"

She turned to face him fully, and the lamplight caught her eyes, and Oscar—who had faced down inquisitors and ferryman and one genuinely alarming statue—actually stepped back half a pace.

"So I need you to understand something," Marisol said, and her voice had dropped into a register he hadn't heard from her before, low and even and absolutely without humor. "I was a courier. Couriers deliver. We don't open the packages, we don't read the letters, we don't ask what's in the jars. It's a good trade for a woman with my particular talents, and I was *good* at it. I want that on the record, bookkeeper, because what I am about to become is not a courier."

"What are you about to become?"

"A problem," Marisol said. "A large, persistent, well-organized problem, with excellent contacts and nothing left to lose. Corvane has a ledger. Fine. Ledgers balance. I'm going to balance his."

* * *

They walked back through the Mire district as the lamps came on, and Marisol planned. Planning was the one thing grief had never been able to take from her; she'd planned deliveries through plagues, blockades, and one memorable flood, and she planned this the same way: routes, timings, contingencies, fallbacks.

"We go back into the guild house," she said. "You walk in the front door with your bookkeeper's face on, and I get us the true ledger."

"Mari." Oscar stopped under a lamp. "Corvane suspects me. He summoned me. He asked me questions I lied about. Walking back in is—"

"Walking back in is what a bookkeeper does," Marisol said. "Bookkeepers go where the books are. Nobody guards the books from the bookkeeper; that's like guarding the well from the water. And besides—" she allowed herself one thin, sharp smile— "you told me yourself. There are entries in your handwriting that you never wrote. Which means either Corvane's forgers are very good, or something stranger is happening, and either way I want to see the original. I want to see the master copy. I want to put my hands on the thing that lists my brother as *stock*."

Oscar was silent for a long moment. Then he said, quietly, "There's a night clerk rotation. Thursdays, the west archive runs short-staffed. I know because I built the schedule."

"Of course you did."

"I build all the schedules. It's how I stay invisible. Being indispensable to the machinery is better than any disguise." He rubbed his jaw, looking less convinced than organized. "Thursday, then. Two days. We'll need a reason for me to pull the true ledger that doesn't sound like a reason."

"Tell them you're auditing for the solstice quarter-close," Marisol said. "Nobody in history has ever questioned a man who says he's doing quarter-close. Quarter-close is the universal solvent of suspicion. People hear 'quarter-close' and their eyes glaze with gratitude that it's someone else's job."

Oscar almost smiled. It was the closest he'd come in days, and Marisol counted it a victory.

"One condition," he said. "When we find the entries—your brother's lot, the vials, all of it—we copy everything. We don't burn anything, we don't take anything, not yet. Evidence first. Justice second. I've seen what happens to people who skip steps."

"Fine," Marisol said. "Evidence first. But Oscar—when the evidence is copied, when it's safe, when it's in five hands instead of one—I'm not skipping any steps. I'm taking *every* step. Straight through the front of whatever's left of him."

Above them, far off, the evening sky over the river had a faint wrongness to it, a shimmer like heat-haze in cold air, where the Veil ran thinnest. Both of them noticed it. Neither commented. Some things you don't say aloud until you're ready to fight about them.

Two days, then. Thursday. Quarter-close.

Marisol touched the blue-door smell of her brother one last time in her memory, folded it away like a letter she intended to deliver personally, and started planning the route.
