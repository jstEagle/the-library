## 16. Infiltrating the Guild House

Quarter-close fell on Thursday, as it always did, and the Guild of Impossible Things kept its books on quarter-close the way churches kept their saints' days: with candles, with silence, and with a certain amount of quiet fraud.

Oscar knew this because he had been the one committing most of it.

He stood across the street from the guild house in the blue hour before dawn, wearing his bookkeeper's coat—the good one, the one with ink stains at the cuff arranged just so, because Corvane believed a man's ledgers should match his sleeves—and told himself, for the eleventh time since midnight, that this was simply an employee returning to work.

It was not entirely a lie. That was the trouble with Oscar's life lately. Every lie had begun arriving pre-mixed with truth, like wine cut with river water.

"You're stalling," said Marisol from the doorway beside him, where she was doing an excellent impression of a woman waiting for a fish cart. She had been doing impressions all morning: a laundress, a beggar with a cough, a widow. She was better at it than he was, and she knew it, and she was insufferable about it in the specific way of people who are right.

"I'm *observing*," Oscar said. "There's a difference. Stalling is what cowards do. Observing is what tasters do. We look at a dish for four full seconds before it goes anywhere near our mouths. Color, gloss, the behavior of the steam."

"And what does the guild house smell like this morning?"

"Wrong," Oscar admitted. "There are more guards than there should be for quarter-close, and the lamp over the side door has been changed to a red glass one. Red means 'senior business only.' I invented that convention myself, six years ago, after the incident with the bottled Tuesday."

"The stolen Tuesday?"

"*Stolen* Tuesdays are legitimate inventory. The *bottled* ones were a scandal." He sighed. "The point is, I built half the security in that building. Which means I know exactly how insulting it will be when it fails to stop me."

Marisol looked at him for a moment with something that wasn't quite a smile but lived next door to one. "You know," she said, "for a man who's about to burgle his own workplace, you sound almost cheerful."

"It's the coat." He brushed a fleck of lint from the sleeve. "Good tailoring is armor. Ask any priest."

* * *

The front door of the guild house opened to him without a whisper of protest, which was somehow worse than if it had screamed.

That was the thing about being trusted: nobody checked your face. The doorman—a new one, young, with the anxious hands of a man three weeks into a job—glanced at the coat, at the ledger-case under Oscar's arm, at the ink-stained cuffs, and stepped aside with a bow. Oscar returned the bow at precisely the correct depth for a senior clerk greeting a junior porter, a calculation he performed automatically, the way he once calculated whether a sauce had been thickened with flour or with something less survivable.

*Four seconds,* he reminded himself, crossing the entrance hall. *Color, gloss, steam.*

The hall smelled of beeswax and candle-tallow and, underneath, faintly, of cold iron. The iron was new. Iron was what you brought in when you expected trouble, or when you were expecting to make some.

He walked the corridor toward the counting room with the unhurried gait of a man who had walked it nine hundred times, nodding to two clerks who nodded back, and only when he was safely past did he let himself think about what he was actually here to do.

Corvane's true ledger. Not the pretty one—the public ledger, the one with its tidy columns and its honest-looking sums that Oscar himself had helped keep beautiful—but the real one, the one that would show where the Veil-thread came from and where the years went and whose names got subtracted along the way. Marisol needed it. The stone signatory needed it. Somewhere ahead of them, a girl with a birthmark and a bedtime-story faith in the world needed it, though she didn't know it yet.

And Oscar—Oscar needed it because he had spent his whole life believing that numbers didn't lie, and lately the numbers had started lying to him so fluently that he wanted to catch them at it, hold the page up to the light, find the watermark of the falsehood.

The counting room was empty at this hour, as he'd known it would be. Quarter-close meant every clerk was upstairs in the scriptorium, copying fair entries by candlelight and complaining about their eyes. Oscar let himself in, closed the door, and stood for a moment in the familiar dark, breathing the familiar smell of paper and sealing wax and, beneath it all, the cellar-damp that never quite left the place.

Then he went to work.

Corvane's private cabinet sat behind his own desk, locked with a lock Oscar had picked twice before—once out of duty, once out of curiosity, and both times feeling about equally guilty. It surrendered in forty seconds. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the true ledger: black binding, no title, pages soft as cloth from handling.

He carried it to the copying desk, spread his own blank book beside it, dipped his pen, and began.

For an hour, the world narrowed to ink. This was the part of the work he loved, and he had never pretended otherwise even to himself: the small holy order of it, one figure becoming another figure faithfully, the whole sprawling mess of human greed compressed into columns that finally, *finally*, made sense. Entry by entry, the shape of Corvane's trade assembled itself in Oscar's careful hand.

*Bottled years, 214 vials, delivered to the Crown, spring quarter.*

*Veil-thread, 60 lengths, purchased — source unlisted.*

*Passage-fees, the Near Faded market, paid to ferryman Silt (see annex).*

*Compensation, family of [name struck through], rendered unremembered, batch error.*

Oscar's pen stopped over that last line. He read it again. Read it a third time. Thought of Marisol's brother, who smelled of blue doors, and made himself copy it out exactly, including the strike-through, because a record was a record and mercy could come later, in the form of justice.

He was halfway down the page when he saw the handwriting.

At first he thought it was a trick of the candle. Then he thought it was a trick of Corvane's—a clerk trained to imitate hands, guilds did keep such men. But the longer he looked, the colder the room became, because the hand on the page was not similar to his handwriting.

It *was* his handwriting. The little forward lean of it. The way his sevens crossed low instead of high. The tiny hesitation-mark he made before a sum he wasn't sure of, a habit so private he'd never noticed it until a tutor had pointed it out when he was fourteen, and which he'd never managed to kill.

There were entries in that hand going back *years*. Dated entries. Entries describing deliveries Oscar had never witnessed, sums Oscar had never counted, approvals signed with a flourish he used only when he was tired and pleased with himself.

*Received, 40 lengths Veil-thread, condition good. O.S.*

*Approved transfer, 12 vials, to His Majesty's private account. O.S.*

*Balance verified. All correct. O.S.*

All correct. In his own hand. Over and over, year after year, like a prayer he'd never prayed.

Oscar set down the pen very carefully, the way you set down a cup when you've begun to suspect the poison is already inside you.

"That's not possible," he said aloud, quietly, to the empty room. The room declined to argue, which he found unfriendly of it.

Because here was the thing—the thing that made his scalp prickle in a way no poison ever had. He *knew* his own hand the way he knew his own tongue. A taster learns to distinguish a thousand flavors; a bookkeeper learns to distinguish a thousand scripts; and Oscar had been both long enough that he could spot a forgery of his own writing faster than anyone alive. He'd caught two forgers in his time. He'd been rather smug about it.

This was no forgery. Or—if it was—it was a forgery done by someone who had studied his hand with a devotion that bordered on love, who knew about the sevens, who knew about the hesitation-marks, who knew that when Oscar wrote the word *correct* he pressed hardest on the final *t* like a man driving in a nail.

Which raised a question Oscar did not want to ask, sitting alone in a dark room surrounded by evidence in his own hand:

What if it wasn't forgery at all? What if some nights, some hours, some stretches of his careful, precautioned, double-ledgered life had simply gone missing—and this was what he'd written in them?

He laughed. It came out wrong, thin as the shimmer over the river.

"No," he told the candle. "I don't sleepwalk through my sins. I itemize them."

But his hand, when he looked down at it, resting on the desk beside the open ledger, seemed briefly like a stranger's hand—an instrument that might have been played by someone else while he slept, or while he walked, or while he stood at a banquet tasting quail and feeling quietly proud of his own survival.

*Color, gloss, steam.* Four seconds. He made himself take four seconds, breathing, before he went back to copying.

* * *

He worked fast after that, and fast work was its own kind of penance, because speed was sloppiness and sloppiness was death in a place like this. He copied the current year entire. He copied the annex on Silt. He copied a page headed *Disposition of Assets, Contingency: Warden Incident*, which contained the single chilling phrase *the bearer is accounted for* in Corvane's own elegant script, dated three days ago.

Accounted for. Him. As if he were a jar on a shelf, a length of thread, a vial with a cork in it.

He was rewrapping the black ledger when he heard the footsteps.

Not the clerks—they were still upstairs; he could hear the scratch of thirty pens through the ceiling like mice in a wall. These steps came from the corridor, slow and heavy, and they stopped outside the counting-room door.

Oscar did the arithmetic of survival in under a second, which was a personal best. Window: barred. Cellar hatch behind the shelving: reachable, but loud. Door: about to open.

So he did the only thing available to a man made entirely of precautions. He sat down at the copying desk, in plain view, bent over his own honest blank book, and became the most boring object in the building.

The door opened. Corvane stood in it.

The master of the guild looked exactly as he always looked: silver-haired, silver-voiced, dressed in gray so fine it seemed embarrassed to be called a color. He held a candle, and the light moved across his face without changing anything about it, the way light refuses to improve certain stones.

"Oscar," Corvane said warmly. "Working early. I've always said you were the best of them."

"Quarter-close waits for no man, Master." Oscar did not rise. Rising was deference; deference invited conversation; conversation invited questions. He kept his eyes on his page and his pen moving. "I'll have the fair copies done by noon."

"Mm." Corvane came into the room. He did not go to his cabinet—he merely glanced at it, the way a man glances at a door he knows is shut—and then he drifted along the shelves, trailing one finger across the spines of old ledgers, reading them the way other men read poetry. "Do you know what I admire most about you, Oscar?"

"My figures, Master."

"Your figures, certainly. But no." Corvane turned, and smiled, and the smile reached all the way to his eyes, which was the most frightening thing about it. "It's your *consistency*. Most men are different people in different rooms. You—" he tapped the desk, once, near Oscar's hand, near the black ledger's oilcloth wrapping, which Oscar realized with distant horror he had not yet moved out of sight— "you are the same man in every room. The same hand. Page after page, year after year. One could set a watch by you."

"Yes, Master," said Oscar, whose heart was performing a rhythm no physician would have called healthy, and whose mouth said, smoothly, "Consistency is the whole of accounting. Anyone can be brilliant once. Being correct *twice* is the profession."

Corvane laughed—a real laugh, delighted, terrible. "I shall have that embroidered somewhere." He moved to the door, paused, and added, without turning around, in a voice as mild as milk:

"You'll lock the cabinet when you're finished, won't you? I do hate to think of things getting *out of order*."

The door closed behind him. Oscar sat perfectly still for the count of ten, then twenty, then a full minute, listening to the footsteps fade and the pens above continue their mouse-scratch, and only then did he notice that his own hand, gripping the pen, had left a small blot on the page—a hesitation-mark, deep and dark, before a sum he hadn't even written yet.

His body, apparently, had already answered the question his mind was afraid to ask.

* * *

He was three streets away, in the grey full light of morning, before he dared open his copied ledger and check the damage. Marisol found him there, sitting on a bollard by the fish gate—the same bollard, though he hadn't planned it, where a dying woman had once pressed a map into his hands—and she took one look at his face and said, "Bad?"

"Sit down," Oscar said.

She sat.

He showed her the entries. He watched her expression travel from professional interest, to alarm, to the particular stillness of a courier realizing that a package she's carrying might be ticking. When he reached the page headed *Warden Incident* and the words *the bearer is accounted for*, she swore with a fluency that he privately intended to learn from.

"He's building a case," Marisol said. "Against you. All those entries in your hand—deliveries, approvals—that's not bookkeeping, that's a gallows being assembled one neat line at a time. When the guild falls, it falls on you."

"So it would seem."

"But how?" She tapped the page, hard. "Nobody writes like that. Nobody can write like that. Forgeries crack under a jeweler's loupe, and your hand doesn't crack, because it isn't—"

"Don't say it."

"—because it isn't a forgery," she finished anyway, because couriers deliver things, even sentences you've asked them not to.

Oscar looked out at the harbor water, grey and patient, doing its impersonation of a surface over a depth. "I know," he said quietly. "I've been avoiding saying it since before dawn. But you're right, and I hate it, and we need to talk about what it means."

"Which is?"

"Either someone has studied me for years with a patience I can't imagine—" He stopped. Started again, because the second possibility was the one that had been sitting in his chest all morning like a swallowed coal. "Or those pages are true. Or partly true. Or true in some way I can't audit." He laughed his thin laugh again. "I have spent my entire life trusting the record, Marisol. Numbers, entries, balances. The record doesn't flatter, doesn't fear, doesn't lie. And now the record says I approved every theft, sealed every vial, verified every balance—all correct, year after year, in my own hand—and I am standing here telling you I remember none of it, and the horrible thing, the truly unbearable thing—"

He made himself say it.

"—is that I can't prove which of us is lying. Me or the book."

Marisol was silent for a moment. Then she took the copied ledger gently out of his hands, folded it closed, and held it against her chest like something rescued from a fire.

"Then we find out which," she said. "Tonight. There's a man who deals in memories—Silt's opposite number, buys instead of sells. If hours went missing from your life, he'll know where they ended up. Everyone's lost time washes up on somebody's shore eventually."

Oscar stared at her. "You want me to ask a memory-broker whether I've been robbing myself in my sleep."

"I want you to ask him whether someone's been *writing* you," Marisol said. "There's a difference. I think."

Above the rooftops, the morning sky over the river shimmered again, that faint heat-haze wrongness, closer than it had been two days ago. Neither of them commented. Some things you don't say aloud until you're ready to fight about them.

Oscar stood, straightened his coat, and discovered that his hands were steady—which surprised him, and then didn't, because steadiness was just another precaution, and he was, whatever else might be entered in whatever ledger, in whosever hand, still a man made entirely of those.

"All correct," he murmured, to no one, testing the weight of the words in his own mouth.

They tasted, for the first time in his life, faintly of poison.
