## 17. The Handwriting Problem

Oscar had spent eleven years learning to read handwriting the way other men read faces.

It was a bookkeeper's necessity. In a guild that traded in bottled memories and borrowed years, ink was currency, and currency attracted counterfeiters. He could tell Master Corvane's hand from his own at a glance—the master's letters leaned forward like men eavesdropping, while Oscar's stood upright, orderly, each numeral in its proper column. He could identify the courier Marisol's quick slanted script, the ferryman Silt's strange wet scrawl that always looked as though it had been written underwater and never quite dried. He kept samples of everyone's hand in a locked drawer, because a man made of precautions kept samples of everything.

So when he sat down in the safehouse above the chandler's shop with Corvane's true ledger open before him—a ledger he had copied three nights ago at terrible risk and had not yet dared to read closely—he noticed the problem within four minutes.

He would have noticed sooner. But he had spent those first minutes steadying his hands with tea, because some discoveries require ceremony.

The entry was dated two years past. *Received: one courage (jarred), quality fair, from the widow Hessel's boy. Paid: six years, small denominations.* It was an ordinary entry. An honest entry. It was written in Oscar's own hand.

Oscar had not written it.

He turned back ten pages. Another entry, his hand again. Then another. He began flipping faster, and the room seemed to tilt gently, like a boat whose mooring has slipped. Entry after entry after entry, stretching back through the years—entries in his handwriting for transactions he had never recorded, goods he had never seen, sums that had never passed through his careful columns. His upright, orderly numerals. His particular way of crossing sevens, learned from a schoolmaster in the Low Quarter who had beaten sloppiness out of him with a ruler. His private flourish on the letter *Q*, which he had invented himself at fourteen because he thought it looked distinguished.

No one else alive knew how he wrote his Qs.

Except, apparently, someone did.

* * *

Marisol found him still sitting there when she came up with bread and hard cheese an hour later. She set the food down, glanced at his face, and wisely poured herself tea before asking anything.

"The ledger," Oscar said finally. "Look at the entries from the winter before last. Tell me who wrote them."

She paged through, frowning. Her professional eye moved down the columns the way his did—hers trained by routes and seals, his by sums. "You did," she said. "Obviously."

"I didn't."

"Oscar." She said his name the way one speaks to a man who has been drinking. "I've seen your ledgers for years. That's your hand."

"That is my hand," he agreed. "Which is precisely the problem. I have never written a single one of these entries. Look at this one—*received, one Tuesday, stolen, provenance disputed*. I have never handled a stolen Tuesday in my life. I would have recorded its condition. Tuesdays arrive bruised."

Marisol stared at him. Then, slowly, she pulled her chair around to his side of the table, and the two of them bent over the book together like surgeons over a body.

It took them until well past dark to map the full shape of it, and what they found was worse than forgery. Forgery would have been simple crime. This was architecture.

The false entries didn't begin all at once. They began small, six years ago—one or two a season, tucked between genuine transactions, indistinguishable. And they told a story. A story of a bookkeeper named Oscar Sandoval who had grown greedy. Who had begun skimming. Who had sold guild inventory on his own account—Veil-thread, no less, listed under euphemisms any magistrate could decode—and pocketed the proceeds. The story was patient. It accumulated. Every few months a new chapter, each one slightly more damning than the last, building toward an ending already prepared: the final pages held drafts of confessions, signed in a practiced copy of Oscar's signature, dated three weeks into the future.

"He's built you a gallows," Marisol said quietly. "Out of your own alphabet."

"And he's been building it since before I ever saw a Veil-thread," Oscar said. "Before Lena. Before any of it." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, thin and glassy. "Six years. Six years I've been dying on paper, and I didn't feel a thing."

"Why you?" But even as she asked it, Marisol's eyes went to the map case on the shelf, and Oscar watched the same arithmetic complete itself behind her face that had completed itself behind his.

"Because I'm the bookkeeper," he said. "When the guild falls—and Corvane has known for years that eventually it would fall, that someone like Lena would come hunting—when it falls, the crown will want a criminal. Not a master. Masters are useful; masters negotiate. But a clerk—" He touched the forged confession. "A clerk hangs beautifully. All the evidence in the world, and every page of it in my hand. I am the most thoroughly documented innocent man in this city."

* * *

He should have stopped there. Despair, like poison, is best taken in small doses. But Oscar had spent his whole life believing that anything could be survived if you understood it completely, so he went back to the beginning of the false entries and read them the way he read everything: looking for the error.

There was always an error. No forgery survives a truly obsessive reader.

He found it near midnight, in an entry from five years past. *Received: one memory, bottled, contents a wedding, seller distressed but willing.* The hand was perfect. The numerals were perfect. The Q was perfect.

But the ink was wrong.

Not visibly. Not to any eye in the world but his, and only because of a habit so small he'd never told anyone about it: for eleven years, Oscar had bought his iron-gall ink from the same stationer on Cooper's Lane, and for the last four of those years the stationer had cut it with a walnut-gall blend that dried a half-shade browner. Oscar's genuine entries from that period were all brownish. The forged ones from the same period were black. Whoever had copied his hand had studied his penmanship down to the flourishes—but had bought fresh ink to do it.

"A forger works from samples," Oscar said aloud, to the candle, to Marisol, to the empty middle distance. "Samples of my writing. Which means someone collected my writing for years without my knowing. Old ledger pages. Receipts. Notes." He paused. "Or practice. Years of practice. Someone sat somewhere copying my hand over and over until their wrist learned me."

"Corvane?"

"Corvane doesn't write his own threats. He pays people to write them." Oscar rubbed his eyes. "Somewhere there is a scribe who knows my handwriting better than I do. Possibly better than I know myself."

"There's something else." Marisol had gone very still, in the way she did when she was deciding whether to say a hard thing. She decided to. "You said Lena pressed the map case into your hands. Dying. On the docks."

"Yes."

"Think about it as a delivery. You're a courier—you think in routes. If I'm carrying something vital, I don't hand it to the first warm body on a fog-bound dock. I deliver to an address. A name. A dead-drop." She tapped the map case. "Who was it meant for?"

Oscar hadn't let himself think it. Now he did, and the thought went through him like cold water through cloth.

Lena had been a Warden. Wardens planned. She had been hunted, poisoned, running on borrowed hours—and still she would have had a destination. Some signatory. Some ally of the old covenant, waiting in this city for the map that would call them to the Well. And instead she had collapsed against a royal food-taster who happened to be walking home along the fish gate at the wrong hour, and had pressed her life's work into his hands with her final breath, and died with her eyes open.

"She wasn't choosing me," Oscar said. His voice sounded far away. "I was a mistake. A misdelivery." He looked down at his hands, the hands that had held her while the silver climbed her throat. "All of this—the map, the doors, the signatories, the Well—I picked it up off a corpse because I was standing in the wrong place. And meanwhile the man who killed her has spent six years writing me into a noose, because he always intended the bookkeeper to take the fall. I was never chosen, Marisol. I was framed. By both sides. The universe and Corvane reached the same conclusion independently."

He said it lightly. He was surprised at how much it hurt.

Marisol was quiet a long moment. Then she said, in the flat practical voice she used for routing deliveries through dangerous districts: "Do you want to put it back? Find whoever she meant it for, hand it over, wash your hands?"

And Oscar, who had spent his entire life being a man made of precautions, discovered that he did not know the answer. That was the worst part—not the despair, but the discovery that beneath the despair there was something stubborn and unaccounted-for, some entry he had never recorded in any ledger, that refused to balance.

"No," he heard himself say. "I don't know why. But no."

"Good," said Marisol. "Because I checked the drop routes. There's a name in Lena's letters we haven't run down yet—someone she wrote to twice, someone inside the palace walls. And if the map was meant for them, they may be waiting still." She stood, and gathered the map case, and paused at the door. "Also, Oscar—if Corvane's scribe can write you, then from tonight on, nothing you sign is safe. We need to change your hand. Tonight. Before you touch paper again."

"My hand took twenty years to build."

"Then it'll take you all night to break," said Marisol, and put a pen in front of him, and turned the paper sideways. "Write your name left-handed. Ugly. Like a man in a hurry. From now on, ugly is the safest thing you can be."

* * *

He practiced until dawn—left-handed loops, altered numerals, sevens crossed backwards, the distinguished Q murdered and replaced with a plain crooked duck of a letter. By lamplight his new hand looked like a child's ransom note. He loved it the way one loves a disguise: for its ugliness, and for the stranger wearing it.

Near morning, too tired to keep practicing, he took out Lena's letters instead and read them again, slowly, hunting the name Marisol had mentioned. And there it was, in the second letter, half-hidden in a phrase he'd skimmed past a dozen times: *the one inside the walls who owes me a debt older than the crown — ask for the Understeward.*

Understeward. Inside the palace walls.

Oscar set the letter down. Eleven years he had walked those service corridors. Eleven years he had tasted at the king's table and memorized the faces of every servant, clerk, and functionary between the kitchens and the crown. And he realized, with a sensation like a floor giving way, that he knew exactly which door the Understeward stood behind—and that he had passed it a thousand times, and that the man behind it had once, years ago, looked at Oscar a moment too long, as if checking a delivery against a list.

As if expecting something that had not yet arrived.

Oscar blew out the lamp and sat in the grey light, listening to the chandler's shop wake below, and understood that the next door he opened might be the one Lena had died trying to reach—or the one Corvane had built to catch whoever came asking.

Either way, he was going to have to knock.
