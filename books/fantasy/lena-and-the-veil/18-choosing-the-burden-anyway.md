## 18. Choosing the Burden Anyway

They took him at the fish market, which Oscar would later consider the only dignified thing about the whole affair.

Not because there was anything dignified about being seized by three large men while haggling over mackerel—there was not—but because a fish market was the one place in Vellamare where a man screaming was merely part of the ambiance. Nobody turned. Nobody cared. A fishwife two stalls down didn't even lower her voice as she argued that six coppers was robbery, and she was right, and she got her way, and all of this happened within arm's reach of Oscar being folded into a fish cart like a misdelivered parcel.

He had time to appreciate the irony. He had, in fact, rather a lot of time, because the cart smelled like low tide's funeral and the ride lasted an hour.

*You are a man made of precautions,* his old master Tallow used to say, back when Oscar was a kitchen boy learning to hold a spoon of arsenic without flinching. *One day your precautions will be all that's left of you.* Oscar had thought it a joke then. Now, bound hand and foot in a cart full of ice-melt and herring brine, he revised his opinion: it was a prophecy, and like most prophecies, it had terrible timing.

The men who'd taken him were guild enforcers—he recognized two from the cellar levels, Brannick and the one everyone called Half-Smile for reasons Oscar had never been curious enough to investigate. They hadn't searched him thoroughly. That was precaution number one working in his favor: Oscar carried nothing on his person that mattered. The map was sewn into the lining of his spare coat, which was currently rolled up under Marisol's bed three districts away, wrapped in oilcloth, guarded by a woman who had made him swear on her ledger—which she assured him was a more binding oath than any god—that he would never again go anywhere alone.

He had gone somewhere alone. He could already hear the lecture.

But the truth was simpler and more humiliating than recklessness: he had gone to buy mackerel because Marisol liked mackerel, and because after two weeks of running, hiding, forged papers, and sleeping in shifts, he had wanted—just once—to do something ordinary. Something a normal man did. Buy dinner. Argue about coppers. Walk home through streets that were only streets.

The city had other plans.

* * *

They put him in a room that was trying very hard to be comfortable, which told Oscar everything about what was coming.

It was above a counting house—the guild kept several such fronts—and someone had arranged it with real care: a fire lit, a decanter of wine breathing on the sideboard, a chair positioned by the hearth at the exact angle of a man who is welcome. There was even a plate of small cakes. Oscar looked at the cakes the way a jeweler looks at paste.

"Poisoned?" asked Half-Smile, following his gaze. He sounded genuinely amused.

"Almost certainly not," Oscar said. "That's the point, isn't it. You want me relaxed."

Brannick grunted, which Oscar had learned was his entire emotional range.

They left him alone for twenty minutes. This too was technique—let the prisoner sit with the fire and the wine and the soft chair until the silence does the work that thumbscrews can't. Oscar used the twenty minutes productively. He examined the wine (unopened seal, but the cork had been drawn and replaced; a faint ring of tannin dust at the neck told him it had been standing open sometime in the last day). He examined the cakes (almond paste, honey, and underneath both, so faint most noses would miss it entirely, the green ghost of something bitter—foxglove, if he had to guess, or its uglier cousin). He examined the room itself and found what he expected to find: one door, one window with a view of a sheer drop, and a servant's hatch behind the sideboard, latched from the outside, leading down to the kitchens.

Every building in Vellamare had a servants' vein. Every building pretended otherwise.

When the door finally opened, it was not Corvane who came through it, and Oscar felt something complicated loosen in his chest—not relief exactly, more like the disappointment you feel when the principal sends a deputy. It meant he wasn't worth the master's personal attention yet. It also meant the offer coming would be official, rehearsed, and deniable.

The man wore merchant gray and a smile calibrated to the half-penny. "Master Sandoval. Thank you for coming."

"I wasn't aware I had a choice."

"There's always a choice." The man sat, poured nothing, touched nothing—a professional. "My employer is a reasonable man. I'm authorized to make you a remarkable one."

"An offer."

"Amnesty." The word landed softly, like a coin on velvet. "Full and written. Whatever the crown finds when this business concludes—and things will conclude, Master Sandoval, they always do—you will be named nowhere. Not in the ledgers, not in the trials, not in the gossip. A clean page."

"And in exchange?"

"The map. And whatever Lena Vess gave you besides. Papers, seals, names. Everything."

Oscar looked at the fire. It was a good fire; someone had built it properly, small logs crossed over kindling, patient heat. He found himself thinking, absurdly, about the first ledger he'd ever kept—age eleven, a stolen slate, recording which of the cook's debts had been paid and which were theater. Even then, he had understood the sacred thing about a ledger: it did not care who you were. It cared only what was true. A ledger was the one place in the world where a kitchen boy and a king weighed the same.

"You're offering me a clean page," Oscar said slowly.

"I'm offering you a clean book."

"No." He shook his head, working it through aloud, because saying things aloud was how he checked their weight. "A clean page is blank. What you're offering is a page with my name struck out. Those aren't the same thing. One means I did nothing wrong. The other means I was never here at all."

The smile held. Barely. "I confess I don't see the distinction matters."

"It's the only distinction that has ever mattered to me." Oscar stood, joints complaining, and walked to the window. Below, the drop. Behind, the hatch. To his left, the poisoned cakes, which he now understood were not for him at all—they were evidence. *The taster took refreshment during negotiations and died of natural causes, tragically, before signing anything.* Or worse: they were for whoever came next. He filed it away. Precaution number forty-something: always ask who the trap is actually set for.

"Let me tell you what my books say," he continued, still facing the glass. "Because unlike yours, mine balance. Entry one: I have spent nineteen years tasting poison for a king who doesn't know my birthday. Entry two: I have spent those same nineteen years keeping accounts for a guild that sells people their own lives back at interest. Neither entry is noble. I've never pretended otherwise. But they're *true*, and they're *mine*, and every line in them was written by my hand."

"The map isn't yours," the man said gently. "It belonged to a dead woman."

"Yes," Oscar said. "And that's precisely why I can't sell it."

Silence, except the fire.

"You understand," the man said at last, and the warmth drained out of his voice like water from a pulled plug, "that refusal has a price. My employer has been very patient. But there are documents, Master Sandoval. Years of documents. In your handwriting."

"I know," Oscar said. "I've read them. Some of them are quite good. The 'g' needs work."

Something flickered across the man's face—the brief, honest confusion of a professional encountering an amateur who refuses to be afraid in the correct order. "Then you understand your position."

"I understand that a framed man has exactly one advantage over a guilty one." Oscar turned around. "A guilty man negotiates. A framed man has nothing left to lose, which makes him terribly expensive. Do give Corvane my regards. Tell him the ledger of my life has been running a deficit since the night a stranger died in my arms on the Fish Gate stairs, and I've finally worked out how it balances."

"How?"

"Only one way," Oscar said. "And it isn't this."

* * *

They did not kill him immediately, which confirmed his read: the map mattered more than his corpse, and a live prisoner could be revisited. They bound him again—properly this time, wrists to ankles, a craftsman's job—and left him in the counting house's cellar among the barrels, with Brannick on a stool by the door and instructions to wait for morning.

Oscar lay on cold stone and took inventory.

Body: bruised, one rib singing, hands numb. Assets: a coat lining containing nothing (he'd moved the map days ago; let them search), a servant's knowledge of this building's bones acquired in four minutes of listening to footsteps overhead, and—precaution number twelve, sewn into his left boot heel where no sane searcher looks—a wax stopper and a vial of *marrowstill*, three drops of which slows a human heart to the pace of a dying clock.

Tallow had taught him that one too. *Every poison is a tool wearing a mask,* the old man said. *Arsenic preserves. Nightshade opens pupils. Foxglove steadies or stops a heart depending on the dose and the prayer you say over it.* Oscar had spent nineteen years learning what each poison wanted to be. Marrowstill wanted to be death. It could be persuaded to be something else.

The problem was dosage, and the problem after that was audience.

Three drops stopped a heart for good. Two drops slowed it to a murmur for perhaps an hour—one hour of being, to any examination, dead. One drop produced a convincing swoon and a pulse like a moth in a jar. He had two drops' worth in the vial. He had one chance.

And he had Brannick, who was not stupid, who had seen Oscar survive things that killed better men, and who might therefore be suspicious of a conveniently timed demise. Suspicion was manageable. Certainty was not. So Oscar needed Brannick to *want* him dead—or at least to want the paperwork that came with dead.

He began, quietly, to moan.

At first it was just sound. Then it found words. "Water," he rasped. "Please. Water." An hour later: shivering, though the cellar wasn't cold enough to justify it. Another hour: the shivering stopped, which was worse, and he let his breathing go shallow and rattling in the specific register of the foxglove decline he had watched take two courtiers and a cardinal. He knew the choreography of poisoning better than any actor alive. He had spent his whole life on the other side of the spoon, taking notes.

Near midnight, Brannick came over, crouched, and studied him with the flat patience of a man who had watched many people die and been paid for most of them.

"Marrowstill?" Brannick said.

Oscar, who was genuinely shivering now because the floor was genuinely freezing, almost laughed. He kept his eyes fluttering instead and whispered, "The cakes. The almond ones. I ate one. I was hungry." Which was a lie, but a beautiful one, because it implicated the negotiation room, the offer, the whole apparatus—*Corvane poisons his own guests*—and gave Brannick a problem that dead-Oscar solved neatly: no witness, no awkward morning conversation, a body to report and be rid of.

Brannick stared at him a long moment. Then he did the thing Oscar had counted on: he pressed two fingers to Oscar's throat, found a pulse—fast, thready, wrong, because Oscar had spent ten minutes hyperventilating into his shoulder to manufacture it—and frowned.

"He'll want proof," Brannick muttered, mostly to himself. "Dead's easier to prove than alive."

"Water," Oscar whispered, and closed his eyes, and let the trembling do the rest.

Brannick brought water. Oscar let most of it spill, drank a little, convulsed once—small, tasteful, technically accurate—and slid toward stillness by degrees, the way the truly poisoned do: not a performance of death but a slow surrender of argument. His heart was hammering. He hated this part. He hated every part. But beneath the fear ran something new and steady, a line of ink straight through the middle of him:

*I refused.*

They had offered him a clean page and he had said no, and it hadn't been courage—it had been arithmetic. Nineteen years of entries, and every one of them pointed the same direction: he was a man who kept true accounts. The moment he sold the map, the books stopped balancing forever. Corvane had built a frame around him, entry by forged entry, and there was exactly one answer available to a bookkeeper so accused: write the true record, whatever it costs, and sign it.

Lena had died pressing the covenant into his hands. Her eyes had stayed open, accusing him, and he had carried that accusation for weeks like a stone in his boot. But tonight, saying *no* in a warm room full of poisoned cake, the stone had shifted. It wasn't an accusation anymore. It was an assignment.

His fingers found his boot heel. The wax gave silently. Three drops would kill. Two would fool a physician. He had one shot at a dose between, and no second vial, and a heartbeat he needed to lie about convincingly for one hour.

*Marrowstill wants to be death,* Tallow said in his memory. *Persuade it otherwise.*

Oscar tipped the vial against his tongue and swallowed, and waited for the world to slow.

* * *

It slowed.

Oh, it slowed. The cellar went distant and blue, sounds arriving late, like letters from abroad. His heart drew out its beats the way a miser draws out coins—one, then a long dark pause, then another, each one smaller than the last. Cold climbed his arms. Somewhere far away, Brannick's stool creaked.

*Two drops,* Oscar reminded himself, drifting. *I took two drops. I am dead for one hour. I am a line item. Deceased, one bookkeeper, cause: almond cake. Write me down and look away.*

Footsteps. Fingers at his throat, pressing hard, patient, thorough. A long wait. Oscar hung in the blue distance and did the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than refusing Corvane, harder than lying to a master's face: he did nothing at all. He let his heart whisper instead of speak. He let his breath go down to the silt at the bottom of him and stay there.

"Huh," said Brannick, in the tone of a man crossing out an entry.

They carried him up—two of them now, Half-Smile summoned from wherever he'd been drinking—and dumped him in the alley behind the counting house among the empty barrels, because a dead taster was the watch's problem, not theirs, and the watch wouldn't come till dawn. Oscar heard the door bar fall. Heard their boots fade. Heard, distantly, a cat knock something over with the total commitment of cats everywhere.

Then there was only the alley, the cold, and the hour.

He lay in the dark and let the marrowstill run its course, and because there was nothing else to do, he thought about his mother.

He couldn't picture her face anymore—he'd paid that to Silt at the ferry, and the receipt was folded in his coat like a splinter of ice—but he could remember *that* he had loved her, the way you can remember a room after the furniture's gone. He wondered if she'd have approved of tonight. He suspected she'd have said something practical, like *you should have eaten before letting them arrest you*, and then, quietly, in the way of mothers, something that undid him entirely.

The hour passed. His heart remembered its arguments. Sensation returned in stages, pins and needles first, then the rib, then the cold, then an appetite so violent it was nearly funny—he had played dead through supper and his body intended to file a complaint.

Oscar sat up in the alley behind the counting house, a dead man resurrected by his own handwriting, and laughed until the rib made him stop.

Then he got to work, because a dead man has advantages and he intended to spend every one. The counting house's kitchen hatch was latched from outside with a cheap padlock, and Oscar had picked locks since before he could write cursive. Inside, past the sleeping scullery boy (he left a copper on the flour bin, habit and apology both), through the corridor that smelled of yesterday's onions, down the stair to the record room where the guild kept its copies—

Where he found the door already open, and a lantern already burning, and Marisol sitting at the master desk with Corvane's true ledger spread before her and ink on her fingers, looking up at him with an expression he would remember for the rest of his life.

"Took you long enough," she said. "Also, you're supposed to be dead. Also—" she turned the ledger toward him, tapped one line with a grimy finger, "—you need to see this. There's an entry here dated tomorrow."
