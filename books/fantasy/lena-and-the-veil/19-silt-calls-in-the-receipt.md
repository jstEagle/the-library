## 19. Silt Calls in the Receipt

The receipt was exactly where Oscar had kept it: folded twice, tucked into the inner pocket of his coat, pressed against the ledger of his own ribs. He had carried it for six days the way a man carries a debt he is trying very hard not to think about — which is to say, constantly.

*One memory, received in good condition,* it read, in ink that shifted color depending on how honestly you looked at it. *Passage rendered. Balance due upon demand.*

Silt had appeared at dusk on the third day after the ambush at the toll bridge, standing at the edge of their campfire's light like a man who had been invited and knew perfectly well he hadn't been. He hadn't spoken then. He had simply stood there, tall and damp, his boat resting on dry land behind him as though water were a courtesy rather than a requirement, and watched them until Marisol threw a stone at him. The stone passed through him with a sound like a bell dropped in mud, and by the time they'd stopped shouting about it, he was gone.

Now, on the sixth day, he was back. And this time he had brought paperwork.

"Master Sandoval." Silt's voice was the sound a river makes when it decides, politely, to go around you instead of through you. "I find myself holding an outstanding balance."

They were sheltering in the hollow beneath a fallen bridge — an actual bridge this time, mortal-built, its stones furred with moss, spanning a stream that ran, reassuringly, downhill. The child signatory, Wren, slept against Marisol's side, her birthmark-key hidden under a scarf. The poisoner's seal was gone, lost on the bridge along with most of Oscar's confidence, but the girl remained, and the girl was the point now. Everything was the point now. Nine days until solstice, the stone signatory had calculated, and every one of those days seemed to be spending itself faster than the last.

Oscar rose slowly, keeping his hands visible. A taster learned early that the dangerous things were rarely the ones that lunged; they were the ones that waited to be served.

"Master Silt," he said. "You've come a long way."

"Rivers go everywhere," said Silt. "It is rivers that make distance possible. Also," he added, with the mild satisfaction of a creditor producing a document, "it is rivers that keep accounts."

He held out the receipt. Not Oscar's copy — the original, the twin of it, written on something that was not quite paper and not quite skin. In the firelight the ink moved like minnows.

Oscar took it. He read it. He was aware, distantly, of Marisol waking behind him, her hand finding the knife she'd taken off a dead guild enforcer, and of Wren stirring, and of all of it receding to the edges of his attention the way the room used to recede when the king lifted a goblet and every eye turned to the small man whose tongue decided whether the king lived.

"You want payment," Oscar said.

"I want what I am owed," said Silt. "There is a difference. Payment is what you give me. What I am owed is what the river says."

"And what does the river say?"

Silt smiled. It was not an unkind smile. That was somehow worse. Unkind smiles you could brace against; kind ones got under your ribs the way cold water does.

"The river says you crossed my water without paying full fare. You gave me your mother's face — a fine coin, I'll grant, better than most, and I took it gladly. But passage to the Deep Faded costs more than a face. You knew that. You haggled anyway, because you are a bookkeeper, and bookkeepers always believe there is a column they haven't checked." He tilted his head. "There wasn't. There was only interest."

"How much interest?" Oscar asked, and hated how steady his voice came out. Steadiness was a habit, like immunity. You built it one poison at a time until even terror barely registered — but it registered. It always registered. It just didn't show.

"One memory," said Silt. "Of comparable weight."

"Comparable to a face?"

"Comparable to why you're doing all this." Silt's eyes were the gray-green of water over stones. "That is the fare the river names. Not the map. Not the mission. The *why*. Give me the reason you walk toward the Well of Names, and we are square, and I will never trouble you again."

Behind him, Marisol said, very quietly, "Don't."

Oscar didn't turn around. Turning around would have meant letting Silt see his face do whatever it was about to do.

Because here was the arithmetic, and Oscar could no more stop doing arithmetic than he could stop breathing: the *why* was everything. Take away a man's reason and you did not need to kill him; he would simply lie down somewhere convenient and wait. Corvane wanted the map. The crown wanted Oscar's head. But Silt — patient, polite, fair in the way floods are fair — wanted the one thing that made all the rest worth carrying.

And the truly elegant cruelty of it, the part Oscar admired even as it closed around his throat like a wet hand, was that Silt wasn't cheating. The receipt said what it said. Oscar had signed it. He had stood on that black boat with his mother's face already dissolving in his chest and he had signed, because the alternative was drowning, and drowning men are famously generous signatories.

"You know," Oscar said, "in the guild, we have a rule about debts."

"Do you?"

"Three rules, actually. One: never borrow what you can't repay. Two: never lend what you can't afford to lose. Three—" he folded the receipt along its old creases, carefully, the way he handled anything valuable, "—never let the creditor set the terms of settlement twice."

Something flickered across Silt's face. Interest, perhaps. Or amusement. With river-folk it was hard to tell tide from current.

"Go on," said Silt.

* * *

Here is what Oscar had learned in twenty years of tasting other people's food: everything poisonous has a shape. Arsenic was sweet-then-bitter, like flattery. Wolfsbane numbed the tongue before it stopped the heart, so the last thing a poisoned man felt was nothing at all. And the deadliest dishes were never the ones that tasted wrong — they were the ones that tasted *perfect*, because perfection was a disguise, and nature was too lazy to bother disguising anything.

A bargain that felt like drowning was honest. A bargain that felt like relief was the one that killed you.

So when Silt said *give me your why*, and Oscar felt the terrible seductive pull of it — because some traitorous corner of him *wanted* to put the weight down, wanted to hand over the grief and the guilt and the image of Lena's open accusing eyes and walk light for the first time since the fish gate — Oscar recognized the flavor. Sweet all the way through. Too sweet.

"No," he said.

Marisol made a small sound. Wren, awake now and pretending not to be, had gone very still under her scarf.

"No?" said Silt.

"No. I won't give you that memory. But I'll pay the debt." Oscar held up the receipt between two fingers. "This says one memory of comparable weight. It doesn't say *past*. It doesn't say *lived*. Read your own instrument, Master Silt — you wrote it, and I've spent my life reading other people's handwriting, and I promise you, the omission is beautiful."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upstream, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"A future memory," Oscar said. "One I haven't lived yet. When I live it — whenever I live it — it's yours. Taken at the moment of its making, full weight, no discount."

Silt was quiet for a long moment. Water sounds moved through the silence, though there was no water near enough to make them.

"That," said Silt, "is not how memories work."

"With respect," said Oscar, "nothing in your inventory works. You sell bottled Tuesdays. You sold a jar of a cooper's courage to a man who then walked into the sea, which suggests the courage was mislabeled or the cooper was. Your entire trade is impossibility with a wax seal on it. Don't tell me how memories work. Tell me how *contracts* work."

"They work," said Silt slowly, "exactly as written."

"Yes," said Oscar. "They do. That's the whole religion of my life."

He crouched, pulled his true ledger from his pack — the real one, the one Marisol had helped him rebuild, page after page of Corvane's thefts and unremembered victims — and tore out a blank leaf. He smoothed it on his knee. He had no proper ink; he used the charred end of a stick, crushed and mixed with a drop of the wine Marisol guarded like a relic, and wrote in his careful bookkeeper's hand:

*Received of Oscar Sandoval: one (1) memory, future-dated, of comparable weight to passage rendered, to be surrendered at the moment of its first living, wherever and however that occurs. The bearer chooses the memory. The river chooses the moment. Neither party may refuse delivery.*

He signed it. Then he looked up.

"There's a catch," Silt said.

"There's always a catch. Name yours."

"Interest," said Silt, and now the smile was fully present, and Oscar understood that he had been negotiating with the polite part of the river, and the rest of it had been waiting underneath the whole time. "A future memory is a young thing. Young things weigh less than old ones. To balance an old debt with a young coin, the coin must be... larger."

"Larger how?"

"It cannot be a small memory," said Silt. "Not a breakfast. Not a kindness from a stranger. It must be one of the great ones. A wedding. A homecoming. The moment you understand something you have wanted to understand your whole life. And here is the interest, bookkeeper, so attend:" Silt leaned close, and up close he smelled like every rain that had ever ended a drought, which was the most heartbreaking smell Oscar had ever encountered. "You will not know which memory it is until you're living it. You will spend the rest of your days walking toward moments wondering — *is this one? Is this the one I'll lose?* Every joy will arrive pre-grieved. That is the terrible interest. That is the fare."

The fire popped. Marisol's hand had found Oscar's shoulder without either of them deciding it should.

"And if I die before I live it?" Oscar asked.

"Then the debt dies with you," said Silt, "and the river writes off the loss, and grumbles about it for a century or two." He shrugged, a motion like a bank giving way. "Rivers are used to things not arriving."

Oscar thought about it. That was the thing people never understood about him — that he actually *thought*, that the pause wasn't fear, it was arithmetic. Lena had died with her eyes open, passing him a burden she'd chosen for reasons she hadn't explained. He was walking toward a Well that would take his name whole and never give it back. His mother's face was already gone; he carried her now only as a fact, the way you carry a country you've never seen. What was one more uncertainty, weighed against the alternative? The alternative was handing Silt the *why*, and then some morning waking up on the road to the Well with a map in his pack and no idea whatsoever why he was carrying it.

Some debts you paid in coin. This one he'd pay in dread.

"Agreed," Oscar said, and wrote the terms, and signed, and pressed his thumb below the signature because Silt insisted, and the thumbprint sank into the strange paper like a stone into still water.

Silt took both copies. He read them — actually read them, lips moving, and Oscar had the vertiginous pleasure of watching a creature older than kingdoms check his figures and find them correct.

"Well struck," Silt said at last, and there was something in it that might, in a being with more face and less water, have been respect. "Your mother's face bought you the crossing. Your cleverness buys you the road. Do you know what you are, Oscar Sandoval?"

"A man made of precautions."

"A man made of *terms*," said Silt. "It's rarer. It's worth more." He stepped backward into shadow that had no business being deep enough to hold a boat, and the boat went with him, and just before the dark took all of him but the eyes, he added: "A gift, free of charge, since you haggled well. The river hears things. The river says: the one you seek next is small, and hidden, and someone has already died keeping her that way. Hurry. Small things are easy to carry off, and everyone in both realms is looking for her now."

Then he was gone, and the owl finally got its answer from somewhere, and the fire burned on as though the world's oldest creditor had never sat at its edge collecting signatures.

* * *

Marisol waited until Wren was genuinely asleep this time — she checked, the way you check a lock twice after a stranger's been in the house — and then she rounded on Oscar.

"You signed a blank contract with a *river demon*."

"Ferryman."

"He's a demon who rows."

"He's a creditor with excellent penmanship. There's a difference. Barely." Oscar fed a stick to the fire. "Would you rather I'd handed him the why?"

"I'd rather you'd handed him *me* and kept walking."

"That's the second time you've offered yourself as payment. I'm starting to take notes."

"Take all the notes you like." She sat down hard beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, which from Marisol was roughly equivalent to a signed affidavit of affection. "What worries me isn't the contract. Contracts I understand. What worries me is that you looked *relieved*. He offered to scoop out the reason you get up in the morning, and your face while you refused it — Oscar, you looked like a man turning down a second helping of the only meal he's had in a year."

Oscar considered lying. He was, after all, newly fluent in it, and getting better all the time. But Marisol had audited his motives once already, on the night they'd met over forged papers, and she had found his books in order, and there was a partnership in that — fragile, unglamorous, real — that he was not willing to cook.

"Because part of me wants to put it down," he admitted. "Every day. The guilt especially. Lena's eyes. The fact that she died delivering a message that was maybe never even meant for me — that I might be a mistake in someone else's ledger. Some mornings the only thing heavier than the burden is wanting to be rid of it." He turned the stick in the fire. "But a man who surrenders his reasons stops being a man and starts being luggage. I've seen it happen. Corvane's customers — the ones who sold their grief, their shame, their loves. They don't become peaceful. They become *available*. Available to whoever holds the next bottle." He glanced at her. "I'd rather be heavy and mine than light and someone else's."

Marisol was quiet a while. The stream muttered to itself in the dark, running downhill like a law-abiding citizen, and Oscar found he loved it a little for that.

"My brother used to say something like that," she said finally. "Before. He said, *Mari, never sell the sore tooth — the ache is how you know it's yours.*" Her jaw tightened. "Corvane made sure nobody remembered he'd ever said anything at all. Except me. I'm the only record of him left. Which means I'm not a courier anymore, am I? I'm a ledger."

"You're a witness," Oscar said. "Witnesses are worse. Ledgers can be burned."

She almost smiled. "Get some sleep, bookkeeper. We move at first light."

* * *

But sleep did not come, and around the coldest hour Oscar gave up pretending and took out the map instead, holding it low so its glow wouldn't wake the others. The second geography shimmered over the first: doors within doors, the uphill river, and at the center the Well of Names, drawn as a small circle with lines radiating out of it like the spokes of a wheel — or like the threads of a Veil, he thought, or like the entries of a ledger, every name in the world a line item flowing toward one account.

Wren's village lay three days east, if Silt's riddle meant what he thought it meant. *Small, and hidden, and someone has already died keeping her that way.* The stone signatory had told them the third mark was a child born after the breaking, carrying the key in her skin. Both realms hunting her now, the river said. Everyone looking.

Oscar touched the map where the eastern roads branched, and noticed — for the first time, and his stomach performed a slow, cold roll that no amount of trained immunity could blunt — that the branch nearest the girl's village had changed since yesterday. A new line ran through it now, fresh-drawn in ink that gleamed faintly silver, the same silver as the veins that had crawled up a dying woman's throat at the fish gate.

Someone else was updating the map.

And they were ahead of him.
