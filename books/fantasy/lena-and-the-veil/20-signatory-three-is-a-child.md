## 20. Signatory Three Is a Child

The village of Thistledown did not appear on any map Marisol had ever carried, sold, forged, or stolen — and she had once forged a map of the entire northern coast for a man who could not read, so she considered herself an authority on what maps contained.

It appeared instead on a rumor.

The rumor had traveled through three markets, two smugglers' dens, and one extremely chatty midwife before it reached them in the form of a baker's boy who accepted two coppers and a pastry for the directions and then followed them to the edge of town out of pure curiosity, walking backward the whole way, narrating.

"You'll want to go past the crooked oak," he said, "but not *too* past it, because past-too-far is where Old Bram says the ground forgets to be ground. And you'll want to arrive smiling. They can tell."

"They can tell what?" Marisol asked.

"If you're from the crown." The boy said *crown* the way other children said *weasel*. "Or the guild." He said *guild* the way they said *second weasel*. "Nobody comes here smiling unless they want something. So practice your smile. It's terrible."

Marisol touched her own mouth, offended. Beside her, Oscar made a sound that was suspiciously close to a laugh.

"It is not terrible," she said.

"It's a courier's smile," the boy agreed. "All teeth and no meaning. Try thinking about something nice. Bread. Dogs. The death of an enemy."

"Go home," Marisol said, and gave him another copper to do it, which was how every negotiation in her life ended: with her poorer and no wiser.

They walked. Past the crooked oak — correctly, neither too far nor too near — the road simply stopped pretending. Not vanished; stopped. One moment there was packed dirt under their boots, hedgerows, the smell of woodsmoke on a cold morning. The next moment there was a lane of white stones winding down into a green valley that Marisol would have sworn, hand on her ledger, did not exist when she'd eaten breakfast.

Oscar stopped at the boundary line and looked down at his own feet as if they had betrayed him.

"I've walked this road," he said slowly. "I'm nearly sure of it. On deliveries. Three years ago, maybe four." He frowned. "There was no valley."

"There was no valley," Marisol agreed. "And now there is one, and a village in it, and I'd wager my best forgery kit that the village has been here the whole time, minding its business, being unfindable by anyone who came looking with the wrong heart." She rolled her shoulders. "So. Smile like bread and dogs."

"And the death of an enemy," Oscar said gravely, and smiled, and it was — annoyingly — a good smile. Warm at the edges. A man who tasted poison for a living had apparently learned to make his face lie beautifully, which Marisol chose not to think about too hard.

They went down into Thistledown together.

* * *

The village received them the way a tide receives a piece of driftwood: completely, calmly, and with a great deal of collective attention disguised as indifference.

A woman beat a rug. Two men repaired a fence that did not need repairing. An old man sat whittling a stick that was already, unmistakably, finished. Every single one of them watched Marisol and Oscar with the corners of their eyes, and none of them spoke first.

It was Oscar who broke the silence, because of course it was. Marisol had learned this about him over the weeks of their uneasy partnership: put the man in front of a locked door, a guarded secret, or a hostile room, and he did not reach for force or cleverness. He reached for *service*. He bowed slightly — the precise, economical bow of a servant to a household — and said:

"We've come a long way and we're hungry. If there's work to be done in exchange for a meal, we'll do it. I'm told I'm tolerable with accounts, and my companion claims she can carry anything short of a house."

"I have carried a house," Marisol said. "Once. It was a small house."

Something shifted in the rug-beating woman's shoulders. The whittling old man snorted. And a girl of perhaps six, who had been staring openly from behind a rain barrel, said the fatal words that children say and adults spend their whole lives learning not to:

"Are you the ones come for Nettie?"

The entire village stopped moving at once. Rug, fence, stick, all of it. Even the smoke over the chimneys seemed to pause mid-curl.

"We've come," Oscar said carefully, "to speak with someone about an old promise. That's all."

The six-year-old, sensing nothing of the landmine she stood upon, nodded cheerfully. "Nettie says the promise is broken but the breaking isn't finished. She talks funny. She got it from the stories." Then, because children are mercifully immune to dramatic tension: "Can I see the small house?"

"Later," said the rug woman, who had crossed the square with remarkable speed and now had the child by the hand. Her eyes, when they found Marisol's, were not hostile. They were worse. They were *tired*, the way a gatekeeper's eyes are tired, the way eyes get after years of standing between something small and precious and everything outside the wall.

"You'll eat at Widow Fern's table," she said. "You'll wash your hands. And then you'll tell me exactly who sent you, and if the answer is Corvane, you'll both be in the millpond before the soup cools, and I say that with love."

"That's fair," Marisol said honestly.

"It's the only offer we make," the woman said, and led them to supper.

* * *

Widow Fern's table was long enough for half the village, which Marisol suspected was deliberate: nobody met strangers alone in Thistledown. The soup was barley and root vegetables and better than anything Marisol had eaten in a month, and she said so, and meant it, and watched the word *meant it* do more work than any credential she could have produced.

The tired-eyed woman turned out to be named Hesta, and she turned out to be the girl's aunt, and she turned out — halfway through the meal, setting down her spoon with the click of a decision made — to be ready to talk.

"The crown came in spring," she said. "Two men, fine coats, asking after a birthmark. Showed us a drawing of it. We showed them a village of children with freckles, scrapes, and ink stains, and let them count moles till dark. They left." She refilled Marisol's cup without asking. "The guild came in summer. Different approach. A friendly merchant, gifts for everyone, questions wrapped in compliments. Our smith took the gifts and our miller took the compliments and nobody answered a single question straight, and eventually the merchant decided we were all too stupid to be worth the trouble." A thin smile. "Stupidity is a fortress, if you maintain it properly."

"And the birthmark?" Oscar asked quietly. "What does it look like?"

Hesta looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw — and Marisol had begun to suspect that what people saw in Oscar was never quite what Marisol saw, that there was some second ledger written on his face that only certain readers could parse — it satisfied her.

"Come and see," she said. "But gently. She's eleven, not a relic. Everyone forgets that part. Everyone always forgets that part."

* * *

Nettie was in the orchard, up an apple tree, reading.

Not climbing. Reading. She had arranged herself in the fork of a branch with the boneless authority of someone who had been doing it since she could walk, a book open on her knees, an apple with one bite gone beside her, and she did not look up when five adults assembled beneath her tree like a delegation.

"Hesta says you're not from the crown or the guild," she said to the pages. Her voice was matter-of-fact, a little bossy, entirely unafraid. "But Hesta also says the millpond offer stands, so I figure the truth is somewhere in the middle."

"It usually is," Marisol admitted.

"What's the book?" Oscar asked.

That got her attention. She looked down — a serious face, round-cheeked, with a smudge of apple on her chin and eyes that were far too old for the rest of the arrangement — and held up the cover.

"*The Warden's Lament,*" she said. "It's the story of the covenant. The real one, not the chapel version. My gran copied it out before the chapel version got... tidied." She pronounced *tidied* with the contempt only an eleven-year-old can fully load onto a mild word. "In the chapel version everybody lives happily and the Veil is a blanket God tucks in at night. In Gran's version, the Wardens made a bargain with things that don't die, and the bargain had a price, and the price is coming due." She closed the book. "You're here about the price."

Marisol felt the hair rise on her arms. She glanced at Oscar, whose face had gone carefully, professionally blank — the blankness, she knew by now, of a man holding very still so the world won't spook.

"We're here about the covenant," Oscar said. "There are three signatories left. We've found two. The third—"

"—is me," Nettie said. "Obviously." She began climbing down with the book tucked in her shirt like contraband. "Born nine days after the Veil cracked the first time. Birthmark shaped like a key, right here—" she twisted to show them the back of her shoulder, where a wine-dark mark indeed curled in the unmistakable silhouette of an old-fashioned key, teeth and bow and all, "—and Gran said a mark like that isn't a decoration, it's an *instrument*, and instruments are for using, and someday somebody would come who needed it used." She landed in the grass, brushed off her skirt, and looked up at them expectantly. "So. Are you the somebody?"

For a moment nobody answered. Marisol found herself looking at this child — this freckled, apple-smeared, bedtime-story-believing child — and feeling the full weight of what they were doing settle onto her shoulders like wet wool. Somewhere south, a stone statue counted days. Somewhere west, a poisoner's seal burned a hole in Marisol's pack. And here, at the center of it, the future of two worlds stood in the grass in one shoe and one boot, having lost a shoe to the tree.

"You know what the covenant is," Oscar said at last. Gently. Testing.

"It's the lock between here and the Faded," Nettie said promptly. "And it's failing, and when it fails, everything that was kept out gets in, and everything that was kept safe gets taken, and my gran said the last time it strained, whole families woke up with holes where their memories used to be." She said it the way children recite multiplication tables — fluent, unbothered, and utterly certain. "Also there's a Well involved. And a name. But that part's in the last chapter and I haven't decided if I believe it yet."

"Why not?" Marisol asked.

Nettie picked up her abandoned apple and considered it. "Because in stories," she said, "the person who gives the name gets to come back. And Gran said that in this story, that part isn't true." She bit the apple. Chewed. Looked at them both with those too-old eyes. "So whichever one of you is planning to be brave — don't decide yet. Decide after you've read the last chapter. That's what Gran always said. *Read the last chapter before you volunteer.*"

Marisol's throat went tight. Beside her, Oscar had gone very still, in the particular stillness she had learned — too late, always too late — to recognize as the stillness of a man who already knows how the book ends and is deciding, right now, whether to say so.

* * *

They stayed the night, because Hesta insisted and because the alternative was the road, and the road had recently developed opinions about them.

Marisol slept badly in the loft above the stable, which was normal, and woke before dawn, which was also normal, and lay listening to the sounds of a village that had spent a generation hiding a child and was very, very good at it: the early stove-lighting, the soft argument between geese, a father singing tunelessly to a baby. Ordinary sounds. Fortified sounds.

She heard footsteps below and found Oscar already awake, sitting on the mounting block in the grey light, turning something over in his fingers. The map case. Lena's map case, worn soft as an ear.

"You didn't sleep either," she said.

"I slept." He said it the way he recorded entries: precisely, and with the faint hope that accuracy would pass for truth. He looked up at her. "Marisol. When we get to the Well. If it comes down to what the girl said—"

"Don't," Marisol said.

"—I need you to hear it anyway, because you're the only one who keeps honest books." His voice was low and even, the voice he used for numbers. "If it comes to a name given freely, wholly, never returned — I need you to know that I've read the last chapter. I've known since the stone told us. And I need you to not waste time trying to stop me, because the time will be better spent making sure it works."

Marisol sat down on the block beside him. For a while she said nothing, because she was a professional, and professionals audit before they sign.

"You understand," she said finally, "that I'm going to try to stop you anyway."

"Yes."

"And that I'll probably fail."

"Probably."

"And that afterward I'll keep your name written somewhere, every day, so it doesn't get lost. Because that's what ledgers are *for*, Oscar. Not the guild's kind. Real ones. Somebody remembering the account."

Oscar looked at her then, and whatever was in his face, he let it stay there for a full three seconds before he folded it away — which, from Oscar, was practically a speech.

"Thank you," he said.

Inside the house, a door banged. Small feet hit the floorboards overhead. And Nettie's voice floated down through the window, bright and imperious and eleven years old to the bone:

"Are you two out there deciding things without me? Because that's *exactly* what happened to the last covenant, Gran's version, chapter six! Come inside! There's eggs!"

Marisol laughed despite everything — despite the wound on her side pulling, despite the nine days ticking down somewhere in a statue's stone throat, despite the last chapter she had not yet read and already dreaded.

They went in to eggs.

And it was while the plates were being passed, while Nettie was explaining chapter six with her mouth half full, that Hesta appeared in the doorway with a face gone grey as ash, and said the words that emptied the room:

"There's a rider on the hill road. Crown colors. And behind him —" her voice caught, steadied, "— behind him, riding easy, like he owns the morning — a man in guild grey, and he's carrying a vial that glows."

Nettie set down her fork.

"Well," she said, in a small clear voice that did not sound afraid, which frightened Marisol more than anything else that morning. "I suppose it's time I read you all the last chapter."
