## 21. The Solstice Deadline

The chapel had no roof, no congregation, and no name that anyone living remembered, which made it, in the opinion of the stone signatory, the perfect place for a committee meeting.

"Sit," the stone said. "All of you. Well—two of you sit. The girl may stand. Children fidget less when upright."

The stone's voice came from everywhere and nowhere in particular, the way thunder does when it has decided to be polite about it. He was—or it was—a man once, carved now into a pillar saint with his hands folded over a book that no longer existed except as the suggestion of a book, worn smooth by centuries of weather into something more like a loaf than a gospel. His face had eroded into an expression of permanent mild disappointment, which, Oscar was learning, matched his personality exactly.

Marisol sat on a fallen pew, her wounded side propped carefully against a cushion she'd stolen from somewhere and refused to explain. The child—Nell, eleven years old, owner of a birthmark shaped like a keyhole on the back of her left hand—stood exactly where she'd been told to stand and immediately began fidgeting.

"You said children fidget less standing," Nell pointed out.

"I said they *should* fidget less standing," the stone corrected. "I did not promise they would. Now. Before we begin, the taster will tell me what he observed on the walk here."

Oscar blinked. "Observed?"

"You are a man who tastes for a living. You notice things other men swallow without thinking. Humor me."

Oscar considered. It had been a long walk from the workhouse district, through streets gone strange at their edges—the map's second geography bleeding through more visibly every day, doorways standing open onto weather that didn't match the rooms behind them, a gutter running uphill with a sound like a contented cat. "The fog is wrong," he said finally. "It comes off the river, but the river's been running uphill since Tuesday, so the fog is coming down from somewhere else. And it smells like—" he paused, hunting for the word, "—like the moment before you sneeze."

"Adequate," said the stone. "The fog is the Veil sweating. Which brings us to arithmetic. Do any of you like arithmetic?"

"I keep ledgers," Oscar said.

"Then you'll hate this one." The stone's eroded features somehow managed to convey the sensation of cracking knuckles. "Marisol. How many vials do you estimate Corvane has cut from the Veil in the last ten years? Estimate generously. You were a courier; you carried some."

Marisol's jaw tightened. "I carried forty-one that I know of. I'd guess he's sold three hundred to the crown alone. More to private buyers. Lords. Merchants. Anyone with gold and a terror of dying."

"Three hundred and forty-one, then, minimum. Each vial holds how much thread?"

"A finger's length. Maybe two."

"And each finger's length of thread holds together—" the stone paused, savoring it like a man delaying dessert, "—roughly one square mile of boundary. Per year. The Veil is not a wall, children. It is a weave. Cut enough threads anywhere and the whole cloth loosens. Now multiply your three hundred and forty-one by ten years of cutting, subtract what the Veil can regrow on its own—which, thanks to certain clever bookkeeping I could mention, is less every year—and tell me, taster, what number frightens you most?"

Oscar did the sums in his head, because sums were the one language in which he had never once been lied to. "Nine days," he said quietly. "Give or take. The solstice."

"*Give or take nothing,*" said the stone, with such sudden force that dust jumped from the ruined rafters. "That is the error in your thinking, boy, and it is the error that killed the last covenant. 'Give or take.' 'Close enough.' 'We'll mend it next season.' The Veil fails at solstice. Not approximately. At the exact moment the sun stands still, because that is the one moment in the year when the boundary between here and there forgets to move, and a thing that forgets to move cannot hold itself shut. Nine days. Nine. Count them on your fingers and then count them again tomorrow, because I promise you there will be one fewer."

Silence settled over the chapel like snow.

Nell raised her hand.

"You don't need to raise your hand," said the stone wearily.

"It's polite."

"It's *slow*. What is it?"

"If the Veil tears," Nell said, "what actually happens? Nobody will tell me. Marisol says I'm too young. Marisol says a lot of things are too young for me."

Marisol opened her mouth—to say *too young*, presumably—but the stone got there first.

"She's right to tell you that," the stone said, "but you've earned better than being managed. So. When the Veil tears, the Faded come through—not all at once, not like an army. Like water finding a crack. They are not evil, child. They are *hungry* in a way that has nothing to do with food. They want shape. Names. Warmth. Memory. They will drink this city down to its foundations and wear its people like coats, and they will not even understand they're doing harm, because harm requires a self, and they haven't got one. And then, when everything mortal has been drunk dry, the boundary will reknit itself around a world that no longer includes us. That is what happens. Are you sufficiently frightened?"

Nell thought about it with the terrible seriousness of the young. "No," she said. "Because you told me, and knowing things makes them smaller. My mum says that."

There was a long pause.

"The mother raised you well," the stone said at last, in a voice that had gone slightly rougher around the edges, if stone can be said to have edges. "Now. The second matter. Corvane."

"He knows we're gathering the signatories," Marisol said. "He has to. He tipped the crown to my rooms; he'll have eyes on the poisoner's garden by now; he probably knows about the child. So the question isn't whether he acts. It's what he's acting toward. And I've been turning that over, and I don't like any of the answers."

"Then let me save you the indigestion," said the stone. "I knew Corvane. Or rather, I knew the man Corvane was apprenticed to, which is nearly the same creature wearing a newer coat. Guild masters do not change. They only compound interest." A pause, the creak of centuries. "Corvane does not want to stop the Veil from tearing. Understand that first, or you will misjudge everything he does. A torn Veil is chaos, and chaos has no price list. No—Corvane wants to *arrive first*. He wants to be standing at the gap when it opens, holding the only rope across."

Oscar felt the shape of it before the words did, the way he sometimes tasted a poison before he could name it. "Passage," he said. "He wants to sell passage."

"Passage, protection, precedence. Think like a merchant, taster, since that's what you are under the livery. Two realms, suddenly touching, both terrified of the other. Who do frightened people pay?"

"Whoever promises to keep them apart," Oscar said slowly. "Or whoever promises to get them through."

"Both. Simultaneously. Forever." The stone's voice took on something almost like admiration, the grudging kind a soldier gives a competent enemy. "On one side: the crown, buying bottled years and walls against the Faded. On the other: the Faded themselves, who will pay in things mortals cannot refuse—memories, names, doors. And Corvane in the middle, the sole gatekeeper, taking tolls in both directions until the end of time. He doesn't need to own either realm. He only needs to own the space between them. Every empire in history was built on a narrower foundation than that."

"But the covenant—" Marisol began.

"—would ruin him. Yes. A reforged covenant means a sealed border, freely maintained, costing no one anything. It is, from Corvane's perspective, a charity. And Corvane has never given to charity in his life. I would know. I audited him." The stone managed, somehow, to convey a shudder. "In 1311 he embezzled four candles from a funeral rite. I have never forgotten. Stone remembers everything. It is our curse and our hobby."

Despite everything—the nine days, the tearing sky, the ache in Marisol's side and the fear sitting in Oscar's chest like a swallowed stone of its own—Oscar laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, but it came out. Nell giggled. Even Marisol's mouth twitched before she wrestled it back into courier-seriousness.

"You find this amusing," said the stone.

"I find it—" Oscar searched. "Humanizing."

"He is human. That is precisely the problem. Monsters are honest, taster. Men like Corvane are merely *ambitious*, which is far worse, because ambition always arrives with references." The stone let that sit a moment. "So. Nine days. Let us review our assets, as a merchant would. Signatory One." A pause. "Me. Immobile, weathered, and bitter, but possessed of the covenant-tongue and a memory going back six hundred years. Signatory Two: the poisoner's seal, which burns when the rite requires it, currently traveling in Marisol's coat, currently the object of every spy in the city's interest. Signatory Three: the child, whose mark is the keyhole the new covenant turns in. And the fourth requirement."

The silence stretched.

"The Well takes a name," Marisol said flatly. "Freely given. Wholly given. Never returned. And we still don't know whose name fits."

"We know more than that," the stone said, gently, in the way that was somehow worse than harshness. "We know the Well accepts very few coins. Most names bounce off it like rain off glass. It wants a name that has already crossed the Veil once and survived. A name with two countries written into it." A pause. "But we needn't discuss that tonight. Tonight we discuss the other problem, which is that Corvane will not wait politely for the solstice. He will move early. He must—if the covenant reforges before the tear, he loses everything. So ask yourselves: if you were Corvane, with nine days, spies everywhere, and a stolen seal to recover, what would you do tonight?"

Oscar's stomach answered before his mind did, dropping the way it dropped when he tasted almond bitterness under honey.

"He won't come for the seal," Oscar said. "Or the child. Too obvious, too guarded. He'll come for the *map*. The original. Because the map shows every door between the realms—including the roads to the Well—and as long as we hold it, we hold the only complete copy of the way in. He doesn't need to beat us to the Well. He needs to make sure nobody gets there at all until he's ready." He looked up. "And the map has been in my coat since the fish gate."

"Then why," asked the stone, with terrible mildness, "are we all sitting in a chapel instead of standing in your lodging house?"

They ran.

Oscar ran the way servants run, which is to say efficiently and invisibly, hugging walls, avoiding lantern-light, taking the service cuts behind the chandler's and the gap between the cooperage and the tannery that no gentleman would ever stoop for. Marisol fell behind—her wound wouldn't allow more—and shouted something about meeting at the ferry stairs, and Nell he sent ahead with her, because a child should not see what he was suddenly afraid he would find.

His lodging was above a pie shop on Candler's Row. Three streets from a marked door. He had chosen it years ago for the cheap rent and the back stair, and had never once wondered, until this week, why the rent was cheap.

The back stair was where he stopped, because the smell reached him first.

Smoke. Not cooking smoke—paper smoke. That particular sweetish char of ink and vellum burning, which every bookkeeper knows the way mothers know fever. His heart went cold and professional at the same time, two halves of him disagreeing about which mattered more.

He climbed anyway. Of course he climbed. A man made of precautions eventually learns that precautions are just a slower way of walking into the fire.

His room was not burned. That was the first wrongness. The door stood intact, the lock unpicked—professionally unpicked, the pins barely scuffed. Inside, his bed was made, his few possessions squared away, everything neat as a ledger line. Someone had searched the room and put it back *better* than they'd found it, which was either courtesy or contempt.

The map case was gone from beneath the floorboard.

In its place, weighted down with his own brass paperweight—the one with the guild's stamp, the one that had sat on his desk at the guild house for six years—was a single sheet of good rag paper, folded once. He opened it with hands that stayed steady through long practice and hated themselves for it.

The handwriting was his own.

*Oscar,*

*You were never meant to carry this. Lena died delivering it to the wrong man, and I have spent six years writing your name into books you never signed, so believe me when I say I know your hand better than you do. The map is mine by right of purchase. You are mine by right of entry.*

*Nine days is generous. I intend to close my accounts in five.*

*Come to the toll bridge on the fifth night and bring the seal, and I will burn the pages that hang you. Refuse, and I will let the crown read them aloud at your trial, which will be short, and the hanging prompt, and your name struck from every record in the city within the month. I have done it before. Ask Marisol's brother.*

*You were always the best bookkeeper I ever had. Don't make me write your ending myself.*

*—C.*

Oscar read it twice. The first time as a threat. The second time as a document, because that was what he was for, and because somewhere under the fear a small cold clerk in his soul had already begun taking notes.

Five days, not nine. Corvane wasn't waiting for the solstice—he was moving the deadline, compressing the market, forcing the sale. Classic. And the letter proved what Oscar had half-known since the guild house: the forged entries weren't just insurance against Oscar. They were a leash, and now Corvane had shown him the collar.

But.

The clerk in his soul tapped the page.

*Ask Marisol's brother.* Corvane knew about the brother. Knew that Marisol had found him, knew that the unremembered man had become part of their company—which meant Corvane had eyes close enough to count them around a chapel pew. And the letter named the toll bridge. Named the fifth night. Corvane wanted them at the toll bridge, seal in hand, on a schedule of his choosing, at a place of his choosing.

Which meant the toll bridge was a trap, and the trap was baited with the only thing Corvane feared: the covenant reaching the Well on time.

Oscar sat down slowly on his neatly made bed, in his neatly searched room, holding his own handwriting like a live coal.

The stone had asked what Corvane would do tonight. The answer was: this. Take the map, set the clock, choose the ground. But traps have a grammar, and Oscar had spent his life reading fine print. A trap tells you where it wants you, which tells you what it fears losing, which tells you—

Which told him that the map, for all its worth, was not the prize. If the map were the prize, Corvane would simply have kept it and said nothing. Instead he'd left a letter, a deadline, an invitation. He wanted them *moving*—panicked, split, predictable, hurrying toward the Well along the routes the map showed, while he followed at leisure and closed the doors behind them.

Unless.

Oscar looked at the paperweight with the guild stamp. Six years on his desk. Six years of Corvane's hand resting on his shoulder, easy and warm, while the other hand wrote in his name.

There was one thing a bookkeeper knew that a merchant forgot: the ledger is not the truth. The ledger is only what someone wrote down. And somewhere in Corvane's true books—in the cellar, behind the third shelf, in the iron box Oscar himself had catalogued years ago and no one had ever thought to move—there was a copy of everything. Every vial. Every theft. Every unremembered name.

Including, possibly, the answer to a question Oscar had not yet dared ask aloud: *whose name does the Well accept, and why has Corvane been so careful, all these years, never to say mine?*

Downstairs, the pie shop bell rang for the evening trade. Somewhere across the city, a stone was counting days, a courier was binding a wound, a child was believing in bedtime stories, and a king was drinking years out of little glass bottles.

Five nights. Oscar folded the letter into his coat where the map used to be, and went down the back stair into the fog that smelled of the moment before a sneeze, already composing, in the tidy dark of his head, the first line of a ledger entry Corvane would very much regret making possible:

*Received: one deadline, forged. To be repaid, with interest, at the Well.*
