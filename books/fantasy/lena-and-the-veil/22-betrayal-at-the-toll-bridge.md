## 22. Betrayal at the Toll Bridge

The thing about trusting people, Marisol had learned over eleven years of carrying other people's secrets through other people's doors, was that you could do it carefully or you could do it completely, and the ones who did it completely were always the ones who got hurt. She had done it completely exactly twice in her life. Once for her brother. Once for Lena.

She was about to make it three times for a man named Teodor Vask, and she would spend the rest of the chapter of her life that began on the toll bridge wishing she had audited him first.

* * *

They came down out of the hills on the fourth morning, the four of them moving like a rumor: Oscar in a borrowed fishmonger's coat two sizes too big, the poisoner's seal wrapped in oilcloth against Marisol's ribs, the child — Nell, eleven years old, birthmark coiled at her wrist like a sleeping adder — riding piggyback on Silt's... no. Not Silt. Silt stayed on his river. The fourth was a mule named Duchess who had opinions about bridges and expressed them at volume.

"Tell it again," Nell said, because she had asked four times already and Marisol, softening despite herself, gave in again.

"The bridge has one tollhouse and one keeper," Marisol said. "Teodor Vask. He ran messages for the guild when I was still wet behind the ears. He taught me how to fold a letter so it reads as a laundry list if the wrong eyes open it. He is fat, he is cheerful, he cheats at cards, and he owes Lena more favors than he can count."

"And he'll let us cross?"

"He'll grumble about the toll, take my coin, look the other way at the mule, and ask me to stay for supper because his wife cooks too much and worries about him." Marisol smiled despite the ache between her shoulder blades. "There are worse men. There are very few better ones, but there are worse."

Oscar walked beside her, hands jammed in the fishmonger's coat, and said nothing for a long while. That was the trouble with Oscar: he counted things. She could practically hear the abacus clicking behind his eyes.

"You're doing sums," she said.

"I'm always doing sums."

"Out with them, then."

He glanced back down the road they'd traveled, the way they'd come, empty and gold in the morning light. "Corvane's enforcers found us at the toll bridge outside Harrowfen. They knew which bridge. They knew we'd have the seal. Someone sold us once already."

"That someone is dead," Marisol said. "I checked personally."

"Dead men can't sell routes. Living men sell them twice." He shrugged inside the enormous coat, looking like a scarecrow that had given up. "I'm not accusing your friend. I'm just saying the numbers want a second look."

"The numbers," Marisol said, "can mind their own ledger."

But she thought about it that night, camped under a hawthorn with Nell asleep against her side and Duchess tied where the wind wouldn't find her, and she did not sleep well, and in the morning she told herself it was the ground.

* * *

The toll bridge crossed the River Lune where the Lune forgot it was supposed to be lazy and cut a gorge instead, all white water and black stone, spanned by a single arched span old enough that its stones had gone the color of old teeth. The tollhouse sat at the far end, squat and red-roofed, smoke rising from its chimney in a fat contented column.

Teodor Vask stood in the doorway of it, wiping his hands on an apron, and when he saw Marisol his whole face opened up like a market stall at dawn.

"Mari! By all the saints and several of the sinners!" He came across the bridge at a waddle, arms wide. "Nobody told me you were coming through, which means nobody knows, which means you're in trouble, which means — " he reached them, seized her shoulders, looked her over with genuine, unguarded delight — "you're thinner. Trouble agrees with you terribly. Who are your friends?"

"This is Ost—" Marisol caught herself. "Fish. And this is Nell. And the mule is Duchess, and she bites."

"All the best ones do." Teodor beamed at Duchess, who pinned her ears and made a low noise like a landslide deciding whether to happen. "Toll's a copper a head, two for the mule, nothing for children because my wife will haunt me. Come, come, I've a stew on, there's far too much as always — "

"Teo." Marisol kept her voice light. "We're expected on the far side before dark."

"Expected! Everyone's expected somewhere. The stew doesn't care." But something flickered behind his cheer, quick as a fish turning under ice, and Marisol felt it in her stomach before her head caught up — the flicker of a man whose eyes went, just for half a heartbeat, to the road behind them.

She laughed. She made herself laugh, big and easy, and clapped his arm. "One bowl. One. And then we're gone, and you'll tell me about your wife's garden, and I'll pretend the carrots were impressive."

"They *are* impressive," Teodor said, wounded. "This year there's a parsnip shaped like a bishop."

And that was the whole of it, Marisol thought later. That was the entire tragedy, served up in a red-roofed tollhouse with too much stew: a fat cheerful man showing off a parsnip, meaning every word, loving every guest, and having sold them all anyway.

Because while Nell was on her second bowl and Oscar was eating with the careful attention of a man who tasted everything whether he wanted to or not, Marisol stepped out to the privy around the back of the tollhouse, and on her way back she passed the front window, and through it she saw Teodor standing at his own counter, alone, holding a small glass vial up to the light.

It caught the afternoon sun and threw it back golden. It moved in his fingers like something alive, like honey with a memory.

Marisol had carried vials before. She knew what a year looked like in glass.

She stood in the yard with the wind coming up the gorge and understood, all at once and completely, the way you understand a card trick after it's too late to matter. The route. The bridge. The only crossing for thirty miles that Corvane's men could watch without splitting their forces. Teodor hadn't needed to betray anyone violently. He'd only needed to be where he always was, charging his copper a head, keeping his stew too full, and mention — casually, over cards, to whoever paid — that Mari Brightwater's crowd would come through on such-and-such a day.

And they'd paid him in the one coin the guild minted that never lost value.

She went back inside. She sat down. She ate her stew, because refusing it would tell him she knew, and knowing was the only weapon she had left, and weapons should stay hidden until the last possible moment. Oscar watched her sit and saw whatever it was he saw — he was infuriatingly good at seeing — and his spoon paused halfway to his mouth, and she gave him the smallest shake of her head: *not now.*

Nell tugged her sleeve. "Mari. The stew's really good."

"Eat it all, then," Marisol said, and her voice held, and she hated how much practice that took.

* * *

They left at dusk, as planned, because changing the plan would confirm what she suspected, and because — this was the part she turned over and over on the walk back toward the bridge — maybe she was wrong. Maybe the vial was Teodor's own purchase, saved from some honest trade, a year bought fair and square for his wife's coughing or his own bad knee. People bought years for decent reasons. That was the whole horror of the guild; it ran on decent reasons the way a mill ran on water.

Maybe.

Duchess stopped dead at the near end of the bridge.

Marisol had known the mule for six days and had already learned that Duchess's opinions were, annoyingly, almost always correct. The animal planted all four hooves, dropped her head, and made the landslide sound.

"She smells something," Oscar said quietly.

"Dogs?"

"No dogs." Oscar's nostrils flared, faintly, the way he did — the taster's habit, reading the air like a page. "Smoke. Oiled leather. And..." His face changed. "Vinegar. Somebody's been handling the poisoner's work. Or wearing her perfume by accident."

Across the bridge, the tollhouse windows glowed warm. Teodor stood in his doorway, lantern raised, waving them on with his free hand, cheerful as Sunday. *Come on, then, hurry across, don't dawdle* — and it was the hurrying that finished it for Marisol, because Teodor Vask had never hurried anyone anywhere in his life except toward a second helping.

"He's waved us onto the bridge," she said flatly. "Onto it. Where there's no cover and no turn-around and the rail comes up to a child's chest."

Oscar said nothing. He didn't need to. The numbers had done their second look.

Behind them, back along the road they'd come, a whistle sounded — two notes, low, answered by two notes ahead. Ahead. Across the gorge. From the trees beyond the tollhouse, where the road bent out of sight.

"They're on both ends," Oscar said.

"How many?"

"If they're good? Six. If they're Corvane's best, four and a crossbow."

Nell's hand found Marisol's. Small, sticky with stew, absolutely steady. "What do we do?" the girl asked, and — this was the thing about Nell that Marisol would carry for the rest of her life — she asked it the way you ask what's for supper. No tears. Eleven years old, born after the covenant broke, raised on bedtime stories that happened to be true, and scared, certainly, but curious about her own fear, like it was weather.

"We do the stupid brave thing," Marisol said, "because the smart thing is to surrender, and we can't."

"Why not?"

"Because of what's sewn into my coat, little one. Because of you." She crouched, took Nell by both shoulders. "Listen. When I say run, you run for the tollhouse door and you get behind Teodor and you stay there no matter what you hear. Can you do that?"

"What will you hear?"

"Nothing good. Run anyway."

She straightened. Oscar had already shed the fishmonger's coat — underneath it, absurdly, he wore a second coat, because of course he did, the man prepared for the loss of his own preparations — and he'd produced from somewhere a long carving knife, the kind used on festival geese.

"That's not a weapon," Marisol said.

"It's a tool," Oscar said, "for a job I'm hoping not to do." He glanced at her. "The seal. If it comes to choosing — "

"It won't."

"It might. I'm just saying the numbers — "

"*Mind their own ledger,*" Marisol and Oscar said together, and despite everything, despite the whistles and the darkening gorge and the lantern swinging in Teodor's too-cheerful hand, she almost laughed.

Then the first crossbow bolt took the lantern out of Teodor's grip, and the night started.

* * *

Later — much later, in a barn, with a hot iron and no anesthetic and Nell holding her hand hard enough to crack knuckles — Marisol would piece the fight together the way you piece together any disaster: in fragments, out of order, each one worse than the last.

The bolts came from the trees above the tollhouse, three archers, shooting down onto the bridge's near end to pen them against the gorge. Which meant the trap wasn't the bridge at all. The bridge was the funnel. Teodor's job had been simpler than selling a route: keep them on the span until the far end closed. A shepherd's work. A sheepdog's whistle.

"Off the bridge!" Oscar shouted, and grabbed for Nell, and that was when Marisol saw the men coming down the near road with the unhurried confidence of professionals who had been paid to be patient — four of them, cudgels and nets, no blades, because Corvane wanted the map-bearer breathing and the child unmarked. Unmarked. As if either of them was merchandise.

Duchess solved the first problem.

No one had untied her, because no one had thought a mule mattered, and this was the last mistake Corvane's men would make tonight that they'd live to regret. Duchess snapped her lead, came down the road like a landslide that had finally decided, and hit the net-carrier square in the chest with the full authority of four hundred pounds of offended opinion. The man went backward off the verge and into a gorse bush, and the gorse bush, Marisol would swear forever after, got the worse of it.

"Run!" she screamed at Nell, and Nell ran — but not for the tollhouse. The girl spun instead and bolted *outward*, along the bridge, toward the middle, toward the open span and the white water below, because Nell had grown up hiding from agents of two realms and had learned the first lesson early: never run where they've already looked.

Which left the gap between Marisol and the child wide open, and filled it with the fifth man — the one from the trees, the crossbowman, dropping to the roadside ten feet away with his piece leveled at the small running figure on the bridge.

Marisol did not think. Thinking was for afterward. She crossed the distance in four strides and got her body between the bolt and the girl, and the world went white and then very, very quiet, the way it does, and she heard herself say, in a voice that seemed to belong to someone calmer, "*Down,*" and pushed Nell flat against the stones as the second bolt cracked off the bridge rail a hand's width above her spine.

Then Oscar was there. She never quite reconstructed how — the goose knife, the vinegar smell, a shriek that was mostly Duchess — but the crossbowman folded around something and sat down hard and did not get up quickly, and Oscar knelt in the wreckage breathing like a bellows and said, "Are you — " and then looked at where she was clutching her side and stopped talking, because Oscar always stopped talking when the numbers stopped adding up.

"It's nothing," Marisol said.

"It's bleeding."

"It's *nothing that's going to stop us.*" She got her feet under her, which cost her a view of the stars she hadn't ordered. "The seal — "

"Gone." Oscar's voice was hollow. In the chaos, in the netting and the shouting and the mule apocalypse, someone had cut the oilcloth packet from her coat — no. Not from her coat. From the satchel. While she'd been eating stew. While they'd all been eating stew, in the warm yellow room, with the parsnip-shaped bishop on the shelf and the host refilling bowls.

Through the tollhouse window, she could see Teodor Vask standing with his back to the room, very still, facing his own wall. Not watching the fight. Not helping. Just standing, the way a man stands when he cannot bear to look at what he's done and cannot stop it either.

The far end of the bridge was closing. Two more silhouettes, moving with purpose. The near end had Duchess, who was holding it magnificently, but mules tire, and men with cudgels learn patience in prison yards.

"Nell," Marisol gasped. "The middle of the bridge. Is there — the maintenance hatch, the old ferry chain — "

"There's a gap under the third arch pier," Nell said instantly. "For the chain mechanism. It's big enough for a person. Probably. I looked, when we crossed the gorge road this morning. I always look."

Marisol stared at her. "You *looked.*"

"You told me to always look."

"I have never loved anyone more than I love you right now, and I've known you nine days. Go. Both of you. Under the pier, follow the chain ledge downstream, don't stop for anything, and if I'm not at the far pool by dawn — "

"You'll be there," Nell said, with the absolute certainty of a child who still believed adults kept their promises, and then she was gone, small and quick along the dark span, and Oscar hesitated one heartbeat — one, his hand hovering over Marisol's wound as though he could audit it shut — and then followed, because someone had to keep the numbers company.

Marisol turned to face the far end of the bridge alone, one hand pressed to her side, blood working warm between her fingers, and watched the silhouettes come on.

They didn't rush her. That was the insult of it. They walked, unhurried, cudgels loose in their grips, and the taller one called out, almost kindly: "Just the packet, miss. Hand it over and walk away. Nobody's paid for you."

"It's already gone," Marisol said, and grinned at them through the pain, because if she was going to bleed out on a bridge she was going to be insufferable about it. "Cut from my bag while I ate your employer's bought friend's stew. You're chasing a corpse's errand, boys. Corvane's paying youth for a leather case full of nothing."

The tall man stopped. Even in the dark she saw the arithmetic land — the fury of professionals discovering the job was botched upstream. And behind them, in the tollhouse doorway, lantern relit and held high, Teodor Vask appeared, and his face in the lantern light was gray as the bridge stones.

"Mari," he called across the water, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's. "Mari, I — the wife's sister, she's got the wasting sickness, and they said one vial, one, and I thought — I thought it was just telling them a road, roads aren't — " He stopped. There was nothing to say and he knew it, and to his credit — his terrible, permanent credit — he didn't try to finish. He just stood there holding the lantern up, lighting the way for the men hunting her, because that was the shape his betrayal had finally taken: not malice, not even greed, just a good man's love pointed the wrong direction by a vial of stolen time.

"I know, Teo," Marisol called back, and meant it, which was the worst part. "I know exactly."

Then she went over the rail.

* * *

The water took her like a debt collector. Cold, total, impersonal. She surfaced in the black churn below the third arch with her side screaming and her lungs full of river, and found the chain ledge by feel, and found Nell's small hand reaching down for hers from the ledge above, and found Oscar's pale face beside the girl's, both of them lit by nothing, waiting, counting seconds the way he counted everything.

She climbed. It took forever. It took four minutes.

They lay together on the wet stone under the arch while boots crossed the bridge overhead and voices argued about the packet and the river and the mule, and Nell held Marisol's hand again, gentler now, and whispered, "You're bleeding a lot."

"I know."

"Is it the kind of bleeding that stops?"

"It's the kind that stops," Marisol said, "if somebody finds me a needle, a flame, and something strong to drink."

"I can do two of those," Oscar said grimly, already tearing strips from the fishmonger's coat. "The drinking will have to wait. And Marisol — " he paused, and in the dark she heard him choose honesty over comfort, which was becoming, annoyingly, his habit — "they have the seal. Corvane has the poisoner's seal. Without it, the rite at the Well — "

"I know what we've lost."

"And the king's men will have heard by morning. Both sides of the Veil will be converging on the Well now, and we're a bookkeeper, a courier with a hole in her, and a child."

"Don't forget the mule."

Above them, distantly, Duchess voiced her opinion of the entire evening, and Marisol laughed, and laughing hurt enough to make her gasp, and Nell patted her hand and said, with terrible tenderness:

"Don't worry, Mari. I always look. And tomorrow, we'll look first."

Far off, past the gorge, past the hills, in a direction none of them could see, something answered — a sound like a bell rung underwater, like a name spoken in a language made of doors — and all three of them went silent, listening, as the river carried the echo away toward the Well of Names.
