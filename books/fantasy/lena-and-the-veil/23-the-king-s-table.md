## 23. The King's Table

They did not bind his hands.

That was the first thing Oscar noticed, because noticing was the whole of his profession. A man who tasted for kings learned early that every detail was a message: which cup sat closest to the royal hand, whether the salt cellar had been refilled or merely moved, whether the guards at the door stood at ease or with their thumbs hooked over their belts. Bound hands would have meant *prisoner*. Unbound hands, walking through the palace he had served in for eleven years, past servants who bowed to him as though nothing had changed—unbound hands meant something worse.

Unbound hands meant they wanted him comfortable.

"They're being polite," said the inquisitor walking beside him. Her name was Vess; he'd seen her at three banquets, always near the wine, never drinking. "His Majesty prefers audiences without chains. He says it puts people in an honest frame of mind."

"And does it?"

"No," Vess said, with the first hint of warmth she'd shown since the fish market. "But it puts them in a *nervous* frame of mind, and nervous people talk faster."

Oscar considered pointing out that he was, professionally speaking, incapable of talking fast. He decided against it. There were six guards behind him and a long corridor ahead, and somewhere in that corridor was a door he knew—the third panel from the corner, the one that opened onto the servants' vein—and knowing it was there and not using it felt like holding his breath underwater. He could do it. But not forever.

The palace smelled different tonight. That was the second thing he noticed, and it troubled him more than the guards did. Eleven years of tasting had given him a nose like a customs officer's: he could identify forty spices blindfolded, could tell you which of the king's cooks had been crying, could smell a lie in a kitchen the way other men smelled rain coming. And tonight the palace smelled of—

He stopped walking before he realized he'd stopped.

"What is that?" he said.

Vess glanced at him. "What is what?"

"That smell." It was faint, sweetish, wrong—a smell like candle smoke and cold iron and something underneath both, something green and growing where nothing should grow. He knew it. He had smelled it seeping out of guild crates marked *fragile* and *do not open near open flame*, crates whose entries he had recorded in a ledger under headings like *thread, Veil, four ells* and *thread, Veil, damaged, discount*. He had written those words in his neat bookkeeper's hand and had not, until this moment, understood what they meant.

"Perfume," said Vess.

"It isn't perfume."

"Then it's above your station to name it." She touched his elbow, gently, the way one guides a guest rather than a captive. "Come. His Majesty is waiting."

* * *

The king's private dining room was small, by royal standards—which meant it was still large enough to hold a cathedral congregation comfortably—and tonight it held only a table set for two.

Two places. Two chairs. Two settings of the good silver, the silver with the crest, the silver that came out perhaps twice a year.

One chair was occupied. The other was empty, and angled toward it, waiting.

"Master Sandoval," said King Aldous the Third, "sit down."

Oscar did not sit down. Sitting down was what a man did when he accepted the shape of a conversation, and Oscar wanted very much to keep standing, because standing kept his legs informed of their options. But there were guards at both doors now, and Vess at his shoulder, and the king was watching him with an expression Oscar had never seen on a royal face before.

It was hunger. Not the ordinary kingly appetite that Oscar tasted for daily—not the appetite for quail and venison and sauces with names in three languages. This was older and stranger, the look of a man looking at something he had lost.

"You're not eating," the king observed. "I'm told you rarely eat. You taste, and you don't eat. Eleven years, and my staff say you've gained maybe four pounds."

"I'm careful," Oscar said.

"You're *careful*." The king smiled, and there was something in the smile that made the hair on Oscar's arms stand up—not menace, nothing so simple. Recognition. The smile of a man greeting a relative across a crowded room. "Sit, Master Sandoval. I promise you, nothing on this table will harm you. Nothing on this table will harm anyone ever again, if I have my way about it."

Against every instinct he owned—and he owned instincts the way a miser owned coins, counted nightly, each one polished—Oscar sat.

A servant poured wine. Oscar watched the pour the way a hawk watches a field mouse: the angle of the bottle, the film on the inside of the glass, the half-second hesitation before the servant set it down. Nothing. It was honest wine, good vintage, untouched. Which meant the danger wasn't in the wine, and a table where the danger isn't in the wine is a table where the danger is in the host.

"I'll confess," the king said, lifting his own glass, "that I asked for you specifically. Corvane wanted you taken to the guild house. Vess wanted you taken to the cells. I said no to both." He drank. "Do you know why?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Because I looked at your file." The king set down the glass and leaned forward, and the candlelight moved across his face, and Oscar saw—for the first time, really saw—how old the king was. Not the official age, sixty-one, the age printed on coins and read aloud at ceremonies. Something else. Something tired underneath, the way a wall is tired underneath its paint. "Eleven years of service. No family listed. No history before your twentieth year. Do you know how unusual that is? Every servant in this palace has a village, a mother, some cousin who writes asking for money. You arrived out of clean air, fully formed, with excellent references nobody could verify." He tilted his head. "I should have had you investigated years ago. Instead I promoted you twice. Isn't that funny?"

"Hilarious, Your Majesty."

"There it is," the king said softly. "That tone. My grandmother had that tone. Dry as December." He was staring now, openly, rudely, the way no one was permitted to stare at a king and yet kings stared however they liked. "You have her chin. Did you know that? You have my grandmother's chin exactly."

"Coincidence, Your Majesty. Chins are common."

"Chins are common," the king agreed pleasantly. "Yours is not." He raised one hand, and a servant appeared from the shadows carrying a small lacquered box, and set it beside the king's plate, and vanished again, all in the time it took Oscar's heart to make two beats.

The box opened with a click that sounded, to Oscar's ears, exactly like a ledger closing.

Inside, nested in blue velvet, lay six vials.

They were beautiful. That was the horror of them—that was the horror of everything the guild sold, and Oscar understood this now with a clarity that felt like a fever breaking. They were slender things of smoked glass, and the liquid inside caught the candlelight and held it, swirled it, made it move the way water moves when something alive is swimming just beneath the surface. Each vial was sealed with wax stamped with the guild's mark. Each one represented, he now knew, a strip cut from the boundary between worlds. Each one was a piece of the sky's hem, snipped off and bottled and sold.

And here they sat, on the king's table, between the salt cellar and the bread.

"Do you know what these are?" the king asked.

"I keep the guild's books, Your Majesty. I know what they cost."

"Cost." The king laughed, and it was a real laugh, startled out of him, and for one absurd moment he sounded like a tired old man at a tavern rather than the most powerful person in the realm. "Cost. Yes. Corvane charges me a fortune, and I pay it gladly, and do you know why?" He picked up one vial and turned it slowly, watching the light swim. "Because I am dying, Master Sandoval. Slowly, in the ordinary way, the way everyone dies—but I can feel it now. Mornings are heavier than they used to be. Stairs have opinions. And I am the king, and I find that I am not finished." He looked up. "Would you refuse these, in my place? Honestly. You're a careful man. Careful men want to live."

Oscar thought of the Near Faded market. Of the twilight stalls and the goods that whispered. Of the strips of Veil-thread hanging like laundry, and the sound they made—a thin, high keening, almost below hearing, the sound of a world unraveling at its edge. He thought of Marisol's brother, erased. He thought of Lena's eyes staying open.

"I don't know what I'd do in your place, Your Majesty," he said. "I know what they're made of. And I know the bill hasn't come due yet, but it will, and bills like that don't come due in coin."

The king studied him for a long moment. Then, unhurried, he unstoppered the vial.

"Your Majesty—" said Vess.

"Leave us."

"Your Majesty, protocol requires—"

"*Leave us.*" It was not shouted. It didn't need to be. Vess bowed and withdrew, and the guards went with her, and the doors closed, and suddenly it was only the two of them and the candles and the swimming light.

The king raised the vial in something like a toast. "To honesty," he said. "I'm told it's rare at this table."

He drank.

* * *

For a moment, nothing happened. The king set the empty vial down, and exhaled, and smiled at some private satisfaction—and then the candles guttered, all of them at once, though there was no draft, and the light in the room went strange and greenish, the color of the underside of leaves.

And the king's face began to change.

Oscar had no word for what he watched, so his mind supplied several bad ones and then, finally, a true one: *remembering*. The king's face was remembering something. The lines around his eyes deepened and rearranged themselves—not aging, nothing so simple as aging, but *sorting*, the way a face sorts itself over decades into its final argument. The jaw squared. The brow grew heavy. The mouth thinned into a line that Oscar had seen every morning for eleven years, shaving, in a cheap mirror, in a servant's room three floors below this one.

The king's face became Oscar's face.

Not Oscar's face as it was—as it might be. As it would be, worn forward forty years, weathered by whatever weather waited in him. An older Oscar sat at the king's table wearing the king's robes, and when the old man opened his eyes they were Oscar's eyes, gray-green, skeptical, ringed with the particular sleeplessness of a man who counts things at night.

"There you are," the king said, in a voice that was mostly his own but fraying at the edges into another voice, a voice Oscar knew intimately because it came out of his own throat every day. "There you are. Blood calls to blood, boy. Across the Veil, across the years, across every lie someone wrote in a ledger to keep you hidden—it calls."

Oscar's hands were flat on the table. He did not remember putting them there. "What are you?"

"I'm asking myself the same question." The king pressed his palms to his own transfigured cheeks, wonderingly, like a man touching a scar he'd forgotten. "Every time I drink, it gets stronger. The first vial showed me nothing. The second showed me a door. The third—" he laughed, low and unsteady, "—the third showed me a woman with silver in her veins, standing in a doorway, holding a bundle. And she was angry. Gods, she was angry. And last month, the fifth vial—" His gaze fixed on Oscar, and it was unbearable, being looked at that way, being *recognized* that way. "Last month it showed me a child. Being carried. Being carried *away*, through something that screamed at the edges. And I woke up weeping, and I didn't know why, and I sent for Corvane, and Corvane said the visions were a side effect, harmless, and I believed him because I wanted to believe him. Because I wanted my vials."

The green light pulsed. Somewhere far away, or perhaps not far away at all, Oscar heard that keening again—the sound of thread pulled tight.

"You look like my grandmother," the king whispered. "You look like *me*. Who are you? What are you? Corvane says you're a foundling with forged papers. Foundlings don't have my chin. Foundlings don't make my poison-taster's post feel like a *homecoming*—did you know that? When you were assigned to me, eleven years ago, I walked into the tasting chamber and saw you bent over the plates, checking everything twice, and I felt—" He stopped. His borrowed face worked. "I felt like I'd found something I'd buried. Like grief with the address filled back in."

Oscar's mouth was dry. His careful, trained, professional mind was doing what it always did under pressure: itemizing. *The vials are Veil-thread. The Veil shows what it holds. The king sees family. The king sees a smuggled child. Lena carried children across the Veil. Lena was the Warden. Lena put a map in my hands and died saying reforge it.* The items stacked themselves into a shape he refused to look at directly, the way one refuses to look at the sun.

"Your Majesty," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect, "what did the woman in the doorway look like?"

"Dark hair going gray at the temples. A scar on her left wrist, old, straight, deliberate." The king frowned, dredging. "She had a seal on a chain. Some kind of knot-work design. And she said—" his borrowed brow furrowed— "she said, *he'll be safe if no one remembers to look for him.*"

The room tilted. Or Oscar tilted. The distinction seemed academic.

Lena. Younger, alive, furious, standing in a doorway between worlds with an infant in her arms—an infant with a chin that would one day sit across a king's table and taste his food and survive his poisons and keep two sets of books and trust numbers more than people, because numbers had never once lied to him the way his own history apparently had.

"The sixth vial," the king said quietly. He was looking at the lacquered box. Five vials remained, swimming in their velvet. "Corvane delivered it yesterday. He said it was the strongest batch yet. He said—" the king's mouth twisted— "he said it would show me *where the child went.*"

"Don't," Oscar said.

"It's my vial. Bought with my gold."

"It's a strip of the border between life and everything else, cut off and corked, and every one you drink makes the hole bigger, and when the solstice comes the hole will be big enough to walk through, and what walks through will not be buying." The words came out of him fast and certain, and he realized, distantly, that he meant every one, and that meaning things was new to him, and that he liked it terribly. "Your Majesty. Aldous. Whoever you are to me—you're being farmed. Corvane isn't selling you youth. He's selling you *mirrors*, and charging you for the privilege of watching yourself age in them, and meanwhile he's unpicking the sky."

The king went very still.

Outside the door, boots. Several pairs, moving with purpose, the particular purposeful rhythm of men who had been waiting for a signal. Vess's voice, low, giving an instruction. The green light in the room thickened like fog off a river that ran uphill.

The king looked at the sixth vial. Then at Oscar. Then at the vial again, and Oscar watched a war cross the transfigured face—his own future face, warring with itself—and understood with sick certainty that the war was not yet won, and that the price of losing it would be paid by everyone on both sides of the Veil.

"Guards," the king said, not loudly.

The doors opened.

"But first," the king added, and his hand closed around the sixth vial, and his eyes—Oscar's eyes, aged and hungry and grieving—never left Oscar's face, "tell me one thing, whoever you are. Tell me why my heart has been beating differently since you walked into this room. Tell me why it feels like it's answering something."

Because blood calls to blood, Oscar did not say. Because you are drinking the border and it is showing you yourself. Because I think, though I cannot prove it, though there is no entry in any ledger anywhere that proves it—

"—because I'm your grandson," Oscar said. "Or something close enough that the difference doesn't matter. And I think Lena gave me to the world to save it, and I think Corvane knows, and I think that's why he wants me in a cell instead of in front of you."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Oscar counted them; counting was what he did.

Then the king lifted the sixth vial to the green light, and smiled with Oscar's mouth, and said:

"Then let's see where you come from."
