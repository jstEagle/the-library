## 24. Faded Blood

They took his boots first.

Oscar noted this the way he noted everything: precisely, without panic, in the part of his mind that had spent twenty years cataloguing other people's mistakes so he wouldn't die of them. Boots meant they feared him running. Taking boots from a prisoner was a statement of respect, in its way — a compliment delivered by confiscation.

The room they gave him was better than most cells and worse than most rooms: a round chamber somewhere deep in the palace's bones, where the walls were old enough to have opinions. A single candle. A table. Two chairs, one of which was clearly meant for him and one of which had been chosen for its ability to make sitting in it feel like an accusation.

He'd been left alone for two hours. He knew it was two hours because he counted, the way he always counted, the way his whole life had been one long act of counting — spoonfuls, heartbeats, entries, lies.

*One lie,* he thought. *I've told exactly one lie in my life, and it bought me this.*

There was a pitcher of water on the table. He looked at it for a long time and did not drink.

That was the thing about being who he was — whoever that turned out to be. You never drank the water.

* * *

The door opened at what would have been dawn, if dawn were permitted down here. The man who entered wore the grey of the Royal Inquisition, but he wore it badly, like a coat borrowed from a taller brother. He was perhaps fifty, soft around the middle, with the kind of face that made you want to confess just to relieve him of the trouble.

"Oscar Sandoval," the man said, settling into the accusing chair with a small grunt. "Royal food-taster. Eleven years of service. No family recorded. No debts recorded. No—" he consulted a paper, "—*anything* recorded, really, before your seventeenth year. You appear in the palace rolls fully formed, like a mushroom after rain."

"I was raised in the kitchens," Oscar said. "Kitchens don't keep good records. They keep good secrets instead."

"Mm." The inquisitor set a leather folio on the table. Oscar recognized the folio. He had inventoried forty identical ones in the guild cellar, and the recognition went through him like cold water finding a crack. "Do you know what this is?"

"It looks like a ledger."

"It is a ledger. It is *your* ledger, Master Sandoval. Or rather, it is the guild's ledger, kept in your hand, under your seal, over a period of six years." The inquisitor opened it with the tenderness of a man handling evidence he liked very much. "Bottled memories. Borrowed years. And these last pages — Veil-thread. Quantities of Veil-thread sufficient to unmake a parish."

"Those aren't my entries."

"No?"

"No."

"That's unfortunate," the inquisitor said, "because they're in your handwriting."

And here was the moment Oscar had been dreading since the fish gate, since the map, since the door that hadn't been there yesterday. Because he had examined those forged pages himself, in another life, three chapters ago — and he had known, with the certainty of a man who has balanced books since childhood, that the handwriting was perfect. It was not merely like his hand. It *was* his hand, every loop and hesitation, even the little tick he put on his sevens that no one had ever noticed except, apparently, whoever had taught themselves to be him.

"I know," Oscar said quietly. "That's the problem. They're too good."

The inquisitor blinked. "You'll have to help me there."

"When a man forges a hand, he copies what he sees. But my sevens have a tick on them that I only make when I'm tired — I don't do it on purpose, I couldn't stop doing it if I tried. Whoever wrote those pages didn't copy my writing." Oscar leaned forward. "They copied *me*. Every flaw. Including the flaws I only produce at the end of a long night. Do you understand? There are pages in that book dated to nights I can prove I slept four hours. Nobody tired enough to make that tick was awake to make it."

The inquisitor stared at him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a short, rueful sound, quickly strangled.

"You're arguing forensics," he said. "With the Inquisition. In a cell. Over whether your treason is *authentic*."

"I'm arguing that someone built me a gallows out of my own handwriting, and I'd like to know who holds the pen."

"The answer is Corvane, obviously. Everyone knows it's Corvane. That isn't why you're here." The inquisitor closed the folio and folded his hands, and something in his manner changed — the borrowed-coat awkwardness falling away, leaving something older and more careful underneath. "Master Sandoval. Three days ago, His Majesty drank from a vial. Do you know what's in those vials?"

"Veil-thread. Cut years. Immortality, sold by the dram."

"Yes. And do you know what happens when a man drinks them?"

"He stops aging."

"For a while." The inquisitor's eyes were steady. "And then he starts aging *toward something*. The thread remembers what it was cut from, you see. It pulls. His Majesty looks at you sometimes now, Master Sandoval, and goes quiet. Yesterday he asked me to bring you to him personally. Not to the Inquisition. To *him*. He said—" the man hesitated, visibly uncomfortable, "—he said, 'Bring me the boy with my brother's chin.'"

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"I don't have an uncle," Oscar said.

"No," agreed the inquisitor. "You don't have anything. No family, no history, no origin. Which brings us to the interesting question, doesn't it? What *are* you?"

* * *

They came for him at midday and brought him up through corridors he knew — of course he knew them, he had carried trays down them for eleven years, he knew the palace the way a worm knows its apple — and into a room he did not know, which unsettled him more than the chains did. A high room in the oldest tower, round, with windows on every side and a table in the center bearing a single decanter of wine and two cups.

The king stood at the window with his back turned.

Oscar had seen His Majesty perhaps a hundred times, always at distance, always across the length of a banquet table, always slightly blue about the lips from the various things Oscar had tasted on his behalf. Up close, the king was smaller than his portraits and older than his coinage. The bottled years had done their work strangely: his skin was smooth as a young man's, but his eyes had gone ancient, and there was a translucence to him at the edges, as though he were becoming a watermark of himself.

"You may leave us," the king said, and the guards and the inquisitor withdrew, and then it was just the two of them and the wine and the enormous weight of everything unsaid.

"Do you know why I summoned you?" the king asked, still facing the window.

"They say I poisoned the realm's ledgers, Your Majesty."

"They say a great many things." The king turned. And Oscar felt the floor tilt, gently, the way the city had tilted when he first read the map — because the king's face, in this light, in this half-turn, was *familiar*. Not like a stranger's face. Like a face seen in a mirror that has been moved to a different wall. "Look at me properly, boy."

Oscar looked.

It was the jaw. It was the set of the ears. It was the particular way the brow came down on the left side, which Oscar saw every morning in his washbasin and had always assumed he'd inherited from nobody, because he had come from nobody, because he appeared in the world fully formed, like a mushroom after rain.

"Sit," said the king.

"I'd rather stand, Your Majesty."

"*Sit.*" And there was something in the voice — not command, exactly. Something rawer. The king sat himself, heavily, and poured the wine with hands that were not quite steady, and pushed a cup across the table. "Drink."

Oscar looked at the cup. Old habit rose in him like a tide: test the color, test the smell, breathe across the surface, watch for the oil-sheen of almond, the sweetness that hides bitterness—

"Your Majesty," he said slowly, "you're asking your food-taster to taste for *you*. In your own presence. From your own decanter."

"I am asking you to drink wine with me," the king said, "which is either perfectly safe or perfectly fatal, and which, I suspect, you already know is the former. Drink, and we will both learn something."

So Oscar drank.

The wine was excellent. Of course it was; it was the palace cellar. And nothing happened, because nothing ever happened, because in eleven years and some four thousand dishes, nothing had ever happened to him — and standing in that tower room, watching the king watch him with an expression of terrible confirmation, Oscar finally understood that the nothing had never been skill.

"How long?" the king asked softly. "How long have you been immune?"

"All my life, sire. I built the tolerance myself. Small doses, increased over years. Any taster can—"

"Any taster can build tolerance to arsenic," the king said. "To nightshade, yes. To the slow metals, certainly. But not to what was in that quail at the winter banquet. Not to widow's-tears distilled through Faded salt. That poison unmakes the boundary between a body and the rest of the world. Mortal flesh comes apart. Yours—" the king's mouth twisted, "—yours *refused*. Didn't it. You felt it try, and you felt yourself refuse, and you told yourself it was training."

Oscar said nothing. Because it was true. There had been a moment — he had never told anyone, had barely admitted it to himself — a moment at the banquet when the poison had reached for him like a hand reaching through water, and something in his blood had said, with a calm that was not his own: *no. This side.*

"Your mother," the king said, "was called Ysolt."

The name landed in the silent room like a dropped glass.

"She was of the Faded — truly, wholly, one of the people of the far shore, before the word *Faded* meant what it means now. She crossed for love of a man she should not have loved, and the crossing cost her, the way all crossings cost, and when the child came — when *you* came — she knew what you were. Half of here. Half of there. A creature of both shores, belonging wholly to neither." The king's voice had gone rough. "She could not raise you on either side. On her side, they would have unmade you for the crime of being partly mortal. On this side..." He gestured at the tower, the city, the whole sunlit world beyond the glass. "Well. You see what we do to things we fear."

"You're saying she gave me away."

"I'm saying she *hid* you. In plain sight. In the one place no one would look for a child of the Veil — a kitchen. Among servants, whose records no one keeps." The king laughed, and it was a horrible sound, dry as old paper. "Among food-tasters, boy. Where your body's one great secret would look like a *profession*."

The room seemed to recede. Oscar gripped the edge of the table. Twenty years of precautions rearranged themselves in his mind, entry by entry, ledger line by ledger line, and every one of them suddenly meant something different. The immunity wasn't discipline. The counting wasn't caution. The sense — the lifelong, bone-deep sense of being a man made of defenses against a world that might, at any moment, notice him — that wasn't temperament.

It was camouflage. Instinctive, inherited, *installed* camouflage.

"Who carried me across?" he heard himself ask. His voice sounded very far away.

"A Warden," the king said. "One of the covenant's own. She arranged it against the law of both realms — smuggling a half-Faded infant through the boundary was the single most forbidden act imaginable, and she did it anyway, because Ysolt was her friend, and because the covenant was already dying, and because—" he paused, choosing, "—because she believed the Veil would one day need mending by a hand that belonged to both sides of it."

"Lena," Oscar said.

The king went very still. "How do you know that name?"

Because she died in my arms, Your Majesty. Because she pressed a map into my hands and said *reforge it*, and I have been carrying her across the country like a debt I can't balance. Because everyone I meet seems to have known me longer than I've known myself.

"She's mentioned," Oscar said carefully, "in certain documents."

The king studied him for a long time. Outside the window, a gull cried, absurd and mortal, and the ordinary sound of it nearly undid him.

"There's more," the king said. "You'll want to sit for this part, and I'll want to stand." He rose, moving to the window, and when he spoke again his back was to Oscar, which was perhaps mercy. "Ysolt did not cross alone, and she did not love alone. The man she loved was of this shore. He was of this family." A pause, vast as a held breath. "My younger brother. Who died — officially — the same season you appeared in the palace kitchens. Officially of fever. Unofficially of grief, and of the Inquisition's questions, and of the general impossibility of loving across the Veil in a kingdom that bottles the Veil and sells it by the dram."

Oscar's hands had stopped gripping the table. He observed them distantly, as though they belonged to the ledger of someone else's life.

"So when His Majesty looks at me," he said at last, "and sees his brother's chin—"

"I see my brother," the king said simply. "Young again. Alive again. Standing in my tower refusing my wine until he's tested it." He turned, and his ancient-young face was wet. "Do you understand what I'm offering you, Oscar Sandoval? Whatever Corvane says you did, whatever is written in whatever book — I can unwrite it. You are not a servant. You never were. You are the last blood of my house besides my own. Take a name. Take a place. Take *my* protection, and let the map and the Well and the whole doomed covenant go to the devil who inherited them."

And there it was. The trap with no bars — the offer so warm it scalded. Family. Name. Home. Everything the mushroom-after-rain boy had never had, laid out on a table beside a decanter of excellent wine.

Oscar thought of a woman with silvered veins dying against him on the docks, her eyes staying open, accusing him. He thought of a stone in a chapel who spoke of betrayal in the covenant-tongue. He thought of Marisol's face when she talked about her brother, and of a girl of eleven who still believed in bedtime stories, and of nine days — eight now — until solstice.

Mostly, he thought of the ledgers. All of them. Every entry in every book he had ever kept, including the one no one else could read: the account of his own life, debits and credits, the long audit of a man made of precautions.

And he understood, with the calm of a man watching the final column sum itself, that the numbers had finally, at long last, added up.

"Your Majesty," he said gently, "may I tell you what's in your wine?"

The king frowned. "What?"

"Not the decanter. The *vials*. The years you've been drinking." Oscar rose, and found that his legs held, and that the strange new knowledge in his blood — the half of him from the far shore — was not horror at all, but orientation. He had spent his whole life tasting for other people. It was, apparently, time to taste for the truth. "Corvane cut them from the boundary between worlds. Every drop thins the Veil. Every year you buy, the realm next door leans a little harder on the door. At solstice, it comes down — and what comes through won't be your wife's ghost or your brother's memory, sire. It will be the things the Veil was built to hold. Corvane doesn't mean to save you. He means to be the toll-keeper on the only bridge left standing, and you — with respect, Majesty — you are his best customer and his biggest fool."

The silence stretched.

"You would speak to your king this way," the king said at last, "on your first day of having a king who is *family*."

"Yes," said Oscar. "Precisely because of it. Family tells the truth. Everyone else just keeps accounts."

Something moved behind the king's eyes — grief and rage and, buried deepest, a terrible flicker of relief, the look of a drowning man hearing that the water has a bottom.

Then the door opened, and the inquisitor returned, and his face was wrong.

"Sire," he said. "Forgive me. There's been a development." He swallowed. "The prisoner's lodging was searched again, on Lord Corvane's instruction. Under the floorboards. There was a map, Your Majesty. And letters. Sealed with the Warden's mark." His eyes slid to Oscar, and there was no borrowed-coat awkwardness in them now at all. "Lord Corvane says the boy has been the Veil's agent in the palace all along. He says the proof is in the dead woman's own hand. And he's requested — formally, with the council's assent — that the prisoner be transferred to guild custody. For questioning."

The king looked at Oscar. Oscar looked at the window behind him — the tower's outer wall, the leaded glass, and, three stories down, the shadowed seam of the old servants' stair that every member of the household staff knew and no member of the nobility had ever once remembered existed.

"Well," said Oscar, rising, and reaching for the decanter as though for a weapon. "Before anyone transfers me anywhere — Your Majesty promised me wine. Let me pour us both one more cup. We have a great deal of family business to discuss."

And beneath the table, out of the king's sightline, his fingers found the little vial of widow's-tears residue he had palmed from the inquisitor's own belt-pouch on the way up the stairs — because a man made of precautions is never searched quite thoroughly enough, and a taster's tongue, it turns out, knows a hundred ways to stop a heart, and at least one way, at the right dose, to merely *slow* one.

Eight days to solstice. One servant's stair. One king who might, just possibly, choose blood over ledgers.

Oscar smiled his kitchen smile, and began to pour.
