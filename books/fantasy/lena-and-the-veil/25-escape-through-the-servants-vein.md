## 25. Escape Through the Servants' Vein

They locked Oscar in a room with a very good lock.

He knew this because he had catalogued it. Entry four hundred and eleven, *Royal Palace, East Wing, third floor*, iron bolt-lock, Brammish make, three tumblers and a false one to punish the impatient. He had recorded it years ago in a private ledger he kept of every door in the palace — not for the guild, but for himself, because a man who survived by knowing things found comfort in knowing where the doors were.

The lock was excellent. The room, unfortunately for his captors, was a linen store.

And linen stores, like every room in that palace, had been built around the servants' passages, not the other way around. The architects drew their grand halls first and let the people who actually ran the house figure out the rest. Which meant that behind the shelving on the north wall — behind three hundred folded tablecloths and a stack of pillows that smelled faintly of lavender and someone else's ambitions — there was a panel. It looked like wall. It was not wall.

Oscar pressed his ear against it first. Habit. Then he counted the shelves, found the one with the loose bracket, and pulled.

The passage exhaled at him: dust, tallow, cold stone, and the particular smell of a house's hidden skeleton. Somewhere below, water moved through pipes. Somewhere above, an inquisitor was no doubt congratulating himself on a job well done.

"Entry four hundred and twelve," Oscar murmured to himself as he slid the panel shut behind him. "Linen store, east wing. Lock: excellent. Room: full of holes."

He allowed himself exactly three seconds of satisfaction. Then he started walking.

* * *

Here is what nobody in a palace understands until they need to understand it: the servants know everything.

They know which councilor drinks before noon because they carry the bottles up. They know which lady's maid reads her mistress's letters because she mentions the contents while changing the linens. They know the king has not slept well in eleven years, that the crown prince owes money to a man in Salt Row, and that the kitchens burn the bread on purpose every Thursday because the head cook's late husband hated Thursdays and she keeps the ritual like a candle.

Oscar had spent nine years being invisible in that house. Nine years of carrying trays through corridors that parted around him like water around a stone. Nine years of standing behind chairs, tasting dishes, nodding, dying a little inside at each course he was required to praise. And all that time, his feet had been learning the house the way a blind man learns a street — by repetition, by touch, by the thousand small negotiations between body and architecture that no map ever records.

The grand corridors belonged to the powerful. But the veins — the service passages, the dumbwaiter shafts, the stair that went from the wine cellar to the queen's old dressing room for reasons nobody remembered — those belonged to him.

He moved through them now with the ease of long habit, and it occurred to him, somewhere around the second turn, that this was the first time in his life his invisibility had ever felt like a gift rather than a sentence.

There was a version of Oscar Sandoval who had spent nine years resenting that. The boy who'd come up from nothing, who'd learned to taste poison by nearly dying of it, who'd stood behind so many chairs that he'd begun to feel like furniture himself. That Oscar would have walked these passages with his jaw tight and his ledger-heart balanced on the edge of bitterness.

But that Oscar had died somewhere on the docks, holding a stranger whose veins were turning to silver. The one who remained had bigger problems than resentment.

He had a deadline. He had a covenant. And he had, thanks to the king's own interrogation chamber, a piece of information worth more than everything in Corvane's vaults: the king's face wearing his own, aged forty years. Blood calling to blood across the Veil. Whatever Oscar was, the king knew it too — or suspected it, or feared it, which amounted to the same thing.

Which meant the vials were evidence. And evidence, in Oscar's experience, was the only currency that never lost value.

* * *

The problem, of course, was that the vials were in the king's private cabinet, behind a door guarded by two men who had never once, in Oscar's memory, both blinked at the same time.

Oscar crouched in the dark of the passage behind the royal bedchamber — a space barely wide enough for a man and his regrets — and did what he always did when facing an impossible problem. He made a list.

Item one: the guards could not be fought. Oscar had the upper-body strength of a man whose primary exercise was carrying soup.

Item two: the guards could not be bribed. They were palace guard, which meant they were paid adequately, watched closely, and terrified of the inquisitors currently crawling through the building.

Item three: the guards could, however, be *moved*. Because item three was the oldest truth in any great house, older than the throne itself:

Nobody guards a door during the changing of the wine.

Every second evening, the cellars sent up the king's evening bottle via the dumbwaiter in the west corridor, and the guards nearest the cabinet door took turns receiving it, checking the seal, and logging it — because even paranoia had paperwork. During those four minutes, one guard stepped away. Always the same one: Hobb, the younger, who had once told Oscar, in the confessional intimacy of three in the morning, that he dreamed of opening a pie shop in the country.

Oscar checked the passage's peephole — a knothole drilled by some servant dead two hundred years, bless his anonymous heart — and waited.

The palace breathed around him. Distant footsteps. A door closing. The particular silence of a household pretending it wasn't listening to itself.

Then: wheels on stone. The dumbwaiter's rattle. Hobb's boots receding down the west corridor, and the other guard shifting his weight, relaxing by exactly one degree.

One degree was enough. One degree was always enough; that was the whole secret of poison, too.

Oscar slipped out of the passage, crossed eight feet of open corridor with his heart hammering a rhythm he was fairly sure the guard could hear, and let himself into the king's bedchamber through the servants' door that didn't officially exist.

The cabinet stood beside the writing desk, walnut and brass, locked with a key the king wore on a ribbon around his neck.

Oscar stared at it. Then he laughed — silently, helplessly, a wheeze of air — because of course. Of course the last lock in this whole cursed errand would be the one he couldn't pick.

Except.

He had spent the afternoon in an interrogation chair, and before that a cell, and before that the king's presence, and in all that time he had done the thing he always did under stress, the thing that had kept him alive through nine years of poisoned dinners: he had observed. And he had observed that when the king grew agitated — when Oscar mentioned the word *Veil*, when the old man's face flickered into something younger and wronger — his hand went to the ribbon at his throat. Not to hide it. To *touch* it. The way a man touches a locket. The way a man touches something he is afraid of losing but also afraid of wearing.

A man afraid of a thing hides it. A man *torn* about a thing fidgets.

And a man who fidgets with a ribbon all day, every day, in a hot room, drinking wine —

Oscar went to the decanter tray by the fireside. Third glass from the left. He held it to the light, tilted it, and there, caught in the dried wine at the bottom, glinting like a fish scale: a single thread of gold embroidery. Ribbon-floss. Picked loose by restless royal fingers and swallowed with the evening's claret, as such threads always eventually were.

It wasn't the key. But it told him the key spent its days fighting a losing battle against its own mooring.

Which meant it might be found wherever a tired old king set down the things he couldn't bear to hold.

Oscar turned in a slow circle. Writing desk. Bed. Prayer stool — the king was not, by reputation, a praying man, but the stool's cushion bore the deep permanent dent of regular use, and men who prayed were men who confessed, and men who confessed needed somewhere to put the evidence of their sins.

He lifted the prayer stool's cushion.

The key lay in a shallow hollow worn into the wood, nestled there like an egg, warm still from the king's hands.

"Oh," Oscar said softly, to the empty room, "you poor old monster."

Because that was the thing he was only now beginning to understand about the king: the man was frightened. Frightened of dying, yes — everyone knew that, it was the whole engine of Corvane's trade — but frightened of something else besides. Frightened of the vials themselves. Frightened of what forty years of borrowed time had shown him in the mirror on the bad nights. A man does not hide his key under his prayers unless the two things have gotten tangled together in the dark.

Oscar took the key. He unlocked the cabinet.

Inside, on velvet, in a row like sleeping soldiers: seven vials of Veil-thread. Seven strips of the boundary of the world, cut thin and bottled, each one humming faintly against his palm like a struck bell heard through a wall. His half-Faded blood recognized them the way a dog recognizes thunder — a low, unhappy certainty that something natural had been broken.

He wrapped all seven in a linen napkin — stolen, appropriately, from the king's own sideboard — and paused over the eighth slot in the velvet. Empty. The vial the king had drunk that morning, in front of him, wearing Oscar's future face.

Seven left. Seven arguments, seven pieces of proof, seven small bottles of apocalypse that he intended to wave in front of every court and covenant that would listen.

He returned the key to its hiding place — under the prayers, where fear kept it — and was halfway back to the servants' door when the bedchamber's main door began to open.

* * *

Later, Oscar would maintain that what happened next was skill. In the honest ledger of his heart, he filed it under luck, cross-referenced with panic.

The main door opened, and Oscar did the only thing available: he stepped backward through the servants' door and pulled it shut, and the last thing he saw through the narrowing gap was the king himself, entering alone, moving toward the prayer stool with the shuffling gait of a man much older than his years.

Oscar froze in the passage, napkin-wrapped vials clutched to his chest, listening.

The king reached the stool. There was a long silence. Then a sound Oscar knew intimately, because he had made it himself a hundred times over ledgers that wouldn't balance: the small, defeated exhale of a man discovering something missing.

"He knows," the king said, to no one. His voice was cracked and strange. "Of course he knows. Corvane, you thieving spider. You've known since the docks."

Silence again. Then, quieter, in a voice that made the hair rise on Oscar's arms — because it was, for one uncanny moment, almost *his* voice, pitched down through forty years of wear:

"Come back, then. Whoever you are. Come back and tell me what I am."

Oscar pressed himself against the passage wall, heart slamming, and did not answer. Some questions you answered with words. Others you answered by surviving long enough to bring the receipts.

He fled down the vein of the house, past the wine cellar, past the laundry, past the kitchen where the head cook was burning Thursday's bread out of love and grief, and out through the delivery gate, where the night porter — old Tam, who owed Oscar nine years of small favors and one very large one involving a forged sick note — looked at him, looked at the linen bundle, and asked no questions whatsoever, which was the truest loyalty the palace had to offer.

"Evening, Mr. Sandoval," Tam said, lifting the bar. "Nice night for forgetting you were ever here."

"The nicest," Oscar agreed, and stepped out into the city.

* * *

The fog took him the way the docks always did — gently, completely, erasing the palace behind him as though it had never existed. For a moment Oscar simply stood in it, breathing, letting the terror drain out through his shoes.

He had done it. He was out. He had seven vials, a king's confession overheard, a face-against-face revelation that confirmed everything Lena's letters had claimed, and a body that had, once again, declined to die.

By any accounting, it was a triumph.

So why did his chest feel like a drawer that wouldn't close?

He knew why. He was a bookkeeper; he knew exactly why. It was the king's voice, following him out of that room like a debt. *Tell me what I am.* Because whatever the king was — monster, victim, aging thief drinking the world's border through a straw — he was also, apparently, family. Blood called to blood. And somewhere in the interrogation, beneath the fear, Oscar had felt the pull of it himself: the strange gravity of looking at an old man's face and seeing your own future staring back, hungry and hollowed.

Corvane would know by now that the vials were gone. By dawn, every enforcer in the guild and every inquisitor in the crown's employ would be hunting a food-taster with a linen bundle and a talent for vanishing. Oscar had perhaps hours. Maybe less.

He turned toward Marisol's safehouse in Tanner's Row, and had taken three steps when the fog ahead of him shifted, and a shape resolved out of it: slight, silver-veined, unmistakable.

Lena stood at the mouth of Fish Gate Lane, translucent as breath on glass, watching him with an expression he was beginning to suspect she practiced — that terrible mixture of apology and resolve.

"You," Oscar said. His voice cracked. "You're supposed to be at the Well. Or gone. Or *something*. Not standing in my fog judging my escape routes."

"I'm not judging," Lena's ghost said. "I'm warning. The vials are already missed, and Corvane doesn't send soldiers after them." Her eyes, dead and kind, fixed on the bundle against his chest. "He sends collectors. And Oscar — the one he's sending collects *memories*. Yours included."

Somewhere across the city, a bell began to ring. Not the hour. Not the alarm.

The solstice count. Eight days.

Oscar tightened his grip on the king's stolen sins and started to run.
