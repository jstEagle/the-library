## 26. Marisol's Choice

The fever came in waves, and each wave had a personality.

The first was polite. It arrived around dusk as a warmth in her shoulder, spreading from the wound like spilled tea soaking through linen. *Excuse me,* it seemed to say. *Pardon the intrusion.* Marisol had been stabbed before—courier work had its occupational hazards, mostly dogs and one memorable incident involving a jealous locksmith—and she knew this stage. This was the stage where you told yourself you were fine.

The second wave was less polite. It arrived sometime in the small hours and did not knock. It kicked the door down, took her boots hostage, and started rearranging the furniture of her mind. She dreamed of ledgers with pages that turned themselves. She dreamed of her brother, Tomás, standing in a workhouse yard with his back to her, and when she called his name he didn't turn, because somewhere in the machinery of the world his name had been sold off like scrap iron, and names were the only handles a person could be picked up by.

She woke—or surfaced, rather—in a room that smelled of beeswax and dried yarrow, on a bed that was not hers, under a roof that creaked in a dialect of old wood she didn't speak. The poisoner's house. Vessa's house. They had carried her here after the bridge, after the ambush, after the seal went into the river and the child went into hiding and Marisol's blood went into her boot.

Oscar was there. Of course Oscar was there. He sat in a chair dragged up beside the bed, reading by candlelight, and the book was upside down.

Marisol stared at him for a long moment, gathering her strength the way she gathered everything: carefully, and with an itemized list.

"Your book," she said. Her voice came out like gravel poured through a sieve. "It's inverted."

Oscar looked down, looked at the cover, looked at her, and turned the page anyway. "I know what it says," he said. "I've read it eleven times. I'm on principle now."

"What book is worth reading upside down twelve times?"

"Vessa's formulary." He set it aside. "I'm memorizing which plants kill you slowly versus quickly, in case anyone ever poisons me again. Statistically, someone will."

"That is the most depressing sentence I've ever heard a man say while keeping vigil."

"I find depression keeps the mind sharp." He leaned forward, and his face did something careful and kind that Marisol distrusted immediately, because kindness in her experience was usually an invoice waiting to be issued. "How much pain are you in? Honestly. On a scale. I'll write it down."

"You would."

"Numbers don't lie. People lie to numbers all the time, but numbers themselves are honest."

"The number is seven," Marisol said, "and if you write it down anywhere Corvane could someday audit it, I will haunt you specifically and personally."

"Noted. Seven. Down from nine last night, according to Vessa's chart."

"There's a *chart*?"

"There's a chart. There have been charts since we got here. Fever curves, poultice changes, your pulse taken at intervals so regular I could set a clock by them." He said it lightly, but Marisol heard the thing underneath it, the thing Oscar always kept underneath things: fear, filed neatly. "You've been talking in your sleep. You apologized to a ledger."

"It was a very good ledger."

"You also threatened it."

"It was also a very bad ledger. Both things can be true. I contain multitudes and most of them are bookkeeping."

He laughed—the real laugh, the rare one, the one that made him sound briefly like a man who had never tasted anything suspicious—and then the laughter faded into that watchful quiet of his, and Marisol knew what was coming, because she'd been carrying the knowledge herself since the bridge, since the moment her fingers had closed around the vial in the king's stolen bundle and thought, without meaning to, *this one.*

Oscar reached into his coat and brought out the vial.

It was small enough to hide in a closed fist. The glass was dark, almost black, but when he tilted it the liquid inside moved wrong—not like water, not even like oil, but like something that was remembering how to be liquid and resented the assignment. A single thread of silver coiled through it, slow as smoke. Veil-thread, cut and bottled. A year of somebody's life, or a year off of somebody's death; the guild had never been precise about which direction the arithmetic ran, and that imprecision had paid for a great many guild dinners.

"This is the last intact one," Oscar said. "I checked them all against my notes. Two cracked in the escape. One was already empty—I think the king drank it the night before we ran. This is the one left. And Vessa says—" he stopped, and Marisol watched him decide whether to finish the sentence, and watched him decide to finish it, because Oscar lied about feelings but never about facts—"Vessa says the wound has gone bad. Not badly bad. But the fever should have broken yesterday, and it hasn't, and there's a smell to it she doesn't like, and Vessa has smelled more deaths than anyone I've ever met, including the ones she caused personally."

"Charming woman."

"She's growing on me. Like the garden grows on people. Fatally." He held the vial out over the bed, not quite offering it, not quite withholding it. Just holding it in the space between them, where decisions live. "A drop in wine, three times a day, and the fever breaks by morning. That's what the vials do for the king. They hold him at the edge of his own decline and refuse to let him fall in. It would do the same for you. It would buy you the time to heal properly instead of healing fast and crooked."

Marisol looked at the vial the way she looked at any entry that didn't balance: with suspicion first, understanding second, and grief third, in that order, always.

"And the rite needs it," she said.

"The stone thinks so. The stone thinks a lot of things, loudly, at three in the morning, in a voice like a landslide arguing with itself." Oscar's mouth twitched. "But yes. Every thread we carry is a thread cut out of the Veil. Putting one back isn't just symbolic—it's material. It's literally returning stolen goods. The covenant was woven out of those threads once. We're short on weaving material, and the solstice is—" he counted silently, and Marisol watched his lips move, because even his counting was honest where other men's would be hidden—"six days away."

"So."

"So." He set the vial on the table beside the bed, within her reach, deliberately, exactly within her reach, because Oscar Sandoval did not make choices for other people; he made sure they had accurate information and then suffered visibly while they chose. "It's yours. Your blood is on your boot, your wound is in your shoulder, and your name is the one the fever is trying to erase. If anyone has a claim on a year of borrowed life, it's you. I only ask that you choose with your eyes open."

"My eyes are open. They've been open for two days. That's part of the problem."

* * *

Vessa came in at moonset with fresh bandages and the bedside manner of a woman who had poisoned four kings and regretted none of them.

She was older than Marisol had first taken her for—the exile years had weathered her like a fence post—but her hands were still beautiful, in the way that weapons are beautiful, in the way that Marisol supposed her own ledger-handwriting was beautiful: precisely, functionally, with a hidden edge. She unwrapped the wound without asking permission, because exiled royal poisoners did not ask permission, they informed.

"Hm," Vessa said.

"Hm," Marisol repeated. "In the extensive vocabulary of medicine, is 'hm' good or bad?"

"'Hm' is honest. Everything else I might say would be flattery, and I retired from flattery when I retired from court." She cleaned the wound with something that smelled like a forest fire and stung like a personal insult. Marisol gripped the bedframe and recited shipping rates to herself until it passed. "The flesh is fighting. That's good. The flesh is losing ground. That's bad. In six days it will either have won or lost, and I genuinely cannot tell you which, and I want you to appreciate how rarely I say that. I am a woman who once predicted a man's time of death to the quarter hour. His widow thanked me for the precision."

"Warmth. Real warmth. From the poisoner."

"I contain multitudes," Vessa said, "and most of them are grudges." She bound the wound tight, snapped her kit shut, and then paused, one hand on the bedpost, looking down at Marisol with an expression that Marisol recognized, because she wore it herself whenever she caught someone lying with numbers. "The boy told you what the vial does."

"He tells everyone everything. It's his worst quality. Well. Second worst. His worst quality is that he counts stairs."

"He counted our stairs?"

"Out loud. On arrival. There are forty-one."

"There are forty-one," Vessa confirmed, grudgingly impressed. "Good. Then he knows the house better than I do already, and I've lived here nineteen years." She straightened. "Listen to me, courier. I was at court long enough to watch men drink those vials. I mixed some of them myself, back when I still believed the crown's business was the kingdom's business. Do you know what a bottled year does to a body that isn't dying?"

"Preserves it?"

"Freezes it. There's a difference. Preservation is for jam. The king isn't being kept alive; he's being kept *paused*, like a letter half-written, and every year he drinks, the ink dries a little more." Her eyes went distant, the way they did when she talked about the parts of her past she hadn't finished hating yet. "And the body knows. The body always knows. It starts treating the borrowed time like a foreign substance. Fevers. Dreams. A restlessness that no physician could name, because it wasn't illness—it was the self, trying to evict the tenant." She looked at the vial on the table. "You're not dying. Not yet. You're infected, and infected bodies run fevers because fevers are how bodies argue. If you pour a cut year of the world's boundary into that argument, you will win the argument and lose something else. I don't know what. Nobody knows what. That's the trade. That's always the trade."

"Thank you," Marisol said dryly, "for the medical reassurance."

"I'm not here to reassure you. I'm here to make sure that whatever you choose, you choose it knowing the price tag. I spent a century silent because a covenant demanded it. Silence taught me one thing above all others: nobody should ever pay a debt they haven't seen itemized."

* * *

They left her alone with it, eventually. Oscar first—he resisted leaving, was physically removed by the combined efforts of Vessa and a yarrow poultice that needed changing downstairs—and then the night itself, which settled over the little house like a lid.

Marisol lay in the dark and audited her own life, because that was what she did instead of praying. Prayer asked for things. Auditing told you the truth about what you already had.

Assets: one working arm, one shoulder that was currently staging a rebellion, a head full of routes and faces and forged seals, a brother alive in a workhouse who did not know her name, a debt to a dead woman named Lena that Marisol had never managed to put a number on, and six days.

Liabilities: the same list, read aloud in a fever voice.

She turned the vial over in her fingers. It was warm from the table, or from her hand, or from itself—impossible things generated their own climate, she'd learned that much from the guild cellar, from the jar of courage that sweated in winter and the stolen Tuesday that was always, somehow, slightly damp.

One drop, three times a day. By morning the fever breaks. By week's end she'd be strong enough to run, to fight, to carry the child signatory over whatever ground needed covering. That was the case for drinking it, and it was a good case, and Marisol was honest enough with herself to admit it was a good case. The company needed her upright. The child needed guards who could walk. Oscar needed—well, Oscar needed someone to keep him from counting stairs at Corvane's enforcers, which would get them all killed.

And the rite needed the thread. Six days out. A covenant reforged from scraps, and here was a scrap, sitting in her palm, whispering its availability.

Her fever climbed as she deliberated, as though the infection itself had opinions about the outcome, and maybe it did; maybe the wound was Corvane's last agent inside her, doing its master's work, arguing for the vial in the only language it spoke. *Drink,* the heat said. *Drink and live. Everyone chooses the easy cure. No one will blame you. Blame is for survivors anyway.*

"No one will blame me," Marisol said to the ceiling. "That's exactly what worries me."

Because she had seen what blameless choices built. She had worked for the guild ten years, and the guild was nothing but blameless choices stacked in a cellar—every courier who carried a package without asking what was in it, every clerk who entered a figure without asking where it came from, every person who took the easy cure and let someone else absorb the cost. Tomás had been unmade by a bad batch, and the batch had been mixed by a man who was only following the formula, and the formula had been written by a man who was only filling an order, and the order had come from a man who was only serving a client, and nowhere, nowhere in that whole tidy chain, was there a single person willing to say *I chose this.*

Marisol had promised herself, in a workhouse yard, watching her brother's back, that she would be the person who said it.

So she lay in the dark and she did the arithmetic she trusted more than any prayer. The vial was one year—one thread—one piece of the boundary between everything human and everything Faded. Drunk, it bought her perhaps a season of strength. Returned, it became part of a wall that would stand longer than any season, longer than her, longer than the guild, longer probably than the crown, given how the crown was aging these days.

One season against forever. Even a fever couldn't make that math dishonest.

But oh, it would have been easy. That was the thing nobody warned you about sacrifice—that the hard part wasn't the pain. Pain was just weather. The hard part was that the alternative stood right there, gleaming, reasonable, defensible, one uncorked bottle away, and every rational voice you owned lined up behind it like witnesses for the defense.

Marisol sat up. It hurt. She did it anyway, slowly, the way she did everything, and swung her legs off the bed, and crossed the cold floorboards to the window, and stood there holding the vial up to the moon.

Inside the glass, the silver thread coiled and uncoiled, patient as anything that had been stolen and knew it would be stolen from again.

"I know what you are," she told it. "I've carried your cousins across three borders. I've signed for your cousins. I've lied to customs officers about your cousins." Her breath fogged the glass, and the thread seemed to lean toward the warmth of it, hungry, the way all the guild's inventory was hungry—hungry for hands, for veins, for use. "And I know what you cost. Not me. Everybody else. Always everybody else."

Somewhere below, the house creaked. Somewhere beyond the hills, the Veil hung thin as wet paper over a world that didn't know it was fraying. Somewhere in a workhouse, a man without a name slept through another night that didn't know he was in it.

Marisol thought about Tomás. She thought about how she'd get him back—not his body, she had his body already; his *name*, the handle the world used to pick him up. And she understood, with the terrible clarity that fevers grant and health takes away, that there was only one forge in all the world hot enough to re-stamp a soul, and it was six days east, and it was running low on metal.

"You're going home," she told the vial.

She corked it tighter. She wrapped it in cloth, three layers, the way couriers wrap things that matter, and she carried it back to the table and set it down where Oscar would see it first thing, because Oscar needed to know, and because—she admitted this to herself in the honesty of the small hours—she wanted a witness. Sacrifice without spectacle, yes. But a ledger entry, at least. Someone to write it down. That wasn't spectacle. That was just good practice.

Then she went back to bed, and the fever broke at dawn, not all at once but in pieces, like a wall coming down brick by brick, and Vessa pronounced her "hm, better," which from Vessa was a parade with trumpets.

Oscar found her awake near midday, gray-faced and grinning, and the empty space on the table where the vial had been, and the cloth-wrapped shape beside the water pitcher, untouched, sealed, labeled in her own hand: *RETURN TO SENDER.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he took out his little notebook, and wrote something, and showed it to her, because Oscar communicated important emotions exclusively through documentation.

It said: *Entry 1,204. One thread of the Veil, returned voluntarily by M. Reyes, courier, against medical advice and personal interest. Witnessed. No charge for the service. Some things aren't transactions.*

Marisol read it twice, and her eyes did something inconvenient, and she blamed the fever, which was nearly gone and therefore couldn't defend itself.

"Add a line," she said.

Oscar waited, pen ready.

"*Interest accrued: one brother, to be repaid in full.*"

He wrote it. He wrote it in his careful bookkeeper's hand, and beneath it, unasked, he added a date—the solstice—because Oscar never recorded a debt without recording its deadline.

Outside, the light was turning gold and strange, the way it did lately, the way it had begun doing everywhere, as though the sun itself were checking its watch. And down the road, hoofbeats—fast ones, too many of them, coming from the east, from the direction of the chapel where a stone man waited in the dark, talking to himself, counting down days in a voice like a landslide.

Oscar was already reaching for his coat. "That'll be the boy from the village," he said. "He was told to ride hard if anything changed." He paused at the door, and his face did the careful thing again. "Marisol—the stone wants to speak to us. All of us. He says he's remembered something. Something about what the rite actually requires."

Marisol pushed herself upright against the headboard, wound and all, and reached for her boots.

"Of course he has," she said. "Nobody remembers good news a century late."
