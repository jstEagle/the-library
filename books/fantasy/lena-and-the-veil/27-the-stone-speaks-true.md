## 27. The Stone Speaks True

The chapel had no roof worth mentioning—just a suggestion of rafters and a great deal of open sky—and so the dawn came in without knocking, the way dawn does in ruined places, and laid itself across the floor like a guest who intended to stay all day.

Oscar had not slept. He sat on a fallen pew with his back against the wall, watching the stone signatory the way one watches a pot that has already boiled over twice.

The stone's name was Aldous. It had been a man once—a warden, a lawyer of covenants, a signer of things—and now it was a statue kneeling at a broken altar with its hands folded as if in prayer, though Aldous insisted the posture was actually "a very good argument I was making when the petrification took." It could not move. It could talk. It talked a great deal.

"You're staring," said Aldous.

"I'm thinking."

"Thinking is staring with excuses. I've had two hundred years to learn the difference, and I'll tell you what I learned: everyone who stares at me is about to ask for something."

Marisol stirred on the pew opposite, where she'd been sleeping sitting up with her arms crossed like a woman guarding her own ribs. She woke the way she did everything—abruptly, suspiciously, and already halfway to an objection. "Is it lecturing again?"

"It's always lecturing," said Oscar.

"I can hear you," said Aldous. "I have excellent hearing. It's one of the few senses left to me, along with bitterness, which is less a sense than a climate."

From the corner near the altar came a small, sleepy sound: the child. Wren, she'd told them—Signatory Three, eleven years old, born after the covenant broke, carrying a birthmark on her wrist shaped like a keyhole. She had slept through most of Aldous's night-long discourse on betrayal, which Oscar privately considered the greatest mercy anyone in the chapel had received.

She sat up now, hair full of straw, and looked around with the unembarrassed frankness of children and cats. "Did the rock say anything useful while I was asleep?"

"Aldous," said Aldous.

"The *rock*," said Wren.

"He knows the covenant-tongue," said Oscar. "He's been teaching me."

"And what he knows," said Aldous, with the gravity of a man setting down a heavy load after carrying it too long, "is why none of you are going to enjoy the rest of this conversation."

The chapel went quiet. Even the wind through the missing roof seemed to lean in.

*

Aldous explained it the way he explained everything: slowly, precisely, and with the air of a man who had rehearsed his grievances until they'd worn smooth as river stones.

"When we signed the first covenant," he said, "there were four of us. Four names, freely given, woven into the Veil at the Well of Names. Not written. Not spoken once and forgotten. *Given.* Do you understand the difference, bookkeeper? A signature can be forged. A word can be retracted. But a name given wholly—" the stone jaw ground, a sound like a millstone remembering its work, "—wholly means it does not come back. You do not lend your name to the covenant. You surrender it. Forever. The Well keeps it. The Well *is* kept by it. That is the price of a boundary between worlds, and there is no discount for sincerity."

Oscar felt the words land somewhere below his ribs, in the place where he kept his ledgers—the inner one, the one no guild ever saw, where he recorded debts he could never repay and payments he could never take back.

"Given how?" he asked. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. He had spent his whole life training his voice to come out steady; it was the taster's first skill, to swallow something terrible and say nothing about it. "Spoken into the Well?"

"Spoken, surrendered, released. The name leaves you the way breath leaves you in winter—you see it go, and it does not return." Aldous's carved eyes could not move, but somehow they managed to rest on Oscar with terrible gentleness. "And here is the part you've already guessed, because you have Faded blood and blood is honest even when its owners aren't: the Well accepts only certain coin. Mortal names wash through it like water through a net. Half-Faded names—" the grinding pause, "—half-Faded names hold. They're the only thread strong enough to bind both sides of the Veil at once. Which is why Lena chose you. Which is why you're here. Which is why—"

"Don't," said Oscar.

"—which is why," Aldous continued, merciless as arithmetic, "the covenant will only take *your* name."

The silence afterward had texture. Oscar sat in it and examined it the way he examined any new substance placed before him: color, weight, smell, the faint bitter note underneath that told you what it would cost.

Marisol stood up so fast the pew rocked. "No. Absolutely not. There are four signatories—we have three seals, we have the girl, we have him. There must be another way. Rites have alternatives. Contracts have clauses. You were a *lawyer*, you miserable boulder, tell me there's a clause."

"There is no clause," said Aldous. "I wrote the original terms myself, and I wrote them well, which I regret daily. A boundary must be held from both sides by something that belongs to both sides. We four were mortal, and our names frayed—that's why the covenant weakened, why Corvane found threads to cut. A half-Faded name doesn't fray. It holds until the end of things." A pause. "It also cannot be taken. Only given. Freely. That part matters. The moment anyone tries to force it, the Well closes, and everything we've done becomes decoration."

"So he has to *want* to," said Marisol bitterly.

"So he has to want to," Aldous agreed. "Which is the cruelest clause of all, and yes, I wrote that one too."

Wren had gone very still, the way children go still when they understand more than the adults wish they did. She looked at Oscar—not with pity, which he could have borne, but with the grave attention of someone deciding whether a story was going to end sadly.

"You'd forget your name?" she asked.

"Worse," said Oscar, before he could stop himself. "Everyone else would forget it. I'd still know I'd lost something. I just wouldn't know what."

He heard himself say it and realized it was true, and that some part of him had known it since the fish gate, since Lena's silvered veins and her open accusing eyes, since the map. He had spent his life as a man made of precautions—immunities built dish by dish, exits memorized, receipts kept even for memories sold to ferrymen. And every precaution had been, all along, preparation for the one transaction that could not be hedged, receipted, or survived by tolerance.

He got up and walked out into the chapel's roofless morning, because there are conversations a man needs to have with the sky instead of with people.

*

The sky offered nothing useful. It was just blue, indifferent and enormous, with a few clouds going about their business.

Oscar stood among the nettles behind the chapel and did what he always did with unbearable information: he broke it into entries.

*Item:* the covenant requires a name given wholly, never returned.
*Item:* the only acceptable name is his.
*Item:* it cannot be forced, only chosen.
*Item:* nine days remain—or fewer now; eight and a waking.
*Item:* Corvane rides for the Well from one side, the crown's inquisitors from the other, and the Veil above them all is thinning like old cloth.

He noticed, distantly, that his hands were steady. They were always steady. That was the joke of his life, wasn't it—a body trained to survive poison, a mind trained to survive ledgers, and neither of them trained for this.

Marisol found him there. She didn't announce herself; she just appeared beside him the way she did, arms crossed, eyes doing their audit.

"You're itemizing," she said.

"Is it obvious?"

"You get a particular look. Like a man counting spoons at a funeral." She was quiet a moment. The wind moved the nettles. "I owed Lena a debt. You knew that. What you don't know is what it was. She pulled my brother's name out of a Corvane batch-list—one page, one line, Tomas, rendered unremembered—and she couldn't give him back, but she gave me the *truth* of him. She told me he existed when the whole world had agreed otherwise. Truth over loyalty, she called it. Her order's oldest rule, and the one nobody follows." Marisol looked out at the hills. "I've been paying that debt forward ever since, badly, one courier run at a time. So I'm not going to tell you it'll be fine. I'm a professional; I don't deal in fine. I'm going to tell you that whatever you decide, I'll write it down. All of it. Every entry. So that even if the Well takes your name, the ledger keeps it. Names die when no one writes them. As long as I'm alive and my ink holds, yours won't."

Oscar looked at her. Something in his chest did a thing he didn't have an entry for.

"That's not nothing," he said.

"No," said Marisol. "It isn't. It's the only thing I've got, and I'm giving it freely, which according to the boulder in there is the only currency that counts."

*

Inside, Wren had climbed onto the altar—climbed onto *Aldous*, rather, perched herself on the stone shoulder like a sparrow on a monument, which Aldous bore with the resigned dignity of two centuries' practice.

"—and then the ferryman said, 'that's not a memory, that's a rumor,' and Silt doesn't lie, so—" Wren stopped mid-story when she saw them come in. "Oh. Good. I was keeping him company. He gets sad when people walk away from him. He pretends he doesn't, but rocks are bad liars."

"I am standing right here," said Aldous, "and I contain multitudes, none of which are sadness."

Wren patted his stone head anyway. Then she turned to Oscar, and her face did the thing children's faces do when they've decided to be brave on purpose, which is worse and better than being brave by accident.

"If you give your name away," she said, "will you still be able to hear stories? About yourself?"

"I don't know."

"Because I remember things," she said simply. "That's my mark. That's what the keyhole's for. I was born after the covenant broke, and everything that fell out of the Veil when it tore—all the lost bits, the forgotten Tuesdays, the names people dropped—I catch them. They stick to me." She held up her wrist, the birthmark pale against sun-browned skin. "So if you lose yours, I'll keep it safe until you need it back. That's what keyholes are *for*. Things go in. Things come out."

"Not this thing," said Aldous quietly. "Not this time, little catcher. What goes into the Well stays in the Well. Don't promise the boy what the Well won't allow."

Wren's chin came up. For a moment she looked older than the stone. "Then I'll keep it anyway. Not to give back. Just so it exists somewhere besides nowhere. Somebody should remember him *being* somebody."

Nobody answered that. There was nothing in any ledger, guild or otherwise, that could balance it.

*

They spent the afternoon preparing, because preparation was the only prayer Oscar knew.

Aldous dictated the rite in the old covenant-tongue, and Oscar copied it out in his small, exact bookkeeper's hand, sounding each syllable until the stone grunted approval. Marisol inventoried their seals: the poisoner's, recovered at cost; the stone's own mark, which would burn free of him at the Well, he said, "finally, thank the deep places"; and Wren's birthmark, which needed no seal at all, being itself. Three signs. One name wanting.

Eight days. Two hundred miles of Faded country between them and the Well of Names, and Corvane's riders somewhere ahead or behind or both, and the crown's inquisitors crossing over through doors that should have stayed shut.

At dusk, Oscar sat alone at the broken altar and spoke to Aldous without preamble, because the stone hated preamble.

"If I refuse," Oscar said, "what happens?"

"The Veil tears on the solstice. The Faded come through—not as invaders, exactly. Worse. As weather. Reality stops holding its shape near the tear, and everything the guild ever bottled, borrowed, or stolen comes home to roost. Your king dies of his own bottled years. Corvane becomes either a god or a smear, depending on how the boundary falls. Millions of ordinary people discover that the world under theirs was never a story." The stone paused. "Also, I remain a statue, which frankly I deserve."

"And if I accept?"

"You become a doorway that remembers being a man. You stand at the Well forever, visible at thresholds, present wherever two things meet. Alive. Unnamed. Kept." Aldous's voice dropped into something almost tender, which from a statue sounded like gravel learning gentleness. "I won't dress it up, bookkeeper. It is a terrible bargain and a necessary one, and the fact that it must be *chosen* is the only dignity in it. I signed the first covenant with ink and pride and never once counted the cost, and look at me. You have the chance I didn't: to count it first. To say yes with your eyes open. Whatever you choose—" the grinding sigh, "—choose it as a man, not as a precaution."

Oscar sat with that a long time. Out past the broken walls, the first stars were coming up, and he thought about thresholds—doorways, dusk, the space between strangers—and found, to his considerable alarm, that some quiet room in him had already begun arranging the furniture.

Dread and acceptance sat down across from each other at his inner table and began their long negotiation, and Oscar, who had audited a thousand bargains, recognized the shape of this one: it was the kind where both parties were right.

"I'm not saying yes tonight," he said aloud.

"Good," said Aldous. "Yes said too fast is just fear wearing a brave coat. Say it slow, at the Well, with the Veil tearing overhead and everyone you love watching. Make it cost you everything to say. That's the only yes the Well respects."

*

He was nearly asleep on the pew when Wren shook him awake, urgent and whispering.

"Oscar. Oscar. The keyhole's humming."

He followed her to the altar. She held up her wrist, and in the starlight the birthmark-keyhole had begun to glow—a thin, patient gold, like light under a door.

And through the broken chapel entrance, far off across the dark hills, Oscar saw a second light answering. Far away, at the horizon's edge, in the direction of the Well of Names, something golden had opened in the night like an eye.

Or like a door.

And standing in front of it, small with distance, unmistakable even at two hundred miles—

someone was waiting for them.

"*That,*" said Aldous, in a voice that had not been afraid in two hundred years and was afraid now, "*is not supposed to be visible from this side.*"
