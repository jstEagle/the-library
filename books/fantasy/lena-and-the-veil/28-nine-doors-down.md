## 28. Nine Doors Down

**Oscar**

The first door was a wardrobe.

Not a grand one—a peasant's wardrobe of warped pine that stood alone in a field where no house had ever been, its doors swinging open on darkness that smelled of rain on hot stone. Oscar looked at it for a long moment before stepping through, because he had spent his whole life checking things twice, and because a wardrobe in an empty field was exactly the sort of thing his ledgers would have flagged with a red line.

"You're counting them," said Marisol behind him. Not a question. She'd known him long enough now to read the movement of his lips.

"I'm counting everything," Oscar said. "Doors. Hours. People. Especially people."

"And when you run out of numbers?"

He stepped through into the twilight market he remembered from months ago—the stalls gone dark now, the vendors fled, the lanterns burning low and blue. "Then I'll start over," he said, "and hope I'm wrong the second time."

Behind them, far off across the hills of the Near Faded, the golden eye at the horizon did not blink.

* * *

**Marisol**

Marisol kept her own count, but she counted differently. Oscar counted doors; she counted debts.

The poisoner owed Lena nothing—that debt had been paid in seals and refusals—but Marisol owed the dead woman a great deal, and debts were the only currency Marisol had ever trusted more than coin. So she walked at the rear of their small company with her hand never far from her knife, watching the way you watch a ledger for entries that don't belong.

The girl—Wren, eleven years old, birthmark glowing faintly through her sleeve like a coal wrapped in cloth—walked in the middle, where everyone could protect her and no one had to admit they were doing it. The stone signatory rode in a handcart. This was not a joke anyone had planned. It had simply become necessary, and Marisol had learned that most of the world's absurdities worked that way.

"Aldous," she said quietly, as they walked. "You've been quiet."

"I am a rock," said Aldous. "Quiet is my native tongue."

"You've been quiet the way a man is quiet when he's doing arithmetic."

A pause. The cart wheels creaked.

"The eye at the Well," Aldous said at last. "Two hundred years ago, when the covenant was whole, the Well could not be seen from this side. It was not hidden. It simply *wasn't there*, the way tomorrow isn't there. Something has opened it early. Something is drawing through."

"Corvane."

"Corvane is a merchant," said the stone, with the contempt only a two-hundred-year-old betrayal can season properly. "Merchants wait for doors to open. Someone else built the hinge."

Marisol filed that away in the ledger behind her eyes, under *questions I cannot afford yet*, and walked faster.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren counted the doors too, but hers counted themselves. Every time they came within twenty paces of one, the keyhole-shaped mark on her wrist warmed, and she could feel which way the door went—not left or right, but *toward*, the way a compass feels toward north if north were something you loved.

"This one's a good one," she announced, standing before what appeared to be a hollowed-out tree with a door nailed into its trunk. The door was painted green, once. There was a knocker shaped like a fish.

"How do you know?" asked Oscar, who asked this every time, and whom she suspected asked it mostly so she'd have something to answer.

"It hums," Wren said. "Bad doors buzz. Like a wasp in a jar." She touched the fish knocker. "This one hums like my grandmother's kettle."

Oscar wrote something in his little book. Wren had decided some time ago that this was how he prayed.

She opened the door onto a stairway going down through cloud, and the company went through, and somewhere behind them—far enough back that it was only a sound, like a giant turning a page—something began to follow.

* * *

**Oscar**

The second door was a well shaft without a well. The third was the back of a mirror in a house where nobody lived. The fourth was a gap between two gravestones that was wider inside than out, and Oscar did the arithmetic of that with his eyes closed and decided some numbers were better left unbalanced.

By the fifth door he understood the pattern, and understanding it made his stomach drop the way it dropped the first time he'd tasted arsenic and felt his own blood shrug it off.

"They're getting closer together," he said.

Marisol glanced back. "Meaning?"

"In the city, the doors were streets apart. Out here—" he gestured at the twilit country rolling past, hills the color of dusk held forever, rivers running politely uphill—"they're minutes apart. The geography is compressing. As if the Faded is..." He searched for the bookkeeper's word and found it and hated it. "*Consolidating.* Like accounts being called in."

"Or like a net," said the poisoner, who had spoken perhaps ten words since the chapel, "being drawn shut around a school of fish."

Nobody answered that, because nobody could improve on it.

They went through the sixth door—a cellar hatch in the middle of a meadow—and came out on a ridge overlooking a valley, and stopped, all five of them, because the valley was full of torchlight.

Not campfire light. Processional light. A column of figures moving along the valley floor in strict formation, armored, bannered, and even at this distance Oscar knew the banners: the crowned chalice of the royal inquisitors. The king's men. On *this* side of the Veil.

"That's impossible," Marisol breathed.

"No," said Oscar quietly. "It's just expensive. Somebody bought them passage." He thought of vials cut from the boundary itself, of years bottled and sold, and felt the old familiar nausea that was no poison at all. "And I think I know who."

* * *

**Inquisitor Hale**

Hale had served the crown for thirty years and had burned, broken, and buried a great many impossibilities, and he had a professional rule about them: *an impossibility is just a crime you haven't found the paperwork for.*

Crossing the Veil had been paperwork. Expensive paperwork—three vials of the grey liquid, poured on the ground in a ring while the guild's man recited words that made Hale's teeth ache—but paperwork nonetheless. And now here he was, on the wrong side of the world, hunting a food-taster, a courier, a child, a woman who smelled of nightshade, and, according to his informant, a talking rock.

He had expected to feel foolish. He did not. He felt *justified*, which was worse and better.

"They're on the ridge," said his lieutenant, pointing. "Five figures. They've seen us."

"Of course they've seen us. We're four hundred men with torches." Hale lowered his spyglass. "We are not meant to catch them tonight. Read your orders again."

The lieutenant read. His face did something complicated. "'Press them toward the Well,'" he recited. "'Do not overtake. Do not engage except in extremity.'"

"We are the shepherd's dog," Hale said, "and someone else is the shepherd. Move them along. Torch high, drums steady. Let them run."

Because that was the thing about sheep, Hale thought, as the drums began. Sheep always believed they were escaping.

* * *

**Marisol**

They ran.

The seventh door was a coffin lid standing upright in a peat bog, and Wren touched it and flinched and said, "This one *buzzes*," and the poisoner put her shoulder against it anyway and said, "Buzzing means watched, not barred," and they went through into a corridor of black glass where their reflections lagged half a step behind them, and Marisol did not look at her reflection because her reflection was limping slightly and she refused to learn that about herself yet.

The wound from the toll bridge had gone from ache to argument. She kept it to herself. She was very good at keeping things to herself; it was practically her trade.

But Oscar fell into step beside her, the way he did, unobtrusive as a servant, and said nothing for a hundred paces, and then said, "You're favoring the left leg."

"I'm favoring the left leg because the left leg deserves it."

"Mm." He produced, from some infinite pocket of his coat, a small twist of paper. "Chew this. Don't ask what's in it. You'll walk better and dream badly."

She chewed it. It tasted like licorice and regret. Within fifty paces the argument in her leg subsided to a grumble, and she hated how grateful she was, and hated more that he'd noticed at all.

"When did you see?" she asked.

"The coffin door. Your reflection told on you."

"I didn't look at my reflection."

"I know," said Oscar. "That's why it told on you."

* * *

**Aldous**

Aldous had been a signatory of the old covenant, a warden of the western marches, a man of flesh who had argued theology with kings and won twice. He had then been stone for two hundred years, which taught patience, and carried in a cart, which taught humility, and both lessons were currently being tested.

Because the eighth door was a door he knew.

It stood alone on a hill of white grass: oak, iron-banded, carved with nine names in a ring. *Their* door. The council door of the original covenant, the one through which the nine signatories had come and gone for a century of vigilance. He had walked through it the day the covenant was signed, young and proud and certain.

It should have rotted two hundred years ago. Nothing in the Faded lasted unless it was *kept*, and no one had kept anything of theirs.

Someone had kept this.

"Do not touch it," Aldous said, and his voice cracked the air like frost.

The company stopped. Wren's hand hovered an inch from the iron band, her birthmark blazing gold now, bright enough to cast shadows.

"Why not?" she whispered.

"Because doors do not survive untended," said Aldous, "and this one has been tended. Because we were meant to come this way—we were *always* meant to come this way, the map saw to that—and whoever kept this door wanted us to arrive rested, on schedule, at the place of their choosing." The stone's voice dropped to gravel. "Children, we are not fleeing through these doors. We are being *delivered*."

Silence, except the wind in the white grass, which sounded almost like pages turning.

Behind them, down the slope, torchlight crested the ridge they'd crossed an hour ago. Ahead, past the hill, the golden eye of the Well filled half the sky now, close enough that they could see it was not an eye at all but a doorway seen edge-on, a sliver of unbearable morning.

Nine doors, Aldous had promised them, from chapel to Well. Eight stood behind.

"One door remains," he said. "And we must go through it, because there is nowhere else. But hear me: whatever waits on the other side has paid for our arrival in advance. Walk through as debtors, not guests. Expect the bill."

* * *

**Corvane**

Corvane stood before the Well of Names and admired his own handwriting.

Not the forged ledgers—those were done their work, tucked in the inquisitors' evidence chests, hanging a dead taster in absentia. These were new books, forty of them, arranged on a reading stand of black wood before the Well's threshold: blank ledgers, waiting, their covers stamped with a seal no guild had ever used, because no guild had ever governed two worlds.

The Well itself was... changing. For two centuries it had been a stillness, a silence, a place where names went to sleep. Now it stood open like a struck bell, pouring gold light up into the torn sky, and through the tear overhead he could see the other realm's stars sliding sideways, and he found, to his mild surprise, that he was not afraid.

Fear was for men with something to lose. Corvane had spent forty years converting everything he had into leverage. Leverage could not be lost. Leverage could only change hands.

"They're at the eighth door," said the ferryman beside him.

Silt had come up from his river for the occasion, dripping, patient, holding his pole like a man holding a pen. Corvane had paid him handsomely and knew it bought him nothing but delivery. Ferrymen ferried. That was the whole of their ethics.

"The taster will bargain at the last door," Corvane said. "He's a bookkeeper. Bookkeepers always believe the final entry can be negotiated."

"And if it can't?"

Corvane turned the first blank page of the first blank ledger. Somewhere in the hills, a drumbeat pressed a handful of frightened sheep toward him, and among them, whether he knew it or not, came the only freely given name in either world.

"Then," Corvane said pleasantly, "we proceed to collection."

* * *

**Oscar**

The ninth door was a door Oscar had known all his life.

It was the pantry door of the palace kitchens—oak, iron-hinged, scarred by two hundred years of hurrying cooks. He had slipped through it ten thousand times. He had hidden behind it as a boy. It stood now on a hilltop of white grass under a torn sky, utterly alone, utterly familiar, humming like a kettle.

"Oh," said Wren softly, looking at his face. "Oh, it's *yours*."

Every eye turned to him. Marisol's hand found her knife out of pure habit. The poisoner's expression did something rare and almost gentle.

"It's not mine," Oscar said. His voice came out steady, because he had trained it to, because he was a man made of precautions and this was the last precaution failing. "It's the palace's. It's everyone's. It's—"

It was the door he'd come through. He knew it the way you know a face in a dream. He had been carried through it as an infant, wrapped against a smuggler's chest, half-Faded and screaming, and the door had hummed for him then too, kindly, like a kettle, like a grandmother, like home.

Behind them: drums, closer now, and torchlight, and the inquisitors' patient shepherd-dog press. Ahead: the golden threshold, and the small silhouette before it that might have been a man or might have been a hinge.

Aldous spoke from the cart, gently, for a stone. "Debtors, not guests."

Oscar looked at the door that had been waiting for him his entire life without ever telling him, and understood, finally, why the map had chosen him, why Lena had died pressing it into his hands, why every ledger he'd ever kept had been leading here—to the one account that could only be settled in person.

He put his hand on the handle.

It was warm.

It opened before he pushed.
