## 30. The Answer Is No

The Well of Names did not look like a well.

It looked like a wound in the geography of the world—a circle of pale stone at the bottom of a valley that existed in neither realm entirely, where the sky was the color of a bruise deciding whether to heal or spread. The water, if it was water, lay perfectly still and perfectly dark, and when Oscar looked into it he had the distinct impression of being looked back at by something patient. Something that kept accounts.

He would have found that comforting, under other circumstances. He was, after all, a man who trusted things that kept accounts.

The trouble was the company.

Corvane's enforcers held the eastern rim of the valley—two dozen of them in grey livery, crossbows loaded, faces arranged into expressions of professional indifference. On the western rim stood the crown's inquisitors in their bone-white coats, led by Inquisitor Halloway, whose smile Oscar had last seen across an interrogation table. And between them, at the Well's edge itself, stood Master Corvane, holding the forged ledger under one arm like a man carrying a hymnal to his own coronation.

Behind Oscar: Marisol, fever-bright-eyed despite the vial she'd refused to drink, her arm bound and her jaw set. The child signatory—Nell, though she'd only told them her name twice and made them promise to forget it properly until it mattered—clutching the poisoner's seal in both fists. And the stone signatory, which Marisol and Oscar had hauled the last three miles on a handcart, because even miracles deserve practical transport.

"You see how it is," Corvane said pleasantly. He always said things pleasantly. It was his most expensive skill. "Two armies, one Well, nine minutes to solstice by my reckoning. And you, Oscar, standing in the middle with a handcart." He smiled. "You were always better with numbers than with exits."

"I was always better with numbers than with people," Oscar corrected. "Exits I've become quite good at lately."

"Have you?" Corvane opened the forged ledger. "Because your exits are all accounted for. Every room you've rented. Every passage you've bought. Every entry in this book, in your own hand, dating back six years. When the Veil fails—and it will fail, Oscar, in minutes—the Faded come through, and both these armies will need someone to blame. Someone whose guilt is already written down." He patted the ledger fondly. "I can offer you partnership instead. Two worlds, one gateway, and we write the terms ourselves. You'd never taste another quail as long as you live."

"That *is* tempting," Oscar admitted.

Marisol shot him a look that could have curdled milk at forty paces.

"But," Oscar went on, "you've made one error. A bookkeeping error."

Corvane's smile thinned by a degree. "I don't make errors."

"You do," Oscar said. "You've been making them for years, and I've been reading them for years, because that's what I do. You forged my entries beautifully—the ink matched, the pressure of the pen matched, even my little habit of crossing sevens, which I flatter myself very few people notice. But you never understood *why* I keep ledgers the way I keep them."

"And why is that?"

"Because a ledger isn't a record," Oscar said. "It's a promise. Every line says: *this happened, and someone will answer for it.*" He reached into his coat and drew out the true ledger—his ledger, the one he'd copied from the guild house vault page by page while Corvane slept above him, believing his bookkeeper tame. "You wrote a book designed to hang me. I wrote a book designed to do exactly what books are for."

"And what is that?" Corvane asked, and there was something new in his voice now. Not fear. Corvane didn't frighten. But attention. The attention of a man realizing the mouse has brought its own trap.

Oscar turned to face the valley—not Corvane, not the enforcers, not the inquisitors. Everyone. The stone signatory at his back seemed to lean forward, which was impressive for a man who hadn't moved in a century.

"It's a witness," Oscar said. "And it speaks now."

* * *

He began at the beginning, because that was where honest books began.

"Entry one," Oscar read, his voice carrying in the strange acoustics of the valley, off the pale stone and the still dark water. "Bottled memory, vintage unknown, taken from a fisherman of the Saltrow district without consent or payment. Purchased by a crown minister to forget a debt. The fisherman's name was Aldous Wren. He spent two years looking for a Tuesday he could no longer remember losing."

Somewhere on the eastern rim, an enforcer shifted.

"Entry fourteen. Borrowed years, cut from the boundary itself—Veil-thread spun into vials. Sold to the aging and the afraid. Each vial thins the covenant between realms. Each customer signs no receipt, because the receipt would say: *I have purchased my extra decade from the skin of the world.*"

"Entry thirty-one," Oscar continued, and here his voice did not waver, though he wanted it to. "One batch of years, poorly cut, sold to a family in the Tanners' Quarter. Four buyers. Three survived the transaction. The fourth—" He looked at Marisol. She nodded once, small and fierce. "The fourth was not killed. Killing leaves a body, and bodies leave grief, and grief leaves names. No. He was *unrendered*. Struck from every memory that held him, every record that named him, every door that knew his knock. His mother woke one morning with milk in the icebox and no idea whose it was."

On the western rim, Inquisitor Halloway had stopped smiling.

"The victim's name," Oscar said, "was Tomás Ferran. Age nineteen. He liked—his sister tells me he liked—carving little boats. He is alive today in a workhouse on Cordwainer Street, sweeping floors for men who cannot see him, because the man who sold the batch that unmade him is standing at this Well holding a ledger he wants you all to believe is mine."

"Careful, Oscar," Corvane said softly.

"No," Oscar said. "That's rather the point of the next several minutes."

* * *

He read for a long time.

He read the entries about the doors that opened onto other people's houses, and the names that were sold to open them. He read about the widow who had pawned her wedding day and couldn't remember why she owned a dress she couldn't account for. He read about the child's laughter bottled and sold to a magistrate with insomnia, and how the child had grown quiet afterward, "the way a room goes quiet after a theft, even if the thief took nothing you can name."

He read the entries Corvane had tried to hide—the ones in cipher, which Oscar had broken during three sleepless nights using nothing more exotic than frequency analysis and spite. He read the payments to the crown's own ministers. He watched, as he read, the bone-white ranks of the inquisitors begin to glance at one another, because some of those names belonged to their colleagues, and possibly to their superiors, and truth does not check whose tent it blows into.

He read the entry about himself—or rather, about the fiction of himself. Six years of fabricated crimes in his own handwriting, purchased silence, planted evidence, a scaffold already measured for his height.

"And finally," Oscar said, "the master entry. The purpose beneath all of it. The plan to let the covenant fail, to stand alone at the broken gate between worlds, and to sell passage—forever—to whoever can pay. Not to mend the Veil. To *own* the hole in it."

He closed the ledger.

"That's the whole book," he said. "Every theft. Every unmade person. Every year stolen from the world's own skin. I've kept accounts since I was a boy tasting poison so that kings could eat safely, and I will tell you what I know about poison: the dose makes the truth. A lie in small amounts keeps everyone comfortable. In large enough quantities, it kills. Master Corvane has been dosing two realms for twenty years." He held up the ledger. "Here is the antidote. Read it yourselves, if any of you still remember how."

Silence, of the enormous kind.

Then Corvane laughed. It was a good laugh, warm, genuinely amused, and it frightened Oscar more than anything else that had happened all day.

"Oh, *beautifully* done," Corvane said. "Truly. The little speech, the trembling sincerity, the dead boy's name deployed at the perfect moment—you've been practicing. But consider your audience, Oscar." He gestured at the enforcers, at the inquisitors. "Soldiers follow paymasters. Inquisitors follow orders. Nobody follows a bookkeeper."

"He's right about that," called a voice from the eastern rim, and there was some scattered laughter.

But on the western rim, Inquisitor Halloway had drawn out her own notebook and was writing. Fast. Oscar recognized the posture; it was his own. And when she looked up, she did not look at Corvane. She looked at the ledger in Oscar's hands the way a hungry person looks through a bakery window.

"Inquisitor," Corvane said, warning in the pleasantness now.

"My commission authorizes me to investigate corruption in service of the crown," Halloway said mildly. "It doesn't specify whose." She raised her voice. "If half of what that book contains is true, then certain ministers of His Majesty's government are going to have a great deal of explaining to do, and I intend to be present for all of it."

"You'd take the word of a servant over mine?"

"I'd take the word of a *ledger* over anyone's," Halloway said. "It's the only witness that never blinks."

Corvane's pleasantness finally, fully, fell away. What remained underneath was older and colder, and Oscar understood suddenly that he had never actually seen Corvane before—only the shopfront.

"Then we're past negotiation," Corvane said. "Enforcers. Take the ledger. Take the girl. Kill the rest."

* * *

Later—much later, in the telling and retelling—people would argue about what happened in the next four seconds.

What Oscar remembered was this: the crossbows coming up along the eastern rim, the inquisitors drawing steel along the western, and Nell—the eleven-year-old with the birthmark-key on her wrist—stepping forward past his knee and pressing the poisoner's seal against her birthmark, and the seal *burning*, blue-white, and the Well answering.

The still dark water shivered.

The stone signatory spoke for the first time in a hundred years, and its voice was the sound of a mountain clearing its throat: "*Names, girl. It wants names.*"

"I know," Nell said, steady as Sunday. "I've got mine ready. Has everybody else?"

Marisol drew her knife and pressed it to her wounded palm without hesitation, without ceremony, the way she did everything. "Ready."

And Oscar—Oscar put the true ledger down on the pale stone at the Well's edge, gently, the way you set down something you love, and put his hand flat upon it.

"Not yet," he said quietly, to the Well, to the stone, to the whole listening dark. "There's one more entry. It isn't written yet. Give me until the end of this, and I'll dictate it myself."

The water stilled again. Waiting. It was, he thought, the most professional thing he had ever encountered.

Then Corvane's enforcers fired the first volley, the inquisitors charged, the sky overhead split with a sound like wet paper tearing from horizon to horizon, and the Battle at the Well of Names began.

Oscar picked up the ledger, tucked it inside his coat, over his heart, where books belong—

—and ran toward the fight, because someone had to keep the accounts, and everyone else was busy.
