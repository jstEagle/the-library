## 33. The Name Given

The Well of Names did not look like a well.

Oscar had expected—had braced himself for—a shaft of black water, a mouth in the earth, something with teeth to it. Instead it was a circle of pale stone no wider than a kitchen table, set into the floor of a cavern that had no ceiling he could find when he looked up. The stone was smooth as the inside of a spoon, and where its center should have been there was simply *depth*—a stillness that was not dark and not light and not empty either, but full, the way a held breath is full.

Around it stood everything they had gathered.

Marisol, gray-faced from her wound, one hand pressed to her side, the other holding the poisoner's seal so tightly the edges had bitten crescents into her palm. The child signatory—Nell, though she'd only told them her name an hour ago, shyly, as if it were a gift she wasn't sure they'd want—stood with her birthmark-key held out over the stone, her small face solemn with the terrible importance of being eleven and correct about bedtime stories.

And behind them all, filling the cavern's entrance like weather, came Corvane.

He did not run. That was the worst of it. He walked, unhurried, his boots ringing on stone that had never known boots, and his voice arrived before him, warm as a ledger's leather cover.

"Oscar." A pause, savoring. "My bookkeeper."

*Not my bookkeeper,* Oscar thought. But aloud he said nothing, because his mouth had gone dry in the specific way it went dry before a tasting—the body's old warning that what came next might kill him.

* * *

There had been no time for speeches on the road. Nine doors down, Silt had called it, and Silt had been right in the way ferrymen are right: the geography had compressed around them like a fist closing. Doors that should have taken days had taken minutes. The Faded realm seemed to *want* them at the Well—or wanted something at the Well—and had hurried them along its twilight roads with the gentle impatience of a tide.

But Corvane had hurried too. And the crown's inquisitors, with their iron lamps and their warrants signed in the king's own shaking hand, had come through from the other side of the Veil, so that now the cavern held every thread of the story at once: guild and crown, mortal and Faded, and in the middle, one former food-taster standing at a stone circle with nothing left to offer but himself.

"You could have taken my offer," Corvane said. He stopped ten paces away, close enough that Oscar could see the fine stitching of his collar, the faint silver dust on his cuffs—Veil-thread residue, weeks of harvesting worn into the fabric like flour on a baker. "Two worlds, Oscar. Two ledgers. You'd have kept the accounts of everything. You'd have been the most important man who ever lived, and no one would ever have known, which I know—" and here his smile turned almost tender, almost kind, "—is how you prefer it."

That was the trap of Corvane, Oscar thought. Not the cruelty. The *accuracy*. He offered you exactly the thing you secretly wanted, wrapped in exactly your own handwriting.

Behind him, Oscar heard Marisol shift her weight, readying herself for something violent and probably futile. He raised one hand slightly. *Wait.*

"Master Corvane," Oscar said, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out. Taster's training. You never let the cook see you flinch. "Before anything else happens—I'd like to read an entry."

Corvane's eyebrows rose, genuinely amused. "An entry."

"The guild keeps two ledgers. You know this. The false one for the crown's eyes, the true one for yours." Oscar reached into his coat and drew out the book he had copied in the guild house, page by page, in the long sleepless nights after he'd learned what Veil-thread actually was. "This is neither. This is the third ledger. Mine."

"Oscar," Corvane said gently, the way one speaks to a servant who has picked up a sword, "whatever you think you've recorded—"

"Entry four hundred eleven." Oscar opened the book. His voice took on the cadence of the counting-house, flat and clear, each word a coin placed precisely on a scale. "One Tuesday, stolen from a cooper named Aldous Finch, sold to a merchant who wished to skip his daughter's funeral. The cooper spent the rest of his life unable to say why Tuesdays frightened him. Entry four hundred ninety. Six years, bottled, drawn from the Veil itself, sold to His Majesty. Cost: the boundary between worlds, thinned by one part in nine hundred. Entry five hundred two. One brother, unremembered. Name struck from every record, every memory, every door. Surviving evidence: one sister, who remembers anyway, because love keeps better books than grief does."

Marisol made a small sound behind him. He did not turn to look at her. If he looked at her he would stop, and he could not stop.

"Entry five hundred thirty," Oscar continued. "The covenant of the Veil, broken deliberately, by its own steward, for profit. Estimated damages: both worlds." He looked up. "Shall I go on? There are nine hundred more entries. I have time. Or rather—" and here he felt something loosen in his chest, something that might have been laughter, "—I don't. None of us do. Which is rather the point."

* * *

The inquisitors had gone very quiet. Their iron lamps burned low, and in that low light their faces looked uncertain, men who had come to arrest a bookkeeper and found themselves attending an audit of heaven.

Corvane's amusement had cooled by several degrees. "You always were sentimental about the inventory," he said. "It's why I liked you. It's why I chose you." He spread his hands. "But sentiment doesn't reforge covenants, Oscar. Blood does. Marks do. Seals do. And even if you had all of those—" his gaze flicked, quick and hungry, to Nell's outstretched hand, to the seal burning in Marisol's grip, "—the covenant wants a name. A name given freely. Wholly. Never returned." He smiled. "You've read the requirements. You know what 'wholly' means. Are you prepared to stand at that stone and unmake yourself? Because I've watched you for years, my boy, and I can tell you what you'll do. You'll hesitate. Men made of precautions always hesitate. And in that hesitation, I will take what's owed."

And that was true. That was entirely, precisely true, and Oscar felt the truth of it settle over him like cold water.

Because here was the thing he had not told them. Here was the thing he had carried up the last nine doors like a stone in his boot:

His name was not Oscar Sandoval.

It never had been. Somewhere across the Veil, in the first days of his life, another name had been spoken over him—a crossed-Veil name, half-Faded, the only coin the Well would accept—and then folded away, hidden, buried under twenty years of ledgers and precautions and the careful, careful life of a man who tasted other people's food so they would not die. Lena had known it. Lena had chosen him for it. And the name lived in him still, coiled under his ribs like a second heartbeat, waiting.

All he had to do was speak it.

All he had to do was let it go, wholly, forever, and become—

He hesitated.

Of course he hesitated. He was a man made of precautions. He had built his whole self out of them, brick by brick: taste everything twice, trust numbers above people, keep receipts, keep copies, keep your real name where no one can reach it. And now the last precaution of his life stood between him and the stone, whispering the only argument it had left:

*If you give it away, there will be nothing left of you to keep.*

* * *

"Oscar." Marisol's voice, rough and low. "Whatever you're about to do—do it fast or don't do it at all. He's reaching for the girl."

He wasn't, quite. But he was drifting, angling, the way a chess player drifts, and Nell had begun to look up at him with a trust so complete it hurt worse than any blade.

"I'm scared," Nell said. Simply. The way children say things, having not yet learned that fear is supposed to be managed, presented, filed. "Is it going to hurt?"

Oscar knelt down beside her. Up close he could see the birthmark-key on her palm, a spiral like a fingerprint, like a signature written before she could write.

"Yes," he said honestly. "I think some of it will. But you know what I've learned about hurting things? They're usually the ones worth doing." He smiled at her. "You told me a bedtime story once. About the taster who became the door. Do you remember?"

"You laughed at the right places," she said gravely.

"That's how you know a story is true." He stood. "Watch the ending. I've read ahead."

* * *

He stepped to the Well.

Up close, the depth in the stone was worse and better than it had seemed—worse because he could feel it *attending*, the way a room attends when someone says your name; better because it did not feel hungry. It felt patient. It had waited since the beginning of the boundary for someone willing, and it could wait a moment longer while a bookkeeper got his affairs in order.

Which was, after all, what bookkeepers did.

"One final entry," Oscar said aloud, to the Well, to the cavern, to Corvane, to Marisol, to the ghost he could almost feel at the edge of his vision, silver-veined and patient, the woman whose death had started all of this. "For the record."

He closed his eyes.

And he spoke the name.

It was not a word in any language he knew, and yet his mouth knew it, the way his blood had always known how to refuse poison, the way his tongue had always known the difference between harm and nourishment. It was a name with a river in it, and a threshold, and his mother's voice saying it over a cradle on the far side of the world. It was the name of a child smuggled across the boundary between realities, wrapped in blankets that smelled of lavender and danger, carried by a Warden who had broken every rule to save one impossible thing.

It was his. It had always been his. And he gave it away.

He felt it leave—not torn, not stolen, but *released*, the way you open your hand to let a bird go, feeling the push of wings against your palm and then the astonishing absence where the weight used to be. The Well received it. The depth in the stone shivered, brightened, and the covenant rose out of it like a tide.

It took him the way the sea takes a swimmer: totally, irresistibly, and without malice. Every thread of the failing Veil, every unraveling strand that Corvane had spent years cutting and selling, came seeking him at once—and found in him a name to hold onto, a hinge, a crossing-point, a thing that was by its very nature *between*. The pain was enormous and clean, like being rewritten. He heard himself make a sound that was not quite a cry and not quite a laugh.

Through the tide, distantly, he saw Corvane move.

He saw the master of the guild lunge forward with his hands outstretched, fingers grasping for the moment—for the power, for the gate, for the transaction of a lifetime—and Oscar tried to shout a warning, or perhaps a farewell, or perhaps just *no*, the shortest entry in any ledger.

He didn't need to.

The boundary Corvane reached for was no longer a thing that could be cut. It was a thing that had just been *given*, freely, wholly, and it closed around the master's grasping hands the way a ledger closes around a lie. For one instant Corvane's face showed not fear but astonishment—the pure, offended astonishment of a man discovering that the arithmetic works both ways—and then the Veil passed through him, itemizing him, auditing him, striking the balance, and where he had stood there was a swirl of silver dust and a single leather glove, falling slowly, landing softly, belonging to no one.

The inquisitors did not fire their iron lamps. They simply stood, and stared, and began, one by one, to kneel—which is what soldiers do when they witness something their warrants did not prepare them for.

* * *

Afterward—after the tide had settled and the Veil ran taut and bright through the cavern like a harp string tuned at last—Marisol came and stood beside him.

She looked at him for a long moment. He could see her doing sums; she was a courier, a professional, an auditor of motives, and he watched her arrive at the total and not like it.

"You're still here," she said.

"I'm still here." His voice sounded different. Further away, and closer, both at once. "Mostly."

"Nell's mark burned out. The seal's ash. My blood's on the stone." She touched her bandaged side. "We did our parts. What's yours?"

Oscar considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. Then he held up his hand, and let it pass, slowly, through the edge of the Well's stone—through it, the way light passes through a doorway.

"I'm the entry now," he said. "The bookkeeper became the book."

Marisol's jaw tightened in the particular way it did when she refused to cry in front of witnesses. "Does it hurt?"

"Some of it." He smiled at her—the same smile, he hoped, the same warmth, the same man underneath the new mathematics. "But you know what I've learned about hurting things."

"Don't," she warned.

"They're usually the ones worth—"

"*Don't.*"

Above them, unseen, the tear in the Veil began—at last, at long last—to knit.

And somewhere past its mending, faint as a bell rung in another room, something answered.
