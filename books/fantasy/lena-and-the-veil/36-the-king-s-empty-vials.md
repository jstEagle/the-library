## 36. The King's Empty Vials

The pardon arrived on a Tuesday, which Marisol considered fitting, since Tuesdays had always been the guild's busiest day for impossible deliveries and it seemed only right that the crown should finally send one back.

It came by royal courier, sealed in green wax with the stag-and-crown, addressed in a clerk's careful hand to *Marisol Vey, Keeper of Accounts, the Guild of Honest Exchange*. She read the title twice. *Keeper of Accounts*. Three months ago the guild's official letterhead had still said *Master of Acquisitions*, and the acquisitions had included bottled Tuesdays, borrowed courage, and — though she hadn't known it then — strips of the world's own hem.

Now the inventory was ash. Now the ledgers were true. And now the crown wanted them.

She set the summons down on her desk — a real desk now, oak, with a drawer that locked, a luxury she'd bought herself out of the guild's remaining honest funds — and looked across the room at her brother, who was mending a kettle with the intense concentration of a man relearning how objects worked.

"Tomas," she said. "The king wants to see me."

He didn't look up from the kettle. "Tell him I said hello."

"You don't remember him."

"I remember what you told me about him." He soldered a seam, blew on it, squinted. "Fat rings under his eyes. Drinks little brown vials like a man taking medicine he doesn't believe in. Talks to his food." He glanced up, and there it was again — that flicker of something behind his eyes, not memory exactly, but its ghost, the shape a memory leaves in the wall when you pull the nail out. "Do I actually remember him, Mari? Or do I just remember your remembering?"

"Does it matter?"

He considered this with the seriousness Tomas gave everything now, as if the whole world were new stock needing inspection. "I think it might be the only thing that matters."

* * *

The palace had changed, and not in the way palaces usually changed, which was to say gilding over rot. The change was subtler and more alarming: the corridors smelled of bread.

Real bread, baking somewhere below, in quantities that suggested actual people were going to eat it. Marisol had been in the palace twice before — once as a courier with forged papers, once fleeing through the servants' vein with stolen vials in her coat — and both times the air had carried only candlewax, lilies, and the particular cold sweetness of a house where nobody cooked because nobody was hungry, the king least of all, the king being fed on years that belonged to other people.

Now there were guards at the doors who looked fed themselves, and clerks who looked slept, and when she was shown into the audience chamber she found the King of Aldermere standing at a window instead of enthroned behind it, and she stopped dead in the doorway because she almost didn't recognize him.

He had aged ten years in three months. It sat on him strangely well.

The flesh had gone loose at the jaw. The hair, formerly the color of expensive nothing, had gone grey at the temples in a way that looked decided rather than defeated. He wore a plain wool doublet, no chain of office, and he was holding — she squinted — an apple. He turned it over in his fingers while he listened to some functionary murmuring at his elbow, and when the man finished, the king said, "No. Tell the millers' guild we'll pay the fair rate or none at all. If they can't feed their own district at a fair rate, then we'll find millers who can."

The functionary bowed and withdrew, looking, Marisol thought, less crushed than relieved, the way men look when a mad order turns out to be merely honest.

Then the king saw her, and smiled, and the smile had cracks in it like old porcelain, and it was somehow far worse and far better than any smile she'd seen him wear in portraits.

"Mistress Vey." He gestured her forward. "You're smaller than your reputation."

"Your Majesty is taller than his coinage."

That startled a laugh out of him — a real one, rusty, with a cough hiding at the bottom of it. "So I'm told. Sit. Please." He sat too, in an ordinary chair, and she noticed how carefully he lowered himself, the way men do when their bodies have recently begun sending bills. "You're wondering why I summoned you instead of having you arrested."

"The thought had crossed my mind. Briefly. In passing. The way one crosses a river full of crocodiles."

"Good. A healthy suspicion of crowns is the first qualification for keeping honest books." He set the apple down on the table between them, precisely centered, and Marisol understood it was a kind of prop — something for his hands to do so they wouldn't shake, or wouldn't be seen shaking. "I want to show you something first."

He drew a small key from his pocket and unlocked a cabinet against the wall. Inside, on a velvet tray, lay six glass vials.

Empty ones.

They caught the light like little winter mornings, and Marisol felt her stomach tighten anyway, the way it did around anything from the guild's old stock, even husks. "Those are—"

"The last of them. Mine." He touched the tray's edge without picking anything up, the way you touch a wound to check it's still healing. "Oscar Sandoval stole four from my own cabinet the night he escaped. You'll forgive me if I keep the count exact; I've had three months to learn it. These six are what remained after the inquisitors seized Corvane's stores, and I ordered every one drained into the fire myself. Watched them burn. They burn green, did you know that? Like the light through leaves. Like something alive being rendered down."

"I knew what they were made of," Marisol said quietly. "My brother was made of one of them. Or rather, one of them was made of my brother."

The king flinched. It was a small flinch, well-practiced, but it reached his eyes. "Yes. I've read the ledger entry. I've read all of them." He closed the cabinet. "Forty-one names in the unremembered rolls. Forty-one people cut into years and sold to a man who told himself he was buying time for the realm." He laughed, short and terrible. "For the realm. That was the phrase I used. I'd like to tell you Corvane deceived me, but the truth is uglier: I paid without asking what the vials contained, because asking would have obliged me to refuse, and I was afraid of the alternative. Do you know what I was afraid of, Mistress Vey?"

"Dying."

"Fading." He shook his head. "Dying is a door. Fading is a drain. I could feel myself thinning — not aging, *thinning*, becoming less a man and more a portrait of one — and I told myself the realm needed me sharp, needed me *present*, and every year I bought made the present man smaller and the need larger. It's a very tidy circle. Corvane built it. I lived in it willingly." He looked at her. "And then a food-taster emptied my cabinet, and I discovered something remarkable."

"That you could survive without them."

"That I could *think* without them." He tapped his temple. "There's a fog I didn't know was a fog until it lifted. And underneath it — this is the humiliating part — underneath it I found I agreed with almost none of what I'd been deciding. The grain tariffs. The border levies. The way we let the eastern districts' petitions sit unread for years because reading them might oblige us to act. I signed those things. My hand. And I have spent three months un-signing them, and the realm has not collapsed, and the millers are eating, and I am going grey and losing arguments with my own council, and I have never governed better in my life." He spread his hands. "Mortality, it turns out, is an excellent advisor. She never lets you defer a decision past the date you'll be alive to make it."

Marisol studied him for a long moment. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because of what I'm about to ask." He straightened, and for a moment she saw the king again, the office settling over the man like a coat that had been let out and now fit differently. "The crown wishes to offer a pardon. Full and public, to every surviving member of the Guild of Honest Exchange who was not a principal in the Veil-thread trade. Forgiveness of all past offenses, licensed status going forward, protection from the inquisitors' remnant factions." He paused. "In exchange for the true ledgers. All of them. Every account, every name, every debt — surrendered to the crown, to be entered into law."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, faintly, the bread smell drifted up, absurdly domestic.

"And the names," Marisol said. "The forty-one unremembered. What does the crown intend to do with them?"

"Restore them." He said it simply, but she watched his hands. "As best as restoration can be done. Their property returned where it can be traced. Their records re-entered. Pensions to the families who kept faith with them — like yours, Mistress Vey, who kept a brother alive in one memory for eleven years, which is more than the crown managed to do with all its archives." He met her eyes. "It won't give Tomas back what was taken. Nothing can. But the law can at least stop pretending it never happened, and I have learned — late, expensively — that pretending is the most expensive thing a government buys."

Marisol sat with it. She was a professional; she audited offers for a living, and she knew this one was good — better than good, suspiciously good, the kind of generous that usually had a second page.

"There's a condition," she said. "There's always a condition."

"One." The king held up a finger. "The ledgers come to the crown, but copies stay with the guild. Public ones. Posted where anyone may read them — what was traded, who profited, what it cost, including what it cost *me*. I will not have this history locked in a palace archive where it can quietly become myth. My council thinks this is madness. They say a crown that publishes its own crimes invites every crank and pamphleteer to—" he waved a hand "—to govern better than I do, apparently." The dry smile again. "I've decided to risk it."

"And if we refuse? If the guild keeps its books?"

"Then no pardon, and the inquisitors get their remnant war, and everyone loses slowly." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him; she saw him pay for it. "I'd prefer not to threaten you. Threats are what Corvane did. But I won't pretend the offer is costless either way. Honest terms, Mistress Vey. That's the whole of my new policy, and I'm told it's revolutionary."

She thought of Oscar. Of course she thought of Oscar; she thought of him at every threshold now, which was appropriate, since thresholds were where he lived. He would have liked this scene, she thought — the king counting empty vials like a penitent counting beads, the apple centered on the table like a small green sun. He would have noted the ledger logic of it: *the crown, purchasing truth at par value, first buyer in history not to haggle.*

"All right," she said. "One counter-condition. Small."

"Name it."

"The pardon lists the dead, too. Lena. By name. Warden of the Veil, in the service of the crown she technically betrayed by serving something older than it." She held his gaze. "If the law is going to learn honesty, it can start by admitting whose side she was on."

The king was quiet a moment. "Lena," he repeated, tasting the name. "The woman from the fish gate." He nodded slowly. "Done. Full honors in the record. It will annoy the council immensely, which I'm told is how you know a decision is correct."

* * *

The signing happened a week later, in the guild hall, which was itself a small miracle — a year ago the hall had been a cellar of shadows and locked drawers, and now it had windows, actual windows, cut into the street front at Marisol's insistence, because an honest exchange ought to be visible from the road.

Half the city seemed to come to watch. The guild's survivors stood in a line — eleven of them, couriers and appraisers and one extremely nervous locksmith — while Marisol carried the true ledgers forward on a cloth-draped trolley like relics, which, she reflected, they were: the bones of the old covenant, minus the miracles.

Tomas stood beside her. He'd insisted on coming, and he'd dressed in his good coat, and he'd spent the walk over asking questions about the ceremony with the bright, slightly sideways curiosity that was his now — not the old Tomas, but a Tomas rebuilt from her telling, a house reconstructed from a description, livable if you knew which walls were new.

"Why does the king want our old lies?" he'd asked.

"So the law knows what lying costs."

"And why do we want his pardon?"

"So the law owes us something back." She'd squeezed his arm. "Debt and credit, little brother. It's all debt and credit. Just sometimes the currency is strange."

The king arrived without much pomp — a deliberate choice, everyone understood, a king learning to travel lighter than his fear — and he read the pardon aloud in a clear voice, and when he reached Lena's name the crowd made a sound Marisol hadn't expected: not cheering, but a low collective *ah*, like a congregation recognizing a psalm.

Then the king did something not in the script. He walked to the trolley, opened the first ledger himself, and read one entry aloud to the assembled city: the record of a Tuesday, stolen from a baker's widow in the Lowmarket, sold twice, and never returned. He read it flatly, without performance, the way you read a bill. And then he said, "This was done in my name, purchased with my seal, and I am sorry," and the word *sorry* landed in the hall like a coin dropped in a silent church.

Marisol watched the faces in the crowd. Nobody wept dramatically. A few people nodded, slow, the nodding of people entering something into their own internal ledgers. An old woman near the front — the widow herself, Marisol realized, her Tuesday long gone but her attendance free — called out, "About time somebody counted," and the hall laughed, warm and rough, and the king laughed too, and Marisol thought: *there. That's the sound of a law being rewritten. Not trumpets. Bookkeeping.*

Afterward, in the guild hall's back room, the king spoke to her privately, just a moment, while his guards pretended to examine the shelving.

"The child," he said. "The third signatory. The girl with the mark. My inquisitors' remnant still has her village on a list. Old orders, never rescinded — I've rescinded them formally this morning, but paper travels slower than fear." His jaw tightened. "I'm telling you because you'll tell the right people. Consider it the pardon's fine print, delivered early."

"She's safe," Marisol said. "We keep her close."

"I know. That's why I'm telling *you* and not sending soldiers." He paused at the door, and for a moment the tired man and the king occupied the same face, and it was almost bearable to look at. "Mistress Vey. Your guild traded in impossibilities for two hundred years. Bottled memories. Borrowed years. Names that open doors." He smiled, crooked. "It occurs to me the most impossible thing you ever handled was making a crown admit fault. Don't lose the knack."

* * *

That evening she went to the threshold.

She went most evenings now, though she'd never admit the regularity of it even to Tomas, who had figured it out within a week and had the grace not to say. This one was a doorway in a garden wall off Cooper's Lane, half-ruined, ivy-hung, the kind of place where two things met — wall and air, garden and street, dusk and whatever came after dusk — and therefore, these days, the kind of place where Oscar was.

He stood in the doorway the way light stands in a doorway: present, warm, and impossible to hold. He'd gotten better at manifesting, or at persisting; his edges used to fray when she talked too long, but tonight he held steady, and he was wearing the expression she'd learned meant he was listening hard.

"Well?" he said. "How was the fat cat?"

"He's not fat anymore. Grief and honesty are an excellent diet." She settled onto the low wall opposite him, unwrapped the bread and cheese she'd brought — an offering, a ritual, dinner shared with a doorway — and told him all of it. The vials burning green. The apple on the table. The pardon, the widow's shout, the king's apology landing like small change from heaven.

Oscar laughed at the right places. He always did, and it always undid her a little, because the laugh was *his*, unmistakably, the dry precise chuckle of a man auditing a joke and finding it sound.

"A pardon in exchange for the ledgers," he said, when she finished. "The crown buying truth at face value. Do you realize that's never once happened in recorded history? There should be a commemorative coin."

"There should be a commemorative *entry*. Which reminds me." She pulled the new ledger from her satchel — her ledger, the daily one — and opened it to today's page. "Entry for the ninth of Harvestmonth. 'The king burned his vials and aged into wisdom, which is the only trade in history where the seller profits.'" She glanced up. "Too flattering?"

"Accurate," Oscar said. "Which is the same thing, in a well-kept book." Then his gaze drifted past her shoulder, toward the lane, and softened in a way she'd learned to recognize. "Ah. You have a visitor."

She turned. At the mouth of the lane, small and solemn in the last light, stood the girl — the signatory, the child with the birthmark-key wrapped in a strip of linen on her wrist, eleven years old and carrying the future of two worlds in a body that still believed bedtime stories were load-bearing architecture.

She was carrying something else, too. A book. A battered, hand-lettered book that Marisol had never seen before, held against her chest with both arms.

"Marisol," the girl said, stopping a respectful distance from the threshold, nodding to Oscar the way you nod to weather — familiar, awed, unembarrassed. "I found something. In the chapel. Under the stone signatory, where he sits." She held up the book. "It's Lena's. Her own hand. And there's a page folded over, marked, and—" her voice wobbled, steadied, eleven-year-old steel underneath "—and it's got *names* on it. Names I don't think anybody's read yet. Not even yours."

Oscar had gone very still in the doorway, stiller than a doorway should allow.

"What kind of names?" Marisol asked quietly.

The girl looked at Oscar, then at her, and said the sentence that would send them all running before morning:

"Yours. His. And forty-two others — one for every person the Veil took. Lena kept a list of everyone it owed."
