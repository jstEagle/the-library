## 38. Thresholds

The first thing Oscar learned about being a threshold was that he was very bad at it.

He had assumed—because he had been a careful man all his life, a man of ledgers and precautions and contingency plans—that his new existence would come with rules. A manual. Perhaps a small card. *Congratulations on your apotheosis; please review the enclosed terms of service.* Instead he got a girl of eleven who visited him at doorways and told him stories about himself, and a body that appeared wherever two things met, whether he wished it or not.

In the beginning, he arrived places by accident.

He woke—if "woke" was the word for it—in the seam between a bakery's back door and the alley behind it, at the exact minute when night became morning. He stood in the gap between two strangers arguing over a fish stall, invisible to both, feeling their anger pass through him like weather. He spent one entire afternoon as the space between a mother's hand and her child's, which was not unpleasant, actually, but made concentration difficult.

"You'll get the hang of it," the child signatory told him, sitting cross-legged in a doorway in the Tanner's Quarter, eating an apple she had absolutely stolen. She had refused to tell anyone her name since the Well—"names are currency now, I'm saving mine"—so everyone called her Wren, which she tolerated because she'd chosen it herself. "You're thinking of it wrong. You keep trying to *go* places."

"I am trying to go places," Oscar said. "That is generally how travel works."

"No." She gestured with the apple core at the doorframe around them—or rather at him, since he was currently occupying most of the doorframe. "You don't go to thresholds. You *are* thresholds. You show up where things meet. So stop asking 'where do I want to be' and start asking 'what wants meeting.'"

Oscar considered this the way he once considered a suspicious dish: thoroughly, from several angles, with a small taste first.

"What wants meeting," he repeated.

"Exactly."

"That's terrible advice. It's vague and unaccountable and there's no way to audit it."

"I know," Wren said happily. "Isn't it wonderful?"

* * *

It was, infuriatingly, correct advice.

Once he stopped fighting it, Oscar found that his arrivals began to make a kind of sense—not ledger sense, not columns-that-balance sense, but the sense of a tide. He came to be where he was needed, or where need was near, which was nearly the same thing and occasionally much funnier.

He appeared at dusk more than any other hour, because dusk was the day and the night shaking hands. He appeared at shorelines, at the edges of markets, at the exact spot where a crowd's murmur became silence. And he appeared, most often, at doors.

Doors, he discovered, were prayers people made out of wood. Every door was a question: *who is on the other side? will they let me in? am I still welcome?* He had spent thirty years on the service side of doors—the servants' passages, the butler's entrance, the corridor behind the wall—and he had never once thought of them as anything but shortcuts. Now he understood: every hidden passage in every great house was a confession that the house had two lives, and only one of them was true.

So he stood in doorways. And things came to him.

* * *

The first lost soul he guided was a Faded child—a small creature of grey light and unfinished edges, the kind of being the old covenant had been written to keep *out*, except that nobody had ever asked what happened to the ones born on the wrong side of the boundary, the ones who had never seen a mortal dawn and ached for it like a tooth.

She stood at the edge of the Fish Gate at low tide, weeping light, unable to cross the line where wet sand became dry.

Oscar felt her before he saw her—a pull, like a hook behind his sternum. He stepped out of the space between two mooring posts and said, "You're standing on a threshold."

The child looked up. Her face was almost a face, the way a sketch is almost a portrait. "I can't," she said. "It won't have me."

"It isn't a matter of it having you." He crouched down to her level, an old habit from years of speaking to frightened kitchen boys. "A threshold belongs to neither side. That's its whole profession. You don't cross it—you stand on it, and then you choose." He held out his hand. "I know exactly how that feels, if it helps. I used to be a man. Now I'm mostly a doorway. We're practically colleagues."

She took his hand, and her weight was like holding a handful of cold evening.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"I haven't got one," Oscar said, and found—to his genuine surprise—that saying it didn't hurt anymore. It had hurt terribly for the first few weeks, a phantom-limb ache where *Oscar Sandoval* used to live. But pain, like everything else, eventually balanced. "But I can walk you to someone who does. There's a woman in the city who keeps a book of names, and she writes new ones in every morning. She'll give you one that fits. She has excellent penmanship."

"Will it be a good name?"

"It'll be yours," Oscar said. "Which is better than good. Good names are for other people to admire. Yours will just be true."

The child stepped onto the dry sand beside him, and the world did not end, and somewhere in the deep architecture of the Veil—which was now, in a manner of speaking, his ribcage—something settled into place like a well-kept account.

* * *

He guided mortals too, though they were harder, because mortals rarely knew they were lost.

They came to thresholds at the turning points of their lives and stood there dithering—a young guardsman outside the door of the woman he meant to propose to, pacing like a cat in a sack; an old cook who had worked the same palace kitchen for forty years and could not make herself hand in her resignation; a boy with a forged letter of apprenticeship, sweating outside a printer's shop, certain that at any moment the ink would confess its fraudulence.

Oscar could not open doors for them. That was the one rule he had learned clearly, and it was iron: a threshold may hold the space, but the crossing must always be chosen. The covenant had been reforged on that principle, and he was the covenant now, so he supposed the principle was also him.

But he could talk.

"You've paced that flagstone smooth," he remarked to the guardsman, materializing—gently, the way fog arrives—beside a garden gate. "Another hour and you'll owe the city repairs."

The guardsman jumped a foot. "Where did—who—"

"Neighbor," Oscar said vaguely. "I'm always here. Question for you. When you imagine her saying yes—and you do imagine it, you've imagined it eleven times tonight, I counted—what does she say after?"

"After?"

"After yes. People plan the yes and forget everything after it. What comes next, in your imagining?"

The guardsman blinked. And then, because he was a young man in love and therefore already half-lost, he answered honestly: "Nothing. I never get past the yes."

"There's your problem," Oscar said. "You're not afraid of her answer. You're afraid of the rest of your life arriving all at once. That's not a proposal problem, that's a Tuesday problem. Everyone has it." He smiled—he was fairly sure he smiled; his face did what it intended most days now. "Knock. The rest of the life sorts itself out one dish at a time."

"One dish at a time," the guardsman repeated, frowning.

"Trust me. It's how everything gets eaten."

The guardsman knocked. She said yes. And then—this was the part Oscar stayed for, lingering in the hinge like a good host—she laughed at something he said, badly, joyfully, and the threshold between them dissolved because it wasn't needed anymore. Two things had met. His work there was done.

He kept no record of these moments. He regretted nothing else about his condition, but he regretted that. Some part of him, the bookkeeper part, the part that had spent decades balancing columns in candlelit cellars, wanted a ledger of every door that opened because someone finally chose to knock. Marisol had offered to keep it for him. He'd told her no.

"Why not?" she'd asked, arms crossed, in the doorway of her rebuilt guild house—which was, these days, honest, and smelled of paper instead of bottled thunderstorms.

"Because then it would be an account," Oscar said, "and accounts get audited, and debts get calculated, and the whole point of what I did—" he gestured at himself, at the doorway, at the dusk coming down soft and violet over the rooftops— "is that it was given. Freely. Never returned. If you write it down, someone will eventually try to pay me back, and I have nowhere to put payment. I'm mostly hinges, Marisol."

"You're mostly a sentimental fool who used to hide in pantries," she said fondly.

"Also true. Both entries are accurate."

* * *

There were hard nights.

He would be honest about that, insofar as honesty applied to a man who was also a liminal space. Some nights the loneliness arrived like weather, and it was a specific loneliness: the loneliness of being *between*. Not of one world or the other, not mortal nor Faded, not remembered nor forgotten. Lena remembered him—but Lena was past the Veil now, kept-dead, and their conversations came rarely and sideways, like light through water. Wren remembered him. Marisol wrote his name daily in her ledger, and he could feel each entry the way a man feels a coat put over his shoulders: warm, and slightly embarrassing.

But most hours, most nights, he was alone in the way a bridge is alone. And some nights he wondered if the Well had made a mistake—had taken his name and given him, in exchange, merely a different kind of invisibility. He had been invisible as a servant. Invisible as a spy. Was eternity just the pantry, enlarged?

On one such night he stood in the threshold of the old guild cellar—the real one, the deep one, abandoned now, its shelves empty, its candles long burned to stubs. He had spent so many nights of his life down here with his ledgers. He stood in the doorway and did not enter, because entering was a choice and he was testing something, and he heard, faintly, the ghost of his own younger self scratching quill on paper, recording the sale of a stolen Tuesday, a jar of borrowed courage, quantities of Veil-thread that hadn't added up.

"The numbers didn't add up," he told the empty room. "And I trusted numbers above all people. That was the whole disease, wasn't it. I thought if the columns balanced, the world was safe."

The room said nothing. Rooms are poor conversationalists.

"But they never balanced," he went on, warming to it, the way he'd once warmed to a monologue delivered to a locked pantry door. "Not really. There was always a remainder. Corvane's thread. Lena's map. Me. The remainder was always the true total." He laughed quietly. "Thirty years of double-entry bookkeeping and the entire lesson was that everything important lives in the margin."

From the dark of the cellar, something scratched.

Oscar went still. Thresholds feel things; it is their nature. And what he felt just then was not memory, and not draft, and not rat. It was attention. The particular pressure of being watched by something that was deciding whether to speak.

"Hello?" he said, in the old covenant-tongue, without quite meaning to. The language came out of him now the way his immunity once had—unbidden, native, a side effect of what ran in him.

The scratching stopped. Then, from the deepest shelf, in a voice like dust settling, something answered:

"You're the new one. The nameless warden. We heard you'd be coming round."

"We?" said Oscar.

And the shadows along the cellar wall unfolded—one, three, seven shapes, thin as bookmarked pages, beings that had lived in the margins of the old guild house for longer than the guild had existed, feeding, perhaps, on all those years of recorded impossibility. The oldest of them inclined what might have been a head.

"We kept the ledgers' dreams," it said. "Every entry ever written here left a shadow in the binding. You read thousands of them, nameless one. You touched every page." A pause, delicate as a turned leaf. "We have been waiting to ask you something. You are the only one who ever read us all the way through."

Oscar felt the pull behind his sternum, the tide-tug, the sense of a threshold opening onto something that had no door until this moment.

"Ask," he said.

The oldest shape leaned close, and its voice dropped to the register of things whispered across boundaries:

"What happened to the Tuesday?"
