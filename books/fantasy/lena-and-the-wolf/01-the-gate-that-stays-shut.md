## 1. The Gate That Stays Shut

There are three rules to keeping a gate like mine, and I wrote all three myself, which is the only reason I trust them.

One: check the seals every day, even when the snow is up to your hips and the wind sounds like it's filing a complaint with the mountain itself. Two: let no one through, no matter what they offer, no matter what they weep about, no matter whose face they wear. Three — and this is the rule that keeps the other two honest — never tell anyone why.

I have kept Thornfell Pass for twenty years. In that time I have been paid in salt, in whiskey, in a goat I did not want, and once, memorably, in a hat. The crown believes I am a customs man guarding a trade road that no longer trades. The smugglers believe I am a corrupt customs man who can be bought with the right whiskey. Both beliefs are useful. Neither is true. What I actually guard sits forty feet below my cabin, behind a door of black oak bound in iron older than any kingdom that has ever claimed this valley, and it stays shut because I say so, and I say so because I am the only man alive who knows what happens when it doesn't.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. It was winter. It is always winter here; the mountain simply varies the dosage.

* * *

The morning of the solstice I woke before dawn out of habit rather than necessity, which is how most of my habits work now. The fire had burned down to a sullen orange eye. I fed it, dressed in layers, and took down the seal-checking kit from its hook: a lantern with a shuttered flame, a length of chalk, a small brass bell with no clapper, and a knife I have never once used for anything but cutting bread, because the door has never needed me to be brave, only punctual.

The tunnel down from my cabin is cut into the living rock, and it does something strange to sound. My own footsteps arrive at my ears a half-beat late, as if someone else were walking behind me, matching my stride. Twenty years ago that used to frighten me. Now it's just company.

The door waits at the bottom of nine steps — nine, always nine, though I have measured them and there are eleven. The two extra are a courtesy the mountain extends to visitors who don't exist anymore. The iron bands across the oak are worked with script in no alphabet any living scholar would recognize, and every morning I walk their length with the lantern held close, reading them the way a shepherd reads his fence: not for meaning, but for damage.

That morning, all seven seals were intact. The chalk lines I renew each month still held their pale geometry. The clapperless bell, when I passed it along the lintel, stayed silent, which is the entire point of a clapperless bell — it rings only when something on the other side of the door is paying attention, and nothing had paid attention in twenty years, and I intended to keep that streak running until one of us died of old age.

"Morning," I said to the door anyway. Habit. You spend enough time alone and you start narrating to furniture. "Still shut. Well done, both of us."

The door said nothing, which I chose to interpret as agreement.

I climbed back up, made porridge with too much salt because the goat had been generous before she'd been eaten, and settled into the long arithmetic of a gatekeeper's winter: count the lamp oil, mend the second shutter, sharpen everything that holds an edge whether or not it needs it. Somewhere around midday the wind changed and came down the pass carrying the smell of woodsmoke that wasn't mine, which meant the smugglers were early.

* * *

They come up the hidden road twice a season, the ones from the low valleys — six or eight men wrapped in grey wool, leading mules loaded with things that are easier to move without paperwork. And once a season, alone, comes Sefa, who is less a smuggler than an institution with a walking stick.

Sefa arrived at dusk looking like a bundle the wind had given up on. She stamped the snow off her boots on my step — she is the only person alive who bothers — and squinted up at me from under her hood.

"You look terrible, Kavanagh."

"I look exactly the same as last time."

"That's what's terrible about it." She pushed past me into the warmth with the confidence of a woman who has done it thirty times, set her pack on my table, and began unpacking it while I was still latching the door. Tea. Dried fish. A bolt of good wool. A jar of honey. A newspaper from the coast, three weeks stale. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a bottle of the plum brandy I pretend not to ask for.

"I can't imagine why you bring that," I said.

"No," she agreed. "You never could."

We traded. She overpaid me for wolf pelts I hadn't trapped yet and I overcharged her for passage rights she didn't need, and the ledger came out somewhere in the middle, as it always does. Then she sat by my fire drinking tea and studying me with those bright bird eyes of hers, and I knew the real cargo was coming.

"There's talk in the valley," she said.

"There's always talk in the valley. Last time it was that I was a retired bandit. Before that, a defrocked priest."

"This talk says the old road under the mountain's waking up. Says shepherds found a lamb standing at the treeline in the dead of January, not frozen, not hungry, just standing. Facing uphill." She sipped her tea. "Facing your direction, Kavanagh."

I kept my hands busy with the pelt I was salting. "Sheep are stupid. It was probably lost."

"It walked home the next morning and wouldn't go near that slope again."

"Sheep are stupid and they hold grudges. There's nothing under the mountain but cold rock and my excellent door."

"Mm." She looked at me a moment longer, and I gave her back the flat, pleasant nothing I've spent two decades polishing, and eventually she sighed and let it go, the way she always does, the way everyone always does, because the whole trick of an invisible life is that people will do most of the work of not-seeing-you themselves if you just stand still enough.

She stayed the night in my spare room — the room I keep made up and have never once offered to anyone, because Sefa simply walks into it, and arguing would require conversation longer than four sentences. In the morning she stood at the edge of my clearing, pulled her hood up, and said, without turning around:

"Whatever you're keeping at arm's length up here, old man, one day it's going to learn to run faster than your arms."

Then she was gone down the white road, and I went inside and finished the brandy, and told myself the warmth in my chest was the drink.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about building a life out of distance: it works. That's the horror of it. It works beautifully.

I want to be clear that I am not unhappy. Unhappiness requires wanting something, and I have arranged my wants with the same care I arrange my seals — checked daily, renewed monthly, kept small enough to carry. I want the fire lit before dark. I want the seals intact. I want the door shut. I want another winter exactly like this one, and then another after that, until the ledger of my life balances out quiet and unremarkable and closes without incident.

Some nights, sitting up with the lamp, I catch myself listening to the silence of the pass the way other men listen to music. No voices asking things of me. No names being called. Nobody needing me to be anything other than a shape that checks a door. It's peace. I have the paperwork to prove it.

It occurs to me, writing this, that I have just described a man hiding in a cellar and calling it a house. But the cellar is warm, and the door is locked from my side, and there is no one left outside who remembers what I'm hiding from. That's not fear. That's management.

* * *

Three days after Sefa left, the weather turned vicious, the kind of blizzard the mountain sends when it wants to remind the valley who's in charge. I spent it the way I spend all bad weather: indoors, productive, and lying to myself about how much I enjoyed the excuse.

I rehung the loose shutter. I rendered tallow. I read the stale newspaper twice, including the advertisements, and developed strong opinions about a corset shop in a city I'll never see. On the second evening the storm screamed hard enough to rattle the chimney, and I sat by the fire doing the thing I do perhaps four times a year: taking out the memory the way you'd take out a knife to oil it, just to confirm it still cuts.

Twenty years ago I was not Odile Kavanagh. That name is a coat I put on at a crossroads in spring, a coat that fit so well I stopped noticing the weight of it. Before that I had another name, and a life that lived below rather than above, and a debt I settled in the only currency the Undercourt accepts, which is pieces of yourself. I paid. I walked away. I built this quiet arithmetic of a life on top of the hole where that name used to be, and for twenty years the arrangement has held, because I follow the rules, and the rules work, and the door stays shut.

The fire popped. The wind clawed at the roof. Down the tunnel, forty feet of rock away, the door kept its silence, and I kept mine, and between us we maintained one of the longest-running agreements in the history of this mountain.

I banked the fire and went to bed. In the morning I would check the seals, as always. All seven would be intact, as always. The bell would not ring, as always.

I fell asleep to the sound of the storm, and if you had asked me then — comfortable, competent, alone in the way I had chosen, alone in the way I had earned — I would have told you that this was peace, and I would have believed it, and I would have been almost right.

Almost.

Because the storm broke sometime in the small hours, sudden and total, the way storms sometimes surrender all at once, and in the ringing quiet that followed, I heard — faint, from the direction of the pass, from the direction of the treeline Sefa's stupid unfrozen lamb had faced — the crunch of small bare feet on fresh snow.

Coming uphill.

Toward my door.
