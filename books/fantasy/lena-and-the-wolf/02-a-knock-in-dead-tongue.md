## 2. A Knock in Dead Tongue

The blizzard came up the pass on the third day of Deepwinter, the way blizzards always do—without manners, without warning worth having, and without any respect for a man's carefully laid plans.

I had plans. I want that on the record. I had a pot of barley soup on the stove, a fresh seal to check on the eastern lintel, and a book of smugglers' debts half-sorted into piles of *payable* and *laughable*. It was going to be a good evening. A quiet one. The kind of evening I had spent twenty years perfecting: warm, solitary, and utterly unremarkable.

The mountain disagreed.

It started as a sound under the wind—not through it, *under* it, which is the sort of distinction you learn to make when you live above a door that should never open. A knocking. Three beats, pause, three beats. Patient. Polite, even.

Nobody knocks at Thornfell Pass. That's rather the point of Thornfell Pass. Smugglers use the postern and the hand-signals; the mountain cats use the gaps in the south wall because they have no respect for architecture; and everything else with sense stays home when the sky turns white. There is no door at Thornfell that answers to knuckles. Except, of course, the one beneath my feet, and it doesn't knock. It *waits*.

So when the knocking came again—three beats, pause, three beats—I did what any sensible gatekeeper does. I picked up the iron poker, checked that the salt line across the threshold was intact, and went to see who was fool enough to be out in weather that could strip the paint off a wagon.

* * *

The front door of the waystation isn't grand. It's oak, iron-banded, older than the kingdom that thinks it owns this pass, and it has one virtue: it opens inward only after I've seen who's standing there, through a slit I cut myself the first winter. Twenty years of habit. You'd think twenty years would make me slow.

I looked through the slit and saw nothing but white.

That should have been the end of it. White means snow, snow means nobody, nobody means back to the soup. But the white moved wrong. Snow drifts; this shifted, the way a held breath shifts. And then, down near the bottom of the slit, at knee height where no grown person stands, something small looked back at me.

An eye. Dark, calm, entirely unbothered by the fact that it belonged to someone standing barefoot in a blizzard.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the one luxury I've allowed myself these two decades: my first thought was not *a child in danger*. My first thought was *the cold doesn't touch her*, and my second thought was the old rhyme every child in these mountains learns before they learn their letters:

*Barefoot in the snow, what walks and doesn't know?*

Fae-touched. Changeling-bought. Something wearing a child's shape like a borrowed coat. Every story ends badly for the man who opens the door.

My third thought arrived slower, the way the worst thoughts do, and it was simply: *she's looking at me like she knows me.*

I opened the door.

I've spent a long time not examining why. The poker was in my hand, the salt was laid, the iron was cold against my palm—all the wards of a careful life—and I opened the door anyway, into a wall of screaming white, for a pair of dark eyes at knee height. If you asked me, I'd say it was because no child freezes on my watch, whatever she is. If you asked me at three in the morning, which nobody does, I might say something less comfortable about doors and the men who swear to keep them shut.

She walked in without hurrying. Bare feet on my stone floor, leaving prints that were dry. Not wet—*dry*, as though the snow had declined to touch her, or as though she had declined to be touched by it. She was perhaps seven years old, thin as a winter wren, wrapped in a nightdress that had been fine once and was now mostly memory. Her hair was black and heavy with frost that didn't melt.

She stopped three paces inside, which is exactly the distance a well-trained guest keeps, and looked up at me.

"Close the door," I said. "You're letting the cold in."

She didn't answer. She watched me close it instead, with the particular attention of someone cataloguing how it's done—the latch, the bar, the order of things—as if doors were new to her, or as if she were memorizing mine.

"Can you speak?" I asked. "Nod if you can."

She nodded. Then she said something.

I'd like to tell you I didn't understand it. That's the lie I told myself for the first hour, anyway—that it was just wind-noise, some gutter-tongue from the far coast, sounds without shape. But I understood every word. That's the trouble. I understood it the way you understand your own heartbeat: not with the mind but somewhere lower and older, in the place where I keep the things I don't look at.

*"Cold-walker comes to the lit house,"* she said, in a language that predates every kingdom on this side of the mountain, a language that was old when the Undercourt was young, a language I had personally buried along with everything else, twenty years ago, under a different name and a different life. *"Is the keeper home?"*

The poker shook in my hand. Just once. Just enough.

"The keeper's home," I heard myself answer—in Common, thank all the merciful gods, Common and nothing else—and then, because my mouth apparently had opinions my brain hadn't authorized: *"and the keeper asks what brings a small one to his door in the teeth of the storm."*

In the dead tongue. Fluent. Unhesitating. Twenty years of silence, and it came out of me like water finding an old channel, smooth and worn and *waiting*.

The girl's face did something extraordinary. Nothing, actually—perfect stillness—but her eyes went wide and bright, and she took one step forward, breaking her own careful distance, and said, rapid and tumbling, *"You speak! The grey lady said none above would speak. She said I would have to show them, they wouldn't hear me, they never hear—"*

"Stop." My voice came out harder than I intended. Hard enough that she flinched, and I hated myself for it, and hated more the relief underneath the hate, because flinching children are ordinary children, and ordinary children are problems I know how to have. "Slowly. Who is the grey lady? Where are your parents?"

She tilted her head. And then, with the terrible patience of the very young dealing with the very foolish, she pointed at herself, then at the floor, then made a walking motion with two fingers, then pointed back the way she'd come, toward the pass, toward the lowlands, toward—presumably—a village, a family, a whole ordinary life that had somehow produced a barefoot child who spoke the language of the thing under my mountain.

"No," she said then, in Common, and her Common was good, accented strangely, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "They ask me not to say names. Names are..." She frowned, hunting. "...expensive."

"*Expensive.*"

She nodded solemnly, as if this explained everything, and perhaps it did. I felt the bottom drop out of my stomach, quietly, the way it does, no drama, just the long fall beginning somewhere below the ribs.

Because I knew that word too. In that tongue. In that grammar. *Expensive* — dear — costly — *beloved*, depending on context, depending on who was paying.

"Right," I said, mostly to myself. "Soup first. Questions after."

* * *

Here is what I learned over soup, or rather, here is what I failed to learn, itemized like a smuggler's ledger, because ledgers are how I think when I'm frightened:

One. She would not, could not, or had been instructed not to say her name. When I pressed—and I pressed gently, I'll have you know, I'm not a monster—she pressed her lips together and shook her head with such finality that I let it go. Instead she tapped her own chest twice, the way you'd tap a locked box.

Two. She was hungry in a normal, human, heartbreaking way—she ate three bowls of barley soup and burned her tongue on the second, and cried a little at that, ordinary tears, ordinary child—and yet her feet, which had crossed four miles of killing snow, were pink and warm as a sleeping cat's.

Three. When I asked where her mother and father were, she pointed down. Not south, not home. *Down.* Then she caught herself, went very still, and changed the gesture to a vague wave toward the lowlands, the way a child changes an answer when they've been coached—or warned.

Four. And this is the one I keep circling back to, the one sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone: she talked in her sleep. Later, much later, when I'd banked the fire and put her in my own bed because there was nowhere else and I wasn't going anywhere anyway, I heard it through the wall. Not words. Not quite. A rhythm, a rise and fall, the cadence of the dead tongue spoken soft and continuous, like a prayer, or a recitation, or—

Or a contract being read aloud.

I sat up most of the night with that thought, and the fire, and the poker within reach, feeling like the world's worst host and the world's most predictable fool. Because here is the truth I've built twenty years of walls around: I recognized that cadence. I've heard it before. I heard it every night for years of a life I do not talk about, in halls I do not describe, under a name I do not answer to. Somebody taught this child to speak in her sleep, and whoever taught her learned it in the same school I did.

There are no coincidences at Thornfell Pass. There's only the mountain, and what it sends up, and why.

* * *

Near dawn the storm broke—not ended, storms don't end up here, they just rotate their grip—but it thinned enough that the moon came through in ragged pieces, silvering the snow. And the girl woke, silent as a cat, and padded out to the main room barefoot, and found me at the table with my ledger and my cold tea.

She didn't speak. She climbed onto the chair across from me, took the stub of charcoal I kept for marking crates, and pulled a scrap of brown paper toward her with both hands, the gravity of a scholar approaching a great work.

Then she drew a door.

Not a house. Not a family, not a village, not the sun-with-a-smile that children draw when adults interrogate them kindly. A door. Tall, narrow, arched at the top, with a keystone, and hinges drawn on the wrong side—on the left, where mine are on the right—and beneath it, at the base, a small careful line of marks that I read before I could stop myself, because reading that script is apparently like breathing: something you can quit doing for twenty years and pick up mid-word.

*The ninth gate. The sealed one. Ask it who holds the key.*

I put down my tea very slowly.

"That's not a request," I said, in Common, because Common was safe, Common was mine. "That's an instruction. Who gave it to you?"

She looked at me with those dark, patient eyes, and she did the thing I would remember for the rest of my life, however long that turned out to be. She reached across the table, took my hand—my scarred, iron-hard, gatekeeper's hand—and turned it palm up. Then she traced one finger down the center of my palm, slow, deliberate, following a line I have stared at a thousand times and called a scar, a crease, a trick of the skin.

And she said, softly, in the tongue I had buried and that had evidently not buried me:

*"It remembers you, keeper. The door knows its own."*

Outside, far below us, deep under forty fathoms of rock and twenty years of my careful, ordered, invisible life, something knocked.

Three beats. Pause. Three beats.

Patient. Polite. Waiting for an answer.
