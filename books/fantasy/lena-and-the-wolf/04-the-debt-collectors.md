## 4. The Debt Collectors

They came on the third morning after the blizzard broke, when the snow had settled into that particular silence that makes a man feel like the last living thing in the world. I was splitting wood behind the station house—Wren watching from the window with her chin on her fists, the way she did when she thought I couldn't see her counting my axe strokes—and I heard the horses before I saw them.

That was wrong, for starters. Nothing comes up Thornfell Pass in deep winter without me hearing it an hour beforehand. The smugglers announce themselves with whistles at the treeline. The weather announces itself in my knees. But these three arrived the way bad news arrives: all at once, already there, as if they'd simply decided to exist on my road and expected the road to be grateful.

Three riders in grey. Not the grey of wool or the grey of storm-cloud—this was the grey of things that have been washed too many times and given up pretending, except that their clothes were immaculate. Fine grey clothes, cut close, without a single buckle or button out of place. They sat their horses like men who had never once been thrown and never once been carried.

I set down the axe. Some instincts you don't argue with.

"Stay inside," I said through the window, and Wren looked at me with those eyes that were too old for her face and too young for what lived behind them, and she nodded. She'd learned three words of common speech by then—*yes*, *no*, and *door*—and none of them were needed here. She just pressed her small palm flat against the glass, which is either a child's goodbye or a warding sign in a language I'd spent twenty years not thinking about, and I honestly could not have told you which.

I walked out to meet them.

* * *

"Greetings to the keeper of the pass," said the one in front. He dismounted first, and his boots found the snow without disturbing it, which I want to stress, because it's the detail that turned my stomach cold. No prints. Three horses and three men stood on fresh powder and left it smooth as a bedsheet.

He was unremarkable in the way that a well-made chair is unremarkable. Middle height, middle years, a face you'd forget while looking at it. His companions stayed mounted—one woman, one man, both equally forgettable, all three dressed from the same bolt of that impossible grey.

"Pass is closed," I said. "Deep winter. Nobody crosses till thaw."

"We're not crossing." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had been fitted to his face by professionals. "We've come to call on a resident."

"There's no residents. There's me, a stove, and forty years of dust."

"And a child." He produced something from inside his coat—a folded paper, handled with the reverence other men reserve for relics. "Wren. Approximately seven years of age. Arrived at your door three nights past, barefoot, in a blizzard, unharmed." He glanced up, mild as milk. "You'll forgive the precision. We are precise by trade."

I kept my face doing nothing at all. Twenty years of practice at that. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"Of course you do." No anger in it. That was the worst part—the total absence of anger, of urgency, of any human friction whatsoever. Talking to him was like talking to a ledger. "But we can proceed as if you didn't. We're patient. Patience is also our trade. In fact—" he unfolded the paper with two fingers, delicately, "—everything is our trade. Debts, chiefly. This one, specifically."

I should have turned around then. Walked back inside, barred the door, waited them out. Grey men who leave no footprints will eventually grow bored; even the Undercourt respects the physics of tedium. That was the plan I would have advised, if some younger, stupider version of myself had come asking.

Instead I heard myself say: "Show me."

* * *

The contract was beautiful. That's the thing nobody tells you about evil done properly—it arrives typeset. Vellum fine enough to see light through, ink the brown-black of dried blood but not blood, signatures in a flowing hand that made even the ugliest clause look like a love letter.

I read fast. It's a skill from another life, and it woke up in my hands before my head agreed to it.

The names at the bottom were plain farmer's names, inked shakily, the way people sign when their hand knows better than their heart. *Marta and Tam Reeve, of Hollowmere village.* Below theirs, in older script, a witness line. And below that, in letters that seemed to sit slightly deeper in the vellum than ink has any right to sit, a seal I recognized.

I recognized it the way you recognize a scar in a mirror.

"It's a straightforward instrument," the grey man said. "The parents sold one-half of the child's soul to the Court Beneath in exchange for relief of famine-debt. A fair bargain, honestly conducted, freely signed. The Court has honored its side. Grain came to Hollowmere through the winter. The child thrived." He tilted his head. "Well. Thrived in the manner of children who sleep with one foot in another country. You may have noticed."

"I haven't noticed anything. There's no child here."

"You're doing very well," he said warmly, as if I were a student reciting. "Truly. Most surface-folk break by now. But here is where precision serves us both." His finger touched the vellum, and the words rearranged themselves—not moving, exactly, but becoming more legible, the way text does in a dream. "Clause nine. Upon maturity of the debt, the Court collects its portion. The soul-portion remains where it is—that's permanent, I'm afraid, no refunds, the Court doesn't do refunds—but the body is remanded to the Court's custody, so that the soul-portion and the body may be... reconciled."

"Reconciled," I repeated.

"A gentle word. We have many gentle words. It's how you know the work is serious." He refolded the contract. "The debt matured eleven days ago. The child walked out of Hollowmere the same night. She wasn't running, keeper. She was *delivering herself*. The terms compel it. She'll keep walking toward us no matter where you hide her—though I confess the blizzard was inconvenient. Weather above ground is so uncooperative." He sighed the sigh of a man whose only complaint in life was climate. "So. You may give her to us today, and this stays a pleasant errand. Or you may continue this charming fiction, and we return with paperwork that is less pleasant to hold. Either way, keeper, we collect. The only variable is your comfort."

Behind me, faintly, I heard the station house door open.

I didn't turn. I knew what I'd see: a small barefoot girl standing on the threshold, unhurt by cold, watching three grey strangers discuss her the way farmers discuss a heifer.

"She's under my guardianship," I said.

The words came out of me sounding like someone else's voice. And here is the thing I have had twenty years to think about and still cannot fully explain: they weren't a lie. I hadn't planned them. I am a man who plans everything, who keeps his words rationed like hardtack, and those four syllables simply walked out of my mouth wearing my coat.

The grey man raised his eyebrows. His companions shifted on their horses—not alarmed, just interested, the way crows go quiet before deciding whether something is food.

"Guardianship," he repeated. "Interesting. On what grounds?"

"On the grounds that I say so."

"Ah." He nodded slowly, savoring it. "Surface law?"

"Any law you like."

"Keeper." He dismounted fully now, closed the distance between us until I could smell him—or rather, until I could smell the absence of him, a person-shaped hole in the air. His voice dropped into something almost kind. "I'm going to offer you professional courtesy, because you're clearly not a fool, and fools bore us. Whatever you were, whoever you were—you have buried it deep, and we respect a well-dug grave. So hear me plainly. Guardianship requires standing. Standing requires a name. And you, gatekeeper of Thornfell Pass..." He smiled again, and this time the smile fit badly, like a borrowed coat. "...are a man with no name worth the law's attention. You bought your invisibility. Invisible men cannot stand surety for anyone. That is not an insult. It is arithmetic."

The wind moved through the pass. Somewhere up-slope, snow slid off a pine with a soft hiss.

And Wren came and stood beside me.

She didn't take my hand. She didn't hide behind my leg. She planted her bare feet in the snow—which did not melt around them, did not even dimple—and looked up at the grey man with an expression I have seen on soldiers and on wolves and once, memorably, on a cornered bear: the expression of a creature informing you that the cost of proceeding will exceed the prize.

Then she spoke. One word, in the dead tongue, in a voice that was hers and also, briefly, something older using her throat.

The grey man took one step backward. Just one. His boot finally left a print in the snow—a single dark mark, like a bruise—and he looked down at it as though it had betrayed him.

"Well," he said, recovering, smoothing his coat. "Well. The asset has opinions. That's within tolerance." He produced a second document from his coat, smaller, and laid it on a flat stone at the roadside, weighting it with a pebble. "A notice of claim, keeper. Seven days. Produce proof of guardianship, or surrender the child, or the Court escalates—and escalation, I promise you, is the one department where we truly excel." He mounted. His horse turned without being asked. "It has been a genuine pleasure. You're more interesting than your file suggests."

They rode back down the pass the way they'd come, and the snow closed over their tracks, and the only evidence any of it happened was the notice on the stone and the single footprint that was already filling with shadow instead of meltwater.

* * *

Wren picked up the notice before I could stop her. She held it at arm's length, frowning at it upside down, then righted it and studied the script with the fierce concentration of a child doing sums. Her lips moved. Whatever language it was written in, she read it faster than I ever could.

"What does it say?" I asked.

She looked at me. Then she did something new: she took my hand—her fingers freezing-cold and utterly unbothered about it—and pressed my palm flat against the paper, the same gesture she'd made against the window glass.

And for half a heartbeat I felt it. Not read it—*felt* it, the way you feel heat off a stove. A weight. A hook, sunk into something soft at the center of her, with a long grey line running away beneath the mountains, and something patient at the other end of the line, reeling, reeling, reeling.

Seven days, the paper said, in the part of me that understood without reading.

She let go. I snatched my hand back like a man burned, which, in fairness, I was.

"They'll come back," I said stupidly. "In seven days."

Wren nodded. Then she pointed at the notice, and then at me, and then—delicately, with one small finger—at the space directly over my heart, where a name used to live.

"No," I told her. "No. Whatever you're suggesting, whatever Brannoc's going to suggest, whatever that look means—no. I'm the gatekeeper. I keep a gate. That's the whole of my story, and it's a short one, and I like it short."

She kept looking at me. Children have a talent for silence that can flay a grown man, and she had it in abundance, and underneath the silence, faint as a bell rung in another room, I could feel that half-soul of hers turning toward me like a compass needle.

Because there was a precedent. I knew there was a precedent. I had *been* the precedent, once, in a life I'd paid good money to forget—and the payment, I remembered suddenly, with a cold that had nothing to do with the snow, had been made to *them*.

Which meant they had a file on me too.

I looked down the empty pass, at the smooth unbroken snow, and for the first time in twenty years the silence of Thornfell felt less like peace and more like the held breath before a knock.

"One week," I said to Wren. "Then we ride. There's a man in the valley who owes me a hangover and knows contract law."

She smiled. It was the first smile I'd seen on her, and it was missing a tooth, and it scared me more than the grey men had.
