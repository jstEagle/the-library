## 5. Smuggler's Roads

There are three ways through the mountains at Thornfell, and the maps only know one of them.

The first is the King's Road, which is wide, well-kept, and watched — useless to a man carrying a stolen child away from three polite monsters in grey wool. The second is the Shepherd's Cut, which floods in spring and avalanches in winter and is therefore beloved of shepherds and madmen. And the third is the road I have spent twenty years pretending I don't know: the smugglers' paths, stitched together out of goat trails, dry riverbeds, an abandoned mine shaft, and one stretch that is technically the inside of the mountain itself.

I had sworn never to walk it again. But oaths, I was learning this week, are like doors — they only hold as long as nobody on the other side really wants them open.

"Stay close," I told Wren, "and if I say *down*, you get down."

She looked up at me with those eyes that were too old for her face and too young for her soul, and she nodded once, gravely, like a general accepting terms. Then she reached up and took two fingers of my hand in her whole fist, because her hand was too small to hold more than that, and we went down into the dark together.

I want it noted, for whatever ledger keeps accounts of men like me, that I did not take her hand. She took mine. There's a difference, and the difference matters, or it did to me, at four in the morning, packing a bag I'd promised myself I'd never pack.

* * *

The smugglers' roads begin behind my own hearth, which is either excellent planning or a confession, depending on how charitable you're feeling. Twenty years ago, when I built the gatehouse at Thornfell Pass, I built it the way a guilty man builds everything: with two exits. One faces the King's Road, where I could be seen being boring and reliable. The other goes down through the old cellar, past the wine racks I never filled, into a seam in the rock that the mountain forgot to close all the way.

Wren carried her own bundle. It contained: one wool dress, one heel of bread, the drawing of the door she'd made on my kitchen table, and nothing else. Children travel light when everything they own fits inside themselves.

The seam in the rock was tight enough that I had to turn sideways, and Wren slipped through ahead of me like water finding its level. That should have frightened me. A child who moves through stone like it's glad to see her. Instead I found myself thinking, absurdly, *good girl*, and hating myself for the warmth of it.

Here is what nobody tells you about keeping people at arm's length: your arms get tired. Twenty years of holding them out, and they ache like weather coming on. And here is what nobody tells you about a child who can't speak your language: she doesn't need to. She just needs to reach up and hold two of your fingers, and suddenly your arms aren't holding anything off anymore. They're holding *her*.

I didn't ask for this. I want that in the record too.

* * *

We came out of the seam above the Hollow Stair around dawn, if you could call it dawn — the light came down the pass grey and grudging, like a debt collector itself. Below us the smugglers' path switchbacked along the cliff face, invisible unless you knew to look for the cairns shaped like sleeping birds. I'd learned these paths from a man named Corvin Hale, who'd run brandy, salt, and occasionally fugitives through the pass for forty years and taught me the routes partly for coin and partly, I think, because he liked the idea of his knowledge outliving him.

Corvin was dead now. Most of the people who'd known anything about me were dead now. It's one way to keep a secret.

Wren stood at the lip of the stair and looked down at the drop — a good sixty feet of air between the path and the frozen river below — and then looked at me, and pointed at the drop, and then at herself, and raised her eyebrows. The whole conversation happened without a word. *You want me to walk down there? Me?*

"There's a rail," I said, pointing at the rope line Corvin's people had strung along the worst stretch. "Mostly."

She considered this. Then she stepped onto the path ahead of me, small boots sure on the frost-slick stone, and I realized she wasn't afraid of heights because she probably wasn't entirely convinced by the concept of falling. A child whose body had already been signed away. What's gravity to someone who belongs to somebody else?

I followed her down and hated every step, not because of the drop but because of what it meant: I was running. Odile Kavanagh, who had spent two decades standing perfectly still at a door so that nothing would ever come through it, was now moving, fast, toward the very thing he'd built his life to avoid. Because a barefoot girl had knocked during a blizzard and said a word in a dead tongue, and some part of me I'd buried under twenty years of routine had sat up in its grave and answered.

*Down*, I'd told her. *If I say down, you get down.* As if I were the one protecting her. As if she hadn't already fallen further than any cliff could take her.

* * *

By midday we were in the low country, where the pines gave way to birch and the snow went soft and dirty at the edges. The village of Marrowgate sat in the valley like something the mountains had coughed up and couldn't be bothered to swallow — thirty houses, one chapel, two taverns, and a population whose collective ambition was to be left alone until spring.

Perfect. Nobody in Marrowgate asked questions. Asking questions in a smuggling town is like ringing a bell in a library: technically allowed, socially fatal.

I left Wren in the shadow of the chapel wall with instructions I delivered mostly in gestures, since my vocabulary of her language remained at exactly one word — the word she'd spoken at my door, the one that meant *door*, or maybe *help*, or maybe both — and her vocabulary of mine was growing at a rate that frankly alarmed me. She'd already mastered *no*, *bread*, *stay*, and *bad idea*, the last one learned from context, watching me argue with myself over the map.

"Bad idea," she repeated now, solemnly, as I tucked her behind the woodpile.

"It's not a bad idea."

"Bad idea," she said again, with the serene confidence of a prophet.

Children are always right about the things you least want them to be right about. I gave her the bread and went to find Brannoc.

* * *

You could smell the second tavern before you saw it — spilled ale and wet wool and the particular sourness of a room where everyone has stopped hoping for better. Brannoc held court in the corner by the fire, in the sense that a ruin holds court: he was there, he was large, and everyone stepped around him.

He'd been handsome once, I supposed. The bones were still there under the bloat, a big frame gone soft, hands that were too fine for the rest of him — long-fingered, ink-stained at the nails even now, even drunk, the hands of a man who'd been made for writing and had settled for lifting cups. He was arguing with nobody about something, which in Marrowgate passed for entertainment.

"Brannoc," I said.

He squinted up at me with the slow, swimming focus of the professionally drunk. "Don't know you."

"No. You don't." I set a silver piece on his table. His eyes tracked it the way a hawk tracks a vole. "But I know what you were. Undercourt counsel. Third chair, Office of Debts and Tithes, before they exiled you."

That sobered him faster than cold water would have. He glanced around the tavern — nobody was listening, nobody in Marrowgate listened to Brannoc anymore; that was the point of him — and then he leaned in, and the drunkard fell away from his face like a mask slipping, and underneath was something quick and sharp and deeply tired.

"Who told you that?"

"A dead man who owed me a favor. I need a contract read."

"I don't do that anymore." He pushed the silver back toward me with one finger. "I read nothing. Reading is how I ended up here."

"This contract has a child's name on it."

Something moved behind his eyes. He picked up the silver anyway — habit, I suppose, or hunger — and turned it over twice. "Whose child?"

"Not mine. Yet." I heard myself say it and flinched. "Under my protection. For now."

Brannoc looked at me for a long moment, and I watched the arithmetic happen behind his face — the same arithmetic, I'd learn later, that he'd once done for a living, weighing claims and consideration and the going rate of a man's silence. Whatever sum he arrived at, he sighed, drained his cup, and stood up with the groan of a man whose body had filed formal complaints against his decisions years ago.

"Bring the paper," he said. "And bring the child. Contracts about children read differently when the child's in the room. They read *worse*. But they read true."

* * *

Wren had not stayed behind the woodpile. She had, instead, befriended the chapel cat, an enormous scarred tom with half an ear missing, and the two of them were sitting in the snow regarding each other with mutual professional respect. When she saw me come around the corner with Brannoc looming behind, she stood, brushed off her dress, and looked the exiled lawyer up and down with an expression I can only describe as *auditing*.

Brannoc stopped dead in the lane.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you stupid, brave, doomed man." He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at her — at the space around her, maybe, at whatever a man trained in the Undercourt's law could see hanging off a child like a creditor's lien. "Half-souled. They've taken title already. Who signed?"

"Her parents."

"Of course they did." He rubbed his face with those fine ink-stained hands. "They always do. Love makes terrible collateral, and desperate love makes the *best* collateral, because desperate people sign anything and call it hope." He crouched down to her level, which clearly cost him, and spoke slowly, loudly, in the way hearing people speak to the deaf — except he wasn't speaking our tongue at all. He was speaking hers. The dead tongue. The words came out of his mouth round and old and wrong in the daylight, like a song sung in a graveyard.

Wren's eyes went wide. She answered — a flood of it, fast, urgent, her hands moving, her voice cracking on the edges of sounds she hadn't made in weeks.

"What did she say?" I demanded.

Brannoc straightened up slowly. He looked older than he had five minutes ago, and sicker, and more sober than he'd been in years.

"She said," he translated, and his voice caught, "she said, *the tall one keeps the door. Ask him what name he buried.*"

The lane went very quiet. Somewhere a shutter banged. The scarred cat yawned.

"She remembers," Brannoc said, watching me. "Whatever they took from you, Kavanagh — however clean you bought your escape — it didn't take everywhere. It didn't take *below*. And it seems it didn't take in children who speak the old tongue, because the old tongue doesn't forget what it's said to whom." He held out his hand. "The contract. Now. Before those grey gentlemen finish their tea and start walking."

I gave him the paper. My hands were steady, which surprised me, because the rest of me felt like the cliff path — narrow, exposed, a long way down.

Brannoc read. I watched his face do several things it clearly didn't want to do. And when he finished, he folded the contract with the care of a man handling something venomous, and said:

"We need to talk somewhere with a lock on the door. And Kavanagh—" he glanced at Wren, who was watching us both with those too-old eyes, her fist still curled around the memory of my fingers, "—you'd better sit down for the terms. Because whoever drew this up knew their craft, and I say that as the man who taught half of them."

He paused at the tavern door, silhouetted against the smoky light, and added, almost gently:

"They gave you a month in the clause. You've used nine days getting here. So let's hope, old friend, that you read faster than you run."
