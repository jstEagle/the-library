## 6. The Lawyer in Exile

There are three things a man learns when he drinks himself out of the Undercourt and into the world of mud and honest bread: one, that ale is a poor substitute for law but an adequate substitute for memory; two, that villagers will forgive a drunk anything except sobriety, which frightens them; and three, that the past does not stay bought.

I was on my third mug of the morning — it was barely past noon, but I keep generous hours — when the door of the Sow's Rest opened and let in a slice of winter along with the gatekeeper of Thornfell Pass.

I knew him at once, though he'd have been insulted to hear it. Twenty years had put grey in his beard and a stoop in his shoulders, but I'd have known that walk anywhere: the walk of a man who has spent two decades listening for footsteps behind him. Exiles recognize each other the way dogs recognize thunder. We don't need introductions. We just need to be very careful.

"Brannoc," he said.

"Odile," I said, because that was the name he wore these days, and I am nothing if not courteous. "Sit down before you drip on something valuable. The floor, for instance."

He sat. And then he did the thing that made my stomach drop through the floorboards into whatever cellar the Undercourt keeps beneath all cellars: he set a child on the bench beside him.

A small girl, maybe seven years old, with frost-white hair and eyes the color of deep water over old stone. She was barefoot in February. Her feet were clean. That detail alone would have told me everything if nothing else had, but there was plenty else. There was the way she looked at the room — not like a child looks at a tavern, all noise and wonder, but like a customs officer looks at cargo. Cataloguing. Weighing. There was the way the candle flames leaned toward her, very slightly, the way flowers lean toward a window.

And there was her shadow, which sat a half-second behind her movements, like a servant who has grown lazy about pretending to obey.

"Well," I said, and reached for the ale, and found my hand wasn't steady enough to trust with it. "Well," I said again, because lawyers are trained never to say what they actually mean on the first attempt.

"She needs someone who can read this." Odile put a folded paper on the table between us. Grey paper. The kind that isn't made anywhere above ground. "I can read some of it. Not all. And what I can read, I don't want to believe."

I didn't touch it. Not yet. A man in my former profession develops certain instincts, and the strongest of them is: *never touch the contract until you've decided whether you're going to be its lawyer or its victim.*

"Who are you?" I asked the girl instead.

She looked at me. She opened her mouth. What came out was not any language spoken under any sun I know. It was older than the kingdoms, older than the roads, older than the word *kingdom*. It was the tongue they speak below, where the candles never gutter and the wine is always someone else's blood-price, and hearing it come out of a child's mouth in a mountain tavern was like hearing a lullaby sung by a landslide.

"Iron-tongue," Odile said quietly. "She can't speak ours. She draws doors instead." He showed me the edge of a page in his pocket-book, covered in small, obsessive sketches of the same door, drawn from every angle a door can have. "Her name's Wren. I named her that. It's not hers."

"No," I said. "It wouldn't be."

Because here is the thing about children sold below: they lose their names first. Names are collateral. You cannot hold title to a soul until you've stripped it of everything it might answer to. I knew that. I had *written* that, once, in clauses so elegant that the judges below still use them, or did, before I left — before I was helped to leave, which is the more accurate verb.

I picked up the paper at last.

* * *

You should understand something about Undercourt contracts before I tell you what this one said. They are not documents. Documents record agreements. Undercourt contracts *are* the agreement — the thing itself, wearing ink as a disguise. When you sign one, you are not writing your intention down. You are building a small door in yourself, and the other party keeps the key.

This one was written in the high formal register, the kind we used for sales of significant property. I read the opening clause and felt my breakfast turn over.

*"Be it known to all parties, witnessed and unwitnessed, that herein is conveyed, absolutely and irrevocably, one soul entire—"*

"Not entire," I murmured. My finger stopped on the third line. "Half. Look. *One-half portion of soul, being the greater portion, together with name, true name, and all appurtenances thereof.*"

"What does that mean for her?"

"It means she's walking around with less than half of herself, and the half she has left is only on loan." I kept reading. The sellers' signatures were at the bottom — a farm couple, by the look of the names, the letters wobbly with either illiteracy or tears, probably both. Below their marks, in the space where the consideration should be listed, was the standard formula: *"in satisfaction of debts incurred, nature and extent of said debts to be determined solely by the Creditor."*

That phrase. *Solely by the Creditor.* I wrote that phrase. I was twenty-six years old and brilliant and I thought I was drafting protections, because every clause I ever drafted was technically fair — that was the genius of the place, everything was *technically* fair, fairness was the trap itself — and I told myself the people signing understood what they were signing.

They never understood. That was rather the point.

"How much do they owe?" Odile asked.

"The court will decide. That's the beauty of it, from the court's perspective. The parents signed away the greater part of their daughter's soul against a debt whose size only the court determines. Which means the debt can grow. Debts below always grow. Interest isn't charged, exactly — it's *accrued*, the way moss accrues, the way grief accrues. Every year the debt is uncollected, it gets heavier, and every year the girl gets lighter." I set the paper down. My hands wanted a drink and I denied them, mostly out of spite toward myself. "The collectors came for the soul already. That's done; that's why her shadow lags. The next visit is for the body."

"They said as much."

"Of course they did. They're polite about it. They're always polite. Politeness is how you know it's serious. Nobody below bothers being polite about trivia." I finally looked at the girl — Wren — properly. She was watching me read with an expression no seven-year-old should own: the patient, terrible patience of someone who has already learned that adults take a long time to say no to each other on her behalf.

I looked away first. Remember that. When the story gets told later, remember that the exiled lawyer looked away first.

* * *

Odile bought us a private corner and another hour, and I laid it out for him the way I'd have laid it out for any client, except that I've never had a client I liked, so I laid it out gentler than usual, which for me means I only insulted the situation and not the man.

"The sale is binding," I said. "I'm sorry. I know you were hoping otherwise. Everyone hopes otherwise, right up until the moment they learn otherwise. The signatures are genuine — I can feel them from here, the way you can feel heat off a stove. Willing signatures, made in full ceremony, with witnesses. Probably a priest of some hedge-faith who took a fee and didn't ask questions. The court holds legal claim to the greater portion of her soul, and by extension, since the body houses the remainder, a claim of custody over the whole child. They'll collect. They always collect. The only questions are when, and what, and whether anyone standing nearby gets pulled into the current."

"There must be a way out. There's always a way out. That's what you people do — you build the maze and you leave one door in it."

"'You people.'" I smiled at him, and it was not a kind smile, and I saw him notice that. "Yes. I suppose I'm still 'you people,' no matter how much ale stands between me and the job. All right, gatekeeper. Let's talk about ways out, since you asked, and since asking is free while answers cost."

I counted them off on my fingers, the way I used to do for clients in the candlelit antechambers, back when my fingers were steady and my conscience was a theoretical exercise.

"One: repayment. The parents pay the debt in coin or goods or labor. Impossible. The court sets the figure after collection begins, and the figure is always set precisely beyond reach. That's not cruelty, mind you. It's design. Two: trade. Another soul of equivalent value offered in exchange. Possible, in theory. In practice, 'equivalent value' is assessed by the court, and the court finds most surface souls charmingly rustic. Three: escape. Run far enough that the claim can't be enforced." I shook my head. "The claim isn't enforced by men, gatekeeper. It's enforced by the contract itself. Distance doesn't dilute it. Oceans don't dilute it. The girl could stand on the far side of the world and her shadow would still lag, and one morning her shadow would arrive on time and she wouldn't."

Wren, who had been following none of the words and all of the tone, leaned slightly against Odile's arm. He put his hand on her shoulder without looking, the way you'd steady something you'd decided not to lose. I watched that happen and felt something in my chest that I hadn't felt in twenty years and resented feeling now.

"And four?" Odile said.

"Four isn't a way out. Four is a precedent. One precedent, in three hundred years, and it's so obscure that even the judges argue about whether it counts." I turned my mug slowly on the table. "A soul held by the court may be ransomed by another soul the court already holds a claim on. A debtor may pay a debtor's debt. That's all it says. It's buried in the third appendix of the Codex of Holdings, and it's never once been successfully invoked, because to invoke it you'd need someone the court already owns — truly owns, by contract, by name — willing to walk back through the front door and put themselves on the table."

The silence went on long enough that the tavern noise crept back in around it, all that cheerful mortal clatter, glasses and arguments and somebody's dog.

"That's a foolish law," Odile said at last.

"It's not a law. It's a loophole. Laws are what the court uses to trap you; loopholes are what the court leaves lying around for its own amusement. They enjoy watching someone try. It's sport to them." I drained the mug and wished it were stronger and knew better than to order anything stronger, because I needed my head, and my head was the last asset I had left. "Forget it. It requires a man with a claim on him, and claims like that don't fade, they fester, and men with claims like that don't survive twenty years above ground with their names intact—"

I stopped.

I want to be honest, since honesty is the one currency I never managed to counterfeit successfully: I stopped because I was looking at his hands.

They were resting flat on the table, and they were too still. Not calm-still. Held-still. The stillness of a man keeping his hands from doing something, and the something was either reaching for a weapon or reaching for a door. And I remembered — the way you remember things you've spent twenty years not remembering — that the tithe-children who escaped the Undercourt were rare, and the ones who escaped *whole* were rarer than comets, and the ones who escaped whole and then built themselves a life guarding a sealed door in a mountain pass were—

"You're not a gatekeeper," I said softly. "You're a lock. And locks are made from something that used to be free."

"Careful, Brannoc."

"Oh, I'm always careful. Careful is my whole ruined profession." I leaned forward. "What did you give them? Your name? Your memories? Both? Because there's a specific clause for both, I wrote variants of it, and if you gave them both, then the court doesn't just hold a claim on you, gatekeeper. The court holds *you*. Filed. Shelved. Available for retrieval upon presentation of proper cause."

His face did something I imagine it hadn't done in twenty years. It cracked — just a hairline, just for a moment — and underneath the crack was not fear. Fear I could have handled. Underneath the crack was recognition, the awful relief of a man who has carried a secret so long that being seen carrying it feels almost like setting it down.

"You knew," he said. "When I walked in. You knew what I was."

"I knew there was weather coming off you," I said. "Exiles recognize each other. I told myself it was the coat. It was never the coat." I rubbed my eyes. Somewhere behind my ribs, an old guilt that I'd buried under ale and irony shifted its weight, the way a sleeper shifts, the way you shift when you realize the bed you're in belongs to someone dead. "Here's what I can't stop thinking about, gatekeeper. Those signatures at the bottom of that contract. Wobbly letters. Farm folk. Somebody explained the terms to them — that's required, there's a whole beautiful clause about disclosure, I *drafted* the disclosure clause — and somebody explained it in language designed to be understood as little as possible, and they signed anyway, because the alternative was watching their family starve, and the court knows exactly how to price desperation. I know how it's priced. I set prices like that for eleven years."

Wren tugged my sleeve.

I looked down. She was holding up the pocket-page of door-sketches she'd taken from Odile, and she pointed at one drawing — a door with thirteen panels, iron-banded, wrong in a way I couldn't name — and then she pointed at me, and then she made a shape with her small hands that I recognized across three hundred years of legal tradition: the gesture for *witness*. For *you have seen this, therefore you are bound*.

Children below learn the gestures before they learn the words. Someone had taught her that. Someone below had taught a sold child to bind adults with her small hands, because it was the only power anyone had ever given her.

"Yes," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. "I witness it. I see the door. I see the girl. I see the debt." I looked at Odile, and the cynical pity I'd been wearing like a coat all afternoon finally curdled into the thing underneath it, the thing I'd fled rather than feel, and I heard myself say the sentence I'd sworn never to say to any client:

"There may be a way. It's a bad way. It runs straight through the front door of the place I burned my life escaping, and it starts with a question I need you to answer honestly, because everything depends on it, and because I am done — *done* — spending my exile pretending I can't hear what I hear."

He waited.

"What did they call you," I said, "before you were Odile?"

Outside, the wind came down off the pass and rattled the shutters like a creditor's knuckles, and the gatekeeper of Thornfell Pass closed his eyes as if the question had arrived twenty years late and had been expected every day since.
