## 7. Loopholes and Wolves

There are three kinds of clients in this world, and I have served all of them: the guilty who know it, the innocent who don't, and the desperate who will believe anything you tell them if you say it slowly enough. Odile Kavanagh was a new fourth kind—the man who already knew what I was going to say and had come anyway, because hope is a disease that doesn't care how educated its host is.

He sat across my table with the child asleep against his shoulder, and he watched me read the contract with the expression of a man watching a surgeon open his own chest.

I'll give him this: he didn't interrupt. Most laymen can't help themselves. They gasp at the wrong clauses, argue with the preamble, ask what the fancy words mean as though the fancy words were hiding something from them rather than from their opponents. Odile just watched my eyes move down the page, and every time they stopped, his jaw tightened a fraction more, like a man counting the cost of each line item before the bill arrived.

The contract itself was beautiful work. I say that with professional grief. The vellum was Undercourt stock—grown, not made, in the dark pastures below Thornfell where the light comes up through the ground instead of down onto it. The signatures of Wren's parents were genuine; I could taste the duress in the ink, but duress doesn't void a signature, it only seasons it. And the seal at the bottom was the old sigil of the Court of Beneath, pressed deep enough that the wax had gone through to the table beneath it.

"Well?" Odile said, when I'd been quiet too long for even his patience.

I poured myself a drink. Not because I needed one—I've been sober eleven years, or eleven years adjacent—but because a lawyer who delivers bad news without a prop is a lawyer who gets thrown out of houses. The glass gives people something to watch instead of your face.

"The sale is binding," I said. "The court holds legal claim to the child's soul. Half of it, technically—they took title at birth, which is why she's been walking through blizzards barefoot and unbothered. Cold can't get a purchase on someone who's only half here." I turned the page. "The remaining half comes due on her ninth birthday. That's—" I did the arithmetic on my fingers, an affectation that makes clients feel superior and buys me time, "—four months. Perhaps five."

"And if it isn't paid?"

"Then collectors come for the body. Whole. Body and soul travel together after that; it's the law of the Court of Beneath, and it's older than any kingdom you'd care to name." I set the glass down. "You want me to tell you there's a way out. There isn't. Not a clean one."

His hand moved—not toward me, not toward the door, but to the child's back, settling there like it belonged. I noticed that. Twenty years of keeping the world at arm's length, and this man had developed the reflexes of a father without ever having practiced.

"There's always a loophole," he said. "You told me yourself you drafted contracts like this once. Draftsmen leave themselves doors."

"Draftsmen leave themselves doors so *they* can walk out," I said. "Not strangers."

But he was right, damn him. That was the worst part. There *was* a precedent, and I had been circling around it since the moment I saw the seal, the way you circle around a wound you're afraid to look at directly.

* * *

Let me tell you about the Case of the Ransomed Tithe, because it matters, and because no one above ground has ever heard of it, and because I need you to understand that what happened next wasn't sentiment. It was jurisprudence. Mostly.

Two hundred years ago—give or take; the Undercourt keeps time by debts, not years—a tithe-child was sold to the court, the way tithe-children always were. Ordinary story. Ordinary misery. But the child had an older brother, and the brother did something no one had thought to do before: he went down and offered himself as surety. Not a trade. A ransom. He said: *you hold a claim on my sister; I offer you a claim on me instead, and since you already hold a claim on me—you raised me, you fed me, you own three years of my labor under the old fosterage laws—the exchange is lawful.*

And the court, being what it is, couldn't refuse. Not because it was merciful—it has never once been merciful, mercy would crack its foundations like frost in stone—but because it is *lawful*, and lawful things must follow their own law or stop being lawful altogether. The precedent stands. It has stood for two hundred years, gathering dust in the archives beneath the mountain, because in two hundred years no one has ever been able to use it.

Do you see the problem? The ransomer must be someone the court *already holds a claim on*. Someone with debt in their blood. Someone whose name is in the ledger.

I looked up from my notes, and Odile was watching me with those grey eyes that never quite finished a sentence, and I thought: *oh, you fool. Oh, magnificent, doomed fool. You knew. You've known since the day the girl knocked on your door speaking a dead tongue, and you brought her to me anyway, hoping I wouldn't see it.*

"You already know what I'm going to say," I said.

"No."

"You do. Because you've known longer than I have. You looked at that contract and you didn't see a dead end—you saw a door, and you've been standing in front of it praying I'd be too drunk or too stupid to point out what's written over the lintel." I leaned forward. "A soul may be ransomed by someone the court already holds a claim on. So the question, gatekeeper, the only question that matters, is: does the Court of Beneath hold a claim on Odile Kavanagh?"

The fire popped. The child stirred against his shoulder and settled again.

"Because I'll tell you what I see," I went on, softer now, because cruelty is only useful when it's precise. "I see a man who lives alone at a sealed door in a pass nobody needs guarded anymore, checking wards that haven't failed in twenty years. I see a man who knows the old tongue well enough to recognize it in a child's mouth but claims he learned it from books. I see a man who flinched—not flinched, *recoiled*—when I mentioned the tithe-ledger, as though he'd seen his own page in it." I spread my hands. "Odile Kavanagh is a name like a coat worn over something. What's underneath?"

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he stood, carefully, the way you stand when you're holding something breakable, and laid the sleeping child on my bed with a gentleness that would have shamed me if I'd had any shame left to spare. And then he turned around, and everything reasonable had gone out of his face.

"I buried that name," he said. "Twenty years ago. I paid for it. I bled for it. I gave up—" His voice caught, and he mastered it the way you'd master a dog that wanted to lunge. "Whatever I was, whoever I was, that man is dead. I am Odile Kavanagh. I keep the gate. That is all."

"That is *not* all," I said, "because the court doesn't recognize funerals unless it officiates them. If you were theirs—if you were ever theirs—then the claim didn't die when you ran. Claims don't die. They *sleep*. And the moment you walk through that door carrying a soul they hold paper on, every clerk beneath the mountain is going to smell you, and they are going to remember, and they are going to drag twenty years of accrued interest out of your hide—"

"*Enough.*"

It wasn't loud. That was the frightening thing. He said it quietly, the way the mountain says things, and the fire guttered in the hearth as though something enormous had leaned close to listen.

We stared at each other across my miserable little room, the exiled lawyer and the exiled whatever-he-was, and between us slept a child who was half a person on a technicality, and outside the wind came down off the pass carrying the smell of snow and, faintly, unmistakably, of candlewax.

"They're coming," I said. "The collectors. You stalled them once, but the court is patient the way winter is patient. In four months they collect, or we find another way, or—" I gestured at the whole shape of our situation. "Or you go down there and give them a name worth having."

"I would die first."

"Yes," I agreed. "That's rather the point. Dying is cheap. The court accepts payment in worse currencies than death." I rubbed my eyes. "Get some sleep. Both of you. Tomorrow I start drafting, and you—" I pointed at him, and my hand wasn't entirely steady, "—you decide whether you're going to be honest with me before the court makes you honest with everyone."

He gathered the child without another word. At the door he paused, and for one moment the mask slipped—not into anger, but into something so raw and young that I understood, finally, exactly how old the wound was.

"You don't know what you're asking me to dig up," he said.

"No," I admitted. "But I know what's asking you to dig it up. She is. And children don't care what a debt costs the adults. They only care whether anyone pays it."

He left. I sat alone with the contract and my cold drink and the memory of my own name signed at the bottom of documents very much like it, and I thought about how the past is like a wolf: it doesn't knock. It waits until you open the door for some other reason, and then it walks in like it owns the place.

Somewhere out in the dark, I could have sworn I heard one howling.
