## 8. The Name He Buried

The storm had been gone for two days, but the fire was still the best company in the house.

Wren sat cross-legged on the hearthstone with her blanket pulled over her head like a hood, only her face showing, watching me the way she watched everything—without blinking, without hurry, as if I were a door she intended to open eventually. I'd been putting this off for three days. I'd run out of chores to hide behind. The woodpile was stacked to the rafters, every seal in the pass checked twice, and I'd even mended the hole in my second-best coat that I'd been ignoring since autumn.

There was nothing left between me and the telling except the telling.

"You know I'm not good at stories," I said.

She tilted her head. *Liar,* said the tilt.

"Fine." I fed another split of pine to the fire and watched it catch. "But you don't get to interrupt. And when I'm done, you can decide whether you still want to sleep under my roof."

Her eyes went wide and fierce at that, and she shook her head so hard the blanket slipped off one shoulder. She grabbed her slate—the one Brannoc had sent up with the smugglers, along with a note reading *the contract is real, the deadline is real, stop being a coward*—and wrote in her careful, crooked letters:

**I STAY.**

"All right," I said. My voice came out rougher than I meant it to. "Then here's what you're staying in."

* * *

The man you know as Odile Kavanagh has been gatekeeper of Thornfell Pass for twenty years. That part is true. What isn't true is that Odile Kavanagh has been alive for twenty years. A name is a small thing—a sound, a shape on a ledger—but down where you came from, girl, names are load-bearing walls. Take one out and the whole house comes down.

So when I tell you that I sold mine, understand I'm not being poetic. There was a transaction. There was a price. There is a page in a book below this mountain with a scorch mark where my name used to be, and everything I was before I bought that door shut went into the fire of it.

Before Odile, there was a boy called Odran.

He was born above ground, in a village not so different from the ones in the valley below us, to parents who loved him—I have to believe they loved him—in the way that poor people love: fiercely, and with arithmetic. When the tithe-year came around, and the grey-clothed collectors walked the roads with their ledgers and their patient smiles, his father did the sums. One child sold, and the rest of the family fed through seven winters. Seven winters. You could buy a lot of bread with a boy.

Or maybe there was no arithmetic at all. Maybe they simply wanted the debt gone. I've spent twenty years deciding which version to remember, and I've never once managed to remember either one honestly. That's the thing about selling your memories, Wren—you don't get to choose which ones go. You hand over the whole chest and let them pick through it. What came back to me was mostly weather. Rain on a roof. The smell of a particular stew. A woman's hands kneading dough, no face attached. I don't know if those hands were my mother's. I don't know if I ever knew.

Odran went down the mountain at six years old, barefoot like you, though he felt the cold, because he was only half-bought then. His soul was signed over entire, but bodies take longer to collect. The court is patient about bodies. They like them seasoned.

And here is the part I need you to hear properly, because it's the part I'm most ashamed of, and shame kept too long goes rotten and poisons everything near it.

It wasn't terrible, at first. That's the trap. Nobody chained him. Nobody starved him. The Undercourt raises its tithe-children like gardeners raise orchard trees—carefully, tenderly, with an eye always on the fruit. He had tutors. He had warm rooms and candlelit halls and food that tasted like music. He learned the old tongue until he dreamed in it. He learned etiquette until etiquette was bone. He learned that a promise made in the old tongue cannot be broken, only paid, and that the court's whole architecture—every hall, every mask, every courteous knife—is built out of promises that someone, somewhere, regrets.

They were kind to him the way a lender is kind. Every kindness was an entry in a book.

And the boy—here's the shameful part, are you listening?—the boy *liked it*. For years, he liked it. He forgot there was anything else to compare it to. He forgot the rain-on-the-roof place entirely. He made a friend—a real friend, the first and last one—who was not a child of the court at all but something older, something with amber eyes and a laugh like ice cracking, who used to slip away from her own duties to race him along the underground river. She told him about the sky. She'd seen it once, from a high pass, before her own debts bound her to the dark. She described it so well that he began to ache with wanting a thing he couldn't remember ever having seen.

That friend's name was Maelis. You'll meet her, if the road takes us where I think it must. Be polite. Politer than politeness. She has reason to hate me, and unlike the court, she doesn't dress hers up in courtesy.

What broke the spell wasn't cruelty. It was a ledger.

When Odran turned sixteen, they showed him his account. It was a beautiful thing, his account—illuminated margins, gold leaf, columns in a hand so fine it looked like frost. Twenty years of kindnesses, itemized. Tutoring. Bread. Warmth. Friendship, entered under Maelis's name, marked *disbursed, awaiting return*. And at the bottom, the balance outstanding, the sum total of a life lived on credit: his body, due upon maturity. Due soon. The court doesn't eat souls the way the village stories say—they wear them. They fold a soul into the fabric of the halls, and the halls grow warmer, and the candles burn brighter, and somewhere in the glow there's a boy forever setting tables or carrying candles or laughing at masked jokes, and he is very comfortable, and he is never again anyone.

Odran read his account twice. Then he asked the question that saved and damned him in the same breath: *what would it cost to close the book?*

You have to understand how the court thinks. They don't want your money; they mint their own. They want weight. And there is almost nothing in creation heavier than a soul that wants out—that's rare coin down there, girl, rarer than gold, because nearly everyone who wants out has already spent the parts of themselves that could have bargained. A whole soul, intact, freely offered against itself—the clerks got excited. I could see it behind their masks, the way a dog gets excited. They'd never held one.

But there was a complication, and the complication was the door.

Thornfell Pass wasn't sealed yet, in those days. It was merely closed, and closed doors below the mountain are like unattended purses above it. Things came up. Things went down. The court had use of the pass, and the pass had a keeper's line going back further than any kingdom, and the current keeper was old and tired and owed the court a favor of his own. When Odran asked what it would cost to close his book, the clerks consulted among themselves, and then the oldest of them smiled—it's worse when they smile—and named the price.

His name. His memories. Everything that made him Odran, rendered up entire, and in exchange: his freedom, his body, and one service. He would walk out through Thornfell Pass, and he would seal the door behind him with the last of what he was, and the pass would stay shut for as long as he lived. A living lock. A man-shaped bolt. The court would lose its back door and gain, in trade, the single richest payment it had collected in a century.

Do you see the elegance of it? They didn't lose anything. They gained a fortune in soul-stuff *and* a locked door *and*, tucked away in the fine print where lawyers live, a claim. Because a soul sold—even a soul sold to buy its own freedom—is still a soul that passed through their hands. Somewhere in their books, in some margin, in some clause I was too empty-headed by then to read, they kept a thread. And threads, Wren, can be pulled.

I didn't know that part. I signed anyway. I'd have signed anything. I walked up those stairs feeling lighter every step, because I didn't understand yet that what I was shedding wasn't weight. It was ballast. By the time I reached the top I couldn't have told you why I was crying. I sealed the door with words I no longer understood in a language I no longer knew I spoke, and I sat down in the snow, and when I woke the next morning there was a man sitting in the snow, and the man's name was Odile Kavanagh, and he had papers that said so, and twenty years of memories that said so, and a hollow in his chest shaped exactly like everything he'd never been.

* * *

The fire had burned down to a red heart. Wren hadn't moved. Her eyes were enormous, and wet, and fixed on me with an expression I couldn't read until she lifted her slate.

**THEY TOOK YOUR MOM'S BREAD SMELL?**

I laughed. It came out cracked down the middle. "Yes. Among other things."

**THAT'S THE WORST ONE.**

"It is, isn't it." I rubbed my face. "You'd think losing your mother would be the worst one. But I don't remember her, so I can't miss her. The bread smell I remember missing. Does that make sense?"

She nodded slowly, gravely, the way she nodded at everything, as if I were a legal document she was reviewing. Then she wrote, and turned it around, and her letters had gone jagged:

**MY SOUL IS IN THEIR BOOK TOO. LIKE YOURS WAS.**

"Yes."

**DID IT HURT? WHEN THEY TOOK IT?**

I thought about lying to her. I thought about it for perhaps half a second, and then I looked at her face—six years old, maybe seven, barefoot in a blizzard, already sold once—and decided she'd earned better than comfort.

"No," I said. "That's the horror of it. It didn't hurt at all. It felt like setting down something heavy after a long walk. It felt like relief. And then the relief wore off, and underneath it there was just... less of me. Every year, a little less. I told myself it was age. I told myself everyone thins out. But it wasn't age, Wren. It was interest. The court doesn't take all at once. They lend you yourself back, piece by piece, and charge you for the privilege. That's why I stayed up here alone. Not because I liked the quiet. Because every person I let close was another entry in somebody's ledger, and I couldn't bear to be worth anything to them ever again."

She set down the slate. She got up—small, deliberate, blanket trailing like a misfit cloak—and crossed the hearthstones and climbed onto the bench beside me and leaned against my side with the full weight of a child who has decided trust is a door she's opening whether the man behind it likes it or not.

I put my arm around her. My hand wasn't steady. Hers was.

"I'm telling you this," I said to the top of her head, "because in a few days we're going to walk down to that door, and I'm going to open it, and everything I just described is going to try to pull me back the moment I do. The court keeps what it's paid for, and it paid for me fair and square—or it believes it did, which down there amounts to the same thing. So if I start walking wrong, if I start talking in that pretty dead language like I belong to it, you grab my hand. Hard. Can you do that?"

She looked up at me. And then she did something she had never done in all the weeks she'd been under my roof.

She spoke.

One word. Small and cracked, a voice unused so long it had rust on it, in the old tongue—the tongue I'd sworn I'd forgotten, the tongue that rose in my throat to meet hers like water finding its level. I knew it instantly. Of course I knew it. You don't sell a language out of your bones.

*Home.*

Not the word itself, exactly. The old tongue doesn't have *home* the way we do—home as a place. It has a word that means *the fire that waits for you*, and that's what she said, looking at me, meaning me, meaning this room, meaning the ridiculous woodpile and the mended coat and the twenty years of a man pretending a hollow was a life.

I held her and I didn't say anything for a long time, because if I opened my mouth I was going to come apart, and a man about to walk into the Undercourt can't afford to arrive in pieces.

But here's what I understood, sitting there with a bought-back child asleep against my ribs while the fire ticked and the mountain stood silent over us both: I had spent twenty years believing I'd escaped by selling myself, and that the sale was the shame. It wasn't. The shame was smaller and meaner than that. The shame was that I'd believed the court's arithmetic—that I was worth exactly what they'd paid, that the boy was gone, that the hollow was permanent. I'd audited myself with their ledger and declared myself worthless, and then spent two decades guarding a door to keep anyone from checking the math.

And the math was wrong. It had always been wrong. Maelis had raced him along the river. His mother's hands had kneaded bread. A mute child had just handed him back a word worth more than everything in their gilded books, free of charge, no interest, no terms.

The fire settled. Wren's breathing went long and even. I sat in the dark with my own name—both of my names—sitting in my chest like coals, and for the first time in twenty years, neither one burned.

Three days, Brannoc's note had said. Three days before the collectors' patience ran out and the law came for her body the way it had already come for her soul.

I banked the fire. I got down the old strongbox from behind the loose stone, and took out the key I hadn't touched in twenty years, and put it in my pocket, where it lay against my leg like a second heartbeat.

In the morning, we'd go down to the door. And I would have to say the name.
