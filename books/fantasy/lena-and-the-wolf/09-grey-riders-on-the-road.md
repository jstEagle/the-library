## 9. Grey Riders on the Road

The man Brannoc walks like a door that has been left open in a wind. He sways. He talks to himself. He calls the road *darling* when it turns and *traitor* when it doesn't.

I like him.

This is a strange thing for me to like, because I do not like most things. Most things are too loud or too bright or too full of mouths. But Brannoc's mouth is mostly kind-shaped, even when the words coming out of it are sour as old milk. And he does not ask me questions I cannot answer. He asks the big man Odile questions instead, and Odile answers with his shoulders more than his mouth, which is a language I understand better than any other.

We have been walking since the grey before morning. My feet know this road even though my eyes don't. That is how it is with me now: half of me knows things the other half was never told. The half that knows lives somewhere else, down under the mountain where the candles never go out, and sometimes it sends letters up through my blood. *Turn left at the split stone.* *The water ahead tastes of iron; don't drink.* *They are coming.*

That last one arrived while Brannoc was complaining about his boots.

I stopped walking. The forest went on without me for a moment — leaves finishing their falling, wind finishing its long sentence — and then everything caught up, and I said, in the voice I keep for the knowing-half:

"They're behind us."

Brannoc stopped mid-complaint like a clock with its gears pulled out. "Behind us," he repeated. "How far behind us, little bird?"

I held up my hand and showed him. Not a number. I don't have numbers for this. I showed him the feeling, which is cold, and the distance, which is close, and the certainty, which is total. He looked at my fingers for a long time and then at Odile, and something passed between the two men that had no words in it at all.

"Off the road," Odile said. His voice is like stones settling in a riverbed. "Now."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about me, if you are going to understand anything: I did not always know I was sold.

For a long time I thought everyone lived the way we lived. Cold house, hungry house, parents who looked at you the way you look at a purse you're afraid to open. I thought the night visitors were normal. Tall people in fine grey clothes who came at the dark of the moon and sat by our fire and drank tea they never touched and spoke in the old tongue, the tongue that lives in my mouth like a second set of teeth. I thought every child had a shadow that sometimes walked a little ahead of her, or a little behind, like a dog on a leash it didn't like.

Then one night my mother cried into her hands and my father wouldn't look at me, and the tall grey people wrote things in a book, and after that my shadow started walking toward the mountain whenever I wasn't paying attention. Pulling. Always pulling.

So no. I did not know I was sold. But some part of me knew, the way a lamb knows the shape of the gate it will be led through. And that part of me has been awake ever since the blizzard, ever since the door in the snow, ever since the big sad man with the buried name took me in out of the white and gave me soup and called me Wren.

Wren is not my name. But it fits through doors my real name can't fit through yet. So I wear it.

* * *

We left the road and went up through the trees, and Odile moved like the trees had told him their secrets years ago and he'd kept them. Branches parted for him. Stones offered their flat faces. Even the mud seemed embarrassed to hold him. Brannoc, meanwhile, fought the forest the way he fought everything — loudly, drunkenly, and with an undercurrent of surprising competence, like a man who had once been very good at something and resented having to prove it again.

"The collectors," Brannoc panted, "are not soldiers. You understand that, yes? They are clerks. They are *bureaucrats*. They cannot be fought, only delayed, and delay is a fine art I practiced for—" he counted on his fingers, lost count, gave up, "—a long time, before I was exiled for being too good at it."

"What happens if they catch us?" Odile asked. He asked it quietly, looking back down the way we'd come, so maybe he thought I couldn't hear. But my ears belong to the knowing-half now, more and more each day, and the knowing-half hears everything.

"They serve papers," Brannoc said. "And then the papers serve them. And then the girl goes home with them, and home, in this case, is a place with a great many candles and no windows." He wiped his mouth. "And anyone who stands between paper and its purpose becomes an accomplice. Accomplices go into the ledger too. There is always room in the ledger. It is the only thing about the Undercourt that is generous."

I felt my shadow pull. Hard. Toward the mountain, always the mountain, like a fish on a line.

*They are close,* said the knowing-half, in the voice that isn't a voice, the one that comes up through the soles of my feet. *They are reading the road. Roads remember who walks them.*

*Can roads lie?* I asked it. This is how we talk, the other half of me and I. Questions. It likes questions. It says questions are the only currency that holds its value underground.

*Roads can be bribed,* it said. *But these ones walk beside the road, not on it.*

* * *

They came at the hour when the light goes thin and everything looks like a memory of itself.

I saw them first because my shadow stood up. I want to be clear about this: my shadow, which had been lying flat and sulky on the ground all afternoon, stood up. Stretched. Turned its face — my face, but wrong, smoothed out, candlelit from below — toward the slope behind us, and pointed.

Three figures came down through the trees the way frost comes through a window. No hurry. No sound. Grey clothes so fine they made the forest look shabby, and faces so smooth and pleasant they made the forest look honest. The middle one carried a book. The book was open. The book was reading *us.*

"Wren," Odile said, and put his whole body between me and them, which was foolish and wonderful and made something hot bloom in my chest that I didn't have a name for yet. "Get behind me."

"No," said Brannoc, stepping sideways, which put him not behind Odile but *beside* him, which I understood later was the bravest geometry there is. "No, actually, let us be adults about—"

"Herr Brannoc." The middle collector smiled. Its smile was beautiful the way a trap is beautiful, all hinges and patience. "What a coincidence. We were just speaking of you. Your name appears in our margins."

"I appear in your *margins?*"

"As a note. A footnote, really. *Possible accomplice, pending review.*" The collector turned a page. The page turned itself. "You've been very helpful to our subject. Guidance. Shelter. Direction. These are services, Herr Brannoc, and services rendered to a debtor in flight are billable."

"You can't—" Brannoc's voice cracked down the middle like old ice. "I am exiled. I am *outside* your jurisdiction. That was the entire point of the exile, I negotiated it myself, clause by clause—"

"And clauses," said the collector gently, "can be amended. All it takes is a signature." It produced a pen. The pen was black and wet-looking and I knew, the way the knowing-half knows, that whatever it wrote would be true forever, whether it happened or not. "Or," it said, "you could step aside, and we amend nothing, and everyone keeps the names they were born with."

The forest held its breath. Even the wind stopped mid-sentence.

Odile didn't move. But I was standing close enough to him to feel it — the trembling. Not fear. Something older than fear. It was like standing next to a door that wants very badly to open.

"Take me instead," Odile said.

And the three collectors, all together, all at once, turned their smooth heads and *looked* at him. Really looked. The way you look at a locked box you suddenly remember the key to.

"Oh," said the middle one, softly, delightedly, terribly. "*Oh.* What interesting margins *you* have."

* * *

I want to tell you what happened next properly, but my memory of it is broken into pieces, the way ice breaks, along lines that were already there.

Piece one: the collectors stepped forward, and the step made no sound, and the space they crossed seemed shorter than it should have been, the way distances lie near the Undercourt's business.

Piece two: Brannoc shouted something legal — I think it was a citation, an appeal, some old law flung like a rock — and the lead collector caught it out of the air the way you'd catch a leaf, and looked at it, and said, "Expired," and Brannoc made a sound I hope I never hear a grown man make again.

Piece three: they took him. Not violently. That was the worst part. One of them simply offered an arm, courtier-fashion, as if inviting him to dance, and Brannoc looked at the arm, and at me, and at Odile, and I watched him decide. I watched a drunk coward choose, in the space of one breath, to be neither.

"Little bird," he said to me, quick and low, while the grey arm waited. "The precedent. Chapter and verse, the Ransom of the Held Soul — tell the big man, make him listen, make him *remember*—" And then the arm closed around his elbow, polite as a handshake, and the trees swallowed him, and the forest was quiet in the way a mouth is quiet after saying something final.

Piece four: the collectors turned back to us. To me. The middle one opened the book to a page I could feel humming against my own ribs, because it was my page, my entry, my name written in the old tongue in ink that was still wet after all this time.

"It's time to come home, little debtor," it said kindly. "Your parents signed. Your soul is ours. The body follows the soul. That is simply how ledgers work."

And my shadow — my traitor shadow, my leash-shadow — began to walk toward them, dragging me an inch, two inches, my heels furrowing the leaves—

* * *

Here is the thing nobody told me. Here is the thing my parents didn't know, or didn't care, or maybe did know and were counting on:

They took half. Only half.

A soul cut in two does not stop being a soul. It stops being *obedient*, is what it does. It stops fitting neatly in books. The Undercourt holds half of me down there among the candles, and that half is theirs, signed and sealed. But this half — the half that walks, and fears, and likes drunk lawyers and stone-voiced men who give away their soup — this half was never in the contract. This half is loose. This half is *mine.*

And when the collectors reached for me, and my shadow pulled, and Odile roared something wordless and huge, and the book opened wider to swallow my name —

this half of me got *angry.*

I don't remember deciding. I remember the feeling, which was winter. Which was the blizzard, the one I walked through barefoot, except now it came *out* of me instead of at me. The cold went down through my feet into the roots of the mountain and up through my hands into the air, and everywhere it touched, everything stopped.

The collectors stopped. Mid-stride, mid-smile, mid-word — the middle one's mouth stayed open around a syllable it would finish someday, but not today. The book stopped turning its own pages. Even the light stopped, hanging in the branches like honey. Frost bloomed across the grey coats in fern-patterns, beautiful, and I thought distantly: *I did that. The ugly thing inside me made something beautiful. Nobody told me it could do that either.*

Only two things kept moving.

One was Odile, who stood in the frozen air breathing steam and staring at me like I was a door he'd sealed twenty years ago finally cracking open.

The other was my shadow. My shadow stood at the edge of the frost-line, straining toward the collectors, and for the first time since I'd known it, it looked *afraid of me.*

Good, I thought, with a savagery that surprised me. *Be afraid. Everyone else gets to be afraid of things. Why shouldn't you.*

*Little one,* said the knowing-half, and its voice was different now — awed, almost gentle. *You froze the creditors. Do you understand what you did? Courts freeze debts. Debts do not freeze courts.*

*I don't care what I did,* I told it. *I care that we run.*

* * *

We ran.

Odile carried me when the ground steepened, and I felt his heart going like a hammer on a nail, and I pressed my cold-still-cold hands against his neck and felt him flinch and not complain. Behind us the frost broke. I heard it break — a sound like a chandelier letting go all at once — and then voices, smooth and unhurried and absolutely certain, calling down the slope after us:

"No rush, little debtor. Ledgers are patient. We'll see you at the mountain. We see *everyone* at the mountain."

But they didn't follow. Not really. They walked, unhurried, taking Brannoc with them, and I understood with a sick drop in my stomach that they didn't need to chase us. Every road bent toward the mountain eventually. They were simply going to arrive first, and wait, and add our names to the margins.

"Brannoc," Odile said once, hoarsely, not stopping. Just the name. Like setting a stone on a cairn.

"He told us something," I said into his ear. "Before they took him. About a ransom. About a held soul. He said make you remember."

Odile stumbled. Just one step. But I felt it through his whole body, a fault-line running through him, and when he spoke his voice had gone somewhere deep and dark and old.

"I remember," he said. "God help me, Wren. I remember everything."

Ahead of us, past the last ridge, the mountain stood up against the sky with its head in clouds, and my shadow pulled toward it, and this time — just for a moment — I pulled *back,* and for one stride, one single stride, my shadow walked beside me like a friend instead of a leash.

Then the mountain called, and it turned its face home, and I let it.

Because that was where we were going anyway. Down. All the way down. To give the court beneath the world exactly what it wanted —

and then, somehow, take it all back.
