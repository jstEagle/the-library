## 10. The Wolf-Queen's Toll

There is a saying among smugglers that the high passes belong to no one and the low passes belong to everyone, which is a polite way of saying that the low passes will rob you blind while the high passes will simply kill you. What the smugglers never say—because none of them know it, and I have spent twenty years making sure of it—is that there is a third territory. The middle country. The pine-dark folds between Thornfell and the deep valleys, where the snow lies blue in the shadows and the trees grow too close together to count.

That country belongs to Maelis.

I had hoped, with the particular hopeless hope of a man who knows better, to go around it. Brannoc's map—the one he'd scratched into a strip of birchbark with a burnt match, hands shaking, breath sour with last night's drink—showed a route along the western scree. It added two days. Two days with three grey collectors somewhere behind us and a child who left no footprints walking beside me, was not a price I minded paying.

Wren minded. Not the cold—she didn't feel the cold, which still unsettled me more than I let my face show—but the waiting. She had developed, over our four days on the run, a habit of stopping at intervals and looking back down the trail with her whole body, the way a deer does, all ears and spine. Each time she did it, I checked too. Each time: nothing. Empty white road. Which meant either that we were free, or that whatever followed us had learned patience, and I did not care for either option.

"Keep moving," I told her, for perhaps the hundredth time. She looked at me with an expression I was beginning to recognize as her version of speech—a flat, ancient *obviously* that no eight-year-old should be able to produce—and kept moving.

We made the tree line at dusk on the second day, and that was when the birds stopped.

You learn to hear silence in the mountains. There is the ordinary kind, the kind that means weather coming, and then there is the other kind, the kind where every small thing with wings has decided, unanimously and without discussion, to be elsewhere. Wren felt it before I did. She stopped mid-stride, turned her face up into the pines, and made a sound—not a word, not in any tongue, just a low note in her throat like a struck string.

"She smells you," I said, before I could stop myself.

Wren looked at me. *Her?* said the look.

"The wolf," I said. And then, because lying to this child had apparently become impossible somewhere on the road behind us: "An old friend."

The truth was more complicated and less comfortable, as truths tend to be. Maelis was not a friend the way men have friends. She was the last living creature who remembered Odran Kavanagh entire—who remembered the boy I had been before I sold my name and my memories to buy a door shut behind me. Twenty years ago she had stood at my shoulder while I carved the sealing marks, and when it was done she had asked me one question, and I had answered it with silence, and she had walked into the treeline without looking back.

I had told myself, for twenty years, that she understood. That wolves understand necessity the way rivers understand downhill. I had told myself a great many things.

The pines opened onto a clearing I knew, though I had never seen it: a ring of standing stones furred with moss, a spring running black beneath a skin of ice, and snow that lay unbroken in a perfect circle fifty yards wide. No tracks crossed it. No branches hung over it. The forest itself seemed to lean away from it, politely, the way crowds lean away from a fight.

In the center of the circle sat a wolf the size of a plow horse.

She was grey the way winter is grey—all the colors of it at once, silver and ash and the blue-white of old ice—and her eyes were amber, and they were fixed on me from the moment the trees released us, so that walking toward her felt like walking into a sentence already spoken.

Wren's hand found mine. Small, warm, gripping hard. I realized I had stopped at the edge of the snow.

"Toll," I said quietly. "When we cross, there'll be a toll. Whatever she asks, let me answer."

Wren looked up at me, and then at the wolf, and something passed across her face that I couldn't read—recognition, maybe, or the memory of recognition, the way you remember a smell from a house you lived in before you could talk. Then she stepped out onto the snow first, dragging me after her, because apparently even my courage now required a child's supervision.

The snow held us. Of course it did. The snow always holds whoever Maelis wants held.

* * *

She let us come within ten feet before she spoke, and her voice was not what a man expects from a wolf. It was a woman's voice, low and unhurried, with an accent from a kingdom that no longer exists—the vowels rounded, the words placed carefully, like stones in a wall.

"You've brought a child into my country, Odile Kavanagh."

Not Odran. She gave me the name I'd bought, and somehow that was worse than if she'd torn it off me in front of the girl.

"Maelis." My voice came out level. Twenty years of practice at level. "You're looking well."

"I am looking *old*," she said, "which for my kind is the same thing as looking well, and you know it, which means you are stalling, which means you are afraid. Good. Fear is honest. Sit."

There was nothing to sit on. I sat anyway; the snow took my weight like a chair. Wren folded down beside me with the boneless ease of children and began, with great dignity, to eat a heel of bread she had produced from somewhere inside her coat.

"The girl doesn't speak," Maelis observed.

"She speaks fine. Just not in anything anyone above ground has used in four hundred years."

"Undercourt tongue." Maelis's ears tilted forward. "And you are running from the Undercourt, and your route home runs through my country, and you hoped—" here she did something with her lips that on a human face would have been called a smile, "—you *hoped* to go around me. Along the scree. In winter. With a child."

"It's been done."

"By dead men." She rose. Standing, she was taller than me, and the shadow she threw went the wrong way, long across the snow toward the setting sun instead of away from it. "Here is what happens now, gatekeeper. You pay toll, or you turn back, and we both know you cannot turn back, because I can smell them on the wind behind you. Three of them. Grey. They walk like lawyers and they leave no prints, and they have been following your girl since the pass."

"They took Brannoc," I said. "Two nights ago. On the river road."

Something moved through her, some ripple under the fur. "The drunk lawyer?"

"He wasn't always drunk. He was counsel below, once. He read Wren's contract for me. Told me the truth about it." I looked down at my hands. "They took him for aiding us. I couldn't stop them. I've spent twenty years being unable to stop things, Maelis, I'm very good at it."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going down there. Under the mountain. Through the door I shut." I heard myself say it aloud for the first time, and the strangest part was how ordinary it sounded, like announcing I intended to fix a fence. "And I'm asking you for a guide through the middle country, because the scree route adds two days and I don't think we have two days."

Maelis walked a slow circle around us. Snow did not print under her feet either; I noted that, and filed it next to all the other things about her I had spent two decades not thinking about.

"Toll," she said at last, and sat again, facing me, tail wrapped over her forepaws like a queen settling onto a throne. "You know my price. You have always known my price. I ask it of everyone who crosses, and most of them pay it gladly, because their truths are small. *What do you fear. What do you love. Whom have you wronged.* Cheap coin, most lives." Her amber eyes did not blink. Wolves' eyes don't blink the way ours do, and I had forgotten, and being unable to look away was its own punishment. "But you, Odile. You crossed my border once before, carrying a name like a stolen coat, and you paid me nothing, because I let you pass out of friendship, and friendship, I have learned, is a currency that degrades."

"Maelis—"

"*The truth.*" The word came out with weight enough to bend the pines at the clearing's edge. "Why did you leave me guarding nothing? Twenty years, Odile. Twenty years I kept the middle country because a boy I loved swore an oath on my back, with his hand in my fur, that what he built would need keeping. And then the door sealed, and the oath was finished, and you walked away and never came down to tell me it was finished. Do you know what I thought? For the first year, I thought you were dead. I searched. I ran the passes until my pads split. And then I found the smugglers talking about the mad gatekeeper of Thornfell, the one who checks his seals twice a day and speaks to no one, and I understood that you were alive, and that you had simply—*decided*—that I was part of the life you had buried." Her voice stayed level throughout, which was somehow worse than roaring. "So. Toll. Why did you leave me guarding nothing?"

The silence stretched. Wren had stopped eating. She looked between us with the terrible attention of children watching adults break.

And here is the thing I discovered, sitting in that snow: I didn't know the answer. I had assumed I did—I had carried the shape of an answer for twenty years, some smooth stone of justification, *it was cleaner that way, she deserved her freedom, the past was poison*—but faced with her actual eyes, the stone crumbled, and underneath it was not wisdom. It was just the small, squalid, human thing.

"Because you would have argued," I said.

Maelis went very still.

"I sold my memories, Maelis. My name, my childhood, everything Odran was—I poured it into the seal to buy the door shut. But the sale needed a witness. Someone who knew what I was giving up, so that the court couldn't later claim I'd been cheated, that the trade was made in bad faith. A contract needs a witness who remembers." My throat hurt. "You were the only one who knew me. If I'd told you what I was doing—if I'd come down and explained—you'd have fought me. You'd have said *there's another way, there's always another way,* and you'd have been right, probably, you were always right about the ways around things, and I couldn't—I couldn't risk being talked out of it. So I let you believe the oath was ongoing. I let you keep faith with a promise I'd already emptied. Because it was easier than arguing with someone who loved me."

The clearing was silent except for the spring muttering under its ice.

"That's the truth?" Maelis said at last. Quietly now. "All of it?"

"All of it. I was a coward. I chose the version of the story where I was noble and alone, because noble and alone doesn't require apologies." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Twenty years I've told myself I sealed that door to protect the world. Some days I even believed it. But the day I did it, the truest reason, the reason under the reasons, was that I wanted it *over*. I wanted to be done being Odran, done owing, done belonging to anyone—including you. Especially you. Belonging to you meant I could be lost, and I had already been lost once, in there, and I swore I'd never—"

I stopped. Because Wren had taken my hand again, and her grip said, in the language she actually spoke fluently: *keep going.*

"—and then a child knocked on my door speaking the dead tongue," I said, "and I found out that 'never' is just a word men use between catastrophes."

* * *

Maelis did not speak for a long time. The sun went down behind the stones and the shadows swung around and the cold came up out of the ground like something rising from sleep, and Wren leaned against my side and shivered once, experimentally, and then decided against it.

"You could have lied to me," Maelis said finally. "Half-truths would have served. I cannot tell a well-made lie from truth; that is the curse of my kind—we smell fear and hunger and blood, but deceit sits on the tongue like any other spice. You owed me a toll and you paid it in full, knowing full payment would cost you my good opinion." She rose, shook herself, snow scattering off her coat like sparks. "That is the first honest thing anyone has given me in this country in a hundred years."

"So I've paid."

"You've paid the toll. The toll buys passage." She turned her head toward the darkening north, where the mountains stepped down in black folds toward the valley that led, by ways I did not want to think about, to the door. "It does not buy a guide. A guide is a separate arrangement."

"Name it."

"No." She looked back at me, and there was something in her face now that hadn't been there before—something older and sadder and, impossibly, warmer. "No price. Not tonight. Tonight I guide you because a debt of honor stands between us, and I will not have it said that Maelis of the Middle Country abandoned a sworn friend in his worst hour, even a friend who was too much of a coward to say goodbye." Her muzzle wrinkled. "But hear me, Odile Kavanagh. When this is finished—when the girl is free and the door stands open or shut as fate decides—then you will come back to this clearing, and you will pay me the deferred price, and the price is this: you will tell me everything you sold. Every memory you poured into that seal. All of Odran, from the beginning, every year, every story, and you will not stop until I say it is enough. Because I was his friend too, and I have been grieving him for twenty years without a body to bury, and I want him *back*, even if only as words in the snow."

I stared at her. "I don't have those memories anymore. They're in the seal. Maybe they're gone entirely."

"Then you'll tell me what it's like to miss them," she said. "And it will be enough. It will have to be enough." She stepped past us, toward the northern trees, and paused there, enormous against the last grey light. "Bring the child. Walk in my footprints; the snow will hold her better there. We move tonight, while the collectors are still untangling themselves from the river road—Brannoc slowed them, before they took him. He signed something at them. I don't know what. But they stopped for half a day, and half a day is a life, out here."

Wren was already on her feet. She looked up at Maelis—way, way up—with an expression I had not seen on her before. Not fear. Something like recognition completing itself, a door opening in her face. She reached out one small hand and laid it, very gently, on the wolf's foreleg.

Maelis went absolutely still.

Then she lowered her great head until her nose nearly touched the girl's brow, and breathed in, long and slow, the way wolves take in the news of the world.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor little soul. You smell of *him*."

My blood went cold. "Him who?"

Maelis raised her head and looked at me, and her amber eyes held something I had never seen in them before, not in all the years of oaths and borders and snow.

"Not who," she said. "*What.* Odile—the collectors aren't following this child because of a contract. Contracts they can enforce anywhere. They're following her because of what her half-soul is *made of*." She turned north, into the dark, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Walk fast, old friend. And pray the door is still sealed when we reach it—because if it isn't, then someone below has already begun to open it from the other side."
