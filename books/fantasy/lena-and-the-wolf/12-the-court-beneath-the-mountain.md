## 12. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The stairs went down for a very long time.

I want to be precise about that, because "a very long time" is the kind of thing a man says when his mind is refusing to do arithmetic. We counted at first—Wren counted aloud in her silent way, tapping my knuckle once for every hundred steps—but somewhere past four thousand she stopped, and I stopped pretending I wasn't counting too, and after that we simply descended. The steps were black stone worn into shallow bowls by feet that had been walking them since before anyone thought to invent shoes. They were warm. That was the detail that undid me, more than the dark or the depth or the way Maelis's eyes caught the candlelight like green coins tossed down a well. The stairs were warm underfoot, like a floor someone had just left, like a house expecting you.

"They heated it," Maelis said behind us. Her voice came out strange in the stairwell, flattened and multiplied. "The whole mountain. They've been keeping your chair warm, Odran."

"Don't call me that yet," I said.

"You'll want practice."

"I've had twenty years of not wanting practice."

Wren tugged my sleeve. She pointed up—not back the way we'd come, but at the ceiling of the stairwell, which was so far above us it had stopped being a ceiling and become a sky with ambitions. Far overhead, tiny as frost-flowers on a window, hung chandeliers. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A galaxy of candles burning downward into the dark, all of it descending toward us in tiers as the staircase spiraled, so that walking down felt like sinking through the inside of a bell made of light.

She signed something. *Big,* her hands said. Then, after a moment's consideration: *Pretty.*

"Wait until you meet the people," I told her. "They're prettier. That's the trap."

Maelis made a sound that in a smaller wolf would have been a growl and in her was laughter. "You always did know how to make a homecoming festive."

I didn't answer. My mouth had gone dry somewhere around step three thousand, and everything I said now was just noise to keep the silence from getting ideas.

Here is what nobody tells you about going home to a place you hated: your body remembers it better than you do. My feet knew the rhythm of these stairs. My shoulders knew how to hold themselves here—looser, lower, a courtier's slouch I hadn't worn since before Wren was born. Twice I caught myself glancing at corners where sentries used to stand, checking sightlines I hadn't checked in two decades, and both times there was nothing there, because the Undercourt didn't need sentries. It had etiquette. Etiquette is a wall no one can climb because no one can find where it starts.

* * *

The stairs ended at a door.

Of course they did. Everything below ends at a door; it's the one thing the gentry and I have always agreed on. This one was silver-veined oak twice my height, carved with a procession of figures whose faces had been deliberately left blank, and it stood open—which somehow was worse than shut. A closed door you can brace against. An open door is an invitation, and invitations are binding.

A herald stood beside it. He looked like a young man the way a portrait looks like a person: correct in every line, assembled rather than grown. His coat was grey silk shot through with thread the color of candleflame, and when he saw us he bowed exactly deep enough to honor a returning noble and not one inch further.

"Lady Maelis of the High Thicket," he announced, though she hadn't given her name, and his voice was pleasant the way a well-tuned instrument is pleasant even playing a funeral march. "The wolf-queen honors the court." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "And the gentleman who accompanies her—the court will wish to know by what name he comes."

And there it was. The toll at the threshold, collected before I'd taken a single step inside. Not coin. Not blood. A name.

Wren's hand found mine. She couldn't speak, but she squeezed once, hard, the way she did when she wanted me to understand that she understood—that this was the thing Brannoc had warned us about in the smuggler's den, the thing the whole descent had been building toward. You cannot enter their law without entering their ledger. And the ledger wants a name.

I had rehearsed this. On the road, at night, while Wren slept against Maelis's flank, I had stood in the dark moving my lips around two syllables like a man testing whether a broken bone would bear weight. *Odran.* It should have been easy. It was only a word. I had said harder words in harder places—I'd read a burial service over a smuggler once, in a snowstorm, with my hands too frozen to cross themselves properly.

But rehearsal is a lie we tell ourselves in safe places, and standing at that door, the word wouldn't come. Twenty years of being Odile Kavanagh, gatekeeper, keeper of seals, a man whose name was a tool and nothing more—and now the tool was asked to become a man again.

"The gentleman," the herald repeated, still pleasant, still patient, and I understood suddenly that his patience was the most frightening thing I'd seen all day, because it meant they could wait. They were very good at waiting. Waiting was how they'd gotten Wren's parents to sign.

"Odran," I said.

It came out rough, half-swallowed, but it came out, and the moment it crossed my lips the door swung wider—not pushed, just *wider*, the way a face widens into a smile—and warmth rolled out over us like water over a weir. Candle-warmth. Kitchen-warmth. The warmth of a house that has been expecting you since before you were born and sees no reason to pretend otherwise.

"Odran returns," the herald said, and somewhere far off in the halls ahead, a bell rang once. "The court rejoices."

"I doubt that," I said.

His smile didn't move a hair. "The court rejoices," he said again, with the gentle insistence of a man correcting your grammar, and I understood that down here, joy wasn't a feeling. It was a procedure. And procedures, unlike feelings, could not be refused.

* * *

I had forgotten the scale of it. That's the honest confession: twenty years of guarding a door and I had let myself believe the place behind it was small—a cellar, a warren, something contained. But the Undercourt does not believe in containment. The hall we entered was a cavern dressed as a palace, or a palace dug like a cavern, pillars rising out of sight into the candle-galaxy above, galleries stacked on galleries, and everywhere people. Gentry in their hundreds, beautiful the way winter is beautiful, all greys and silvers and colors that seemed lit from inside rather than falling on them from the candles. Music played from somewhere unseen—not loud, just present, the way weather is present.

And every single one of them turned to look at us.

Not rudely. That's the thing I'd forgotten, the thing that curdled my stomach even as some traitor part of me thrilled to it: they weren't rude. Rudeness implies you matter enough to offend. What they gave us instead was *attention*, total and courteous, three hundred perfect faces tilting toward the door with expressions of mild, pleased interest, as if we were a particularly elegant dessert arriving at table.

"They're staring," Wren signed, pressing close to my leg.

"They're appraising," I corrected. "Staring is free. This costs."

An old woman detached herself from the crowd—or old in the way the gentry are old, which is to say her face was unlined but her eyes had the sediment of centuries settled in them. She wore grey velvet and a necklace of what I sincerely hoped was jet, and she crossed the marble toward us at a stately pace, arms opening.

"Odran." My name in her mouth sounded like it had never belonged to anyone else. "Little Odran. Look how tall."

"Mistress Corvane," I said, because some names survive even when you burn the rest. She had been steward of the tithe-children when I was one. She had taught me which fork to use and which questions never to ask, and she had done it with genuine kindness, which was the cruelest thing about her.

She took my hands before I could decide whether to allow it, and turned them over, examining my palms like a horse-trader. "Calluses," she said, delighted. "Rope and iron. You've been doing *work*, darling. How vulgar. How *wonderful*. The court will be fascinated." She released me and her gaze slid down to Wren, and something in her expression shifted—the way ice shifts on a pond, imperceptibly and with terrible implications. "And this is the little debt. Smaller than the ledger suggested. Souls photograph poorly, I'm afraid; the paperwork always makes them look bigger."

"She's a child," Maelis said, low, from behind me. The temperature of the hall did not change, but several hundred gentry leaned in a half-inch closer, the way wolves lean when the prey animal shows its teeth.

"And you're a guest, highness," Corvane said pleasantly, without looking at her. "Do try to remain one." Then, back to me, warm as fresh bread: "You've come to settle accounts. Everyone always says they won't, and everyone always does. Supper first. Negotiation on a full stomach is a courtesy we extend even to ourselves." She gestured, and the crowd parted down the length of the hall like a wound closing in reverse. "This way. Your old rooms have been kept."

"I don't want my old rooms."

"Of course you don't." She smiled with real affection, which was worse than any threat. "You'll have them anyway. That's what coming home means, Odran. It means the house wins."

* * *

Supper was served in a room sized for a cathedral and set for six: myself, Wren, Maelis, Corvane, the herald—who ate, I noted, though I'd have sworn he was furniture—and one empty chair at the head of the table that no one explained and no one sat in, though the wine in front of it stayed warm and steaming faintly, as if recently poured by someone we couldn't see.

The food was obscene. That's the only fair word. Courses arrived that I remembered from childhood with a hunger I'd spent twenty years forgetting: honey-bread that tasted the way safety feels, venison in a sauce that shifted flavor depending on what you were afraid to want, small sweet things that dissolved before you could decide whether you liked them. Wren ate carefully at first, watching the adults, then ate like a child finally, and I watched her shoulders come down from around her ears by degrees, and I hated myself for noticing that mine were doing the same.

That's the vertigo I keep circling and haven't landed on yet, so let me land: the horror of the Undercourt is not that it's cruel. Cruelty you can fight. The horror is that it's *comfortable*. Somewhere between the second course and the third, I caught myself sitting the way I used to sit, elbows in, chin up, answering Corvane's pleasantries with the old rhythms—*yes, mistress; no, mistress; I hadn't heard, mistress*—and each answer fit my mouth like a shoe worn in, and each fitting was a small surrender. The boy who grew up here wasn't dead. He'd just been waiting, patient as a debt, and now that I'd spoken his name at the door he was climbing back into me joint by joint, and the worst part, the part I'll deny under oath, is that parts of it didn't feel like an invasion. Parts of it felt like relief. It is so much easier to be Odran here. Odran knows the forks. Odile only knows the door.

Wren kicked me under the table. Not hard. Just enough.

I looked at her. She looked at me, gravy on her chin, and signed one word: *You?*

Meaning: *are you still in there?*

"Yes," I said aloud, and it came out steadier than I deserved. "Still here."

Corvane followed none of this and all of it. "You were always clever," she said, refilling my glass. "Cleverer than the others. Do you know, we kept your ledger page? Of course we did—we keep everything—but yours we kept *close*. There was always hope you'd come back and finish the transaction." She said it like a compliment. "Most tithe-children who run, we write off within a decade. You we never wrote off. Twenty years, and the entry's still wet."

"It's been dry for twenty years," I said. "Ink dries, mistress. So do claims."

"Mm." She smiled at the empty chair at the head of the table, briefly, as if sharing the joke with someone. "We'll see what the judges say. They do love a bold debtor. Boldness means assets." She rose, and the herald rose, and the meal ended the way tides end, without anyone deciding it. "Rest tonight. The hearing will be called tomorrow, when the candles are tallest. Sleep well, Odran. Truly. You're among family now."

"That," I said, "is what frightens me."

Her smile widened by a degree, and for one flicker the sediment in her eyes showed through, all the way down. "Good," she said. "Fear is the first honest thing you've brought us."

* * *

Our guest rooms were exactly as I'd left them at fourteen years old, which is to say preserved like a crime scene. Same narrow bed. Same window that looked out on painted rock made to look like a night sky, because the gentry consider real stars vulgar. Same toy soldier on the shelf, tin paint flaked, one arm raised forever in a salute to nothing.

Wren picked it up and looked at me with a question in her face.

"A gift," I said. "From Corvane. The year I decided to leave." I took it from her and set it back on the shelf, facing the wall. "I told her I was going to be a soldier. She said the court would make me one eventually, one way or another."

Maelis stood in the doorway, too large for it, making no apology. "You slept here," she said. It wasn't a question. "As a child. In this room. While they—" She stopped. Maelis has torn out the throats of things older than kingdoms, and I have watched her do it calmly, but she could not finish that sentence.

"While they taught me," I said. "That was the word they used. Taught."

She crossed the room—there wasn't far to cross—and put her forehead against mine, the way she used to when we were young and the world was simpler, which is to say when we were both lying to ourselves about our respective worlds. "Tomorrow," she said, "we take them apart."

"With law?"

"With *teeth* if I must. Law if I can. Brannoc always said the difference between the two was a good night's sleep." She pulled back and looked at Wren, then at me, and her voice dropped into something private. "Odran. Whatever they offer you tomorrow—and they will offer, they always offer—remember what you told me on the mountain. Remember why you left."

"I remember why I left," I said. "That's not the problem."

"No?"

"The problem," I said, picking up the candle, watching my shadow and Wren's lean together on the wall—hers whole, mine fraying at the edges like smoke deciding which direction to drift—is remembering why anyone would ever stay."

Down the corridor, faint and patient, a bell began to ring. Once. Twice. Counting the hours until morning, or counting something else entirely.

Somewhere below us, deeper than the deepest stair, I heard the sound of a very large book opening.
