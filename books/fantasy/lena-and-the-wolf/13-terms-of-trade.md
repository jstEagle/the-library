## 13. Terms of Trade

I have been drunk in a great many rooms, but never one like this.

The hall beneath the mountain is lit by ten thousand candles that do not flicker, and the wax does not drip, and the flames do not consume anything at all, which strikes me as the single most dishonest thing I have ever seen—and I once watched a magistrate eat a bribe off a silver spoon while sentencing the man who'd paid it. The ceiling is carved with faces. Not decorative faces. *Portraits.* Every soul ever sold to this court, I'd wager, smiling down from the stone in bas-relief, their features smoothed by centuries of candlelight into something like contentment. Propaganda in granite. The Undercourt has always understood that the dead are best represented when they cannot argue.

They had brought me down in chains, which was flattering, and then removed the chains, which was worse. A prisoner they keep bound expects mistreatment. A prisoner they seat at a table with wine and a fresh quill and an inkwell of something dark and honeyed has to wonder what he's being dressed for.

"You were counsel here once," said the collector called Vane—Vess? Vell? They all had names like the sound a coin makes dropping on marble. He sat across from me now, grey-gloved hands folded, patient as arithmetic. "Before your exile."

"I drafted contracts," I said. "I didn't sign them. There's a difference, though I grant it's a difference most people only appreciate after the ink dries."

"And yet you remember the forms."

"I remember everything." I turned my cup—it was real wine, or something wearing wine's face—and did not drink it. Twenty years above ground had taught me that nothing offered freely below is free. "That was rather the problem."

Across the hall, at the far end of a table long enough to have its own weather, Odile Kavanagh sat with his back straight and his jaw set like a man attending his own funeral and disapproving of the flowers. Wren sat beside him, small and silent, her hand tucked inside his. The girl hadn't spoken since we came down—not in the old tongue, not in any tongue—but her shadow lay stretched out behind her chair at an angle the candles couldn't account for, pointing toward the far doors like a compass needle finding south.

The judges presided from their thrones of black glass, masked, motionless. I had stood before them twice in my former life. They had not aged. I suspect they had not *been* anywhere.

"Let us begin," said the middle judge, and its voice was the sound of a ledger closing.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about the Undercourt, if you have the misfortune to negotiate with it: they do not lie. That is the trap. Every story you've heard about the gentry—the riddles, the tricks, the words with double edges—is true, but people mistake the mechanism. They think the danger is deception. It isn't. The danger is *precision*. The court will tell you the exact truth, in exact terms, at exactly the moment most calculated to ruin you, and then hold you to it forever.

They opened, as I knew they would, with generosity.

"The child," said Vane, unrolling a scroll so fine it seemed woven from frost, "was sold to this court by her parents, of sound mind if unsound fortune, signatures witnessed and sealed. The debt stands. However." He let the word hang. "The court is not without mercy. We propose a settlement."

Odile said nothing. Good. I'd told him on the stairs down: *let them talk first, and whatever they offer, look at what they're not offering.*

"The child's freedom," Vane continued, "in exchange for a new tithe. Ten children, from the surface world, delivered within a year and a day. Healthy. Willing or unwilling—" he waved a gloved hand, "—the court is flexible on that point, historically speaking."

The silence that followed had texture. I felt it settle on my shoulders like wet wool.

Beside me, Odile's hand closed slowly around the arm of his chair until the wood creaked.

"No," he said.

And the court smiled.

All of them. The collectors, the clerks lining the walls, the judges behind their masks—even the candleflames seemed to bend toward him in something like delight. It was the smile of a fisherman watching the trout take the fly: not surprise, not even satisfaction. *Recognition.*

"Master Kavanagh," said the left-hand judge gently, "you misunderstand your position. You came below under parley, bearing the child, claiming standing as her guardian. Parley grants voice. Voice grants vote. And vote—" the mask tilted, "—binds. You have entered negotiation. Refusal of a lawful offer without counter-offer is breach of negotiation, and breach carries penalty."

"What penalty?" Odile asked, and I could hear the mountain-man in him, the man who'd spent twenty years checking seals and trusting iron, asking the question the way you'd ask where the next blow lands.

"The negotiation continues," said Vane, "until agreement is reached. The court is patient. The child remains our guest"—the word landed like a key turning in a lock—"for the duration. Her parents' contract accrues interest daily. And interest, Master Kavanagh, on a soul, is paid in years."

There it was. The machine, fully assembled, every gear polished. They had never needed him to say yes. They only needed him to sit at the table. Every hour we negotiated, Wren's debt grew; every refusal added another clause; and the only way out was an agreement, and every agreement they would offer would be worse than the last, because worsening was the point. Lawful evil isn't a philosophy. It's an architecture. You build the room so that every door leads deeper in, and then you wait for the guest to tire of standing.

I cleared my throat.

"Counsel for the guardian," I said, "moves to make a counter-offer."

The masks turned to me. For the first time since we'd descended, something moved behind them—a flicker, a recalibration. They remembered me. That was either an asset or a death sentence, and I genuinely did not know which.

"Proceed," said the middle judge.

* * *

Here is what I offered, and I want it recorded that I offered it knowing exactly how it would land, because a lawyer's job is not to win. A lawyer's job is to make the other side spend something getting past him.

"The court holds claim on the child's soul," I said. "Half of it, by the original instrument—I've read the deed, and the parents sold the greater portion but reserved the lesser, whether through sentiment or sloppiness I can't say. The claim is valid. But validity requires proof of consent, and consent, under the Old Forms—which this court itself codified, Article Nine, the Duress Clauses—must be *knowing*. These parents signed in famine winter, in desperation, believing the stories that the gentry take only what's freely given and never more. Were they shown the full terms? Was the reservation clause explained? Was the interest schedule disclosed?"

Vane's smile thinned by a degree. "The contract was read aloud."

"In the old tongue," I said. "Which the parents do not speak. I checked. I'm thorough. Reading a contract aloud in a language the signing party cannot understand is not disclosure. It is theater. And theater, honored judges, is not consent. It is *impersonation* of consent."

The hall went very quiet. Somewhere above us, faint as a memory, water dripped in some deep shaft—real water, doing real dripping, and I found I envied it.

"The Old Forms were superseded," said the right-hand judge.

"By whom?"

Silence.

"By whom?" I repeated, rising, because there are moments when a drunk man's greatest gift is that he has nothing left to lose and therefore perfect balance. "Show me the instrument of supersession. Show me the date, the seal, the authority. Because if you cannot—if the Old Forms still stand in law, merely ignored, the way old laws are, quietly, conveniently—then every contract signed under obscured terms for three hundred years is voidable. Including," and here I let myself savor it, slowly, like the last swallow of a bottle you know you shouldn't finish, "*including* certain older instruments held by this court against certain older debtors. Names beginning with O, perhaps."

I should not have said that last part. I knew I should not have said that last part. But twenty years of exile curdles a man, and there is a particular pleasure in watching a courtroom flinch.

The masks did not flinch. That was the terrible thing. They went *still*—stillness like deep water, like held breath—and the candles dimmed all together, and for a moment I saw the hall as it truly was: not lit, but *watching*, ten thousand flames that were ten thousand eyes, and every carved face on the ceiling turned slightly, slightly inward, listening.

Then the light returned, warm and golden and false as ever, and the middle judge said:

"A bold interpretation, Counselor. The court will consider it." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "In the meantime, the offer stands. Ten children for one. Or the negotiation continues, and the interest compounds. Master Kavanagh—you may confer with your counsel."

* * *

We conferred in a side chamber they gave us, which was furnished like a rich man's idea of a poor man's cell: clean straw, a real fire, bread that smelled of bread. Everything below was like that. Honest surfaces over dishonest bones.

"They're going to grind us down," Odile said. He stood with his back to the fire, arms crossed, watching Wren sleep on the straw with her shadow curled around her like a second blanket. "Hour after hour, offer after offer, each one a little worse, until I take the least bad one just to stop the bleeding. That's the play."

"That's the play," I agreed. "It's a good play. I helped write it."

"Then counter it."

"With what? I bought us a day, maybe two, with the Duress argument. Long enough for Maelis to get clear of the pass, if she's coming. Not long enough to win. To win we need either proof the contracts are void—and the court will bury that precedent under six layers of procedure—or we need something they want more than they want her." I looked at him. He was already looking at me, and I saw he knew, the way you know a thing days before you admit it. "And there's precious little in this world they want, Odile. They own nearly everything already. Debts, mostly. Souls, mostly. What they don't own is what walked out from under them twenty years ago and left a hole shaped like a boy."

"Don't," he said.

"I'm not saying anything. I'm describing furniture."

"*Don't.*"

Wren stirred. She sat up in the straw, hair wild, eyes too old for her face the way they always were in the hour between sleeping and waking, and she looked at Odile with an expression I couldn't parse—pity, maybe, or recognition—and then she raised one small hand and drew, in the ash at the hearth's edge, a shape.

A door. Open. And beside it, a stick figure walking away, and behind the figure, smaller, another figure sitting down. Waiting.

She looked up at him. She pointed at the waiting figure. Then, with terrible gentleness, she shook her head.

Odile turned away. I poured myself the cell's one kindness—there was a jug of small beer, weak and honest—and drank it straight, because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

"The hearing's at candle-dawn," I said to his back. "Brannoc argues. Maelis testifies, if she comes. And then they ask the question, Odile. They'll dress it in procedure, but it's the same question every creditor asks in the end: *what will you stake?* And you should decide, before they ask it, what your answer is. Because if you stand up in that hall and say 'nothing,' they will take the girl, and they will take her slowly, and you will live a long time afterward, which I promise you is not the mercy it sounds like."

He was quiet a long moment. The fire cracked. Above us, faintly, the carved faces smiled their granite smiles.

"When I was a boy down here," he said finally, not turning around, "they taught us that a debt is a shape. That a soul owed is a soul *shaped*—bent toward the creditor until it fits their hand. I spent twenty years trying to unbend mine." His voice dropped. "I'm starting to think the unbending was the debt. All of it. Every year at that gate. They weren't waiting for me to come back, Brannoc. They were waiting for me to come back *willing*."

"Then don't be willing," I said. "Be something else. Be desperate. Desperation voids more contracts than duress ever did."

He almost laughed. Almost. It came out as breath, no sound, and he crouched by the sleeping girl and pulled the blanket higher over her shoulder with a tenderness he'd have denied under oath.

Outside the door, soft as snowfall, came the sound of many feet arranging themselves. Our escorts, patient as interest.

"Rest," I told him. "Tomorrow we lose gracefully, or we find the door they haven't thought to lock."

But as the guards led us back through the candlelit halls, past the clerks with their endless ledgers, I glanced sideways at an open book on a clerk's desk—one glance, a drunk's reflex, reading upside-down out of habit—and the title page stopped my heart for a full beat.

*Register of Held Souls.* And beneath it, in faded ink, a column heading I had written myself, in another life, with a steadier hand:

*Redeemable by substitution — see precedent, the Odran Clause.*

They had named it after him. They had written him into the law itself, twenty years ago, the day he ran—a clause with his name on it, lying dormant all this time like a seed in frozen ground.

They hadn't been waiting for him to come back willing.

They had been waiting for him to come back at all.
