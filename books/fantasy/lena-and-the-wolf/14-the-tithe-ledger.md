## 14. The Tithe Ledger

The room they gave me had a bed with too many pillows and a window that showed a garden that did not exist.

I knew it did not exist because I counted the roses. There were nine on the first morning. Nine the second morning. On the third morning there were still nine, and one of them was blooming in exactly the same way, petal for petal, like a flower that had forgotten how to be anything but a picture of itself. Real gardens are messier than that. Real gardens have dead things in them.

Everything down here was like that rose. Beautiful, and repeating, and wrong.

The room was called a guest-room. I could tell because a small silver card on the door said GUEST in letters that rearranged themselves when I wasn't looking, so that sometimes it said GUEST and sometimes it said something older, in the tongue I spoke without meaning to, and once — only once — it said *MINE*, and I stopped reading the card after that.

Here is what nobody down here seemed to understand about me: I could not talk. My voice had gone into the bargain my mother and father made, along with other pieces of me, and what was left of my soul sat inside my chest like half a loaf of bread — enough to keep a body going, not enough to feel full ever again. But not-talking is not the same as not-noticing. Not-talking means you get very good at watching, because watching is all anyone lets you do.

So I watched the guest-room.

* * *

The bed was soft the way clouds are supposed to be soft, which is to say it swallowed you and gave nothing back. The pillows smelled of lavender and underneath the lavender, faintly, of candle smoke and old coins. The fire in the hearth never needed wood. It burned a color I don't have a word for in the surface language — a green-gold, like sunlight through bottle glass — and when I stared at it too long it stared back, politely, the way a cat does when it has decided you are not worth getting up for.

There were no locks on the door. That was the first thing I checked, pressing my palm flat against the wood, expecting iron or at least a bar. Nothing. The handle turned. The door opened onto a corridor lit by more of the green-gold candles, stretching away in both directions until it blurred like breath on glass.

I walked down it. I walked for what felt like an hour, past doors with silver cards — GUEST, GUEST, GUEST, and one that said something I couldn't read at all, that made my teeth ache — and then I turned around and walked back, and the door to my room was the first door again, as if the corridor had folded itself up behind me like paper.

That was the cage. Not bars. Geometry.

A servant came twice a day with food on covered dishes. She was beautiful the way everything down here was beautiful, which is to say her face was perfect and her eyes never moved unless she wanted them to. She would lift the covers — bread that tasted of honey and memory, fruit that tasted of the summer before last — and she would smile at me, and the smile was always the same width, and she never spoke, because servants below the mountain are not permitted words any more than guests are permitted exits.

I tried giving her one of my drawings once. I drew every day; it was how I talked before Odile learned to read me, and it was how I talked now, since nobody down here bothered to learn. I drew the garden with the nine roses and I drew a tenth rose growing outside the wall of it, wild and crooked, and I held the paper out to the servant girl.

She looked at it for a long moment. Her smile stayed exactly the same width. But her hand, when she took the plate away, trembled — just once, just slightly, a tremor small as a spider's footstep — and I understood two things at once.

One: she had been a child sold below the mountain, once, a long time ago.

Two: she remembered.

I drew her another picture the next day. And the next. I filled the margins of every scrap of paper in the room with little wild crooked roses and pushed them across the table at her, and she never smiled wider, and she never spoke, and she never threw them away either. I checked. They were always gone when I looked for them later, but the wastebasket stayed empty, and somewhere in that vast repeating palace there was a servant girl collecting paper roses like a woman saving coins for a door.

Adults fail children. I had learned that already, in the worst way, in a farmhouse kitchen where my mother cried while my father signed. But I was beginning to learn its opposite, too, which is that some adults go on failing *beside* you, quietly, for years, keeping whatever small things they can keep. The servant girl could not open the corridor. But she could carry a drawing. I decided that mattered.

* * *

It was on the sixth day, I think — days down here were suggestions rather than facts — that I found the ledger.

I had been pulling the room apart, the way bored imprisoned children do. Under the bed: dust that glittered. Behind the wardrobe: a mirror that showed the room from above, as if the ceiling had opinions. Inside the writing desk, beneath a false bottom I found because the wood grain ran the wrong way (Odile taught me that — *wood tells the truth even when carpenters lie*, he'd said, tapping the smuggler's cabinet in his hut, and I had filed it away the way I file away everything), beneath that false bottom: a book.

It was bound in grey leather, soft as skin, and it was heavy in a way that had nothing to do with weight. When I lifted it, the room got quieter. The fire leaned toward me. Even the nine roses outside the window seemed to hold their bloom.

The cover had no title. It didn't need one. I opened it and knew what it was the way you know the taste of your own name.

It was a ledger. Every page was a soul.

They were written in ink that shifted colors depending on what kind of bargain had been struck — desperate brown for the ones sold out of hunger, cold blue for the ones sold out of ambition, a sickly pink I didn't understand until I read enough entries to understand it, for the ones sold out of love, which is somehow the worst color of all. Each entry had a name, a date, a price, and a line at the bottom in smaller script: *consent given*, or *consent witnessed*, or, rarely, *consent uncertain*.

I found my parents' entry on the third page I turned.

**WREN — no surname recorded. Age six years. Sold by Eamon and Sorcha [surname withheld at sellers' request] in settlement of debt incurred winter prior. Consideration received: remission of debt, plus one favor, unspecified, redeemable at the court's discretion. Consent given.**

*Consent given.* Four words in my father's handwriting — I recognized the loop of his letters, the way his *g*s always leaned like tired men. He had written it himself. He had sat at our own kitchen table with the collectors' grey paper in front of him and written *consent given*, and my mother had stood behind him with her hand on his shoulder, and neither of them had asked me anything, because asking a six-year-old whether she consents to being sold is a question with only one answer a grown man can bear to hear.

I did not cry. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of me. I sat on the floor of the cage-room with the book open on my knees and I did not cry, because crying was for the version of me that still believed the entry might say something else. What I felt instead was colder and more useful. It was the feeling of a lock being studied.

Because here is what the entry taught me, the thing my parents' signatures had already told the collectors and Brannoc and everyone: adults fail children. They fail us out of hunger, out of fear, out of love, out of the simple animal desire to survive one more winter. The ledger proved it. Page after page after page, hundreds of years of it, brown ink and blue ink and that terrible loving pink, and every single entry had been made by a grown person about a young one, and every single one said *consent given* in handwriting that shook.

But — and this was the thought that made the fire turn its head — the ledger also proved something else. It proved that the court kept records. Meticulous ones. Records of *everything*: names, dates, prices, consent. A court that keeps records is a court that fears something. You do not write *consent given* four hundred times unless somewhere, someday, someone might ask you to prove it.

And if someone could prove the opposite — if someone could show that a consent was false, or forced, or bought from parents who had no right to sell what was never theirs —

Well. I was six. I was mute. I was half a soul in a gilded cage. But I could read, and I could draw, and I had a memory like a wolf-trap: it closed on things and did not let go.

So I kept turning pages.

* * *

Near the middle of the ledger — though "middle" meant nothing in a book this old — the ink changed. It became a deep red-black, the color of garnets, and the handwriting changed too, becoming formal and ceremonial, the way writing looks when lawyers are showing off.

And there, under a heading that read **TITHE-CHILDREN, RAISED IN SERVICE OF THE COURT**, I found a page with a burned-out name.

I mean that literally. Where the name should have been, the parchment was scorched — not torn, not scratched out, *burned*, in a neat circle, as if the name had been deliberately set alight and allowed to smolder exactly long enough to destroy itself and nothing else. Around the burn, the rest of the entry survived:

**[NAME CONSUMED BY BEARER'S OWN ACT AND PRICE]** — entered year [the date was in the old reckoning; I couldn't read it]. Tithe-child, taken at age [burned]. Raised in the Undercourt under the instruction of [several names, all in the ceremonial hand]. Attained the rank of [burned]. Departed the court in the night, having purchased closure of the gate with the surrender of his name, his memories of this place, and all claim upon his own soul, which remains — 

and here the entry stopped, mid-sentence, unfinished, as if even the ledger's keeper had not known how to end it. *Which remains.* Which remains what? Held? Ours? Waiting?

The page smelled faintly of woodsmoke, after twenty years.

I sat with it for a long time. The fire had gone quiet. Somewhere far off in the repeating corridors, a bell rang a note that was almost but not quite sad.

I knew whose page it was. Of course I knew. Odile — who was not called Odile, who had been someone else once, someone raised down here among these green-gold candles by these beautiful unblinking people, someone who had burned his own name off the world's page to buy a door shut behind him. He had told me by firelight, back in the hut, in the voice of a man confessing to a crime. I had drawn him a picture afterward: a boy standing on one side of a door, and on the other side, the same boy, waiting.

He had looked at that drawing for a very long time. Then he had put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, which is where he put the things he could not say.

Now here was the proof of it, in garnet ink and ash. His whole childhood, itemized. His rank, burned. His soul, *remaining*. Still down here. Still on the books. Twenty years, and the court had never closed his account, because courts do not close accounts; they only wait for the debtor to walk back through the door.

And he had. He had walked back through the door. For me.

I looked at his burned-out name for a long moment, and then I did something I had not done since the farmhouse kitchen. I spoke. Out loud, in the old tongue, the one that lived in the half of my soul they hadn't collected. Just one word, small, into the empty room:

*Odran.*

The candles all bent toward me at once, like grass under wind. The fire stood up straight. Somewhere in the walls, something that had been pretending to be architecture stopped pretending.

Good, I thought. Let them hear. Let them know the tithe-child remembers her letters.

Because here is what I had decided, sitting on the floor of the cage with the ledger open across my knees, my parents' trembling *consent given* on one side and Odran's ashes on the other: adults fail children. That is almost a law of nature, like the roses repeating, like the corridor folding shut. Adults are frightened and hungry and tired, and children are the things they trade away first.

But laws can be broken. I was living proof; my whole existence was a breach of contract waiting to be argued. And the court below the mountain had made one mistake, the same mistake every adult in the ledger had made, the mistake that ran through four hundred years of brown and blue and loving pink ink:

They had all assumed the child would stay quiet.

My mother and father had assumed it. The collectors had assumed it. This court, with its records and its rituals and its servant girls collecting paper roses in secret, had built its entire house on the assumption that children who are sold will stay sold, and children who are silent will stay silent, and children who are caged will not read.

I closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping thing, and slid it back beneath the false bottom, wood grain running the wrong way, exactly as I'd found it.

Then I took out my paper, and I began to plan.

Not a drawing this time. A map — of everything I knew. The corridor that folded. The servant girl and her trembling hand. The exact wording of my entry, memorized, letter-perfect, *consent given* and all. The burned page. The unfinished sentence. *Which remains.*

Because Odile was somewhere below, trading himself away piece by piece to buy me back, and he was doing it the way adults always did it: alone, silently, assuming the child would stay quiet and let herself be saved.

He was about to learn what I had learned in the ledger.

Children read.

And three floors down — I felt it through the soles of my feet before I heard it, a vibration like a great door being thrown open, followed by shouting in a voice I knew, a voice like winter wind over stone — something with four legs and no patience whatsoever had just come down the stairs uninvited.
