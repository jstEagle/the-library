## 15. Maelis Comes Down

I have been alive long enough to know that the worst decisions of my life have all begun with the phrase *I'll just see for myself.*

The truce between the mountain and the forest is older than either of us. Older than me, which is saying something, because I remember when the river at Thornfell's foot had a different name and a worse temper. The terms are simple: what happens beneath stays beneath, what runs above stays above, and neither queen nor court crosses the threshold uninvited. I have kept that truce for two hundred years with the discipline of a creature who has learned, painfully and repeatedly, that her appetites are not always her friends.

But Odran Kavanagh walked into my territory with a child who smelled of candlewax and stolen things, and then he walked down into the dark, and I sat on my ridge all night listening to the mountain swallow him.

I told myself I was being patient. Patience is a virtue in wolves the way moderation is a virtue in fire — technically true, rarely practiced, and mostly a lie we tell while doing exactly what we wanted.

By the third dawn I had stopped lying to myself.

* * *

There are three ways into the Undercourt. The front door, which Odran opened — a stair going down forever, lit by nothing, guarded by everything. The smugglers' roads, which end at a wall of stone that only opens for those already owed. And the way I chose: straight down through the roots of the oldest pine on my ridge, where the mountain's stone goes soft and remembers being soil.

It is not an easy road. It is not supposed to be. The mountain does not like being burrowed, and it said so, squeezing at my shoulders like a bad-tempered innkeeper who resents late guests. But stone and I have an old arrangement of our own. My pack has denned against this mountain since before it had a name, and there is a debt of warmth between us — den-space given in the hard winters, bones left at the treeline in thanks — and debts, as I was about to remind a certain court, cut both ways.

I came out of the roots into a corridor of black glass, shook the dirt from my coat, and considered my options.

Option one: arrive as a wolf. Honest, but easily dismissed. They would set the hounds of the lower galleries on me, and while I am flattered by the reputation that precedes me, even I cannot fight forty hounds in a hallway built for them.

Option two: arrive as a woman. The court respects shape-changers the way merchants respect counterfeit coin — grudgingly, and with an eye toward arrest.

Option three, which occurred to me somewhere around the fourth gallery of candles: arrive as neither. Arrive as a creditor.

Every creature down here thinks in ledgers. It is their whole language, their law, their love-language and their war-cry. So I would walk into their hall not as an intruder but as a party to an agreement — because I *was* one. Maelis, queen of the Thornfell wolves, holder of a toll unpaid, witness to a bargain made on the mountain's doorstep three nights past. Let them try to throw out someone they owe.

I let the woman-shape come over me like a coat. It never fits quite right — my shadow keeps the wrong number of joints, and I have never bothered to fix it, because a shadow that lies about itself is useful in a place full of lawyers.

Then I followed the sound of arguing.

* * *

You can hear the Undercourt's great hall from three galleries away. It sounds like a dinner party where everyone hates everyone politely: the rustle of silk, the chime of glasses that hold no wine, and underneath it all a low continuous murmur like bees negotiating over a corpse.

I came in through the tall doors at the west end, the ones reserved for ambassadors and catastrophes, and I will grant the court this much: they know how to make an entrance go quiet. Two hundred masked faces turned toward me in the same instant, like a field of flowers deciding whether to be beautiful or poisonous.

At the center of the hall stood a dais of white candles, and before it, my old friend.

Odran looked terrible. He looked, in fact, exactly as he had the day he climbed out of this place twenty years ago, which I recognized because I had watched him do it from the treeline and done nothing, because he asked me to do nothing, because I loved him enough to honor a stupid request. Twenty years had put grey in his beard and a limp in his pride, but the set of his shoulders was the same: a man braced for a blow he has decided to catch rather than dodge.

Beside him stood the child — Wren, the smugglers called her, though names down here are currency and she was holding hers close. Her hand was in his. Good. At least one of them remembered how to hold on.

And across the candlelight, arrayed like a jury of elegant knives, sat the judges in their masks, and beside them the three collectors in their fine grey clothes, and — this made my hackles rise under my borrowed skin — Brannoc, the exiled lawyer, seated at a counsel's desk with chains of paper instead of iron on his wrists. Brought below as counsel. Which meant the court had decided he was property again the moment he proved useful.

"An ambassador," said the nearest judge, in a voice like a drawer closing, "was not summoned."

"I'm not an ambassador." I walked forward, letting my heels click on the glass floor, letting every head track me. "I'm a creditor. And I've come to collect."

The murmur changed key. Fear, curiosity, and — from the collectors — something colder. Recognition. Grey clothes, grey eyes, grey patience. They knew what I was. I hoped they also knew what I was carrying, which was two hundred years of grievances and a very good memory.

Odran's face did something complicated when he saw me. Relief and horror, in equal measure, which is the correct response to my arrival anywhere.

"Maelis," he said quietly. "You shouldn't be here."

"No," I agreed pleasantly. "I shouldn't. That's rather the point."

* * *

Here is what I know about the Undercourt that most of the surface has forgotten: they are not strong because their law is just. Their law is a horror. They are strong because their law is *consistent*, and consistency, applied without mercy or fatigue, will grind down anything softer. They have outlasted kingdoms by simply continuing to exist while everyone else got tired.

Which means you do not beat them with force. You beat them with an older law.

Fortunately, I am very old, and I was there when the older laws were written.

"Before your honored judges rule on whatever matter is before them," I said, pitching my voice to carry, "I invoke the Rite of Prior Claim. Any party holding a lawful grievance against a debtor may enter the proceedings as an interested party." I paused. "Article One. Of the Accords of Root and Stone. Drafted before this court had its current name, signed by the First Queen of the Undermost Hall — whose seal, unless you've melted it down for candlesticks, still hangs in your archive."

Silence. The good kind. The kind where lawyers are doing arithmetic behind their masks.

"The Accords," said the middle judge slowly, "are honored."

"They'd better be. You wrote them so you could poach game from my forests during the famine years, and I let you, because I was young and flattered that anyone asked." I smiled, and made sure the smile showed slightly too many teeth for the woman-shape to plausibly contain. "Now I'm calling in the courtesy. Here is my claim: three nights ago, on the threshold of Thornfell Pass, Odile Kavanagh — formerly Odran, formerly yours — struck a bargain with me. Passage through my territory in exchange for a toll deferred. I am therefore a creditor of the principal debtor in this proceeding, and I claim my seat."

"And your toll?" asked the judge. "What did you defer?"

"The truth," I said. "Of why he left me guarding nothing." I let that sit in the hall for a moment, because I am not above making a courtroom uncomfortable. "A truth, I might add, that bears directly on the matter at hand. Because I knew Odran when he was a tithe-child in these halls. I knew what was done to children here. And I know—" I turned, slowly, deliberately, until I was facing the three collectors, "—exactly how the contract now under dispute was obtained."

The lead collector inclined her head. She had a face like a portrait of patience. "The contract bears genuine signatures," she said. "Freely given. Witnessed and sealed."

"Genuine," I agreed. "Signed by a starving father in a famine winter, with a gentry-man sitting at his table eating food the family couldn't afford, promising relief that arrived only after the ink dried. Signed by a mother who had been told — by whom, I wonder? — that the debt was small, and temporary, and surely nothing would ever truly be collected." I let my voice drop into the register I use for hunting, low and certain. "Duress on one side. Deception on the other. A child sold by parents who were themselves half-caught in your nets before they ever held the pen."

"Consent is consent," said the collector. "The law does not weigh the signer's stomach."

"No," I said. "*Your* law doesn't. But there is an older article, and you know it, because you've spent four hundred years burying it under footnotes. The Accord of the Small Ones — the first law this court ever ratified, back when it still needed the surface's goodwill to survive. Debts contracted against children under duress are void if the creditor cannot prove consent. Not the parents' consent. *The child's.*" I gestured at Wren, standing small and straight beside Odran with her chin up in a way that made my chest ache. "She was an infant. Produce her signature. Produce her *understanding*. Produce one scrap of evidence that the soul you claim was sold by anyone with the standing to sell it — and I will walk out of here and trouble you no more."

The hall went very quiet.

It is a strange thing, watching lawyers realize they are trapped by their own architecture. The collectors did not move. The judges' masks did not move. But underneath the stillness I heard it — the tiny, frantic sound of a court searching its own archives for a loophole and finding, with dawning horror, that the loophole had been sealed *by them*, centuries ago, precisely so that no one could ever use it against them.

They had built a door and locked it. And now I was standing on the far side, holding the handle.

* * *

What happened next was chaos, if by chaos you mean the most orderly panic I have ever witnessed.

The lead collector spoke first, smooth as poured cream: "The Accords predate the current charter. They are ceremonial."

"Then your charter is void," said a new voice, dry as old paper, "because charters derive their authority from the accords that authorized them, and a court that disowns its foundation disowns itself."

Brannoc. On his feet, paper chains rattling, wearing the expression of a man who has just realized he is enjoying himself and hates himself for it. "I drafted half those footnotes myself, before my exile, and I can tell you exactly which shelf the original Accord of the Small Ones sits on. Third archive, western vault, under the famine ledgers. Shall we fetch it? Or shall we stand here while the honorable judges decide whether their entire legal existence rests on documents they're suddenly embarrassed by?"

"You were brought here as counsel for the court," snapped the collector.

"I was dragged here as *property*," Brannoc said, "which means everything I say is the muttering of an object, and objects cannot commit contempt. Also, I resign." He tore one of the paper chains with a sound like a sigh. "I'm counsel for the child now. Pro bono. God help us all."

The murmur became a roar became a din. Judges rose. Collectors conferred in whispers that moved faster than whispers should. Somewhere a candelabra toppled and was caught by hands that moved too gracefully to be entirely hands. And through it all, Wren stood beside Odran, watching the machinery of the court seize and grind, and I saw her do something that frightened the court more than anything I had said.

She smiled.

Not a cruel smile. A child's smile — the fierce, astonished delight of someone discovering that the enormous adults who run the world can, in fact, be made to stumble. That the ledger is only paper. That the wolves are coming down the mountain.

Odran caught my eye across the chaos. He didn't say thank you. He didn't need to; twenty years ago he'd taught me that words between us were mostly decoration. He just nodded once, the way you nod to someone across a battlefield, and I nodded back, and that was the whole conversation.

But I had not come all this way merely to open doors. I had come to finish something.

"Honored judges!" I raised my voice over the din. "One more item, while your archives are warm. The prior claim of Maelis, Queen of Thornfell, extends beyond procedure. I hold a toll unpaid by the debtor Odile Kavanagh — a truth owed. And I hereby assign that claim, that truth, as *evidence* in this proceeding. Whatever he buried, whatever name he traded away, whatever he did to escape these halls twenty years ago — it belongs in your record now. By his own bargain. By his own word." I looked at him, and gentled my voice for the first time since entering. "You promised me the truth, old friend. I'm sorry. I'm collecting it here, where it can do some good, instead of on a mountainside where it would only break my heart privately."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, there was no anger in them. Only the exhausted gratitude of a man who has been running for twenty years and has finally been caught by someone who intends to carry him rather than eat him.

"The court," said the middle judge, in a voice that had lost all its drawer-snap certainty, "will convene a formal hearing. Trial by candlelight. All claims to be weighed."

"All claims," I agreed sweetly. "Including the ones you buried."

* * *

They escorted me — escorted, mind, as though I were a guest and not an invasion — to a gallery room with a window that showed a painted sky, because the real sky is apparently too much honesty for one building. Brannoc was deposited nearby, muttering case citations in his sleep before he'd even fallen asleep. Through the walls I could smell Odran and the child, two floors up, and I lay down on the floor rather than the bed because beds are for people who trust ceilings, and listened to the mountain breathe around me.

It was not pleased with me either. Breaking truce has a cost, and the mountain intended to bill me for it eventually. Fine. Let it join the queue.

Tomorrow there would be a trial. Tomorrow the collectors would produce their signatures, and the judges would weigh their masks against my memory, and somewhere in the balance a child's soul would sit like a pebble on a scale built for boulders. Tomorrow Odran would have to say aloud the name he'd sold, and I would finally learn why he left me standing at the treeline with nothing to guard.

But tonight, for the first time in four hundred years, the Undercourt was afraid of the dark outside its own halls.

I curled up on the cold floor of my borrowed room, laid my tail — which the woman-shape had never quite managed to hide — over my nose, and grinned into the gloom like the thing with teeth that I am.

Sleep well, creditors. The wolf is inside the walls, and she has read the contract.
