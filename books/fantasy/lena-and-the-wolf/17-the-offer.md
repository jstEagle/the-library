## 17. The Offer

The hearing had gone on for six hours, and I had learned something important about the Undercourt: it did not tire. It *savored*.

The masked judges sat in their crescent of high seats, seven of them, faces like polished bone behind the slits of their hoods. They had not moved in hours except to turn their heads, slowly, when a new argument was made, the way owls track a mouse not because they're hungry but because the watching is the pleasure. Candles burned around the hall without diminishing. Somewhere above us, water dripped upward.

Brannoc had fought well. I'll give him that, and I'd never given him anything freely in thirty years of acquaintance. He had walked the judges through Maelis's point about duress—*a contract signed by parents starving in a famine winter, with collectors at the door, is consent the way drowning is a swim*—and the judges had listened with what might have been interest, if interest were something you could tax. Then the eldest judge had produced Wren's parents' signatures, genuine as grief, and read aloud the clause Brannoc himself had once drafted, in another life, in another ink: *voluntary conveyance, witnessed and sealed.*

"Duress," the judge said, "is a matter of pressure. There was pressure. There was also choice. The court honors both."

It was a beautiful sentence. It was also a noose tied in silk.

Brannoc sat down beside me, and under the table his hand found my sleeve—not gripping, just touching, the way you touch a man to remind him he still has a body. "There's one road left," he murmured. "You know which one."

"I don't," I said, which was a lie, because I'd known since the trial began, maybe since the blizzard, maybe since twenty years ago when I'd sold my name to buy a door shut. The truth was I'd known since Wren drew that door in the frost on my window, over and over, until her fingers went blue and her eyes stayed clear.

"The precedent," Brannoc pressed. "A soul already claimed may ransom another. Odran—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and hard, a stone dropped down a well. "Not here. Not that name. Not yet."

The eldest judge's head turned toward us. Slowly. Owl-patient. "The court hears whispers," it said, "even across a table."

I stood up.

I hadn't planned to stand. My legs decided it for me, some old muscle memory from a boy who used to stand up in this hall when the tithe-children were called forward, before I learned that standing up got you nothing but noticed. But I stood, and the whole court went quiet in that particular way courts do—not silence, but *attention*, a thousand years of etiquette leaning in like a held breath.

"I have an offer," I said.

Maelis, at the edge of the hall where uninvited guests are permitted to stand but not sit, lifted her head. Her grey eyes caught candlelight and held it hostage. Wren, beside her, looked up at me with that face she made—the one that wasn't fear exactly, more like a person watching weather come in and deciding whether to run or build.

"The child's soul was sold," I said. "Half of it. The court holds the deed. Fine. The court loves deeds. So let me offer the court a trade it can't refuse, because I know how much this place hates refusing things."

The judges waited. Of course they waited. Waiting was free here; everything else cost.

"There is a soul in this room older than hers," I said. "One the court has held a claim on for twenty years. A soul that was raised on your bread and your law and your candlelight. A soul that walked out of this mountain and left a debt behind—a debt of a name, unpaid, accruing interest all this time." My mouth had gone dry as old paper. "I am that debt. And I've come back to settle it."

The eldest judge rose. When it moved, its robes didn't rustle; they flowed like smoke remembering it used to be fire. "Name yourself."

And there it was. The whole trap, sprung open and waiting, dressed up as a formality. Because to make the offer I had to claim the name, and to claim the name was to hand them the handle of the knife and say *please, hold this while I talk.*

I looked at Wren. She looked back. She couldn't speak—her voice had been part of what they took, though she'd found ways around it, drawing doors in frost, tapping rhythms on my arm in the dead tongue, *come home, come home, come home*. But right then she didn't tap anything. She just looked at me with those too-old eyes and gave me the smallest nod, the kind adults give children at gravesides, except reversed, and it undid something in me that twenty years of iron rules had kept rusted shut.

"My name," I said, "is Odran Kavanagh."

Saying it was like swallowing a hook. I felt it go down through my chest and set itself somewhere behind my ribs, barbs out. The candles all leaned toward me at once, flames bending like courtiers bowing. Somewhere in the dark of the hall, something laughed—not cruelly, worse than that: *delightedly*.

"Odran," the eldest judge repeated, tasting it. "The tithe-child who fled. Who sold his name and his memories to seal the door behind him. Who left his sworn sister guarding a road to nowhere." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Who left *us* holding the paper."

"You hold paper," I said. "I'm offering you payment in full."

"Speak the terms."

So I spoke them. And I want it recorded, wherever such things get recorded, that my voice did not shake until the last sentence, and even then only a little.

"Wren's soul, returned whole, her debt dissolved, her freedom absolute—no claims, no liens, no collectors at any door she ever stands behind. In exchange, you receive me. All of me. The name Odran. Every memory you bought from me twenty years ago, returned to your ledger. And my soul entire—staked against hers. One condition."

The judge's hood tilted. "Conditions complicate contracts."

"This one simplifies everything else. I walk out of this mountain carrying all of it—name, memories, soul, the whole ruined shipment. If I reach the surface without faltering, both of us go free and your ledgers balance forever. If I look back—" I made myself say it, made the words leave my body like blood leaving a wound, "—if I falter, if I turn, if I so much as hesitate past the threshold, the court keeps everything. Me and the girl. Principal and interest. Paid in full."

The silence that followed was the loudest thing I'd ever heard.

Brannoc had gone white. Actually white, like candle tallow, like the masks on the judges. "Odile," he said quietly, using the false name like a rope thrown to a drowning man, "you understand what they'll show you on that walk. You understand what they *have*."

"I know what they have," I said. "I put most of it in their vault myself."

"And you think you can carry it out?"

"No," I said honestly. "But I think I can try while holding her hand, and that's worth more than certainty ever bought anyone."

The judges conferred. I use the word loosely—they didn't speak. They turned their masked faces toward one another, and between them passed something invisible and heavy, and the candles dimmed and brightened in rhythm with whatever transaction was occurring. Maelis's hackles were up; I could see them from across the hall, the wolf-shape of her shadow bristling along the wall. Wren had taken two steps toward me without seeming to move at all, the way children do, the way that makes your heart clench.

Then the eldest judge spoke.

"The court accepts."

Just like that. No flourish, no fine print recited, no cruelty delayed for pleasure. That frightened me more than any haggling would have. Courts like this only accept quickly when they're certain they're going to win.

"A contract will be drawn," the judge continued. "Witnessed and sealed, per the old forms. The girl's soul returns to her at the threshold. Your assets—" and here the mask managed, somehow, to smile, "—remain in escrow until the terms resolve. Begin the ascent at moonrise. The court wishes you a pleasant journey, Odran Kavanagh."

My name again. It rang in the hall like a coin dropped on marble, and every creature in that place watched it roll.

They brought the contract on a tray of black wood, and Brannoc read it three times, hunting for the trap, and I watched him find it—or rather, watch him fail to find it, which was worse. "It's clean," he said finally, and his voice sounded like a man reporting a flood. "That's what frightens me. They want you to walk. Odile—Odran—they *want* it. Twenty years they've held your name like a fishhook waiting for you to swim back, and now you've offered to swallow it walking."

"Then they believe I'll look back," I said.

"Don't you?"

I crouched down then, in front of Wren, so we were eye to eye. Up close I could see the flicker in her, the half-soul guttering like a lamp low on oil, and I hated them for it, hated them with a clean hot hate that felt better than anything I'd felt in two decades.

"Listen to me," I said. "On the walk up, they're going to whisper. They're going to show me things. My mother's voice. My old room. Maybe worse. And there's a chance—one chance—that I stumble. If I do, you grab my hand and you *pull*, and you don't care what I say or what I see, because none of it will be real. Do you understand? None of it. The only real thing on that stair is you."

Wren reached into the pocket of the coat I'd given her and pulled out the stub of charcoal she carried everywhere. On the stone floor, quick and certain, she drew a door. Then she pointed at it, and then at me, and then she tapped my chest twice with two small fingers.

*I know,* said the gesture. *I've opened doors before.*

"Yeah," I said, and my voice cracked like river ice in spring. "Yeah, you have."

* * *

Moonrise came down the long stairwell like a rumor, silver light pooling on steps worn hollow by centuries of feet—most of them small. Tithe-feet. I tried not to think about that. I failed.

They gave us the gate at the bottom of the great hall, the inner door, the one that opened onto the ascending way. Beyond it, stairs spiraled up into dark, and the dark was not empty. I could feel it *thinking*.

Maelis met me at the door. She wore her woman-shape for it, which she saved for moments that mattered, and her face was the same face I'd betrayed on a mountainside twenty years ago, aged by exactly the years I hadn't lived.

"If you look back," she said, "I will drag you up by your hair. I want you to know that. Sentiment ends at the threshold."

"That's the most comforting thing anyone's said to me in twenty years."

Her hand closed on my wrist, hard, wolf-strong. "Odran. Listen. Whatever they show you—and they will show you everything—it isn't a memory. It's bait wearing a memory's clothes. Memories don't ask you to stay. Only traps do."

"And if the trap looks like my mother?"

"Then you tell her," Maelis said, and her voice broke, just once, just slightly, on the word, "that her son chose to live. And you keep climbing."

She stepped back. She became a wolf again between one heartbeat and the next, because some truths are easier to hear from something with teeth.

Brannoc pressed a folded scrap of paper into my pocket. "Don't read it on the stairs," he said. "Read it after. If there is an after. It's my bill."

"Your bill?"

"For services rendered. It says: *you owe me a drink, and an apology, and the story of why you never told me.* In that order. Survive, and pay it."

Wren took my hand. Her fingers were warm, which they should not have been, given everything, and I decided to take it as an omen because I needed one.

The inner door opened without being touched. Of course it did. Doors in the Undercourt know who they're waiting for.

Beyond it: the stair, rising into black, and the black rising to meet us, full of voices warming up like an orchestra before the curtain. I heard my mother's laugh in it. I heard rain on a roof I'd slept under when I was six. I heard the first lie I ever told and the last person I ever failed.

I stepped through.

Behind me, faint as a candle seen through fog, the Undercourt began, very softly, to sing my name.

*Odran. Odran. Come home, Odran.*

I gripped the small warm hand in mine, fixed my eyes on the dark ahead, and started to climb.

I did not look back.

Yet.
