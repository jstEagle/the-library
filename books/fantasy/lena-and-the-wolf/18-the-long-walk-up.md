## 18. The Long Walk Up

**Wren**

The dark under the mountain is not empty dark. It is a dark with teeth in it, or maybe hands. Wren decides it is hands, because hands are what she knows. Hands have taken things from her before. Hands signed a paper once, when she was too small to hold a pen, and the paper took the loud bright half of her and left her with the quiet half, the half that sees doors.

Now she is walking up out of the mountain holding the hand of the man with two names, and the dark is trying to take him too.

She can hear it working. The dark talks in a voice like candle-smoke, and it says things to him that Wren cannot hear, only feel — the way you feel a dog growling through a floor. His hand has gone stiff in hers. His steps have gone strange, like a man walking in his sleep, or walking in someone else's sleep, which is worse.

She squeezes his fingers. Hard. The way you squeeze a bird so it doesn't fly into a window.

He squeezes back. Good. Birds can be reminded.

* * *

**Odile**

The stairs went down forever, Brannoc had said, and Odile had assumed the way up would be shorter. It was not. It was longer, because the court was in the stairs.

He heard his mother before he heard anything else.

*Odran. Odran, the porridge is going cold, and you'll not have it warm again until you've fetched the water.*

Her voice came out of the wall to his left, out of stone that had never been a kitchen, and it was perfect — the exact grain of it, the little catch when she said his name, the way she'd always made his name sound like both a scolding and a song. He had not heard that voice in twenty years. He had not let himself remember it, and here it was, unbroken, preserved in the dark like a fly in amber.

*Don't listen,* Wren's hand said. She squeezed his fingers, small and fierce, and he understood that she could feel the pulling the way he could feel weather in an old scar.

"I know it's not her," he said aloud. His voice came out cracked.

But knowing and believing are different doors, and the court was very good at doors.

* * *

**Wren**

The man-with-two-names stops walking. That is bad. Wren has learned on this climb that stopping is how the dark wins a little piece of you. You stop, and the dark puts something in front of your eyes like a plate at supper, and if you eat from the plate you forget the stairs.

She tugs his hand. He doesn't come.

So she does the thing she does, the only thing, the thing her quiet half is good for. She steps in front of him, into his eyeline, and she opens her mouth and says — in the old tongue, the dead tongue, the one the dark cannot counterfeit because the dead don't lie —

*Here. You are here. Walk.*

He blinks. His eyes come back from wherever they were, like birds coming home to roost.

"Right," he says, in her language, which sounds wrong in his mouth but right in her ears. "Right. Here. Walk."

They walk.

* * *

**Odile**

The childhood room came next. He smelled it before he saw it — tallow candles and wool and the particular dust of a place long shut — and then the wall of the stair opened like a curtain and there it was: his bed, his blanket with the worn corner he used to rub between finger and thumb, the window with frost ferns on the glass, and outside the window, impossibly, the valley where he'd been born, green under a summer sun that did not exist anywhere near this mountain.

He was eight years old. He could feel it. His knees were small. His hands were small. The whole shape of him was folding down toward that boy like a coat sliding off a hook.

*Odran. Come inside, love. It's late. You've been out in the dark long enough.*

He took one step toward the window.

Wren hit him.

Not hard — she was a child, she was half a child, half a soul in a small body — but she hit him square in the shin with the flat of her hand and then stamped her bare foot, and she looked up at him with those eyes that had seen the Undercourt's ledger, and she said in the old tongue, flat and furious:

*That is not a window. That is a mouth.*

And it was. He looked again and the frost ferns were teeth and the summer valley was a gullet and the whole warm room was the soft wet inside of a thing that ate tithe-children and kept them, kept them, kept them forever at eight years old with the porridge going cold.

He backed away. His heart was slamming. "Thank you," he said, and his voice was his own voice, forty-some years and scarred and hoarse, and he had never in his life been so glad to sound old.

Wren took his hand again. She did not let go after that. He noticed, distantly, that he did not want her to.

* * *

**Wren**

The dark tries a new thing. It stops talking to the man and starts howling, and the howl is a wolf's howl but wrong, a wolf made of candle-smoke, and it says a name over and over in a voice that is almost the real wolf-queen's voice but not, because the real one, Wren thinks, would never say the name like a question.

*Odran? Odran, where did you go?*

The man stops again. His face does something Wren has not seen it do. It comes apart, a little, like bread in milk.

Wren does not know this wolf-queen. She met her only at the edge of the trees, a shape of grey and gold eyes and a voice like a drawn blade, and the wolf-queen had looked at Wren and said *so you're the one he's breaking his whole life open for,* which Wren had not understood then and understands now, walking up these stairs, holding the hand that is coming apart.

She tugs him. Nothing.

She stamps. Nothing.

So she does the last thing. She puts her arms around his leg — she is not tall enough for anything better — and she holds on, and she says the only words she has that are hers, the ones she said the day she arrived at his door with no name and no voice and no cold in her bare feet:

*Door. I see a door. You are the door-keeper. Doors open. Doors shut. You choose.*

He looks down at her. The coming-apart face knits itself slowly back together, like frost melting in reverse.

"You're a bossy little creature," he says, in the common tongue, and his voice wobbles, and Wren decides that wobbling is allowed. Wobbling is what strong things do right before they hold.

They walk.

* * *

**Odile**

Somewhere past the thousandth step — he had stopped counting, counting was a way of measuring how much of himself was left — the whispers changed tactics. They stopped offering him the past and started offering him the future.

*You could stop here,* they said, in a voice that was almost his own. *You could rest. You've walked far enough. You've paid enough. No one would know. No one would blame you. The child could go on without you; children always do.*

And that one was dangerous, because it was half true, and the court only ever used true things as bait.

His legs were lead. The stair went up and up and the candle-smoke voice poured into his ears like warm water, and he thought: *I could sit down. Just for a moment. Just to rest my eyes.*

He was actually turning, actually lowering himself toward the stone, when Wren yanked his hand with both of hers, all her small weight behind it, and said — in the common tongue, in his tongue, the first time she'd spoken it on the whole climb, her accent terrible, her grammar a ruin:

"No. *Up.*"

One word. His word, thrown back at him.

He laughed. It came out of him like something breaking — a laugh and a sob in the same breath — and he got his feet under him and stood, and said, "Yes. Up. You're right. Up."

And he thought, with a clarity that frightened him more than any whisper: *This is what I was for. Not the gate. Not the twenty years of seals and solitude. This. A hand in the dark that doesn't let go.*

* * *

**Wren**

Near the top — Wren knows it is near the top because the dark has stopped whispering and started screaming, which means it is losing — the stairs show them the worst thing.

It shows Wren her mother.

Not the mother who signed the paper. The other mother, the one from before, the one who braided her hair by the fire and sang songs in the old tongue and called her by a name that Wren has been carrying all this way like a stone in her shoe, a name she has not said aloud since the day the collectors came.

*Little one,* the dark says, in her mother's voice, perfect, perfect, perfect. *Come back down. It's warm down here. We didn't mean to sell you. Come back and we'll unsay it.*

And Wren stops, because it is her mother's voice, and a body cannot help stopping for its mother's voice, any more than a bird can help flying at a window.

But then she feels the man's hand tighten on hers. He is not pulling her. He is just holding on. Being there. The way she was there for him at the window-that-was-a-mouth and the bed that was a boy's bed and the howl that was almost a wolf.

*It is not her,* she tells herself, in the old tongue, in the language the dead gave her and the dark cannot wear. *My mother's voice has a floor under it. This voice has stairs.*

She looks at the shape of her mother in the dark, the warm shape, the open arms, and she says — aloud, her first full sentence in the common tongue, the language of the world above, the language of doors that open outward:

"That is not my mama. My mama is *up.*"

The shape folds like smoke. The screaming stops.

The man looks down at her and his eyes are wet and he says nothing at all, which is right, because some things do not need words, only a hand.

* * *

**Odile**

They climbed the last stretch in silence, and the silence itself was a kind of whispering — the court's final argument, delivered not in voices but in the weight of everything he'd buried.

He felt all of it rising in him as they went: the tithe-cart, the smell of the Undercourt's candles, his mother's real voice and his real name and the bargain he'd struck at this very threshold twenty years ago — *take the name, take the memories, take all of it, and let me buy the door shut behind me.* He had walked out of here a hollow man and called the hollow peace. He had built a life in the shape of the hole.

And now he was climbing back up carrying all of it, every piece the court had ever held, and it was heavy, gods, it was heavy, heavier than any tithe, and Wren's hand in his was the only rope.

"Almost there," he said. His voice echoed strangely. The stair was changing — the stone under his boots was older, colder, and somewhere far above there was a grey thread of something that might be light.

Wren squeezed his hand.

Ahead, the last step. Ahead, the threshold where the dark ended and the world began, and Odile felt the court gathering itself for one final offer, one last thing to put on the plate.

He did not know yet what it would show him. But he felt Wren beside him, small and stubborn and half a soul, and he thought: *whatever it shows me, I will not be looking at it alone.*

He took the next step. And the next.

And then the dark, in its last desperate wisdom, showed him the boy.
