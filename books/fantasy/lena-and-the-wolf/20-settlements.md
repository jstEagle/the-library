## 20. Settlements

There is a particular kind of hangover that comes not from drink but from doing the right thing after twenty years of practice at the opposite. I woke on Odile's floor—his floor, though he'd have said the mountain's floor, and the mountain would have said nothing, being a mountain—with my mouth tasting of candlewax and old law, and my hands still shaking.

Not fear-shaking. I want to be clear about that, for the record I was about to start keeping. The shaking of a man who has spent a night arguing with masked judges who are older than the concept of argument, and won, and does not entirely trust the winning.

Below us, the Undercourt had gone quiet in the way a market goes quiet when the soldiers come through—not empty, just holding its breath. Above us, dawn was doing its slow grey work on the snowfields of Thornfell Pass, and Wren was asleep against Odile's shoulder with her whole shadow lying properly beneath her for the first time since I'd known her, both halves of it hers again, dreaming whatever half-souled children dream when they become suddenly, entirely, one person.

Odile caught me looking.

"Don't," he said.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say something lawyerly."

I was. It's a disease. "I was going to observe," I said, "that under the third covenant of the Deep Accord, a soul ransomed by wager and carried out without backward glance reverts to the claimant free of all encumbrance, which means—" I gestured at the sleeping child, at the man she slept against, at the whole ridiculous architecture of what they'd done down there— "you two are now legally each other's."

"We're not *legally* anything," Odile said. But he shifted so Wren wouldn't slide when he stood, and I let him have the point, because some victories you grant an opponent out of professional courtesy.

* * *

The collectors dissolved over breakfast.

That's the only honest word for it. We came up to the gatehouse to find the three of them standing in the yard where the snow hadn't marked them, because snow knows better than to mark paper. They were still in their fine grey clothes, still polite, still patient—but patience, it turns out, is a garment, and underneath it they had never been anything but contract. And the contract was void.

The tall one spoke first. His voice had been smooth as vellum before; now it had a rasp in it, like a page being torn very slowly.

"The debt is discharged," he said. "The account is closed. We are—" and here something moved across his face like weather, like confusion wearing a face, "—we are uncertain what we are."

"Unemployed," I offered.

They turned to me as one. There was a moment where I genuinely thought I might die in a snowfield in my shirtsleeves, and I noted, distantly, that this would be an ironic end for a man who had survived the Undercourt by never volunteering information.

But the tall one only inclined his head. "Counsel Brannoc. Your argument was... irregular."

"It was *sound*. Irregularity is how sound arguments feel from the losing side."

"Consent," said the second collector, the woman, tasting the word like a foreign fruit. "We were built to collect debts contracted freely. If consent can be proven absent—if duress voids the signature—then there was never a debt. Only a theft wearing our paperwork." She looked at her own hands. They were coming apart at the edges, gently, the way smoke comes apart when you open a window. "We did not know we could be theft."

"You didn't ask," I said. "That was always the flaw in your design. You verified signatures. You never once asked whether the people signing could afford to mean it."

The third collector—the short one, the one who had smiled throughout the entire hearing in a way I had wanted to break teeth over—looked up at the sky. Actual sky. Grey cloud and blowing snow and a single stubborn bird crossing it, and I swear on whatever's left of my oath to the bar below that the creature looked at that bird like a man reading a language he'd forgotten he knew.

"Is it always," the short one asked, "like this? Up here? So much... *weather*?"

"Every day," Odile said. "You get used to it."

And then they went. Not dramatically—that would have been too kind, too story-shaped. They simply grew less certain of their edges, and then less certain of themselves, and then there was a smell of cold ash and old ink, and three sets of fine grey clothes lay flat on the snow with nobody in them, and the wind began the long patient work of burying them.

Wren watched from the doorway. When the last of it settled, she walked out, picked up one grey glove, turned it over twice, and dropped it.

Then she looked at me and made a gesture I've thought about every day since: she pointed at the clothes, then at her own chest, then shook her head slowly. *Not mine. Never was.*

"No," I told her. "It never should have been anybody's."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, decided I had passed some private examination, and went back inside to steal the last of the honey bread. Which, I note for the record, she did. Repeatedly. The child had spent months as collateral in a soul-debt and emerged with an unshakeable conviction that honey bread was hers by right of conquest, and honestly, who was I to argue with precedent?

* * *

I stayed at Thornfell eleven days. I kept telling myself it was to draft the dissolution filings—there are forms even for void contracts, especially for void contracts, the Undercourt loves its paperwork the way a spider loves silk—and that was true, in the way that most of my excuses have been true the way a coin is true: technically, and only worth something if you squint.

The real reason was that I couldn't decide what to do with myself.

Understand my position. I had been, in my day, a rising advocate of the Undercourt. I drafted tithes. I wrote the clauses that made parents' grief into binding consideration and children's souls into transferable property, and I wrote them *beautifully*, with such elegant reciprocity clauses that the judges below used to read them aloud for pleasure. Then I made the mistake of reading one of my own contracts as if I were the child in it, and the edifice of my career collapsed in an afternoon, and I fled upward with nothing but my knowledge and my shame, and I spent nineteen years drinking myself into a shape that couldn't hold either.

Now the contracts were void. All of them, potentially—the Maelis precedent, once filed, would unravel decades of sales. Every clause I'd ever written was suddenly a liability instead of an asset. Somewhere below, masked judges were already convening emergency sessions, and somewhere above, in villages all along the mountain's skirt, there were families whose sold sons and daughters might come walking home.

Somebody would have to argue those cases. Somebody who knew the forms. Somebody who had written them.

"Somebody," Odile said, watching me not-drink the tea he'd given me, "who owes a debt of his own, if you're keeping ledgers these days."

"I'm always keeping ledgers. It's the drinking I've given up." I turned the cup. "You know what they'll call me. Up here. A lawyer from below. They'll spit at my door."

"They spat at mine for twenty years and I was only a gatekeeper."

"Yes, but you were a *useful* gatekeeper. Smugglers need passes opened. What do villagers need lawyers for except funerals and land disputes, and they'll handle those themselves with fists and grudges as they always have."

"They'll need you," Odile said, "the first time something comes up out of the ground and asks a family for a signature. And something will. The court doesn't die because three collectors did. It just gets more careful. More polite. It'll come up smiling, offering bargains, and every village on this mountain will need one person who can read the trap before they sign." He shrugged, that economical shrug of his that always seemed to carry more weight than the motion justified. "Or don't. It's your soul. That was rather the theme of the week."

I hated him a little, in the way you hate a mirror.

* * *

Wren's parents arrived on the ninth day.

I saw them coming up the pass road from the gatehouse window—a man and a woman, thin as winter branches, dressed in the patched good-clothes of people who own one set, walking with the particular posture of the penitent, which is to say bent forward slightly, as if perpetually about to apologize. A farmer's cart stood abandoned at the treeline; they'd walked the last miles rather than arrive with anything between themselves and judgment.

Odile met them at the door. I hung back, professionally, the way counsel does when the parties must speak without advocacy—though God knows what advocacy I could have offered. There is no filing for *we sold our daughter*. No clause elegant enough to dress it.

The mother spoke first. Her voice was wrecked, days of crying and road behind it. "We heard. In the valley, they're saying—the debt's dead, the child's free, the mountain opened and shut again. We heard and we came." She twisted her hands. "We don't—we aren't asking—"

"You are," Odile said quietly. "Everyone asks. It's all right to ask."

The father knelt. Actually knelt, in the snow, in front of a door he'd probably cursed a hundred times for closing off the high road. "We signed," he said. "Nobody held our hands to the paper. There was famine, there was the tithe-man smiling, there was the winter coming, and we told ourselves it wasn't really selling, it was *placement*, it was—God forgive us, we told ourselves she wouldn't remember, she was so small—" His voice broke and he let it. "We will stand wherever you put us. We will take whatever she gives. We only need to see her alive and whole, and then we'll go, if that's her answer. We'll go."

From inside the house, Wren had been listening. Of course she had. The child had spent her whole life learning to listen through doors—it may have been the one skill her ordeal sharpened rather than stole.

She came out barefoot into the snow—I'd stopped being alarmed by this; the cold had never owned her, not even when half of her belonged elsewhere—and stood on the step above her kneeling father, and looked at her mother, and the silence went on so long that even Odile, who could out-wait a glacier, shifted his weight.

Then Wren did something I will carry to my grave as evidence that the law understands nothing.

She reached out and touched her mother's face. One hand, small fingers, laid flat against the cheek the way you'd test whether a thing is real. Her mother went absolutely still, tears freezing on her lashes, afraid that any movement would end it.

And Wren nodded. Once. Slowly. The nod of a judge accepting a plea—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not for years, but *acceptance*. Acknowledgment. *I see you. I see what you did. I see that you came.*

Then she pointed at herself, and at Odile, and folded her arms.

The mother's face did several things at once. "You want to—to stay here? With him?" Not accusation. Bewilderment. The bewilderment of a woman who had braced for hatred and received instead a decision she had no category for.

"She chooses," Odile said. "That's the settlement. Nobody signs anything. Nobody trades. She stays because she stays, and she visits because she visits, and if one day she walks down that road to you for good, that's hers to give." He paused. "In spring. When the pass opens. She'll come down and stay a week, or a month, however long she wants. And you'll write to her. Letters. She's learning her letters"—this was true; I had been teaching her, mostly so she could someday read contracts, and had discovered she learned everything the way fire learns a wick— "and she'll write back."

The father was still kneeling. Wren looked down at him, frowned with the specific irritation of a child confronted with adult theatrics, and tugged his sleeve until he stood. Then she took his hand and her mother's hand and walked them, briefly, around the yard—three turns, I counted, an old circuit, a warding circuit, one I recognized from below and said nothing about—and brought them back to the door.

And there she stopped, and pointed at the house, and pointed at the table visible through the window with the honey bread on it, and raised her eyebrows.

They ate with us. It was the strangest meal of my life, and I have dined with things that wear masks over no faces. The parents barely spoke. Wren distributed bread with imperial generosity. Odile passed the butter in silence. And gradually, degree by degree, the parents' shoulders came down from around their ears, and the mother laughed once—a startled, broken-glass laugh—at something Wren mimed about a goat, presumably, or possibly a smuggler, and the ice didn't melt, exactly, but it cracked in places where light could get through.

Imperfect. I want to be honest about that, because everyone who tells this story later will make it clean. The mother flinched, twice, when Wren moved suddenly. The father couldn't look at Odile at all. Nothing was resolved; nothing was *resolved* over honey bread in a gatehouse in the mountains. But justice, real justice, the kind that lives outside courtrooms and ledgers, is almost never resolution. It's a door left unlocked. It's a visit scheduled for spring. It's a child deciding, with the terrible clean authority of the very young, that she will not let what was done to her be the whole of her story.

When the parents left, walking back down toward the valley in the late light, Wren stood on the step and watched them until they rounded the bend. Then she came inside, sat down next to me, leaned against my arm—the first time she'd initiated contact with me, and I sat rigid as a witness under oath lest I spoil it—and fell asleep.

"Well," I whispered to Odile. "That's that, then."

"That's that."

"I'm staying, you know. In the valley. Taking clients." I said it fast, before the coward in me could file an objection. "Someone has to watch the tithe-men. Someone has to teach the villages what a bad contract looks like. And someone owes—" I gestured at the sleeping child, at the whole impossible household— "well. Everything."

Odile almost smiled. On his face it was a seismic event; birds should have gone up from the trees. "The valley will hate you."

"The valley hates everyone. It's how you know you're home." I looked out the window at the pass, at the great sealed-and-now-locked door of the world, at the smoke of the gatehouse fire rising straight up into the windless evening. "Besides. I hear the new arrangement needs a witness. Three keys, isn't it? Yours, the wolf's, the girl's. Someone ought to keep the record of who comes through. A ledger-keeper above ground, to balance the books below."

"You're volunteering."

"I'm *drafting terms*. You can accept them or counter. Those are your options. I'm a lawyer; it's how I say I'd like to stay."

He accepted. He didn't even counter. Amateurs.

* * *

On my last night at the pass, Wren found me packing and presented me with a piece of charcoal and a look of command. She pulled the slate I'd been teaching her letters on, wiped it clean, and wrote—slowly, tongue between her teeth, each character a small act of will:

**BRANOC**

Missing an N. I pointed at it. She glared at me, added the N with the air of a queen granting a pardon, and then, beneath it, drew a small figure with a bottle, crossed it out, and drew a small figure with a book instead.

"There's no taking that back," I warned her. "Drawn contracts are binding in at least four jurisdictions I know of."

She nodded solemnly. She knew. She'd lived under worse jurisdictions than any of them.

I walked down out of the pass the next morning with a satchel of blank paper, a head full of clauses I intended to spend the rest of my life unpicking, and—for the first time in nineteen years—no drink waiting for me at the bottom of the road. Just work. Just the valley, and the villages, and the long war of small mercies against beautiful paperwork.

Behind me, high on the mountain, the gatehouse smoke rose straight and steady, and I could have sworn—though I'd deny it under oath—that I heard a wolf howl once, far off, in a tone that sounded suspiciously like *get on with it, counselor*.

I got on with it.

The first letter from Wren arrived at midwinter, six weeks later, delivered by a smuggler who claimed a wolf had handed it to him personally and that he would thank me never to mention it again. Inside, in enormous careful letters, was a single line:

**DEER BRANOC. THE DOOR IS LOKED BUT NOT SHUT. COME AT SPRING. BRING BRED WITH HONY.**

I framed it. I'm not ashamed. Some documents are worth more than anything they contract for—and besides, in at least four jurisdictions I know of, a letter bearing a request and a signature constitutes an appointment.

Spring was coming. I intended to keep it.
