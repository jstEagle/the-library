## 21. Two Names, One Door

The hinges were the first thing I fixed.

Twenty years I'd kept that door shut with iron bands and salt and a stubbornness my neighbors would have called piety if they'd known it existed at all. Now I stood in the cold mouth of the gatehouse with a pot of rendered tallow in one hand and a hinge the size of a coffin lid in the other, wondering—not for the first time—whether the mountain was laughing at me.

It probably was. Mountains have long memories and no sense of humor about being managed.

"Left side," Wren said. She didn't say much, still, but when she did say something it was always the thing you needed to hear exactly when you needed to hear it, which is either a gift or a curse depending on how much you enjoy being wrong in private. "You're lifting wrong. Let me."

"You're nine."

"I'm strong." She set down her cup of tea on a flat stone, rolled up sleeves that had been too big for her in autumn and now fit her like they'd been cut for her, which unsettled me more than I let show. Children who spend a winter below grow strange. Not wrong—strange. Like saplings that come up through a crack in a wall: nothing bad about them, but you know the wall's there.

She took the other end of the hinge and we swung it together into its brackets, and the sound it made going home—a deep, satisfied *chunk*—was the sound of twenty years of fear deciding to retire.

I wiped my hands on my coat and looked at what we'd done.

The gate of Thornfell Pass had been sealed since before I came to it. Sealed with lead poured into the lock, with wards stitched into the lintel by a man who charged me three months' wages and never told me his name, with a rule I recited to myself every night like a prayer: *the door stays shut, the past stays buried, the man stays nobody.* It had worked. That was the terrible joke of it. It had worked perfectly, right up until the night a barefoot girl walked out of a blizzard speaking a dead language, and every lock I'd ever built turned out to have been built from the inside.

Now the door hung open on fresh hinges, oiled and true, and the light came down the passage behind it like water finding an old channel.

"Close it," Wren said.

"Not yet." I fished in my pocket. "First, this."

* * *

The key had taken Brannoc two weeks to make, and he'd complained about every hour of it, which is how I knew he cared.

Brannoc, for those who haven't had the pleasure, is an exiled lawyer of the Undercourt living above ground as a village drunk, except that since the trial he has been drinking considerably less and practicing law considerably more, and the village of Hollowmere doesn't know yet whether it's been blessed or cursed. He made the key out of cold iron and silver and a sliver of bone he refused to identify, and when he handed it over he held onto it a moment longer than necessary.

"Three teeth," he said. "One for each name on the lock. You understand what that means, legally?"

"That none of us can open it alone."

"That none of us can open it alone," he agreed, "and that none of us can be locked out without the others' consent. Do you know how rare that is? A lock built on mutual obligation? The Undercourt would rather die. They'd call it sentimental." He'd smiled then, and it had been the old cynical smile but with something new underneath it, like green under burned ground. "Good. Be sentimental. It's the only loophole they never closed."

So now I held the key, and Wren watched me with her winter-grey eyes, and somewhere on the ridge above us a wolf was pretending not to be listening, which meant she was listening very closely indeed.

"Say the words," Wren said.

"There aren't words. It's a lock, not a spell."

"Brannoc said there were words."

"Brannoc says a lot of things. Brannoc once told me the Undercourt respects a well-argued letter. He was right, but that's not the point."

"What are the words?" she asked again, patient as stone, and I sighed and gave in, because I always give in with her, and because—though I'd have denied it under oath—the words wanted saying.

"*By choice and not by blood,*" I said. "*By keeping and not by keeping-out.*"

The lock took the key the way dry earth takes rain. Three teeth, three names. Mine went in first, and the mechanism turned a quarter-turn and stopped, waiting.

"Wren."

She took the key from me with both hands, solemn as a priestess, and turned it another quarter. Stopped again. And then she looked up the ridge, toward the grey shape sitting among the rocks with the studied indifference of someone who has been waiting to be invited for a very long time.

"Maelis," she called. "Your turn."

* * *

Maelis came down the slope the way she does everything—like the landscape itself had decided to move and the rest of us should feel honored to witness it. In her wolf shape she stands as high as my chest, silver-grey with a scar across one shoulder that I gave the location of, if not the wound, in a life I spent twenty years pretending hadn't happened. She shifted as she reached the gatehouse, because Maelis considers four legs honest and two legs ceremonial, and this was clearly a ceremony.

She looked older in human shape than she had below the mountain. Or maybe not older. Maybe just less guarded. Grief will do that—it wears you smooth instead of wearing you down, if you're lucky, and Maelis has always been lucky in the ways that hurt most.

"Well," she said. "The famous gate."

"It's not famous."

"It's infamous. Every smuggler from here to the coast tells stories about the madman of Thornfell Pass who guards a door that leads nowhere." She ran a hand along the wood, over the place where the lead seal used to be. "They say you sleep sitting up. They say you count the wards every morning like a miser counting coins."

"I do sleep sitting up."

"And now you're hanging a lock instead of a seal." She looked at me, and there it was—the question underneath all her questions, the one she'd demanded as her toll on the road, the one I'd paid in full on the descent and still felt lighter for paying. "You're sure, Odran?"

Hearing the name still landed like a hand on my shoulder. I was working on that. Brannoc said it would take about a year per decade of denial, so I had roughly two years of flinching left in me, and I intended to spend them productively.

"I'm sure," I said. "A sealed door is just a promise the dark can't touch anyone else. A locked door is a promise that the people you love can decide for themselves." I nodded at Wren. "She taught me that. Took her about a week. I'm slow."

"You're a rock," Maelis said. "Rocks are slow. Rocks also hold up mountains." She took the key from Wren's hands and turned it the final quarter, and the lock closed with a sound like a word being spoken gently, and that was that.

Three names. One door. None of us alone; none of us shut out.

Maelis handed the key back and looked at it a moment too long. "I'll want it back for the winter solstice. There are dens on the far side of the pass, and my young shouldn't have to go around."

"The far side of the pass is Undercourt territory."

"The far side of the pass," Maelis said, showing teeth that were mostly a smile, "is whatever I say it is, now that their best lawyer lives in a village and drinks tea. Things change, Odran. Doors open. Get used to it."

* * *

That evening I did the thing I'd been circling for days the way a dog circles a bed before lying down.

The pass ledger hangs in the gatehouse, a fat leather book chained to the desk because even honest records need protecting from dishonest weather. Every traveler through Thornfell signs it. For twenty years the entries read the same way: *Odile Kavanagh, keeper, checked seals, no traffic, no incidents.* Twenty years of the same sentence in the same handwriting, a man writing himself into existence one identical day at a time.

Wren sat on the desk swinging her legs while I opened it. She'd taken to supervising my paperwork, on the theory—which she had learned from Brannoc, God help us both—that anything written down becomes real, and therefore everything real should be written down properly.

"Sign it," she said.

"I'm getting to it."

"You've been looking at the page for a long time."

"It's a complicated page. It says 'no incidents' forty times."

"Sign your name, Odran."

And here's the thing I learned about names, the thing I could not have learned any way but the way I learned it: a name isn't a label. It's a load-bearing wall. Tear it out and the whole house comes down quietly around you while you tell yourself you're freer for it. Put it back, and you find out the house was never the prison. The missing wall was.

I dipped the pen. My hand shook, a little. I let it.

*Odran Kavanagh.*

Two names, one line. The boy sold to the court beneath the mountain, and the man who kept the door above it, and between them twenty years of salt and silence—and none of it erased, none of it wasted. Just joined. Welded. A hinge, not a scar.

Wren leaned over and read it upside down, lips moving, sounding it out. Then she looked up with an expression I was beginning to know very well and dread only slightly less: the expression of a child about to correct an adult.

"You spelled it wrong."

"I spelled it right."

"You wrote 'Kavanagh.' But Maelis says your mother's family name was—" she frowned, digging for it in the dead tongue where half her vocabulary lived, "—*Vaelcarn*. Wolf-friend. She says the court gave you that name when you were six, and you never used it because using it would mean admitting you belonged to someone."

The pen hovered. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pass, and the new-hung gate creaked on its oiled hinges—not the groan of a prisoner, just the ordinary voice of a door doing a door's work.

"She told you that?"

"She tells me things. She says I ask better questions than you do."

"She's right." I set the pen down. "Wolf-friend. She waited twenty years for me to use that name, and I couldn't even manage it in a ledger."

"So write it."

"It's not my legal name. Legally I'm Odile Kavanagh, keeper of the pass, and the magistrates in Hollowmere would—"

"Brannoc says," Wren said, with the gravity of a barrister of perhaps eleven months' standing, "that a name freely given outweighs a name merely registered. He says it's the oldest principle there is. Older than the court. Older than the wolves." She slid the pen back into my fingers and folded them around it, small hands over mine, the way you'd steady someone learning to shoot a bow. "He says you should stop hiding behind his law and start using mine."

"Which is?"

"Belonging is chosen," she said. "Not inherited. You told me that. On the mountain, coming up. When I was scared." Her grip tightened. "You don't get to forget it just because it's your turn to be scared."

So I crossed out nothing—for I am done, permanently done, with crossing things out—and beneath *Odran Kavanagh, keeper*, I added a second line:

*Odran Vaelcarn, wolf-friend.*

The ink dried. The wind moved. The door creaked its ordinary creak, and the mountain, if it was laughing, was laughing kindly, and I found—I tested it, the way you test a healed bone—that I didn't mind being laughed at by something that large. It meant I was finally small enough to be part of the landscape instead of at war with it.

"There," Wren said, satisfied. "Now it's real."

"Now it's real," I agreed.

She hopped off the desk, retrieved her cold tea, and drank it anyway, because children are indestructible in exactly the ways that matter. At the doorway she paused, silhouetted against the last of the light coming down the pass, and said, without turning around, in the old tongue, three soft syllables I didn't yet understand.

"What does that mean?" I asked.

She glanced back, and grinned—the first whole grin I'd seen from her, wide and crooked and entirely alive.

"You'll learn in spring," she said. "I'm teaching you. First lesson starts tomorrow. Bring paper."

And she was gone into the evening, and I sat alone in the gatehouse with a ledger full of names, a lock full of promises, and a door that was, at last, only a door.

Outside, far up the ridge, a wolf began to sing, and for the first time in twenty years, I recognized the song as a welcome home.
