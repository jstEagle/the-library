## 22. The Language of Coming Home

The old tongue has no word for *sorry*.

I learned this in the first week of spring, when I tried to teach it to Odile and he frowned at me like a man reading a contract with the last page torn off.

"What do they say instead?" he asked. He was sitting on the doorstep of the gatehouse with his boots off, which was itself a miracle worth writing down, because for twenty years—Brannoc told me this, smug as a cat in cream—Odile Kavanagh had slept in his boots. In case. In case of what, nobody ever asked, because the answer was obviously *in case the door needed keeping*, and everyone at Thornfell had learned not to ask about the door.

I drew it in the dirt instead of saying it, because some things are easier drawn. A circle. Then a smaller circle inside it. Then two hands, one on each side.

"Keeping," Odile said slowly. "They say *keeping*. You don't apologize for a thing, you—" He stopped. His jaw did the thing it does when a word is too big to swallow all at once. "You take it back into your keeping."

I nodded. That was the whole lesson, really. That was the whole language. The old tongue doesn't have sorry because sorry is a door you close behind you, and the Undercourt never closed doors. It only left them open and made you walk back through them forever.

My name means something else entirely. But we'll get to that.

* * *

Spring came to Thornfell Pass the way smugglers come: quietly, by roads nobody officially admits exist, and suddenly there it is in your kitchen eating your bread.

That's how Maelis described it, anyway, when she came loping down from the high snowfields with her pack stretched out behind her like a grey ribbon unspooling, and planted herself in front of the gatehouse, and announced that winter was over and therefore so was her patience.

Maelis talks the way avalanches start. You hear a small sound first and then everything comes down.

"You," she said to me, on that first morning, lowering her great head until her amber eye was level with mine. Her breath smelled of cold stone and pine sap. "Little bought-back one. The mountain says you sing in your sleep."

I do sing in my sleep. Brannoc told me that too, and then told me what I sang, and went very quiet, because it was a court-lullaby, the kind the gentry hum over cradles they intend to collect on. I can't help it. Half my soul spent its childhood down there before Odile carried the whole of me out, and half a soul keeps old habits the way a broken leg keeps the weather.

"I could teach you to sing something else," Maelis said. "Wolves know better songs."

And that is how spring actually began for me: with a wolf-queen teaching me howls in the pass at dawn while Odile stood in the doorway with his tea going cold, wearing the expression of a man who has lost control of his household and is beginning to suspect he never had any.

He loved it. Don't let him tell you otherwise. I've seen him lie to judges; I know the face.

* * *

Here is what Odile taught me that spring, in exchange for the words:

He taught me the smugglers' roads. All of them. The goat-track behind the frozen waterfall that isn't frozen after the third week of thaw, if you know which stones sweat. The hollow under the roots of the split oak where three generations of contraband have slept, and where somebody long dead scratched a map into the bark that Odile refuses to explain. The ford across the Greywater that looks deep but isn't, and the ford that looks shallow and absolutely is, and once tried to keep my boot as a toll.

"Why teach me all this?" I asked him, somewhere around the tenth road, when my legs had gone strong and brown and my feet had finally forgiven him for the boots he'd bought me, which were excellent boots but arrived with feelings attached. Feelings being: *you were barefoot in a blizzard and I will never recover.*

"Because," he said, in the tone of a man laying a card face-down, "a person should always know more than one way home."

I made him say it again slower, because the old tongue has a word for that sentence and I wanted to carry both versions. He grumbled. He said it again. He said it slower. And then, because he is Odile, he pretended to be very interested in a rock.

The old tongue version goes: *the-road-that-remembers-you-is-not-one-road.* It's a mouthful. Most things in the old tongue are mouthfuls, because the people who spoke it first lived underground and had nothing to do but talk carefully.

But here's another thing the old tongue taught me, the thing I want to set down properly, because Brannoc says a thing isn't finished until it's written and I am writing this, aren't I:

The old tongue has no word for *orphan* either.

It has a word for *child whose people are far away.* It has a word for *child whose people are dead.* It has a word—which took me three days and one tantrum to make Odile understand—for *child who chooses new people, and the choosing counts.* That last one is the word the court never used for me. The court used *asset.* My parents, when they came at the end of winter, hats in their hands and shame coming off them like heat off a forge, used *our girl,* which was true and also not enough, because you cannot sign a name you don't own onto a contract and then call yourself family. Brannoc handled that meeting. Brannoc, who once drafted contracts like theirs, sat across from my mother and father and read their own signatures back to them in a voice like frost forming, and made them listen to every clause, and then negotiated the visiting rights himself, free of charge, which for Brannoc is roughly equivalent to a saint performing miracles.

I go to them in spring. That was the bargain I chose, and choosing is the part that matters—I checked. I asked the word. The word agreed.

But I live at Thornfell. Because Thornfell is where the door is, and the door is where my people are, and my people are a man who slept in his boots for twenty years, a wolf-queen who sings worse than I do, and a disgraced lawyer of the Undercourt who now practices honest law in the valley village and complains about it constantly and would fight anyone who suggested he stop.

* * *

One evening in late spring—it was the evening the first fireflies came up out of the grass like the mountain exhaling sparks—Odile asked me to teach him his own name.

Not Odran. He'd said Odran plenty by then. He'd signed it in the ledger, said it to judges, shouted it up an endless stairway while a whole court whispered at his heels. Odran was paid for, spent, settled.

He meant the old tongue version. Names have versions, down below. A name is a sentence, and sentences have translations, and the translation of *Odran* —the one his mother must have used, the one the tithe-ledger burned away—was a thing he had carried out of the mountain without knowing he was carrying it, the way you carry a song you heard before you could talk.

"How do you know I ever had one?" he asked. "Half my memories went into that trade. Maybe the name-version went with them."

"If it went with them," I said, "it would be in me."

Because that's how the trade worked. His sold self didn't vanish. It went into the ledger, and the ledger went into the court, and the court poured what it couldn't use into its tithes—the children. Me. I had a burned-out page living in my chest for years without knowing what it was. It was why I drew doors. It was why I sang court-lullabies. It was why, the night I walked out of the blizzard to his gate, my feet knew the way before my head did.

Some of him had been waiting in me all along. Waiting for him to come down and get us both.

So I taught him. I put his hands flat on the doorstep stone, the way the old tongue wants you to learn names—hands on something solid, because a name spoken without ground under it drifts—and I said the sentence of him, slow, syllable by syllable, and made him repeat it until his voice stopped shaking.

It translates roughly as: *he-who-keeps-the-door-from-inside.*

Which is to say: the old tongue knew, before he did, before any of us did, that he wasn't the gatekeeper standing outside the door. He was the one holding it shut from within. Twenty years. From inside. Nobody escapes a place by leaving it; you escape it by turning around and making it safe for the ones still in there.

He cried a little. He'd deny it. There was a lot of very intense interest in the fireflies.

* * *

Now. The last page. Brannoc says save the best clause for last, and for once I agree with him.

All through spring, people kept asking me my name. Not cruelly. The valley folk are kind in a practical way—they bring bread first and questions second—but a child needs a name the way a door needs hinges, and everyone could see I was swinging loose. The schoolmistress asked. The smith asked. Even Maelis asked, once, delicately, in the way wolves are delicate, which is to say she asked by lying down next to me and waiting.

And I couldn't answer. Not because I didn't have a name. Because I did, and it was down below, in the ledger, on the page where my parents' signatures sat like two small cages, and I had left it there on purpose. I thought. I told myself I'd left it there on purpose, the way Odile had told himself he'd left *Odran* buried. We are a household of liars, the three of us, mostly lying to ourselves, and Brannoc charges no fee for calling it out.

But here is what the trial taught the court, and what the court taught me, though it never meant to: when Odile staked his whole self against mine, every claim the court held got dissolved. Every entry. Every page. The judges' masks cracked, the collectors came apart like smoke in wind, and the ledger—

Well. Brannoc went down and collected the ledger. Legally, he says, it was abandoned property. Morally, he says, it was loot, and he has never been happier about anything in his long slippery life. It sits in the gatehouse now, in a chest under the window, and Odile uses it to press flowers, which Maelis finds hilarious and the ghost of every judge beneath the mountain would find blasphemous, which makes it better.

My page was in there. Of course it was. And one warm evening in the last week of spring, I climbed up on a chair and got the chest down myself, because I was done letting adults decide which parts of me stayed locked away.

My parents' signatures were still on it. Signatures don't dissolve; they just stop meaning what they meant. I looked at them a long time. They looked small. They had always been small; I just hadn't been tall enough to see it before.

And above them, in the column where the court writes what a soul is *for*—they always write it down, the use they intend, because the gentry believe naming a purpose is the same as owning it—there was a blank space. The ink had lifted off it the day the contract broke. Whatever they'd planned for me, it was gone, unwritten, unowned.

A blank space. In the old tongue, a blank space on a ledger page has a word. Everything has a word. The word is *unclaimed,* and it is considered, down below, the most dangerous word there is. Unclaimed things belong to themselves.

I took the page outside, where the fireflies were starting, and I read my name aloud for the first time.

It sounded like water finding a channel. It sounded like a door opening onto morning. It sounded like exactly what it means, which the old tongue renders as a short bright phrase that fits in a child's mouth precisely because it was always meant to be spoken by one:

*The-one-who-was-bought-back.*

Not stolen. Not rescued, even, though I was that too, and I have thanked him, in the wordless way the old tongue prefers—with keeping, with hands, with staying. Bought *back.* As if I had belonged to something once, before the sale, and the sale was the mistake, and everything since—the blizzard, the door, the stairway, the trial, the long climb with his hand around mine while the dark called him home in his dead mother's voice—was the world correcting its own arithmetic.

Two souls went down that mountain owing each other everything. Two souls came up even. That's the trick nobody down below understands: a debt paid in full doesn't end a thing. It *starts* it. Everything after is given, not owed, and given things are the only things that count.

Odile came out when he heard me say it. He didn't ask what it meant. He'd earned the translation already, hands on stone, syllable by syllable. Instead he sat down on the step beside me, bootless, ridiculous, his flowers pressing in the ledger behind him and his wolf singing badly somewhere up the pass, and he said my name back to me. Once, carefully, getting it right. Then again, easier, the way you say a thing you intend to say ten thousand more times.

"The-road-that-remembers-you," I told him, "is not one road."

"No," he said. "It isn't."

And the door behind us stood open to the evening—not sealed, not shut, just locked, three keys between three keepers—and the fireflies rose out of the grass like the mountain learning, at last, to breathe out instead of hold its breath.

My name is the-one-who-was-bought-back.

Come in. The kettle's on.
