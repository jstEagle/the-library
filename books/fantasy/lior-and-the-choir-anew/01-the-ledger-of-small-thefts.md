## 1. The Ledger of Small Thefts

The counting house of the Vellum Guild smelled of tallow, ink, and other people's lives.

Cyrus Amberdale had worked there eleven years and still could not decide which scent bothered them most. Tallow was honest. Ink was at least a tool. But the third smell—the one that clung to the scales, to the brass weights, to the velvet-lined trays where the vials sat in their little rows like teeth—was the smell of memory itself, faintly sweet, faintly sour, like milk that had decided against turning. You stopped noticing it after your first month, Ondrey had told them. You never do, Cyrus had learned instead. You simply learn to breathe through your mouth when it matters.

The morning's first client was already waiting in the antechamber, which meant the morning's first memory was already decaying by a candle's worth. Memory kept poorly outside the skull. Everyone knew this; it was why the Guild's fees began the moment you crossed the threshold. Time is our rival, Ondrey liked to say, and time always collects.

Cyrus set the day's ledger on the counter—a fat book bound in gray calfskin, its spine cracked into softness from years of hands—and opened it to a fresh page. Around them the counting house woke in its usual order: the shutters rattled up by Pell, who did the weighing and pretended not to hear what he weighed; the brazier fed with coal by Marta, who ran the extraction room below and came up each morning with her sleeves rolled and her eyes red-rimmed from other people's weeping; and above it all, through the floorboards, the slow creak of Ondrey's boots as the guildmaster paced his office, doing whatever guildmasters did before the doors opened. Praying, probably. Cyrus had never asked. There were things you didn't ask a man who bought grief by the ounce.

"Send her in," Ondrey called down, or perhaps it was only the pipes. It hardly mattered. Cyrus lifted the hatchet-shaped bell pull and gave it one short ring.

* * *

The woman who entered was perhaps fifty, dressed in mourning black gone gray at the seams, with the particular posture of someone who had spent months learning to carry a weight without letting anyone see it. She looked at the scales the way most clients did on their first visit—with hunger and horror in equal measure, the way a starving man looks at meat he knows came from somewhere.

"You're the clerk?" she said.

"I'm the keeper of ledgers." Cyrus gestured to the chair across the counter. "Please sit. May I have your name for the book?"

She hesitated. They all hesitated. "Widow Sarel Voss."

"And what would you like to sell today, Mistress Voss?"

The question was ritual. Every client got it word for word, because every client needed a moment to say it aloud before they could bear to have it done. Widow Voss's hands found each other in her lap and gripped.

"My son," she said. "He died in the spring. Fever. He was nineteen." Her voice did the thing voices did—not breaking, but going flat, pressed smooth like cloth under an iron. "I can't—I wake up and for half a breath I've forgotten he's dead, and then I remember, and it's like losing him again, every morning, every morning since April. I want it out. All of him. The fever, the dying, the—" A breath. "All of it. I'll pay whatever the Guild asks."

Cyrus wrote her name. Wrote the date. Wrote, in the neat clerk's hand that eleven years had ground into their fingers: *One (1) grief, maternal, re: deceased son, age 19, fever.*

"The Guild will need to know the weight before quoting a fee," they said. "Marta does extractions below stairs. It's painless. You'll feel it leave like a long exhale. Afterward you'll remember that you had a son, that he lived, that he died—but the shape of it won't hurt anymore. That's what we sell, if we're being honest. Not forgetting. Anesthesia."

"That's what I want."

"It's what most people want." Cyrus dipped the pen. "There are options, before we proceed. We can take the whole of it—all memories touching his death—or we can take only the worst hours, leaving you the rest. Some clients find that the whole removal leaves a strange hole. Grief has roots. Pull all of it and you may find yourself weeping at things you don't understand—a song, a coat in a shop window—for years. The partial extraction is gentler. Slower to heal, but it heals toward something."

"No." Widow Voss shook her head, quick, before Cyrus had even finished. "No, all of it. I don't want to heal toward anything. I want it gone."

And there it was. Cyrus had heard it a hundred times, in a hundred registers—rage, exhaustion, numbness—but underneath it was always the same sentence, the one no one said outright: *I want to stop paying for having loved someone.*

They wrote the fee. It was high. It was always high; grief was heavy, and heavy memories fetched good prices downstream, though where exactly they went was one of those questions the Guild discouraged. To the rich, who wished to experience sorrow safely, secondhand, in vials. To certain collectors. To certain offices of the crown whose seals Cyrus was not supposed to recognize on the delivery manifests but did.

"Mistress Voss." Cyrus set down the pen. "Before we go downstairs. I'm required to tell you that the extraction is permanent. What leaves cannot be returned. If, a year from now, you should want even one hour of him back—one ordinary hour, him at breakfast, complaining about the porridge—you will not be able to buy it. No one will be able to sell it to you. It will exist nowhere in the world except possibly in a stranger's head, and strangers do not give things back."

"I know what I'm asking."

"People say that." Cyrus kept their voice level, clerkish, kind in the way a wall is kind. "I'm required to say it twice."

Something flickered across the widow's face then—not anger. Recognition. She leaned forward, and her voice dropped, and for a moment she was not a client but simply a mother in a room with another human being.

"Clerk. My son used to sing while he shaved. Badly. He sang so badly the neighbors complained, and I'd stand in the hall listening to it and think, *that boy will be the death of me,* and now he is, he is the death of me, every single morning, and I cannot—" Her hands unclenched, clenched again. "You keep asking me if I understand. I understand better than you do. I've been understanding for six months. Take it. Take all of it. Please."

Cyrus looked at the ledger page. At the neat line of ink. *One (1) grief, maternal.* As if it were a sack of flour. As if it were small.

They thought—briefly, treacherously—of the second book.

* * *

Every counting house keeps two sets of books. This was not corruption; it was architecture. The true ledger recorded everything that passed through the Guild's hands, because the Guild answered to its creditors, and creditors were shown truth. The presentation ledger recorded what the crown's assessors were permitted to see, because the crown taxed memory-trade at a rate that assumed honesty and punished it anyway. Ondrey maintained both with the serenity of a man who believed, sincerely, that the difference between the two books was merely a difference in audiences.

But Cyrus Amberdale kept a third.

It lived inside the true ledger itself, which was the safest place a secret could live—in plain sight, in arithmetic. A column of figures that reconciled perfectly. A weight here, a tare there, a rounding error of two drams that any assessor would wave past and any colleague would blame on the scales. Eleven years of two-dram errors, each one real, each one deliberate, each one a memory that had come off the scale lighter than it went on.

Because Cyrus took. Not much. Never much. A dram here, three there, skimmed from the top of the heaviest lots before weighing—the way a miller's thumb presses the scale, the way any thief works, invisibly, patiently, inside the tolerance of the system. And what Cyrus skimmed, Cyrus kept. In the vault of their own skull, behind a door only they could open, because Cyrus's gift was not the Guild's gift. The Guild extracted with needles and rites and Marta's red-rimmed patience. Cyrus needed only to touch.

They had never told Ondrey. They had never told anyone. The third book was written in the only ink that couldn't be seized or burned: a ledger of small thefts, kept in the dark of their own head, entries like—

*A dram of bread-warmth, from lot 44, an old man's memory of his wife baking. Kept.*

*Three drams, lot 112, a girl's first snowfall. Kept.*

*A single hour, lot 209, a soldier's letter home, unread. Kept.*

Cyrus did not fully understand why they did it. Or rather—they understood it the way you understand hunger, which is to say completely and uselessly. The world poured its worst days into the counting house, and the Guild weighed them and sold them, and somewhere in the arithmetic of it all, Cyrus had begun quietly refusing. Refusing to let every beautiful small thing be priced. Someone had to keep the bread-warmth. Someone had to keep the snow. Otherwise the world's account balanced entirely toward loss, and Cyrus could not bear to be the clerk of that book alone.

So they skimmed. So they kept. So the third ledger grew, entry by entry, a hidden treasury of other people's joy—which was, when Cyrus was honest, the only honest wealth they had ever held.

* * *

"Then we'll proceed," Cyrus said aloud, to Widow Voss, and rang for Marta.

The extraction took less than an hour. Cyrus stayed upstairs, because staying downstairs was a mercy they could not afford daily, and listened to the muffled sounds through the floorboards—the low murmur of Marta's voice walking the widow through it, the creak of the extraction chair, and once, briefly, a sound that might have been singing or might have been sobbing and was probably both, because at the end, just before the memory let go, clients almost always made that sound. The body protesting what the mind had ordered.

At noon the widow came up the stairs on her own feet, pale and steady, and looked at Cyrus with eyes that were already beginning to smooth over, like water settling after a stone.

"Do I owe anything else?" she asked. Polite. Almost pleasant. Six months of mornings, gone between one breath and the next.

"No, Mistress Voss. The fee is settled." Cyrus counted out her change from the purse she'd left, and their hand paused over the coins—just a moment, just a heartbeat longer than procedure allowed. "Was there—forgive me. Procedure asks. Was there anything of him you'd have wanted kept? The Guild destroys nothing without consent. His name, perhaps. Names are light. We can hold a name in trust for a nominal sum, return it to you whenever you wish."

She frowned, genuinely puzzled, a woman looking for a word in a language she'd been made to forget. "Kept? Whose name?"

And that—that was the moment. Cyrus had watched complicity crack in themselves a hundred times over eleven years, in small ways, in the dark, in the privacy of the third ledger. But this was the first time it cracked in daylight, in front of a client, in front of God and the scales.

Because the widow had sold *all of it*, and *all of it* included his name, and no one had thought to warn her that *all of it* meant *all of it*, and now a woman was standing in the counting house of the Vellum Guild unable to remember that she had ever had a son at all—only a smooth gray absence where nineteen years of loving someone had been, and a vague conviction that she was owed something she couldn't name.

"He sang badly," Cyrus said. Their voice came out strange. "While he shaved. The neighbors complained."

The widow blinked at them. Smiled politely, the way one smiles at a madman or a priest. "How odd," she said, kindly. "Why would you say that?"

She took her change. She thanked them. She walked out into the ash-gray morning of Copperlane, and the door closed behind her with the small, decent sound of a lid going onto a box.

* * *

Cyrus stood at the counter for a long time.

Pell glanced over from the scales. "Client trouble?"

"No."

"You've gone white as vellum."

"I'm fine." Cyrus sat, opened the true ledger, and looked at the day's entry. *One (1) grief, maternal, re: deceased son, age 19, fever. Weight: 41 drams. Fee settled in full.* Forty-one drams. Somewhere downstream, some collector would pay a fortune for forty-one drams of a mother's love for her dead boy, and would weep over it in a drawing room, safely, deliciously, and would never know his name either, because his name had gone wherever names go when no one holds them.

Names are light, Cyrus had said. We can hold a name in trust.

Nobody had.

Cyrus picked up the pen. And then, with the practiced ease of eleven years, they made the entry lie. Not the weight—the weight was witnessed, weighed, sealed. Just the disposition. In the true ledger, in the column only Ondrey and the creditors saw, Cyrus wrote: *Lot 311, 41 drams, held pending buyer.*

And in the third book—the one written nowhere, the one kept in the dark behind their own eyes—they made a new entry, the largest they had ever made, larger than all the skimmed drams of eleven years put together:

*One (1) grief, maternal, 41 drams. One (1) son, aged 19, sings badly while shaving. Name unknown, name lost, name mine now until someone claims it. KEPT.*

It wasn't restitution. It wasn't even close—it was theft of a different color, taking what no one had sold them, hoarding a stranger's dead child in the vault of their own skull because no one else would. Cyrus knew exactly what it was. It was a clerk cooking a book because he could not bear the arithmetic of the world.

But when they closed their eyes, they could feel it settle into place among the other kept things—the bread-warmth, the snow, the unread letter—a new weight in the treasury, warm and grieving and alive. Somewhere in Cyrus's head, a boy was still singing badly in the hallway of a house that no longer existed anywhere else on earth.

Someone had to keep it.

"Cyrus." Ondrey's voice from the top of the stairs, and something in its tone—careful, too casual—made Cyrus look up. The guildmaster stood on the landing with a folded paper in his hand, and he was not pacing, and he was not serene, and the paper bore a seal that Cyrus was not supposed to recognize but did. "Close the book. Come up. There's been a proclamation read at the palace gates this morning, and I think—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I think the Guild's accounts are about to be audited."

Down in the street, faint through the shutters, a crier's bell had begun to ring.
