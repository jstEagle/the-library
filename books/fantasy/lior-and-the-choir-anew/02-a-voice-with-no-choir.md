## 2. A Voice With No Choir

The dead man had been a cooper by trade, and his widow wanted the mourners to remember him happy.

That was all Lior ever asked for, when they took a commission. Not *make them weep*—weeping came cheap, and any street singer with a cracked voice and a sad song could buy tears by the pound. What Maren Cooper wanted was harder and kinder: she wanted forty people in a smoke-dark parlor to remember her husband laughing at his own bad jokes, remember him lifting their daughter onto his shoulders in the orchard, remember the two of them dancing badly at their wedding while the fiddler gave up trying to keep time.

None of those things had happened. Lior would make them true anyway.

They stood in the corner of the parlor where the lamplight pooled thinnest, hands loose at their sides, and let the room settle into them the way water settles into a cup. Grief has a shape. So does guilt—Lior could feel it on the widow like damp wool, the terrible arithmetic of a life measured against itself: *I should have said, I should have gone, I should have stayed.* Forty mourners, forty private ledgers of debt, all of it pressing down until the air in the room felt thick enough to chew.

Lior breathed it in. Then they began to sing.

It wasn't a song anyone knew. That was the trick of it—the first rule Ondrey had ever taught them, back when Lior was fourteen and still flinched at their own gift. *Never sing something they can hum along to. If they know the tune, they'll know it isn't theirs. Sing the space between songs, and their hearts will fill it in themselves.*

So Lior sang the space between songs.

The sound went through the parlor like warmth through cold glass. It didn't push. It never pushed. It only opened a door inside each listener—a door they hadn't known was there—and behind that door, light. The widow's shoulders dropped an inch. The daughter, red-eyed and rigid in the front row, blinked as though waking from a nap in summer grass. Somewhere in the room a man laughed before he could stop himself, and then pressed his hand over his mouth, ashamed, and then—this was the miracle—wasn't ashamed.

Because now he remembered. He remembered old Tam Cooper standing in the cooperage doorway with sawdust in his beard, telling the joke about the bishop and the barrel hoop, laughing so hard he had to sit down on his own anvil. He remembered it the way you remember anything true: completely, without question, from the inside.

He had not been there. There had been no such afternoon. But grief is a hungry thing, and hunger doesn't check its food for rot.

Lior sang the memory into the room the way you'd pour wine into cups—not looking at any single face, letting it find its level—and watched forty people lean, almost imperceptibly, toward the warmth. The widow closed her eyes. Her lips moved. From the shape of them, Lior thought she was saying *oh*, over and over, soft as prayer. *Oh. Oh. I'd forgotten. I'd forgotten how he—*

You haven't forgotten, Lior thought, singing. You never knew. But you'll keep this one. You'll keep it your whole life, and it will be more real to you than the true days, because I made it out of what you needed instead of what happened.

Is that a kindness or a theft?

They had stopped asking themselves that question years ago. Mostly.

The song ended the way tide ends—not stopping but withdrawing, leaving the shore changed. For a moment nobody moved. Then the daughter turned to her mother and said, "Do you remember the orchard?" and her voice broke open with something that wasn't grief anymore, and the widow said "I remember," and held her, and both of them wept the good kind of tears, the kind that wash rather than drown.

Lior slipped out through the kitchen while the room was still soft, taking their fee—eleven marks, half up front, as always—and their coat, and the particular loneliness that followed every job like a dog that wouldn't be sent home.

* * *

Outside, Ashmarket was doing what Ashmarket did after dark: burning its refuse and its secrets together. The chandlers' quarter sent up a greasy column of smoke, and the ash-sellers were raking their gray harvest into sacks, and the whole street smelled of char and tallow and wet stone. Lior pulled their hood up and walked with the crowd, eleven marks heavy in one boot, lighter than eleven marks should weigh.

They hated this part. Not the singing—they loved the singing, loved it with a ferocity that frightened them sometimes, loved the moment when the room tilted and forty strangers became one breathing animal. They hated the afterward. Walking away from people who now carried a piece of them inside their heads, warm and permanent, while Lior carried nothing of anyone at all. A voice with no choir. That was what Ondrey called it, the first year, not cruelly—Ondrey was never cruel—but as a diagnosis. *You give and give, boy, and nothing comes back. That's not a voice, that's a wound that sings.*

A choirless singer. Even the guild's word for what Lior was sounded like a debt unpaid.

The Vellum Guild's counting house sat behind the ash market, up three crooked streets, identifiable by nothing except that its windows were always shuttered and its door was always answered on the second knock. Lior knocked twice. The slot slid open, showed one eye, and closed. Bolts turned in a sequence Lior had never bothered to memorize because Ondrey changed it monthly, paranoid as a cat in a room full of rocking chairs.

Inside smelled of vellum, ink, and cold tea. Ondrey was waiting in the counting room, which surprised Lior—he usually made them wait, a small tyranny he probably believed was discipline. He was a narrow man, gray as his own ledger paper, with ink-stained fingers and eyes that weighed everything they landed on. Tonight those eyes were doing arithmetic Lior didn't like the look of.

"You're late," he said.

"The widow wanted a second verse."

"Maren Cooper." Ondrey said the name like he was filing it. "Husband died of the lung-rot. Bad business. Half the chandlers' guild will be hollowed within five years; you may get to sing for all of them." He gestured at a chair. Lior didn't take it. When Ondrey offered chairs, it meant the conversation would be long, and long conversations with Ondrey always ended with Lior owing something.

"I'm tired, guildmaster. Pay me my balance and I'll—"

"There's a commission."

Something in his tone stopped Lior's hand halfway to their coin purse. Ondrey picked up a lamp and carried it to the far end of the counting room, to the iron-banded chest he kept beneath the window, and Lior felt their stomach drop, because Ondrey only opened the chest for two kinds of business: commissions worth a fortune, and commissions worth a grave.

"Sit down, Lior."

Lior sat.

Ondrey unlocked the chest and drew out a single sheet—not vellum, actual paper, which in the guild's economy was either very cheap or very dangerous—and laid it flat, weighting the corners with coins. Lior leaned forward. The page bore a seal Lior didn't recognize: a tower with a keyhole where the door should be.

"Palace work?" Lior said. "We don't do palace work. You've said so yourself a hundred times. Palace work is how guilds die."

"Palace work is how guilds get rich and then die, yes." Ondrey lowered himself into the chair opposite, and for the first time since Lior had known him, the guildmaster looked old. Not gray—old, the way stone looks old, like the aging had been happening somewhere deep and had finally reached the surface all at once. "This client isn't the crown. This client is *inside* the crown, and they're paying in advance, and the sum is—" he named the figure.

Lior's breath actually caught. The figure was obscene. It was a decade of funerals. It was a house with a hearth and a door that locked from the inside and no more second knocks, no more shuttered windows, no more being a wound that sang.

"What's the job?"

"A door," Ondrey said. "In the palace vaults, below the treasury, there is a door that does not open. Locks don't answer it. Keys don't fit it. The King's engineers have tried sappers, fire, prayer. Nothing. But our client believes—has reason to believe—that the door isn't locked at all. It's *closed*. And there is exactly one kind of person alive who can sing a closed door open."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from under the shutters. Lior found they had gone very still, the way animals go still.

"Sing it open to what?"

"That," said Ondrey, "is the part you're not paid to ask."

Everything in Lior said stand up, say no, walk out into the ash-smell and the dark and keep singing lies to widows until the lung-rot or the poorhouse got them, honest work for a dishonest gift. Everything in Lior said that. And underneath it, quiet as a coal under ash, something else said: *a door that opens for your voice alone. Out of all the singers in all the world, yours.*

"Doorsong," Lior said slowly. "You think I have doorsong."

"I think," Ondrey said, "that when you were sixteen you sang a dead drunk out of a locked cellar because you were too squeamish to leave him there, and you told me the door was already open, and I let you believe I believed you. I think that last winter, in the Hollows, you sang for the flood refugees and three of them swore they'd walked home by streets that were torn up years ago. I think you have doorsong and you've been hiding it the way a miser hides coin, and I have let you, because hidden things are safe things." He folded his ink-stained hands. "But safe things starve, Lior. And so do safe people."

Lior looked at the sealed paper. At the tower with the keyhole where the door should be. Their heart was doing something complicated and embarrassing, some rhythm between terror and want, and they recognized the want with a flush of shame: it was the same want they felt every time they finished a funeral and watched the mourners hold each other. Belonging. Purpose. To matter to something larger than one grieving parlor at a time.

To have, just once, a door open *toward* them instead of away.

"If I do this," Lior heard themselves say, "I want the whole fee tonight. Before. Not after."

"Done."

"And if it goes wrong—if whatever's behind that door goes wrong—"

"Then you'll be rich and gone before it matters," Ondrey said, "which is the only promise this trade has ever kept for anyone."

He slid the sealed paper across the table. Lior looked at it for a long moment. Outside, the ash-sellers' bells rang the closing hour, and somewhere below the city, below the treasury, below the reach of any bell, a door that did not open waited for a voice with no choir.

Lior took the paper.

It was heavier than paper should have been. Later—much later, in the dark, on the run, with blood on their collar and the country unmaking itself around them—Lior would remember that weight, and understand that some doors begin opening the moment you agree to walk toward them.

But that night they only pocketed the seal, counted their fortune in gold into their coat lining, and stepped out into the ash-fall feeling, for the first time in their life, like a singer who might soon have something worth singing to.

Behind them, in the shuttered counting house, Ondrey fed half the guild's ledgers into the stove and watched them burn, and did not look up once.
