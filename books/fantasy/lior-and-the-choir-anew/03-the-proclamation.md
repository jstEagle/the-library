## 3. The Proclamation

The criers came at dawn, as criers always did, because the crown believed that dread was best administered on an empty stomach.

Cyrus Amberdale heard the first of them from the counting house window, where they had been standing since the fourth bell pretending to review a column of figures that had not changed in three days. The man stood in the square below with a scroll and a voice like a bell cracked down the middle, and he read the proclamation in the flat cadence of all official grief: *By order of His Majesty, let it be known that certain persons gifted with the unlawful taking of memory are declared enemies of the crown. Let them be found. Let them be tuned.*

*Tuned.* The word had been drifting through the city for weeks like smoke under a door, but hearing it spoken aloud in the king's own phrasing made it real in a way rumor never could. Cyrus watched the faces in the square below—the baker's boy, the fishwives, two off-duty soldiers leaning against the fountain—and saw the word land on each of them differently. Most of them simply blinked and went back to their mornings. That was the horror of it, Cyrus thought. Not that people cheered. That they didn't need to. A Hunt did not require enthusiasm. It required only paperwork, patience, and the ordinary human appetite for looking away.

The crier moved on to the next square. Somewhere behind Cyrus, in the depths of the counting house, someone dropped a ledger.

* * *

By midmorning the Vellum Guild's counting house had the atmosphere of a sickroom. No one said the word *Hunt* aloud. They said *the situation*, or *the weather*, or nothing at all. The weighing room, where memories were decanted from their vials onto the brass scales and priced by weight and vividness, stood empty for the first time in Cyrus's eleven years there. The scales themselves seemed embarrassed to be idle, their pans hanging still as held breath.

Cyrus worked at their desk in the corner, the one with the good light, doing what they had always done: keeping two sets of books. The false ledger lay open on top—neat, honest-looking, every transaction accounted for, every memory sold and delivered and taxed. Beneath it, locked in the drawer with a key worn smooth as a river stone, sat the true one. The true ledger recorded everything the Guild actually kept. And beneath even that, folded into the binding where no clerk would think to look, lived the third thing, the private thing: the column of names Cyrus had never shown anyone, the debts of conscience they carried against themselves.

A knock. Ondrey stood in the doorway, and Cyrus knew before he spoke, because guildmasters who have run a business in stolen souls for forty years develop a smell for catastrophe, and Ondrey smelled of lamp oil.

"Close the books," he said. "All of them."

"The morning entries aren't—"

"Cyrus." He said their name the way you set down something heavy. "Close them."

He led them down into the cellar, past the wine racks that were not wine racks, past the vault door with its three locks, into the archive where the Guild's true history slept in rows: two hundred years of ledgers, every theft, every sale, every client who had paid to be unburdened of a face or a failure or a love. Ondrey lit no lamp. In the grey light from the grate he began pulling volumes from the shelves—not at random; Cyrus realized within minutes that his selections were precise—and carrying them to the iron brazier in the corner, which was already hot.

"You're burning evidence," Cyrus said.

"I'm burning half our history," Ondrey corrected. "There's a difference. Evidence is what they can hang us with. History is what they can use to find the rest." He fed the first ledger to the flames and watched the cover curl. "Names of clients. Names of sources. The routes we used to move goods north. If the Tuner's people get their hands on these, they won't just empty the Guild. They'll empty everyone we've ever touched."

Cyrus watched a decade of careful bookkeeping go up in orange light. "And the other half?"

Ondrey turned. In the firelight his face looked older than it had yesterday, which Cyrus would not have thought possible. "The other half leaves this building tonight," he said, "in your coat."

* * *

They argued about it for three hours, which for Ondrey and Cyrus constituted a warm and affectionate relationship.

"It has to be someone unremarkable," Ondrey said. "Someone whose face doesn't open doors. Someone who can walk past a checkpoint looking bored."

"I'm your chief accountant. I'm the most remarkable person in this building."

"You're the least remarkable person in any room you've ever entered, and it's the finest gift you have. Besides—" and here he did the thing Cyrus hated, the thing where he stopped arguing and started simply stating facts in a gentle voice, "—you're the only one left I trust."

"Left." Cyrus caught it the way you catch a falling glass, too late to pretend it hadn't happened. "Who?"

Ondrey didn't answer, which was an answer. He crossed to the shelf and drew out a slim volume wrapped in oilcloth, and Cyrus recognized the binding before he'd finished unwrapping it: dark leather, triple-stitched spine, the Guild's mark stamped in blind—that is, unstamped, invisible unless you knew to feel for it. The true ledger. The whole of it. Every kept memory, every hidden account, every name.

"Merric didn't come home last night," Ondrey said quietly. "Neither did Sefa. Or the Corvane twins. Four houses, four empty beds, and in each one, the sheets were still warm and the windows were still latched." He set the ledger on the table between them. "They're not arresting people, Cyrus. Arrests make noise. Whatever comes through those windows leaves no noise at all."

Cyrus thought of Merric's laugh, of Sefa's habit of humming while she weighed, of the twins' uncanny habit of finishing each other's sentences—which, given their trade, had always been less charming than it sounded. Four absences with warm sheets. The dread arrived not as a shout but as a form filled out in triplicate, filed, stamped, and shelved. That was how the crown killed things: bureaucratically, in advance, so that by the time the knife fell everyone had already signed the receipt.

"Why keep the ledger at all?" Cyrus asked. "Burn it with the rest. Start clean."

"Because a ledger is a list of debts," Ondrey said, "and debts are promises. One day—maybe not this year, maybe not in our lifetimes—someone will want to give back what we took. When that day comes, they'll need to know who owes whom." He pushed the volume across the table. "You taught me that, if you recall. Your own words, year before last, over the Hallowmas accounts: *a ledger isn't a record of what we've done, it's a record of what we haven't undone.*"

Cyrus had no memory of saying it, which meant either that Ondrey remembered better than they did, or that Cyrus had taken the memory from themselves at some point and filed it somewhere even they couldn't reach. Both possibilities were equally uncomfortable. They took the ledger.

"There's a woman in Marrowgate," Ondrey said, as Cyrus wrapped the oilcloth and stowed it inside their coat, against the ribs, where a heart would be in a more honest body. "Name of Pell. She keeps a safehouse for people like us. Tell her the ash count from autumn. She'll know."

"And you?"

"I'm going to sit in my office," Ondrey said, "and do sums, until someone comes through my window. It's what I'm best at." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Cyrus had ever seen on him, because it reached his eyes. "Go out the coal chute. Don't take the north road. And Cyrus—if anyone asks, you were never a member of anything. You're a bookkeeper. You've always been a bookkeeper. Bookkeepers survive wars; it's the only thing they're reliably good at."

At the foot of the stairs Cyrus stopped. "Ondrey. The client. Yesterday. The woman with the son."

"What of her?"

"Did she ever come back for her refund?"

Ondrey looked at them for a long moment. "No," he said. "And you should hope she never does. Hope she forgets entirely where she's been." He paused. "These days, forgetting is the safest trade there is. We're the last ones still keeping accounts."

Cyrus climbed toward the coal chute and the grey morning beyond it, a stranger's childhood pressed warm against their ribs, listening to the criers begin again in the next square over, reading the same words to a new crowd of people learning, politely, to look away.
