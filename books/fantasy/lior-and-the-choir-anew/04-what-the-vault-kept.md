## 4. What the Vault Kept

The palace vaults were colder than any grave Lior had ever sung in.

That was the first thing they noticed, standing in the service corridor beneath the east wing with a stolen livery coat buttoned to their throat and Ondrey's instructions folded into memory like a knife folded into cloth: *down past the wine cellar, past the cistern, third door on the left, and don't sing until you're standing in front of it.* The cold here wasn't the honest cold of stone and winter. It was a held breath. A cold that had been waiting.

Lior pressed a palm against the wall and felt it hum.

Not loudly. Not the way a bell hums after being struck. This was deeper, older—the hum of something that had been singing so long it had forgotten how to stop, the way a river forgets it is water. Lior's fingers went numb against the stone, then warm, then strange, as though the wall were remembering them back.

*Third door on the left.*

It was not a door. That was the second thing they noticed. It was an absence shaped like a door—a rectangle of darker dark set into the granite, iron hinges rusted to lace, no handle, no keyhole, no seam where wood might meet frame. Just a suggestion of a door, the way a scar is a suggestion of a wound.

Ondrey had paid them half up front, which meant Ondrey was afraid, which meant the job was worse than he'd said. He'd said: *a strongbox, old, from before the Union. The crown wants it opened quietly. Your voice opens what keys can't.* He'd said it in his counting voice, the flat one he used when the numbers didn't add up to anything he wanted spoken aloud.

Lior had taken the coin anyway. They always took the coin. A singer without a choir took what work came, and this work at least was *theirs*—no one else could do it, no one else could stand in front of a dead door and make it remember being alive.

They cleared their throat. The sound was swallowed whole by the corridor, as if the palace itself disapproved of noise.

"Right," Lior whispered, to no one, because whispering to no one was a habit of people who worked alone. "One song. In and out. No looking around."

They sang.

Not loud. Doorsong never needed to be loud—it needed to be *true*, and truth carried better than volume. Lior chose the oldest song they knew, a cradle-thing their mother had sung in a dialect the maps had stopped listing, three notes rising like a question and falling like an answer. They put their longing into it, all of it—every funeral sung for strangers' griefs, every night in rented rooms, every choir audition where the masters had gone quiet and then polite, which was worse. They put into the song the specific ache of having a gift that made doors out of nothing and no place that wanted one opened.

The door remembered itself.

Lior heard it happen before they saw it—a sound like a held note finally released, decades of silence exhaling—and the absence filled in. Wood grain bloomed across the dark like frost forming in reverse. The hinges groaned, shedding rust in red snow. And the door swung inward, slow as a coffin lid, breathing out air that smelled of—

Lior stopped breathing.

Hay. Rain on warm earth. Woodsmoke and bread and cut grass and river water, all of it layered, all of it *alive*, the smell of a summer morning in a country that had been dead for sixty years.

They should have run then. Later, bleeding through the ash market with the Tuner's hounds singing behind them, Lior would have time to catalog every moment they should have run. But in that corridor, with the door open and the smell of home-that-was-never-theirs pouring over them like light, they did the only thing a singer could do.

They stepped inside.

* * *

The vault was not a vault.

It was a valley.

Lior stood on a hillside under a sky that had no right to exist underground, blue and enormous, streaked with cloud. Below them, terraced fields climbed a river valley, gold with late wheat. A village sat in the crook of the river—thatched roofs, whitewashed walls, a church tower with a copper spire gone green. Laundry hung between windows. Smoke rose from chimneys. Somewhere down there, a dog barked, unhurried, certain of its world.

And underneath everything, faint as a pulse heard through a wall, the country was singing.

Lior knew that song. Everyone in the lowlands knew it, or had known it once, before the Union, before the new king's men went parish to parish collecting hymnals and leaving receipts. It was the harvest round, the one grandmothers still hummed in defiance or forgetting, four voices braided into one line that never ended because each voice rested while the others carried it. The valley was singing it to itself. The wheat was singing it. The river was keeping time.

"Oh," Lior said, and the word fell away from them and rolled down the hillside like a dropped coin.

They walked. They couldn't not walk. Their feet found the path as if the path had been waiting for them, and the grass whispered against their boots, real grass, dew-wet, and overhead a bird crossed the sky trailing its shadow across Lior's face. Underground. Under the palace. Under the throne of a king who had spent twenty years erasing everything this valley remembered.

A slice of the old kingdom. Preserved. Kept like a pressed flower in the pages of a book no one was allowed to open.

Lior understood then, with a clarity that felt like falling, why the crown had wanted the door opened *quietly*. Not for the treasure behind it. Because the treasure *was* it. Because somewhere in the palace archives there was a map with a room marked on it, and a king who unmade countries could not sleep knowing one piece of the old one still breathed beneath his floorboards.

They reached the village edge. The first house stood with its door open, and inside, an old woman sat at a table setting two places for a meal, moving with the slow patience of someone who has done this ten thousand times. She did not look up. Her lips moved with the song. On the table, the bread was whole and unbitten and would be whole and unbitten forever, Lior understood suddenly, because nothing in here *happened*. Nothing changed. The valley was a memory so perfectly kept that it had forgotten it was a memory.

The woman's hands paused over the second plate.

She turned her head—not toward Lior, exactly, but toward the *sound* of them, the small discord a living person makes in a room full of preserved ones. Her eyes were pale as riverwater and utterly empty, and yet when she looked at Lior, her mouth curved into something that was almost recognition.

"You're new," she said. "You're the first new thing in such a long time."

"What is this place?" Lior's voice cracked on the last word. "Who are you?"

"I'm the keeper." She resumed setting the table, plate, cup, spoon, each item placed with liturgical care. "Or I'm the kept. It's hard to remember which, after so long. The king's grandfather built this room, you know. Before the Unmaking. He couldn't bear to lose it all, so he kept one valley. One morning. He used to come down and walk the fields." Her smile deepened, sadder. "He stopped coming when his son took the throne. The son wanted to forget."

"The Hunt—" Lior started. "The proclamation, the tuning, all of it—is it because of this? Because he's afraid of—"

The woman raised one finger, and the valley's song faltered.

For the first time, Lior heard what was underneath the harvest round. A second voice, buried deep, so far below the surface of things that it barely qualified as sound. It was vast and slow and grieving, and it was coming from everywhere—from the soil, the sky, the wheat, the church tower—and it was saying, in the wordless way that places say things:

*I am still here. I am still here. I am still here.*

"They'll come for it eventually," the keeper said softly. "Kings always do. That's why the door only opens for a singer. Only a singer could hear it calling." She looked at Lior fully now, and her empty eyes were suddenly not empty at all but terribly, unbearably full. "It's been calling for sixty years, child. You're the first one who answered."

Lior's chest hurt. "I can't—I'm just here for a job, I didn't know, I—"

"No one ever knows." The keeper folded her hands. "The door will close soon. It closes when it's done being open. You should decide what you're going to carry out of here, because you can't carry nothing, and you can't carry everything."

"Carry? I'm not taking anything—"

"You already have." She nodded at Lior's chest, and Lior realized the ache there had a shape now, a weight, like a stone swallowed whole. "The song. It gave you the song. That's what happens when a singer answers a country's call. It gives you its voice to keep, so somewhere, outside, the singing continues." She smiled again, and this time it broke Lior's heart clean in two. "Congratulations. You have a choir now. There are only two of you, and one of you is underground, but it counts. Oh, it counts."

Somewhere above them—far above, muffled through sixty feet of granite and the whole sleeping mass of the palace—came a sound Lior knew from funerals and bad dreams.

Boots. Many boots. And beneath them, thin and wrong, a whine of metal finding a pitch.

Tuning forks.

* * *

They ran.

Up the hillside, through the wheat that bent around them whispering *stay, stay*, past the church where the copper spire caught a sun that existed nowhere but here. Behind them the valley's song rose, urgent now, the harvest round breaking apart into its separate voices as if the country itself had understood, at last, that its visitor was leaving—or being taken.

The door stood at the top of the hill where no door had been, hanging open onto darkness, and beyond it, growing louder, the boots and the forks and a voice giving orders in the clipped cadence of the palace guard.

Lior skidded to a stop at the threshold.

Behind them: a whole country, singing, alive, doomed. Ahead: a corridor, a hunt, a crown that emptied people for the crime of remembering. The keeper's words followed them up the hill like a hand on the shoulder: *you can't carry nothing, and you can't carry everything.*

The song in Lior's chest pulsed, heavy and warm, a second heartbeat.

"Come with me," Lior said aloud, to the valley, to the wheat, to the keeper standing small and patient at her table in a house that would never change. "I can sing it wider. I can find a way to—"

The keeper's voice reached them across the distance, gentle and final: "Doors only go one way for the dead, child. And we've been dead a very long time. Go. Be our voice. That's all anyone alive can be for us now."

The boots rounded the corner of the corridor outside. Torchlight spilled through the doorway, and shadows with it—four guards, five, and behind them a figure in grey whose silhouette made the torch flames lean away, the way flowers lean from frost.

The King's Tuner. Here. Already.

Lior did the thing they had sworn never to do, the thing that had gotten every doorsinger before them killed or hollowed: they improvised.

No cradle-song. No learned verse. They reached into their chest, into the new weight the valley had given them, and pulled the harvest round out through their own throat—two voices braided into one, theirs and the dead country's, sung at the threshold of the door between them. The notes tore going out. Every doorsong cost; Lior had always known that the way you know fire is hot, abstractly, from other people's burns. But this was different. This was *expensive*. They felt something leave them with the high third phrase—something with a name attached, a face attached, a whole afternoon attached, gone as cleanly as if it had never been—and they kept singing anyway, because the alternative was the Tuner's forks.

The door began to swing shut.

The last thing Lior saw through the narrowing gap was the keeper, standing at her table, raising one hand in farewell—or blessing, or warning, the three being indistinguishable at that distance—and the wheat bowing in a wind that had no business existing underground, and the whole valley lifting its song one final octave, joyous and furious, a country refusing to whisper.

Then the door was a door, then a seam, then a scar in the stone, and Lior was alone in a black corridor with five guardsmen's torches swinging toward them and a hole in their memory where a whole day used to be.

There was no way out. The corridor ended in granite. The guards were closing ranks behind them with the bored efficiency of men who had done this many times, and the grey figure was still too far away to see clearly and too close to ever forget.

So Lior listened.

That was the secret no one had ever taught them, the thing they'd only begun to suspect in the vault, in the valley, in the presence of a country that sang itself into continuing: every wall was a door that hadn't been asked properly. Every place remembered being something else. The granite around them had been, forty years ago, wet clay in a mason's yard; it remembered the yard, the rain, the apprentice who'd carved his initials in the mold.

Lior put their palm flat against the stone and sang it back to what it was.

The wall softened. Opened. Became, for the length of one held breath, a doorway into a memory of mud and rain and a boy laughing at his own carved name—and Lior stepped through it, into the past of a wall, out the other side into a coal chute, down the chute, into darkness, running.

Behind them, muffled through solid rock, they heard the Tuner's voice for the first time. It was not angry. That was the worst part. It was soft, and patient, and almost tender, the way a hand is tender when it closes over a bird.

"Find the singer," it said. "They've heard it now. They'll run straight for the rest of it."

Lior ran. Through the dark, through the palace's buried veins, with a stolen country singing in their chest and a day missing from their life and the Tuner's words chasing them like hounds:

*Straight for the rest of it.*

They burst up through a grate into the ash market at dusk, into the smoke and the crowd and the beginning of the rest of their life—and the first thing they saw, cutting through the market stalls with ledgers under one arm and a face like a closed door, was a stranger who looked at Lior and went pale with recognition of something Lior couldn't possibly have explained.

The stranger's eyes dropped to Lior's bloodied hands.

And the hounds, somewhere behind, began to sing.
