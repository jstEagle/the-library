## 5. Runners in the Ash Market

The ash market opened at dusk, because ash was cheaper to sell after dark and the buyers of it were the kind of people who preferred not to be seen buying anything.

Cyrus Amberdale moved through it with their hood up and their ledger flat against their ribs, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a coat that had seen better decades. The market sprawled through the old tannery district like something spilled: stalls of gray powder sold by weight, hearth-sweepers' sacks split open on trestle tables, children sifting the ground for cinders worth a halfpenny. Above it all hung the smell—char and lye and wet stone—which was precisely why Cyrus came here. No one's nose worked in the ash market. Not even theirs.

And their nose, though they would never have called it that in company, was the finest instrument the Vellum Guild owned.

Gift had a scent. Ondrey said it was nonsense, sentiment, a trick of the mind—but Ondrey also kept a pressed flower from a funeral forty years gone, so his opinions on sentiment could be weighed and found wanting. Cyrus knew what they knew: memory left a trace on the air when it moved hands uninvited. Taken memories smelled of rain on hot iron. Given ones smelled of bread. And memories torn out fast, ripped loose by someone frightened or desperate or both, smelled of copper and cut stems—the green wound-smell of a garden bed the morning after frost.

Tonight, under the char and the lye, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, Cyrus smelled copper and cut stems.

They stopped mid-stride. A woman jostled past with a sack of sweepings, cursing them for a drunk. Cyrus did not move. They turned their face slowly, the way one turns toward a draft in a sealed room, and let the market's noise fall away until there was only the scent-thread, thin and bright, running between the stalls toward the tannery's ruined drying sheds at the district's edge.

Someone nearby had taken a memory tonight. Recently. Badly.

Every instinct Cyrus owned said walk away. The Hunt had been proclaimed nine days ago; criers still worked the corners with their painted staves, and the word *tuned* had entered the language of the streets the way rot enters a wall—quietly, then all at once. Guild members were vanishing. Master Ferren had not come to the counting house in four days, and no one asked where he'd gone, because asking was how you got followed. A smart thief, a surviving thief, walked away from the smell of torn memory in an ash market at dusk.

Cyrus walked toward it.

Later—they would have years, or would not, to examine this—Cyrus could never say why. Curiosity, perhaps. Or the ledger against their ribs, and the column of names only they read, and the thought that somewhere in this city another person might be carrying a secret the way Cyrus carried theirs: folded small, hidden deep, costing more every day to keep.

* * *

The drying sheds were skeletons of timber and stretched canvas, long abandoned since the tannery failed, now rented by no one and used by everyone. Cyrus slipped between two leaning walls and found the gap where the scent pooled thickest, and in the dark beyond it, curled behind a collapsed rack of stretching frames, they found the source.

A young man—or young enough, hard to tell in the shadow—sitting with his back against the wood and one hand pressed to his side. Blood between his fingers. Not gushing; seeping. He'd been hurt some hours ago and had run anyway, which told Cyrus more than any interrogation could. His other hand lay open on his knee, and around its wrist, faint in the gloom, glimmered the residue that Cyrus had seen only twice before in their life: the silver-gray shimmer of a gift spent hard and recently. Doors, it looked like. The shimmer sat on him like the smell of smoke sits on a chimney sweep—in the skin, inescapable.

He wasn't a guild member. Cyrus knew every face on the rolls. This was something else.

"You're bleeding on valuable real estate," Cyrus said quietly.

The man's head came up fast—too fast; he swayed—and Cyrus saw his eyes find them in the dark and go wide with a fear that had nothing to do with the knife he didn't have. "I'm not—" His voice cracked, and he steadied it with visible effort. "Whatever you've heard, I haven't done anything."

"Everyone in this city has done something. Most of them just haven't been caught." Cyrus crouched, slow, hands visible, the way you approach a wounded animal or a client deciding whether to weep. Up close the blood was worse than it had looked, and the shimmer at his wrist was brighter, and underneath the copper-and-cut-stems smell there was something else now, something that made the hair rise on Cyrus's arms: a resonance, like a struck bell still humming below hearing. "You went through a door tonight. Not a door with hinges."

The man stared at them. "You can smell that?"

"I can smell everything. It's my affliction." Cyrus pulled the oilcloth bundle partway from their coat—not far enough to show what it was—and let him see the care with which it was handled. Thieves trusted thieves with evidence of caution. "Here is what happens next. In about twenty minutes, give or take the length of the ash market, men are going to come through those sheds with dogs. Not normal dogs. Tuned hounds—bred on the same principle I just used to find you, except they were bred to it instead of learning it the hard way. They will find you exactly the way I did, except they won't crouch down and talk. Do you understand me?"

"They're looking for me." Not a question. His hand tightened on his wound. "The palace job. Someone talked."

"The palace." Cyrus closed their eyes briefly. Of course. Of course it was the vaults; the whole city had whispered about the night the palace bells rang wrong, three nights ago, and the guards who swore a singer had stepped out of a wall. "You're the singer."

"And you're dead if you stay," the singer said, with a flash of something under the exhaustion—pride, maybe, or just nerve. "If they're tracking me, anyone near me is—"

"Yes, thank you, I've read the proclamations." Cyrus glanced over their shoulder at the gap in the walls. The market noise was changing. Not louder—*organized*. Boots finding rhythm. The particular silence of a crowd making way for authority. "Listen carefully, because I'll only offer this once, and then I'll leave and you can bleed alone with your pride. You need stitching, a place the hounds can't parse, and someone who knows how hunted people move. I need—" What did they need? Cyrus hadn't decided yet, and lied fluently even to themselves. "I need to not be standing next to you when the dogs arrive. So we're going to trade silences. You never saw me. I never saw you. We were never here together. And separately, quietly, right now, we both leave."

"That's not a bargain. That's two people walking away."

"It's the only kind of bargain worth keeping." Cyrus stood. "Well? Can you walk?"

He tried. He got one foot under him and the shed tilted, and Cyrus caught his arm before the falling became loud, and the moment their bare fingers closed on his wrist—on the shimmer—Cyrus felt it.

Not a taking. They were careful; they were always careful; they took nothing without consent and nothing at all tonight. But touch ran both ways along gift, and for half a heartbeat Cyrus felt the shape of him: a voice like a corridor, vast and hollowed and echoing, doors standing open down its whole length, and at the center of it a single note held so long and so lonely that Cyrus's throat ached in sympathy.

Lior. The name arrived in their head unbidden, riding the resonance, and Cyrus let go as if burned.

"What did you—" Lior breathed.

"Nothing. I took nothing. That's rather the point of me." Cyrus flexed their fingers, heart hammering. In nine years of careful theft and carefuler silence, they had never once felt another gifted soul from the inside. "My name is Cyrus. Don't repeat it. Now walk, and lean on me, and if you sing us out of this I will be extremely annoyed, because I know what it costs you."

* * *

They went out through the tannery's bone-yard, where old bleaching pits had become gardens of gray weeds, and Cyrus chose paths the way water chooses—a slope here, a shadow there—never straight, never hurried, always boring. Boring was survival. Lior learned fast, which surprised Cyrus less than it should have; singers memorized, and a man who could hold a door open with a held note could certainly hold a route in his head.

The hounds announced themselves by absence. One moment the market's murmur washed against the sheds behind them; the next, a silence spread through the ash district like ink through water, stall by stall, voice by voice, the sound of a hundred people deciding not to be interesting.

Then the baying started, and it was wrong—too harmonic, too many throats in unison, a chord with teeth.

"In here." Cyrus hauled Lior into the lee of a fallen lime kiln, into a pocket of dark that smelled of old fire, and pulled a twist of ash-sweepings from their coat pocket—cheap trick, bought from a sweeper for a copper—and crushed it over them both. Char dust bloomed, gray and choking, coating their clothes, their hair, the blood on Lior's side. Under the ash, gift-scent died. Cyrus had hidden from their own guildmaster with this trick for six years.

The hounds came through the bone-yard thirty feet away.

Cyrus had imagined them, in the bad hours, and the imagination had been kinder. They were dogs the way a chair is a tree—something had been done to them, something that had emptied them and filled the emptiness with a single purpose, and they moved in formation like notes on a staff. Their handler walked behind them: a gray woman in crown livery with a case of instruments slung at her hip, and she turned her head as she passed—turned it *toward* the kiln, toward the dark, toward the two fugitives close enough to touch if either had dared reach—and for three seconds, three eternities, her eyes rested on the shadows where they crouched.

She was smelling for them. Cyrus could feel it, the delicate pull of it, a hand passing over the surface of their mind like a palm over still water. They did the thing they had practiced ten thousand times in front of cracked mirrors: thought of nothing. Thought of ledgers. Columns. Sums. The dullest arithmetic in the world, recited inward like a rosary. Beside them, Lior trembled—not with fear, Cyrus realized, but with effort, holding some inner silence the way he held notes.

The gray woman's gaze slid on. The chord of hounds bayed once, frustrated, and swung away toward the river, and the boots followed, and the silence unclenched, and the ash market exhaled around them like a living thing.

Neither of them moved for a long count of fifty.

"He almost had us," Lior whispered.

"She did have us. She chose not to see." Cyrus didn't know why they said it—only that it was true, some instinct older than sense. Something in the handler's pause had been hesitation, a fractional softness, a grief worn smooth by use. "Which means she has a reason. Which means she's a person. Which means, eventually, she'll be a problem. Come on. We're not safe, we're merely unfound, and those are different countries."

* * *

The safehouse was a cooper's loft above a shuttered workshop, reached by a ladder through a trapdoor, paid for in guild scrip under a name that had never existed. Cyrus lit one candle, no more, and set Lior on a barrel while they fetched needle, thread, and a bottle of plum brandy that had survived three generations of guildmasters by being terrible.

"Shirt," Cyrus said, and then, seeing Lior's face, added, "I stitched my brother's leg shut in a ditch when I was fourteen. Whatever you're hiding under there, I promise I've seen worse and charged money for it."

Lior laughed—one short, startled note—and then winced his way out of the coat and shirt, and there was the wound: a slash across the ribs, guard-made, and around it the skin marked with the same silver shimmer, fading now, spending itself. Cyrus cleaned it, stitched it, and worked in silence while Lior drank the brandy and made a face that confirmed its reputation.

"Why did you come back for me?" Lior asked at last. "In the sheds. You said walk away. You walked toward me."

Cyrus tied off the last stitch and sat back on their heels, and considered lying, and found—to their mild alarm—that they didn't want to. "Because I smelled what they tore out of you," they said. "When you escaped. You didn't just spend your gift going through that door. You lost something getting out—I could smell the wound of it. Copper and cut stems." They looked up. "I keep a list, Lior. Of everyone whose memories pass through my hands. It's a long list, and most of it is ugly, and tonight I found someone new on it who isn't mine, isn't theirs, and doesn't have anyone else keeping count. Somebody should keep count. That's all. That's the whole grand reason. Somebody should keep count."

Lior looked at them for a long time across the candle flame. The shimmer at his wrist had gone out entirely now, and in its place was just a wrist, just a man, just a stranger in a strange loft with a stitched side and a stolen country behind his eyes.

"Cyrus Amberdale," he said quietly.

"I said don't repeat—"

"I know what you are." Lior set down the bottle. His voice didn't shake. "The memory-taker. The Vellum Guild's quiet hand. My teacher told me about you—years ago, when I still had a choir. She said if I ever met you, I should run." He smiled, tired and crooked and utterly without fear. "But she also said you were the only one in the city who ever gave anything back."

Outside, far off toward the river, the hounds bayed again—a single harmonic note, patient, circling. The candle guttered. And Cyrus Amberdale, keeper of ledgers, thief of memories, liar to kings and guildmasters alike, heard themselves say the second truest thing of their life:

"We leave before dawn. There's a road north, and a border on it that isn't on any map, and if the stories are true, it's the only direction left that isn't behind us." They stood, and blew out the candle, and in the dark their voice was steady and their hands were not. "Sleep. Tomorrow we learn whether a thief and a singer can outrun a kingdom."
