## 6. The Tuner

The room beneath the Ministry of Harmonics had no windows, which Serah Vane considered a kindness. Windows invited the sky in, and the sky invited memory, and memory was the one thing she had been professionally trained to remove from other people and personally forbidden to keep.

She sat across the table from the prisoner and waited.

Waiting was most of the work. People thought tuning was violence—thought it was like cutting, like burning, some brutal surgery of the self. It wasn't. Tuning was listening. You listened to a person until you found the note they were built around, the one tone that held all their lesser tones together, and then you sounded it until everything else fell away. A house could stand with its walls cracked, its roof gone, its floors rotted through. Pull out the keystone of its song, though, and the whole structure forgot how to be a house at all.

The prisoner was a guild man named Pellam Wick, sixty-odd, soft-handed, a weigher of memories rather than a taker of them. He had been caught with forty-one vials of stolen recollection sewn into his coat lining, each labeled in a clerk's careful hand: *Widow Marsh, age nine, first snowfall. Widow Marsh, age twelve, her father's voice.* The labels were the guild's whole obscenity, Serah thought. Not the theft—the inventory. Men who wrote receipts for souls.

Pellam was sweating. They always sweated at first. The room was not cold, but fear made its own weather.

"You're a bookkeeper," she said. "Not a thief."

"I—I kept accounts, madam. I never took anything off anyone. I only ever handled what others brought in." His hands fluttered on the table like birds deciding whether to fly. "I have a family. A wife. In Lowmarsh. If you could write to them, tell them where—"

"I know where your wife is." Serah unfolded a paper without looking at it. She did not need to look; she had read it on the ride over. "Maren Wick. Two daughters. Your youngest has your eyes, according to the field report, though I note the report also says you haven't been home in eleven months."

His face did something complicated. Grief and guilt braiding together, and under both of them, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, the thing she was looking for: love. Love for the wife, worn smooth by long absence but still load-bearing. That was his keystone. She could hear it already, almost—a low warm tone, domestic, patient.

She made herself listen harder anyway. Method before instinct. Instinct was what happened when you stopped being careful, and stopped being careful was how tuners became monsters, and Serah Vane had sworn an oath about monsters that she intended to keep even if it killed everyone else.

"Tell me about the ledger," she said.

"What ledger?"

"The true one. Ondrey burned half his books two nights ago—we watched the smoke from the tower. But he kept one back. He gave it to someone to carry north." She laid her hands flat on the table, palms down, a gesture she'd learned from her first instructor: *the tuner's hands are always at rest, because the person across from you must believe nothing is about to happen.* "Who carries it?"

"I don't know any ledger. I'm a weigher. I sit in a room and—"

"Pellam." She said his name gently, the way you'd touch a bruise to test it. "You are going to tell me. The only question is whether you tell me now, while you are still someone who can remember telling me, or later, when you are no longer anyone at all. I would prefer now. I suspect you would too."

He wept then, quietly, the way men of accounts do—all leakage and no noise—and told her what he knew, which was little enough. A courier. Young. Gifted. Gone north on the Ash Road three days ahead of the riders. No name given, but Ondrey had called the courier something when he handed over the wrapped ledger, something Pellam hadn't quite heard through the door, except—

"Except?"

"It sounded like a name," Pellam whispered. "But wrong. Like a name said backwards. Or... like half a name. Amber-something. Amberdale? I don't—it might be nothing. I heard so many names in that house."

Serah wrote it down anyway. *Amberdale.* The ink dried gray in the lamplight.

* * *

Afterward, when they had taken Pellam away to holding—not tuned yet; the crown preferred to tune in batches, efficiently, like a harvest—Serah stayed in the windowless room and let herself do the thing she never did in front of witnesses.

She tried to remember why.

It was a strange exercise, and she performed it the way other people performed penance: regularly, privately, and without expectation of relief. She knew the facts. Facts were safe; facts lived in documents, in reports, in the neat columns of her own service record. *Serah Vane, appointed King's Tuner at age twenty-four. Voluntary subject of the Loyalty Clarification, per protocol. All personal memories assessed as potential security liabilities and removed by her own hand.*

By her own hand. That was the part the priests liked, when the priests came to bless the instruments. She had tuned herself. Held the fork against her own skull and rung out every note of her own life until what remained was clean, quiet, devoted. There had been reasons. There was always a form for reasons; she had signed it. The form still existed somewhere in the archive, and she had read it once, early on, hunting for herself the way she hunted for keystones in other people.

The form said: *Subject requests removal of all memories pertaining to a romantic attachment deemed compromising to crown service.*

A romantic attachment. Someone she had loved.

She knew that much. She knew it the way you know a room you've never entered—you know there is a door, and that the door is locked, and that behind it is something that used to be yours. And she knew one more thing, the single splinter the Clarification had missed or perhaps had chosen to leave, a flaw in an otherwise perfect emptiness:

She remembered loving him.

Not him. Loving. The shape of the feeling without its contents. It came to her sometimes at night, unasked, the way a song comes back to you whose words you've lost: a warmth in the chest with no face attached, a tenderness aimed at nothing, at empty air, at a doorway where someone had once stood. She remembered *that she had loved*, wholly and stupidly and young, and the memory had no object, and so it hung in her like a bell with no clapper, ringing and ringing at every passing wind.

And she knew his name. That was the cruelest mercy of it. The Clarification had stripped the face, the voice, the rain, the duet, the promise—all of it, gone—but names were filed differently, names were administrative, and so somewhere in the clean white country of her mind stood a small signpost pointing at nothing:

*Lior.*

* * *

She climbed the stairs to the tower office where the Minister of Harmonics kept his ledgers of a different kind, and found him at his window watching the city smoke.

"Amberdale," she said.

Lord Casserly turned. He was a dry stick of a man with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone who had read too many confiscated books to remain entirely comfortable in his own faith. He was useful. He was also, Serah suspected, afraid of her, which she encouraged.

"The guild clerk?" he said. "We have a file. Minor talent. Memory-taking by touch, unregistered since birth, as far as we can tell. Clever with numbers. Kept the guild's second set of books—the honest ones, ironically." He slid a folder across the desk. "Why?"

"Because Ondrey trusted them with the true ledger. And because—" She paused. She was not a woman who paused. "Because the courier traveling with them may be a singer."

Casserly went very still. In the Ministry, *singer* was not a word you said above a whisper. Singers belonged to the old country, the country that had been folded up and put away like winter clothes, the country the Hunt existed to make sure stayed folded.

"A doorsinger," Serah said. "Untrained, probably. Alone. Which means the Last Door isn't just a rumor anymore. Someone is trying to reach it."

"And you want this assignment."

"I want the singer."

"Why?" Casserly asked, and it was not an idle question. Ministers asked it idly; this one asked it the way a physician asks about blood. "Tuners don't take singers personally, Vane. Singers are assets. We bring them in, we clarify them, we put their voices to work closing what needs closing. There's no need for—enthusiasm."

Enthusiasm. She turned the word over. What she felt when she imagined the singer was not enthusiasm. It was that bell again, swinging in its empty tower, and she could not have said—if pressed, if tortured, if tuned—why the thought of Lior's throat under her tuning fork filled her with something that was not quite dread and not quite grief and tasted, horribly, like homesickness.

"Professional interest," she said. "Singers resist tuning. I've read the old files. Their gifts root deep. I want to be the one who does it right."

"Do it right," Casserly repeated, and something in his face suggested he did not believe her, and something in hers suggested she did not care.

* * *

She rode north two days later with six hounds and a portable instrument case lined in velvet, and on the third night, in a waystation inn smelling of tallow and wet wool, she dreamed.

She did not dream often. Emptied people rarely did; dreams needed material. But that night she dreamed of rain—not falling rain, played rain, rain drummed out on a tabletop by quick clever fingers while someone laughed—and in the dream she was happy in a way that had no features, no face, no name, just happiness itself, raw and enormous, and a voice beside her was singing harmony to a melody she almost knew, and she woke with tears on her face and her hand pressed flat over her heart as if holding a door shut.

She lay in the dark and did the exercise.

*I loved someone. I chose the crown instead. I removed the reason I cried for a week afterward, and then I removed the crying, and then I removed the week.*

That was the whole of it. That was everything she had. A devotion built on a hole, a loyalty with a Lior-shaped absence at its center, and the terrible elegant joke of it—the joke she never laughed at—was that the emptiness itself had become her strength. She served the crown perfectly because there was nothing left in her to serve instead. The Clarification had not broken her. It had hollowed her into an instrument, and instruments do not ask questions.

Except that lately, riding north through a country busy forgetting itself, she had begun to notice the questions anyway. Not asking them. Noticing them. The way you notice a missing tooth with your tongue.

*If I find the singer,* she thought, staring at the ceiling beams, *and I tune him, will the bell stop ringing?*

She genuinely did not know. That was the wound under the wound: part of her wanted to find Lior and silence the last echo of a life she couldn't remember, and part of her—some splinter, some flaw the fork had missed—wanted to find Lior and simply *look*, the way you look at a house you once lived in, and say nothing, and take nothing, and leave the door standing.

She got up before dawn. She sharpened nothing, because tuning forks do not sharpen. She fed the hounds. She checked the road reports: two travelers on the Ash Road matching the courier's description, one slight, one watchful, moving fast and sleeping cold. Guild-trained caution. And the slight one, according to a farmer at a crossroads who liked the sound of his own testimony, had hummed while walking—a little wandering tune, half-familiar, that had made the farmer's wife cry without knowing why.

Serah read that line twice.

Then she closed the folder, and her hands were steady, and her heart was doing something she had no protocol for, and she mounted her horse and turned it north, toward the sound of a song she knew without knowing it.

Behind her, the waystation innkeeper watched the King's Tuner ride off through the frost and noticed—though he would never report it, though he would barely admit it to himself—that for one moment, at the turn of the road, the terrible woman had been humming too.
