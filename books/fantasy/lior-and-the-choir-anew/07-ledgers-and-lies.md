## 7. Ledgers and Lies

The road north was not a road so much as a rumor of one—a cart-track stitched through barley gone to seed, running parallel to the old tollway the way a coward runs beside a battle. Cyrus had chosen it for exactly that reason. The tollway had cairns every mile, and cairns meant soldiers, and soldiers meant questions, and questions were the one currency Cyrus Amberdale could not counterfeit.

"Again," they said.

Lior groaned from the ditch where they'd been standing for the better part of an hour. "It's a ditch, Cyrus. I'm standing in a ditch."

"You're *lying* in a ditch badly. There's a difference, and the difference is whether the Tuner's riders see your head above the barley." Cyrus crouched at the field's edge, arms folded on their knees, and pointed with their chin. "Your hair catches light like a struck bell. Get it down. Get it under. And stop looking at me like I'm the enemy."

"You're enjoying this."

"I'm keeping us alive. Those are different pleasures." They let a beat pass, then softened it—Lior's face in the late light had a rawness to it that made cruelty feel expensive. "Hunted people move a certain way. It's learnable. That's the mercy of it. Nobody is born knowing how to disappear; you practice until disappearing is just walking."

Lior climbed out of the ditch, straw in their collar, and stood brushing themselves off with exaggerated dignity. "Fine. Teach me to walk."

So Cyrus taught them. Three days of it, as the barley gave way to hedgerow country and the hedgerows gave way to stone walls older than any kingdom still printing proclamations. They taught Lior to break their stride at random intervals, because pursuit learned rhythm faster than it learned faces. To sleep in shifts even when there was no one to keep watch against but crows. To buy bread in one village and eat it three miles on, so no baker could later swear to having seen them. To carry coin in three places and never count it in public. To look at nothing twice—not a posted notice, not a rider, not a face—because the eye that lingers is the eye that gets remembered.

"And if someone does remember you?" Lior asked, on the third evening, as they walked the spine of a fallow field with the sun going down behind them like a coin dropped into a well.

"Then you give them something else to remember instead," Cyrus said. "A limp you don't have. A name that isn't yours. Fear is lazy—it wants one detail and it'll take whatever you hand it. Hand it a false one and it stops digging."

"That's horrible."

"That's bookkeeping," said Cyrus, and did not explain further, because explaining would have meant admitting how many entries in their private ledger had been kept safe by exactly such small, horrible kindnesses.

They slept that night in the ruin of a shepherd's bothy, roof half-collapsed, walls thick enough to hold warmth. Lior sang very quietly while Cyrus pretended to inventory their pack—an old song, wordless, the kind that made the broken stones feel briefly like a room where people had been happy. Cyrus found themself listening the way they never listened to anything: entirely. It was dangerous. Listening entirely was how you ended up owing people things.

"Where are we going, exactly?" Lior asked, into the dark. "You say north. Everyone says north. But north of what?"

"North of the maps," Cyrus said. "There's a border up here that cartographers stopped drawing two hundred years ago. Not because it moved. Because the country on the other side of it—" They stopped. Chose the words like coins counted out onto a counter. "Because the crown pretends it isn't there. And what the crown pretends isn't there, the Hunt can't officially find."

"But unofficially?"

"Unofficially, everything gets found eventually. We just have to be faster than official."

In the morning there was smoke.

Not the black, wrong-angled smoke of burning—just hearth-smoke, gray and domestic, rising from a croft set back off the track: low stone, turf roof, a garden going wild with kale and something flowering white. A goat watched them from a tether with the flat judgment of its kind.

"We should go around," Lior said.

"We need food, and we're past the last village." Cyrus was already moving toward the croft, which was itself a violation of everything they'd spent three days teaching. But rules bent for hunger, and besides—the smoke was thin. Thin smoke meant one fire. One fire meant one person. One person was arithmetic Cyrus could do.

The farmer was lying in the garden.

He was an old man, or a middle-aged man aged hard by weather, stretched out among the kale rows with his arm flung over his chest and his breathing doing something terrible—wet, uneven, each breath arriving like a debt collector. His lips were gray. His eyes were open, fixed on the sky, and he turned them slowly toward the sound of footsteps with the unhurried calm of a man who had already settled his accounts.

"Frost took the beans," he said. "Tell my sister. In Harrowmere. She'll want to know about the beans."

Cyrus knelt. Some habits were deeper than caution. They put two fingers to the man's wrist and read what was written there: hours, maybe less. The wet rattle in the chest had the sound of water where water should not be, and no fever to fight it with.

"There's no one inside?" Cyrus asked. "No family?"

"Wife went first. Ten years back." He smiled faintly at the sky. "Beans were hers, really. I just kept the rows straight."

Behind Cyrus, Lior had gone very still in the way that singers went still—listening, Cyrus realized, though there was nothing to hear yet except a dying man's breath.

"We'll send word to Harrowmere," Cyrus said. "I promise you."

"Mm." The eyes closed. Opened again. "Funny thing," the farmer said, conversationally, as if discussing the weather with a neighbor come over the wall. "All day now I've been trying to hold on to her face. My wife's. And it keeps—sliding. Like trying to carry water in a sieve. You'd think a man would get to keep that, at the end. One face. Out of all of them."

And Cyrus felt it happen—the thing they had sworn off, the thing the ledger's secret column recorded in a hand smaller and more careful than any other entry. The gift rose in them like heat rising off a summer road. The farmer's memory hung there, unguarded, a lamp in an open window: a woman in a garden, laughing, dirt on her hands, beans climbing their strings. Slipping. Going gray at the edges even as it flickered.

Cyrus reached out and touched the man's wrist, where the pulse beat thin, and *took*.

It didn't hurt him. That was the lie everyone told about memory-thieves, that it hurt. It was more like catching something falling. The image settled into Cyrus whole and bright—the woman, the garden, the laugh—and in the farmer's eyes something eased. Not peace exactly. The particular blankness of a man who has set down a heavy bundle and can't recall what was in it, only that his arms are lighter.

"There," Cyrus heard themself say softly. "Safe now. Nothing gets lost that I keep."

"What," said Lior, "did you just do."

Cyrus turned. Lior stood at the garden gate, and their face was doing something complicated—horror and recognition fighting for the same ground.

"He was losing her," Cyrus said. "He told me so himself. Another hour and she'd have been gone—slipped out through his fingers and nowhere, no grave, no trace. Now she's somewhere. She's *kept*."

"You took it." Lior's voice came out flat and strange. "You touched him and you took his wife out of his head."

"I *kept* her—"

"That's what the Guild says!" It came out louder than Lior had meant it to; the goat startled against its tether. "That's the whole liturgy, isn't it? We don't steal, we *preserve*. We don't take, we *hold in trust*. I've sung at enough Guild funerals to know the hymn, Cyrus. I've watched Ondrey weigh a widow's grief on brass scales and call it stewardship."

"This is not that." Cyrus stood, slowly, hands visible, the way you'd move around a frightened animal—or a frightened lover, though neither of them had named it that yet. "Look at him. Look at his face."

Lior looked. The farmer's breathing had steadied, or seemed to; the terrible effort had gone out of it. He was murmuring something now, contented, shapeless. About beans.

"He doesn't know what you took," Lior said quietly. "That's worse. If he'd fought you—if he'd said *no*—"

"He couldn't have held on to it anyway. Death was taking it regardless. All I did was choose where it went." Cyrus heard their own voice doing the thing it did in the counting house, the reasonable ledger-keeper's cadence, and hated it, and could not stop it. "Every gift does this, Lior. Your doorsong opens doors and costs you memories. Mine costs other people theirs. The only question anyone ever gets to answer is whether they spend it honestly."

"Honestly." Lior laughed, and it was an ugly sound, nothing like singing. "You've been teaching me for three days how hunted people move. How we lie, how we misdirect, how we hand fear a false detail. And the whole time you're carrying—" They gestured, helpless, at Cyrus's head, their chest, the invisible archive of them. "How many? How many people are in there?"

"Lior—"

"*How many?*"

The silence stretched. Somewhere beyond the hedge a lark went up, indifferent.

"Four hundred and eleven," Cyrus said at last. "As of this morning. Four hundred and twelve now." They said it without apology, which was itself a kind of confession: the number was kept the way numbers were kept, clean and current, because a sloppy archive was a betrayal of everyone in it. "Every one entered in the true ledger. Name, date, circumstance. Every one either bought fairly, given freely, or rescued from the fire. I know which is which. I know *all* of them."

"Do you? Do you actually know them, or do you just know the entries?" Lior came a step closer, and now the anger was cracking along its seams into something else, something that looked almost like grief. "When you close your eyes at night, whose life do you see? Is there anything left in there that's only yours?"

And that—that was the blade that found the gap in the armor. Because Cyrus thought of the secret column, the entry labeled only *C.A.*, the memory taken from their own self long ago by their own hand, sealed against their own knowing. Thought of how they could not remember their mother's voice, only the fact of having once known it. Thought of the growing gray acreage inside them where a self should be, and how they had always told themself it was the price of the work, the honest cost, the—

"Don't," Cyrus said, and their voice came out wrong. Smaller than they'd ever let it be in front of another living soul.

Lior heard it. Whatever they'd been about to say next died in their throat, and for a long moment the two of them stood in a dead man's garden with the frost-killed beans between them, breathing hard, closer to each other than they'd been since the ash market and further apart than either could measure.

"I'm sorry," Lior said finally. Roughly. "I'm not—I'm not saying you're the Tuner. I know you're not. I watched you refuse a fee because a woman deserved to grieve her son. I *know* you." They dragged a hand through their hair. "That's what frightens me. I know you, and I still can't tell where you end and the ledger begins."

"Neither can I," Cyrus said. "That's the honest answer. Neither can I, and I've been afraid to check for years."

The farmer stirred. His breathing had changed again—longer gaps now, the tide going out. Cyrus knelt beside him once more, and this time did not touch him with the gift, only with their hand, wrapping his cold fingers around theirs.

"Your sister in Harrowmere," Cyrus said. "I'll find her. And your wife—I have her. The garden. The laugh. She's safe, and someday I'll give her back to someone who loved you both. That's a promise made out loud, with witnesses." A glance up at Lior, who swallowed and nodded. "You can go however you were going to go. Nothing of hers will be lost."

The old man smiled at the sky. "Straight rows," he murmured. "She liked the rows straight." And then, after a while, he wasn't talking anymore, and the tide was all the way out.

They buried him under the flowering white thing in his own garden, because neither of them could stomach leaving him for crows. Lior sang over the grave—not a funeral song from any hire-book, but something older, wordless, the kind of song that made the ground itself seem to listen. Cyrus stood with their hat off and four hundred and twelve lives folded invisibly inside their ribs, and understood, watching Lior sing, that they had just been seen more clearly than they had been seen in twenty years—and that being seen, for a creature like them, was the most dangerous thing that could happen.

They walked on north as the light failed. Neither spoke for a mile. Then Lior said, quietly, without turning: "Someday you'll have to show me the ledger. The true one. All of it—including whatever it is you won't let yourself read."

Cyrus's hand went, before they could stop it, to the oilskin packet against their heart, where the secret column waited with its single unlabeled entry.

"C.A.," Lior said. "I saw you flinch when you thought of it just now. What did you take from yourself, Cyrus Amberdale?"

Ahead of them, where the track crested the hill, the evening mist was gathering in a way that mist did not usually gather—lying in a long, straight line across the land, like a border drawn by a careful hand.
