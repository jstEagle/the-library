## 8. The Country That Isn't

The border was not a line. It was a silence.

Lior felt it before they saw it—a hush in the air like the held breath before a downbeat, as though the whole landscape had drawn itself up to listen. One moment they were walking through scrubland that belonged to the crown's maps, all surveyor's stakes and boundary stones stamped with the royal seal; the next, the stones were gone, and so were the roads, and the wind carried a smell Lior had not smelled since childhood: woodsmoke and wet slate and something flowering they had no name for.

Cyrus stopped at the edge of a wheat field that should not have existed this late in the season. The grain stood gold and heavy-headed, bending under its own abundance, though every almanac in the capital said harvest was three months past.

"This is wrong," Cyrus said. They had their ledger clutched against their chest the way other people held children or weapons. "There's no border here. There's never been a border here. I've read every customs record the Guild keeps. North of the Ash Market there's moorland for forty miles and then the river, and nothing else."

"Maybe your records are wrong."

"My records are never wrong." But Cyrus's voice wavered on it, and Lior watched them look at the wheat the way a man looks at a sum that refuses to balance. "It's not that the map is missing a country. It's that the country is refusing to be mapped. Those are different failures entirely."

Lior stepped off the dead road and into the living grass. It came up past their knees, seeding, whispering. Somewhere ahead—east, maybe, or wherever east was now—a bell rang, and it rang a pattern Lior's body knew before their mind did: three strokes, a rest, two more. The evening bell of a village called Vesseny, where their mother had once taken them to market, except Vesseny had burned when Lior was nine and everyone said so, everyone agreed, there was ash where the village had been and a memorial stone with ninety-one names carved into it.

"Vesseny," Lior said aloud, just to hear whether the name would hold its shape in the air.

Cyrus went very still. "That village doesn't exist. It burned. There's a memorial—"

"I know." Lior's throat was tight. "I've sung at the memorial. Every year. The Guild hired me for it—the anniversary of the fire, mourners who remembered cousins they'd lost, whole families gone in one night." They laughed, and the laugh came out cracked. "I made them remember better days there, Cyrus. Better days that never happened. And the whole time the actual days were—" They gestured at the wheat, the bell, the smoke-smell. "Here. They were here the whole time."

* * *

They walked until dark, following the bell-sound deeper into a country that grew more impossible with every mile. Fences stood mended where fences should have rotted. A shrine to Saint Ovelia-of-the-Threshold stood fresh-painted at a crossroads, though the crown had struck Ovelia from the calendar twenty years ago and melted her statues for cannon. The stars overhead were arranged in constellations Lior half-remembered from a nursemaid's songs—the Miller, the Drowned Girl, the Nine Bells—and not one of them matched the sky over the capital.

They found the inn by its light: a low stone building with a sign of a painted lark, windows glowing amber, standing alone in a fold of hills where no inn had any right to be. There was no road to it. There didn't need to be. Inns in the old country grew where travelers needed them, or so the stories said, and Lior had always thought the stories were lies told to children.

Inside, it was warm and crowded and loud, and every head turned when the door opened—not with suspicion, but with the particular attention people give a stage when the performance is about to begin.

Lior understood a heartbeat too late what that attention meant.

"You'll sing for your supper," the innkeeper said. She was broad and gray-braided, flour to her elbows, and she said it the way you'd say *the sun will rise*. Not a demand. An assumption about how the world worked.

"We can pay," Cyrus began, hand already moving to the coin purse.

"Keep your coin." The innkeeper waved it off without looking at it. "Singers are owed. That's the old law. A voice that carries the songs owes nothing to any hearth it warms." She squinted at Lior then, really looked, and something in her face shifted—softened, saddened. "Though yours is a strange case, isn't it. A choirless singer. I haven't seen one of those since—" She stopped herself, pressed her lips together, and turned away. "Sit. Eat first. Sing after. Bread before music; that's the other law."

She set down food that made Cyrus forget the ledger entirely: dark bread still warm, butter with honey beaten through it, a stew of lamb and barley that tasted of thyme from a garden no map would admit to. Around them the common room hummed with talk in an accent Lior hadn't heard spoken aloud since childhood—vowels rounder, consonants softer, the speech of a kingdom that officially had never been conquered because officially it had never existed.

"They're going to ask me to sing," Lior murmured across the table. "And I don't know if I remember any of their songs. I know funeral music. I know the songs the crown allows. I don't know—"

"You know them," Cyrus said. It wasn't comfort. It was certainty, delivered flat as arithmetic. "You've been singing them your whole life. You just didn't know what they were. Songs don't die when a country does, Lior. They go into hiding. They dress themselves up as lullabies and work songs and mourning hymns, and they wait." Cyrus looked down at their plate. "Believe me. I've handled enough stolen memories to know how long a thing can wait inside a person."

* * *

After supper, the room quieted the way rooms do when they want music—gradually, then all at once, the way snow decides to fall.

Lior stood. Their hands shook. They had sung for money in a hundred halls and gravesides, sung grief into shape for strangers, sung doors open in palace vaults—but this was different. This was singing home, and they could not remember the last time they had done it, or whether they ever had.

They started with the only song that came, unbidden, rising out of them like water finding a crack: a slow tune in a mode the conservatory had forbidden, seven notes climbing and falling, the one their mother used to hum while she worked. Lior had always assumed they'd invented it, or misremembered it, or stolen it from somewhere without knowing.

Halfway through the first verse, the room joined them.

Not loudly. Not all at once. But the gray-braided innkeeper's hands stilled in her apron, and an old man by the fire picked up the harmony a third below, and a woman nursing a baby began to sway, and the words—when Lior finally found them, surfacing from some drowned place in their memory—were in the old tongue, the tongue the schools had beaten out of everyone, and every soul in that room knew them.

*The miller's daughter walks the weir,*
*the water knows her name,*
*and all the bells of Vesseny*
*ring soft to call her home.*

Lior's voice broke on the last line. They kept singing anyway, because that was the training, that was the craft—you sang through the break and let the break become part of the song—and the room sang with them, and the baby slept, and outside the window the impossible wheat bent in a wind that carried the smell of a village that had burned ninety-one souls and somehow kept its bells.

When it ended, nobody applauded. That was the old way, Cyrus would tell them later—applause was a crown-country habit, a transaction, payment rendered. Here you simply sat in the silence the song had made and let it be an offering.

The innkeeper came to the table with two cups of something dark and spiced and set them down without asking. Her eyes were wet. She didn't wipe them.

"That song," she said. "Where did you get it?"

"My mother," Lior said. "I think. Or—I don't know. I thought I made it up."

"No singer makes up that song. It's older than the fire. Older than the conquest." She glanced toward the door, toward the dark, and lowered her voice. "You crossed over tonight. From the map-country into this one. I could taste it on you both—the crown's dust, the fear. So let me tell you the only thing worth knowing here, and then I'll show you to beds, because singers are owed beds too."

She leaned close. Flour-dusted hands flat on the table, steadying herself as much as them.

"The old country isn't a place. It never was. That's why their maps can't find it and their soldiers can't burn it, though gods know they've tried. The old country is the songs. Wherever a song survives—a real one, a root-song, not one of their hymns to their king—that's the old country, alive, right under their feet. They took our fields. They took our king's head. They took our names off everything. But they couldn't take the songs, because you can't confiscate what lives inside a mouth." She straightened. "Every village you passed tonight, every bell, every loaf of bread—they exist because someone kept humming. That's all keeping a country alive takes. Someone humming."

Lior sat with that. Grief and wonder arrived together, braided so tightly they could not be told apart—grief for the memorial stone with its ninety-one names, for the years spent singing false comforts over true ashes; wonder at the wheat, the bell, the inn that grew where it was needed. Both feelings filled them to the brim and neither displaced the other, and Lior understood, dimly, that this was what the old country felt like from inside: not joy, not sorrow, but both, held in one throat, waiting to be sung.

Across the table, Cyrus had gone quiet in the way that meant calculation. Lior knew that quiet now, had learned its textures on the road north. Cyrus was thinking about ledgers. About what could be recorded, weighed, sold—or hidden.

"You're wondering what this is worth," Lior said softly.

"I'm wondering what it costs," Cyrus corrected. "Everything here is a debt, Lior. 'Singers are owed' means someone, somewhere, is paying. Countries don't survive on humming alone. Something is being spent to keep this wheat gold and these bells ringing, and I would very much like to know what, before we walk any deeper into a place that doesn't exist."

The innkeeper, clearing cups nearby, heard. She paused, and her face did something complicated—pity and warning tangled together.

"Clever bookkeeper," she said. "You'd do well here, once upon a time. We had need of careful counters, in the old days." Then, quieter, almost to herself: "Still do. More than ever."

She showed them to a room under the eaves, two narrow beds, a candle, a window facing the impossible fields. Lior blew out the candle and lay listening to the house settle around them, to distant voices still murmuring fragments of the miller's song, passing it along like a coal handed hand to hand.

Sleep came slow. And when it came, it brought the dream it always brought lately: a door, tall as a cathedral, standing in unmown grass. But tonight the door was different. Tonight it was ajar. And through the gap poured not light but sound—an enormous, patient chord, hundreds of voices holding one note, the way a country holds its breath.

In the dream, Lior walked toward it. In the dream, a voice behind them—Cyrus's voice, but strained, thinner than it should be—said:

"Lior. Wait. Look at the hinges."

Lior woke before they could look. Gray dawn stood in the window. Downstairs, the innkeeper was shouting—not in anger, but in alarm—and beneath her voice, rolling up the valley from the direction they had come, came a sound Lior knew from the worst night of their life:

Tuning forks. Dozens of them, struck all at once, ringing the crown's cold perfect note against which every song in the valley suddenly sounded like a cry for help.
