## 9. Price Tags

The inn at Harrow's Ford had a name once, but the sign above the door had been burned blank by some tax collector's zealotry, and now travelers called it the Blank Sign, which suited Cyrus fine. Blank things were easier to trust. They made no promises.

They took the room at the back, up a staircase that groaned like a man being persuaded against his will. One bed, one chair, one window with a view of the ford and the road beyond it—the road they'd have to take north in the morning, into country that no map admitted to. Lior claimed the bed without ceremony, boots off, coat still on, asleep almost before their head touched the pillow. Three days of hard walking had spent them both. But sleep came for Lior easily, the way it came for people whose consciences were simple, and Cyrus sat in the chair with the true ledger on their knees and did not sleep at all.

They had carried it for eleven days now. Ondrey had pressed it into their hands in the burning counting house—half the guild's records already curling to ash in the grate—and said, *You keep honest books, Amberdale. That's why you'll carry the dishonest ones.* A joke, Ondrey-style: dry as vellum, sharp enough to cut. Cyrus had laughed because it was expected, and then hadn't stopped carrying the thing through river crossings and hedgerow nights and one long terrible day hiding in a drainage culvert while Tuner's hounds cast back and forth along the bank above them like questions looking for answers.

The ledger weighed nothing. That was the horror of it. Four hundred pages of calfskin and iron-gall ink, and it weighed less than a loaf of bread, less than a child, less than any of the things written inside it.

Cyrus opened it now, by the light of a candle stub they'd been rationing like a miser rations love.

* * *

Every guild ledger had two columns. The first was inventory: what had come in, from whom, its weight and grade and price. Grief sold heaviest—a dead child's memory could buy a widow a year of bread—but joy fetched better per ounce, because joy was rare and people hoarded it even when selling themselves empty. The second column was payment: coin, favor, silence. Standard bookkeeping. Any crown auditor who seized a Vellum Guild ledger would find exactly what they expected to find, which was the point of ledgers everywhere.

But Cyrus kept a third column. It ran down the outer margin of every page, in a cipher of their own devising—little ticks and hooks that looked like smudges, like accidents of the pen, like nothing at all. Ondrey knew about it. Had always known, Cyrus suspected, and had never once asked, because asking would make it official, and official things got audited.

The third column recorded what Cyrus had kept.

Not stolen. The distinction mattered, though Cyrus had stopped believing in it somewhere around the fourth year of double-books. Stolen meant taken for sale or spite. Kept meant taken because otherwise it would be destroyed—or worse, sold to someone who'd wear it wrong, wear it like a costume, a dead man's grief on a living stranger's shoulders. When a client died before their purchase could be delivered, the memory reverted to guild stock. Stock got melted down, blended, sold by the dram to rich men who wanted to know what drowning felt like without the inconvenience. Cyrus had started intercepting those. Quietly. One tick in the margin at a time.

There were sixty-one ticks now. Sixty-one names in the index at the back, each paired with a date and a weight and a single word of description, because Cyrus had learned early that memories without labels became indistinguishable from your own thoughts, and then you woke some morning weeping at a stranger's funeral and couldn't say whose grief was leaking out of you.

*Maren Cotch, 7 dr., her wedding day.*

*Tobias Fenn, 3 dr., his daughter learning to walk.*

*Aldous Grey, 11 dr., the last conversation with his brother, before the war took the brother's name along with everything else.*

Sixty-one small thefts. Or sixty-one rescues. The ledger didn't care which. Ledgers never did; that was their whole virtue and their whole sin.

And at the bottom of the index, older than all the rest, dated to Cyrus's first year with the guild, there was one entry that broke the pattern. No client's name. No weight. Just two letters and a period:

*C.A.*

Cyrus looked at it the way you look at a scar—familiar, unavoidable, meaning something you've stopped being able to reconstruct. They had taken it from themselves. That much they remembered, or rather, they remembered the act: standing in the guild cellar at nineteen, alone, with the taking-hands trembling, deciding that one memory of their own was too dangerous to carry loose. Too dangerous, or too painful—they genuinely no longer knew which. They had folded it away inside themselves the way you fold a letter you can't bear to read again but can't bring yourself to burn, and they had sealed it with the only technique the guild taught for self-taken stock, which was to forget where you'd put it.

That was the trick of it. You couldn't hide a memory from yourself unless you hid the hiding. So Cyrus had hidden the hiding, and now the hiding was hidden so well that even the hider was lost.

They knew it was heavy. That was all. When they reached inward, carefully, the way you probe a tooth, they found a mass, a density, something with real weight and real gravity sitting in the dark of them. Whatever C.A. held, it held a lot of it. Eleven drams' worth, maybe more. Enough to bend a life around.

The candle guttered. On the bed, Lior turned over and murmured something—half a word, half a note, the way singers talked in their sleep, if singers talked in notes. Cyrus closed the ledger.

Then opened it again. Because tomorrow they crossed into the old country, and after that anything might happen, and it suddenly seemed obscene to be carrying sixty-two secrets across a border when one of them belonged, in some crooked way, to the person sleeping three feet away.

Or perhaps that wasn't the reason. Perhaps the reason was simpler and worse: Cyrus was tired. Tired of being a building with bricked-up rooms. Tired of the specific loneliness of people who are full of other lives and cannot share a single one.

"Lior," they said quietly.

Nothing. Then: "Mm." Not asleep after all, or not deeply. Lior had the hunted person's sleep, surface-skimming, rising at any change in the room's weather.

"Are you awake?"

A pause. "I'm deciding whether to pretend to be."

"That's fair." Cyrus set the ledger on the blanket between them, open to the index. "I need to show you something. And I need you to not hate me until I'm finished explaining, because the explanation is long and the hating can start after."

Lior sat up. In the candlelight their face was all planes and shadows, watchful, the face of someone who had learned that gifts always arrived with invoices attached. "That's an ominous sentence, Cyrus Amberdale."

"I know."

"You're showing me a book. Hunted people don't show each other books."

"This one's different." Cyrus turned it so the candle caught the third column. "This is the true ledger. Ondrey gave it to me the night the guild burned its records. Every memory the guild ever handled that I personally intercepted instead of letting it go to market. Sixty-one of them. Names, dates, weights." Their finger moved down the column of ticks. "I've been carrying them. All of them. For six years."

Lior was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the ford muttered over its stones, patient as arithmetic.

"You *keep* them," Lior said finally. "In you."

"In me. Yes."

"Not in a vault. Not in a strongbox."

"There are no vaults for this kind of stock. There's only carrying it or destroying it, and destroying—" Cyrus stopped. Started again. "I couldn't. Maren Cotch waited forty years to marry a man who died the following spring, and her wedding day was going to be blended into a pleasure-draught for some magistrate. Tobias Fenn's daughter is grown now, married, alive, and she thinks her father forgot her childhood because grief ate it. He didn't forget. I have it. All of it. Every time she fell down and laughed instead of crying."

"And they don't know." Lior's voice had gone very careful, very flat, the voice of someone walking on ice and listening to it talk. "These people. They don't know you're walking around wearing their lives."

"No."

"They wouldn't consent."

"Some of them are dead, Lior. Consent isn't—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip-crack, small and precise. Lior pulled their knees up, wrapped their arms around them, made themselves small and armed. "Don't tell me what consent isn't. I sang funerals for hire. I know exactly what it is and isn't. I made mourners remember days that never happened and told myself it was mercy, and I have hated myself in a low continuous hum ever since, so don't you dare tell me the arithmetic makes it clean."

"I'm not saying it's clean."

"What are you saying?"

Cyrus looked down at the ledger. At sixty-one names that were also sixty-one weights pressing down inside them, shaping their posture, their dreams, the strange way they sometimes reached for a teacup with their left hand although they were right-handed because Aldous Grey had been left-handed and Aldous Grey lived in them now.

"I'm saying I don't know anymore whether I rescued them or collected them. That's the truth. That's the whole ugly truth. Some nights it's rescue. Some nights I wake up and I'm a magpie and I don't remember choosing to be one." They turned the page to the index's end. Turned it deliberately, the way you turn toward a wound. "And there's one more thing. One entry that isn't a client. Isn't anyone."

Lior leaned in despite themselves. Curiosity was the singer's native hunger; Cyrus had watched it override fear a dozen times on the road.

*C.A.*

"Cyrus Amberdale," Lior read aloud. Then looked up. "You."

"Me. Taken from myself. My first year at the guild. I was nineteen."

"Took a memory from yourself." Lior said it slowly, tasting the strangeness of it. "Why would anyone—"

"Because I couldn't live with it loose. Whatever it was. I decided it was safer stored than carried, and the guild method for storing from yourself requires forgetting the storage, so I—" Cyrus spread their hands. Empty. "I don't know what it is. That's what I'm telling you. There is a room inside me with my own name on the door and I sealed it so thoroughly that I can't find the door anymore. I know how heavy it is. I know it's mine. I don't know what's in it."

The silence stretched. The ford went on muttering. Somewhere down the road a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"That's the most frightening thing I've ever heard," Lior said at last, "and I once watched a man get emptied in front of me."

"Thank you. I think."

"It's not a compliment, it's—" Lior dragged a hand through their hair, and Cyrus saw the exhaustion win, briefly, the mask slipping to show the young person underneath, barely older than Cyrus had been at nineteen, carrying a gift that ate its own memories with every use. "You cut a hole in yourself and forgot the shape of the hole. And you've been walking around for years with everyone else's furniture stacked around the gap so nobody notices."

"Yes."

"Does it hurt? The gap?"

Cyrus considered lying. Considered it seriously, professionally, the way they considered everything—with a weighing of costs. But they had opened the ledger. The point of opening the ledger was that you didn't get to close it halfway.

"It doesn't hurt," they said. "It *bends*. Everything near it bends. Like light around something massive. I'll be happy about something ordinary—rain, good bread—and the happiness will curve, mid-feeling, toward that dark spot, and come out the other side as grief. Not my grief. Not anyone's grief I can name. Just grief, aimed at nothing, arriving on schedule." They laughed, short and awful. "I used to think everyone felt that. I was twenty-two before I realized most people's happiness goes where it's pointed."

Lior reached out—not far, just far enough—and laid two fingers on the ledger's open page, on the entry *C.A.*, the way you'd touch a grave marker.

"Open it," they said.

"I can't. That's what I've been—"

"You're a taker, Cyrus. Taking is what your hands do. Take it *back*."

"I don't know where it is. I told you. The hiding is hidden. If I reach for it blind I could tear something. I could take the wrong thing—take the memory of hiding it, and then it's lost forever, floating loose in me with no label, no door, no—" Cyrus heard their own voice climbing and made it stop. Made their hands stop shaking. Sat on them, literally, like a child. "I've thought about it every night for six years. Every night. And every night the answer is the same: leave it sealed. Because whatever's in there was bad enough that nineteen-year-old me chose amnesia over carrying it, and nineteen-year-old me knew me better than anyone since."

"Or nineteen-year-old you was a frightened kid in a cellar full of professional thieves," Lior said, "who learned that feelings are inventory, and applied the guild's logic to the one thing that should never have been inventoried." Their voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "You price things, Cyrus. It's what you do. Weights and grades and columns. And you put a price tag on a piece of your own soul and shelved it, and now you can't read the tag."

Cyrus said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either agreement or defense, and both tasted like ash.

* * *

They sat with it a while. The candle burned down another finger's width. Eventually Lior lay back down, facing the wall, and Cyrus stayed in the chair with the ledger, and neither of them slept, and the silence between them was not the old comfortable silence of the road but a new one, structural, load-bearing—the silence of a house after you've found the crack in the foundation and can't unsee it.

Near dawn, Lior spoke into the dark.

"The settlement."

Cyrus went still. "What settlement?"

"The one the peddler told us about. Two days north, past the ford. Where the tuned went after the crown finished with them. The hollowed ones." Lior's voice was flat with purpose now, sleep entirely abandoned. "People with holes in them. Whole villages of people with holes in them, humming pieces of lives they can't name." A pause. "I want to see it. I need to see it. And I think—" the pause lengthened, and when Lior continued, the words came slow, like stones placed one at a time, "—I think you need to see it too. Before you decide what your hole is worth. Before you decide whether to go digging for it or let it lie."

"Lior—"

"Because here's what I understood tonight, listening to you talk about your sixty-one ghosts." Lior rolled over. In the gray pre-light their eyes were very clear. "You think you're a keeper. A rescuer with a filing system. But a hoard doesn't care what it hoards, Cyrus. Gold or grief, it's all just weight. And I've seen what the crown does to people who carry too much—it empties them. And I've started wondering whether there's more than one way to be emptied." They sat up. "Whether some people do it to themselves, one merciful little theft at a time."

The words landed like cold water. Cyrus opened their mouth and found nothing in it.

"So we go north," Lior said. "We see the hollowed. And then you tell me—honestly, with no columns and no prices—whether you're keeping those memories, or whether they're keeping you."

Outside, the ford ran on. And far down the north road, faint as a note held past endurance, something that might have been singing drifted on the wind—or might have been humming, the sound of many voices remembering nothing at all.
