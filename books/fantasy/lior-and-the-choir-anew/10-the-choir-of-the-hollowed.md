## 10. The Choir of the Hollowed

The song found them before the village did.

Lior heard it first as a wrongness in the wind—a hum with no melody, a dozen voices circling the same three notes like moths around a lamp that had gone out. They had been walking since dawn through birch country where the road thinned to a suggestion, and Cyrus had gone quiet in the way they went quiet when the ledger under their coat felt heavy, which was most of the time now.

"Stop," Lior said.

Cyrus stopped. "What is it?"

"Listen."

The wind moved through the birches, and beneath it, faint and fractured, came the humming. Not one voice. Many. All slightly out of step with each other, the way a congregation falls apart when it has forgotten the words but not the wanting.

"That's not birdsong," Cyrus said.

"No." Lior's throat tightened. "It's people."

They followed the sound off the road, up a fold of hill, through a stand of trees that grew crooked and close, as if leaning together to keep a secret. And then the trees opened, and Lior stopped breathing for a moment.

The settlement sat in a hollow like a cupped hand: perhaps forty houses, built low and close, roofs thatched with heather, smoke rising thin from a few chimneys. It was not hidden so much as folded away, tucked into the land the way a letter is tucked into a book. Gardens grew in the spaces between buildings. Laundry hung on lines strung door to door. It might have been any poor village in the north country, except—

Except for the doors. Every door in the settlement stood open. Not unlocked, not ajar—open, wide, unguarded, though the autumn air came cold down the hill. As if no one inside had anything left worth closing a door against.

And except for the singing. The humming rose from the village like heat haze, dozens of voices, each caught on a fragment—a bar of a lullaby, four notes of a harvest song, the opening phrase of a hymn Lior had known as a child—and each fragment worn smooth at the edges, repeated until repetition had eaten everything else.

"They're tuned," Cyrus said quietly. Beside Lior, they had gone very still, the way thieves go still when they see something they understand too well. "This many of them. This far north. Someone's been emptying people for years and dumping what's left where no map will find them."

Lior didn't answer. They were listening harder now, sorting the fragments the way their mother had once taught them to sort chord tones from overtones. There was a miller's song in there, or part of one. A wedding tune. Something that sounded like a soldier's marching cadence slowed to a crawl. Each voice held its scrap and circled it, and none of the scraps fit together anymore, and all of them wanted to.

"It's a choir," Lior said, and their voice cracked on the word. "They're trying to sing together and they can't remember how."

* * *

The first person to notice them was an old woman kneeling in a garden, pulling carrots with slow, patient hands. She looked up, saw two strangers at the tree line, and smiled—the open, uncomplicated smile of someone who has forgotten to be afraid.

"Visitors," she said, to no one in particular, or perhaps to the carrots. "We don't get visitors."

A man emerged from the nearest house, wiping his hands on his apron. He was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with flour dusted gray into the creases of his knuckles. He looked at Lior and Cyrus with mild curiosity, the way one looks at weather.

"You'll be hungry," he said. It was not a question. He turned back toward his house. "There's bread."

"We—" Cyrus started, and stopped, because the man had already gone inside, and because there seemed nothing to say to a hospitality that expected nothing, remembered nothing, feared nothing.

They went down into the village because there was nowhere else to go, and because leaving would have felt like a kind of cruelty.

Inside the baker's house—it was a bakery; the ovens were warm and the smell of rye filled the single room—the old woman from the garden had already seated herself at the table as if she belonged there, which perhaps she did, or perhaps belonging required no memory at all. More people drifted in. A young man with a scarred chin who sat with his hands flat on the table and hummed his four notes, over and over, soft as breathing. A middle-aged woman who rocked slightly, back and forth, her lips moving around a shape that might have been a name. An ancient man by the fire whose fragment was a single descending phrase, five notes falling like water down steps.

They ate the bread. It was good bread. The baker watched them eat with quiet satisfaction, and when Lior thanked him, he frowned as if the words were coins from a foreign country.

"You sang," he said suddenly, looking at Lior. "When you came down the hill. Under your breath. I heard it."

"I didn't mean to," Lior said.

The baker nodded slowly. "The others will want to hear you, then. We like it when someone sings whole songs." He paused, and something flickered behind his eyes—not memory exactly, but the ghost of a memory, the place where a memory had been torn out and the wall never repainted. "I used to know a lot of songs. I can feel the shapes of them. Like coats in a closet with the hangers empty."

Lior set down the bread. Their hands were shaking.

"What happened here?" Cyrus asked gently. "Do you remember?"

"The King's Tuner came," the old woman said placidly, still working at a carrot top. "In the spring. Or maybe two springs ago. They put the forks on our heads and took the bad parts out." She looked up, serene. "I don't remember what the bad parts were. That's how I know they worked."

"Forks?" Cyrus asked.

"Tuning forks," Lior said. Their voice came out flat and strange. "Silver ones. You'd know them if you'd seen one. They ring a note that makes everything else in your head come loose, and then the Tuner pulls the threads out one by one." They had never seen one. They knew this the way they knew the taste of ash: completely, without evidence. Some knowledge lived deeper than memory. "It doesn't take the bad parts. It takes everything. The bad parts are just the excuse."

The room hummed around them. Four notes, five notes, a descending phrase. The choir of the hollowed, singing to itself in the dark of what it used to be.

* * *

They stayed the night, because the villagers would not hear of anything else, and because night in the open hills with the Tuner somewhere behind them was its own kind of death. Cyrus slept in fits near the door, one hand always on the ledger. Lior lay awake listening to the humming, and near midnight, unable to bear it any longer, got up and walked out into the cold.

The village green was empty under a half moon. But the humming was louder out here, and Lior understood suddenly that the hollowed sang at night on purpose—that this was their hour, the hour when the fragments rose highest, when forty people stood in their doorways or sat on their stoops and offered their scraps to the dark, hoping the scraps would recognize each other.

Lior stood in the center of the green and began, very softly, to sing.

They chose the harvest round first—the one every child in the old country learned before they could write their own names, the one about the wheat and the wheel. Their voice carried in the cold air, clear and small, and one by one the fragments began to turn toward it. Not the people—the fragments. The baker's empty-hanger shapes leaned toward the melody. The old woman's humming shifted, found the key, clung to it. The young man with the scarred chin stopped his four notes and wept silently, his mouth open, his face lit with something that was not quite joy and not quite grief but was made of the same substance.

For a minute—for one full turning of the round—forty broken voices became a choir. Ragged, imperfect, missing verses the way amputees miss limbs, but a choir. The harmony rose off the green and into the birches, and Lior sang with their eyes closed and their heart breaking in a way they could not have described, because this was the thing they had wanted their whole life: not an audience, not a commission, not a guild contract. A circle of voices and a place inside it.

Then the round ended, and the fragments drifted apart again, back to their separate orbits, and the green was only a green, and the people were only the hollowed, smiling at Lior with their emptied faces.

"Thank you," whispered the young man with the scarred chin. "I don't know what that was. But I think I loved it once."

* * *

It was the old music teacher who found them at dawn.

Lior was packing their few things in the bakery when the door opened and a small woman came in out of the mist, moving with the careful precision of someone navigating by habit rather than sight. She was perhaps seventy. Her hair was white and cut short, practical, the way a working musician cuts it. Her hands were extraordinary even before Lior noticed anything else about them—long-fingered, strong-knuckled, the hands of someone who had spent sixty years shaping other people's voices.

She stopped in the doorway. She tilted her head, the way a musician tilts toward a pitch.

"You," she said. "You were singing last night."

"Yes," Lior said.

"Your breath support is excellent," the woman said. "Who taught you?"

And Lior's world went sideways.

Because Lior knew those hands. Had watched them move through the air of a small schoolroom above a chandlery in the river district, drawing phrases out of children like thread from spindles. Had heard that exact voice—dryer now, frayed at the edges, but that voice—say *again, from the top, and this time mean it*. Had sat in the third chair by the window at age eleven, terrified, and been told: *you have a door in your throat, child. Most people only have a window.*

"Maestra Corvane," Lior said.

The woman's head came up. For a moment—half a heartbeat—something moved behind her eyes, deep down, like a fish passing under ice. Her brow furrowed. Her extraordinary hands opened and closed at her sides.

"That name," she said slowly. "Someone gave me that name. I can't—" The furrow deepened. The fish sank. Her face smoothed back into its gentle blankness, and she smiled. "I'm sorry. I don't remember you. I don't remember me. But my hands remember teaching. When I sit still, they conduct. Isn't that funny? Empty hands, still keeping time."

Lior crossed the room without deciding to and took those hands in theirs.

The teacher's fingers curled around Lior's grip, strong and sure, and her body swayed slightly, unconsciously, to a tempo only her muscles recalled. Up here, Lior thought. In the hands, some of her survived. The crown had taken her memories, her name, her sixty years of students—but it had not thought to take the music stored below memory, in tendon and bone, in the body's own deep ledger.

"Maestra," Lior said, and their voice broke, and they didn't care. "It's me. Lior. Your student. The one with the door in their throat."

"Lior." She tried the syllables like foreign fruit. Then, softer, wondering: "That sounds like a note. A high one. Why does that sound like a high note?"

"Because you named me for one," Lior said. "You said every singer should carry their pitch with them everywhere they go."

Behind them, Cyrus stood very still by the door. Lior could feel them watching, could feel the weight of the ledger under their coat, and knew they were both thinking the same thing: *this is what the Hunt makes. This is what tuning leaves.* Somewhere south, Serah Vane had put silver forks on this woman's head and pulled out sixty years, and the crown called it justice, and the woman's hands still kept time.

"Will you sing for me?" the maestra asked. "Before you go. My hands want to hear it. I don't know why, but they do."

So Lior knelt on the bakery floor, an adult before a teacher who no longer knew them, and sang the first exercise she had ever taught them—a simple ascending scale, five notes, the ladder every voice climbs. And Maestra Corvane's hands rose from her lap and conducted, precise and tender, correcting breath Lior didn't know they were wasting, shaping a phrase thirty years of muscle memory wide, and when the scale topped out on the high note that was Lior's name, her hands closed slowly into fists against the air, holding it, holding it, the way a conductor holds a final chord past all reason.

"There," she whispered. "That's the door. I told you there was a door."

* * *

They left at first light, because staying longer would draw the Hunt down on forty defenseless heads, and because Lior could not watch the maestra forget them twice.

At the tree line, Lior turned back. The old woman stood in the middle of the green, hands raised, conducting the morning humming of the hollowed—shaping their fragments, coaxing them toward each other, giving the choir a spine it hadn't had yesterday. She couldn't remember why she did it. She did it anyway.

"She'll keep them together," Lior said hoarsely. "Even emptied. Even hollowed. She'll build them into something."

Cyrus said nothing for a long moment. Then, quietly: "The ledger has a column of names. People whose memories I'm carrying." They hesitated. "One of them is a music teacher from the river district. I took a memory from her years ago—a small one, a fee she couldn't pay. I've never known whose it was until now."

Lior stared at them. "You have one of hers?"

"A winter morning. A student's first perfect note." Cyrus's jaw was tight. "I could give it back. If we ever come this way again."

"Do it now," Lior said. "Give it to her now."

But Cyrus was already shaking their head, looking south, down the long road they had come by, where the wind carried no humming yet but someday might carry riders. "Not yet," they said. "Because on the road behind us, three days back, I passed a rider asking questions in every village. Gray coat. Silver instruments on his saddle. And he was heading north." They met Lior's eyes. "He wasn't following us, Lior. He was following the humming. He's coming here."

Behind them, faint and brave, the choir of the hollowed rose into the cold morning air—and this morning, for the first time, they were singing in harmony.
