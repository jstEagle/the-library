## 11. What Serah Remembers

The road to the hollowed settlement was the kind of road that made a liar of maps.

Serah Vane rode it alone, as she preferred, with her instruments wrapped in oilcloth against her back and her warrant sealed in wax inside her coat. The King's Tuner did not travel with retinues. Retinues made noise, and noise made witnesses, and witnesses remembered things—and remembering things was, these days, a crime she was licensed to punish.

Behind her, three days' ride and falling further, the trail of Cyrus Amberdale and the singer Lior wound north like a thread pulled through cloth. She had their pattern by heart now. Amberdale moved like an accountant: measured distances, careful campsites, fires small enough to be deniable. Lior moved like water finding a crack—always toward sound, always toward some ruined chapel or standing stone where a song might still live in the walls.

They were heading for the settlement. She had known it before the scouts confirmed it. The hollowed drew singers the way wounds drew flies; there was nowhere else in the northern reaches with anything left to sing to.

She should have felt satisfaction. The trail had been long, and the crown's patience shorter, and her superiors in the Office of Harmonic Order had begun sending letters whose tone she could hear in her teeth: *the Tuner hesitates; the Tuner grows sentimental; perhaps the Tuner requires tuning herself.* They said it politely. Everything in the capital was polite now, the way a held breath is quiet.

So she rode, and told herself the cold in her chest was only the weather.

* * *

She smelled the settlement before she saw it.

Not smoke—though there was smoke, thin cookfires rising from behind a ridge of black pines—but something under the smoke. The hollowed had a scent, if you spent your life among them as she did. It was the smell of houses after the family has gone. Bread baked once and never again. A sweetness gone slightly wrong, like fruit remembered rather than eaten.

Her hounds—she had two left, grey and silent, bred from stock that could taste memory on the air—stopped at the tree line and would not go further. That was unusual. Her hounds feared nothing except, once, a locked room in the palace vaults, and she had never learned what they heard through that door.

"Up," she told them softly.

They looked at her with pale eyes and lay down in the snow.

Serah dismounted and walked the last half-mile herself, leading the horses, because whatever waited beyond the pines had made even animals trained to hunt memory refuse the work. The snow deepened. The light went the color of old paper. And then the trees opened, and she stood at the edge of a bowl of land where forty or fifty structures leaned together like tired people, and the hollowed settlement spread below her, and someone was singing.

She stopped breathing.

It was nothing. That was the first thing she told herself, the professional thing. The hollowed sang all the time—fragments, husks, songs with the middle chewed out of them. She had catalogued hundreds of such fragments in her years of service. *A lullaby missing its second verse. A marching song with no country attached. A love song where every name had been tuned away, so the singer just sang oh, my dear, oh, my dear into the empty space where a person used to be.*

This was one of those. It had to be one of those.

But her hands were shaking on the reins, and she did not know why, and that—that was the thing she could not catalogue.

* * *

Rain.

That was the first fragment, arriving uninvited, the way the worst ones did. Rain on a slate roof, and she was seventeen, and her fingers hurt from practicing, and someone was laughing at her scales—not cruelly, never cruelly—with a laugh like a door opening onto a warm room.

"You're playing it like it's a punishment," the someone said. "It's a duet. Duets are conversations. You keep answering questions nobody asked."

"I don't know what to ask," young Serah had said, mortified, wet-haired, in love in the way you are at seventeen, which is to say completely and without any plan for survival.

"Then ask me anything." A hand closed over hers on the frets, adjusting them, warm through the rain-damp air. "Start with *stay.*"

She had not thought of that afternoon in eleven years. She had not been able to think of it in eleven years, because thinking of it required the years around it, and those were gone—surrendered, sealed, paid into the crown's great ledger of taken things when she took her oath. That was the bargain every Tuner made. *Give us what ties you, and we will give you purpose instead.* She had signed. She had wept, she was told, though she didn't remember weeping. She remembered being told she'd wept the way you remember reading about weather in another country.

The song below her reached the place where the second voice should come in.

And her mouth opened, and she almost—almost—harmonized.

* * *

Serah Vane had interrogated ninety-one people. She knew the number the way other officers knew their kills. Ninety-one souls sat in her memory—or rather, did not sit anywhere, having been lifted out through silver tines and crystal resonators and her own trained hands, which could find the seam of a person the way a midwife finds the turn of a child. She was good at it. She was the best in the kingdom. That was not pride; it was inventory.

She knew what happened to people who lost pieces. She had watched it nine hundred times from the outside: the flattening, the politeness, the way the tuned smiled at their own children like friendly strangers.

What she had never admitted, in eleven years of service, was what it was like from the inside.

From the inside, it was this: you were full of holes, and the holes did not feel like absence. They felt like certainty. You did not miss your mother's face; you simply knew, with total conviction, that mothers were not important. You did not ache for the rain and the slate roof and the hand on yours; you simply knew, with total conviction, that you had chosen this life freely, from the beginning, out of pure devotion to the crown.

The holes defended themselves. That was the design. Ondrey's guild stole memories for coin, but the crown stole them for loyalty, and loyalty built on a hole is the strongest mortar in the world, because nothing can dissolve what isn't there.

So Serah stood at the tree line listening to a broken song, and her training said: *this is nothing, report it, move on,* and her holes said: *you have always loved your work,* and underneath both voices, faint as a pulse under snow, something else said:

*You know this song. You know this song. You have known it since the rain.*

* * *

She should have turned around.

Later—insofar as later existed for a woman built out of omissions—she would try to reconstruct why she didn't. Duty said go. Self-preservation, that animal instinct even Tuners kept, said go. The settlement was a target now, not a lead; her orders from the capital were explicit. *Assess the hollowed encampments of the north for collaboration. Singers who consort with the emptied are assets of the traitor Lior. Assets may be repurposed.*

Repurposed. The word the Office used for tuning children.

She stayed. She told herself it was reconnaissance. She left the horses and walked down into the bowl of the settlement with her hood up and her warrant hidden, because a Tuner walking openly among the hollowed caused a specific kind of panic—a stillness, a silence, mothers stepping in front of sons—and she wanted, she needed, to hear the song without that silence around it.

The hollowed did not fear her. That was the horror of them. They had nothing left to take. They nodded to her in the paths between the lean-tos, courteous as innkeepers, and one old woman pressed a heel of bread into her hands and said, "You look hungry, dear. Singers are owed, but everyone's owed something."

"I'm not a singer," Serah said.

"No?" The woman smiled with vast, unhurried kindness. "Your mouth was moving, love. Back on the hill. I saw you from the wash-line. You were singing along to Aldric's song, and you knew the part most folk don't know—the high line, the answering part. Where did you learn the answering part?"

The bread went heavy in Serah's hands.

"I don't know," she said, and it was the truest sentence she had spoken in eleven years.

* * *

Aldric. That was the singer's name, the old man by the central fire, the one whose song had climbed the hill and broken something open in her. She found him at dusk, sitting wrapped in blankets with his eyes on the fire, humming the fragment again and again the way the hollowed did—polishing a shard because the whole was gone.

He had been a music teacher. Everyone in the settlement said it with reverence, though none of them could say how they knew. He had taught in the capital. He had had students. One of them, he sometimes said, staring into fires, had been the finest he ever trained, and he could not remember the student's name, or face, or gender, or instrument—only the fact of finest, like a headline with the story torn away.

Serah sat down across the fire from him.

"Do you know the answering part?" she asked.

His head came up slowly. His eyes were the pale washed blue of things left out in weather. "Nobody knows the answering part," he said. "I wrote it for—" He stopped. The hole did its work; she watched it happen, watched the question form behind his eyes and get smoothly, painlessly paved over. "I wrote it for someone," he finished, satisfied, and began to hum.

And Serah Vane, King's Tuner, breaker of ninety-one lives, opened her mouth and gave him back four bars.

The high line. The answering part. The conversation he had tried to teach a terrified girl on a rainy afternoon while rain ticked on slate and everything that mattered was still intact.

Aldric's face changed. It did not fill in—you could not fill in the hollowed, she knew that better than anyone alive—but it *turned*, the way a sunflower turns, toward something. Toward her. Tears ran down his face and he laughed and said, "There you are. There you are. I couldn't remember who I wrote it for but I remember now that it was *you*, don't I? Some part of me kept it. Some part of me kept you."

"I don't understand," Serah whispered.

"No," he agreed gently. "Neither do I. Isn't it wonderful?"

* * *

She walked out of the settlement in the dark and did not stop walking until the fires were behind the ridge, and then she stood in the snow among the pines and shook.

Fragments came now whether she willed them or not, and she understood, with the clinical precision that was the last intact piece of her, what was happening. The song was a key. Not a metaphor—a mechanism. Memory was stored in the body as much as the mind; the guild's own texts said so, the ones Ondrey burned. A song lived in the throat, the hands, the lungs. The crown had taken her *records* of Lior—taken the rain, the roof, the promise—but it had never taken her *voice*, because a Tuner's voice was an instrument of office, and instruments of office were exempt.

They had left the key in the lock for eleven years and called the lock secure.

*Lior.* The name arrived whole, and it undid her. Not the name as a target designation, not the fugitive, the asset, the doorsinger, the crown's priority one. The name as it had been said in a practice room with rain on the roof, said softly, said the way you say a thing you are afraid to believe you're allowed to want. Lior. Her first—her first what? The word *love* floated up and the hole beneath it yawned and she stepped back from the edge of it, gasping, because if she fell in she did not know what would be left standing on the bank.

A duet. They had promised a duet. Somewhere there had been a promise—on a stage? in a garden?—that when Lior's voice was ready, they would sing together, properly, in front of everyone, and the world would have to make room.

Instead the crown had made room in her, and put duty in it, and sent her to hunt the person the promise had been about.

"They'll have gone to the Door," she said aloud, to the pines, testing the treason in her mouth like a coin bitten for gold. "Amberdale will take them to the Last Door."

It was obvious. It had been obvious for weeks. Every clue pointed north-northwest, to the border no map admitted, and she had reported—what had she reported? Delays. Weather. Trails gone cold in the ash market. Small falsities, each defensible alone, accumulating into something that was no longer defensible at all.

Her orders regarding the settlement were in her coat. *Assess for collaboration.* The assessment was due by courier within two days. Forty hollowed, one of them her teacher, all of them guilty of nothing except having been emptied already—punished twice, once by the crown and once by the crown's arithmetic.

Serah took out the courier's tube. She took out the blank report form. She took out her pen, and her hand did not shake, because the holes held her steady; the holes were very good at holding her steady; that was what they were for.

*Hollowed encampment located in the northern fells,* she wrote. *Population approximately twelve. Elderly, dispersed, no singers present, no evidence of contact with fugitives. Site assessed as negligible threat. Recommend monitoring only. Proceeding northwest along the Coldwater road in pursuit of primary targets.*

Twelve, not fifty. Dispersed, not gathered. Negligible.

She sealed it, and the wax was the crown's red, and her hands were steady, and somewhere under the steadiness a seventeen-year-old with wet hair was screaming, or singing, or both.

* * *

At dawn she mounted and rode northwest along the Coldwater road, away from the settlement, exactly as her false report promised. Her hounds followed now, readily, as if whatever had stopped them at the tree line had been satisfied by what she'd done there.

Three days behind her, or ahead of her—she was no longer certain which direction the truth ran—Cyrus Amberdale and Lior were making for the Last Door. The crown knew where it stood. The crown had always known. That was the final fragment that surfaced as she rode, arriving not as memory but as instruction, one of the sealed orders she was permitted to know and not permitted to question: *if the Door opens, hold it shut until the Tuner arrives.*

Until *she* arrived. They expected her to stand at the threshold of the old country and tune the last singer in the world.

Serah rode through the snow with her instruments on her back and her warrant in her coat, and for the first time in eleven years she examined them both—really examined them, the way she had been trained to examine everything—and understood that she was carrying the tools of her own final erasure, and that the woman they had been built to unmake was the only person alive who could sing the answering part.

By nightfall she had made her decision. By morning she would have made her excuse.

And far to the northwest, in a field of unmown grass, a Door stood waiting to learn what its Tuner would do when she finally came home.
