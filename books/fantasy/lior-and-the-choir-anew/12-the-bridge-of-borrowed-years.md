## 12. The Bridge of Borrowed Years

The river had no name on any map, which was how Cyrus knew they'd crossed into the true old-country at last. Maps were the crown's way of agreeing with itself about what existed. A river that refused to be written down was a river that remembered something older than agreement.

It ran wide and slow and the color of tarnished silver, and it made a sound Cyrus had never heard water make before — not rushing, not lapping, but *humming*, low and continuous, like a congregation holding one note through centuries. The reeds along the bank bent in time with it. So did Cyrus's pulse, after an hour of walking beside it. Lior had gone quiet too, in that particular way they went quiet when the world was singing something they could almost join.

"There," Lior said.

The bridge stood where the valley narrowed. It was not wood and it was not stone; it was both and neither, a span of grey material that looked like driftwood grown into architecture, arched high over the humming water. And standing at its near end was the warden.

Cyrus had expected a troll out of nursemaid stories, or a ghost, or a toll-keeper with a little booth and a little book. Instead there was a person-shaped absence — a figure like a hole cut from the evening air, wearing the suggestion of a long coat and a hat with a wide brim, and where its face should have been there was only a soft darkening, the way glass fogs when you breathe on it. It did not move except to tilt its head as they approached, and the tilt had the patience of something that had been tilting its head for a very long time.

"Travelers," it said. Its voice came from everywhere the river's hum wasn't, which meant it came from everywhere. "The bridge is open."

Lior exhaled with relief. Cyrus did not. In Cyrus's experience, nothing worth crossing was ever simply open.

"What's the toll?" Cyrus asked.

The warden's fogged face seemed to brighten slightly, the way a merchant's does when the customer asks the right question unprompted.

"Years," it said. "Memory-years. The bridge is built of them. Every plank is someone's summer. Every rope is somebody's wedding day. You cannot cross on what you carry in your hands." It extended a hand that was more gesture than hand. "You pay in what you carry here." It touched, or gestured toward touching, the place behind its own absent face.

"How much?" Lior asked.

"That depends on what you are." The warden turned its headless attention to each of them in turn, and Cyrus felt it *weigh* them, the same sensation as the guild's brass scales settling, the same faint pressure behind the eyes. "The singer is light. Singers spend themselves as they go; there is little left to take, and the bridge doesn't want broken coin." Something in its tone suggested this was not entirely a kindness. "The keeper of ledgers—" the fogged face inclined toward Cyrus "—is heavy. Full. The bridge will want a great deal of you."

"How much is a great deal?" Cyrus kept their voice level, ledger-clerk flat, the voice that had talked down smugglers and talked up fees and once talked a crown inspector out of searching the counting house cellar.

"A season per stride," said the warden. "And the bridge is forty strides long."

* * *

They camped that night within sight of the bridge, because Lior argued — reasonably, furiously — that no one should make a payment like that at dusk, tired and frightened, when mistakes get made. Cyrus agreed easily enough. They agreed because it gave them the night to plan, and planning was the one thing Cyrus Amberdale had never been bad at.

They sat by a small fire fed on driftwood that burned greenish and hummed faintly in harmony with the river. Lior was asleep sitting up, the way they'd learned to sleep on the road, chin dropped, hands loose in their lap, looking younger than Cyrus knew them to be. Above them the stars were wrong — arranged in constellations Cyrus didn't know, patterns some dead astronomer of the old country had named and the new kingdom had forgotten. Even the sky here kept its own books.

Forty seasons. Four decades of memory, gone in forty steps.

Cyrus opened the ledger in their mind, the true one, the one Ondrey had pressed into their hands while the counting house burned behind them. They inventoried. It was what they did instead of praying.

Their mother's kitchen: bread rising under a cloth, the smell of yeast and lamp oil, her hands dusted white to the wrist. Their mother's laugh, which she only let out when her sister visited, a loud unguarded sound like a door blowing open. Their mother's face —

Cyrus stopped there. Made themselves go back. Made themselves look.

The face was still there. But it had gone gray at the edges, the way a print fades in a window. They could see the shape of her, the warmth of her, the fact of her — but when they reached for the specific thing, the exact arrangement of her features, the color of her eyes, the scar or lack of scar above her brow, it slipped. Not gone. *Fading.* And Cyrus understood, with the cold clarity of a clerk reading a column of numbers, that the road north had already been taking its tolls quietly — the ambush at the reliquary, the soldier's childhood they'd swallowed like a stone, the weeks of fear and hunger. Memory was not a purse that stayed full. It was a ledger whose ink dried and lifted away if you didn't keep rewriting it.

Forty more seasons would take the rest.

They looked at Lior, asleep by the fire, breath slow, firelight moving on their face. Lior, who had already paid more than anyone should — a gift that ate its owner one song at a time, a first love hollowed into a hunting hound, a homeland erased from every map. Lior, who had stood in the snow two nights ago and said *we split up* and then, when Cyrus refused, had not pushed it, because underneath all their grief they trusted Cyrus to be the careful one, the one who counted, the one who kept things safe.

That trust was the heaviest thing Cyrus carried. Heavier than the mother's face. Heavier than the name.

So the arithmetic was simple, and Cyrus had always been good at simple arithmetic dressed in complicated clothes.

Lior would cross light. That was what the warden had said, and the warden had weighed them honestly — singers spend themselves as they go. But Lior had also been spending since the palace vaults, since doorsong after doorsong, and Cyrus had watched the bill come due in small ways: Lior reaching for a word and missing it, staring at a village and saying *I think I've been happy somewhere like this* without being able to say where. If Lior paid forty strides' worth of anything, there would be nothing left to walk off the far side. Just a body with a beautiful voice and no one home to sing.

Whereas Cyrus — Cyrus was a hoard. Cyrus had spent ten years double-booking the Vellum Guild, keeping memories that weren't theirs, whole seasons of other people's lives shelved inside them like casks in a cellar. Some of those could be spent instead. The soldier's childhood from the reliquary — heavy, ugly, better given to the bridge than carried another mile. A baker's pride in his first loaf. A widow's last afternoon with a husband whose name Cyrus had never known. Forty strides' worth of borrowed years, and yes, some of it would have to come out of Cyrus's own stock, their own true life, but they could choose *which*. They could choose the gray parts. The parts already fading. They could pay in coin that was half-spent anyway and keep the gold hidden where even the bridge couldn't weigh it.

It was, Cyrus told themselves, simply good accounting. Allocate the loss where it does the least damage. Protect the asset that cannot be replaced.

The asset slept by the fire with driftwood smoke curling around them, and Cyrus sat up all night doing sums.

* * *

At gray dawn the river's hum deepened, as if the water itself woke reluctantly. Lior stretched, rubbed their eyes, and looked at the bridge with the expression of someone preparing to bargain for their life.

"All right," Lior said. "Here's how we do it. We pay together. Half each. Twenty strides apiece. Whatever it takes from me, it takes — I've survived losing things before, I can—"

"No," Cyrus said.

Lior blinked. "No?"

"You're light. The warden said so. You've got maybe a handful of seasons left in you that aren't already promised to your own songs. If you give twenty of them, you'll walk off that bridge as a stranger to yourself." Cyrus heard their own voice come out smooth, reasonable, final — the guildmaster's voice, Ondrey's voice, the voice that closed deals. "I'm heavy. I'm carrying ten years of other people's lives that were never mine to keep. Let the bridge eat the stolen goods. It's practically restitution."

Lior frowned. It was a good frown, a suspicious frown, and Cyrus had learned to respect it. "You'll let it take the stolen ones first? Swear it."

"I swear it," Cyrus said, and it was even true. Mostly true. True in the way that mattered this morning.

The warden waited at the bridge's foot, patient as erosion. Up close it smelled of cold paper and river mud, and its fogged face reflected them both, dimly, like dark water.

"Who pays?" it asked.

"I do," said Cyrus. "Both of us. One toll, two travelers. I carry enough for the pair."

The warden considered. The hum of the river seemed to lean in and listen.

"The bridge accepts a proxy," it said at last, "but the bridge chooses what it takes. Not the payer. That is the nature of years — you do not get to keep the best ones and offer the rest. The bridge reaches into the storehouse and takes what it finds." The fogged face tilted. "Do you consent?"

Every instinct Cyrus owned screamed at the fine print. *The bridge chooses.* Of course it did. Nothing in this country let you keep your illusions; that was rather the point of it.

But if the bridge chose blindly, then it might take anything — including the things Cyrus needed most. Unless the storehouse was arranged. Unless, before stepping onto the planks, Cyrus did what they had done their whole life: moved the valuable items to the back shelf, put the dross out front, made the inventory lie.

"I consent," Cyrus said.

"Cyrus." Lior's hand found their sleeve. "Wait. Are you sure you—"

"It's just bookkeeping," Cyrus said, and smiled, and hated how easily the smile came, and stepped forward.

The first plank took a summer.

Not the idea of a summer — the *summer*, whole and sudden, ripped out with a sound like a page tearing. Cyrus staggered. Heat, cicadas, the smell of cut grass, a festival in a market town when they were nine, a paper windmill dyed red — gone, all of it, leaving only the knowledge that such a summer had happened, a caption without a picture. The second stride took a winter. The third took a stranger's memory, the baker's pride, and Cyrus felt it leave with something like relief, a stone unloaded from a pack.

Stride four. Stride five. The bridge drank steadily, and Cyrus walked into the drinking, and behind them Lior shouted something — *stop, stop, I can hear it taking you* — and Cyrus raised one hand without turning around, a clerk's gesture, *the queue will be served in order.*

Stride ten took the soldier's childhood. Good riddance. Stride twelve took a rainy afternoon in the counting house, Ondrey teaching a young apprentice to tell false grief from real grief by the weight of it, and that one hurt going out, hurt like a tooth pulled, because it hadn't been stolen, it had been *given*, and Cyrus felt the difference in the extraction the way you feel the difference between a thief's cut and a surgeon's.

Stride fifteen took their mother's laugh.

Cyrus knew it was gone before they knew what was gone — a silence opened inside them shaped exactly like a loud unguarded sound like a door blowing open. They gripped the bridge rail. The rail was warm. The rail was made of someone's wedding day and it was warm, and Cyrus thought, absurdly, *at least someone's joy is holding me up.*

Stride twenty. Halfway. The river hummed below, enormous, patient.

And then the bridge stopped choosing the dross.

Cyrus felt it happen — felt the blind reach go past the front shelves, past the arranged inventory, past every decoy and sacrifice they'd spent the night positioning, and go *deep*, into the back of the storehouse, into the vault where the true gold was hidden, and Cyrus realized with a lurch that the warden had never been blind at all. The bridge chooses. The bridge had chosen. It had taken the trash first because trash was also weight, and now it wanted the treasure, and there was nothing in Cyrus's whole treacherous clever ledger-keeping soul that could stop it mid-stride.

Stride twenty-three took their mother's face.

Not the fading gray copy — the original. The true one, the one Cyrus had guarded through every danger, the one they'd moved to the deepest shelf precisely so that no power in heaven or the old country could reach it. The bridge reached it anyway. Bread rising under a cloth. Hands dusted white to the wrist. The exact arrangement of her features, the color of her eyes, the—

Gone.

Cyrus stood on the twenty-third plank of the Bridge of Borrowed Years and discovered that they could no longer see their mother's face. They could remember *that* she had one. They could remember loving it. The love remained, entire, ferocious, pointing at an empty chair. Somewhere in the world there was a woman who had made them bread and laughed like a door blowing open, and Cyrus would walk past her in the street and know nothing, feel everything, and never know why.

They kept walking. There was nothing else to do. The bridge demanded completion; a toll half-paid stranded you forever, that much the warden had said plainly. Stride thirty took the smell of the counting house. Stride thirty-four took their grandmother's name. Stride thirty-seven took the taste of the first meal they'd ever eaten alone as an adult, which they hadn't known they remembered until it left.

Stride forty.

The far bank. Grass, unmown, whispering. The sky full of wrong constellations going pale with morning. Cyrus stepped off the bridge and their legs held, which surprised them, because inside they felt like a house after a flood — walls standing, floors stripped, waterline visible on everything.

Lior hit them at a run.

"You *liar*—" Lior's fists beat against Cyrus's chest, not hard, not really trying to hurt, just trying to make contact with someone who had done an unforgivable kind thing. "You swore — you swore you'd pay in the stolen ones — I could *hear* it, I could hear it taking *you*, forty strides, you made me stand there and count them—"

"Yes," Cyrus said.

"Why?" Lior's face was wet. "Why would you—"

Because you're light, Cyrus almost said. Because you've already paid more than any ledger can hold. Because someone has to arrive on the far side of this country whole enough to sing the Last Door open, and it was never going to be me.

What Cyrus actually said was: "It's fine. Most of it was stolen goods anyway. Consider it restitution."

Lior stared at them. And Cyrus watched — with the last clear part of their mind, the part still keeping books — watch Lior look at them and *not quite recognize them*, just for a heartbeat, just a flicker, the way you look at a room that's been rearranged. Because forty seasons had gone out of Cyrus Amberdale, and though the name and the voice and the cleverness remained, some essential weight had gone out of them, and people who love you can feel the weight change even when they can't name what's missing.

"You're different," Lior said slowly. "Cyrus, what did it take?"

"Nothing important," said Cyrus Amberdale, who no longer knew what their mother looked like, and smiled the guildmaster's smile, and turned to lead the way into the deep old-country.

Behind them, the warden tilted its fogged head, and the river hummed on, and far down the valley — so far it might have been imagination — Cyrus saw riders. Three of them, small against the dawn, moving fast along the riverbank, following the only road there was.

Straight toward the bridge. Straight toward them.
