## 13. Doorsong

The wight had been waiting for them at the ford for three days.

Lior knew it the way they knew weather coming—not by evidence but by a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like struck flint on the back of the tongue. The river ran shallow and loud over its stones, and on the far bank stood a doorway with no house around it: a frame of black oak, taller than two men, standing alone in the reeds as if it had grown there, as if the house around it had been eaten by time and left only the part that mattered.

"It's been watching us since the bridge," Cyrus said, low.

"I know."

"You could have told me."

"You'd have wanted to talk about it." Lior kept walking. "I'm trying not to think about it too loudly."

They had crossed into the country that wasn't nine days ago, and every mile north the world grew more stubbornly itself. Roads that maps called rivers. Hills that hummed at dusk in a key Lior's bones recognized before their ears did. And doors—doors everywhere, standing free of walls, some rotted to lacework, some painted fresh and bright, all of them closed, all of them *waiting*. The innkeeper in the last village had fed them for nothing because singers are owed, and when Lior asked what she meant she had looked at them with an old woman's terrible patience and said, *You'll find out at the ford.*

The wight was not what Lior expected. It did not step out from behind the doorframe; it was simply there one moment, the way a note is there once you've stopped listening for it—a figure made of the space between things, tall and narrow, wearing darkness the way other people wore coats. Where a face should have been there was a suggestion of one, like a face seen through fogged glass, and when it spoke, its voice came from everywhere the wind touched.

*You are the one who sings places awake.*

Lior's hand found Cyrus's sleeve without asking permission. "And you're the thing that lives in doors."

*I live in thresholds. Doors are only where you keep me.* The wight drifted closer, or perhaps the distance between them simply decided to be smaller. *You opened a door in the palace vaults. You have been opening them since you were small enough to fit under tables. You have never once understood what you were doing.*

"That's not true," Lior said, stung. "I sing, and doors open. I know how it works."

*You know how a hammer works,* said the wight. *Tell me what happens inside the nail.*

* * *

They made camp within sight of the doorframe, because the wight refused to speak anywhere else—it said, with something like distaste, that open ground made its voice thin—and because Cyrus, after one long look at the black oak, said quietly, "If we're going to learn something that changes everything, I'd rather be sitting down."

So they sat. Cyrus built a fire with the economy of someone who had done it while being hunted, and Lior wrapped their hands around a tin cup of nettle tea and waited for the lesson they were suddenly, horribly sure they didn't want.

"Start with the nail," Lior said.

The wight settled across the fire from them, or arranged itself so that the firelight passed through it without warming anything. *When you sing to a door, what do you sing?*

Lior frowned. "Whatever the place wants. That's how it feels. The vault under the palace wanted—" They stopped. Swallowed. "It wanted morning. It wanted bells. It wanted the whole city waking up on a day when nothing had gone wrong yet. So I sang that."

*Yes. And why did it want that?*

"Because... places remember?"

*Places do not remember,* the wight said, and there was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. *Places are stone and timber and river-mud. They cannot remember. They can only be remembered. When you sang to the vault, you were not calling up its memory. You were remembering it—so completely, so fiercely, that the place had no choice but to agree with you. A door opens, little singer, when a place remembers itself. And places only ever remember themselves through you.*

The fire cracked. Somewhere downriver a heron complained about the night.

"So the old country," Cyrus said slowly. Lior watched their fingers tighten on the cup. "The villages that still sing the old songs. The roads that aren't on any map."

*Survive because they are sung,* the wight agreed. *Every harvest song is a nail holding a roof on. Every lullaby is a wall. Your kingdom burned the country and then discovered, to its fury, that a country is not made of land. It is made of people insisting, in harmony, that it is still there.*

"And the Hunt," Lior said. Their voice came out flat. "The Tuner. Emptying the gifted."

*Is not madness,* the wight said. *It is strategy. A crown that has unmade a country must silence everyone who might sing it back. Your King understands doors better than you do. He has simply chosen to stand on the wrong side of all of them.*

Lior set down the cup because their hands had begun to shake, and they didn't want Cyrus to see, and they knew Cyrus would see anyway, because Cyrus saw everything—that was the whole architecture of the person, ledgers and double-ledgers, watching, recording, keeping. "Fine," Lior said. "I remember places until they remember themselves. That's the gift. That's the whole of it. Why does it matter?"

*Because,* said the wight, *you have been paying for it your entire life, and no one has shown you the bill.*

* * *

Later—much later, when Cyrus had finally slept, curled against their pack with one hand loose near the knife the way hunted people sleep—the wight took Lior to the doorframe and showed them.

*Touch it,* it said. *And listen with the part of you that sings.*

The oak was cold and older than kingdoms. Lior pressed both palms against it and closed their eyes, and the wight's voice came from inside the wood now, from inside their own skull, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck.

*Count your songs.*

"I don't—"

*Count them. Every door you have ever opened. Every place you have sung awake. Begin with the first.*

Lior began with the first, because there was no refusing that voice. They were six years old, locked in a cellar by an uncle who drank, singing to the dark because singing was the only thing that didn't make the dark worse—and the cellar door had swung open, and their uncle had stared at it, and Lior had learned the first great secret of their life: that doors loved them.

Then the second. The schoolhouse, when the master locked the music room and threw away the key, and Lior sang the room's own memory of being open until the lock forgot its purpose. Then the third, the fourth, the funeral in Chapter—no, the funeral in Marrowfield, where they'd made forty mourners remember a happiness that never happened; the guild commission; the palace vaults; the escape door in the guard corridor, the one they'd heard singing before it existed—

*Seventeen,* Lior whispered. "Seventeen doors."

*Now,* said the wight, *for each one, tell me what you have lost.*

"That's not—"

*The cellar door. What did you lose after?*

Lior opened their mouth to say *nothing*, and found the word wouldn't come, because something behind the word had shifted, the way furniture shifts in a house when you're away. They thought about being six. They thought about the cellar. They thought about their mother—

Their mother.

There had been a mother. There was a shape where a mother should be, warm and definite, a voice, hands, a smell of bread or lamp oil or rain—but when Lior reached for her face the way you reach for a word on the tip of the tongue, the reaching came back empty. Not gone. *Hollowed.* A room in them swept clean, with the outline of the furniture still pale on the floorboards where it used to stand.

"No," Lior said aloud.

*One memory per door,* the wight said, quiet as snowfall. *That is the toll. It is not collected by me. It is not collected by anyone. It is simply the nature of the work: you cannot make a place remember itself without spending memory, because memory is the only coin a place accepts. Each doorsong takes one. Always the same kind—not the largest, not the dearest, but the one most tangled in the place you woke. The cellar took your mother's face, because the cellar was where you first needed her and she did not come.*

Lior's knees went. They caught themselves against the doorframe, and the wood hummed under their palms like a struck string, sympathetic, almost sorry.

"The funerals," they heard themselves say. "The ones I sang for hire. I gave those people memories of days that never happened, and I—"

*Paid for each one out of your own past. Yes. You have been selling pieces of yourself for years and calling it a living. The mourners walked home rich, and you walked home poorer by one true thing apiece, and you never noticed because the wound closes smooth, the way water closes over a stone. That is the mercy and the horror of it. You cannot miss what you cannot remember missing.*

Lior turned around. Across the dead fire, Cyrus was awake—had been awake, Lior realized, for some unknowable length of time, propped on one elbow, watching with an expression Lior had no name for. Not pity. Pity would have been easier.

"How many?" Cyrus asked softly.

"Seventeen."

"And you felt none of them go?"

"I felt *something*," Lior said. "After the vault, I couldn't remember the name of my first street. I thought I'd just—I thought it was fear. Fear does that, doesn't it? Scours you out a little?"

Cyrus got up and crossed the ground between them, and did not touch them, which was its own kind of touch. "What else is already gone? Can you tell?"

Lior tried. It was like running a tongue along a row of teeth and finding the gaps: the name of the street, yes. Their mother's face. A winter—they could feel the absence of a winter, a specific winter, cold and important, gone. Something about a promise, made or received, they couldn't tell which; there was a groove in them shaped exactly like a promise, worn smooth, empty. And one more, near the center, that they refused to look at directly, because looking at it felt like leaning over a well.

"There's a hole," Lior said at last, "and I think whatever was in it, I loved it."

Neither of them spoke. Downriver, the heron cried again, and the sound fell into the silence like a coin into deep water.

*Now you understand the bill,* the wight said. *And now you understand why your king fears you, and why he is right to. A singer who knows the price can choose which memories to spend. A singer who knows the price can spend deliberately—can walk up to the Last Door and pay, note by note, until nothing is left of them but the song. The Last Door does not take one memory, little singer. It takes the singer entire. That is why it stands shut. That is why it has always stood shut. No one who loves anything has ever been willing to become nothing in order to open it.*

"The Last Door," Cyrus repeated. Their voice had gone very careful, ledger-voice, the voice they used when the numbers were bad. "Where is it?"

*North,* said the wight. *Through the reliquary valley, where the crown keeps everything it has confiscated—every stolen gift, every tuned instrument, every memory it has pried out of a traitor's head and bottled. Your king stores his plunder at the threshold of the one exit he cannot lock, because he believes proximity is control.* A pause, and something moved in the fogged-glass face that might have been, on a human, a smile. *He is wrong. But he will send soldiers anyway. Soldiers are what kings send instead of understanding.*

"When?" Lior asked.

*Soon. They know you are in the country that isn't. They have known since the bridge-warden sold your friend's years north of the river—years leave a scent, even spent ones.* The wight began to thin, unraveling into the night air the way smoke unravels, and its last words came from everywhere and nowhere, from every threshold in the dark: *Rest, singer. Count what remains. And when you sing again—choose, this time, what you are willing to lose. That is the whole of the craft. Everything else is just breath.*

Then it was gone, and the doorframe stood alone in the reeds, and Lior stood with both hands still flat against the cold oak, counting backward through their own life like a merchant closing shop, itemizing everything that might already be missing.

Behind them, very quietly, Cyrus said: "Lior. There's something I have to show you in the ledger."

Lior turned. In the last coals of the fire, Cyrus's face was the color of ash, and their hand was already reaching for the book they never let out of arm's reach.

"It can wait till morning," Lior said.

"No," Cyrus said. "It really can't."
