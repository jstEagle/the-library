## 14. Ambush at the Reliquary

The reliquary had been a chapel once. You could still see it in the bones of the place—the ribs of vaulting overhead, the lancet windows gone dark with grime, the font in the entryway filled now not with holy water but with sand, and in the sand, keys. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Keys to doors that no longer existed, confiscated from houses that no longer existed, gathered up by clerks who believed that if you locked enough doors behind enough locks, a country would stay put.

Cyrus stood in the doorway of the nave and felt their gift wake like a dog lifting its head.

"Here," they said quietly. "This is it."

Lior came up beside them, hood back, breath fogging in the cold that lived inside stone buildings whether or not winter permitted it. "It's a church."

"It was." Cyrus stepped over the threshold and the feeling sharpened—familiar, awful, like walking into a room where someone had died recently. "Do you feel that? Under your feet?"

Lior paused. Their face did something complicated. "It's like standing on a beehive," they said. "If the bees were screaming very politely."

"That's the gifts." Cyrus moved down the aisle, boots silent on stone worn smooth by centuries of kneeling. "Confiscated ones. The crown doesn't destroy what it takes. It stores it. There will be a catalog somewhere—there's always a catalog—shelves of jars, boxes, reliquaries proper, whatever the local quartermaster decided was appropriate. Every gifted person caught since the first Hunt. Everything they were, filed and dusted."

"And we're breaking in because—"

"Because Ondrey said the northern reliquaries kept duplicates of the guild's own records. Insurance. If the true ledger is ever seized, the crown's copy proves nothing without the originals." Cyrus knelt at the chancel steps and ran their fingers along the seam where step met riser. "And because I need to know if my name is in one of these boxes yet."

Lior crouched beside them. In the gray light their eyes looked like water over slate. "You think they've already taken you?"

"I think the Hunt has been running for four months, and I think Serah Vane is thorough, and I think there is a version of me in a jar somewhere with everything I've ever stolen poured out of me like wine from a bottle." Cyrus found the seam, pressed, felt the faint give of old craftsmanship. "I'd like to meet her. The jar-me. I have questions."

The panel swung inward on a breath of air that smelled of cedar and cold iron.

* * *

The crypt below was not large, but it was deep, and it was full.

Shelves climbed the walls into darkness. On them sat vessels of every kind—stoppered flasks, sealed clay pots, lacquered boxes, lead canisters stamped with the crown's double-tower sigil, and here and there, incongruously, jam jars and tobacco tins, because confiscation does not wait for proper equipment. Each bore a paper label in a clerk's careful hand: a name, a date, a parish, and a number. The numbers ran high. The numbers ran very high.

Cyrus walked the aisles slowly, reading labels by the light of a shuttered lantern, and the gift under their skin read along with them, tasting each vessel the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. Grief here. A whole childhood there. Somewhere on the third shelf, something so old and patient that Cyrus's hand flinched away from it without asking permission.

"There," Lior said softly. They were standing before a shelf near the far wall, where the vessels changed character—older, better made, labeled in a different ink. "These are guild records. Look at the dates."

Cyrus joined them. The labels confirmed it: Vellum Guild provenance marks, decades of them, the crown's clerks having faithfully catalogued every cache seized in every raid since before Cyrus was born. And there, in the middle of the row, a box of black walnut with brass fittings, labeled in Ondrey's own slanted hand—a duplicate ledger, the insurance copy, exactly where the old man had sworn it would be.

Cyrus lifted it down. It was heavier than paper should be. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the duplicate ledger and beneath it a second bundle, and Cyrus knew before unwrapping it what the second bundle was, because their fingers remembered the shape of it through the cloth.

Memories. Loose ones. Guild stock, seized in the same raid, never sold.

"Cyrus." Lior's voice had gone strange. "Don't."

"I'm just looking."

"You're doing the thing where you look with your hands."

Cyrus almost smiled. Almost. Then the lantern guttered—not from wind; there was no wind down here—and every hair on their arms rose at once, and they understood, three full seconds before the sound arrived, that they had made a mistake.

The mistake arrived as bootsteps on the chapel floor above. Many bootsteps. Moving in the disciplined silence of people trained to walk loudly only when it served them.

"Lantern off," Cyrus breathed.

Darkness. In it, Cyrus took Lior's wrist and drew them both behind the shelving at the room's far end, into the angle between wall and rack where the shadows pooled thickest. Below the floorboards, the searching began—measured, methodical, the scrape of furniture, the ring of a pry bar against the font of keys. A voice, low and bored: "Crypt access?"

"Behind the chancel. Sealed from below."

"Then they're already down there."

Cyrus counted the footsteps as they spread across the ceiling. Six. Maybe seven. Not Serah's team—Serah worked with hounds and silence, and this was neither. This was the Tuner's *second* team, the one even guild rumor spoke of reluctantly: the men who did the taking when the Tuner herself was elsewhere. Led by a captain named Brannoc Hale, who had once, according to Ondrey, tuned his own brother and requested the assignment.

The chancel panel opened above. Lantern light spilled down the stair in a hard yellow wedge.

"Down slow," said a voice that could only belong to Hale. "They're thieves. Thieves hide. Sweep the shelves."

Cyrus put their lips against Lior's ear. "Back wall. There'll be a coal chute or a drain—chapels always had one for the crypt-keeper. When I say, you run and you don't stop."

"When *you* say? What are you going to be doing?"

"Buying the sentence."

Lior's grip closed on their arm, hard. "Cyrus—"

"Count of thirty," Cyrus whispered, and stepped out of the dark.

* * *

Later, Cyrus would try to reconstruct those minutes and find them strangely missing, the way a dream loses its middle. What remained was this:

Lantern light, sudden and total, six soldiers turning as one, and Cyrus standing in the open with their hands visible and their voice pitched to carry, saying, "Guild business, gentlemen, I'll come quietly"—and Hale, a broad man with a face like a closed door, smiling without any part of his face agreeing to it, saying, "No, I don't think you will. Kneel."

And Cyrus kneeling, because kneeling bought seconds, and seconds were what Lior needed—and then Hale drawing the tuning rod from its case, a slim silver instrument that hummed at a frequency Cyrus felt in their teeth, and saying, almost kindly, "The Tuner wants you alive and empty. Those are different things. I only need the second one."

And the rod coming down toward Cyrus's brow—

And Cyrus's hands coming up on instinct, on the oldest instinct there is, the one older than conscience—

And skin meeting skin. Cyrus's palm flat against Hale's bare throat, where the collar of his uniform gaped.

The world went white.

Not pain. Worse than pain: *inflow*. Cyrus had taken memories before—small ones, chosen ones, weighed and paid for. This was a floodgate kicked open. Hale's life came through Cyrus's arm like a river through a burst dam, and Cyrus, who had spent a lifetime learning to take gently, could do nothing at all but hold on and drown.

A boy on a farm in the east, learning to whistle through a blade of grass. A mother's hands kneading bread, flour to the wrists. First snow. First blood—a nose, punched by a cousin, laughing through it. A dog named Sorrel who slept against his back every winter night for eleven years. A girl he'd loved at sixteen and never told, whose name was Wren, whose laugh he could still hear, whose face he was losing even as Cyrus gained it. His brother. God, his brother—two boys in an orchard, one apple between them, splitting it down the middle, *you take the sweet half, Bran, you're growing*—and then the academy, and the oath, and the day he stood in a white room and watched his brother's eyes go quiet and empty and obedient, and told himself it was mercy, it was mercy, it was—

Cyrus tore their hand away.

Hale dropped like a puppet with cut strings. Not dead. Cyrus could feel him at the edge of their awareness the way you feel a bruise—a vast, hollowed absence, a man-shaped room with all the furniture carried out. Breathing. Blinking at the ceiling. Knowing nothing, not even that he had known anything.

The other soldiers had frozen. Cyrus understood why a half-second later: they had seen it happen. Whatever the tuning rod did slowly and clinically, they had just watched done in a heartbeat, by touch, by a thief in a stolen coat—and every one of them was suddenly, vividly aware of his own bare skin.

"Run," Cyrus rasped, and heard their own voice come out wrong, layered, a boy's voice under theirs saying words it didn't know. "*Run.*"

They ran. All of them. Boots hammering up the stair, the yellow light swinging wild, and then Lior's hand finding Cyrus's in the dark, and the coal chute—it was there, of course it was there, chapels always had one—and then cold air and ash-scented night and the two of them stumbling out into the ruin of an old garden behind the chapel, alive, uncaught, free.

Free. Cyrus knelt in the dead nettles and was sick.

* * *

Lior held them through it. That was the thing Lior did—arrived exactly when the arithmetic got ugly and simply stayed, arms around Cyrus's shoulders, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a knife.

When the shaking stopped, Cyrus sat back against a crumbling garden wall and became aware, gradually, of the weight.

That was the only word for it. Weight. Something enormous had settled into them, low behind the sternum, dense as ore. They could feel pieces of it distinctly if they reached: snow. Bread dough. A dog's warm spine. A girl called Wren. An apple split in two. A brother in an orchard, and after the orchard, a white room, and after the white room, years of a man telling himself a story about mercy until the story wore a groove in him deep enough to walk through blind.

"He gave it to me," Cyrus said. Their voice sounded borrowed. "I didn't—I didn't mean to take all of it. I've never taken a whole person before. Just the edge of one. A hour. An afternoon. This is—" They pressed a fist below their ribs, as if they could press it deeper, quieter. "He whistled through grass. He had a dog. He loved someone and never said. And then he emptied his own brother and built his whole life on top of the hole, and now all of that is *in me*, Lior, and I can't give it back because there's no him left to give it back *to*."

Lior knelt in the nettles in front of them. In the moonlight their face was very still, and very pale, and Cyrus watched them arrive at the understanding the way you watch someone wade into cold water—one inch, another inch, until it takes their breath.

"I saw you," Lior said quietly. "In the lantern light. For a moment your face wasn't yours. It was his. You were wearing him."

"I know."

"You took a man's entire childhood, Cyrus. His whole—" Lior stopped. Started again, gentler, which was worse. "The ledger. The column of names. I asked you once how many entries it had, and you wouldn't tell me, and I let it go because I thought—I thought you were keeping memories safe. Like the farmer. Like the dying man on the road. I thought that was what this was."

"It was."

"And now?"

Cyrus opened their mouth to answer and found, to their genuine surprise, that they did not know. The weight behind their sternum shifted, settled, and it did not feel like safekeeping. It did not feel like theft, either. It felt like *possession*—the good kind and the bad kind braided together past separating. Somewhere inside them, a boy was whistling through a blade of grass, and Cyrus was the only place in the world he existed anymore, and some cold practical corner of their mind had already begun to think of that as *value*.

That corner of their mind frightened them more than Hale's soldiers had.

"Now I don't know," Cyrus said. "That's the honest answer. Now I'm carrying a man who isn't anywhere, and he's heavy, and part of me wants to put him down and part of me—" They laughed, and it came out cracked. "Part of me wants to keep him. Because he's mine now. Because I paid for him. Because that's what I am, Lior. I finally saw it tonight, in the lantern light, on your face. I'm not a keeper. I'm not a thief. I'm a *hoard*. I'm a room filling up with other people, and the door only opens inward, and someday there won't be enough of me left to hold the rest, and it will all come down, and everyone I've ever taken will come down with it."

The garden was silent. Somewhere beyond the wall, the chapel bell—ropeless, tongueless—shifted on its beam in the wind and made no sound at all.

Lior reached out and took Cyrus's hand. Turned it over. Looked at the palm as though they might see Hale's childhood written there, and maybe they could; Cyrus's skin felt marked, branded from within.

"The wight told me singers pay for doors with their own memories," Lior said. "Every song, a piece of me. I've been trying not to count what's left." Their thumb moved across Cyrus's palm, slow, tracing nothing. "And you pay with other people's. Do you understand what we are, Cyrus? We're not two travelers. We're two debts, walking north, pretending to be a person apiece."

"Then we'd better reach the Door before the collectors do."

But Lior didn't smile. They were looking past Cyrus's shoulder, at the dark garden, and their expression had gone taut and listening, the way it did when they heard a door that no one else could hear.

"Cyrus," they said. "How many soldiers went up those stairs?"

"Six. Seven. Why?"

"Because I can hear eight hearts in that chapel. And the eighth one knows this song." Lior stood, pulling Cyrus up with them, and their voice dropped to something barely above the wind. "She's been standing in the nave this whole time. She let the others chase us out. She wanted us outside the walls, alone, where the taking couldn't be witnessed."

Cyrus felt the new weight in their chest turn over like something waking.

Out past the garden wall, at the edge of hearing, someone began to hum a tune that Cyrus had never heard before and recognized instantly anyway—because Lior had sung it once, half-asleep by a fire, and called it *the duet*, and refused to say who they'd sung it with.

"She remembers loving you," Cyrus said quietly.

"She remembers *that* she did." Lior's hand found theirs in the dark, grip fierce. "Not why. Not when. She's a hole shaped like me, Cyrus, walking on legs—and she's between us and the road north."

Above the ruined garden, clouds slid off the moon, and the nettles threw long shadows, and somewhere in the dark the humming stopped—as if she had heard them listening.
