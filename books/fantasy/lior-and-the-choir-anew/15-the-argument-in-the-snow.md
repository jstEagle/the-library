## 15. The Argument in the Snow

**Lior**

The snow began an hour past dusk, fat and silent, the kind of snow that erases roads as it falls. Lior watched it bury their backtrail with something like gratitude and something like dread. Erasure was a thing they understood too well now.

They had sheltered in a shepherd's bothy above the tree line—dry-stone walls, a roof of turf, a hearth that Cyrus coaxed alight with hands that no longer trembled from cold but from something else. Lior had been watching those hands for days.

Cyrus counted things now. Not coins—they'd spent the last of those at the bridge—but everything else. Steps to the well. Splinters in the doorframe. The number of times Lior coughed. And behind the counting, behind the gray eyes that used to be all ledger-precision and dry humor, there was a *fullness*. A crowdedness. Lior had sung doors open before; they knew what a threshold looked like when too much pressed against it from one side.

Cyrus was becoming that.

"You're staring," Cyrus said, without looking up from the fire.

"You're hoarding."

That got their attention. The poker stilled. "I'm *carrying*."

"A soldier's childhood." Lior heard their own voice go flat and hard, the way it did when they were afraid. "A farmer's last morning. Half a bridge-warden's toll you paid in secret—don't look surprised, I heard you weeping in your sleep and calling for someone whose name you wouldn't tell me after. You're not carrying anymore, Cyrus. You're *storing*."

The fire popped. Snow hissed faintly against the turf roof, soft as breath.

"And what would you have me do?" Cyrus said quietly. "Drop them by the road? Give a dead soldier's childhood back to the snow?"

"Give them *back*." Lior stood. Their knees cracked; they were so tired. "You started this with a ledger, remember? Debts and credits. Every memory you hold belongs to someone. Some of them belong to people still alive, still walking these hills, hollowed out and humming. We're marching toward them anyway."

"I know where this is going." Cyrus rose too, slow, deliberate, the way they did everything now—like a person conserving themselves for some final expenditure. "You want to split up."

Lior hadn't planned to say it tonight. But it was true, and true things in Lior's mouth had always had a way of becoming songs whether they wanted them or not. "Yes."

"Say the rest of it, then. Since we're being honest."

"You're becoming a hoard." The words came out colder than Lior meant, so they made themselves finish. "And I'm becoming a key. Every doorsong takes a piece of me—my mother's kitchen is gone, Cyrus. My first winter in the city. The name of the street I grew up on. I am being *spent*, coin by coin, and you are being *filled*, sack by sack, and neither of us is going to survive the Door if we keep traveling as one weight. Split up. You take the ledger north to the settlement. I go on alone to the Door. Two trails in the snow instead of one. The Tuner can't follow both."

Cyrus stood very still. Firelight moved on their face and none of it seemed to reach their eyes.

"No."

"It's arithmetic—"

"I don't care. No."

"You've never once refused arithmetic in your life!"

"This isn't arithmetic." Cyrus's voice cracked down the middle, and Lior saw, with a lurch, how thin the wall behind it had gotten. "This is—" They stopped. Pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. When they spoke again it was barely above the fire's whisper. "You don't get to hand me a ledger entry and call it love, Lior. *Two trails instead of one.* As if you're a column of numbers I could balance out and walk away from clean."

Something in Lior's chest went tight and hot. "Then say what it is."

"Don't ask me that."

"*Say it.*"

**Cyrus**

The snow outside had buried the world, and inside the bothy there was only the fire and Lior's face, furious and frightened and lit gold at the edges, and Cyrus felt every stolen life inside them lean toward that face like plants toward a window.

They had taken a soldier's childhood two nights ago and it sat in them like a stone with a boy inside it—a boy running through wheat fields, a boy afraid of dogs, a boy who grew up into a man who burned villages for a crown. Cyrus carried him everywhere now. They carried the farmer's last morning, the smell of cut hay and blood. They carried seasons they'd paid away and couldn't account for, holes in their own history shaped like a mother's voice.

And underneath all of it, older than the ledger, older than the guild: one entry marked *C.A.*, taken from themselves so long ago they no longer knew what it held. Sometimes, lately, when Lior sang—even just humming while walking—something behind that sealed door stirred. Something that felt like this moment. Like firelight. Like fury that was only fear wearing armor.

"Lior." Their own voice sounded foreign. "I refuse because I have done the arithmetic. Believe me, no one alive has done more of it. And every sum comes out the same: apart, you die alone at that Door, or I die alone in a ditch with a ledger full of other people's lives and nothing of my own. Together—" They laughed, and it came out wrong. "Together the sums don't work either. That's the joke. There is no column where we both come through. I've checked. I check it every night."

"Then why—"

"Because I would rather fail *with* you than succeed anywhere else." It came out of them like a splinter leaving flesh, fast and bleeding. "There. Is that what you wanted? Not arithmetic. Not strategy. I stay because when you sing, even your humming, even off-key in the rain—the thing I took from myself, the one I can't open—I think it's *you*. I think I gave it up years ago, before I ever met you, and every day since has been me trying to buy it back without knowing its price. And I will not split up because you are the only entry in any book I have ever kept that I refuse to lose."

Silence. The fire settled. Somewhere under the turf roof, snow slid and thumped.

Lior stared at them. All the fury had gone out of their face and left something naked behind, and Cyrus realized, with distant horror, that they had just said aloud the thing they had been carrying longer than any stolen memory—and that it hadn't been in the plan, hadn't been in any ledger, and could not now be un-said.

"That's not fair," Lior whispered.

"No," Cyrus agreed. "It isn't."

"You don't get to say that *now*. In the middle of a fight. In the middle of *snow*, in the middle of—" Their voice broke. They turned away, arms wrapped around themselves, shoulders shaking in a way that had nothing to do with cold. "I was going to leave while you slept. I had it all worked out. I'd leave the wight's warning carved on the doorpost so you'd know the toll. I'd—"

"You were going to *leave*?"

"Of course I was!" Lior whirled back, eyes wet and blazing. "You're turning into a vault, Cyrus. A locked room full of everyone but yourself. And I'm a key wearing down to the stem. One of us has to stop spending before there's nothing left to spend, and it was always going to be me, because you—" They stopped. Swallowed. Started over, quieter, wrecked. "Because I can't watch it. I can't watch you give away your mother's face and call it a toll. I can't watch you fill up with strangers until there's no Cyrus Amberdale left, and then stand at that Door and pay *that* too, and smile about the accounting."

Cyrus crossed the space between them in three steps.

Later, neither would be able to say who moved first—whether it was Cyrus reaching or Lior breaking forward—but they collided like weather fronts, and Lior's fists beat twice against Cyrus's chest, not hard, and then stopped beating, and then were hands gripping fistfuls of coat, and Cyrus's forehead came down against theirs, and the fire threw their joined shadow huge against the stone.

"I'm not letting go," Cyrus said into the small space between them. "Not to spare you. Not for strategy. If you walk out into that snow alone, I will follow your trail until it kills me, and I will die full of other people's lives and one true thing, and the true thing will be this: I love you. Past the ledger. Past the Door. Past whatever's waiting on the other side of it."

Lior made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "*Now* you say it. At the worst possible moment. In a shepherd's hut. With a bounty on us and a Tuner on our trail and—" Their breath hitched. "Say it again anyway."

"I love you."

"Again."

"I love you, Lior. Choirless, door-breaking, impossible—"

Lior kissed them, and it tasted like salt and woodsmoke and the end of an argument that had really been a surrender on both sides, and outside the snow kept falling, patient and total, erasing every path but the one between them.

* * *

**Lior**

They lay afterward in the fire's declining light, Cyrus's heartbeat under Lior's ear, steady as a metronome. Lior should have been sleeping. They had maybe four hours of dark left and a pass to cross. Instead they listened to that heartbeat and thought about keys and locks and doors, and about the wight's words: *the singer pays in what they cannot replace.*

"Cyrus."

"Mm."

"The entry. *C.A.*" They felt the heartbeat change—slight, but Lior had spent their life listening for changes in sound. "When you took it. Do you remember anything about the taking? Where you were. How old you were."

A long silence. Snow ticked against the stones.

"I remember the ledger line," Cyrus said at last. "My own handwriting. I remember deciding it was necessary—that keeping it was safer than knowing it. And I remember..." A pause so long Lior thought they'd fallen asleep. "Lately, when you hum. There's a shape under the humming. Like a room behind a door. And I think—I think whatever's in that room, I put it there myself, on purpose, and I was *right* to, and I have never been more afraid of anything than I am of opening it."

"What could scare you that much?"

"I don't know. That's what scares me." Cyrus's arm tightened around Lior's shoulders. "What if I took it because knowing it would have stopped me from becoming the kind of person who survives? What if it's the conscience I cut out so the ledgers would balance?"

"Or," Lior said softly, "it's the reason you refused the widow her fee. The reason you kept the farmer's morning safe instead of selling it. The reason you're lying in a freezing hut with a hunted singer instead of counting coin in Vellum Hall." They tilted their head up. "Maybe you didn't cut it out, Cyrus. Maybe you *saved* it. The way you save everything worth keeping."

Cyrus didn't answer. But their heartbeat slowed, and eased, and Lior chose to believe that was an answer.

Sleep finally took them both, tangled together like a chord.

* * *

**Cyrus**

Cyrus woke to wrongness.

Not a sound—the snow swallowed sound. A *texture*. The silence outside had changed, the way a room changes when someone stands in it breathing. They were upright with a knife in their hand before their mind finished waking, and Lior was already awake too, eyes wide, one finger raised to their lips.

Then Lior's head tilted, the way it did when they heard doors no one else could hear. Their lips shaped words without voice:

*Riders. Many. Below the ridge.*

Cyrus moved to the gap between the stones where the smoke-vent let in a thread of gray dawn. Far below, at the edge of the white field, a column of horsemen moved along the frozen river road—crown black and steel, and at their head a banner Cyrus knew from proclamations nailed to every church door since the Hunt began. But these riders weren't searching. They weren't fanning out across the slopes.

They were riding, unhurried and inevitable, toward the hollowed settlement. Toward the valley where the emptied ones hummed their fragments in the ruins of a chapel. Toward the people Cyrus had promised, silently, on the long march, to make whole.

"They're not following us," Lior breathed beside them, horror dawning in time with Cyrus's own. "They know where we're going. They're going to get there *first*."

Cyrus's hand found the ledger-bag at their belt—half-empty now, lighter every day as they returned what they'd taken, and heavier somehow for it. Inside were names. Owners. People waiting in that valley for their own lives to come home to them.

The riders crested the far rise, small as ink strokes, and began to descend.

"We're three hours behind them," Cyrus said. "Over the pass, in fresh snow."

"Then we'd better start singing," Lior said, "because I know a door through the mountain—and I know exactly what it costs."

They looked at each other, and Cyrus saw the question in Lior's eyes, the same arithmetic they'd fought about all night: *what piece of you is this song worth?*

Cyrus closed their hand around Lior's.

"Pay it," they said. "We'll grieve later. Together."
