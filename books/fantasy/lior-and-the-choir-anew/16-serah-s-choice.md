## 16. Serah's Choice

The orders came at dawn, written in the King's own hand and sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

*By decree of the Hunt: the settlement known as Hollowmere, harboring the tuned and those who shelter them, is declared in collaboration with enemies of the crown. The Tuner will proceed at first light and render judgment. No survivors of rank. No records kept.*

Serah Vane read it three times by candlelight while her hounds whined at the tent flap, sensing something in her hands she could not name. Judgment. That was the word they used now instead of the older word, the true one. *Emptying.* She had carried out a hundred judgments and never once flinched, because flinching was a luxury the crown did not issue along with its seals.

She folded the order and put it in her breast pocket, over her heart, where she kept all the things that weighed nothing and cost everything.

Then she rode for Hollowmere.

* * *

The settlement sat in a fold of hills like a secret someone had tried to bury and failed. From the ridge above it, Serah could see the smoke of cookfires rising straight in the windless morning, and hear—faintly, absurdly—singing.

Not singing, exactly. Humming. The hollowed hummed the way rivers ran: without decision, without end. Fragments of lives they could no longer hold whole, leaking out of them note by note. She had heard it before, in the tuning houses of the capital, in the long wards where the emptied sat in rows like instruments nobody played anymore. It had never troubled her. Sound without memory was only noise.

But this morning, on the ridge, with the order against her heart, the humming reached into her chest and found something.

A tune. A small one. Six notes, rising, then falling away like a hand letting go.

She knew it.

That was the impossible part. She knew it the way you know your own front door in the dark—not the name of it, not where she'd learned it, but the shape of it, worn smooth as a stair by years of climbing. Her throat tightened around it. Her hands, gloved in black leather, went cold on the reins.

*Tuning takes what is taken,* her instructors had said. *It does not give anything back.*

And yet there it was, six notes old as rain, humming up out of a village of people who had nothing left to lose, and Serah Vane, King's Tuner, first blade of the Hunt, sat her horse on the ridge and could not remember why she was crying.

* * *

They had built cages. Of course they had; the crown's engineers traveled with the second team, and cages were cheaper than mercy. Wooden frames wrapped in wire, set in rows at the edge of the settlement, and the hollowed herded into them overnight—the ones who had wandered too close to the road, the ones some neighbor had reported, the ones who hummed too loudly. Forty-one souls, according to the tally sheet. Serah counted them herself from horseback, because counting was the last honest thing left in her work.

An officer met her at the settlement's edge, saluting smartly. Captain Brase, second team, a man with a good jaw and no imagination.

"Tuner Vane. We've held position as ordered. The collaborators are gathered in the meetinghouse." He gestured toward a low stone building where, Serah noted, a thin line of chimney smoke still rose. People were cooking breakfast in there. People were making tea, believing that morning was an ordinary country they still lived in.

"And the tuned?"

"In the pens, as you see. They're docile. They always are." He smiled, and it was meant to be reassuring. "Easier than the guild thieves, eh? These don't even beg."

Serah looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere behind his eyes, she supposed, there had once been a boy who wept at funerals or fed stray dogs or did whatever boys do before the world files their edges down. She wondered what six notes lived in him, waiting.

"Captain," she said, "who gave the order to build the pens?"

"Your office, Tuner. Standard containment protocol."

"My office," she repeated. Yes. Her office. Her signature, probably, on some form she did not remember signing, in some week that had dissolved like sugar in rain. The crown used her name the way water used a riverbed—shaping everything, remembering nothing.

"Open the pens," she said.

Brase blinked. "Tuner?"

"I wish to inspect the prisoners before judgment. Individually. Unwire the gates."

"That's not—" He caught himself, which was wise. "Of course, Tuner. Shall I have my men—"

"No." She dismounted, unhurried, settling her coat so the order in her breast pocket lay flat against her ribs. "I'll do it myself. And clear your men from this field. All of them. Inspection protocols are sealed; you know that."

He didn't know that. There were no such protocols. But he knew her reputation, and reputation, in the absence of memory, is the only self a person can wear, so he saluted again and barked the withdrawal, and within minutes Serah Vane stood alone in a trampled field with forty-one hollowed souls watching her through wire, and the morning held its breath.

* * *

She opened the first cage because her hands decided before she did.

The latch was simple—a hook and eye, soldier's work—and when it swung free, the woman inside did not run. That was the thing no one understood about the hollowed, the thing that made them easy to cage and unbearable to look at: they had nowhere to go. Running requires a destination, and a destination requires a past, a reason, a face you are going toward. The woman simply stood, blinking in the sudden width of the open gate, humming her fragment—four notes, descending, patient as snowfall.

Serah stepped aside. After a moment, the woman walked out. Not fleeing. Just... continuing, the way a river continues when you remove a stone.

The second cage held a man who had been tall once and had begun to stoop, as if his body were trying to make itself smaller than its history. His fragment was rhythmic, almost martial—da, da-da, da—and when Serah unlatched the wire he looked at her with eyes like clean windows and said, "Did I march?" and she said, "Yes," though she did not know it, and he nodded and walked out into the grass.

Cage after cage. Hook and eye. Hook and eye. Her hands never shook, because shaking was also a luxury, but somewhere around the twentieth gate she realized she was humming under her breath, matching each prisoner's fragment as she freed it, a duet with every stranger, and the six-note tune from the ridge had come back and would not leave.

*Rise, rise, fall away.*

Rain. The tune smelled like rain. Why did a tune smell like anything?

In the twenty-ninth cage sat an old man with a music teacher's hands—long, precise, one finger crooked from an old break badly set—and when the wire fell open he did not move at all. He looked up at her, and his empty eyes went strange, and he said:

"You're the girl from the conservatory."

Serah went very still.

"You used to wait outside the practice rooms," the old man said, in the flat, wondering voice of the hollowed, who speak of the past the way divers speak of light. "You had a friend. You both sang. There was a song you made together, about—" His brow furrowed, reaching. "About doors. About a door that opens if two people remember the same place at the same time. You promised her something. I can't hold what you promised. But I can hold that you promised."

The field tilted. The morning went white at its edges.

"Her name," Serah said, and her voice came out sanded down to bare wood. "Tell me her name."

"I'm sorry," the old man said gently, the way teachers do. "Names are the first thing they take. But I remember her voice. She'd have been a singer, that one. She'd have opened every door in the world."

He rose, stiffly, and walked out past her into the unmown grass, and Serah Vane stood alone among forty-one empty cages with a promise she could not remember ringing in her skull like a struck bell.

*Lior.* The name surfaced from nowhere, from the hole inside her, from the wound the crown had dressed and called service. Lior. She did not know why the name made her knees soften. She did not know why she had loved—was loving, present tense, the feeling stubbornly alive though its reasons were gone—but she knew it the way the hollowed knew their fragments: not the story, just the sound.

She had been sent here to empty these people completely. Final judgment. No survivors of rank.

Instead she stood in the ruins of her own orders and remembered—without remembering—that she had once been someone who sang.

* * *

The meetinghouse door opened before she knocked.

Inside, the villagers stood in a silent row, farmers and grandmothers and one boy of perhaps twelve, all of them holding bread and knives and whatever courage they'd baked that morning. A gray-haired woman stepped forward, flour on her apron, fear and defiance fighting openly on her face.

"We know what the order says," the woman said. "We heard your riders in the night. If you've come to empty us, you should know we've already given most of what we had. We sheltered the tuned because singers are owed, Tuner. That's the oldest law in this country, older than your king. Singers are owed."

"I know," Serah said.

And then she did the thing that could not be undone, the thing that turned a career into ash and a name into a target: she reached into her coat, drew out the King's order, and held it up so they could see the seal.

Then she dropped it to the floor and set her boot on it.

"The pens are open," she said. "Your people are walking home through the north grass. In ten minutes I am going to burn my instruments in your yard, and the smoke will be visible for miles, and every rider in the second team will know exactly what it means. When they ask me, I will tell them I found the settlement already emptied by persons unknown. Whether they believe me depends on how fast you can scatter, and how much of this village is standing when the sun goes down. So take your bread, take your children, and go west, toward the river. There's a bridge-warden there who trades in years. Tell him the Tuner sent you, and that she's paying her own way."

The gray-haired woman stared at her. "Why?"

It was the only question, and Serah had no answer that would survive daylight. *Because a man with a teacher's hands told me I made a promise. Because six notes have been living in my chest like birds in a burned house. Because I was emptied too, once, by choice, and I called it devotion because the alternative was calling it mutilation.* 

"Because I remember something," she said finally, "and I don't know what it is. That's worse than forgetting. Ask any soul in those cages."

* * *

She burned the instruments in the yard at noon.

The tuning forks went first—two dozen of them, graduated brass, each one pitched to a different depth of soul, each one singing a single obscene note as it melted. Then the resonance wires, the wax molds, the little velvet case of needles she had used to pin memories open for extraction. The fire took them greedily. Brass screams when it dies; she had never noticed before, or had noticed and filed it away in the part of herself the crown owned.

The second team watched from the ridge, too far to intervene, too disciplined to act without orders, and Serah stood in the smoke with her coat open and her hair coming loose and felt—for the first time in longer than she could measure, which was the entire point, which had always been the entire point—*present*. Here. In a body, in a yard, in a single burning moment that belonged to her and no institution.

Treason, she thought, is just remembering with your hands.

* * *

She wrote the message that evening, by the light of a fire she allowed herself in a shepherd's hut three valleys west, far from the second team's camp. She wrote it in the old conservatory cipher, the one they'd taught first-years for passing notes about broken reeds and stolen practice hours—childish, obsolete, beneath the crown's notice. Which was precisely why it would work.

*To the singer with no choir,* she began, and stopped, and laughed at herself, a short ugly sound, because she did not know how she knew that Lior had no choir, only that the knowledge sat in her like a stone sits in a streambed: shaped by water long gone.

She tried again.

*Lior. I don't remember you. I want that understood—I am not writing to a memory, because they took my memories when I took the crown's coin, and what's left is a shape where you used to be. But shapes remember pressure. My hands remember wanting to hold yours. My throat remembers a duet about doors.*

*Here is what matters: the Last Door is real. I have seen the survey maps they don't show the Hunt's own captains. A threshold in unmown grass, cathedral-tall, older than the border. And the crown knows where it stands—they have known since midwinter. They are not racing to find it. They are racing to reach it before you do, and they have saved a special instrument for whoever sings it open. Me. They intend to send me. They think I am their best knife.*

*They are right. So I am choosing, today, to be a bad knife.*

*I have burned my tools and opened your hollowed. I will slow the second team however I can, but I am one traitor against a kingdom, and arithmetic is not on our side. Go now. Go fast. And when you sing—if you ever sing the song we made, the one about two people remembering the same place—know that somewhere behind you, a woman who cannot remember your face remembers everything else.*

*—S.V., who loved you once, and loves you still, on evidence she cannot produce.*

She read it twice. Then she did the hardest thing left: she did not burn it.

There was a courier she trusted—or rather, a smuggler whose sister Serah had once released from a tuning house on a technicality, a debt the man had been waiting years to repay. She found him at a crossroads inn by midnight, pressed the letter into his hand along with every coin she carried, and told him: a singer traveling north with a guild clerk, likely disguised, definitely hunted. Find them. This letter finds them before the army does, or don't come back to anywhere I can hear of you.

The smuggler looked at the seal-less letter, at the famous Tuner in plain clothes with singed sleeves, and asked the question everyone asks eventually:

"What's in it that's worth treason?"

Serah looked east, toward the capital, toward the king whose hand had signed her orders, toward the hole in herself wearing her name like a borrowed coat.

"A door," she said. "And the person I'd burn the kingdom to hold open."

* * *

At dawn she turned south, deliberately, visibly, riding hard along the road the second team expected her to ride, drawing pursuit like a lure draws fish—away from the river, away from the grass, away from the Door.

Behind her, somewhere in the wide world, a letter moved north at the speed of a debt repaid.

Ahead of her, in three days' time, riders would catch her at the ford at Marrow Bridge, and she would give them the fight of her life, and lose it slowly, loudly, buying hours with her freedom and possibly her skin. She had done the arithmetic in the dark. One Tuner, captured and questioned, might buy a singer enough time to reach a field of unmown grass.

It was, she reflected as the sun rose over a country being unmade, the strangest trade of her career: the emptier, emptying herself on purpose—one hour at a time, one mile at a time—all the way to the Door.

She only hoped she'd be wrong about needing to lose.

Because deep in the hole where her memories had been, six notes were rising, and falling away, and rising again—and for the first time since she'd taken the crown's coin, Serah Vane let herself wonder what waited on the other side of a door, if you were the one who opened it.
