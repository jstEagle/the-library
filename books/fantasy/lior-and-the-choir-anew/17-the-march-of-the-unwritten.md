## 17. The March of the Unwritten

The road out of the settlement was not a road so much as a scar—two ruts worn into old grass by carts that had stopped coming years ago, running north through country that grew wilder and more forgiving with every mile. The crown's maps ended at the river. Beyond it, the world was rumor.

We left at dawn, in no particular order, because an army has ranks and we were not an army. We were perhaps two hundred people walking in the same direction, which is a different thing entirely. The hollowed moved slowly. They had spent their lives being emptied, and emptiness is heavy in its own way; it drags at the feet like wet wool. They carried nothing. Most of them owned nothing to carry. A woman with gray in her hair had tied her one possession—a wooden button—to a string around her neck, though if you asked her why, she would have looked at you with those wide, washed eyes and said, *I don't know. It seemed like mine.*

Lior walked at the front. Not leading—Lior would have hated the word—but singing, low and continuous, fragments of songs stitched together from half a hundred villages: a harvest round from the eastern valleys, a lullaby in a dialect dead thirty years, the first four notes of a hymn whose ending even Lior didn't know. The hollowed hummed along where they could. Sometimes they could only manage the rhythm, tapping it against their thighs as they walked, keeping time with a life they couldn't name. It was the strangest music I have ever heard, and I have heard doors open. It sounded like a congregation praying in a language they'd forgotten but their hands remembered.

I walked at the back, which surprised no one who knew me. Someone has to watch the rear. And someone has to carry the ledger.

I had it wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, under my coat, where it had lived since Ondrey pressed it into my hands on the worst night of the old life. Half its pages were blank now—or rather, half its entries were struck through, because since the march began I had been doing something I had never once done in all my years with the Guild.

I had been giving the memories back.

* * *

It started on the second morning, with the button woman.

Her name—I learned it later, when she got it back—was Marta. On the second morning she fell into step beside me without knowing why, the way the hollowed sometimes drifted toward me, drawn by something they couldn't feel anymore. She studied my face for a long while as we walked, and then she said, apologetic as an apology itself, "I think I knew your mother."

"You can't know that," I said gently. "You don't know anything."

"No," she agreed, serene. "But I think it anyway. Isn't that strange? Thinking without knowing." She touched the button at her throat. "I keep thinking things. A kitchen. Rain on a window. Someone laughing at me—not unkindly—for burning bread. I don't know if they're mine. But they won't stop."

I should tell you what a memory feels like when you're carrying it. People imagine a weight, a stone in a sack. It isn't that. It's more like a coin that stays warm. Each one I held had a temperature, a texture, a faint insistence, like a cat wanting to be let out. And Marta's—the rain-on-the-window one, the burned-bread one—had been warm against my ribs for three years, ever since I'd lifted them off a dying woman in a border town whose name I'd also taken, because names are memories too.

I stopped walking.

Marta stopped too, patient, the way the empty are patient, having nothing inside them hurrying anywhere.

"Put out your hand," I said.

She did. And I will be honest with you, because the ledger demands honesty even when I would prefer lies: I hesitated. Three years I had kept that memory warm. In three years of gray roads and colder nights, it had been one of the few bright coins in my pocket, a stranger's rain on a stranger's window, and some small, shameful part of me wanted to keep it, the way a miser wants one last coin even on his deathbed. That is what the Guild does to you. That is what I had let it do to me. You start by taking memories to sell them, and you end by taking them because taking is the only shape your hands remember.

I took Marta's hand in both of mine, closed my eyes, and gave it back.

I don't know how to describe the giving except that it was the taking reversed—everything the theft was, this was its mirror. The warmth left me and went home. Her fingers tightened around mine. When I opened my eyes she was crying, great silent tears, and she was saying, "The bread. He laughed at me about the bread. His name was—" and then she made a sound I hope never to hear again and never to forget, the sound of a person catching their own name out of the air like something thrown back from drowning.

"Tomas," she whispered. "My son's name is Tomas. Where is—" and then her face changed, because the memory came whole, with its edges, with its ending, and grief arrived in her like weather. She wept and laughed and gripped my hands until my knuckles ached, and I stood there in the middle of the marching column letting a stranger crush my fingers, feeling lighter than I had felt since I was a child, and hating myself for how good it felt, because redemption should not feel like profit and yet here I was, counting.

That night I opened the ledger by firelight and drew a line through two entries. Then, before I could talk myself out of it, I read down the column of names—all ninety-one of them—and made a decision that frightened me more than the Tuner's hounds ever had.

I would give them all back. Every one. Even the ones I needed. Even the one marked *C.A.*

* * *

You should understand what the ledger was, really. Not a book of accounts. A book of debts—the other kind, the kind that runs the opposite direction. Every entry was a name, a place, a date, and a description of what I held: *the smell of a ship's tar, belonging to Joren Hale, taken Vellum Quay, autumn.* Small thefts, mostly. That was always our trade at the Vellum Guild—we dealt in the ordinary. Griefs too large to carry, shames too bright to look at, and the small warm furniture of a life: first kisses, mothers' voices, the taste of a particular stew. Clients paid to forget. We weighed the memory like coin, because that is what it was, and someone else bought it, and everyone walked away lighter and poorer and called it commerce.

Except the ones I kept. The double-column, the secret ledger inside the ledger, the entries Ondrey never saw. Ninety-one warm coins against my ribs. I told myself I was a curator. I told myself I was saving them from buyers who'd wear them threadbare. I told myself every lie a thief tells, and the worst one, the one I believed longest: *someday I'll return them.*

Someday is where thieves keep everything.

Now someday had a date, and the date was the road north, and the Door at the end of it, and whatever toll the wight would ask. If I was going to be emptied anyway—if the price of Lior's passage was everything I carried—then I would choose where each coin went. Not surrendered to a threshold. Returned to its owner. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole distance between a thief and a man trying, badly, late, to be something else.

* * *

The riders appeared on the fourth day.

They came out of the south at a walk, eight of them, crown-blue cloaks dusted gray, and they did not charge us or hail us or do anything at all. They simply matched our pace, half a mile off our eastern flank, close enough to count, far enough to be deniable. Waiting. For orders, I thought. For the Tuner. For us to give them a reason.

The column felt them the way sheep feel a wolf on a ridge—not seeing, not naming, just pressing closer together, the humming going thin. Old Fen, the ex-soldier among the hollowed who had appointed himself our rear guard by virtue of being the only one who still remembered how to hold a stick, spat and said, "Cavalry doesn't shadow. Cavalry charges. Whatever they're waiting for, it isn't us."

"It's the Door," Lior said quietly, when I brought word forward that evening. We sat apart from the fires, sharing a heel of bread. "Serah's message said the crown already knows where it stands. They're not hunting us anymore, Cyrus. They're herding us. Letting us gather every gifted soul in the country into one slow, tired procession and walk it straight to the one door they need shut forever." Lior turned the bread over in their hands without eating it. "We're not escaping. We're delivering ourselves."

"Then why haven't they closed the trap?"

"Because a Door that opens needs a singer. And they need me to reach it. Or they need Serah to bring me in before I do." Lior finally bit the bread, chewed, swallowed. "Either way, they're patient because they think patience costs them nothing."

"And does it?"

Lior looked at me across the firelight, and there was something in their face I hadn't seen before—not fear exactly, but the specific sorrow of someone doing arithmetic they don't like. "Patience costs them nothing," Lior said. "It's costing us everything. Have you looked at your hands lately?"

I had. My hands were getting lighter every day. I hadn't told Lior that each returned memory took something with it on the way out—not the memory itself, but the callus I'd grown around the space where it used to sit. Ninety-one coins, and around each one, a shape worn into me to hold it. Give them away and the shapes remained, hollow, aching, like the sockets of pulled teeth. By the time we reached the Door I would be mostly socket.

I said none of this. Instead I said, "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we cross the ford at Millbrook, and there's a name there I've been carrying for six years."

* * *

Millbrook was a ruin now—Hunt-burned, like half the towns behind us—but the ford was still good, and on the near bank, living in a lean-to of salvaged roof-boards, we found an old man mending nets for a river that had almost no fish left in it.

His name was Aldous Wren. Entry forty-four in the ledger: *a summer afternoon, age nine, a dog named Biscuit, the exact quality of light through water.* Taken from him not by choice—he'd been a client, paying to forget the year his daughter drowned, and the Guild, thorough as guilds are, had skimmed the bright top layer off his whole childhood along with the grief. He had been paying for that skimming ever since, in a currency he couldn't count: he simply could not remember being happy, ever, and had concluded he never had been.

I found him alone at his nets and knelt in the mud beside him, which my knees protested and my conscience did not.

"Aldous Wren," I said. "In the autumn of the Year of the Twin Floods, you sold a memory to men in gray coats. I was one of them. I've come to return what we took."

He looked at me the way the hollowed look at everything—no, not quite. He wasn't hollowed. He was worse: full of holes he'd paid for. "I don't remember selling anything," he said. "I don't remember much of anything before I was fifty."

"I know." I took his rope-hardened hand. "Close your eyes."

I gave him back the summer. All of it—the dog, the light through water, the taste of blackberries stolen from a hedge, his mother calling him in at dusk, the whole warm hour of it, six years I'd kept it against cold nights and told myself it was safer with me. It went home like a bird released, and Aldous Wren made a sound halfway between a sob and a laugh, and said, "Biscuit. God in heaven, Biscuit. He used to swim out after the sticks. He used to—" and then he stopped, because the memory's edges are part of the gift, and somewhere past the edges was the daughter, and I watched the grief arrive and I did not take it back out again, because grief is the tax on love and I had no right to waive it.

When I stood, my legs shook. The socket where entry forty-four had lived ached like weather.

Lior was waiting for me at the ford's edge, watching the crown riders watch us from their distant line. "How many left?" they asked.

I did the count against my ribs, the way I counted coin in the old days. "Sixty-three."

"And how many are yours? Truly yours, I mean. Things you took before the Guild. Things that were never anyone else's."

I was quiet for a moment. The river ran brown and patient under the ford.

"One," I said. "Maybe. The one marked C.A. I don't know what it is. I took it from myself so long ago that all I have left is the fact of having taken it."

"Then give it back too," Lior said. "At the Door. Whatever it is—give it back to yourself. Walk through owning nothing, not even the hole where you used to keep yourself." They said it lightly, but their hand found mine in the dark, and their hand was not light at all. "Promise me, Cyrus. Promise me you won't arrive at that threshold still hoarding. I've seen what the wight does with hidden pockets."

I promised. Thieves are good at promises; they're just words, and words weigh nothing.

I meant it anyway. That was the strange new thing about me. I meant it.

* * *

We crossed the ford at dawn on the sixth day, two hundred souls and one ledger with sixty-three warm lines left, and the crown riders crossed behind us at a respectful distance, eight blue shadows on the southern bank, patient as debt.

By noon the road bent north through hill country, and the procession stretched out long and slow, a ribbon of the emptied winding through the grass, humming. Marta walked near the front now, upright, fierce, her son's name worn smooth on her lips. Aldous Wren had joined us at the ford, nets abandoned, saying only, "I remember a dog. I'm going to go where dogs are." Behind us the riders kept their half-mile, and ahead of us the hills climbed toward a horizon that no map admitted to, and somewhere past it, in a field of unmown grass, a Door stood waiting with soldiers under orders to hold it shut.

I walked the line that evening with the ledger open on my forearm like a peddler's tray, and one by one, in the firelight, I read the names aloud and asked, "Is this yours?" and one by one, hands reached out of the dark to take back what was theirs.

Entry fifty-one: a wedding dance, returned to a woman who wept into her husband's ghost of a shoulder. Entry fifty-eight: a father's voice, returned to a boy of maybe nineteen who had been hollowed at fifteen and had not known until that moment what the humming in his chest had been reaching for. Entry sixty: the smell of bread. Just that. Just bread. A man held his own two hands over it like a candle flame and said thank you eleven times.

And with every return, the sockets in me ached wider, and the ledger grew lighter, and I understood at last what Ondrey had known when he put the book in my arms and said, *carry the true one*: that a ledger of debts can only be balanced one way, and that I had been carrying the sum total of ninety-one lives, and that the march north was not a flight at all.

It was a repayment schedule. And the final entry, the one marked *C.A.*, would come due at the Door.

Near midnight, Lior's song changed. I heard it from the rear before I understood it—a new fragment threading into the stitchwork, one I didn't recognize, older than the rest, sung softer than the rest. The hollowed caught it and passed it back down the column like water down a chain, hum to hum, until it reached me at the end, and I felt it land in one of my empty sockets and ache there, sweet and terrible.

Because I recognized it after all. Everyone did. It was the first song of the old country—the one the innkeeper beyond the border had wept over, the one the door-wight had called *the sound a place makes when it remembers itself.*

And from the ridge to the east, faint on the night wind, came an answering sound: the crown riders, eight shadows in the dark, had stopped. One of them was humming it too.

The orders hadn't come yet. But songs travel faster than orders, and tomorrow, the Tuner's answer would come down that southern road like winter.

I closed the ledger on sixty-two remaining debts, and walked faster.
