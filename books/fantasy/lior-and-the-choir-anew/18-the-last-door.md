## 18. The Last Door

They smelled it before they saw it: cut grass and rain and something older underneath, the smell of a house you lived in before you could speak. Lior stopped on the ridge with the wind pulling at their coat and knew, the way they had always known which walls were thin enough to sing through, that they had arrived.

"There," Cyrus said.

Lior had been bracing for a door in a wall, a door in a hillside, a door at the bottom of a stair—some small thing, some hinge and handle. What stood in the valley below was not small.

It was a threshold the size of a cathedral, standing alone in a field of unmown grass that ran to its base like a congregation come to kneel. No building around it. No wall. Just the frame itself, vast and pale as bone, rising out of the green into a sky that seemed to lean toward it. Through its opening Lior could see—not the far side of the field, not the hills beyond, but a light like the hour just after dawn, gold going to white, and within that light the suggestion of rooftops, of a river, of bells.

The old country. Standing open a crack, all this time, like a house whose owners had only stepped out.

"It's real," Lior said, and their voice cracked on it. "Cyrus. It's *real*."

"I told you." Cyrus's hand found theirs, cold-fingered, steady. "I never doubted it. I only doubted us."

Below, at the foot of the Door, something moved that was not grass.

* * *

They came down through the field slowly, bent low, though Lior suspected it made no difference. The wight would know they were there the way a room knows it has been entered. It stood—or seemed to stand—at the threshold's left edge: a figure of gray-green, tall as three men, its body less a body than an arrangement of the field itself, stalks and shadow and old stone worn smooth. Its face, if it had one, was a hollow where the wind made a sound like a held breath.

And between the wight and the road, camped in the grass with their cookfires smoking, were soldiers.

Two score of them, maybe more. Lior counted shields, counted horses picketed by the stream, counted the ballistae mounted on carts with their arms cranked tight. Crown livery, but wrong—the blue dyed too dark, the sigils sewn on by hands that had never seen them worn. The King's new men. The men who had been raised since the Hunt began, who had never known any country but this one, this thinning one, and so did not understand what they had been ordered to guard.

An officer stood at the Door's base with a scroll in his fist, reading from it again and again to soldiers who did not look comforted. Even at this distance Lior caught the shape of the words on the wind: *hold it shut until the Tuner arrives.*

"They're waiting for her," Cyrus murmured. "Not attacking it. Waiting."

"Because they can't close it." Lior's mouth was dry. "You can't close a door that remembers being open. They can only stand in front of it and hope nothing comes through—and hope she gets here before anything does."

"Then we've arrived at exactly the wrong moment," Cyrus said, "or the only right one."

Lior looked at them. In the weeks since the snow, since the argument that had become a confession, Cyrus had changed in ways Lior was still learning to see—the grayness behind their eyes where whole seasons used to be, the way they sometimes paused mid-sentence as if reaching for a word that had been sold. The bridge-warden's toll. The reliquary. The ledger emptying itself down the long road, name by name, memory by memory, given back to farmers and innkeepers and hollowed strangers who wept to receive them. Cyrus Amberdale was half what they had been, and lighter for it, and Lior loved them with a ferocity that frightened them more than any soldier.

"We could go around," Lior said, because honesty deserved the saying even when the answer was no. "Wait for dark. Find another way."

"There is no other way. There never was. That's the whole point of a Last Door." Cyrus squeezed their hand once and let go. "So. How does this go? You sing, I make noise?"

"You make noise," Lior said, "and then you stop making noise and get behind me, because the moment it starts opening, everything changes. The wight said—the wight *told* me—" They faltered. The door-wight's words from the mountain pass still lived in them like a splinter: *the singer who opens it cannot pass carrying anything, not even themselves.* They had not told Cyrus the whole of it. There had never been a right moment, and now there was no time left to find one.

"Cyrus—"

"Later." Cyrus's eyes were on the camp below, and there was something in their face Lior recognized from the ash market, from the bridge, from every moment Cyrus had decided something without saying so. "Whatever it is, tell me after. Tell me when we're through. I'm owed that much, and you're owed the chance to be a coward about it a little longer."

Lior laughed despite everything—a short, broken sound—and hated how much they loved this person, this ledger-keeper turned thief turned saint, who could read them like a column of figures.

"Left flank of the camp," Lior said. "The ballistae are the real danger—if they loose into the Door while it's opening, I don't know what that does, and I don't want to learn. Get them turning around. Fire, noise, anything."

"And you?"

"I'm going to walk up to the biggest door in the world," Lior said, "and sing to it like it's a locked tavern room in Ashfall with a drunk baron on the other side."

Cyrus smiled—the old crooked smile, the one from before the road had worn it down—and for one heartbeat they were back in the counting house, two strangers smelling of smoke and blood, choosing each other out of necessity.

"Sing loud," Cyrus said. "I want to hear it from everywhere."

Then they were gone, down the slope through the grass, moving the way hunted people move, which is to say like water finding its way downhill—unhurried, inevitable, invisible until too late.

* * *

Lior went down the long way, along the stream, letting the water's chatter cover their steps. The grass grew taller near the Door, waist-high and seeding, and it bent away from Lior as they passed—not in fear, they thought, but in courtesy, the way a crowd parts for someone expected.

The wight watched them come. It had no eyes to watch with, and yet the watching was total, like standing inside a struck bell.

"You came back," the wind said, in the hollow of its face. "The singer who remembers."

"I came to open it."

"It is never closed." The stalks of its body stirred. "It is only held. There is a difference, little song. Held things can be let go. Closed things must be broken."

"Then let it go," Lior said. "Please. I'm asking. I've paid—I'm *paying*—"

"You are paying still." The wight's voice gentled, which was worse. "Every note you sing from now until the end will cost you something you cannot name yet. Do you understand what you have left to spend?"

Lior did not answer, because the answer was the splinter, the warning they had swallowed: *not even themselves.* They had spent memories on every doorsong since the vault—their mother's lullaby, the smell of their first winter, the name of the street they grew up on—and each loss had been bearable, each one a coin from a purse that still had coins. But this door was not a tavern room. This door was a country. And the bill for opening a country—

"Lior."

They turned. Cyrus stood at the edge of the grass where it met the trampled ground of the camp, and behind them, the camp was burning.

* * *

It had gone wrong almost at once, and then gone right, which was how everything with Cyrus went.

They had fired the supply carts—oil from a lamp, a stolen taper—and the smoke had done half the work, boiling up black and greasy into the windless sky so that half the soldiers were coughing and blind before anyone shouted an order. But the officer with the scroll had not panicked. Lior watched him from the tall grass as he got his men into lines, as he pointed—not at the fire, not at Cyrus, but at the ballistae, turning them, cranking them around to face the field.

To face the Door.

And there, walking out of the smoke toward the burning carts with his sword drawn and his voice carrying over the whole valley, was Cyrus Amberdale, calling out in a clear carrying voice: "You're guarding the wrong thing! Look at your scroll—look at what you're *for*! You weren't sent to keep people out, you were sent to keep a country in! Open your eyes and ask whose orders those are!"

It was madness. It was brilliant. Half the soldiers hesitated, because the words landed on the bruise every one of them carried—the unnameable wrongness of serving a king whose face no one could quite recall, in a kingdom whose maps redrew themselves between seasons. Lior saw a sergeant lower his spear. Saw two soldiers by the stream simply sit down, as if the question had knocked their legs out.

But the officer was made of sterner stuff, or emptier stuff, and he screamed the counter-order, and the ballistae crews began to winch.

Lior stopped thinking.

They walked out of the grass into the open, into the space between the burning camp and the Door, and they began to sing.

Not a doorsong. Not yet. First, the gathering note—the one Ondrey had taught them in the counting house cellar a lifetime ago, the note that made a place remember itself. It went out over the field like heat off a stone, and the grass stood up straighter, and the smoke bent away, and every soldier in the camp felt, all at once and without knowing why, the memory of a country they had never seen: bells on a Sunday morning, bread on a windowsill, a king who walked among his people unarmed. Some of them wept. Some of them dropped their weapons entirely. One young soldier turned to the man beside him and said, in a voice of pure bewilderment, "*That's* what it's supposed to feel like," and sat down in the burning grass and did not get up.

The ballistae loosed anyway. Two bolts, huge and hissing, arcing toward the Door.

Lior sang the second note, the turning note, and the air between the bolts and the threshold thickened like water, and both bolts slowed, and slowed, and dropped into the grass ten feet short, quivering, harmless as thrown reeds. The effort of it tore through Lior like a hook—but they had sung harder for less, in vaults and cellars and funeral parlors, and they held the note, and walked forward, and the soldiers parted before them the way the grass had, not in fear but in courtesy, in awe, in the oldest recognition there is:

*a singer is owed.*

The officer drew his sword and came at them alone, and Cyrus took him down from the side without ever seeming to hurry, a ledger-keeper's tackle, graceless and effective, and the sword skidded away into the grass, and the officer lay staring at the sky and did not rise again.

And then Lior stood before the Last Door, close enough to touch the pale bone of its frame, and looked up, and up, into the gold-going-white light, and heard beyond it—faint, unmistakable—a choir. The old country, singing its morning song, the way it had sung every morning since the border moved and the maps lied and the world forgot. It was singing to itself. It had never stopped singing to itself.

All it needed was someone to remember it hard enough.

Lior opened their mouth, and gave the Door everything.

* * *

What came out of them was not one song but all of them, braided: the funeral songs sung for hire, the doorsongs sung in flight, the lullabies they had already lost and reached for anyway, finding the shape of them in the silence where they'd been. And the Door answered. That was the miracle and the horror of it—it *answered*, the way a tuning fork answers, the whole vast frame beginning to hum, the light within it widening like an eye opening after long sleep, and through the widening gap poured the smell of rain on old stone, the sound of a river, the first gold rooftops of a city that no map admitted to.

Behind Lior, the soldiers were kneeling. All of them now. Even the officer, sitting up in the grass with blood on his lip, staring past Lior at the light with the expression of a man shown the face of a parent he'd been told never existed.

The wight bowed. A cathedral-tall bow, slow as seasons, the field folding around it like a garment.

"It opens," the wind said. "Little song, hear me now, while there is still time to hear: the toll is taken as you cross, not as you sing. Whatever you carry when you step through—that is what it takes. Nothing more. Nothing less. Weigh yourself before you weigh your life."

And Lior, holding the note, holding the Door open with their whole body and their whole history, finally understood the splinter all the way through. *Cannot pass carrying anything, not even themselves.*

They understood, too, why Cyrus had said *tell me after.*

"No," Lior tried to say, around the song. "No—Cyrus—*Cyrus!*"

Because Cyrus was already moving. Coming up the field toward the light with the crooked smile and the empty ledger and the grayness behind their eyes where a lifetime of small thefts used to be, and Lior saw in their walk everything the wight had just said, saw that Cyrus had known longer, had weighed themselves in some quiet hour on the road and found the arithmetic and said nothing, and the song tore in Lior's throat as the Door widened and the old country leaned close and the first riders appeared on the southern ridge—crown riders, many, and at their head a figure in gray with tuning instruments glinting on her belt like a surgeon's kit—

Serah Vane had arrived. And the Door was open. And Cyrus Amberdale was not slowing down.

"*Cyrus, don't you dare—*"
