## 19. Duet for a Dying Country

**Cyrus**

The Door stood in a field of unmown grass taller than a man, and it was not a door at all until you were close enough to be wrong about everything else in your life.

From the ridge it looked like a wound in the air—a cathedral-sized absence where the morning light went and did not come back. No frame of stone, no hinges, no wood. Just an edge, drawn clean as a knife-cut across the world, and beyond it a darkness that was not dark but *unwritten*, the color of a page before ink. The grass around its base grew in a perfect circle, bent outward, as if the Door had been exhaling for a thousand years.

Cyrus Amberdale had carried ledgers through fire and flood and one memorable week of plague-quarantine, and they had never once been afraid of paper. They were afraid now. Because they knew what stood on the other side of that edge, waiting to be paid.

"Soldiers," Lior said beside them. Not singers' eyes—soldiers' eyes, counting. "Two companies. Pikes on the flanks, crossbows behind. And those—" They stopped.

Cyrus saw them too. Cages on wheels, iron-barred, rolling up the road from the south with the unhurried confidence of things that had never once been told no. Tuning wagons. Cyrus had inventoried their like once, years ago, in a guild ledger under the heading *royal contracts, recurring*.

"They're going to hold it shut," Lior said. Their voice was very quiet, which was how Cyrus knew they were terrified. Lior loud was performance; Lior quiet was truth. "The wight said. Hold it shut until she comes."

"Then we don't wait for her."

"Cyrus."

"We start now." Cyrus took their hand—the hand with the ledger-scar across the palm, the hand that had given away a mother's face and two whole summers and did not regret a single year—and squeezed it once, hard, the way you'd press a seal into wax. "Sing."

Lior sang.

**Lior**

They had told Lior once, in the first choir they'd ever been thrown out of, that a voice alone was nothing. A voice needed a choir the way a flame needed air. Lior had spent thirty years proving them wrong, and thirty years being lonely while doing it.

Now, alone before the Door, Lior proved them wrong again—and understood, finally, why the teachers had been right all along.

The doorsong came up out of them like water finding a crack. Not the small songs, the key-opening songs, the pocket-miracles they'd sold at funerals and weddings and one disastrous coronation. This was the whole of it, every note they had ever swallowed, and it cost what the door-wight had promised it would cost: Lior felt their fourth birthday go, felt it leave like a bird off a wrist, and kept singing. Their mother's lullaby went next. They let it. There would be time, after, to grieve what could still be grieved—if there was an after.

The Door shivered. The unwritten dark along its edge trembled like the surface of a struck bell.

And the soldiers came.

They came at a run across the grass, pikes leveled, and the crossbows loosed, and Lior did not stop singing because stopping meant dying and also because—this was the strange thing, the thing Lior would remember if they lived to remember anything—the arrows *slowed*. Not stopped. Slowed, drifting through the air near the Door like seeds on a wind, falling short, falling soft. The old country leaning against the skin of this one, pushing back.

Then Cyrus stepped in front of them, arms wide, and began to do the most terrible beautiful thing Lior had ever seen.

They started giving memories back.

Not taking. Giving. Cyrus walked toward the charging line with their hands open and their ledger-heart pouring out, and the first soldier to reach them—a boy, no older than the ones Lior had sung funerals for—staggered mid-stride as something entered him. Lior saw it happen: the boy's face flooded, confused, wet. He dropped his pike. He sat down in the grass and began to cry for a mother he had suddenly remembered, or half-remembered, or been given the shape of.

"You'll empty yourself before they even reach us," Lior sang, because the song carried words now whether Lior willed it or not.

"That's the plan," Cyrus called back, and laughed, and gave another soldier back a wedding day.

**Serah**

Serah Vane had tuned forty-one people, and she remembered every face and none of the reasons.

That was the joke she never told anyone. That was the hole she lived inside. She could recite the names on her commission rolls, the dates, the instruments used, the depth of each emptying measured in the crown's tidy units—shallow, deep, *total*—and underneath all of it, like a watermark in paper, there was Lior. Just the name. Just the fact of having loved, preserved like a pressed flower with the color gone out of it. She remembered loving without remembering why, and she had built a career on the assumption that the why didn't matter.

The King's orders had reached her at dawn: *the Door is found. Hold it closed. Tune the singer.*

She rode ahead of her column because she always did, because the hollowed feared her and the soldiers needed her and both facts tasted like ash, and she crested the ridge above the field of unmown grass in time to see the battle that wasn't a battle.

It was a choir.

The thief—Amberdale, the ledger-keeper, the one whose file she'd read so many times the pages had gone soft—was walking unarmed into a line of pikes, and the line was *breaking*, men sitting down in the grass weeping over returned childhoods. And at the center of it, at the edge of the great unwritten Door, Lior was singing.

Serah's horse stopped without her asking it to.

Because she knew the song.

Not the melody—she had no memory of melodies, the tuning had taken music itself, taken rain-on-roofs and tavern-choruses and every hymn—but her body knew it. Her hands knew it. Her hands, gloved on the reins, curled into the shape they had curled into twenty years ago around the neck of a viol she no longer remembered owning, and her throat ached with the specific ache of a harmony part waiting for its cue, and somewhere beneath the hole where her heart's history should have been, something turned over like a sleeper.

*Rain,* thought Serah Vane, who did not remember rain. *There was rain, and someone was laughing at me for missing my entrance, and I swore I'd never miss it again.*

"Ma'am?" said her lieutenant. "Your orders?"

She looked at the cages. She looked at the wagons, the tuning forks racked in oiled rows, the instruments of her own trade gleaming with the care she'd taught their keepers. She looked at Lior, singing the world's edges loose, and at the thief standing between Lior and the pikes with nothing left in their hands but gifts.

"The singer is mine," Serah heard herself say. "Alone. That was the King's instruction—*the Tuner tunes the singer.* Hold the line here. No one follows me down."

It was even true. It was, word for word, the royal instruction. Treason, she would think later, is easiest when it wears the uniform of obedience.

She walked her horse down into the grass, and dismounted, and drew her tuning fork—not to strike it, but because her hands wanted to hold something, and it was the only thing she owned that had ever touched Lior's world.

Lior saw her coming. Serah watched the song falter—one note, half a breath—and watched it pick itself back up, because Lior had always been braver than smart.

"I'm not here to tune you," Serah said, and her voice cracked on the second word like a boy's voice changing, like a voice remembering how to break. "I'm here to—"

To what? She didn't have the word. She had a hole where the word should be.

But the Door had the word. The Door, trembling on Lior's note, opened its darkness a finger's width wider, and out of it came a sound like a country clearing its throat, and the wight was suddenly there—tall, gray, made of the space between pages—standing between all three of them with its featureless face tilted like a listener's.

"*Three voices,*" it said. Its voice was the sound a library makes at night. "*I warned the last singer who asked. A door this size was never hung for one hinge. One voice opens a latch. Two voices open a gate. Three voices—three voices sing a country home.*"

Serah looked at Lior. Lior looked at Serah. Twenty years of emptied devotion and abandoned love stood in the tall grass between them, and neither of them could find a single sentence equal to it.

So Lior did the only thing either of them had ever been any good at.

Lior held out a hand, and changed the song—opened it, widened it, left a space in it shaped exactly like a harmony line that Serah's hands already knew, that Serah's traitor throat already ached to fill.

"Come home," Lior said. "You're missing your entrance. You *swore* you'd never miss it again."

And Serah Vane, King's Tuner, emptier of forty-one souls, who did not remember rain or violins or the smell of a rehearsal room, opened her mouth—and found that some things are cut too deep into the body to be carved out—and *sang*.

**Cyrus**

Later—much later, if there was a later—people would argue about what the three voices did to the field. The survivors of the crown's second company, the ones who sat down in the grass and refused all further orders, would describe it differently each time: a bell, a tide, a harvest, a dawn. Cyrus, who was closest, and who was by then holding perhaps a tenth of what they had carried out of the Vellum Guild counting house all those months ago, described it the only way a ledger-keeper could.

It balanced.

Every note Lior spent, the Door answered. Every note Serah added—rough, rusted, but *true*, truer than anything the crown had ever wrung out of her—was answered twice. And the Door, which had been an absence, became a presence: the unwritten dark filled with light the color of remembered mornings, and through it, pouring like water through a breached weir, came the old country.

Cyrus saw orchards. Saw a bridge over a river that maps refused, crowded with people who looked up startled, caught mid-life, mid-laugh, mid-grief, suddenly aware that a window had opened onto a world that had unmade them. Saw rooftops under snow, a harbor with fishing boats, a hillside of graves tended and named. Saw, impossibly, a choir—hundreds of them, robed, in some cathedral of the preserved past—turning as one toward the opening, and beginning to sing *back*.

The soldiers broke. Not routed—*dissolved*, pikes dropping, men kneeling in the grass with their faces wet, some reaching toward the light like children toward a kitchen window. The cage-wagons rolled to a stop, and the things locked inside them began, faintly, to hum.

And the wight turned its blank face to Lior, and its voice went through the music like a stone through glass:

"*Hear the toll, singer, and hear it now, for there will be no hearing it twice. The one who opens cannot pass carrying anything. Not gold. Not grief. Not name. Not self. The Door takes the opener entire—pours them through as song, and what steps out the far side is new-made, or nothing. Choose with your whole throat.*"

The music faltered.

Only for a moment. Only for the length of one heartbeat, which Cyrus, standing guard below with empty hands and a nearly empty chest, counted the way they counted everything.

One beat. Lior's eyes found theirs across the grass, across the kneeling soldiers, across the whole pouring brightness of a resurrected country—and Cyrus saw the choice arrive there, saw Lior begin to turn the song toward refusal, toward stepping back, toward *we find another way*—

"No," Cyrus said.

They said it quietly. But the field was quieting, the soldiers weeping themselves still, the old country's choir swelling on the far side, and a quiet word carries.

"Lior." Cyrus walked forward, through the bent grass, through the falling light, and took the hand that was not singing. "Look at me. You don't get to stop. You don't get to make this a tragedy we both walk away from. Everything I am is already spent—I gave my mother's face to a bridge, my summers to a road, my stolen years back to their owners one by one down the whole length of this march. What's left of me wouldn't fill a pamphlet." They smiled, and found that it was easy, that it was the truest thing left in them. "But you—you're a whole song. And a song isn't meant to be *kept*, love. It's meant to be *sung through*."

"Cyrus—"

"There's a column in my ledger I never showed you. Entry marked C.A. Whatever it was, whatever I took out of myself all those years ago—it doesn't matter anymore. I'm paying it. All of it. Every theft, every keeping, every name." They lifted Lior's hand and pressed it flat against their own hollowed chest, over the place where the ledger lived. "Sing me through if you can. But sing *yourself* through if you can't. Promise me the second one first."

Behind them, Serah's voice rose—steady now, found, a harmony locking into place like a key turning—and the Door widened, and the wight bowed its gray head as though in mourning, or in welcome, or in both.

And Cyrus Amberdale, keeper of the Vellum Guild's books, thief of other people's yesterdays, turned to face the light with their hands open and their accounts ready to close, and began, in a voice cracked and small and entirely their own, to sing the counter-melody that would carry Lior home.

The Door began, gently, terribly, to take its payment.

The last thing Cyrus would have time to hold—though they did not yet know it, though it was still a full breath away—was a face, lit by borrowed morning, singing their name.
