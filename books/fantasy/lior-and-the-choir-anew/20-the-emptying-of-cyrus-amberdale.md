## 20. The Emptying of Cyrus Amberdale

The Door was singing before Lior touched it.

Cyrus felt it in their teeth, in the hollow behind the sternum where a stolen memory of rain once lived. The threshold stood in its field of unmown grass like the ribcage of something that had died standing up—stone gone gold with age, taller than the cathedral in Vell, wide as a harvest moon lying on its side. Through it: not darkness, not light, but *elsewhere*, a country breathing on the other side of the world's thin skin.

And through it, faintly, came singing. The old country, pouring itself toward the gap like water finding a crack in a dam.

"Hold it open," Lior said. Their voice was wrecked from the doorsong, scraped down to wire and breath, but underneath the ruin of it the gift still rang, and Cyrus could hear the Door answering, could feel the stone leaning toward them like a flower toward sun. "However long it takes. However much it costs."

"I know the price," Cyrus said.

"You don't." Lior grabbed their wrist—the hand that had taken so many memories by touch, the thief's hand—and pressed it flat against the stone. "It doesn't take coin, Cy. It takes *you*. All of you. Everything you've ever carried."

"Then I'm rich," Cyrus said, "and I've been meaning to travel light for years."

Lior laughed—a broken, furious sound—and then the first arrows came out of the grass, and there was no more time for anything but the work.

* * *

They had bought the minutes with blood already spent. Serah Vane held the soldiers' line at the field's edge, her tuning instruments shattered at her feet, her voice raised over the din not in command but in song—a fragment of a duet she shouldn't have remembered, sung badly, sung anyway. The wight stood at the Door's hinge like a statue that had decided, after centuries, to move. And the hollowed were coming across the meadow in their slow procession, humming their fragments, walking into arrowfall because they had nothing left to lose except the fragments, and even those they carried like candles into wind.

Cyrus sang.

Not well. Not like Lior. But the wight had told them the truth in the reliquary's aftermath, when everything hung by a thread: *the Door does not care for beauty. It cares for willingness.* So Cyrus stood at the threshold with Lior's hand in theirs and sang the only song they knew whole—the counting-song of the Vellum Guild, the one every guild-child learned while weighing memories like coin, *one for the sorrow and two for the sell, three for the keeping and four for the tell*—and the Door drank it like wine and stayed open.

Lior stepped toward the light.

And stopped.

"No," Lior said. Turned. "No, we go together, that was always—"

"The singer who opens it cannot pass carrying anything." Cyrus said it gently, the way you say the worst things. "Not even themselves. You heard the wight. If you carry me—if you carry *anything*—the Door closes on both of us and stays closed forever, and everyone behind us stays hollowed, and the crown wins by default. That's the arithmetic, love. I've kept ledgers my whole life. I know when the column won't balance any other way."

"There has to be another way."

"There never is. There's only what you're willing to pay, and who you're willing to pay it for." Cyrus smiled, and found, to their surprise, that it wasn't a lie. "I stole my first memory when I was nine years old. A baker's grief for her son. I've been carrying other people's lives ever since—hundreds of them, Lior, a hoard, a dragon's bed of other people's sorrows and joys. Every one of them goes back today. Every debt paid. Every name in the ledger crossed off. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means you'll be empty."

"It means I'll be *paid*." Cyrus took Lior's face in both hands. Behind them the battle snarled; an arrow hissed past close enough to part hair; somewhere Serah's voice cracked on a high note and held anyway. "Listen to me. Listen, because this is the last time anyone will say it and mean it this way. I love you. I loved you in the ash market when we were strangers choosing each other out of necessity. I loved you on the road north when we fought about the dying farmer. I loved you in the snow when I finally said it aloud, and I have loved you every hour since, and here is the last thing I will ever give you—not a memory, not a theft, but the truth, freely given, which is the only thing I was never able to steal: *go through the Door, and live, and sing the old country back into the world, and do not look back, because if you look back I will follow you into the fire and we will both burn.*"

"Cyrus—"

"*Go.*"

Cyrus put both hands flat against Lior's chest and pushed.

* * *

The toll began immediately.

It did not hurt. That was the terrible thing. The wight had warned them it would feel like nothing at all—like setting down bundles after a long road, like unbuckling armor, like sleep. The memories left Cyrus in reverse order of taking, newest first, streaming out through their palms into the Door like light through a draining glass.

The soldier's childhood went first—all of it, the boy with the wooden sword, the mother in the doorway, the dog whose name Cyrus had briefly owned. Cyrus felt it settle into the Door like a stone into deep water, felt it pass *through*, and knew without knowing how that somewhere on the far side or the near side a young man would wake tonight weeping over a childhood he'd sold to the crown and never expected to see again.

Then the farmer's last afternoon. Then the baker's grief—the first theft, the oldest sin, returned at last to wherever griefs go when they are finally allowed to rest. Then the rest, hundreds of them, a lifetime's hoard pouring out of Cyrus Amberdale like sand through opened fingers: the merchant's wedding day, the cobbler's shame, the queen's lullaby, the traitor's confession, the child's fear of the dark, the old woman's memory of her own mother's hands. Each one named in the ledger. Each one surrendered. Cyrus stood in the Door's golden light and emptied themselves the way a river empties into the sea—one tributary at a time, then all at once, then not at all.

And beneath the stolen memories, deeper, the kept ones. Their own.

Their mother's face went gray first—it had been going gray since the bridge, since the years paid to the warden, and now the last color drained out of it entirely, and Cyrus held onto the shape of her smile the way you hold water in cupped hands, and lost it, and grieved, and kept singing. *Four for the tell. Four for the tell.*

The counting house went. Ondrey's burned ledgers went. The smell of vellum and ink. The weight of coins. The taste of the first apple they'd ever eaten, the name of the street they'd grown up on, the sound of their own laughter at seven years old—all of it, all of it, the Door taking its toll with the patience of geology, and Cyrus sang louder to fill the spaces where themselves used to be.

Serah reached them then—Serah, bleeding from a graze along her jaw, her instruments ash, her eyes wide and wet and full of a remembering she'd only just begun. She caught Cyrus's arm as they swayed.

"The ledger," she said. "The true ledger. Where—"

"Gave it away," Cyrus said. Their own voice sounded strange now, thinner, like a bell with a crack in it. "Chapter seventeen. Gave every memory back along the road. There's nothing left to confiscate, Tuner. You're too late to rob me. I've already been generous."

"You fool," Serah said, and it broke in the middle, and it wasn't an insult at all. "You absolute—"

"Take care of them." Cyrus nodded toward the procession of the hollowed, still coming, still humming, still crossing the grass under the Door's widening light. "That's your penance and your purpose now, both together. See them through. Every one."

"I don't remember how to be that person."

"Neither did I," Cyrus said. "We learn. It's the one thing they can't tune out of us—the learning. Now get clear. When this Door closes, it closes like a verdict."

Serah held their gaze for one long moment—two people who had spent the whole story trying to unmake each other, seeing each other plainly at last—and then she let go, and turned, and walked back into the smoke shouting orders in a voice that had finally chosen its side.

* * *

Alone at the threshold, Cyrus sang the last verses.

There was almost nothing left. They could feel the edges of themselves going soft, like a drawing left in rain. They knew they had been someone. They knew that someone had done a great wrongness and a greater rightness, in roughly equal measure, and that the order of those things mattered less than the choosing. They knew there had been a name. *Amberdale.* The word surfaced like a bubble in dark water—*Amberdale*—and the Door took it, gently, the way you take a coat from a guest who is staying forever, and Cyrus felt the shape of their own identity slide off and dissolve, and did not scream, because there was no one left inside to do the screaming, only a witness watching the last room of a house being carried away piece by piece.

One thing remained.

It had hidden itself deeper than the name, deeper than the mother, deeper than the childhood—buried in the place where thieves hide their truest treasure, which is to say: in plain sight, disguised as nothing, carried so long it had become load-bearing. A face. Rain on an ash market. A bargain struck between strangers. A voice with no choir, singing.

*Lior.*

The Door reached for it.

And Cyrus—who had no name now, no history, no self to defend, nothing left to be brave *with*—found that the body still remembered how to refuse. For one heartbeat, one single heartbeat, the last citizen of Cyrus Amberdale clenched around that final memory like a fist around a coal, and thought: *this one. Let me keep this one. Let me cross into whatever comes next knowing at least that I was loved, that I loved, that once, in an ash market, two frightened people chose each other and it became everything—*

But the Door required emptiness. And Lior was through. And the arithmetic, for once, balanced perfectly.

Cyrus opened their hand.

The face went. The rain went. The market went. The name *Lior* went—dissolving not into darkness but into the song pouring through the Door, so that the last thing Cyrus Amberdale ever was became, quite literally, music; became a note in the old country's returning chorus; became indistinguishable from the thing they had died to save.

What remained standing in the narrowing light was not a person. It was a shape. An emptiness wearing the habit of attention. It looked at its own hands without recognition. It looked at the closing gap in the world—stone grinding toward stone, the old country's song fading to a thread, the field of unmown grass bending in a wind that smelled of beginning—and it felt nothing, because feeling required a feeler, and the feeler had been spent.

The Door narrowed to a slit. To a line. To a hair.

Through the hair-thin gap, from very far away, from the other side of everything, a voice was singing—wrecked, furious, refusing arithmetic, paying its own unthinkable price note by note. And though the emptiness had no memories, no name, no reason, no self, something older than memory stirred in the marrow of it, some tune the soul remembers when the mind cannot:

*a door is only closed until someone sings it open again.*

The gap sealed. The light died. The field stood silent, and the hollowed came stumbling out of the smoke to find no Door at all—only grass, only sky, only Serah Vane on her knees in the ruins of her old life, whispering a fragment of a duet into her cupped hands like a coal she refused to let go out.

And the emptiness that had been Cyrus Amberdale stood alone among the unmown grass, holding nothing, knowing nothing, waiting—for it did not know what, or why, or for whom.

Only that it should wait.

Only that someone, somewhere, had promised.
