## 21. On the Other Side of the Song

Lior woke in grass that had never been mown by anything but wind.

They knew it before they opened their eyes—the smell of it, green and deep and old, the way a meadow smells when no one has ever thought to call it a field. Then the light through their lids, gold and unhurried. Then the sound: somewhere far off, bells. Not the cracked bronze of the capital's mourning towers but true bells, rung by hands that trusted what they rang for.

Lior lay still and let it all arrive.

Whole. That was the word that came first, and it frightened them with its sweetness. Their voice sat in their chest like a bird at rest—not spent, not scraped hollow, not paid out note by note across a hundred doors. Whole. The door-wight had told them what the toll would be, and Cyrus had sung them through anyway, and here they were on the far side of the song with everything intact except—

Except.

Lior opened their eyes.

The sky was the blue of the old banners, the blue from before the Hunt, the blue that had been dyed out of every flag in the kingdom they'd fled. Hills rolled away toward a city with spires, and the spires were whole, and smoke rose from chimneys in fat, unafraid columns. A road ran near the field's edge, and on it a woman drove a cart of apples, humming something Lior knew—knew in their bones, knew in the place where music lives before words get to it. *The Miller's Round.* Banned in three provinces. Sung here by an apple-cart woman who didn't even glance up, as if songs were simply things people had, the way they had hands.

The old country. It was real. It had come pouring through the Door when the three voices joined, and it was here, and it remembered itself.

Lior sat up too fast and the world tilted.

"Cyrus."

The name came out cracked. They said it again, louder, and the apple-cart woman slowed her horse and looked at them with mild concern—the look you give a stranger who has shouted a name into an empty field.

There was no one else in the grass.

* * *

They walked the road into the city because walking was the only thing that kept the screaming on the inside.

The city was Vellamere. It had to be—Vellamere as it had been, the capital of the country that wasn't, the one from the maps that predated the Hunt, the one whose memory-slice Lior had seen behind the palace vault door and fled through a door only they could hear. Here it lived at full size. Markets where the ash market should have been. Children chalked hopscotch squares on cobbles instead of tallying which neighbors had been taken in the night. A crier stood on a corner calling out—Lior flinched, hand flying to Lior's wrist as if to check for irons—but the crier was announcing a festival. A choir festival. Three days hence, all singers welcome.

All singers welcome. Lior stood in the crowd and felt tears slide down their face without permission, and no one looked at them strangely, because here a weeping stranger was just weather.

They found an inn by following the smell of bread. The innkeeper, a broad man with flour to his elbows, took one look at Lior's travel-worn state and set down a bowl of stew and a heel of bread and a cup of small beer without asking for coin.

"You're a singer," he said. It wasn't a question. "Singers are owed."

"I—" Lior's voice caught. "You know that? You can tell?"

"Everyone can tell, here." He frowned slightly, puzzled by the question, then leaned in, kind. "Though I'll say, friend—you sing like someone carrying a heavy tune. If there's grief in it, the festival's the place to put it down. The choirs take everyone."

Grief in the tune. Lior looked at the stew and could not lift the spoon.

Because here was the arithmetic, and Lior hated arithmetic, had always hated it, had watched Cyrus do it in ledgers their whole life: the Door had taken a price. The wight had warned them—the singer who opens it cannot pass carrying anything, not even themselves—and Cyrus had sung Lior through and stayed, and paid, and the last thing Cyrus had lost, the very last, was—

Lior pressed their palms against their eyes.

They couldn't remember Cyrus's face.

That was the shape of it. They remembered everything else—the ash market, the blood on Lior's own ribs, the thief's bargain struck between strangers; the fight in the snow; the confession neither planned; the march of the unwritten; the battle at the Door, Serah's voice joining theirs, the old country pouring through like light under a rising gate. They remembered Cyrus's hands turning ledger pages. They remembered the sound of Cyrus breathing when they slept sitting up, watchful even in rest. But the face—when Lior reached for it, the way you reach for a lamp in a familiar room—it wasn't there. It had gone through the Door ahead of them, or been left behind as toll, or been spent buying the seconds that bought the song. Somewhere in the arithmetic of that threshold, Cyrus Amberdale's face had been the final coin.

And now Cyrus was on the other side of it, nameless, emptied, holding nothing—not even themselves.

"No," Lior said aloud, to the stew, to the innkeeper, to the whole forgiving blue afternoon. "No. That's not how this ends."

* * *

The door-wight found them at dusk, or perhaps Lior went looking and the looking was how you found it. Doors in the old country were honest things, and one of them—a low door in a garden wall, painted the blue of the banners—opened onto a corridor that was not a corridor, and the wight stood in it, folded out of shadow and keyhole-iron, its voice like a hinge remembering oil.

"You are weeping in my wall," it said.

"My person didn't come through."

"The Door does not carry those who pay. It carries those who are carried." The wight's face—if the arrangement of shadows could be called a face—tilted. "Your singer sang you through. Beautifully. I have kept doors for nine hundred years and I have not heard its like."

"Then open it again."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere down the impossible corridor, a lock turned over in its sleep.

"You know what you ask," the wight said.

"I know exactly what I ask." Lior stepped closer. "I've been learning the bill since the bridge at the river. Every gift has a price. Every doorsong spends the singer. I know. Sing it backward and I pay with my past—all of it, the way Cyrus paid. Down to the name. Down to the face." Their voice shook and they let it. "I'm not asking you to waive the fee. I'm asking you to take it."

"It is forbidden," the wight said. "Not by law. By mercy. No one has ever done it, little singer, because no one survives the singing twice. The voice is not a well. It is a window, and you are asking to climb out of it."

"Then I'll fall."

"Lior." The wight said their name, and it sounded like a door closing gently. "You woke this morning whole. In a country that remembers its songs. You could live here. You could sing at the festival in three days and be owed bread for the rest of your life. You would be happy. Is that not worth more than a person who no longer exists? The one who came back through your Door will not be Cyrus Amberdale. There will be no Cyrus Amberdale anywhere, ever again. You would trade everything you are for a stranger with empty eyes."

"Yes," Lior said.

It came out without hesitation, and hearing it, Lior understood it completely for the first time. Not bravado. Not romance. Just the plain refusal of a false sum. Because the wight was doing arithmetic—weighing a whole Lior against a hollowed stranger—and arithmetic was Cyrus's language, and Lior had learned enough of it on the road to know when the ledger was lying. Some things don't balance. Some things just are. Cyrus had stood at the narrowing Door and chosen love over survival, and if Lior chose differently now—if Lior chose the bread and the festival and the happiness—then everything they had been to each other became a transaction after all, and Lior would rather be nobody on the right side of that Door than somebody on the wrong one.

"Love refuses arithmetic," Lior said. "Someone taught me that without ever saying it. Now open the door, or find another singer, because I will stand in this wall until it wears through."

The wight regarded them for a long time. Wind moved the garden behind Lior; the festival bells rang the hour, unhurried, trusting.

"You will forget your mother's lullaby," the wight said at last, softly, like a priest reading terms. "You will forget the first room you ever slept in. You will forget the rain on the night of the duet, and the duet, and the promise in it. You will forget why your own voice makes you cry. You may keep one thing—one sound, one syllable, one held breath of memory—to carry across, and even that will cost you double, and even that may not survive the crossing. Do you understand the terms?"

"One thing," Lior whispered.

"Choose carefully. It is the rudder of whatever self you build on the other side."

Lior closed their eyes and went down through their own life like a diver, past the funeral gigs and the hired grief, past Ondrey's dangerous commission, past the vault and the preserved country singing faintly behind its door, past the ash market and the hounds and the innkeeper who fed singers because singers were owed, past the snow and the confession, past the march and the Door and the three voices braided under fire—and stopped.

There. In the deepest room. Smaller than a coin, worth more than the vault's whole country.

Cyrus Amberdale, saying their name.

Not shouting it across a battlefield. Not gasping it through the narrowing gap. Just—saying it. Early, maybe, some evening on the road north when the fire was low and Lior had half-dozed against a fencepost, and Cyrus had said it quietly to see if they were asleep, and Lior hadn't answered because answering would have ended the moment, and so they had lain still and let the sound of their own name in Cyrus's mouth settle into them like a stone settles into a riverbed, changing the water forever around it.

*Lior.*

Two notes. Their whole name, and the particular grain of the voice that said it—that careful, ledger-keeper's voice that had refused a grieving mother her fee, that had given away seasons at a bridge without telling anyone, that had emptied itself to the last syllable of its own name so that Lior could walk through into the mown-grass smell of a saved world.

"I choose that," Lior said. "The sound of my name in their voice. That's the one thing."

The wight was silent a moment longer. Then, almost gently:

"Then sing, singer. And mind the fall."

* * *

Lior sang the Door from the far side.

It was nothing like opening one. Opening a door was remembering a place so hard the place remembered itself—Lior had learned that from the wight weeks ago, a lifetime ago. This was the reverse. This was forgetting a place so hard that it forgot being shut. Lior stood in the garden with the blue door before them and poured their life into the song: the funeral halls where mourners wept for better days that never happened; the first time their voice had made a locked thing open; the teacher whose face they were losing even as they sang, whose name dissolved mid-note like sugar in rain; the mother, the lullaby, the first room, the rain, the duet, the promise—gone, gone, each verse a window going dark, and Lior sang through the dark because the dark was the toll and the toll was the point.

The blue door widened. Behind it, the other side: unmown grass under a grayer sky, the battlefield's litter, the Door itself standing cathedral-vast and dimming, its edges going soft as breath on glass.

And at the edge of the grass, small in the failing light, a figure.

No name. No past. Standing the way people stand when they cannot remember why they are standing, when the body outlasts the reasons. Turned toward the Door as if toward a sound already fading.

Lior's voice tore. The song was spending them to the studs—they could feel whole years going, rooms of the self going dark two at a time now, three—and the Door narrowed, and the figure did not move, because there was nothing in it left that knew to move toward anything.

But Lior had kept the rudder. One sound, one syllable, one held breath.

Lior gathered the last of the song, the last of themselves, and threw it across the grass—not a doorsong now, just a name, just two notes in a torn voice, the exact pitch and grain of a firelit evening on the road north:

"*Lior.*"

The figure turned.

And in the instant before the Door shut forever, before the song ended and took the singer with it, Lior saw the emptied face change—saw something move behind the eyes that had nothing left to move behind, some tide answering a moon it couldn't name—and the figure took one step, then another, toward the sound of a name it didn't know was its own to say—

The Door closed.

Darkness. Silence. The garden, the wall, the bells of a country that remembered its songs.

And then—faint, from nowhere, from everywhere, from the other side of everything—

a voice, hoarse and halting and utterly unfamiliar, trying the shape of a word it had just heard, getting it wrong, trying again:

"...Lior?"
