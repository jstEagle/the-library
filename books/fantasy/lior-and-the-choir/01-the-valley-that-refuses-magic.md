## 1. The Valley That Refuses Magic

The valley of Brightwater has exactly three opinions about the world beyond its ridgelines: that magic is a rude guest, that the dead should be allowed to finish their sentences, and that strangers are best met at the ford with questions rather than bread. I have lived here long enough to agree with all three, though only the second one pays my rent.

My name is Tamsin Brightwater, and before you ask—yes, the valley named me, not the other way around. My grandmother found me on her doorstep one frost-rotted October with nothing but a blanket and a stubborn heartbeat, and she decided that any child left in Brightwater belonged to it. She was a bone-carver too, and a better one than I'll ever be, though I'd carve that sentence into my own femur before saying it aloud.

I was working that morning on a charm for Widow Merrin Hale, whose son Tam had gone through the ice of the millpond in late winter and come up nowhere at all. The river had kept him until the thaw gave him back, bloated and apologetic, and now his mother wanted something to hold when the nights got long. They always do, for mothers like her. The nights get long and the house gets loud with all the things a son never said.

Bone is honest work. You take a shin of deer or a knuckle of ox, you boil it clean, you dry it in the rafters until it stops smelling like anything but itself, and then—and this is the part people don't understand—you listen to it. Not with your ears. With whatever it is inside a carver that isn't quite flesh. Bone remembers being alive, the way a bell remembers being rung, and if you hold it right, in a place where nothing louder is shouting over it, you can hear what it carries.

Which brings us to the valley's first opinion, and the reason I can do this work at all.

Magic doesn't enter Brightwater. It can't. The wardens say the valley sits in a hollow of the world where the old powers simply slide off, like rain off oiled wool, and whether that's true or a story we tell ourselves to feel special, the practical effect is this: no witchcraft, no curses, no charms, no blessings, no hauntings that ride on power. Magic is loud. Even the gentlest spell hums like a struck string, and that hum drowns out everything quieter—including the dead, who are, as a rule, the quietest things there are.

So everywhere else in the world, the dead have to shout to be heard, and shouting distorts them. Grief gets tangled up in fear, fear in anger, anger in old arguments about who owed whom six coppers in a life that ended forty years ago. A medium in the capital will tell you your father's last words were a curse on your name, because that's what came through loudest. Here, in the silence where magic can't follow, the dead speak at their natural volume. Which means they speak *true*.

That's why they bring me their bones. Not because I'm gifted—I'm competent, which is rarer—but because Brightwater is the only room in the world quiet enough to hear them clearly.

* * *

Widow Hale arrived just past dawn, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, carrying a small bundle wrapped in linen like it might wake up. Inside was a knucklebone from Tam's own hand—the undertaker had offered her a choice, and she'd taken the finger he'd broken falling through the ice, because grief is strange and specific that way.

"You'll tell me if he suffered," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a sentence she'd rehearsed on the walk up from the low farms, and she delivered it standing very straight in my workshop doorway, daring me to be kind instead of honest.

"Sit down, Merrin," I said. "Tea first. The bone doesn't hurry."

"The bone doesn't hurry," she repeated, and something in her face loosened by a degree, because I think she'd been afraid I'd make it quick and easy, and quick and easy would have felt like another lie. People come to me wanting truth even when they say they want comfort. Especially then.

I set the knucklebone on my workbench while the kettle muttered. My tools lay in their roll of oiled leather: gravers, scorers, a burnisher of polished antler, and my grandmother's tuning fork, which sat apart from everything else in its own slot because it was the only object in my possession that had ever left the valley and come back unchanged. More on that later, or possibly never. Some inheritances you don't talk about until you have to.

The carving itself takes an hour or two, depending on what the bone wants to be. That's the part apprentices never believe: the carver doesn't decide the shape. The memory in the bone does. With Tam Hale's knuckle, the shape came fast—a charm in the form of a reed, slender and jointed, because the boy had spent half his life down among the rushes at the pond's edge, cutting whistles. His mother confirmed it without looking up from her tea. "He could make music out of anything green," she said. "Drove his father mad."

When the last facet was cut and smoothed, I held the finished charm in both palms and let myself go quiet. This is harder than it sounds. Quiet isn't empty; it's a discipline, like holding your breath underwater. You push down everything you are—your worries about the roof needing new thatch, your irritation at the warden who'd glared at me across the market, the low persistent ache of being thirty-one years old and alone in a house built for a family—and you leave a space. And into that space, if the bone has anything to give, the dead will lean.

Tam Hale leaned.

Not words at first. Cold. The particular cold of water closing over your head in February, the shock of it, the way the whole body becomes one single shout of *no*. Then the cold settled into something else, and the something else had a voice, thin and clear as the whistle he used to cut from green reeds:

*Mum'll be angry I went out on the ice. Tell her the ice looked solid. Tell her I wasn't showing off, I was testing it for the little ones.*

And then, fainter, almost amused, the way the dead get when they've had time to make peace with the ridiculousness of dying:

*Tell her the whistle was under the east bank. I hid it there. She hated the noise.*

I came back to the room. Widow Hale was watching me with the terrible patience of someone who has already imagined every possible answer and is waiting to learn which one she'll have to live with.

"He didn't suffer long," I said. "The cold took him faster than the water did. And he wasn't foolish—he thought he was checking the ice so the younger children wouldn't go out on it. He was being careful. Careful just wasn't enough."

Her breath came out of her like something leaving a locked room. "That sounds like him," she whispered. "That sounds exactly like him."

"There's more." I turned the reed-shaped charm in my fingers. "There's a whistle hidden under the east bank of the millpond. He wants you to have it. He says—" and here I hesitated, because some messages are private even when they're meant for you, but she was leaning forward with her whole soul, so I finished it— "he says you hated the noise. He hid it where you'd never find it by accident. He wants you to find it on purpose."

Widow Hale put her face in her hands and cried the way people cry when a wound finally gets cleaned—horribly, and with relief. I sat with her until the tea went cold, and when she left, the reed charm was closed in her fist and her step going down the hill had a rhythm to it that hadn't been there coming up.

That's the job. That's the whole job. I don't raise the dead, I don't banish them, I don't work wonders. I'm a translator for people who can no longer speak for themselves, and most days, that feels like enough.

Most days.

* * *

Here is what I didn't tell Widow Hale, because it wasn't hers to carry: Tam's voice was unusually clear. Most of the dead I hear come through like voices heard through a wall—true, but muffled, worn smooth by time the way river stones are worn smooth. Tam had been dead barely a season, and his knucklebone had lived in warm pockets and grieving hands since, and so he came through bright as a bell. But even the old ones, the fifty-years-dead, come through cleaner here than anywhere else on earth. I know this because travelers tell me, in the roundabout way travelers tell you things they think you already know, that bone-carvers in the lowlands work blind. That they guess. That they dress up their guesses in ceremony and charge triple for the dressing.

In Brightwater, we don't guess. We listen. The valley keeps the world's noise out, and what's left is true.

You'd think a gift like that would make a man popular. What it actually makes him is careful. Because hearing the dead truly means hearing *everything* they carry, and people don't always want everything. They want the blessing, not the grudge. The forgiveness, not the accounting. I once told a farmer that his dead wife's last coherent thought was not of him but of a debt she owed the miller, and I lost a customer and nearly lost a tooth. Since then I've learned to sort. To give each mourner the piece of truth that will heal rather than the piece that will merely be accurate. My grandmother called this mercy. Her customers, I suspect, called it something less flattering behind her back.

She also taught me the other rule, the one that matters more: *stay small, stay dull, stay here.* The world outside, she said, had uses for people who could hear the dead truly, and none of those uses involved paying fairly. Kings and priests and men with ledgers—they all want the dead for their own purposes, and a man who can fetch the dead on command is a tool, and tools get used up. So Brightwater keeps its carver the way it keeps its silence: deliberately, and behind walls of boredom. To anyone asking, I'm a craftsman of trinkets. Memorial pieces. Nothing that works, certainly nothing that listens. The wardens enforce the fiction with the flat faces of men who have practiced it.

It's a good life, honestly. I mean that. My workshop smells of boiled bone and beeswax. My garden produces more turnips than any reasonable person needs. Once a month I walk the ridge trail and look out at the world beyond—hazy blue distances, roads like threads, the smoke of towns I'll never visit—and I feel the way a man feels looking at the sea from a cliff: awed, and glad to be standing on stone.

Loneliness is the tax on that life, and I pay it. There's no one here who hears what I hear, no one to tell about the strange harmonies the dead sometimes fall into when several of them linger in one bone, no one who understands why I flinch at bells. My grandmother understood, but she's eleven years gone, and I carved her shinbone myself and heard her last words—*keep the fork sharp, boy, and keep the door shut*—and I've done both, and I still talk to her ghost in my head like she can hear me, which she can't, because she's properly dead, past all bones, past all listening. The dead I serve are guests passing through. The living are what remain, and the living, in Brightwater, number few and know me mostly as the odd fellow with the smell of marrow on his hands.

Some evenings I sit on my doorstep with the tuning fork across my knees and I think: this is it, then. This is the whole shape of a life. Small, useful, quiet, and mine. And I find I can live with that. I've made my peace with it the way you make peace with weather.

* * *

The trouble with peace, my grandmother used to say, is that it makes you predictable, and predictable men are easy to find when someone comes looking.

I didn't know yet, that morning, that anyone was looking. I finished Widow Hale's charm, swept the workshop, ate bread and hard cheese standing up because sitting down felt like an admission that the day was over, and stepped outside to check the sky. It was doing that thing autumn skies do—half gold, half iron, undecided—and the ford below glinted where the road crossed the Brightwater river, the only thread connecting us to anywhere else.

A thread, I noticed, with a figure on it. Moving fast. Too fast for a peddler, wrong gait for a pilgrim, and stumbling, which fit neither.

I stood in my doorway and watched the stranger come up the valley road, and I felt the first cold touch of the feeling I would come to know very well in the months ahead: the feeling of the quiet about to end.

He was a courier—I could see the satchel strap across his chest even at distance—and he was leaving a dark smear on the pale road dust with every third step. And though he was still far off, too far for any ordinary ear, I heard him begin to speak. Not to me. To himself, or to the road, or to whatever god looks after men who ride too hard for too long with something precious strapped to their ribs.

"Hold together," he was saying. "Hold together, hold together, hold—"

And underneath his voice, faint as a whistle under the east bank of a millpond, I heard something else. Something that had no business being audible at that distance, in a valley where the dead speak softly and the living aren't supposed to reach me at all.

The valley's silence, which had held unbroken for as long as anyone could remember, seemed—for just a moment—to lean toward that road and listen.

I went inside and shut the door, and stood with my hand on the latch, telling myself the day was over.

It wasn't. It was only beginning.
