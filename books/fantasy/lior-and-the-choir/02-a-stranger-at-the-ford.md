## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The trouble with living in a valley that refuses magic is that everything unusual has to walk in on its own two feet. Nothing arrives by enchantment. No prophetic dreams, no mysterious lights, no convenient omens. If something strange is going to happen to you in Brightwater, it has to make the same journey you would—down the switchback road, past the wardens' stones, through the birch flats—and by the time it reaches you it's usually too tired to be properly dramatic.

I was thinking about this, or something adjacent to it, when the courier came over the ford.

I'd been at the water's edge since midmorning, working a charm for Widow Marsh. Her boy had drowned in the millrace three weeks back, and she'd brought me his shinbone wrapped in a flour sack like it was a loaf of bread she didn't want the neighbors seeing. Grief does that. Makes people secretive about the strangest things.

"Sit," I'd told her, and she'd sat on the flat rock while I set the bone across my knees and took up my finest graver.

Here is what I do, for those who haven't heard—and nobody outside Brightwater has heard, which is rather the point of me. Bone remembers. Not the way flesh remembers, all soft and unreliable and eager to flatter itself, but the way stone remembers weather. A bone that lived inside a person holds the shape of that person's last days, and if you carve it just so—if you find the grain of it, the true line—it will speak. And because Brightwater lets no magic past its ridgelines, what speaks here speaks clean. No echoes, no interference, no dead voices tangled up with each other like fishermen's nets. Just one voice, one truth, said once and finished.

Everywhere else in the world, the dead are a crowd. In my valley, they get to be themselves again.

So I carved, and Widow Marsh watched the water, and her son's shinbone began to warm under my hands the way bones do when they've got something to say.

*Cold,* said Tam Marsh, who had been a strong swimmer and knew better. *It was so cold, Mum. I wasn't showing off. The ice just looked thicker from the bank.*

"He wasn't showing off," I told the widow, without looking up from my work. "He wants you to know that."

She made a sound I've heard a hundred times and never gotten used to—the sound of a mother being handed back a piece of her child. Then, because he was a considerate boy even dead: *Tell her the third jar of plum preserves behind the loose stone in the pantry. She thinks Da found them.*

"There's preserves," I said. "Behind the loose stone."

And that should have been the whole of my day. A charm, a widow comforted, a few coins and a jar of plums I hadn't asked for but certainly wouldn't refuse. A quiet afternoon of sanding and polishing ahead of me, then supper, then bed, in the valley where nothing strange can happen unless it walks.

Then the courier came over the ford, and he did not walk so much as fall forward into it.

* * *

I heard him before I saw him, which was odd, because he was still alive. Living people don't usually register in my ears unless they're speaking. But this man came down the far bank making a noise—a wet, dragging rattle, like a bellows with a split seam—and his horse was already gone, I'd guess, because he was on foot and leaning against nothing.

He was maybe thirty, dressed in courier grey gone dark with blood along his left side, and he crossed the ford with the stubbornness of a man who has decided that if the river takes him, the river will have earned it. The water was only knee-deep at the crossing, but twice he went down to it anyway and twice hauled himself up again, and each time his eyes found mine across the water with an expression I recognized from carvings I'd done for the dying: not fear exactly. Urgency wearing fear's clothes.

"Tamsin Brightwater," he said, when he reached the shallows on our side. My name. He knew my name. That was the first wrong thing, though I didn't yet understand how many wrong things were stacked behind it like winter firewood.

"I'm Tamsin," I admitted, because lying seemed pointless to a man leaking that much.

"You're the listener." He got the words out between breaths. "They said. Down the road. The valley where the dead talk straight." He laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing brought up something red that he spat into the ford with great dignity. "Straight talk. Been a long time since I heard any."

"You're hurt."

"Observant." He took another step and his legs quit their employment without notice. I caught him under the arms, which is how I came to be sitting in the shallows of the Brightwater ford holding a bleeding stranger who weighed as much as a wet door, while Widow Marsh stood on the bank wringing her apron and asking whether she should fetch someone.

"No," said the courier, and then, louder, with a strength that surprised us both: "No wardens. Nobody official. You hear me? Just him. Just the listener."

"They'll come anyway," I told him. "You're inside the border stones now. The wardens feel it when strangers cross—they always know." This is true. Old Marta and the others keep watch over the boundary cairns, and their whole sacred duty amounts to keeping anything with magic in it out of the valley, and anything with trouble in it out long enough to think better of it. A bloodied royal courier staggering over the ford was precisely the sort of thing they existed to intercept.

"Then I'll say my piece before they come." He gripped my wrist. His hand was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the river. "You're the one who hears them. So hear me. There's a thing wearing the king."

I waited. It seemed like the sort of sentence that had more to it.

"Ten years now," he said. "Aldric sat the throne healthy as an ox, and then one winter he changed. Everyone says grief—his queen died that autumn. But grief doesn't make a man sing in his sleep. Grief doesn't fill a palace with choristers who never age. I carried dispatches for the Crown eleven years. I know what a court sounds like. And that court—" he stopped, gathered himself, "that court hums. Like a hive. You stand in the throne room and your teeth ache."

"That sounds like a problem for people with teeth in the capital," I said, which was wry, and which I immediately regretted, because he looked at me with such naked pleading that I felt about two inches tall.

"It's a problem for everyone," he said. "It's eating the songs. Every village shrine between here and the capital, every old hymn, every bell that rang true—they're flattening it all. Harmonizing. You ever hear a country forget its own voice?" He shook his head. "No. Of course you haven't. You live in the one place it can't reach."

"The wardens' stones," I said automatically. "Nothing magical crosses."

"Right." He smiled, and there was blood on his teeth. "Lucky valley. Lucky carver." Then he pressed something into my hands—hard, small, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax stamped with a sigil I didn't recognize: a circle broken by a straight line. "Map. Route to the capital. Names. Everything I could carry out before they noticed me noticing."

"I don't want it."

"Nobody sensible would." He actually grinned at that. "But you're going to take it, because I'm going to die in about an hour, and dead men are famously difficult to argue with."

Behind us, up the slope, I heard voices. Wardens. Marta's high dry tones and the heavier tread of Bren beside her, coming down through the birches at the particular pace of officials approaching a situation they intend to contain.

The courier heard them too. He used what he had left to sit up straighter against me.

"One more thing," he whispered. "When I'm gone. Don't open the map. Not here. Not until—" His eyes rolled toward the trees where the wardens' voices were getting closer. "Not until you're well clear of the border stones. Promise me."

"Why?"

"Because it isn't only paper." His grip tightened. "Because they made it listen for them, and if it hears where it is—"

That was when I heard the second voice.

Understand: I hear the dead. That is my gift and my trade and, most days, my entire personality. But this man was not dead yet. His heart was still laboring away in his chest like a tenant behind on rent. And nevertheless, beneath his living voice—thin as a hair drawn through honey—there was another voice, already speaking. As if some part of him had gotten ahead of the rest. As if death had sent an advance rider.

*Tamsin Brightwater,* said the voice that was somehow also his voice, *do not open it. Whatever it tells you. Whatever it promises. Do not open the map.*

The wardens broke through the birches.

* * *

Marta Halloway has been warden of Brightwater's western stones since before I was born, and she has the temperament of a woman who has spent forty years telling the weather no. She took in the scene—the bleeding stranger, the widow, me soaked to the waist in the shallows holding a dying man—and her face did something complicated and settled on *displeasure*.

"Well," she said. "What's this, then."

"Courier," said the courier. "Royal. Dying. Passing through."

"Not passing through," said Marta. "Stopping. Here. Inside the border." She said it the way you'd report a rat in the granary. "Bren, go ring the stone. We need him moved before sundown. Anything that dies inside the valley leaves a mark, and we've enough marks."

"Marta," I said, "he's hurt."

"He's hurt *elsewhere*, ideally." But she came closer, and I watched forty years of hard borders soften by perhaps a quarter inch as she looked at his face. "Who cut you, lad?"

"Friends of the court." He coughed. "Justiciars. Grey cloaks. They don't chase you, mind. They follow. Slow. Singing." He shuddered, a full-body thing. "You hear them behind you for miles and miles and you start to want to stop running. That's the worst of it. You start to *want*—"

"Enough," said Marta briskly, but her hand had gone to his shoulder. "Bren, the stone. Now."

Bren lumbered off. The courier sagged against me, and I adjusted my hold, and the oilcloth-wrapped thing sat in my lap between us like a sleeping animal I'd been talked into fostering.

"Give it here," Marta said, holding out her hand. "Whatever he gave you. Sealed post comes under warden law. We'll hold it at the cairn till we decide."

And the dead-voice-that-wasn't-dead-yet spoke again, urgent as a hand on my collar: *Don't. Don't give it to her. Not because she's false—she's true as iron—but because whatever holds it, it listens through. Give it to no one. Keep it against your skin. Keep it quiet.*

"He gave me nothing," I said.

Marta looked at me for a long moment. I have a respectable face. I've been told so at funerals.

"He gave you nothing," she repeated.

"He was delirious. Grabbed at me. I gave it back to the river." I gestured vaguely downstream, committing a minor sin against a ford that had never done me harm.

Marta's eyebrows expressed skepticism in a dialect I understood fluently, but the courier chose that moment to convulse, and after that there was no time for cross-examination. We got him up the bank together, Widow Marsh running ahead to spread blankets on the sun-warmed rock where I do my summer carving, and the courier lay there looking up at a sky he was clearly trying to memorize.

"Good valley," he murmured. "Quiet. Can't hear it here, can you. The singing. First place in ten years I haven't heard it." He turned his head and found my eyes. "That's why they'll come for you eventually, listener. Not for the map. For the quiet. A kingdom built on a song can't abide one silent room."

"Rest," I said, uselessly.

"I'm Wren," he said. "Corwin Wren. If anyone asks. And if a woman comes asking after me—grey cloak, kind eyes, don't trust the eyes—" his breath caught, held, released, "—her name is Vane. Sera Vane. She was different. Once. Maybe still. Maybe."

Then the wardens' stone rang, a single deep note rolling down the valley, and Corwin Wren closed his eyes as if the sound had given him permission.

* * *

He lasted until dusk, which by my count meant he'd beaten his own estimate by half an hour, and I choose to believe he'd have counted that a professional triumph. Marta sat with him. Widow Marsh sat with him. I sat with him, holding his hand because he asked, and because a man walking into the dark deserves something warm to leave behind.

Near the end, his lips moved, and no sound came out, and I heard him perfectly.

*Don't open it,* said Corwin Wren, dead now in every way that mattered, his living mouth still twitching its last instructions. *I know what you are. I sought you out special. The archive woman said—she said there's a carver in a silent valley, the last one who could hear them plain. Twelve names, carver. Twelve singers. Find their names and say them where the Choir can hear, and the thing wearing the king will starve.* His ghost-voice frayed like rope ends. *But it hears you now. The moment you touched the map, it heard you. So be small. Be quiet. Be nothing, until you decide.*

And then, softer, almost kindly, the way you'd speak to a child at bedtime:

*I'm sorry. I looked for a brave man and found a hiding one. But hiding men live longer, and living men can still choose.*

He died with the sun, which I have always thought showed style.

* * *

The wardens buried him above the ford, outside the border stones, because a body that dies inside the valley leaves a mark and rules are rules. Marta let me say a few words. I kept them honest and short: that he'd carried his burden a long way, that he'd put it down at last, that wherever he'd gone, I hoped it was quieter than where he'd been.

Then everyone went home, because that's what valleys do, and I walked back to my cottage carrying a dead man's secret against my chest, inside my coat, against my skin, exactly as his voice had told me.

I sat at my workbench. I lit my lamp. I looked at my carving tools, laid out neat as teeth, and at the half-finished charm for Widow Marsh, and at the small tidy life arranged around them like furniture.

The map hummed.

Not loudly. Not the way a bee hums or a struck string hums. It hummed the way a full jug hums when a singer stands near it—answering a note pitched somewhere below hearing, resonating with a sound that hadn't quite arrived yet. Through the oilcloth, through my coat, I felt it against my sternum like a second heartbeat that belonged to somebody else.

*Be small,* the dead courier had said. *Be quiet. Be nothing, until you decide.*

Outside, far off toward the western ridge, past the border stones, past the wardens' watch, the evening wind came down the valley the way it always did.

Except tonight, for the first time in thirty-one years, the wind was singing.

It was very far away. It was very slightly flat. And it knew my name.
