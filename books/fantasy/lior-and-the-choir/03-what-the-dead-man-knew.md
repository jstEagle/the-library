## 3. What the Dead Man Knew

The courier lasted until dusk, which by my reckoning was about four hours longer than he had any right to.

I'd moved him to the cot in my workshop—the one I keep for the rare occasions when someone in Brightwater drinks too much elderwine and can't make it home. He'd bled through two of my good linen wraps already, and when I peeled back the third, the wound beneath told me everything a wound can tell: he wasn't going to recover from this. Something had gone into him low on his left side, deep and angled upward, and whatever it was had done its work with the patience of a professional.

"You should have let the wardens put you outside the border," I said, wringing out fresh cloth in the basin. "You'd have died faster, but you'd have died somewhere with better views."

He laughed. It turned into a wet rattle that I chose, generously, to interpret as laughter. "Wardens," he managed. "They looked at me like I was a rat that had wandered into their pantry."

"That's more courtesy than they usually show rats."

"What is this place?" His eyes had drifted around the workshop—my bone blanks hanging in rows, the carving knives ranked by fineness, the little brass scale for weighing ash and memory. People always look at the bones first, then at me, as if the arrangement of the room might explain why a man would choose this trade.

"A valley," I said. "It doesn't like magic. Magic returns the favor."

"And you carve—" He gestured vaguely at the charms.

"Bone charms. Keepsakes. Sometimes messages." I pressed the fresh wrap to his side and felt him flinch all the way down to his boots. "The valley's silence lets the dead speak clearly here. Without all the... interference."

"Interference." He said the word slowly, tasting it, and something in his face went grim in a way that had nothing to do with the hole in his side. "There's interference where I come from too. Only nobody calls it that. They call it harmony."

I didn't ask what he meant. That was the rule I'd kept for eleven years in Brightwater: hear what the dead need heard, deliver it, and never, ever go looking for more. Curiosity was how you ended up with a life like mine instead of a life like anyone else's. But the courier watched me not-ask, and he smiled with blood on his teeth, and I understood that he knew exactly what kind of man he'd found at the ford.

"Good," he whispered. "You didn't open it."

"Open what?"

But he was past answering. The fever took him properly around midafternoon, and after that it was just the long, familiar work of sitting with a dying man—which is mostly listening, if you're me, because the dying start talking to people who aren't in the room well before they stop talking to people who are.

* * *

He talked to his horse first. The horse, a gray mare named Peridot who had followed us up from the ford with the resigned air of an animal whose person has done something stupid before, stood with her head over the half-door while he apologized to her at length. Something about a delivery. Something about being sorry she'd carried it. She flicked one ear and went back to watching me with the flat judgment only horses and grandmothers can manage.

Then he talked to a woman called Liss. Not a wife—I gathered that from the shape of the words, the way grief wears different clothes depending on what it's lost. A sister, maybe. Or someone who should have been. He told her about a house in the terraces above the tanneries, blue shutters, the third one from the corner, and how he'd meant to fix the loose step in spring, and how spring kept not coming because there was always one more run.

And underneath all of it, threaded through every word like wire through wax, he hummed.

That was the part that set my teeth on edge. Dying men pray, or curse, or recite the names of everyone they've ever wronged. They don't hum. And this wasn't any tune I knew—not a harvest song, not a dirge, none of the old round-dances the valley still remembered even though we'd stopped dancing them before my grandmother was born. It was simple. Four notes, maybe five, rising and settling and rising again, the way water finds the same groove in stone no matter how many times you divert it.

"Stop that," I said, without meaning to say it aloud.

His eyes opened. Fever-bright, unfocused, but looking directly at me, which dying men rarely bother to do. "Stop what?"

"The humming."

"I'm not humming." He frowned, tried to lift his head, failed. "Am I humming?"

"No," I admitted. Because he wasn't. His lips hadn't moved. Which meant the sound was coming from somewhere else—from the saddlebag the wardens had dumped beside my workbench, from the oilcloth-wrapped thing inside it that I had been very carefully not thinking about since noon.

Four notes. Rising and settling and rising again.

I crossed the room, picked up the bag, and set it outside the door as far from the cot as the workshop allowed. The humming faded to almost nothing. Almost. Like a fly in another room—you stop hearing it, but some animal part of you keeps track of where it is.

When I came back, the courier was watching me with terrible clarity. "It does that," he said. "Gets louder near dark. Near death, too, I think. Maybe those are the same thing." He coughed, and the cough cost him something he couldn't afford. "Listen. When I'm gone—there'll be a few minutes. There always are, in places like this. Places where the dead can get a word in edgewise." His hand found my wrist, grip surprisingly strong. "Don't waste them on condolences. Condolences are for the living, and the living are the least of anyone's problems tonight."

* * *

He died at dusk, as the last light went amber on the eastern ridge and the valley did its nightly trick of gathering all the shadows into the hollows at once.

I've sat with perhaps two hundred deaths. I know the choreography: the breathing goes irregular, then spaced, then stops; the body does its small surrendering things; and then, in the silence, if you're listening—and I am always listening—there's a sound like a held note finally released. The dead leave most places in a rush, torn away by whatever currents move them, their words shredded into static. In Brightwater they linger. The valley holds them the way a bowl holds water, and for a few minutes, sometimes a few hours if they're stubborn, they can speak as cleanly as the living do.

He lingered. Of course he lingered. Stubbornness like his doesn't stop at the border of the body.

He stood by the window, translucent as breath on glass, and looked down at his own remains with the mild disappointment of a man reviewing a badly kept account book. Then he saw me seeing him, and straightened, and gave me a soldier's nod.

"Brightwater," he said. "That's your name? The wardens said it like a curse."

"Tamsin Brightwater."

"Halric Dane. Courier, fourth rank, Royal Post." He paused. "Formerly."

"Former courier," I agreed. "My condolences. You said those were wasted on you."

Something moved across his face that might have been a smile. "You're exactly what the reports said. Annoying and precise." He glanced at the saddlebag outside the door. "You haven't opened it."

"No."

"Why not?"

Because the dead man at the ford had begged me not to, I thought. Because the wardens had looked at the bag the way farmers look at a lightning-struck tree. Because I have spent eleven years building a small, quiet life out of other people's endings, and I know precisely how much trouble fits inside a sealed thing.

"Instinct," I said.

"It'll do." He drifted closer, and the temperature of the room dropped the way it always does, and I noticed—for the first time—that he cast no reflection in the basin water. "Then listen, Tamsin Brightwater, because I only get minutes and I've been carrying this since the capital. Since before the capital. Since the night I understood what it is that sits on the throne of Aldmere."

"The king," I said.

"The *thing wearing the king*." His voice cracked across the words like ice on a pond. "Aldric hasn't spoken in his own voice for ten years. Ten years, carver. I carried dispatches for six of them. I watched him hold court, lead processions, review the fleet—all of it with his mouth moving and nothing behind his eyes but music. The Hollow Choir, they call it, in the rooms where people still dare to whisper. A chorus of the dead bound into the crown itself, singing through him, and through everyone the song reaches far enough into." He leaned close, and cold poured off him like air off a winter river. "The court calls it harmony. The bells ring it. Every hymn in every temple has been rewritten to carry it. You walked out of your valley and onto the road and you thought the world was quiet. It isn't quiet. It's *tuned*."

I wanted to laugh. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept: my first instinct was to laugh, because the alternative was to believe him, and believing him meant my small life was already over. "Dead voices bound into a crown. Singing through the king. And you brought this to a bone-carver in a valley that hates magic because—"

"Because you're the only kind of person who can hear true names." He said it simply, the way you'd tell a man he was tall. "The Choir sings false names. Worn ones, smoothed ones, names worn down until they fit whatever shape the singer wants. But the dead keep their true names, and men like you can hear them, and a true name spoken aloud is the one thing a binding can't survive." His image flickered, thinned. The minutes were going. "The map shows the way in. Under the palace. There's a route the old kings used, before the Choir, when kings still needed to be able to leave their own castle without being seen. The seal will answer to your hands—it was made to answer to a listener."

"I don't want it to answer to anything."

"Nobody sensible wants anything," Halric Dane said. "That's how they win. Sensible people stay home." He was fading faster now, edges dissolving like sugar in rain, and he must have felt it, because he hurried. "One more thing. The important one. If you learn the Choir's true names—if you ever hear them, carver, even once—they will hear you hearing. It works both ways. That's the trap inside the gift. Anyone who knows what they really are becomes prey. They sent hunters after me within a day of my finding the first name. Hunters whose eyes sing when they blink." His hand rose toward me, passed through the space between us, cold as a draft under a door. "Burn the map. Forget the ford. Go back to carving trinkets for grieving mothers and live to be old, Tamsin Brightwater. That's my advice, given freely, by a man who ignored all his own good advice right up until someone opened his side with a knife."

"And if I don't take it?"

"Then be fast," he said, "and be brave, and forgive yourself in advance. Because the Choir doesn't leave debts unpaid, and neither, I think, do you."

He went out with the last of the light. Not dramatically—no wind, no guttering candles. Just a man-shaped absence where a moment before there'd been a man, and the particular silence that follows a ghost leaving, which is different from ordinary silence the way a struck bell's aftermath is different from never ringing at all.

Peridot whickered softly from the yard.

* * *

Here is what I did next, in order:

I did not burn the map. I want to be honest about that, because everything afterward grew out of that single failure of sense. I took the oilcloth bundle from the saddlebag, set it on my workbench, laid my knife beside it—the good knife, riverbone handle, the one my grandmother left me—and told myself I was only going to look at the seal. Wax, black, stamped with a device I didn't recognize: a circle of twelve small marks arranged like notes on a stave. I told myself I'd hold it, weigh it, and put it in the stove, and that would be the end of my involvement in the affairs of kings.

I held it. It was warm. Wax shouldn't be warm, not hours away from any fire, in a valley where magic lies down and sleeps. Warm like a held breath. Warm like something waiting.

I did not burn it.

I told no one. This was easier. The wardens had made their position clear at the ford—whatever the courier carried, Brightwater wanted no part of it, and Elder Maren-of-the-Ford (no relation to anyone important, thank the quiet) had looked at me with her clouded old eyes and said, "Whatever he gave you, give it to the river." The river takes things out of the valley. That's its job. I said I would.

I lied to an elder. Eleven years of careful, invisible, useful life, and I lied to an elder about a dead man's parcel. Somewhere in the arithmetic of my soul, that lie weighed more than the map did.

And then I sat up through the dark with the bundle on my bench, and the map hummed.

Not loudly. Not constantly. It hummed the way a tuning fork hums after you've struck it against your knee—a thin, patient thread of sound that lived in the teeth rather than the ears, so that I felt it in my jaw, behind my eyes, in the fillings of my back teeth. Four notes. Rising and settling and rising again. The same phrase the courier had hummed without knowing it, the same phrase that had leaked from the bag all afternoon. Except now I understood it wasn't a tune at all. It was a summons. Or a beacon. Or—worst thought of all, arriving around midnight with the certainty of a stone dropped down a well—

It was a *count*. Somewhere far to the north, something was counting the miles between itself and this warm little package in a bone-carver's workshop, and adjusting.

I got up three times to put it in the stove. Three times I stood with the stove door open and the bundle in my hands, feeling the heat on my knuckles, and three times the humming changed—just slightly, just enough—when I drew near the fire. Not louder. *Pleading.* The way a child cries differently when the punishment is actually coming.

"You're wax and parchment," I told it. "You don't get to plead."

The humming continued, patient as water.

Near dawn I wrapped it back in the oilcloth, set it in the bottom of my tool chest beneath thirty years of blanks and shavings, and put the chest in the root cellar, and put a sack of turnips on top of the chest, and stood in the cellar breathing the smell of earth and cold storage and telling myself that buried was dealt with.

From the top of the cellar stairs, faint as memory, I could still feel it. Humming. Counting.

* * *

I spent the morning doing the most normal things I could invent. I finished the charm for Widow Cally—her drowned son Tam's last words, carved into a knucklebone of river-ox: *Tell Mum the water was warm at the end, and I wasn't scared, and the heron got my cap.* She cried the way mothers cry, gratefully, and paid me in bread and honey and a lecture about eating properly, and for ten whole minutes I was only Tamsin Brightwater, carver, odd-jobber, the valley's quiet ear.

Then she paused at the door, charm clutched to her breast, and said, "You look peaky, love. Bad night?"

"Bad night," I agreed.

"Heard something strange myself, walking home. Singing, out past the ford. Faint." She frowned at the middle distance. "Nobody sings out there. Nobody's sung out there since I was a girl."

The floor did not open and swallow me, though I gave it every encouragement.

After she'd gone I stood in the doorway of my workshop and looked east, toward the pass, toward the road, toward the capital I had never once wanted to see. The wind came down the valley the way it always did at midmorning, smelling of pine and cold stone, and underneath the wind—so faint I could dismiss it, so faint I nearly did—four notes rose and settled and rose again.

The map was in the root cellar under a sack of turnips.

The singing was coming from the pass.

I am not a brave man. I want that written somewhere permanent. I am a careful man, a hidden man, a man who built himself a life inside a silence the way a snail builds a shell, and every instinct I own was screaming at me to dig a deeper cellar, stack more turnips, wait for the humming to die.

But Halric Dane had died on my cot asking me to be fast and brave, and the dead don't linger in Brightwater unless they mean it, and somewhere under the palace of Aldmere twelve voices—or twenty, or two hundred—were singing false names into the ears of a kingdom, and I was the only man for fifty leagues who could hear the difference.

Also, less nobly: the humming was getting louder, and I suspected that staying was no longer one of the options. Invisible men only stay invisible as long as nothing looks for them. Something was looking now. I could feel its attention the way you feel weather change in an old scar.

I went inside and began, quietly, to decide what to pack.

The tuning fork would come. Grandmother's fork, the one object that had ever left the valley and returned unchanged—I didn't yet know why that mattered, only that it did, the way you know which tool to reach for before you know the job. My knives. The good linen. Bread, if Widow Cally's generosity stretched to travel.

Outside, faint and patient, the singing on the wind rose and settled and rose again.

It had learned a fifth note.
