## 4. Leaving Brightwater

The wardens of Brightwater have exactly three talents: growing suspicious, growing beards, and growing absolutely nothing else. Old Hesk is the champion of all three at once, which is why I spent two days letting him watch me do nothing in particular.

I mended fences. I carved a charm for the miller's rheumatic knee that would do nothing for the knee but might ease his mother's ghost, who had followed him around complaining about damp since her funeral. I bought bread. I complained about the price of tallow to anyone who'd listen, and a few who wouldn't. By the second evening, Hesk had watched me so thoroughly that he'd stopped watching anything else, and I could have walked out of the valley wearing the miller's house on my head without him noticing.

That was the plan, more or less. Walk out wearing nothing remarkable at all, which in Brightwater is the best disguise there is.

Packing took ten minutes, and most of that was deciding what counted as leaving versus what counted as cowardice. The carving tools went first—no debate there. My grandmother's knives, bone-handled, worn to the shape of her grip and then mine. The whetstone she'd sworn was quarried from a saint's shinbone, which was nonsense, but I'd never once contradicted her while she was alive and saw no reason to start now. A change of clothes. Dried fish. The wax-sealed map, wrapped in oilcloth and buried at the bottom of my pack like a corpse I couldn't bring myself to bury deeper.

And the tuning fork.

It sat on my table where it always sat, a plain steel fork with a hairline crack down one tine, worth perhaps three coppers to anyone else in the world. My grandmother carried it out of the valley once, when she was young and foolish in the way that runs in families. She said she took it to the capital itself, held it in her hand through streets and gates and years, and when she came home it was unchanged—not a spot of rust, not a degree of dullness, as if the world outside had looked at it and decided not to touch. Everything else she brought back from that journey wore out or wore away. The fork didn't. She never explained why she went, and she never explained why she came back, and by the time I was old enough to ask good questions she was dead, which is the traditional arrangement.

I picked it up. It hummed against my palm the way it always did, faint and constant, like a wasp dreaming. In the valley, where magic can't enter, that hum was just a hum. Outside—

Outside, I suspected, it was an argument.

*Don't go.*

The courier's voice again. He'd been dead three days now, and his ghost still hung around the ford like a man who'd missed his stop, repeating himself with the patience of the truly drowned. *Don't open it. Don't follow it. You're safe here. Nobody's safe anywhere, but you're safest here.*

"You've mentioned," I said to the empty room. "Twice nightly, in fact. You're becoming tedious, and you're dead, which should make that impossible."

*I mean it.* And underneath the words, the thing I hadn't told anyone, the thing that had finally tipped me out of my own life like a bucket of cold water: under his fear, the courier's dead voice carried something else. Not fear for himself. He'd made his peace with dying somewhere on the road, blood loss and distance having done their slow arithmetic. What remained in him, what echoed loudest now that everything softer had drained away, was shame. He'd been carrying that map for someone else. Someone who'd trusted him. And he'd failed, and now the failure was sitting in a bone-carver's cottage at the bottom of a valley, humming.

Ghosts don't beg for themselves. I've listened to hundreds of them, and they beg for other people—the daughter they didn't apologize to, the debt unpaid, the body unburied. The courier begged for whoever came next. And since the courier was dead and the map was in my hands, "whoever came next" had narrowed considerably.

I put the fork in my shirt, against my chest, where I could feel it arguing.

* * *

Leaving Brightwater at night is a tradition honored mostly by fools and lovers, and I was about to become the former. The valley has no walls—it doesn't need them. The borders are marked by standing stones older than any kingdom, and the moment you pass them, sound changes. Inside, the world is quiet in a way that isn't silence; it's more like a held breath. Outside, noise comes back like water through a broken dam: wind with texture, birds with opinions, your own heartbeat suddenly audible, as if it had been waiting years for the room to clear.

I knew all this. I'd crossed the stones maybe six times in my life, always within sight of home, always back before dark. I'd never stood on the far side and looked back.

I did it anyway, because apparently I wanted the grief to have a face.

Brightwater at night is a handful of lamps and a lot of dark, smoke rising straight from chimneys because even the wind walks softly here. Somewhere down in the village, the miller was sleeping with his knee charm under his pillow, complaining to his mother in his dreams. Somewhere the wardens were sleeping too, Hesk with his beard across half the bed. Ordinary lives. The kind I'd spent thirty years believing were the whole point—that the world was a loud, hungry thing, and the valley was the one place a man could hear clearly enough to do honest work, and honest work was enough.

It had been enough. That was the terrible part. Right up until a bleeding stranger pressed a map into my hands and said *the thing wearing the king*, it had been completely, comfortably enough, and I would have gone on being comfortable until I died and joined the polite chorus of Brightwater dead who ask after each other's health and want nothing resolved.

Now comfort had a taste like river mud.

I turned my back on the lamps. That's the trick of it, if you're wondering—there's no trick. You just turn your back and walk, and every step gets louder, and you keep walking, and eventually the grief runs out of things to say that you haven't already agreed with.

* * *

The first mile was the worst, and not for sentimental reasons. For practical ones. Because at roughly the point where the valley's hush gave way to the ordinary racket of the world, I heard the singing.

Faint. Behind me. On the wind coming down out of the valley passes, riding air that shouldn't have been able to carry it, because—and I want to be precise here, because precision is the only tool I own that doesn't rust—*magic cannot enter Brightwater*. That is the fact the valley is built on, the fact my grandmother staked her whole strange life on, the fact that lets the dead speak cleanly inside those standing stones instead of shouting over each other like they do everywhere else. Nothing sings in Brightwater. Nothing magical, nothing bound, nothing hungry. The dead talk. They don't perform.

And yet.

It wasn't a song I knew, and I know every song the valley dead ever hummed, going back to my grandmother's notes and further. This was lower, wider, many voices braided into one voice the way a rope is many threads, and it did not come from any direction so much as from underneath everything, the way a headache comes from behind your eyes. It rose out of the passes behind me and stretched toward me across the miles like a hand testing the width of a table.

It stopped at the standing stones.

I felt it stop. Felt it the way you feel a wave break short of your boots on the sand—a pressure, a wet cold reaching and withdrawing. Whatever sang behind me could reach the border of my whole life and no further, and knowing that should have comforted me. Instead I understood two things at once, the way you understand falling only once you've already stepped off the edge.

First: whatever wore the king had known about Brightwater for a long time. You don't test a door unless you intend, someday, to open it.

Second: it had heard *me*. Not the valley—me. The courier's ghost had clung to me for three days, and grief and ghosts leave traces, and somewhere in the capital a chorus of murdered voices had felt a small, specific silence moving around at the edge of its hearing, and had leaned toward it the way you lean toward a sound in the night.

The singing faded. The wind kept blowing, ordinary and indifferent. I stood on the trade road with my pack straps cutting my shoulders and my grandmother's fork humming against my sternum like a second heart, and I waited to feel afraid.

I did feel afraid. Let's be honest; I'm writing this honestly or not at all. Fear sat in my stomach like swallowed river stones. But underneath it, doing the structural work, was something harder and less familiar, and it took me a while to name it because I'd never needed the word before.

Resolve. That's the word. It turns out resolve feels almost exactly like fear that's made up its mind.

"Right," I said aloud, to the road, to the dark, to the dead courier whose voice had finally, mercifully, gone quiet—as if my answer had released him, or as if he simply had nothing left to add. "You wanted me not to come. Consider this my formal apology."

The road ran north and east toward the lowlands, toward the River Road, toward towns where shrines stood defaced and bells hung silent—I'd heard travelers mutter about it for years and filed it under *the world being the world*. The map said otherwise. The map, shut up in its oilcloth at the bottom of my pack, had begun to do something maps shouldn't do.

It hummed.

Not loudly. Not the fork's clean silver note—something rougher, woollier, like a voice remembered rather than heard. But I could feel it through the canvas, through the dried fish, through the spare shirt, a pulse keeping time with my footsteps, and every hundred paces or so it shifted pitch slightly, the way a singer adjusts to stay with an accompaniment.

My accompaniment. It was harmonizing with the fork.

I stopped in the middle of the road and took the pack off and stared at it for a long moment, because there are limits to what a man will accept before breakfast.

"No," I told it.

The humming continued, patient, pleasant, horribly alive.

"I am not a pilgrimage. I'm a bone-carver with bad knees and a supply of dried fish. Whatever you think I am, adjust your expectations downward accordingly."

The humming, if anything, grew warmer. Maps, I reflected, are worse liars than ghosts, because ghosts at least have the decency to want something simple—an apology, a burial. A map wants you to arrive.

I shouldered the pack again and walked. What else does one do? Behind me the valley sank into its held breath, keeping my mother's grave and my grandmother's tools' first home and thirty years of quiet competence, and ahead of me the road unspooled through country I'd only ever seen from the tops of passes, flat and wide and full of noise. Somewhere out there, men whose eyes sang when they blinked were already looking for a courier who would never report in. When they finished looking for him, they would start looking for his map.

And the map, traitorous little document, was announcing itself to anyone with ears tuned for it.

So I did the only sensible thing available to a man walking away from everything he loves at midnight: I thought about my grandmother. She'd stood on this same stretch of road, presumably, at some hour of some night, with the same fork against her chest and the same lamps at her back, and she'd gone out anyway, and she'd come back unchanged—or seeming to. She'd never said what she found out there. She'd only ever said, in the last year of her life, when her hands had grown too shaky to carve and her mind wandered the valleys of the past:

*"The valley keeps us, Tam. It doesn't hide us. There's a difference, and the difference matters."*

I'd nodded the way you nod at the old. Now, walking north with murder-music fading behind me and a humming corpse of parchment against my spine, I began to suspect she'd meant it as instructions.

The singing came again near dawn, very faint, from far behind and far below, threading up out of the passes one last time. Testing the door again. Or saying goodbye. With the dead, it's rarely possible to tell the difference, and I've stopped pretending otherwise.

I didn't look back this time. Looking back is how you turn a departure into a wound, and I'd need both hands free for what was coming. I put one foot in front of the other, matched my stride to the fork's stubborn little note, and let Brightwater fall behind me like a stone dropped into deep water—gone from sight long before the ripples stopped.

By full light I could see the lowlands, and the first town on the River Road, and above its rooftops a bell tower with a bell hanging motionless in the morning wind.

The wind was blowing hard enough to bend the trees.

The bell didn't move.

I adjusted my expectations downward, as instructed, and kept walking.
