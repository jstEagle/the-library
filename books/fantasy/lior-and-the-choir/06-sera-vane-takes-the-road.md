## 6. Sera Vane Takes the Road

The first thing you learn as a Justiciar is that most crimes are boring. Someone steals a pig. Someone's cousin insults someone else's cousin with unusual creativity. Someone forges a tax stamp so badly that the forgery office takes it as a personal insult and demands prosecution. You write it down, you stamp it, you go home.

Murder-by-royal-courier is not boring. Murder-by-royal-courier comes with a sealed writ, a purse of coin heavy enough to bruise your hip, and orders written in High Justiciar Ossler's own cramped hand: *Recover the map. Recover or silence any who have seen its contents. Report to no one but me.*

I'd been a Justiciar-sergeant for nine years, and in nine years I had never once been told to report to no one but Ossler. Orders travel down chains. Chains have links. A man who hands you a chain with one link is not giving you an order; he's handing you something he wants buried with you if it goes wrong.

But I took the writ anyway, because I'm good at my job and because the alternative was standing in his office while he looked at me with those pale eyes and decided whether I was a problem yet. I wasn't a problem yet. I intended to keep it that way, mostly out of habit.

The courier had been found at the Brightwater ford, which meant three days' hard ride north into country where the road gave up pretending and became a suggestion. His name was Pellam Orrin, according to the dispatch log, though by the time I read it he was already ash in the valley's burial ground, because the Brightwater folk burn their dead — no magic past their border stones, they say, and apparently no patience for corpses either. The wardens' report was short and unhelpful. *Stranger died at dusk. Carried nothing of value. Valley peace maintained.*

*Carried nothing of value.* A royal courier rides three days from the capital with a wax-sealed map sewn into his coat lining, bleeds half his life out at a ford, gasps about *the thing wearing the king* — I'd read the ford-watcher's statement twice — and carries nothing of value?

Somebody had already been through that coat before the wardens got to it. Or somebody wanted me to think so.

So I rode north with a dead man's description, a living man's orders, and the growing suspicion that I was the mop, not the investigator.

* * *

You can track a man through the hills if you know what to look for, and Tamsin Brightwater — bone-carver, hearer-of-dead-things, according to a file so thin it might have been a pamphlet — left tracks like a man who had never once needed to hide. Not carelessness. Something sadder. He traveled like a person who had spent his whole life somewhere nobody came, and hadn't yet learned that outside the valley, being noticed is how you die.

He stopped at the shrine at Harrow's Bend. I know because the shrine keeper told me, and then kept talking for another quarter hour, because shrine keepers are lonely in a way that makes garrulousness look like a survival strategy.

"Quiet fellow," she said, polishing a bell that hadn't rung in years — Crown order, harmonization, all the old village bells melted down or muzzled. "Bought bread, asked after the road south. Polite." She lowered her voice as if the defaced stone saints might overhear. "Then he stood at the shrine a long while, touching the carvings. Like he was listening."

"To what?"

She shrugged. "Nothing. There's nothing left to listen to. They took the bells, they scraped the names off the stones. It's just rock now." She said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "Still. He listened like there was something there."

I wrote it down because writing things down is what I do, and because every small wrong fact about this assignment was stacking up like kindling. A decade of harmonized shrines. Silent bells. Names scraped off holy stone by royal decree, and nobody in the villages seemed angry about it anymore — just tired, the way people get tired when grief goes on long enough to become furniture.

Tamsin Brightwater had slept at Harrow's Bend and moved on toward the crossroads inn called the Nine Bells, which, the shrine keeper noted with the ghost of a smile, had never had more than four.

* * *

The Nine Bells was the kind of inn that survives by being too far from anywhere for better options. Low beams, a fire that smoked on principle, ale that tasted like apology. I paid for a room, hung my Justiciar's badge inside my coat rather than outside it, and settled in the common room to watch the door and listen.

Listening is the other thing I do. My brother Tam used to say I could hear a lie through a wall. Tam said a lot of things. Tam has been dead eleven years, and I still catch myself saving up observations to tell him, which is either devotion or damage, depending on the day.

The common room filled as the evening did: drovers, a wool merchant, two pilgrims whose piety seemed mostly theoretical, a tinker. Ordinary people being ordinary. And then, around the second round of ale, the innkeeper's wife asked the room, casually, the way you ask about weather:

"Anyone else dream of the singing last night? Third night running, our whole house has."

And the room — the whole room — nodded.

Not everyone. But most. The drover said it was like a choir heard through water. The merchant said it had woken him weeping and he didn't know why and he'd thank us not to make a joke of it. One of the theoretical pilgrims said her dreams had been silent her whole life until the road brought her this way, and now she dreamed of voices singing a song she almost knew, and waking felt like being pulled out of church mid-hymn.

"And the strange thing," the innkeeper's wife said, wringing a cloth, "is it's not a sad song. It should be sad. It sounds like it's trying to be glad. That's what gets under your skin."

I sat very still with my ale and felt the hair rise on my arms.

Here is what unsettled me, and I want to be precise about it, because precision is the only weapon I've ever trusted. Travelers dreaming the same dream along the same road is folklore. Folklore I could file. But these were strangers to each other, sleeping under different roofs, describing the same melody with the same detail — *trying to be glad* — and none of them found it remarkable enough to be frightened of. They talked about it the way you talk about rain. As if it were weather. As if something enormous had passed over this road recently, humming, and the country had simply gotten used to the hum.

My orders said the courier had gone mad at the ford. Fever, blood loss, the ravings of a dying man. *The thing wearing the king.* Ossler's note appended to the file had been dry as chalk dust: *Delirium. Do not treat as credible.*

But dying men raving about a king wearing something else, and a road full of travelers humming one shared song in their sleep, and a decade of silenced bells and scraped-off names — those don't add up to delirium. They add up to a pattern, and patterns are my trade, and this pattern smelled wrong in the specific way that institutional cover-ups smell wrong: too tidy, too pre-explained, the conclusions arriving before the evidence like guests who know the house.

I reported nothing. Not yet. A sergeant who files a report saying *the countryside dreams in chorus and maybe the king is haunted* gets relieved of command and possibly of other things. A sergeant who says nothing keeps her badge and her mobility. I told myself it was tactics. It was also, I admit, fear — but the useful kind, the kind that keeps your mouth shut and your eyes open.

* * *

He came in near midnight, and I nearly missed him, because he looked like nothing at all.

That was the trick of him, I realized later. Tamsin Brightwater had a genius for looking like furniture. Medium height, brown hair going grey early, a carver's callused hands, a face you'd forget while looking at it. He took a corner seat, ordered soup, and ate it like a man performing the act of eating in public for an audience he couldn't see. His pack stayed on his shoulder. His instrument case — a lute, or something shaped like one — leaned against his knee, and he kept one hand on it the way new fathers keep a hand on the cradle.

I let him finish his soup. Then I crossed the room and sat down across from him, and put my badge on the table between us, and watched him go pale in the specific way of a man who has done nothing wrong and knows that this protects him not at all.

"Sergeant Sera Vane," I said quietly. "Royal Justiciars. I'd like to talk about the man who died at the Brightwater ford."

"I don't—" He swallowed. "I don't know anything about that."

"Pellam Orrin. Royal courier. Bled out at the ford three days ago, muttering about the crown." I kept my voice low and even, the voice I use for widows and witnesses, because shouting shuts people down and I needed him open. "Before he died, he pressed a sealed map into someone's hands. The wardens' report says he carried nothing of value. I find that interesting, because a courier's whole job is carrying things of value. So either the wardens lied, or someone took the map, and the only stranger in the valley that week was you."

His hand had drifted to the instrument case. Not the lute strings — the case itself, the underside of it. I'd seen smugglers make that exact motion. I'd seen spies make it.

"Tamsin," I said. "I've ridden three days through country where the bells don't ring and the saints have no faces, following a dead man's trail and orders I'm not sure I trust, to ask you one question. Did he give you the map?"

For a moment he just looked at me. And here is the thing I will remember about that moment for the rest of my life, however much of it remains: he wasn't afraid of me. He was afraid *for* me. It was in his face as plainly as a scar — pity, of all things, aimed at me like a lamp.

"You're the one they sent," he said softly. "They always send someone honest first. Honest ones ask questions. That's how they find out whether the questions are dangerous."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," he said, "that I think you should decide right now whether you serve the law or the men who write it down. Because I can answer your question honestly, Sergeant, and once I do, you'll be holding something you can't put back down."

Around us the common room went on being ordinary — dice clacking, ale sloshing, the fire smoking. Above the lintel, where the inn's name promised nine bells and delivered none, hung a single wooden bell, decorative, dumb. I looked at it and thought about a whole country of dumb bells, and a road full of sleepers sharing one song, and a writ with one link in its chain.

"Start with the question," I said. "We'll get to what I serve."

He glanced around the room, and then he leaned forward, and he said, so quietly I had to bend to catch it:

"He gave me the map. And he told me the king is dead — or worse than dead — and that the thing sitting on the throne sings with the voices of murdered singers, and that the map leads to the proof. And Sergeant—" his eyes flicked up to mine, and they were steady, steady as a plumb line— "the dead man stayed. After he died. He stood at the edge of the firelight and begged me not to open it, because whoever holds the map, the Choir hears them thinking about it. It hears me. It's probably heard me say this. Which means it now knows exactly where I am, and who I'm talking to, and if you walk out of this inn and file your report, you won't be a mop anymore. You'll be a witness. And they don't bury witnesses, Sergeant. They *harmonize* them."

The word landed in my chest like a dropped stone. Harmonize. The Crown's pretty word for ten years of silenced bells and faceless saints. I had stamped that word on a dozen routine orders without ever once asking what it meant, because asking is not in the job description, and I had been very good at my job.

"You expect me to believe the ghost of a courier warned you," I said. My voice came out flatter than I intended.

"No," Tamsin said. "I expect you to believe your own ears. Everyone in this room has been hearing the same song in their sleep. Ask them. Ask yourself — did you dream last night, Sergeant? On the road? In the inn at Harrow's Bend?" He tilted his head, and for the first time he looked less like furniture and more like something with edges. "Because you've been on that road three days. And the Choir doesn't just sing for the guilty. It sings for anyone getting close."

I opened my mouth to tell him I didn't dream, that I hadn't properly dreamed since my brother died —

And stopped. Because I had. Last night, at Harrow's Bend, I had woken at some black hour with wet eyes and a melody fading in my skull, a hymn I almost knew, sung by voices that sounded like they were trying so hard to be glad. I had filed it under fatigue and ridden on.

Tam used to hum when he worked. Tam has been dead eleven years.

My hands were not entirely steady when I picked up my badge and put it away. "This conversation didn't happen," I said. "Officially, I lost your trail at the crossroads. Unofficially—" I looked at him, at the case he guarded like a cradle, at the dumb wooden bell above the door. "Unofficially, you're going to tell me everything, starting with why a bone-carver from a valley with no magic is carrying a dead king's truth in a lute case. And then we're going to talk about what happens when we reach the capital. Because if even half of what you said is true, Tamsin Brightwater, then my oath and my orders just parted ways, and I need to know which one I'm following before Ossler sends someone less honest to collect us both."

Outside, far off down the dark road, a sound rose that none of us could name and all of us, I think, heard: thin, distant, patient — a single voice, or many, beginning to sing.

Tamsin went white as bone.

"They've found me," he whispered. "It's starting."
