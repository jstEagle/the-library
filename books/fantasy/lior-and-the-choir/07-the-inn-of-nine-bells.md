## 7. The Inn of Nine Bells

### Tamsin

I had been running for six days when I made the mistake of stopping.

Not a dramatic mistake. No ambush, no arrow in the dark. Just the ordinary treachery of a body that needed sleep and an inn with a lit window and the smell of stew leaking out into the cold like an apology. The Inn of Nine Bells sat at the crossroads where the trade road bent toward the capital, and its sign—nine small bells on a wooden frame, each one painted a slightly different shade of wrong—swung in a wind that carried no voices at all.

That was the strange part. For six days I'd heard singing behind me, faint as breath on glass, always just past the edge of certainty. Here, under the inn's eaves, it stopped. Dead silence, the kind I'd grown up inside. I should have found that comforting.

Instead my skin crawled, because silence that arrives suddenly is never empty. It's held. Something was holding it.

I went in anyway. Hunger makes cowards brave.

The common room was warm and loud and utterly ordinary: drovers arguing over dice, a peddler asleep in his soup, a girl watering wine with the serene dishonesty of her profession. I took a corner table with my back to the wall, ordered stew and whatever passed for beer this far from home, and told myself I would eat, sleep four hours, and be gone before dawn.

Then the bells over the door rang as someone entered, and every head in the room turned—not toward the door, but toward me.

No. Not every head. But enough. A woman by the fire whose eyes were too still. A drover who stopped mid-argument with his mouth open, like a puppet whose handler had paused to think. Two travelers who set down their cups in perfect unison, left hand, right hand, a mirror with nothing between it.

They blinked. And when their eyelids came back up, something behind their eyes *sang*. I couldn't hear the tune, not exactly—it wasn't sound so much as pressure, a note pressed against the inside of my skull—but I knew what it meant. The Choir had sent its regards.

I paid for my stew without eating it. I was calculating the distance to the back door when she walked in.

I didn't know her name yet. I knew her boots—Justiciar issue, oiled leather, worn at the heel by real miles rather than parade grounds—and I knew the way the room rearranged itself around her, the way rooms do around people who carry authority like a drawn blade. She wore a traveling cloak over gray-and-silver, and she scanned the common room the way I imagine a hawk scans a field: not looking for anything, cataloguing everything.

Her gaze snagged on me and stayed.

Of course it did. I was the only man in the room sweating over untouched stew with a lute case clutched to his chest like a shield. Subtlety has never been my gift. Carving is patient work; being looked at is not.

She crossed the room and sat down across from me without asking, which told me everything about how the conversation was going to go.

"Tamsin Brightwater," she said.

"Is that a question or an accusation?"

"Yes." She set a folded paper on the table between us. A warrant sketch, I realized—my own face rendered in charcoal by someone who'd only ever seen me through other people's fear. It wasn't a bad likeness. My nose has always been too honest for its own good. "Sergeant Sera Vane, Crown Justiciars. You're wanted for questioning regarding the death of a Crown courier."

"I didn't kill him."

"I know. He died of wounds taken two valleys east of wherever you found him." She said it flatly, but I caught the seam in it—the part of her that had already done this arithmetic and disliked the answer. "You're wanted because he was carrying something, and that something isn't in his saddlebags anymore."

My hand moved toward the lute case before I could stop it. Her eyes tracked the movement, and I watched her decide not to reach for it, and I understood we were both playing a longer game than arrest.

"The map," I said.

"So you have it."

"I have what he gave me." I leaned forward, keeping my voice under the room's noise. "Sergeant, I'm going to say something strange now, and I'd like you to hear all of it before you put irons on me. Your courier didn't die clean. He died scared, and he died *loud*, and I'm the only person in this country who can tell you what he said after his heart stopped."

For the first time since she'd sat down, Sergeant Vane went very still. Not the stillness of doubt. The stillness of a woman hearing a word from a language she'd sworn she'd forgotten.

"You're claiming," she said carefully, "to speak for the dead."

"I don't claim it. It's just true, the way rain is true. In Brightwater valley there's no magic—none, the ground refuses it—and in that silence the dead can be heard clearly. Properly. Their last words, their unfinished business. I carve bone charms that carry their voices to the living so they can let go." I spread my hands. "It's small work. Quiet work. I want you to understand that I did not *ask* for any of this."

"No one ever does," she said, and there was something in her voice then—not softness exactly, but the memory of softness, worn smooth like her boot heels. "Here's my problem, carver. Every madman who ever murdered a courier claims God or ghosts sent him. If I take your word, I'm a fool. If I don't, and you are what you say—" her jaw tightened "—then I've spent ten years serving a thing I can't name, and I'd rather be a fool."

"Then let me prove it."

"How?"

"Ask me something only the dead would know. Something you told no one. Something you maybe only said once, to someone who's gone."

She stared at me for a long moment. Outside, wind worried the nine bells on the sign, and none of them rang quite true. Inside, the singing pressure against my skull swelled, patient and vast, like a tide deciding whether to come in.

"There was a boy," she said finally, and her voice had gone rough at the edges. "My brother. Tam Vane. He drowned when we were children. I was twelve. I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "If you're lying about this, I will break your hands so thoroughly that even your ghosts won't recognize them."

"Noted," I said. "What did you say to him? After?"

She closed her eyes. When she spoke, each word cost her something.

"I stood on the bank and I told him it was my fault. That I'd dared him onto the ice. And I said—I said *I'll carry it, Tam. You swim free.*" Her eyes opened, wet and furious at being wet. "I have never spoken those words aloud in twenty years. Not to a priest, not to a confessor, not drunk. So either you tortured them out of some record that doesn't exist, or—"

"Or the dead keep what we give them," I said gently. "And Tam Vane kept that. He's kept it a long time, sergeant. He's been waiting for you to ask."

### Sera

There is a moment in every interrogation when you know, absolutely, whether the person across from you is lying. Ten years of Justiciar work had honed mine to a razor. Liars perform grief; they offer details in the wrong order; they watch your face instead of remembering.

Tamsin Brightwater did none of these things. He closed his eyes, tilted his head as if listening to something just past my shoulder, and his face changed—softened, focused, went somewhere I couldn't follow. And then he spoke, and the voice that came out of him was not entirely his.

*"Sera. It wasn't your fault. I went on the ice because I wanted to see the fish under it, and I was eight, and eight-year-olds are idiots. You were twelve and you couldn't have stopped me with a whole regiment."* A pause, and something almost like laughter, thin and bright and unbearable. *"But you promised to carry it, so here's what I need you to know: you can put it down now. I swam free. It's your turn."*

The common room noise rushed back in. Tamsin slumped, blinking, looking suddenly exhausted and about thirty years older than his face suggested.

"He says—" I started, and found my throat had closed. I tried again. "He says it wasn't my fault."

"I know," Tamsin said quietly. "I heard."

I had prepared, on the ride to this inn, three versions of this encounter. In the first, I arrested him cleanly and delivered map and carver both to High Justiciar Ossler, and was commended. In the second, he ran, and I took him down in the yard, and was commended. In the third—unlikely, but a good sergeant plans for unlikely—he was innocent, and I brought him in anyway, because orders are orders.

In none of the versions did I sit in a roadside inn with tears I refused to acknowledge on my face, listening to my dead brother tell me to put down a weight I'd carried since childhood.

"Who else knows?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "About what you can do."

"My grandmother. The grieving families of Brightwater valley. And now you."

"And the men who killed your courier."

"They didn't kill him. Whatever hunted him did. And it knows I have the map, because the map *hums*, sergeant. It sings to anyone with ears for it, and lately I've noticed things with ears for it noticing me back." He glanced, quick and involuntary, at the far table—the woman by the fire whose eyes hadn't moved in ten minutes. "Including, I think, some of the people in this room."

I followed his glance. The woman by the fire. The drover with the open mouth. The two travelers who had set down their cups together. I'd catalogued them on entry and filed them as nothing—tired travelers, a drunk, locals. Now, with new eyes, I saw the wrongness underneath. They weren't looking at anything. They were *attending*, the way soldiers attend a briefing, waiting for the order.

"Those four," I said. "They came in over the last hour. Separately. From different directions." Which was itself the problem. Nobody traveled alone on this road in winter unless they had a reason that outweighed frostbite.

"They started listening when I walked in," Tamsin said. "Or rather—they started being listened *through*. There's a song under this whole valley's silence, and those four are its instruments. I think it's deciding what to do with me."

"Then we leave. Now, through the kitchen."

"It wants the map. Or it wants me. Possibly both." He hugged the lute case tighter. "Running might work tonight. It worked last night. But sergeant—Vane—I need to say the next part plainly, because I've been saying it to myself for six days and it hasn't gotten easier. Your courier died telling me the king is dead. Not the man—the kingship. He said there's a *thing wearing the king*. A chorus of bound voices that speaks through Aldric's mouth and has done for ten years. And your warrant, your orders, your High Justiciar—all of it exists to make sure nobody listens to a carver from a quiet valley."

Ten years. The number landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. Ten years ago, Aldric had survived the fever that killed half the court and emerged changed—gentler, they said. Serene. His rages gone, his whims gone, his *self* gone, and the realm had called it wisdom. I had called it wisdom. I had knelt and sworn renewed oaths to that serene face twice.

And every harmonization decree, every silenced shrine, every village bell melted down for palace bronze—each one had seemed reasonable in isolation. Reasonable things, stacked high enough, make a tower. I was standing at the bottom of it now, looking up.

"If I help you," I said slowly, "I'm not helping you escape justice. I'm helping you find out whether justice still exists to escape to. Those are different crimes."

"I know."

"If you're wrong—if this is madness, or worse, some enemy's trick dressed in ghost-clothes—I'll put you in chains myself and consider it a kindness compared to what Ossler would do."

"I'd expect nothing less." He almost smiled. It was a tired, crooked thing, and it made him look briefly like a person rather than a haunted lute-case. "So here's my offer, sergeant. Take me to the capital. Not in chains—in your custody, officially, so your papers hold at the gates. Once we're inside, I'll do what I do. I'll listen. And I'll tell you the truth about what wears the crown, even if the truth is that I'm mad and the king is fine. That's the bargain. My ears for your escort."

"And if the king isn't fine?"

"Then you'll have to decide what your oath actually protects. The crown, or the country. I've seen them be different things before. Usually right before something breaks."

Outside, the wind rose, and the nine bells above the door shivered into sound—nine notes that should have made a chord and instead made an argument. And beneath them, faint but unmistakable now, the four listeners at the far tables began, very softly, to hum.

All four. The same note. Perfectly in tune.

Every instinct I owned stood up at once. "Carver. We're leaving."

### Tamsin

We went through the kitchen, past a cook who took one look at Sera's face and developed a sudden fascination with her onions. The yard behind the inn was mud and woodsmoke and the stable's animal warmth, and for about forty seconds I believed we'd simply walk away.

Then the four hummers came around both sides of the building at once, unhurried, their mouths closed, their throats working. The note they carried pressed against my skull hard enough to spark light behind my eyes. Behind them, in the inn's doorway, more shapes gathered—guests who'd been laughing over dice minutes ago, now standing shoulder to shoulder with the patience of furniture.

"Sergeant," I breathed. "Your sword."

"Against civilians? I'd hang."

"They're not civilians anymore. They're speakers."

She hesitated—one heartbeat, two—and I watched the oath and the evidence fight behind her eyes. It was the tuning fork that settled it, though neither of us knew it yet. My hand had gone to my grandmother's fork on reflex, the way a frightened child grabs a talisman, and when I drew it out the humming changed. Wavered. The four speakers stumbled, mouths opening in silent confusion, their borrowed note fraying at the edges like rope over a blade.

Sera saw it. I saw her see it.

"What is that?"

"A tuning fork. Steel, old craft, made outside any choir's key. My grandmother used it to check a carving's pitch." My arm shook; the pressure pushing back against the fork was enormous, a flood leaning on a door. "It doesn't hurt them. It just gives them back to themselves for a few seconds. Long enough to run."

"Then run," Sera said, and grabbed my collar, and hauled me toward the stable's back gate as the four speakers dropped to their knees in the mud, clutching their heads, and one of them—a girl no older than seventeen—looked up at me with her own eyes again and mouthed two words:

*Help us.*

We ran. Behind us the inn's nine bells swung wild, ringing every wrong note they had, and above it all—far off, back along the road we'd traveled, and ahead, toward the capital, everywhere at once—the Choir's true voice rose like weather.

It wasn't angry. That was the worst of it. It sounded amused, and patient, and absolutely certain of the ending.

Sera wrenched open the gate and shoved me through it. "The capital's four days' ride," she said, swinging up onto a stolen-looking bay horse with the ease of long habit. "Can you ride?"

"I'm from a farming valley. I can ride, plow, and butcher pigs. Only one of those is relevant tonight."

"Ride, then. And carver—" she wheeled her horse alongside mine, and in the dark her face was pale and set and more honest than I'd yet seen it, "—when we get there, and you listen to whatever sits on that throne—you'd better pray to whatever gods aren't banned yet that you hear a king. Because if you hear a *thing*—"

She didn't finish it. Ahead of us the road unspooled black toward the horizon, and somewhere along it, waiting, the city that hummed.

"—then I'm going to need to know exactly how loud you can sing," I finished for her.

She spurred the horse, and we rode.
