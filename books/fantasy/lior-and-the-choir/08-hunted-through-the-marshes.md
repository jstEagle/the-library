## 8. Hunted Through the Marshes

I have been shot at twice in my career with the Justiciars, stabbed once by a smuggler who apologized the whole time he was doing it, and cursed at by more innkeepers than I can count. None of it prepared me for men whose eyes sing.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me start where a proper report would start: with my own stupidity.

* * *

We'd been on the fen road two days when I realized I was being followed, and one more day pretending I hadn't noticed, because noticing out loud meant admitting that the people following us were almost certainly mine.

That's the part I keep chewing on. *Mine.* My order. My oath. The seal I've carried since I was nineteen years old and so certain of everything that certainty felt like a virtue instead of a symptom.

The courier's map rode inside Tamsin's coat, wrapped in oilcloth because Tamsin worried about rain and I worried about everything else. He'd stopped trying to open it after the third night it hummed against his ribs like a trapped wasp. "It wants to be read," he told me, hunched over a fire we were both pretending wasn't visible from the road. "Maps don't want things, Brightwater."

"This one does," he said, and I didn't argue, because I'd heard it too — or thought I had, which is worse. A faint thread of sound under the wind, patient as water finding a crack in a wall.

The fens themselves were honest country, at least. Flat brown water, black alders, reeds tall enough to swallow a horse. The causeway ran narrow and raised above the mire, built centuries ago by some petty king whose name survived only as a milestone with its letters worn to suggestions. Nobody harmonized the fens. There was nothing here worth singing at.

Which is why I knew, when the reeds went quiet, that whatever came next was not local wildlife. Local wildlife doesn't hold its breath in unison.

"Tamsin." I kept my voice low. "How are you with running?"

"I'm a carver. I sit down for a living."

"Wonderful. Start sitting somewhere else."

They rose out of the reeds ahead of us — four of them, then two more behind, closing the causeway like a hand closing around a moth. They wore no colors. No badges. That was the first wrong thing, and I catalogued it the way you catalogue a wound: no livery means no sanction, which means deniability, which means someone very high up wanted this done quietly enough to lie about later.

The second wrong thing was their faces. Not scarred, not masked. Just *empty*, in a way that made my skin crawl — faces like rooms after the furniture's been sold. And their eyes. When they blinked — and they blinked often, slow, like lizards — I heard it. A note. Each man carried a different note, thin and sweet and utterly wrong coming out of an eyeball, and together the six of them made a chord that made my teeth ache and my knees want to fold.

I've trained since I was twelve. I know fear the way farmers know weather. This wasn't fear. Fear has edges. This was something older, the feeling of standing in a temple as a child and knowing, without words, that you were small and the thing in the dark was not.

"Give me the carver," the lead one said. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. It was the voice of a man asking for directions. "You're not named in the song, Sergeant Vane. Walk away and stay unwritten."

And there it was. My name, in the mouth of a stranger, in the middle of a marsh, attached to a word like *song*. Whatever leash I'd been pretending wasn't around my neck just gave a little tug.

"Sergeant," Tamsin whispered, "what do they mean, you're not—"

"Later." I put my hand on my sword. It felt about as useful as a spoon. Six men, narrow causeway, and me with a blade while they had whatever it was that made their eyes hum. "Tamsin. The fork."

He looked at me. "What?"

"Your grandmother's tuning fork. You said it came back from outside unchanged. You said—" and here I did something I'm not proud of, which is gamble another man's heirloom on a guess, "—you said nothing in the valley ever changed it. Maybe nothing out here can change it either. Strike it. Strike it now."

He fumbled it out of his pack — a plain steel fork, dark with age, wrapped in wool like a relic, which I suppose it was. His hands shook. Mine didn't, which surprised me; apparently terror past a certain depth comes out the other side as calm.

The hunters walked forward. Blinking. Singing through their eyelids. The chord tightened around us like wire, and I felt my own thoughts start to smooth out, start to agree with things — with putting down my sword, with stepping aside, with handing over the thin gray man beside me because the song said he belonged to them—

Tamsin struck the fork against the pommel of my sword.

The sound was small. That's what I'll tell people, if anyone ever asks, though no one will: it was a small sound. A clean A, pure as well-water, and it went through that chord like a needle through rotten cloth.

The six hunters stopped. All six, mid-stride, like puppets whose puppeteer had sneezed. And their faces — gods, their faces *changed*. The emptiness cracked, and underneath it was horror. One of them looked at his own hands as if he'd never seen them before. Another opened his mouth and said, in a voice scraped raw and young, "*Where am I, where am I, where—*" and then he blinked, and the note came back, and the room behind his face closed again.

"Again!" I shouted. "Keep striking it!"

Tamsin struck it again and again, a ragged bell tolling, and each peal bought us seconds — the hunters staggering, weeping, waking, drowning. It was the most terrible thing I have ever watched and I include the year I spent pulling bodies from the Harrow flood. Because I understood what I was seeing. Those weren't monsters. Those were soldiers. Ours or somebody's — men who'd been hollowed out and hung with a note, and every time the fork rang, they got to be men again for three heartbeats, and every time it faded, they lost themselves all over again.

"Run," I said, and shoved Tamsin toward the reed-bank where the causeway forked toward the old eel-stakes. "Off the road. Water's shallow there if we're lucky."

"We're never lucky."

"Then run anyway!"

We ran. Behind us the fork sang and the hunters howled — actually howled, six voices tearing loose of the chord one at a time, and I heard the lead one scream, clear and human and broken: "*It lies, it lies, don't listen, it LIES—*" and then the note took him back and his voice smoothed into that pleasant nothing and he started walking again, faster now, because whatever held his strings had decided to stop being gentle.

The reeds cut us like knives. The mud sucked at our boots. Twice I turned and swung at nothing, because the singing was everywhere now, in my ears, in my skull, telling me to stop, to rest, to lie down in the warm water, and I bit my tongue bloody to have something real to hold onto. Tamsin struck the fork as he ran, badly, off-rhythm, but even a wounded A is still an A, and every strike pushed the song back a few feet like a torch pushing back fog.

The eel-stakes saved us. Old black poles driven into the mire in rows, spaced for flat-bottomed boats, and beyond them a channel of open water deep enough to pole through if you didn't mind leeches. I got Tasmis over the first row of stakes half by strength and half by shoving, and when the first hunter broke from the reeds behind us — face human, eyes singing, tears freezing on his cheeks — I did the last thing I expected to do that day.

I saluted him.

I don't know why. Yes I do. Because he was mine. Because whatever uniform his soul used to wear, it was cut from the same cloth as mine, and someone had taken it off him and hung a note in its place, and the someone who did that signed orders with the same seal I carry over my heart.

Then I poled us into the channel until my arms gave out, and the singing faded behind us into ordinary fen-sounds, frogs and wind and the obscene contentment of herons.

* * *

We made camp on a shingle bar an hour downstream, because Tamsin's hands had stopped working and mine weren't far behind. He sat with the tuning fork cradled like a sleeping bird and didn't talk for a long time. Neither did I. My report-writing voice, the one that files everything neatly, had gone on strike.

Finally he said, "You knew them."

"No."

"You saluted them."

I fed a stick to the fire. "I saluted what they used to be."

"And what was that?"

"Soldiers." The word tasted like ash. "Or wardens, or watchmen, or couriers. Men who swore something to somebody. The Court doesn't brand its property anymore, Brightwater. It tunes it."

He flinched at that. Good, I thought, viciously, and then hated myself for it. He hadn't asked for any of this either. He'd been carving charms for grieving mothers in a valley where magic couldn't follow, and now he was poling through leech-water with a dead man's map humming against his ribs and six hollowed men hunting him across the marsh.

"The way they blinked," he said quietly. "The sound. I've heard bindings before. Grief-bindings, mostly. A mother who can't let go, a widow who ties her husband's voice to a ring. They're clumsy and sad and they fade." He turned the fork over. "This wasn't grief. This was *craft*. Somebody built those men the way I build a charm. Deliberately. To spec."

"Yes."

"That's your king's work."

I should have argued. Every instinct I owned, every lesson from the Academy, every oath I'd sworn at nineteen stood up in me and demanded I argue — that I say *we don't know that*, that I say *the Court would never*, that I say the words that had always come so easily because I'd never once had to test them.

Instead I heard the lead hunter's voice again. *You're not named in the song, Sergeant Vane.*

"They knew my name," I said. "They knew my rank. They offered to leave me out of it. That's not banditry. Bandits don't audit." I looked at the fire. "There's a chain of command for everything, Brightwater. Requisitions, patrols, executions. Someone authorized those men. Someone funded them. And whoever it is sits close enough to the throne that 'no colors' was the instruction, not the oversight."

"So your law—"

"My law sent them." I said it fast, before I could dress it up. "My seal. My order. My chain of command, ending — where? In a throne room? In whatever wears the throne?" I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Do you know what the worst part is? When they stopped being men for those three heartbeats, when the fork rang and they woke up — they were *afraid*, Brightwater. Not of us. Of going back under. One of them begged. And I will hear that begging until I die, and every time I hear it, I'll remember that the institution I gave my life to is the thing he was begging to be saved from."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Then, in the mild voice that I was beginning to learn was his most dangerous one: "You could still take me in. Report the ambush as bandit attack. Recover the map. Get your promotion and your clean hands."

"Yes," I said. "I could."

"Why won't you?"

Because my brother's ghost asked me to find out if the king was a king, and I haven't answered him yet. Because I've spent ten years believing the law was a wall between the strong and the weak, and today I watched it walk out of the reeds with singing eyes. Because Maren Corvane once told me — no. She hasn't yet. Later. Keep the timeline straight, Vane.

"Because unwritten isn't innocent," I said instead. "They offered to leave me out of the song. Which means the song has parts already written. And I want to know who's holding the pen."

Tamsin nodded slowly, and then he winced and pressed a hand to his chest, over the oilcloth bundle, and I saw his face go pale in the firelight.

"What?"

"It's stopped humming," he said. "The map." He pulled it out, and I swear on my brother's grave the wax seal had softened, slumped, like something breathing under it. "Sera. It's not humming anymore because it doesn't need to." He looked up at me, and behind the fear in his eyes was something worse: certainty. "It knows we heard the hunters. It knows where we are. It's not calling to us anymore."

"Then what is it doing?"

"It's waiting," Tamsin said, "for us to bring it the rest of the way."

Out in the dark, far off across the water, something began to sing — one voice, then another, then a dozen, threading together into a chord I recognized with my whole cold spine. Not the hunters. More than the hunters. Coming down the channel toward our little fire, patient as water finding a crack in a wall.

I reached for my sword. Then I reached for the tuning fork.

Tamsin was already striking it.
