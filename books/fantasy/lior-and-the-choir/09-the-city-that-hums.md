## 9. The City That Hums

The capital announced itself three miles before we saw it.

I'd like to say I noticed the wrongness through my gift, that the dead whispered warnings in my ear and I rode on with grim foreknowledge. The truth is less flattering: what told me something was off was a milkmaid humming.

We'd passed her at a crossroads shrine—one of the few shrines we'd seen all week that hadn't been defaced, though someone had chiseled the old names off its lintel and left the stone beneath raw and pale as scar tissue. She had a yoke of pails and a tune in her throat, and she was humming it the way people hum, absent and content, a sound that means nothing because it comes from nowhere.

Except it wasn't from nowhere. It was the same melody Sera had been whittling out of herself for two days whenever she thought I wasn't listening. Seven notes, rising, then a fall that never quite landed where you expected it to. A tune with a limp in it.

"You know that song?" I asked her.

Sera glanced back. "What song?"

"The one you've been humming since the marshes."

"I haven't been humming anything." But her hand went to her mouth, the way a person's does when they've caught themselves doing something they didn't know they did. "It's just—walking music. Everyone has walking music."

"Everyone has *their* walking music," I said. "This one's shared."

She didn't answer, but she stopped humming, which was its own kind of confession. And the milkmaid hummed on behind us, seven notes rising, the fall that didn't land, until the road bent and took her away.

That was the last quiet thing about Corvale.

* * *

They call it the City of Nine Bells, and from the ridge above the river valley I could see why: nine towers rising out of the morning haze like nine fingers pointing at heaven, each crowned with a bell of greened bronze. The city itself spilled down the slopes toward the water in tiers of slate and plaster and gilded weathercocks, and the river ran through it broad and slow, crowded with barges hung with lanterns even though it was barely noon. It was beautiful. I want to be honest about that, because everything else I'm going to tell you happened inside the beauty, the way rot happens inside a sweet apple, and if I only gave you the rot you'd think I hated the place, and I didn't. Not at first. For about ten minutes I was exactly the bumpkin the city expects me to have been, standing on the ridge with my mouth open while Sera checked her horse's girth and pretended not to be embarrassed by me.

"It's bigger than you imagined," she said.

"I hadn't imagined it."

"That's the correct amount of imagination for a Brightwater man."

But here is what I noticed before we ever reached the gates, and what I should have heeded: the bells were ringing noon, all nine of them, and they were *almost* in tune.

Almost. Each bell rang true against itself—I could hear the fundamental clean as a struck coin—but set them side by side and there was a hair of disagreement between them, a flatness in the fourth tower's voice, a sourness in the seventh, small enough that most people would never catch it and would only feel it, a faint unease under the skin of the hour, an itch with no name. In Brightwater, our single cracked chapel bell rings flat every Sunday and everyone loves it for that, because it's *our* flatness, ours alone, a flaw with a family. This was different. Nine bells, nine flaws, and every flaw the same size, the same shape, the same direction off true—as if someone had tuned them not to each other but to something else entirely, some third note none of them could quite reach.

I pressed my palms against my temples. My skull had been aching since the crossroads, a dull pressure behind the eyes like a tooth trying to come in.

"Tamsin." Sera was watching me. "You've gone grey."

"The bells are wrong."

"They're bells. They're supposed to be loud and wrong."

"Not this kind of wrong." I looked down at the city, at the haze of woodsmoke and river-mist, and underneath my ribs something old and animal wanted very badly to turn the horses around and walk home to a valley where nothing sang unless I let it. "In the valley, silence is a wall. Out here—" I didn't have the word yet. I'd have it by nightfall, and I wished I didn't. "Out here the wall is made of something else."

Sera said nothing, but she rode closer to me after that, close enough that our stirrups nearly touched, and I understood that in her own language—the language of oaths and formations and who stands at your shoulder—that was an apology and a promise both.

* * *

The gate guards barely looked at us. That frightened me more than scrutiny would have. Sera wore her Justiciar's badge openly now—we were past the point where hiding it helped—and in the fens that badge had been a target; here it was furniture. The guards waved through a fruit cart, a funeral procession, a troupe of acrobats, two Justiciars and a bone-carver with a lute case full of dead women's finger bones, all with the same bored half-nod, as though the city swallowed so many people daily that the swallowing had become automatic.

Inside, the beauty got thicker and the wrongness got louder.

Corvale's streets were canyons of pale stone, awnings striped in saffron and wine-red, fountains at every crossing throwing up rainbows, shopfronts displaying silverwork and spice and silk in profusions that would have bought my whole valley twice over. Children ran messages between the legs of the crowd. A woman sold roasted chestnuts from a brazier and argued cheerfully with a man about the price. Everything moved. Everything glittered. And everywhere—everywhere—was the melody.

Seven notes, rising. The fall that doesn't land.

A fiddler on the Fountainbridge played it, spinning variations around it the way a vine climbs a post, but the post was always there underneath. Two streets later, a pipe-and-tabor duo played it faster, for dancers, and the dancers' feet found it without seeming to choose it. A beggar sang it for coppers, slow and mournful, and I heard him flatten the sixth note in exactly the way the seventh bell flattened it, and when a woman dropped a coin in his cup she hummed the phrase back to him as thanks, and she got it right—or rather, she got it *wrong*, in the same direction, the same hair off true, and neither of them noticed.

"Sera," I said quietly. "Ask the chestnut woman what the song is called."

"Why?"

"Because I need to hear someone say it out loud."

She asked, casual as buying chestnuts. The woman laughed. "That? That's just the air, love. Everybody knows the air." And she went back to her roasting, humming.

"Just the air," Sera repeated to me, and for once the sergeant's certainty had a crack in it. "She thinks it's nothing. She thinks it's like weather."

"It is weather," I said. "That's the problem."

Because here is what I knew, riding down those bright streets with my skull tightening band by band like a vise being turned by a patient hand: a song you can't escape isn't music anymore. It's architecture. It's the shape of the room you live in, and you stop hearing it the way you stop seeing walls, and anything that shapes the room you live in shapes *you*. Ten years of this. Ten years of seven notes rising and a fall that doesn't land, poured into every ear in the capital every hour of every day, until the whole city hummed it in its sleep—and I remembered the inn at Marsh Hollow, the travelers who'd all heard singing in their dreams, and understood that the Choir's reach was longer than its walls. Much longer. The palace wasn't the instrument. The country was.

And somewhere under all that harmony, thin as a wire pulled taut across the whole city, I could feel the screaming. Not with my ears. With whatever it is in me that hears. The original voices, the twelve, still in there, still pressed flat beneath the melody like flowers in a book, and every time the melody swelled—every fountain-song, every bell-strike—they screamed a little louder, and only I could hear it, and my head felt like it was going to split along the seams.

I must have made a sound, because Sera caught my elbow. "Stable," she ordered, in her sergeant voice. "There's a yard off Cooper's Row. Now."

* * *

The stable yard smelled of straw and honest horse, and I put my forehead against my mare's neck and breathed until the world narrowed to something bearable. Sera stood over me while I shook, and had the grace not to comment, which I intend to repay someday by never mentioning how long it took.

"It's noticing me," I said finally. "The Choir. It noticed me at the gate, maybe before. Every mile closer, it gets—louder isn't right. *Closer.* Like something turning its head toward you across a dark room."

"Can it find you?"

"It already found me. What it hasn't done yet is decide what I am." I straightened up. My hands had mostly stopped. "In the valley, the dead speak and nothing listens back. Here, everything listens back. That's what the ache is. It's not the noise. It's the attention."

"Then we move fast, we stay small, and you keep that case shut." Her eyes flicked to the lute case strapped behind my saddle, where Maren's reliquary lay wrapped in wool among the spare strings. Twelve finger bones, twelve carved names. I'd finished the carving in a fen-camp with my knife shaking, and I hadn't opened the case since except to check, the way you check a wound.

"And if it decides what I am before we're ready?"

Sera looked at me for a long moment. Around us the city went on being beautiful—the light off the pale stone, the smell of bread from a baker's alley, a child laughing somewhere near the mews—and over all of it, faint and constant as blood in your own ears, the melody.

"Then we improvise," she said. "I'm told that's what soldiers do instead of praying."

* * *

We took a room at a chandler's lodging house on Cooper's Row, two floors below the eaves, a cell with a bed apiece and a window looking out on a lightwell where laundry steamed and pigeons muttered. Sera went out to make contact with the one person in the capital she trusted more than half—her words, and I've learned to worry when a soldier uses fractions about their friends—and I stayed in, because walking those streets with my skull in a vise was becoming less survivable by the hour.

I tried to work. Old habit; when the world is too much, carve. I took out a scrap of cow bone and my smallest knife and set myself to cutting a simple warding knot, the first pattern my grandmother ever taught me, the one your hands learn before your mind does.

My hands wouldn't do it.

Not from trembling—though they trembled. From listening. Every stroke of the knife, I kept tilting my head, chasing the melody through the walls, through the floor, through the bones of the building itself. Seven notes, rising. The fall that doesn't land. The chandler's wife hummed it in the yard. The pigeons cooed it, or my aching head insisted they did. Somewhere below, a knife-sharpener's wheel sang against steel in the same rhythm, and I couldn't tell anymore whether the city was following the song or the song was following the city, whether there had ever been a difference.

I put the knife down. That was the moment I understood the shape of the trap, and I want to set it down plainly, because it took me years to say it this simply:

In Brightwater, magic cannot enter, and so the dead speak cleanly, and I am useful, and I am safe. Here, the dead are *loud*, and they are organized, and they are wearing a king, and the whole apparatus of a great city—its bells, its songs, its customs, its very air—has been bent around them for ten years until no one living can hear the seam. I had spent my life believing silence was my shield. It wasn't. Silence was my cage, and the cage had been keeping me the way a scabbard keeps a blade: contained, yes, but also *sharp*.

The Choir didn't want me silenced because I was dangerous. It wanted me silenced because I was the only thing in ten years that had walked into its hall of mirrors knowing what reflections were.

The light was going amber in the lightwell when I heard the footsteps on the stair—not Sera's; hers I knew, brisk and heel-first—and my skull flared white-hot, a spike of pain so sudden and total that I doubled over the little table and bit down on my sleeve to keep from crying out.

Through the window, down in the yard, the chandler's wife had stopped her washing. She stood with her hands dripping in the basin, face tilted up toward my window, and though the lightwell was dim and I was two floors up and shadowed, I swear to you she was looking directly at me. Her lips moved. No sound came up the shaft.

But I heard it anyway, in the voice of a woman I'd never met, pitched with terrible gentleness, the way you'd speak to a child you meant no harm to and intended to keep:

*Welcome, carver. We have been waiting so long for someone who could hear us properly.*

Then she blinked, frowned at her wet hands as if surprised to find them there, and went back to her washing, humming seven notes, rising, the fall that doesn't land.

I sat in the amber light with my heart slamming and my hands cold, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the gate. It knew I was here. It knew what I was. It had chosen courtesy—for now, for tonight, for reasons of its own.

When Sera came up the stairs an hour later, I was packed, the reliquary case strapped and ready, sitting by the door with the lamp unlit.

"We need to see the king," I said, before she could ask. "Tomorrow. Whatever it costs us. Because it just said hello, Sera—and I don't think it says hello twice."
