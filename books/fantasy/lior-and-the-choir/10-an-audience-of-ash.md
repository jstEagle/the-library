## 10. An Audience of Ash

There is a particular kind of lie that cities tell, and the capital told it better than any I'd ever met. It went like this: *you are safe here, because there are so many of us.* A valley tells you the opposite — *you are alone, therefore beware* — and at least that lie has the decency to be honest about its arithmetic.

Sera Vane had been gone since dawn, working whatever levers a Justiciar-sergeant can work when she's pretending she isn't harboring a fugitive bone-carver in a rented room above a chandler's shop. She came back at midday smelling of horse sweat and official ink, and dropped a folded paper on the table between us.

"Midwinter observance. Palace forecourt, open to credentialed military and their... guests." She said *guests* the way you'd say *fungus*. "The king renews the Concord of Voices every year at this time. Public ceremony. Everyone who matters attends."

"And we matter because?"

"Because nobody looks at a sergeant's plus-one. They look *through* you." She sat down across from me and studied my face with the flat professional attention she probably gave to crime scenes. "You said you needed to see him."

"I need to hear him," I corrected. "Seeing is easy. Anyone can put a body on a throne. My grandmother could have carved one that fooled half the court, and she worked in walnut."

"You're not funny."

"I'm extremely funny. It's how I cope." I picked up the paper. It was heavy stock, embossed with a seal I didn't recognize — a circle of small figures, hand in hand, which I supposed was meant to look like unity and instead looked like a noose made of people. "What happens if someone recognizes you? You're branded by now, aren't you? Ossler will have filed something."

"Ossler files everything," Sera said. "That's what makes him dangerous and also what makes him slow. And I'm not branded yet. I'm *delayed*. There's a difference, and it buys me maybe two days." She leaned back, and for a moment the Justiciar mask slipped and I saw just a tired woman in her thirties who hadn't slept properly since a marsh full of singing-eyed men had tried to kill us both. "Tamsin. If you're wrong about this — if the king is just a man, a tired ordinary man on a chair — then we walk into that forecourt, we watch some singing, and I arrest you myself out of sheer embarrassment."

"If I'm right?"

She didn't answer that. She'd stopped answering that question three days ago, somewhere on the road, after the inn where every traveler admitted to hearing the same melody in their sleep. Some questions you stop answering because the answer has started answering itself.

* * *

The forecourt of Aldric's palace was built to make a human being feel like a spelling error. White stone, columns tall enough to have weather opinions, and everywhere — everywhere — bells. They hung from the colonnades in bronze clusters, from the gatehouse, from little ornamental frames set among the flowerbeds as though bells were a plant the aristocracy cultivated. Hundreds of them. All silent.

"That's wrong," I murmured.

"What?"

"Bells are hung to be rung. These are hung to be *seen* ringing." I nodded at a cluster near the gate. "Look at the clappers. Wrapped. Felt and wool, every one of them. Someone spent a fortune on all this bronze and then muzzled it."

"Maybe they value quiet."

"No one values quiet who builds this many bells. They value *control* of quiet." The ache behind my eyes, which had moved in permanently somewhere around the city gates, shifted its weight like a dog settling in for a long stay. "They're not decorative, Sera. They're a choir that's been told to hold its breath."

The crowd gathered in the forecourt was the finest collection of expensive fabric I had ever seen assembled in one place, and I include a funeral I once attended where the widow had upholstered the coffin. Lords and ladies, guildmasters, generals, foreign envoys fanning themselves with documents worth more than my entire valley. And threaded through them, in unobtrusive grey-and-white livery, courtiers — dozens of them, moving through the crowd with trays and programs and pleasant blank faces.

We found our place along the barrier, close enough to the dais that Sera's credentials earned us, and far enough back that we were furniture. An old lord beside me complained to his neighbor about the price of coal. A lady adjusted her fur. Somewhere a child whined about being bored. Ordinary noise. Human noise. I let it wash over me and tried to be only what I appeared: a big rural man in his dead father's coat, standing where he'd been told to stand.

Then the trumpets sounded, and the doors opened, and King Aldric came out to renew his kingdom's voice.

I want to describe him fairly, because fairness matters to me — it's the whole point of my trade, giving the dead a fair hearing. He was tall. He was dressed beautifully. He walked with the measured grace of a man who has done this ten thousand times, and his face —

His face was the problem. Not because it was monstrous. Because it was *nothing*. I've carved memorial pieces for forty years; I know what a face is supposed to do. A face is an argument the muscles are always having — grief arguing with dignity, joy interrupting boredom, all of it messy and alive. Aldric's face had settled the argument years ago and accepted the terms. It was handsome the way a portrait is handsome, and it moved exactly as much as a portrait moves.

And his mouth was already moving.

Not speaking. The trumpets were still playing. But his lips were shaping sounds, small and constant, the way a man mutters to himself — except that as he crossed the forecourt toward the dais, I realized the muttering wasn't coming only from him.

It was coming from everyone in grey and white.

Every courtier. Every servant with a tray. Every guard on the platform whose helmet hid everything but the jaw. Their mouths were all moving, all at once, all in the same rhythm, and none of them were looking at each other, and none of the nobles seemed to notice, or perhaps — worse thought — they had noticed so long ago that it had become scenery.

"Sera," I said quietly. "Tell me you see it."

"I see the king walking." Her voice had gone very level, the way it did before violence. "What do you see?"

"Count the servants' mouths."

She counted. I watched her count. I watched the moment land in her like a stone dropping into a well — that long pause before the splash.

"They're..." She stopped. Started again. "In time. They're all in time with him."

"With each other," I said. "He's not leading. Look — he's not looking at any of them. Nobody's leading. That's the thing about a chorus, Sera. It doesn't need a leader. It needs a *song*."

The king reached the dais. A herald cried out the ceremony's name — the Renewal of the Concord, ten years of harmony, praise upon the peaceful reign — and the crowd applauded, silk gloves on soft palms, a sound like rain on a roof. And beneath the applause, beneath the herald, beneath the trumpets, the hundred mouths kept moving, and now, standing that close, I began to catch the shape of what they were saying.

It wasn't words. That was the first horror. I'm a listener; words are my bread. This was pre-verbal — vowel sounds, sustained, braided, rising and falling in a pattern too regular to be music and too alive to be machinery. A drone with intention. And it was *loud*, if you had ears for it. To the crowd it was nothing, less than nothing, subliminal as a heartbeat in another room. To me it was a wall.

I did the stupid thing. I'll admit that freely. I reached into my coat and touched my grandmother's tuning fork, just to ground myself, just to have one true note to stand on against the flood.

The fork hummed against my palm — that clean, lonely A that had never once lied to me — and the moment it sounded, the wall *noticed*.

I don't know how else to put it. The drone changed. Not much. The way a conversation changes when someone new enters the room. A hundred mouths kept their rhythm, but the sound underneath bent toward me, the way grass bends toward a footstep, and for one vertiginous second I felt the whole vast weight of it turn its attention on the spot where I stood, the way you'd feel a hundred eyes even in the dark.

Then it went back to its business. Whatever its business was.

My hands were shaking. Sera's hand closed hard around my arm. "You've gone white as your own carvings. What happened?"

"It heard me listen." I got the fork back under my coat before anyone saw. "Sera, I need you to get me closer. I need to hear the center of it. I need to hear *him*."

"That's insane."

"Yes. Also necessary. Those are often the same thing."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her weigh it — her career, her neck, her oath, all of it stacked on one side of the scale, and on the other side a roomful of travelers who heard singing in their sleep and a sergeant's instinct that her orders smelled wrong. The oath lost. I think it had been losing for a while.

"Stay behind me," she said. "If anyone asks, you're my evidence escort. Walk like you're bored."

* * *

Walking like you're bored, for the record, is very difficult when every cell in your body wants to run. We drifted along the barrier, Sera flashing her badge at a pair of guardsmen who looked at her lanyard and not her face — rank is a wonderful blindfold — until we stood perhaps thirty feet from the dais, close enough to see the king's hands resting on the arms of the throne that had been carried out for the occasion.

Close enough to hear him properly.

Up close, the drone resolved, the way a chord resolves when you finally find its root note. And what I heard beneath it — beneath the hundred courtiers, beneath the wrapped bells, beneath the beautiful nothing of the king's face — was screaming.

I have heard the dead scream before. It's part of the work; you can't give rest to voices that died badly without hearing how they died. Drowned men scream in bubbles. Burned women scream in smoke. It's terrible, but it's *theirs*, and usually it fades once they're witnessed. This was different. This was twelve screams — I knew it was twelve the way you know the number of voices in a round — and they weren't fading, because they weren't being heard. They were being *used*. Every note of that perfect royal harmony was built on top of them, pressed down on them, the way a roof presses down on the people holding it up. They screamed and the scream was tuned. They screamed and the scream was called peace.

Ten years, the herald had said. Ten years of concord.

"Oh," I said. Out loud. I couldn't help it. It came out of me like a cough.

A courtier nearby turned her head. Grey livery, pleasant face, mouth still moving in time with all the others. She looked at me — actually looked, the first direct gaze anyone had given us all morning — and her mouth kept its rhythm while her eyes did something no mouth was doing. They focused. They *considered*. And then, without breaking stride, without changing the drone by a hair, she smiled at me.

It was the smile of a hostess greeting a guest she has been expecting.

"Tamsin." Sera's grip on my elbow turned painful. "Walk. Now. Don't run, walk."

I walked. It took everything I had. Behind us the ceremony rolled on — the herald's voice climbing, the crowd's applause swelling, the king rising from his borrowed throne to lift his arms in blessing over his people. And as we passed out of the shadow of the colonnade, I heard it one last time, clear as a bell with no felt on its clapper:

The drone shifted key. All hundred mouths together, seamless, effortless. Moving from the song they'd been singing into a new phrase, a short one, repeated twice, sweet and welcoming and absolutely aimed:

*Come and be harmonized.*

The child beside me tugged his mother's sleeve and asked why the flowers were humming. His mother told him not to be silly.

Flowers don't hum. But I understood the boy completely. Once you've heard it, the whole city hums — the bells, the drains, the wind between the towers, all of it resonating faintly with that patient, enormous chord. We walked back through streets that sounded, to me, like the inside of a throat.

At the chandler's shop, Sera bolted the door and stood with her back against it, breathing like a woman who'd sprinted though we'd only walked fast.

"Say it," she said. "Say what you heard. All of it. I need it in words, because what I saw was a ceremony, and ceremonies are boring, and I refuse to believe I nearly died of fear at a boring ceremony."

So I told her. About the drone. About the twelve voices under it, screaming in tune. About the courtier's smile. About the phrase the Choir had sung after me like a toast raised across a crowded room.

When I finished, she was quiet for a while. Then she said, in a voice stripped of all its sergeant polish, "The king. Is the king in there at all? Is there a *him* left?"

I thought about the face that had settled its argument. About the hands on the throne's arms, elegant and empty. About ten years of a man's life spent as a glove.

"There's a man in there," I said. "I heard him. Way down at the bottom, under everything — there's a thread of him still screaming too. He's not the thirteenth voice. But he's the twelfth voice's hostage." I rubbed my eyes, and the ache behind them pulsed in time with the city's hum. "Which means this isn't a haunting, Sera. Hauntings you can banish. This is a *crime scene* that learned to sing. Twelve murdered singers, wearing a king, ruling a country. And today it smiled at me and invited me to join."

"So what do we do?" she asked. "You can't fight a chorus with a carving knife."

"No," I agreed. "But every song is made of names. And names—" I pulled the courier's map from my coat, the wax seal cracked these many days, the humming in it louder than it had ever been, almost eager now, like a dog that smells home, "—names are made of bone. We need to find out who those twelve singers were. Their true names. Where they're written down. Who buried them and why."

Sera pushed off the door slowly. "There's one place in this city where banned things get remembered instead of burned," she said. "The palace undercroft archives. There's an archivist there they call—"

She stopped. Outside, faint and far away, every unwrapped bell in the capital rang the hour — and rang it slightly, unmistakably, flat.

"—Maren Corvane," she finished. "Blind as a stone. Which means she's the only person in this city who listens more than she looks."

I picked up my coat. Somewhere out in the humming dark, something patient had already started learning my name.
