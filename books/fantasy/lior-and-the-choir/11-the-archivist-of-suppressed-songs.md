## 11. The Archivist of Suppressed Songs

I have been blind for thirty-one years, and in that time I have learned that most people are embarrassed by it. They lower their voices around me as though darkness were a kind of grief. They describe things to me unprompted—the color of a tapestry, the shape of a seal—because they cannot bear the thought of a world I am not seeing. They mean well. Most sins begin with meaning well.

So when two sets of footsteps came down my stairs at an hour when no honest person visits archives, I did not announce myself. I sat very still among my shelves and listened, because listening is the one sense I have refined past the point of politeness.

The first set of footsteps was a soldier's: even weight, boots worn on the outer heel, the small pause at every corner where training lives in the body. A Justiciar, then, or something shaped like one. The second was lighter, uneven, with the particular hesitation of a man who keeps checking whether he is being followed. He also smelled of bone dust and tallow and, faintly, of river water—valley water, if my nose still remembered anything, and my nose had never once lied to me about water.

"You said undercroft," the soldier whispered. "This is a crypt."

"This is a *library*," the man whispered back, offended. "The coffins are decorative."

They were not decorative. They were practical. Dead archivists make excellent insulation against damp, and none of them have ever complained about my filing system.

I let them wander far enough down the aisle to lose their nerve, and then I spoke.

"If you're here to burn something," I said, "you've come to the wrong shelf. Everything flammable was confiscated years ago by people who were much better at burning than either of you will ever be."

The soldier's hand went to her sword—I heard the leather creak—and the bone-carver made a sound like a kettle deciding whether to scream. I lit the lamp I keep for occasions such as this, not because I need light but because sighted people trust you more when they can watch your face do nothing in particular.

"Sit," I said. "Both of you. You've been walking for days, you smell like fear and cheap soap, and whatever question you came to ask, you'll ask it better sitting down."

* * *

Her name was Vane. Sergeant Sera Vane, Third Justiciar Company, though she gave it reluctantly, the way a person hands over a knife handle-first. The other one was Brightwater—Tamsin Brightwater—which meant nothing to me until he spoke, and then it meant everything, because I have spent thirty years cataloguing the sounds the Crown forbids, and his voice had the particular quality of a man who has spent his life listening to things the rest of us are not permitted to hear.

He wanted hymnals. Banned ones. He didn't say why, which was wise, and she kept her hand near her sword, which was wiser, and neither of them noticed that I had already decided to help them before they finished their first sentence.

You see, I knew why they'd come. Everyone who comes to me comes for the same reason, eventually. They have heard something they should not have heard, and the hearing has lodged in them like a fishbone, and they cannot swallow it and cannot spit it out, and so they come down into the dark looking for a woman who keeps the songs the palace calls *harmonized*.

"Harmonized," I told them, when Brightwater asked what the word meant. "That's the official term. When a village shrine sings something the Choir hasn't approved, the Crown harmonizes it. Very tidy word. Implies cooperation. In practice it means the shrine's bells come down, the shrine's priests go quiet—one way or another—and the village learns the approved melody instead. There is exactly one approved melody. You may have noticed it on your way through the city. It follows you like weather."

"It followed us from the border," Brightwater said quietly. "Every town. Every bell tower. Slightly flat."

"Slightly flat." I smiled, and I felt him flinch, because a smile from a blind woman always makes sighted people wonder what they're missing. "Yes. That's the tell. The Choir cannot hold pitch across distance—it isn't one voice, you understand, it's twelve, and twelve voices bound together drift, the way any family drifts. The courtiers call it richness. The musicians call it resonance. I call it what it is: the sound of dead women and men being stretched over a throne like drumskin."

The silence that followed had texture. Fear does. It has a grain, like wood, and after thirty years in the dark I can read it the way sighted men read print.

"Twelve," Brightwater said. His voice had gone careful, the way voices do when they're carrying something breakable. "You know how many."

"I know their names," I said. "All twelve. Do you know how rare that is? The court burned the chorister rolls within the year. Burned the pay ledgers, the service records, the letters home. One of them—a girl named Odile Ferrant, first soprano, nineteen years old—her mother wrote to the palace asking where her daughter had gone, and the reply was signed by a clerk whose own signature appears nowhere else in any surviving document. The Crown invented a man to answer one grieving letter, and then unmade him again. That is thoroughness. That is a machine that has thought about what it's doing."

Vane shifted. I heard her armor settle, and beneath it, fainter, the sound of a woman reorganizing her entire understanding of her employer. I have heard that sound many times. It never gets less interesting.

"That's treasonous talk," she said, but her heart wasn't in it. Her heart was somewhere else entirely, probably standing in a throne room watching a king sing.

"Sergeant," I said kindly, "everything in this room is treasonous. This shelf is treason. This shelf—" I put my hand on it, feeling the spines through the wood, each one a small rebellion in calfskin— "is forty years of hymnals from villages that no longer exist, copied out by priests who knew what would happen to them, smuggled to me by couriers who knew even more clearly. Half the men who carried these books died on the road. The other half stopped writing. I keep them because someone must, and because I am, by the standards of everyone who matters, a harmless old blind woman in a basement. Harmlessness is the best disguise I know. Better than yours, frankly. Yours smells of sword oil and conscience."

* * *

Brightwater asked the question I had been waiting for. He asked it gently, the way you ask a wound how it happened.

"What were their crimes? The twelve."

And there it was. The fishbone. So I told him, because it was time someone told him properly, with the names attached, and because—I will be honest, since honesty is the only luxury I afford myself down here—because I was tired of being the only one who knew.

"The old king was called Maric the Second, and the histories will tell you he was a peacemaker, because the histories were written afterward, by the same machine. What Maric actually did, in the winter of his ninth year, was sell the northern garrisons. Sold them—not weakened them, not neglected them, *sold* them—to the Kessarin leagues, along with the census of every settlement north of the Greywater. Fourteen thousand people marched into slavery or worse because their king preferred league gold to levy steel, and the gold bought him ten years of not having to ask the lords for taxes."

"How do you know?" Vane asked. Not challenging. Hungry.

"Because twelve choristers found the correspondence." I traced the shelf edge with my fingers, an old habit, reading the wood like braille. "Royal choristers see everything, Sergeant. They sing at every ceremony, they stand behind every throne, they are furniture with ears. Twelve of them—twelve, and I want you to hold that number, because the number matters—found the Kessarin letters in the old king's private cabinet while preparing for a winter concert. I don't know why they looked. Curiosity, perhaps. Or perhaps one of them had a brother in the northern garrisons. The record doesn't say. The record says only what they did next, which was the one thing the machine could not permit."

"They tried to tell someone," Brightwater said.

"They sang it." I smiled again, and this time I let it be bitter. "That's the part the court understood least, and feared most. They didn't run to the lords—they knew the lords would bury it to protect their own rents. They didn't print it—there was nothing to print with that would survive the week. They did the only thing they had authority over. They changed the music. At the Feast of Saint Aldhelm, before the full court, the royal choir sang the Advent canticle with the Kessarin figures woven into the descant—dates, sums, the count of sold garrisons, hidden in counterpoint where only trained ears would catch it. And the court was full of trained ears. Half the ambassadors' chapels could read a descant the way you read a street sign."

"And the king heard," Vane said.

"The king heard. And Maric—who was, I will grant him this, never stupid—understood instantly that he could not simply arrest twelve beloved singers and call it treason, because then everyone would ask what the treason consisted of, and the answer was sitting in his own cabinet. So he did something worse. He waited until they slept—he had their quarters, he had keys, he had servants who belonged to him and not to God—and he had them killed quietly, all twelve, in a single night. And then, because Maric understood power the way a butcher understands cattle, he did not bury the crime. He *used* it."

Brightwater had gone very still. Stillness has a sound too, when you're listening for it: the absence of breath, the held weight of a body refusing to move.

"There was a binding-rite in the archive even then," I went on. "Older than the dynasty. A way to take a voice at the moment of death and keep it—keep it singing, keep it obedient, keep it *available*. Court sorcerers had used it for generations on songbirds and, once or twice, on inconvenient witnesses. Maric's people took all twelve voices before the bodies cooled. And then they discovered—this is the part I want you to appreciate, because it explains everything that followed—that a bound chorus of murdered innocents does not merely sing. It *persuades*. Grief has a frequency. Wrongness has a frequency. Put twelve murdered voices behind a king's words and every listener feels, without knowing why, that the words are true. That the sorrow in them is real. Because it is real. It is the truest thing in the room. It is just aimed like a weapon."

"That's what wears the crown now," Brightwater whispered. "Not Maric. The Choir itself."

"Maric died eleven years ago, as kings do. And the Choir sang the succession—sang Aldric onto the throne, sang the lords into agreement, sang the realm into a decade of perfect, flat, harmonized quiet. Whether Aldric was chosen or taken, I cannot tell you. Perhaps he was ambitious and thought he could use it. Perhaps he was simply next in line and stood too close. But I will tell you what I have deduced from thirty years of banned hymnals and one very patient correspondence with a dead woman's mother: the Choir does not serve the king. The king serves the Choir. It was built from betrayal, and betrayal is the only command it understands, and every decade or so it requires renewal, because even bound voices fray." I turned my face toward where I judged Brightwater to be standing. "Midwinter, young man. The renewal rite. You've heard of it, or you wouldn't be here."

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. His silence confirmed it the way a seal confirms wax.

* * *

Vane stood up then. I heard the chair scrape and her breathing change, quick and shallow, the breathing of a woman whose oath has begun to digest itself.

"You're saying the institution I've served for nine years is—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way soldiers rebuild a wall that's been shelled. "There are procedures. There are courts. If this were true, there would be—"

"A court?" I said. "Sergeant. Who appoints the judges? Who harmonized the last three tribunals that looked too closely at palace accounts? You are holding a torch inside a house built of paper, and asking why nobody has reported the fire." I softened my voice, because cruelty is easy in the dark and I try to ration it. "Your orders brought you after a courier's map. Ask yourself who gave those orders, and whether that person answers to the king, or to the thing that speaks for him. Ask yourself why a map required a Justiciar at all, when a thief would have been cheaper and quieter. They sent you because you're honest, child. Honest people make the best instruments precisely because they don't know they're being played."

She sat back down. I counted her breaths—seven of them—before she spoke again, and when she did, her voice had changed key. Lower. Truer.

"The courier," she said. "Dennel. He was mine. I trained him. They told me he stole the map and ran."

"He didn't steal it," Brightwater said softly. "He was given it. By someone inside. And he died getting it to me, and his ghost asked me—" A pause. "He asked me not to open it. And then he asked me to open it anyway. Both things. The dead are allowed to contradict themselves. It's one of the few freedoms left to them."

I reached beneath my desk, into the drawer I keep locked with a lock that exists mostly for tradition, and drew out the bundle I had been waiting thirty years to hand to someone. Wrapped in oilcloth, wrapped again in wool, wrapped a third time in a prayer I stitched myself, badly, because blindness and needlework are poor companions.

"Twelve names," I said. "I have spent half my life gathering them. Odile Ferrant. Brannoc Hale. The twins, Isolde and Maren—yes, my namesake; her mother and mine were friends, and naming a child for a murdered chorister was its own small treason, and my parents committed it gladly. I won't recite them all now. The list is in the wrapping, along with something better than a list."

I put the bundle in Brightwater's hands and guided his fingers to the seam.

"Finger bones," I said. "Recovered over years, at absurd cost, from the paupers' trench where the bodies were dumped unnamed. Carve their true names into them—your craft, carver, the one craft the Choir cannot counterfeit—and the binding loosens. But hear the cost, and hear it whole: the names must be spoken aloud, in the Choir's presence, by a living voice. And the Choir does not release what it holds without taking a price. Anyone who sings those twelve names into that hall will trade away something the Choir considers valuable." I hesitated, and hated myself for hesitating, and said it anyway. "Likely the hearing itself. Possibly more. The rite is old and greedy and I cannot promise you which."

Brightwater was quiet for a long moment. Then I heard him unwrap the wool, carefully, the way one handles a sleeping animal, and touch the bones, and I heard his breath catch—the breath of a craftsman recognizing material worth respecting.

"These were people," he said finally. "Before they were weapons."

"They were victims first," I told him. "Weapons second. Remember the order. The order is the whole moral of the story, and the court has spent eleven years teaching the country to read it backward."

* * *

They left before dawn, because sensible fugitives leave before dawn, and I stood at the top of my stairs listening to their footsteps fade—hers even and disciplined, his uneven and afraid, both of them carrying more than they had descended with.

Then I heard a third set of footsteps above. Heavy. Deliberate. Boots with new heels, palace-issue, descending my stairs with the unhurried confidence of someone who has never once been told no.

High Justiciar Ossler had come to my archive twice before in my life. Both times, he left empty-handed, because both times I was harmless.

I blew out the lamp, folded my hands, and began composing in my head the blandest possible greeting, while upstairs, three floors of banned hymnals waited between his soldiers and the truth, and somewhere below the city, twelve unnamed bones lay newly carved in a stranger's keeping.

I had perhaps four minutes. I intended to spend them well.
