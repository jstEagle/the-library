## 12. Names in Bone

Maren Corvane kept her reliquary where other people kept their sins: close, and wrapped in linen.

She brought it out from beneath the floor of her reading cell in the undercroft archives—three boards up, a cavity the size of a bread loaf—and set it on the table between us with the reverence of a woman setting down something heavy she'd carried too long. The candle guttered. Somewhere above us, the city hummed its one flat note, the way it always did now, like a throat that had forgotten how to do anything else.

"Twelve," she said. "Count them."

I counted them. Twelve finger bones, each no longer than my thumb, yellowed to the color of old tallow, laid out in two rows of six on a scrap of velvet that had once, I'd have wagered, been part of something grander. Chorister's robes, maybe. A banner. The Crown was good at cutting things into pieces it could use.

"They were recovered from the crypt," Maren said. "By me, over eleven years, one at a time, under pretense of cataloguing flood damage." Her blind eyes found me with unsettling accuracy. "The archivists who came before me thought they were animal remains. The ones before that didn't think at all. That's rather the point of this place."

"You stole them."

"I *rescued* them," she said, "which is what theft is called when history turns out to be on your side. Ask me again in ten years and I'll tell you which word I prefer."

I looked at the bones for a long time. I'm a bone-carver; I've handled more dead things than living ones, and there's a way you learn to look at bone—not as an object but as a door. Most doors are shut. Most stay shut. These twelve were not only open; they were screaming, quietly, at a pitch only someone like me could hear, and even here, even in the dead valley's cousin-silence of the undercroft, the sound came through like water through a cracked wall.

"They're still awake," I said.

"Oh, they never slept." Maren folded her hands. "That's the cruelty of it. Binding isn't rest, Master Brightwater. Binding is being made to sing when you would rather scream, forever, on command, for the men who killed you. If you've come all this way to give them peace, understand what peace costs them first. They have to be *heard*. Truly heard. Their names, spoken aloud, in the Choir's own presence, by a voice that can carry them."

"And the speaker?"

Her hands went still. For a blind woman she had a remarkable talent for looking away.

"The names are bound," she said. "Bound things hold their binding until something pays the toll. You know this. It's why your valley works—the silence there is a kind of payment, isn't it? The land gave up magic so the dead could speak cleanly. Everything true has a price written underneath it." She turned her face toward me fully then. "Speaking the names aloud will unbind the Choir. And the unbinding will take the speaker's hearing of the dead. Possibly his warmth. Possibly—" she made a small, precise gesture, "—everything."

"*Possibly* everything," I repeated. "How comforting. A range of outcomes."

"I'm an archivist, not a prophet. Precision requires records, and no one has ever done this and lived to file a report." She almost smiled. "Though I suppose if you succeed, you won't be filing anything either."

* * *

I want to tell you I hesitated. Some part of me wants very much to be the sort of man who weighs his life on scales and finds the arithmetic tragic and noble. But the truth is uglier and simpler: I sat in that cold undercroft with twelve murdered singers' finger bones in front of me, and all I felt was relief.

Not joy. Not courage. Relief—the specific, shameful relief of a man who has been carrying a question for weeks and finally hears the answer he already knew. Because I'd known since the ford. Since the courier died holding my wrist and his ghost stood in the reeds begging me not to open the map, and I opened it anyway, because that's what I do: I hear what the dead need and I do it, and somewhere along the road I'd understood that this errand ended with me. There was no version of the story where Sera Vane sang the names. There was no version where Maren Corvane climbed the palace steps. The map led to me. It had always led to me.

So yes. Relief. My grandmother would have boxed my ears for it.

"Tell me about them," I said instead. "The twelve. Not how they died—I've heard enough of how people die. Tell me who they were."

Maren's whole posture changed. This, I realized, was the lecture she'd been waiting eleven years to give.

"Anselm Roke, first voice, forty-one years old, kept bees and wrote letters to his sister every week that the censors never caught because he hid the real sentences in the margins as musical notation. Ilset Vay, contralto, who bit a Justiciar's hand when they came for her and laughed while they held her down. The twins, Perrin and Odile Cass, who shared a bunk and finished each other's runs. Tammas Brightwater—"

I looked up sharply.

"—no relation," she said dryly, "though I did wonder, when your name crossed my desk. He was the youngest. Seventeen. He survived three days in the cells before they took his voice, and the record says he spent those days teaching the others a round so they'd have something to hold onto." She paused. "They used it, afterward. In the Choir. The binding works better when the voices harmonize willingly, at first. That's the filth of it. They made the victims make the weapon comfortable."

I picked up the youngest bone—Tammas's, left index finger, callused ridge along the side where a pen or a lute string had worn it—and held it the way I hold all bone: cupped, warm, listening.

He was there. Faint, frayed, but there, and what came through wasn't terror anymore. Terror burns off after ten years. What was left was something worse and better: patience. The terrible patience of the dead who know someone is coming eventually, if they can just keep singing.

*I taught them a round,* the ghost said, in the wordless way ghosts speak, meaning pressed directly into the bone. *It goes like this. Don't let them forget it goes like this.*

"I'll learn it," I told him. Out loud. Maren tilted her head at the sound of a man answering an empty room, but she didn't ask. Blind archivist. She'd heard stranger conversations in this room, I'd have bet my tools on it.

* * *

Sera found us an hour later, coming down the archive stairs with the particular walk she used when she wanted to look like she belonged somewhere: shoulders square, pace bored, eyes missing nothing. She'd gotten better at sneaking since the marshes. Or worse, depending on whether you were the thing being snuck past.

"Well?" she said.

"He'll do it," Maren answered for me, which was rude, and accurate.

Sera looked at the bones on the table. Then at me. I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd been doing, and I watched her arrive at the sum and hate it.

"There has to be another way," she said. "There's always another way. That's half of what the law *is*—finding the other way so nobody has to die on the steps of the problem."

"Is it?" I asked. Gently, I hope. "Because from where I sit, the law found plenty of ways around this for ten years, and every one of them ended with twelve people still screaming inside a king."

"That's not fair."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is going to be fair. Fairness left the building around the time someone decided murder was a governance strategy." I turned the smallest bone over in my palm. "Ask me if I want to die, Sergeant. Go on."

She didn't ask. She sat down across from me instead, armor creaking, and put her elbows on the table like a woman settling in for a long night, which was exactly what it was going to be.

"What do you need?" she said.

That, more than anything Maren had shown me, nearly undid me. Not permission. Not absolution. Logistics. Sera Vane's love language, if she had one, was procurement.

"Light," I said. "Good light. Time. And everyone in this city to keep not knowing we're here for as long as possible."

"The first two I can manage," she said. "The third is above my pay grade and rapidly deteriorating."

* * *

Carving a true name into bone is not like carving a charm. I should explain, because everyone assumes it's the same work with fancier letters, and everyone is wrong.

A charm is a window. You cut a shape into bone—a spiral, a knot, a running stag—and the shape gives the dead somewhere to lean their voice against, the way a nail holds a picture. Any carver with steady hands and a quiet valley can do it. I did my first at nine, a luck-knot for a fisherman, and got the curve wrong, and the poor drowned man's blessing came out sounding like a complaint about the weather. Grandmother made me redo it three times.

A true name is not a window. A true name is the person. Cut it wrong and you don't get a muffled blessing—you get nothing, ever, because you've spent the only copy. There are no drafts in this work. There is no *redo it three times*. Every stroke I put into these twelve bones was a stroke I could never take back, carved into the remains of people who had already been robbed of everything except the one thing I was now trusted to handle.

No pressure, as Sera would say, with that flat soldier humor of hers.

I worked through the night by three candles and a lamp Maren produced from somewhere illegal. The undercroft was cold enough that my breath showed, which helped—cold keeps bone honest, less likely to chip, more willing to take a fine line. I started with Anselm Roke, the beekeeper, because Maren had his name first in her banned hymnals and because starting with the oldest grief seemed polite.

His name was longer than I expected. True names usually are—they're not the name on the tax roll, they're the name the soul answers to, and souls are wordy. His ran to nine syllables and a rising terminal note, and when I sounded it out under my breath, testing, the bone in my hand warmed like a stone left too near a fire, and somewhere far above us, in the humming city, one bell rang a quarter-tone sharp for exactly one second.

Maren's head came up like a hound's.

"Do that again," she whispered.

"It hurt something up there."

"It *surprised* something up there." She was smiling, and it was not entirely a nice smile. "Ten years, that Choir has sung the same note into every ear in this city, and you just made it flinch. Carve faster, Master Brightwater."

I carved. Anselm. Ilset, whose name tasted of iron and laughter. Perrin and Odile, whose twin names interlocked like fingers, and I carved them on their bones facing each other so the lines would touch when the bones lay side by side. The chorister Tammas, whose name ended in the round he'd taught the others—I carved the melody itself into the whorls of his fingerprint ridges, tiny as thread, because some things belong in the skin and not the surface.

Hour by hour the bones warmed. Hour by hour, faintly, distantly, the city's hum developed cracks. Little ones. Stutters. Twice, walking past the archive stairwell, Sera reported that the guard rotation upstairs had stopped mid-step, all four men at once, like dancers losing the count, and then carried on pretending nothing happened.

"They know," she said, on her third trip down. "Whatever you're doing, the thing wearing the king can feel it. Ossler's been summoned to the palace twice today. When Ossler gets summoned twice in one day, people start disappearing."

"Then we're nearly out of time," Maren said placidly, "and none of you have asked me the important question."

We both looked at her.

"Which is?"

She turned her blind eyes on me with terrible gentleness. "Whether you can actually do it. Speaking the names, I mean. Carving is craft, and you clearly have craft to spare. But the speaking happens in front of the Choir, in front of the court, with the full weight of the binding pushing back at you. It will not be like talking. It will be like shouting into an avalanche and expecting the mountain to apologize." She folded her hands. "Have you ever sung, Master Brightwater? In your life? Once?"

I thought about it honestly. "At funerals," I said. "Under my breath. Mostly I let the dead do the singing."

"Then we have work to do," said Maren Corvane, "that has nothing to do with knives."

* * *

She taught me the way, I suspect, she'd once been taught: mercilessly, by repetition, with commentary. My voice, she informed me within the first quarter hour, was "adequate for lamentation and hopeless for anything requiring breath control." My pitch was "honest, which is a compliment I bestow rarely and means you are consistently, reliably flat." Sera, who had stayed to stand watch and pretended not to listen, laughed exactly once, into her gauntlet, and denied it afterward.

But somewhere around dawn—or what passed for dawn, sixty feet underground, marked only by Maren lighting a fresh candle and calling it morning—something shifted.

I was practicing Tammas's round, the one he'd taught the dying choristers in their cells, the one the Choir had stolen and worn for ten years. I had it wrong. I'd had it wrong for hours, some interval sitting crooked, and I stopped, frustrated, and held his bone up near the lamp and asked him, silently, *where does it bend?*

And the bone answered. Not in words. In the way a tuning fork answers—my grandmother's fork, sitting in my pocket all this while, suddenly hummed against my thigh, unprompted, and the correct interval dropped into my head whole, like a key turning.

I sang it right.

For one bar of music, in a cold room under a stolen city, I sang a seventeen-year-old's round correctly, and every candle in the undercroft leaned toward me like grass in wind, and Maren Corvane wept without any apparent awareness that she was doing it, and Sera Vane stopped pacing and stood very still with her hand over her heart as though something in there had been struck.

And far above us, muffled by stone and distance but unmistakable, the Hollow Choir faltered. All of it. The whole vast flat hum of the capital stuttered, caught, and resumed a half-beat late.

They heard it. The dead heard one verse of their own song, sung back to them rightly, and the machine built out of them tripped on its own strings.

I lowered the bone. My hands were shaking. Not from fear—from the effort, and from something else, something that had been quietly assembling itself in my chest all night and chose that moment to finish.

I was going to die doing this. I'd known it since the ford; I confirmed it somewhere around the second name. And standing there in the candle-smoke with twelve carved bones laid out in front of me like a completed sentence, I found that the knowledge had stopped feeling like a wall and started feeling like a door. Grandmother used to say that a charm-carver's whole life is learning the difference between the two, and that most people never learn it, and that the ones who do never get to explain it properly to anyone.

"Master Brightwater," Maren said softly, "you've gone very calm. It's unsettling."

"I've just realized something," I said. "The Choir needs the court. Midwinter. The renewal rite. It has to sing its binding in front of every noble in the realm, in the throne hall, with the king on the throne." I began packing the bones into their linen, each in its place, careful as a man loading a weapon he intends to fire exactly once. "It's going to gather every voice it owns into one room."

"Yes," Maren said. "And?"

"And I've spent my whole life waiting for the dead to come to *me*," I said. "One at a time. At the ford, at the graveside, in the valley. It occurs to me that in a few weeks, every voice I need to free will be in one room, at one time, already assembled." I tied the linen closed. "I'm not going to survive this. But I'll tell you what else I'm not going to do. I'm not going to spend my last weeks hiding in a basement, waiting for Ossler to find us. We're going to need a way into that ceremony."

Sera had gone pale in the lamplight. "Tamsin. The throne hall is the most guarded room in the kingdom, and I'm a branded traitor-in-waiting, and you're—" she gestured at all of me, "—*that*."

"I know," I said. "So the question, Sergeant, is this: how good are you at getting people into places they're going to die?"

She stared at me. Then, slowly, horribly, she started to smile—the first real smile I'd seen on her since the marshes.

"Better than I am at getting them out," she said. "Give me a day. And whatever you do—" she pointed at the linen bundle, "—don't let anyone hear you practicing."

Too late, I almost said. Far, far too late.

Above us, faint through sixty feet of stone, the bells of the capital rang the hour—and for the first time since I'd entered the city, they rang it slightly, unmistakably, afraid.
