## 13. Betrayal in the Bell Tower

I have been summoned to my superior's office forty-one times in six years of service. I know this because Justiciars count things—it's how we catch liars. Liars round up. Honest people remember the exact number and hate themselves for it.

This was the forty-second.

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a boy who wouldn't meet my eyes, on paper sealed with the full weight of the High Justiciar's office rather than the usual wax stub Ossler used for telling sergeants to stop drinking in the lower barracks. Full seal meant witnesses. Full seal meant record. Full seal meant that whatever happened in that room would happen *to* me, officially, with documentation.

I dressed in my best uniform. If a woman is going to be hanged, she should at least have creased her collar.

* * *

The High Justiciar's office sat at the top of the Bell Tower of Judgment, which was not, despite its name, where bells were kept. It was where judgments were kept—forty years of them, floor after floor of verdicts rendered in Ossler's neat, merciless hand. You climbed past your own history on the way up to be judged again. I had always assumed this was deliberate architecture. I now know it was.

Ossler stood at his window when I entered, hands clasped behind his back, watching the city wake below us. He was a small man, which surprised everyone meeting him for the first time; you expected the architect of a decade of perfect order to be larger. He wasn't. He was precise, the way a scalpel is precise, and he had eyes the color of dishwater that never once in six years had looked at me without already knowing what I'd say next.

"Sergeant Vane." He didn't turn. "You've been in the city nine days."

"Eight, sir."

"Nine." He turned then, and smiled the way frost forms. "You reported to the archive clerk within an hour of arrival. Diligent. You requested access to the ceremony rosters. Thorough. And in nine days you have filed—" he lifted a single sheet from his desk, held it between two fingers like something dead, "—nothing."

"The investigation is ongoing, sir."

"Is it." He set the sheet down. "Sit."

I sat. The chair across from his desk was famously uncomfortable; every Justiciar learned early that Ossler's office offered no comfort to anyone, including Ossler, whose own chair was said to be deliberately hard so that he never grew fond of sitting. The whole tower was built out of the principle that comfort softened judgment. Looking back, I understand that it was also built to keep anyone from ever being comfortable enough to think.

"You recovered the courier's effects," Ossler said. "The body was found at Brightwater Ford. The map was not on it."

"No, sir."

"Where is the map, Sergeant?"

"I don't know, sir."

He studied me for a long moment, and I did what Justiciars are trained to do: I gave him true answers to false questions. I didn't know where the map was. That was honest. Tamsin had sewn it into the lining of his instrument case three days ago, but *now*, at this moment, I genuinely could not have told Ossler its location, because I'd stopped keeping track precisely so that I could say this sentence without my pulse changing. Six years of training, and the most useful skill I ever learned was strategic ignorance.

"You were seen," Ossler said, "at the Inn of Nine Bells. In the company of a bone-carver from Brightwater Valley. A man named Tamsin Brightwater, who is carrying something the Court wants very much returned." He came around the desk and perched on its edge, close enough that I could smell the ink on his fingers. "And here is what interests me, Sergeant. Your orders were to recover the map and detain the courier's contacts. Instead you escorted him through the marshes. You lost two men to Choir-bound hunters—which, incidentally, you also did not report—and you brought him into my city and hid him in a musician's lodging house under a name that fools no one, because you are many excellent things, Sergeant Vane, but you are not subtle."

My heart was doing arithmetic I didn't like. "Sir, if I may—"

"You may not." He said it gently, which was worse. "Let me tell you a story instead. Ten years ago, there was a king. A real one, with his own voice and his own sins, and among his sins was a choir. Twelve singers. They sang for him, and they saw too much, and they died for it—not quietly, not cleanly, but thoroughly. And their voices did not die with them. Voices like that don't. So the Court did what courts do with inconvenient music: it arranged it. Bound it. Tuned it. Gave it a crown to wear and a kingdom to harmonize." He spread his hands, a small shrug, a man describing weather. "King Aldric has been the finest instrument this nation has ever produced. Under his reign—the Choir's reign—there has been no rebellion, no famine, no war. The provinces sing together. The roads are safe. Crime is at its lowest recorded level in three centuries. Tell me, Sergeant: what exactly has been stolen from you?"

I thought about the villages on the road north. Shrines defaced. Bells hanging silent. Grandmothers humming melodies they couldn't name and couldn't finish, the endings sanded off by decree.

"My brother's funeral hymn," I said.

Ossler blinked. Of all the answers, that one he hadn't predicted. Good.

"When I was nineteen," I said, "we buried Tam Vane, and the priest was required to use the Crown-sanctioned rite, and the sanctioned rite has four approved melodies, and we chose the second one because it was the least wrong. My mother cried anyway. Not because of grief. Because the song was flat. Everything since has been slightly flat, sir, and everyone pretends they can't hear it, and I am so tired of pretending."

The silence that followed had texture. Somewhere far below, the city went on humming its one permitted tune.

"That," Ossler said finally, "was almost poetry. Poetry is insubordination with better rhythm." He pushed off the desk and walked to a cabinet against the wall—dark wood, iron-bound, the kind of furniture that exists in offices to make visitors nervous about what's inside. He opened it and took out a scroll. "Here is what happens next, depending on you. Option one: I arrest you now. Concealing evidence. Fraternizing with a person of interest. Failure to report the deaths of two sworn men. That's a tribunal, a stripping of rank, and given the current climate, possibly a cell in the Harmonic Wing, where the acoustics are, shall we say, persuasive." He let that sit. "Option two."

He held out the scroll.

"Captaincy. Your own command, effective immediately. All charges dissolved as clerical error. And all you have to do is walk down the hill to the Lodgeway district, knock on a certain door, and hand over a frightened craftsman and whatever he's carrying. No violence necessary. He trusts you. That's the entire job, Sergeant: deliver the trust."

I looked at the scroll. Captain Sera Vane. My mother would weep, and this time the song could be anything she wanted.

"And the carver?" I asked. "What happens to him?"

"He'll be examined," Ossler said. "Carefully. By people whose business is understanding unusual gifts. And then he'll be either employed or contained, according to what's found." He smiled again, frost forming. "You've seen what he can do. Imagine it in service of order. A man who can hear the truth out of any mouth, living or dead, and carve it into proof. Do you know how many tribunals that ends? How many innocent men go free because the dead can testify? We could *reform* the courts, Sergeant. Real justice, at scale. Isn't that what you joined for?"

And there it was. The terrible thing—the thing I still haven't fully forgiven myself for—is that for about four seconds, I wanted it. Not the captaincy. The *justice*. A world where Sera's testimony mattered because the dead could back it up. Ossler hadn't offered me a bribe. He'd offered me my own oath, polished and returned, and asked only that I ignore whose hands it passed through.

Four seconds. Then I thought of Tamsin's face when he'd relayed my brother's last words to me in that inn room—*tell her the river wasn't her fault*—and how his hands had shaken afterward, how he'd apologized for the pain as though delivering a message were the same as causing it. That man was not a weapon waiting to be employed. He was a gravedigger. He gave the dead rest. Everyone else in this city, starting with the man in front of me, seemed professionally committed to making sure the dead got none.

"I need time," I said. "To consider."

Something flickered behind the dishwater eyes. Disappointment? Or satisfaction—I honestly couldn't tell, and that frightened me more than anything else in the room.

"You have until midwinter court," Ossler said. "Five days. After the renewal rite, the question resolves itself one way or another, and I would prefer it resolve with you standing beside me." He returned the scroll to the cabinet and locked it. "One more thing, Sergeant. A courtesy, since you've been candid with me today, in your fashion." He paused at the window, silhouetted against the waking city. "Your carver has been asking questions in the undercroft archives. About suppressed hymnals. About names." He turned his head just enough that I caught his profile, sharp as a blade's edge. "The archivist there is an old woman named Maren Corvane. Blind, half-deaf, harmless, and deeply illegal in her hobbies. If your conscience needs a nudge toward option two, consider what happens to her when the audit comes. Consider what happens to everyone who helped him. Guilt, in this tower, is a shared meal. Everyone at the table eats."

Dismissed. I saluted, turned, and walked down forty years of judgments toward the door, each step echoing, and I swear to you the stairwell hummed—a low, sweet, perfectly tuned note, like the building itself reminding me it was listening.

* * *

Tamsin knew before I said a word. That's the trouble with traveling with a man who reads the dead: the living might as well carry signs.

I found him in our rooms above the music shop, cross-legged on the floor with the reliquary open in front of him, carving. Twelve finger bones, twelve names, and he worked on them the way other men pray—with his lips moving and his eyes somewhere else entirely. He'd gotten thin. We both had. But his hands were steady, and I envied them.

"Ossler knows everything," I said, and shut the door. "The archive. Maren. The bones. Probably what you ate yesterday."

Tamsin didn't look up. His knife whispered along the curve of bone. "Did he offer you a promotion?"

"How—"

"You're clenching your left fist," he said. "You do that when someone's insulted you by thinking you're for sale. Also, you came in through the door like it owed you money." He finished a stroke, blew bone dust from the groove, and finally raised his eyes to mine. They were tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix. "What did he offer?"

"Captaincy. Clean record. My own command." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "He framed it as justice, Tamsin. That's the obscene part. Hand you over and the courts get reformed. The dead testify. Innocent men walk free. All I have to do is pretend you're a thing instead of a person."

"And Maren?"

"He mentioned her." My throat tightened. "'If the audit comes.' Which means the audit is coming whether I choose option two or not. I'm the delay, not the pardon. He wanted me to feel responsible for her so I'd move fast." I sat down across from him, on the floor, because the chairs in that room belonged to the shop and felt like witnesses. "Five days. Until midwinter court. Then it resolves 'one way or another.'"

Tamsin set down his knife. He picked up the bone he'd been carving and turned it in the light, and I saw the name taking shape along its length—half-finished letters, patient as roots.

"Then we have five days," he said.

"Tamsin." I leaned forward. "Listen to me. What he described—the examination, containment—that's real. I've seen the Harmonic Wing. There's a cell there they keep tuned to a pitch that makes people confess to things they haven't done. Whatever they do to you, it won't be employment. And Maren—"

"Maren stays until the last name is carved." He said it simply, the way he said most unbearable things. "She knows. She's known since she gave me the reliquary. She told me once that an archivist's whole life is making sure things survive long enough to matter. She considers this her final catalog entry." His jaw worked. "She also told me that if I tried to leave early out of sentimentality, she'd have me flogged with a rolled-up hymnal, and I believe her. Have you met her?"

I had. The blind archivist who navigated her forbidden stacks by memory and terrorized trespassers with a cane and a vocabulary that would blister a barracks. Yes. I believed her too.

"So we run," I said. "Tonight. Out the south gate before the watch changes, take the river road—"

"Run where?" He gestured at the walls around us—not the room, the city, the idea of it. "Every gate has your colleagues at it. Every road out has hunters whose eyes sing. And even if we made the countryside, even if we made the valley—" he shook his head slowly, "—the Choir showed me Brightwater burning, Sera. Showed it to me through a child's mouth. It was lying, I'm nearly sure it was lying, but it *can find the valley*. There is no running that puts distance between us and a thing that wears a king. There's only finishing or failing."

I stared at him. "Finishing. You mean the throne room. Midwinter court. Singing twelve names in front of the entire nobility while guards close in, knowing it kills you." My voice cracked on the last part, and I hated it, and I let it crack anyway because some things shouldn't be said smoothly. "That's not a plan, that's a funeral with better attendance."

"It's a witness," he said. "There's a difference. Funerals are for the ones left over. This is for the twelve." He closed the reliquary, gentle as closing a coffin. "Besides. Who says it kills me? Maren says it costs the speaker everything they are to the dead. Maybe that's death. Maybe it's just—" he searched for the word, "—graduation."

"You're not funny."

"I'm a little funny."

I laughed despite myself, wet and short, and then pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes because the alternative was worse. Six years of oath. Six years of believing the law was a wall between the strong and the weak, and learning in one week that the wall had been built by the strong, facing outward, to keep the weak from seeing the view.

"Sera." His voice was quiet. "Whatever you decide. Delivering me buys Maren nothing—you know that now. And refusing costs you everything. I want you to know that I know the mathematics. I won't think less of—"

"Stop." I took my hands away from my face. "Just—stop. Please."

He stopped.

I thought about my brother. I thought about the flat hymn and my mother crying at a funeral that had been harmonized into meaninglessness. I thought about Ossler's scroll with my name on it, and about the fact that I had spent my whole adult life being loyal to an institution, and had mistaken the institution for the thing it was supposed to protect. The law wasn't the wall. The law was supposed to be the *people inside* it. And the people inside it were singing one note, slightly flat, forever, unless somebody broke the song.

"Here's what's going to happen," I said. "Tomorrow morning, I report to Ossler that I need more time. That buys a day, maybe two—he'll enjoy watching me squirm. Tomorrow night, you and I visit Maren, and we get the final name, and we get her out of that undercroft whether she consents or not, because I am not leaving a blind old woman to eat Ossler's shared meal. Day after, we find Sellen—my contact in the palace musicians' guild—and we get you into midwinter court with an instrument case." I looked at the reliquary in his lap. "And on the day of the renewal, you're going to stand up in front of the whole rotten harmony and sing those names, and I am going to stand next to you in full uniform, on the record, by choice, and dare every Justiciar in that hall to explain to history why the law was on the Choir's side."

Tamsin was staring at me. "Sera. They'll brand you. Traitor, oath-breaker, everything Ossler promised and worse. Your career, your name, maybe your life—"

"I know what it costs." I reached out and put my hand flat on the reliquary lid, over the bones of twelve murdered singers who had been silenced by exactly the kind of loyalty I used to have. "I've been doing the sums since I was nineteen years old and a hymn came out flat at my brother's grave. Loyalty isn't obedience, Tamsin. Nobody ever taught me that, so I'm teaching myself now, out loud, where the dead can hear it." I stood, and straightened my collar, and felt—for the first time in nine days, in six years, maybe in my whole careful life—entirely unafraid of the number forty-two. "Loyalty is remembering who you actually serve."

For a moment he just looked at me. Then he smiled—small, exhausted, real—and began wrapping the reliquary in cloth.

It was at that moment, with the last knot tied, that the bell in the shop below us rang.

Not the shop's little doorbell. The great municipal bell in the tower three streets east—the one that marked the hours, the one that had rung slightly flat for a decade like every bell in this city. It rang once, clear and true and *perfectly pitched*, a sound so right it hurt, and beneath it, faint as breath, I heard—or Tamsin heard, and I heard him hearing it—a hundred voices exhale at once.

Tamsin went white. "They know," he said. "Sera—they're not waiting for midwinter. The renewal is moving up. It's happening *tonight*."

Somewhere across the city, the flat bells began to ring in answer—one, then ten, then all of them, a sound like a net closing—and I grabbed my sword and my coat and the last shreds of my lawful life, and ran for the stairs.
