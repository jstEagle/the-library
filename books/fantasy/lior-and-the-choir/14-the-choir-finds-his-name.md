## 14. The Choir Finds His Name

I had carved forty-one bone charms in my life before the courier died at the ford, and I could have told you the exact weight of each one—the heft of a lamb's knuckle, the hollow song of a goose's wing-bone, the way a deer's rib wants to split along its grain if you're careless with the burin. Forty-one charms, forty-one voices given rest, and not one of them had ever asked me for anything back.

The reliquary Maren Corvane pressed into my hands weighed less than any of them.

Twelve finger bones, wrapped in linen gone the color of old teeth, each one waiting under my knife for its true name. I sat in the rented room above the Drowned Lantern with Sera's spare dagger whetted to an edge that could split a thought, and I worked by candlelight because daylight in the capital felt like being watched through a thousand windows. The names came slowly. Maren had given me eleven from her banned hymnals and marginalia and one name—a woman called Vess, second alto, who'd been the first to die—pressed into my memory alone, spoken once, softly, like handing me a coal and asking me to carry it across a river.

*Vess.* I turned it over in my skull while my hands worked on Lirien's radius, cutting the letters small and clean. A plain name. A name someone had shouted up a staircase once, probably, calling her to supper.

"You're doing it again," Sera said from the doorway. She'd taken to standing watch with her back to the wall where she could see both the stairs and the window, which told me everything about how much she trusted this city. "That thing where you go somewhere else and your jaw does the talking."

"I'm not talking."

"Your lips move when you carve. It's unsettling." She came in properly, set down bread and hard cheese she'd bought from a vendor whose stall she'd checked twice for Choir-touched eyes, and sat across from me with the particular heaviness of a woman who had burned her own life down three days ago and was still deciding whether to regret it. "Ossler's men searched the archive district this morning. Maren's undercroft is sealed. They're calling it sedition materials."

"They're right," I said. "It is seditious. The truth usually is, when the lie has been wearing the crown for ten years."

"That's not funny."

"I wasn't joking. That's the worst part." I blew bone dust off the fourth name and held it up to the candle. *Lirien Ashvane.* The letters caught light like frost. Four done, eight to go, and every one of them a debt I intended to pay with interest.

Sera watched me work for a while. Then, quieter: "The child followed you again today."

My knife stopped. "What child?"

"Girl. Maybe ten. Gray dress, no shoes—no, one shoe, left foot. She stood across the lane from the Lantern for an hour this afternoon. When I went out, she was gone." Sera tore the bread and handed me half, an old soldier's habit, feeding the person you might need to carry you tomorrow. "Beggars watch rich doors. We're not rich. She wasn't begging."

I ate the bread without tasting it. Somewhere below us, the city hummed its flat, wrong note, the one note it had hummed since we came through the gates, the melody every street performer played whether they knew it or not. My skull had learned to ache around it, a dull pressure behind the eyes like weather coming.

"She's watching me," I said. "Not us."

"How can you be sure?"

"Because the Choir doesn't care about Justiciars anymore. You're branded. You're noise. I'm the thing it needs to silence." I looked at the reliquary, at the six blank bones still sleeping in their linen. "And it knows exactly where I sleep."

---

It came for me two nights later, and it did not come as a monster.

I should say that plainly, because everything afterward depends on it. It did not come with fire or shrieking or any of the theater you'd expect from a chorus of murdered singers wearing a king like a coat. It came as a child in a gray dress with one shoe, sitting on the step of the Drowned Lantern at dawn, humming.

I knew the tune. Of course I knew the tune. Everyone in the capital knew the tune; that was rather the point. But the child hummed it a half-step flat, deliberately, the way you'd sing a hymn wrong in church to see if anyone flinched—and I flinched, and she looked up at me with eyes that were brown and ordinary and utterly empty, like windows painted onto a house nobody lived in.

"You're the carver," she said. Her voice was a girl's voice. Underneath it, faint as a second heartbeat, something else spoke in unison, a hundred throats compressed into one small mouth. "Tamsin Brightwater. Son of Marta. Grandson of Odile, who made the fork."

Every hair on my arms stood up and saluted. "You've been reading my mail."

"We read everything written in voice." The child smiled. It was a sweet smile. It was the worst thing I had ever seen. "Sit, carver. We would bargain."

"Bargains imply two parties with something to lose." I stayed standing. My hand found the tuning fork in my pocket—grandmother Odile's, cold silver, the only object that ever left Brightwater and came back unchanged—and I didn't draw it. Not yet. Drawing early tells your enemy what frightens you. "I'm listening anyway. Bad habit."

"The valley," the child said, and my heart, traitor that it is, lurched toward the word like a dog hearing its name. "Brightwater. Where magic cannot enter. Where the dead speak cleanly and are given rest. Where you were safe for thirty-one years." She tilted her head. "Do you know what we hear when we listen there, carver?"

"Silence," I said. "It's famous for it."

"We hear *you*. A single thread of true-hearing in a world going deaf. Do you know how loud you are? Like a bell rung inside a tomb." The child rose, unhurried, and the morning market noise around us seemed to lean away, vendors hawking their wares a little further off, cart wheels turning a little softer, as though the whole street had agreed to give us privacy. "We do not want your bones, Tamsin Brightwater. We want your quiet. Give us the reliquary and the names, and we will forget the valley exists. No hunters. No Justiciars. No fire."

"No fire," I repeated. "Odd thing to offer unprompted."

The child's smile widened a fraction too far, the way a door opens a fraction too far into a room you thought was empty.

---

Here is what happened next, and I tell it honestly even though honesty makes me look like a fool, because a man who lies about his own weakness has no business handling other people's dead.

The child reached out and touched my wrist. Small fingers, cold as river stones. And the world folded.

I was standing in Brightwater.

Not remembering it—*standing in it*. The ford, brown and patient. The wardens' turf-roofed hall with its smoke-smell. My own workshop with the burins ranked in their leather roll and the shavings on the floor that I always meant to sweep and never did. Morning light came through the window at the angle it comes in autumn, slanting gold across the carving bench, and I could smell the tallow and the juniper smoke and the wet wool of the mother who'd come to me last spring about her drowned son—

Except the workshop was burning.

Flame moved through it with the slow, deliberate grace of something that had all the time in the world. I watched my bench blacken and curl. I watched the roof beams go. I heard—I *heard*, with the ears that hear the dead and apparently everything else besides—the screams of the wardens, of Old Bram who taught me to read grain, of children I'd carved charms for, their voices rising in the magic-dead air where nothing muffled them, nothing softened them, nothing gave them rest.

The valley that refuses magic, burning. Because of me. Because I'd walked out of it carrying names like lit coals, and the fire had simply followed the thread home.

"No," I said. My voice sounded very young. "No, they're not part of this. They don't know anything. They're *nothing*—"

"Everything burns eventually," the child said, from everywhere, from inside my own mouth. "The question is only what you save. Save them, carver. One small reliquary, and the thread goes cold, and the valley keeps its silence, and no one ever follows you home again." Her fingers tightened on my wrist, gentle as a nurse. "You have until midwinter court. Eight days. Leave the bones at the shrine of Saint Ellowen on Tanner's Row. Walk away. Go home. Carve your little charms. Hear your little ghosts."

"And if I refuse?"

The vision shifted, and I saw the ford again, and this time the water was red, and this time the screaming had a face I knew.

---

I came back to myself kneeling in the street with Sera holding my shoulders and shouting my name, and the child was gone, and my wrist bore five small bruises like a handprint in reverse.

"Tamsin. *Tamsin.* What happened?"

"It talked to me," I said. My teeth were chattering. It wasn't cold. "Through the girl. It offered a trade." I laughed, and the laugh came out broken, one of those laughs that convinces no one, least of all the laugher. "It showed me Brightwater burning, Sera. Every beam. Every face. Bram. The wardens' kids. It showed me the ford running red, and it said—all it said was, give us the bones and it stops."

Sera went very still. Twenty years of soldiering will do that; she processed horror the way other people process weather reports. "Was it true?"

And there it was. The question underneath everything, the crack the whole lie was built to widen.

"I don't know," I whispered. "That's the genius of it. I can't hear Brightwater from here—it's too far, and the city's too loud, and even if I could—" I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "When I left, I heard singing on the wind behind me. Faint. I told myself it was the Choir reaching, but what if it was already there? What if they've been burning since the day I crossed the ford? What if every hour I spend carving these names is another hour the fire eats?"

"Then why hasn't Ossler's report mentioned it?" Sera said sharply. "Think. I was Justiciar-sergeant four days ago. I know how intelligence moves. If Brightwater burned—if a whole valley went up—the Court would crow about it. They'd use it to break you. Ossler would have shown me ash and told me it was my fault for choosing you." She gripped my chin and made me look at her. "There's no news of any fire. There's been *time* for news. The valley stands, Tamsin. It has to."

"*Has to* isn't *does*."

"No," she admitted. "But it's more than the Choir gave you."

She was right, and I hated that she was right, and I sat down on the step where the child had sat and put my head between my knees like a man seasick on dry land.

Because here is the truth I haven't said yet: for one long moment—one breath, maybe two—in that vision, I had wanted to say yes. Not because I believed the fire was real. Because wanting it to be negotiable was easier than carrying the weight of twelve dead singers and a dying king and a friend-turned-fugitive and a valley full of people who'd kept me safe for thirty-one years and deserved better than to become collateral in my war. The Choir hadn't offered me a bargain. It had offered me an exit, and some tired, cowardly cellar of my soul had already started packing.

The dead don't scare me. I've sat with them my whole life; they're just people with the volume turned differently. What scares me is the living, and what they ask, and how much of yourself you have to spend paying them back.

"I need to check," I said finally. "I need to *know*."

"How?"

I pulled out the tuning fork. Grandmother Odile's. Struck it once against my knee, and the clear note rang out over the flat hum of the city, and for a moment the street around us shuddered—the false harmony stuttering, a cart horse tossing its head, somewhere a bell tower ringing a half-beat out of step, annoyed, like a choir master hearing a wrong note.

And in the space the note opened, I listened south. Past the walls, past the fens, down the Road of Small Kings, all the way to a valley that refuses magic.

I heard wind. I heard the ford. I heard, very faintly, a warden's dog barking at nothing, and a woman singing a washing-song to herself, off-key, alive.

Nothing burned.

"They lied," I said. My voice cracked down the middle, relief and fury in equal measure. "Of course they lied. But Sera—they knew to lie about *that*. They looked inside my head and found the exact shape of what would break me, and they built a lie to fit it." I struck the fork again, harder, and let the note fade. "Which means they know about the bones. They know about the names. They know I have eight days of nerve left, and they intend to spend every one of them."

Sera crouched beside me. Up close, I could see how thin she'd gotten, how the fugitive life was sanding her down. "So what do we do?"

"What we should have done from the start." I stood up. My legs held. That surprised me. "Stop hiding. Stop reacting. They keep finding us because we keep standing still long enough to be found." I looked east, toward the palace district, toward the throne room where in eight days a dead chorus would renew its grip on a living king before the assembled nobility of a country that had forgotten what its own songs sounded like. "Maren said the binding weakens at renewal—that for the length of the rite, the Choir pours itself into the king, all of it, every voice, in one place, at one time."

"You want to walk into the lion's den during feeding hour."

"I want to walk into the *choir loft* during rehearsal," I said. "And I want to bring twelve names and a tuning fork, and I want to teach the whole court what their king sounds like when he screams."

Sera studied me for a long moment. Then she did something I hadn't seen her do since the marshes: she smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman who had finally stopped mourning the law she'd lost and started planning with the weapons she had left.

"Eight days," she said. "Midwinter court requires musicians. And I still have one favor owed to me by a Master of Ceremonies who cheats at cards."

* * *

That night I finished the fifth name and began the sixth, and my hands were steady, and my jaw did not move, and I did not look at the window even once.

Mostly.

Because once—just once, in the smallest hours, when the candle guttered—I heard, from the street below, a child humming. Half a step flat.

And I picked up my knife, and I kept carving, and I hummed the true melody back at her, loud enough to carry, until the humming stopped.

Eight days.
