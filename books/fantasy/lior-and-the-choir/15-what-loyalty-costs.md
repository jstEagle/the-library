## 15. What Loyalty Costs

I have been a Justiciar for eleven years, and in that time I have learned that the law is like a good sword: well-made, honest in its balance, and entirely indifferent to whose throat it ends up at. I believed in it the way you believe in a bridge. You don't think about the bridge. You just walk across.

That night, standing in the undercroft of the Royal Archive with Maren Corvane's hand pressed flat against the door and her blind eyes aimed at sounds I couldn't hear yet, I thought about bridges. Specifically, I thought about how they burn.

"They're early," Maren said. "Ossler never could resist being early to anything cruel. It's his one punctuality."

"How many?" Tamsin asked. He was crouched over the reliquary with his carving knife, and he didn't look up. I had begun to understand that this was not calm. It was the opposite of calm—the stillness of a man holding something breakable while the floor tilts.

"Twelve," Maren said. "Boots. And two of them are singing."

Tamsin's knife stopped.

* * *

Let me back up an hour, because the hour matters, and because I want to record it before the tribunals get hold of it and sand off all the edges.

We had spent three days in the undercroft. Three days of dust and banned hymnals and Maren's voice going soft as she read us the court's buried history—twelve choristers, murdered for what they knew about old King Aldric's crimes, their voices bound into an instrument of rule and hung around the new king's neck like a yoke. Tamsin carved names into finger bones by lamplight, one name per bone, each stroke precise as prayer. Eleven names done. The twelfth eluded us; Maren said it was kept somewhere even she hadn't found, sealed in a record Ossler himself had signed away.

I should have known then. A man doesn't sign away a record unless he means to keep it close.

I had sent no reports. That was my first crime, though I didn't know it was a crime yet—I thought of it as diligence, as verifying before I testified. But Ossler has eyes everywhere, and mine had stopped reporting, and silence from a sergeant is louder than any accusation.

So when the boots came down the archive stairs at the wrong hour, in the wrong numbers, I did the arithmetic fast: twelve men, one High Justiciar, two of us who couldn't fight our way out of a wet paper bag, and one blind old woman who refused to leave her books.

"There's a back way," Maren said. "Through the scriptorium. Sera knows it."

"I know it," I said, because I did—from a training exercise six years ago, which meant I knew where it *had been*, which is not the same thing.

"Go," Maren said. "Both of you. Take the reliquary."

"We're not leaving you," Tamsin said.

Maren smiled, and there was nothing gentle in it. "Boy, I am seventy-one years old, blind since birth, and I have spent forty years keeping dead songs alive in a cellar while the world above sang lies. Do you imagine I fear a little fire? Go. That's not a request. That's the last order anyone will give me, and I intend it to be obeyed."

The door shuddered. Once. Twice. Iron on old wood.

* * *

We went through the scriptorium because I'm a coward, or a professional, depending on which tribunal you ask. The back passage was exactly where I remembered it, behind a shelf of tax ledgers nobody has ever wanted to read, and we came up through a grate into the coal yard behind the chandler's row, and we ran.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the theme of my remaining career: I ran well. Eleven years of drills. Tamsin ran badly but persistently, clutching his instrument case like a man carrying a sleeping child through a riot. We made four streets before the bells started.

Not the real bells. The Choir's bells—that thin, flat tone underneath the city's evening ringing, the one I'd trained myself to stop hearing. Except now it wasn't ambient. Now it was *searching*. It moved through the streets like a lantern beam, and wherever it passed, people stopped in doorways and turned their heads toward us in unison, faces empty, mouths slightly open, humming.

"What do they see?" Tamsin gasped.

"Us," I said. "They see whatever the Choir tells them to see." I grabbed his collar and hauled him down an alley between a tannery and a pawnshop, into air so thick with render-fat and despair that even the Choir's song seemed to gag on it. "Listen. There's a safehouse. Old Justiciar cache, off the books, on Tanner's Walk. We can hold there until—"

"Until what?"

Until I figured out which side of the law I was standing on. Until the word *traitor* finished traveling from Ossler's mouth to every garrison in the city. Until I decided whether I was fleeing injustice or committing treason, because from the inside those feel identical, and only the historians get to pick the label.

"Tanner's Walk," I said again, and we went.

* * *

The cache was everything a cache should be: cold, dark, smelling of lamp oil and old decisions. Two bedrolls, a crate of hardtack, a wall of wanted-posters from previous decades that I now read with professional interest and personal horror. Tamsin sat on the crate and took out the reliquary and counted the bones with his fingertips, lips moving, checking his work.

"Eleven," he said. "She died for eleven."

"She died buying time," I said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

I didn't answer, because the answer was yes and no in proportions I couldn't calculate, and because at that moment the door of the cache rattled—not forced, just tested—and then a voice came through the wood, dry as old paper.

"Sergeant Vane. You always did favor the caches on Tanner's Walk. Sloppy sentimentality. I wrote you up for it once, if you recall."

Ossler. Alone, by the sound of it—one set of boots, unhurried.

I put my hand on my sword and looked at Tamsin. He shook his head slowly. I ignored him, because I was still, in some stubborn chamber of myself, a Justiciar, and Ossler was my superior officer, and some part of me wanted—needed—to hear him say it to my face.

I opened the door.

He stood in the alley with a lantern held high, and behind him, filling the street from wall to wall, stood perhaps sixty citizens of the capital, motionless, humming, their breath making little clouds in the cold. Not soldiers. Fishwives and clerks and a boy still holding a broom. The Choir's choir.

"You see the problem," Ossler said mildly. "I could order them to take you, but they're clumsy instruments. They'd damage the package." His eyes went past me to Tamsin. "The bones, Brightwater. And the name you got from Corvane. Give me both, and Sergeant Vane keeps her pension. Possibly her head. I make no promises about the order of those events."

"The twelfth name," Tamsin said quietly. "You have it."

"I have a great many things." Ossler stepped closer. "Do you know why the Choir works, Vane? Why a kingdom of eight million people lets a dead chorus wear its king? Because everyone hears it, just faintly, all the time, and it sings *enough*. Enough harmony. Enough certainty. People would rather be lied to beautifully than governed honestly—it's cheaper. Your oath, Sergeant, is to the lie. It always has been. The only question tonight is whether you're a good liar or a bad one."

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I'm allotted: for three full seconds, I considered it. Three seconds of imagining my old life continuing—reports filed, promotions earned, the bridge holding under my feet. Three seconds in which Maren Corvane was already dead anyway, so what would my betrayal add?

Then I heard the hum behind Ossler change pitch, and in the hum I heard—faintly, horribly—a voice I recognized. A woman's voice, worn smooth by a decade of forced harmony, screaming underneath the melody the way a scream lives under ice.

"No," I said. "I'm a terrible liar. Ask anyone."

I closed the door and threw the bolt, and Ossler laughed—actually laughed, warm as a hearth—through the wood.

"Oh, *good*," he said. "I hoped you'd say that. The alternative was tedious." His boots retreated. Then the humming rose, all sixty voices at once, and the door began to glow at its edges with a light that had no source, and I understood that we were no longer being arrested.

We were being sung at.

* * *

What happened next, I report as fact and decline to explain, because I don't understand it. Tamsin took out his grandmother's tuning fork and struck it against the crate, and the note that came off it was small and clean and utterly wrong for the room, and the glow at the door edges guttered like a candle in wind. The humming outside stuttered. Somewhere in the street, a woman screamed—a real scream, her own—and then another voice, and another, as if the fork's note were a needle pulling threads loose.

"It won't hold them long," Tamsin said. His face was gray. "The fens, maybe ten seconds. Here, with this many—" He struck the fork again. "Back way?"

"There's a sewer grate."

"A sewer."

"You've slept in a fen. You'll manage."

We went into the sewer, and I will spare the reader the details except to note that the Choir does not follow you into filth. Whatever binds those bodies wants vessels that shine. We waded north for an hour by my count, coming up through a storm grate in the tanners' district, and emerged into a city transformed.

Proclamation bills were already going up. I tore one off a post by lamplight. My own face looked back at me, rendered by a court artist who had flattered me even in treason—strong jaw, steady eyes, the words TRAITOR TO THE CROWN beneath. Beside it, a sketch of Tamsin that made him look like a bandit poet. *SERA VANE AND UNKNOWN ACCOMPLICE. THE HIGH JUSTICIAR CALLS ON ALL LOYAL SUBJECTS.*

"Loyal subjects," Tamsin read over my shoulder. "Meaning the ones who hum."

"Meaning everyone," I said. "That's the point. That's always been the point." I crumpled the bill. "Ossler won't come after us with soldiers anymore. He doesn't need to. Every bell in this city is his ear, and half its throats are his hands. We can't stay. We can't run—the roads will be watched by morning."

Tamsin was quiet for a moment. Then he said, in the voice of a man reading his own death warrant and finding the handwriting familiar:

"The palace."

"The palace," I agreed. "Midwinter court. The renewal rite. He'll be at maximum strength, surrounded by the whole Choir, in the most defended hall in the kingdom."

"So it's the worst possible plan."

"It's the only plan. Everything else is dying tired in a ditch." I looked at him—at this bone-carver from a valley I'd never heard of a month ago, holding a case full of murdered singers' fingers, wanted, hunted, out of options. "I'm sorry. This is what loyalty costs. I got you into—"

"Don't." He said it gently, which was worse. "Sera. Nobody got me into anything. I opened the map. I left the valley. I started carving the names." He touched the instrument case. "Maren told me something, the last night. She said: the dead don't need a hero, they need a witness who finishes the sentence. So we finish it."

Above us, the cathedral bells rang the hour, and underneath their ringing, flat and patient, the Choir sang on. Somewhere in that song, I was almost sure, was Maren's voice now—newly added, furious, unmistakable. Singing truth into a machine built to erase it, one note at a time, for as long as the machine let her.

"All right," I said, and picked up my pack. "Midwinter court is in four days. Getting in requires credentials I no longer legally possess, a disguise, and a miracle."

Tamsin smiled for the first time since the archive. It was not a comforting smile.

"I know a man," he said, "who owes a favor to a dead courier. And I know how to sound like a musician."

* * *

We walked toward the palace district through streets that hummed, two traitors in borrowed coats, carrying twelve names where the eleventh was carved and the twelfth lived only in Tamsin's memory now—pressed there by a dying woman's hand against his temple, whispered twice, unrepeatable, unstealable, unless they took the whole man.

Which, I was beginning to suspect, was exactly the price.

The palace rose ahead of us, white and beautiful against the winter stars, every window lit, every tower ringing slightly, perfectly flat.

Four days, I thought. Hold together four days.

Behind us, far off, a single bell tolled out of tune—just once, just barely—and I chose to believe it was Maren, laughing.
