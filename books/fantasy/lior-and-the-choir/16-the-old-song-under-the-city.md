## 16. The Old Song Under the City

The map had been lying to us for three days.

Not lying exactly—more like the way a shy person gives directions. *Go past the tannery, turn where the old thing used to be.* The courier's ink showed a street that no longer existed, a gate that had been bricked over, and a set of stairs drawn in a hand so faint I'd assumed it was water damage. It wasn't. It was reluctance. Whoever drew those stairs had wanted them found by someone, but not by everyone, and not easily.

"Here," Sera said, and stopped so abruptly I walked into her backplate, which is how I've learned most of what I know about armor.

We stood in a cul-de-sac behind the dyers' quarter, where the walls were stained every color grief comes in and the air smelled of vinegar and old urine. A dead-end. Except at the end of it, half-swallowed by ivy and a century of plaster patching, there was an archway with no door—just darkness shaped like a door, and above it, worn nearly smooth, a carving of twelve small figures standing in a row.

I counted them twice. Twelve. My hands went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season.

"That's not on any survey," Sera said. "I've walked this city my whole career. There's nothing under the dyers' quarter."

"There's something under everything," I said. "Cities are just graves that learned to charge rent."

She gave me the look she reserved for my worst habits—the one that said *now is not the time for you to be like this*—and unslung her lantern. The archway breathed out cold air, and underneath the cold, faint as a coin dropped down a well, I heard it.

Singing.

Not the Choir's singing. Not that flat, seamless hum that made my skull feel like a cracked bell. This was rougher. Humaner. Voices that hadn't agreed with each other about anything except the song itself, one of them always a hair ahead of the others, one of them always dragging, the way real choirs sound when they're alive enough to be imperfect.

"Tamsin." Sera had her hand on my shoulder. "You've gone white."

"They're down there," I said. "All twelve."

"You can hear them?"

"I can hear them arguing about tempo." And then, because my mouth does this even when my brain begs it not to: "It's the first honest music I've heard since we entered this city."

* * *

The stairs went down farther than stairs should. That's a thing people say when they mean they got tired, but I mean it literally—the steps kept going after the depth of the foundations made sense, and the walls changed around us, from dressed stone to older stone to stone that had never known a mason's square at all. The city above was built on bones; I'd known that abstractly. I hadn't expected the bones to have opinions.

The humming started somewhere past the third landing. Not in my ears—in my teeth, in the marrow of me, the way the valley's silence lived in my bones. But inverted. Where Brightwater refused all magic, this place was soaked in it, old and patient and waiting, and my gift, which had been screaming quietly since we entered the capital, suddenly went very still.

Like a dog that has finally caught the scent it's been hunting.

"The map," Sera said behind me. "It's warm."

I turned. She held the courier's wax-sealed sheet flat on her palm, and she was right—I could see the faint shimmer of heat off it, or thought I could, though that might have been the dark playing tricks. The ink had shifted too. The faint staircase we'd followed now sat bold and black in the center of the parchment, and around it, letters I'd never seen before had surfaced like drowned things rising.

*They buried us without our names,* the letters said. *Come correct the record.*

"Well," I said. "That's direct."

"Do you ever stop talking?"

"When I'm terrified. You'll know it's bad when I'm quiet." I took the map. It was warm as a living thing, and it trembled slightly against my fingers—not fear, I realized. Eagerness. Ten years folded up in a courier's satchel, waiting for someone to read the fine print.

At the bottom of the stairs was a door.

It was sealed—of course it was sealed—with lead and iron and seven different kinds of ward-marks, the kind the Justiciars use, the kind Sera had spent her life learning to read. She knelt before it with her lantern raised, and I watched her face do something complicated.

"These are Ossler's marks," she said quietly. "His personal cipher. He's been High Justiciar eleven years." Her jaw worked. "He sealed this door himself. The year after Aldric took the throne."

"So he knows."

"He's always known." She said it the way you'd say *the sky is up*, flat and dead, and I understood that something in her had finished breaking during our flight from the archive, and what remained was harder and simpler than what had been there before. She drew her knife and began cutting through the wards with the economy of a woman dismantling her own faith.

The last mark parted with a sound like a sigh, and the door swung inward on hinges that hadn't screamed in a decade because no one had given them reason to.

Cold air rolled out. And with it, the singing stopped.

Twelve voices, mid-verse, cut off at once—and into that silence came something worse. Attention. The specific, weighty attention of the dead, turning toward the living like faces toward a light.

"Oh good," I whispered. "An audience."

* * *

The crypt was round, and wrong, and beautiful.

Wrong first: it wasn't built, it was grown, some ancient root-vault of the old city that the palace's architects had simply walled off and pretended didn't exist, the way you pretend not to see a scar. Beautiful second: someone had tried. Long ago, before the sealing, someone had loved these dead enough to leave offerings—wax stubs burned to pools, dried flowers gone to dust, a child's carved wooden bird placed carefully atop one of the twelve stone shelves that lined the walls.

On each shelf lay a body.

Or what was left of bodies. They'd been buried in choir robes, and the robes had lasted better than the flesh, so what I saw was twelve shapes of rotted silk and bone, laid out straight, hands folded, and every single one of them missing the same thing.

Their voice-boxes had been taken. I knew it the way I know things I wish I didn't—through the bone, through the gift, through the awful clarity that only comes to me in places where magic runs thin or true. Throats cut with surgical care, the instruments removed, carried up into the light, and bound into the thing that wore the king.

"They're here," I said. My voice came out strange. "They've been here the whole time. Right under the palace. Under the court. Under everything."

"Ten years," Sera said. She'd gone pale beneath her tan. "Every ceremony. Every renewal rite. They've been singing over their own graves."

And then the dead spoke.

Not the way ghosts usually speak to me—a thin thread of last words, a dying echo. This was twelve voices together, filling the round room, coming from everywhere and nowhere, and the first thing they said was not what I expected, because I'm a fool who expects beggars.

*You came to free us.*

"Yes," I said. Then, honestly: "To try. Maren Corvane gave us the names. I've carved them. I think—" I touched the case at my side, the reliquary of finger bones Maren had pressed into my hands, each one now graven with a true name in my own careful script. "I think I can unbind you. At cost."

*We know the cost,* the voices said, and there was something almost gentle in it. *We watched you carve. We felt every name go into the bone. Do you know what it is, little carver, to feel your own name written truly for the first time in ten years?*

I didn't trust myself to answer.

*Then hear us,* they said. *Because you have come to free us, and you have it backward.*

Sera's hand moved to her knife. I put mine on her wrist. "Wait," I murmured. "Listen first. Listening is the whole job."

*We do not want rescue,* the twelve said. *Rescue is for the drowning, and we finished drowning long ago. What we want—what we have wanted through every mass and coronation and midnight rite while our stolen throats sang lies over our heads—is smaller and harder.*

The nearest shelf. The oldest of them, by the wear of the robe. As I looked, the dust on it stirred, and I saw her—or the shape of her, a woman with a conductor's hands and a singer's ruined throat—standing beside her own body, looking down at it with an expression I recognized from ten years of grieving mothers in my workshop.

*Remember us rightly,* she said. *That is all. Before you break the binding, before the king falls, before whatever comes next—say our names in the open air. Say what was done. Not in a crypt where no one will ever come. In the light. In front of them. Let the ones who clapped along to our prison know what they were applauding.*

"Justice," Sera said hoarsely. "You want justice."

*Justice is your word, soldier.* The twelve spoke together again, and there was no anger in it, which somehow made it heavier. *Anger burned out of us years ago. What's left is the truth, and the truth wants witnesses. We were murdered for knowing what the old king did—to his brother, to the villages that resisted him, to the children of the borderlands. We sang at his funeral and we sang at his son's crowning and we sang the requiem over his secret graves, and because we knew, we were killed and erased and made to sing silence forever. If we are freed and forgotten, then they win even in death. If we are freed and remembered—*

The voice faltered. For a moment it was just one voice, the eldest, alone.

*—then we were singers once. Not instruments. Singers.*

I have carved charms for the drowned and the burned and the lost, and in ten years I have learned one thing about the dead that the living never believe: they don't want vengeance. Vengeance is a living appetite. What they want is to be *known*—to have the shape of their lives acknowledged by someone still breathing, so that the ending isn't the whole story.

But nobody had ever asked me for it quite like this. Nobody had ever asked to be remembered *before* being freed, as if memory were the greater gift and freedom merely the wrapping.

"We can do that," I said. My voice cracked on the second word, and I let it. "Midwinter court. The whole nobility assembled. Sera can get us in. When I break the binding, I'll sing your names first—all twelve, full voice, in front of every lord and lady in the realm. And Sera—" I turned to her. "You'll testify. Everything. Ossler, the cover-up, the murders. Whatever it costs you."

She met my eyes. I watched her weigh her career, her oath, her life, on scales that had already tipped days ago in a bell tower.

"It was always going to cost," she said. "Better it costs for something true."

*Then it is witnessed,* the twelve said, and the round room seemed to exhale. *And carver—one more thing, and mark it well.*

The cold deepened. All twelve shapes on their shelves shifted, or seemed to, and the eldest's ghost stepped closer, close enough that I could see the wound below her jaw, neat and terrible.

*When you sing, the Choir will fight you. It is not just a binding—it is a hunger wearing a harmony, and it has fed on a kingdom for ten years. It will offer you anything. Your valley. Your silence. Your life back, whole and hidden. It knows exactly what you miss.* A pause, and something like pity moved through the dead air. *It will show you Brightwater burning. It will be a lie. Hold to that. Hold to the lie being a lie.*

My stomach turned over, because I'd already seen it—two nights ago, through a street child's borrowed mouth, the vision of smoke over my valley, and I had nearly surrendered the bones right there in the mud. Nearly. The dead had seen it happen. The dead had *felt* it happen, through the resonance between their binding and my gift.

"It lied," I said. "I know."

*Knowing and believing are different rooms, carver. We only ask that you keep walking between them toward us.*

* * *

We climbed back up through the dark, and the map in my pocket had gone cool and quiet, its work done, its ink faded back to a courier's ordinary scrawl. Halfway up the stairs Sera stopped, lantern raised, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read in the bad light.

"You asked them what they wanted," she said slowly. "Nobody asks the dead that. We ask them to rest. We ask them to move on. We put them in the ground and tell them the story's over."

"Maybe the story's never over," I said. "Maybe that's why the Choir works. Someone told twelve singers their story was over, and instead of listening, they wrote themselves into the kingdom's throat. Wrongly. Terribly. But they weren't done." I climbed another step. "What we're doing isn't destroying them, Sera. It's editing. Giving them back their own ending instead of the one that was written for them."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then: "My brother used to say something like that. About the army's histories. 'Whoever writes the ending owns the war.'" Her jaw tightened. "I reported a man for saying it. Got him reassigned to the border forts. I thought I was doing my duty."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to stand in front of the entire court and finish his sentence."

Above us, faint through the sealed archway, the bells of the capital began to ring the hour—all of them, every tower, slightly flat, slightly wrong, the way they'd rung for ten years. But tonight, for the first time, I heard something else underneath. Twelve voices in a crypt, practicing.

Running through their parts.

And the eldest was counting them in.
