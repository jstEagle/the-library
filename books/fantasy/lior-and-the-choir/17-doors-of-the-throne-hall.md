## 17. Doors of the Throne Hall

There are three ways into a Midwinter court ceremony: be born to it, marry into it, or carry something heavy for someone who was born to it.

I was carrying a lute.

Not mine. I'd bought it off a hungover minstrel in the Gutterlight district for twice what it was worth and half of what I had, and I'd spent two days learning to hold it like I knew which end the music came out of. The case was long enough, deep enough, and—this was the important part—lined with felt thick enough that no Justiciar's wand would find twelve finger bones sleeping inside it.

"You're holding it wrong," Tamsin said behind me.

"I'm holding it like a woman who has never held one, which is the entire point."

"You're holding it like a woman who's about to use it as a club."

"That too is the point," I said. "If this goes badly, I intend to introduce High Justiciar Ossler's teeth to the tuning pegs."

He didn't laugh. He hadn't laughed properly in three days—not since Maren Corvane died in the dark under the archive with her hands pressed flat against a door that was already burning, not since my name went up on the traitor boards in the Marshal's Square in letters tall enough to read from a galloping horse. TAMSIN BRIGHTWATER, HERETIC. SERA VANE, OATHBREAKER. They'd gotten my rank wrong on the board. Sergeant, it said, when I'd been bumped to corporal two years ago and nobody had ever bothered to fix it. Even my treason was administratively neglected. There was a joke in there somewhere, but I couldn't find it, and neither could he.

We stood in the musicians' colonnade beneath the palace's east wing, where the snow came down through gaps in the roof and melted on the flagstones in gray puddles, and around us perhaps sixty actual musicians tuned, argued, drank mulled wine from flasks they thought the guards couldn't smell, and complained about the acoustics of the Throne Hall as though they were being asked to perform in a well. None of them looked at us. That was the bargain Sablewick—the hungover minstrel whose lute I'd bought and whose place on the roster I'd taken—had sold us for the price of his silence and a story about cousins from the coast. Nobody looks at second-chair strings. Second-chair strings are furniture that breathes.

Tamsin, meanwhile, was dressed as furniture too: a plain gray coat, a carver's satchel repurposed as an instrument bag, and the particular posture of a man trying very hard to be no one. He'd been practicing it his whole life, I think. Out here, in the city, with the Choir humming through every stone like water through limestone, his talent for invisibility was the only thing keeping him upright.

"How's your head?" I asked, because I'd learned to ask it quietly and only once.

"Loud." He flexed his fingers inside his gloves. "It knows we're here. It's not—it isn't angry, exactly. It's patient. Like a landlord waiting at the door on rent day."

"Charming."

"It thinks it's already won." He said it without drama, the way you'd report the weather. "That's the worst part. It keeps singing at me in the voice of the drowned boy from home. It wants me to know it can reach all the way to Brightwater whenever it likes."

"And you don't believe it."

"I believe it can *lie* all the way to Brightwater." His jaw tightened. "There's a difference."

Above us, through the colonnade's broken roof, the palace bells began to ring—and rang slightly, achingly flat, every one of them, the way they had for ten years, the way the whole city had stopped noticing because a wrong note repeated often enough becomes the song. Tamsin winced. I'd stopped asking him what it sounded like. The answer was always some version of *like swallowing*.

* * *

Getting onto the roster had cost me the last coin of my credibility, and I want that on the record.

My name was still good enough, three days ago, to open one door: the door of Master Ellery Voss, Provost of the Court Musicians, a small damp man whom I had once pulled out of a frozen canal while off duty and drunk on festival wine, and who had never thanked me because thanking a Justiciar felt, to him, like thanking the axe for missing. When I'd appeared at his lodgings in a hood, with a forged letter of transfer from a choir school that didn't exist, he'd gone the color of old porridge and said, "Sergeant Vane. They say you're a traitor now."

"They say a lot of things," I'd told him. "They also said the canal ice would hold."

He'd let us in. He'd listened. And then he'd done the thing that saved us, though I doubt he'd frame it that way: he'd looked at the roster for the Renewal, seen the hole where Sablewick's name sat, and understood—with the finely honed cowardice of a man who has survived four reigns—that whatever was coming, the safest place in the palace for a small damp man was nowhere near it.

"One condition," he'd said. "When it's done—whatever 'it' is—if the palace still stands, you come back and tell me whether I did right or wrong. I'll want to know before anyone else decides for me."

I'd promised. It was the first promise I'd made since my oath broke, and it sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

So now we were furniture. Furniture with a view of the doors.

The Throne Hall doors themselves were older than the dynasty, black oak banded in bronze gone green, carved with the faces of kings who had mostly been terrible and were therefore rendered magnificently. Between them, in the hour before the court assembled, walked the Honor Guard in their white-and-gold, and among them—I counted them twice, because counting was the only useful thing my hands knew how to do—fourteen men whose eyes sang when they blinked.

You had to see it at the right angle, in the right light. A blink, and for the half-second the lid was down, the eye underneath caught light that wasn't there—a wet glimmer, a shimmer like heat off a road. Tamsin had shown me how to spot them in the marshes, when the hunters came out of the reeds with their faces slack and their blades moving in time to a melody none of them could hear. Fourteen of them, guarding the doors of the hall where their master would renew its lease on a king.

"They'll search the instruments," Tamsin murmured. "They always do at Midwinter. Wand-check at the inner door."

"The wand-check looks for steel and spellwork," I said. "Bone reads as nothing. Maren said—"

"Maren said a great many things she won't finish saying." His voice cracked on it, just slightly, and he pressed his lips together until it passed. "Sorry."

"Don't apologize to me. Apologize by finishing what she started."

He looked at me then—really looked, the first time in days—and nodded once, and that nod was worth more than any speech either of us could have made.

* * *

The court arrived the way a tide arrives: gradually, then all at once, then smelling faintly of fish.

Lords and ladies in winter furs, pages with braziers, chamberlains with lists, ambassadors from three coasts and a mountain range, all funneling through the black doors past the fourteen singing-eyed guards, all of them chattering about the harvest tariffs and someone's scandalous divorce and whether the new regency question would finally be settled this year—which was, I had to admit, a fair question, given that the man on the throne hadn't answered one on his own behalf in a decade.

Tamsin and I took our places in the gallery with the other strings, third row, against the wall, where the felt-lined case sat between my feet like a sleeping dog. Around us the real musicians ran through scales. Beside me, Tamsin held his borrowed viola—an instrument he could not play, chosen precisely because violists are expected to be mediocre—and stared down at the floor of the hall with the expression of a man reading a book written in blood.

"What do you see?" I asked.

"Heat." His voice was very low. "The whole floor. Under the mosaics. Old channels, like the veins in a leaf. Everything sung into the stone flows down toward the throne and comes back up changed." He swallowed. "It's a throat, Sera. The whole hall is a throat."

"Then we're standing in its mouth."

"We've been standing in its mouth since the ford." He almost smiled. "It just hasn't swallowed yet."

The trumpets sounded. The doors at the far end opened, and the court rose, and King Aldric came in.

I had seen him twice before, from a distance, at ceremonies where my job had been watching the crowd rather than the crown. Up close—or as close as a gallery allows—he was worse than the rumors. Not because he looked dead; he didn't. He looked *maintained*. A man of fifty-odd kept in excellent condition, tall, silver-bearded, robed in white and gold, walking with a gait so smooth it seemed rehearsed by committee. And his mouth moved. All the way down the length of the hall, through the whole slow procession, his lips shaped words too quiet to hear, and I remembered what Tamsin had told me after the Ash Ceremony, and I made myself look at the nearest courtiers, and yes—there, a duchess with her fan half-raised, her mouth moving in the same rhythm; there, a treasury lord, same rhythm; a hundred mouths below us all shaping the same silent syllables, a congregation mid-prayer, except the prayer never ended and the god wore a crown.

Beside me, Tamsin had gone gray.

"Breathe," I whispered.

"It's louder today." His knuckles were white on the viola's neck. "Renewal day. It's pulling everything in. Every voice it ever ate, all of them, being wound up like—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I can hear the twelve. Underneath. They're not screaming anymore. They're *waiting*. They know I'm here."

"Do the guards?"

"No. But the Choir does, and the Choir wears the guards." His eyes flicked to the Honor Guard, to the fourteen, to the doors. "Once I start, we'll have maybe a minute. Less. Whatever happens after the first name—you have to get me out or die next to me. Those are the options. I need you to have picked one."

I looked down at the throne, at the maintained man upon it, at the hollow harmony rolling over the court like fog, and I found—to my own considerable surprise—that I had already picked, days ago, probably in the marshes, possibly the moment a bone-carver played me my brother's last words in a room that smelled of tallow and rain.

"Die next to you," I said. "But let's try the other one first."

* * *

The ceremony itself was beautiful, if you didn't listen.

The court musicians—my colleagues, briefly, God help me—played the Processional in D, and it was lovely, and it was wrong, every phrase landing a hair flat in a way that made the lords sway gently instead of wince, because the Choir had spent ten years teaching this city that flat was holy. The Chamberlain read the Proclamation of Continuance. The Lord Justicar—Ossler's office, though not Ossler himself, which meant Ossler was somewhere close and watching, and I kept my hood up and my face angled away—read the Litany of the Realm. And then the trumpets called the Renewal, and the Choir proper came down the center aisle.

Twelve positions. Twelve singers in white, faces veiled in gold gauze, voices raised in the Hymn of the Unbroken Circle—and I watched Tamsin's hands shake as the sound hit him, because those twelve chairs had been empty of living throats for ten years and filled instead with the murdered dead, puppets of flesh animated by puppets of soul, and everyone in this hall clapped for it every single year.

"Now?" I breathed.

"Not yet." His eyes were closed. "They're not all in position. If I break it early, the ones outside the circle stay bound. It has to be whole. It has to be *all of it in one place*, or it scatters and rebuilds and we've killed ourselves for nothing." His eyes opened, and they were wet, and furious. "It's clever. It gathered itself for renewal and left itself nowhere to run. Maren would have loved it."

Below, the twelve veiled figures reached the dais steps and turned, six and six, and the king rose from his throne, and the Hymn shifted key into the Renewal Proper—the binding rite, the lease-signing, the annual murder of a decade renewed—and the whole hall's harmony tightened like a wire being drawn.

And that was when the Chamberlain's voice cut through, thin and startled:

"—the Honor Guard will admit no further persons—the doors—the *doors*—"

I turned. Everyone turned.

High Justiciar Ossler stood in the entrance of the Throne Hall, snow melting on his shoulders, flanked by eight Justiciars in full warrant-colors, and his eyes—his ordinary, blinkless, human eyes—went across the gallery row by row like a man counting sheep before slaughter.

He was looking for me. I knew it the way you know a hand on the back of your neck.

"Sergeant Vane," he called, and the title was a knife with my old rank's handle on it, "and the heretic Brightwater. By warrant of the Crown, I arrest—"

"*Your Majesty*," Tamsin said.

He said it quietly. He said it the way he spoke to the dead—level, clear, pitched to carry—and the strange thing, the impossible thing, was that the hall heard him. Every head turned from Ossler to the throne, because in ten years no one had heard the king addressed directly and answered, and every courtier in that room was starving for the sound of a live voice under the crown.

King Aldric stood at the apex of the Renewal circle, arms half-raised in the binding gesture, mouth moving with the twelve veiled singers—and he stopped.

For one second. One raw, unmeasured second, the king's mouth went still, and his eyes—his eyes came up, found the gallery, found Tamsin, and in them was a horror so old and so patient that I felt it in my teeth. Ten years of drowning, looking out through the water at a man holding a rope.

His lips shaped two words. No sound. But Tamsin made a noise like a man taking a blade, and his hand went to the instrument case between my feet, and he said, in a voice that shook and did not break:

"Now. It's whole. It's all here. Open the case."

Ossler was shouting warrants. The Honor Guard was turning, fourteen pairs of eyes beginning to shimmer. The twelve veiled singers' heads rotated toward the gallery in perfect unison, gold gauze catching the light, and beneath the Hymn—for the first time in ten years, audible, rising, *unavoidable*—the court began to hear what had always been underneath.

I threw open the case.

Twelve finger bones lay in the felt, each one carved fine as frostwork with a true name, and Tamsin lifted the tuning fork from his pocket, and struck it against the rail, and the note that sang out was small and pure and absolutely, catastrophically in tune—

—and the false harmony of ten years shattered like ice on a canal.
