## 18. The Renewal

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before a lie, and I had spent my whole life learning to hear it. The silence of Brightwater was honest—magic-dead, dead-quiet, the hush of a valley that refused to pretend. But the silence in the throne hall of Vael Corren, on the morning of Midwinter court, was a held breath. A thousand people not singing, all at once, so that something else could sing through them.

I sat in the musicians' gallery with Sera's borrowed lute across my knees and twelve finger bones sewn into its lining, and I tried to look like a man who knew which end of a lute to blow into.

"You're holding it wrong," Sera murmured beside me, her mouth barely moving. She wore her Justiciar dress grays, polished to a shine that said *I belong here* louder than any writ. "You've been holding it wrong for three days."

"It's a prop."

"It's evidence. If they search you and find you can't play it, we're both dead before the king finishes yawning."

"The king doesn't yawn," I said. "The king hums."

She didn't laugh. Neither did I. Down on the marble floor, the court was assembling itself into order the way water assembles itself into a level: lords in furs, ladies in winter silks, heralds with staves, priests of the Concordance in their gray-and-gold, and everywhere the small unconscious sway of people breathing in time. I'd noticed it yesterday and hoped I'd imagined it. I hadn't. The whole hall breathed together, four counts in, four counts out, like a lung belonging to something much larger.

And above them all, on the throne, King Aldric sat very still.

I want to describe him properly, because everything that happened after depends on what he looked like in that moment. He was not a corpse. That's the horror of it—he was *fine*. A man of perhaps fifty-five, broad through the shoulders, silver at the temples, a face that might have been handsome once and was still well-fed now. His hands rested on the arms of the throne with the easy weight of a man who has sat there so long the chair has grown around him. His eyes were open. His eyes were *warm*.

They were also empty in the way a house is empty when every room is full of someone else's furniture.

"He's in there," I said quietly. "Aldric. Somewhere under it."

"You don't know that."

"I heard him last night." Through two feet of palace stone, through the Choir's own noise, a thin thread of a man's voice, hoarse and small, counting. Counting days, or winters, or the names of his children—I couldn't tell. But counting. Men who are gone don't count.

Sera followed my gaze down to the throne, and I watched her do the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do since the marshes: ten years of decrees signed by that hand. Ten years of harmonizations, of silenced bells, of burned hymnals, of Maren Corvane dying in an archive stairwell with a name pressed into my memory like a seal into wax. All of it done by a body that was, itself, possibly the first victim in the room.

"Whatever happens," she said, "don't look for me. Look at him. If you lose the thread because you're worried about where I'm standing, this ends badly."

"And if you lose yours?"

"I'm a sergeant of Justiciars," she said. "My thread is the law. It's just taken me eleven chapters—" she caught herself, frowned at nothing, "—a long time to notice the law was written by whoever holds the fork."

That wasn't quite a joke, but it was the closest thing to one either of us had managed in two days, and I took it the way you take bread in a famine: gratefully, and without asking what's in it.

* * *

The ceremony began with bells.

Of course it did. Every bell in the capital rang at once—the flat, slightly wrong ringing I'd hated since we came through the gates—and the sound came down into the hall through high windows and settled over the assembly like snow. And then the court began to sing.

Not loudly. That was the worst part. No one stood, no one raised their voice, no conductor lifted a hand. They simply opened their mouths mid-breath and produced a low, blended tone, a thousand voices in perfect unison, so quiet it was almost conversational, and every single face kept its composure while doing it. Lords went on nodding to one another. Ladies went on smiling their small cold smiles. A page poured wine without spilling. The song ran underneath everything like a river under ice.

It was the melody from the streets. The one every street performer played, the one every bell tower rung slightly flat. Up close, finally, I could hear what it was made of—or rather, what it was *covering*. Because underneath the unison, so faint I had to stop breathing to catch it, there were other voices. Twelve of them. Not singing. Being sung. Pulled through throats that weren't theirs, stretched over notes they never chose, and each one of them screaming in the only way a bound voice can scream: by being forced to sound beautiful.

My hands were shaking. I made them stop. Grandmother taught me that much—your hands are your craft, and your craft does not get your fear. Whatever else was true, I was still Tamsin Brightwater, bone-carver, and I had work to do.

On the throne, King Aldric rose.

It was graceful. That's what I'll never be able to explain to anyone who didn't see it. There was no jerk of puppetry, no lurching horror-show stagger. He simply stood the way a note stands when an orchestra arrives at it—as though his standing were the resolution of a chord that had been building for ten years. The court's song swelled a half-tone to meet him. He raised his arms, palms out, the ancient gesture of renewal, and his mouth moved with the unison, shaping words in Old Concordant that I only half knew and understood entirely:

*What was bound, let it hold. What was silenced, let it serve.*

Below me, in the front rank of the court, High Justiciar Ossler stood with his head bowed and his lips moving along perfectly, and I thought about Maren's stairwell, and my hands wanted to shake again, and did not.

"Now," Sera breathed.

"Not yet." My mouth had gone dry. "The binding isn't whole yet. Look at the throne."

Behind the king, carved into the great seat itself, twelve hollow channels ran up the backrest like fluting on a column, and from each channel, as the rite deepened, a thread of light was rising—pale, gold-gray, the color of a voice given weight. The renewal wasn't a metaphor. It was maintenance. Once a year, before witnesses who could no longer witness anything, the Choir rethreaded itself through the crown, through the king, through the law, and the whole rotten instrument retuned itself for another year.

The threads were nearly all risen. Eleven. Twelve—

I stepped off the gallery.

I didn't decide to. Later, when I try to remember the moment, what I find is not a decision but a fact: my feet were on the stairs, and then they were on the marble, and the lute case was in my hand, and Sera's oath-sworn boots were falling in behind me, and somewhere behind us a herald was shouting something about the sanctity of the rite, and none of it mattered even slightly, because the twelfth thread was rising and I had promised twelve dead people in a crypt under the city that they would be remembered rightly, and I keep my promises. It's most of what I am.

"Tamsin—" Sera, low, warning, because guards were moving at the edges of the hall, because Ossler's head had come up, because everything was happening exactly as badly as planned.

"Keep them off me for thirty seconds," I said. "Sing something. You're a terrible singer; it'll confuse everyone."

I set the lute case down on the marble in the open floor of the throne hall, in front of the assembled nobility of the realm, beneath the king with his arms spread and his borrowed voice pouring out of him, and I opened it.

The tuning fork was in my pocket. It had always been going to be in my pocket. Grandmother's fork, the only object that ever left Brightwater and came back unchanged, tuned—to hear Maren tell it—to a pitch older than the Choir, older than the Concordance, older than the lie. I struck it against the case's edge, the way I'd struck it ten thousand times against bone, and held it up.

The note it gave was small. That's the truth of it, and I love it: it was a small note. One clean tone, unwavering, in a hall full of a thousand voices and a god-shaped chorus, and it should have been swallowed whole.

Instead, the false harmony *cracked*.

I felt it go the way you feel ice go underfoot—a shiver, a give, a sudden honest depth where there had been solid ground. The unison stumbled. A lord in ermine faltered mid-note and looked at his own mouth as though it belonged to a stranger. A lady's song broke into a sob she clearly hadn't authorized. The river-under-ice stuttered, and through the cracks, for one raw second, the hall heard what I had been hearing since the ford:

The screaming.

Twelve voices, torn, furious, grieving, human—screaming through the mouths of the mighty, and every noble in Vael Corren heard it, heard it coming from their own throat, and the sound that rose from the court then was not song. It was the noise a thousand people make when they discover what they've been humming for ten years.

King Aldric stopped mid-gesture.

His arms came down slowly. His head turned—not puppet-smooth now, but stiffly, painfully, like a man waking in a bed that isn't his—and his eyes found mine across the marble, and in them, for the first time, there was someone home. He looked *terrified*. He looked like a man who has just been told how long he's been asleep.

"You—" he said, in a voice like a rusted hinge, a voice with no music in it at all, a voice that was only and entirely a tired middle-aged man's, "—what year is it?"

And that was when the twelfth thread finished rising, the Choir seized back its instrument, and the king's face closed like a door.

"SEIZE THEM," roared High Justiciar Ossler, and the hall erupted.

Guards came from both sides. Sera met the first pair with her baton and her shoulder and ten years of knowing exactly how bodies fail, buying me my seconds, shouting words I couldn't parse over the roaring in my ears. The Choir's scream filled the dome now, no longer hiding, no longer beautiful, and I knelt over the open case with the bones in their reliquary lining and the whole court watching, and I did the only thing I have ever known how to do.

I listened for their names.

Twelve names, carved into twelve finger bones by a blind woman's instruction and my own two hands. Twelve names the court had buried with the singers, unnamed, unmourned, filed away as instruments. The first name rose in my throat like a coal.

I sang it.

The nearest guard froze mid-stride, his sword arm dropping, tears cutting through the dust on his face—because it was *his* name too, in a way, the name of the voice that had been wearing him. The false harmony tore another inch wider. On the throne, King Aldric shuddered like a mast in a storm, both masters pulling at once.

Eleven names left. The guards were closing. Sera was bleeding somewhere above her eyebrow and laughing, actually laughing, and Ossler was screaming for order in a hall that had finally, blessedly, completely lost it.

I took a breath, tasted blood and beeswax and the end of my old life, and sang the second name.
