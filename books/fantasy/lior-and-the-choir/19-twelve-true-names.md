## 19. Twelve True Names

The tuning fork's note died against the marble and left a hole in the world.

I don't know how else to describe it. For ten years the Hollow Choir had sung through this hall like water through limestone, so constant that everyone standing on that floor had forgotten sound could be anything else. Then my fork struck, and the song tore, and every noble in Midwinter court heard—for exactly one raw second—what had been underneath their beautiful harmony all along.

Screaming. Twelve voices' worth of screaming, folded into one chord, pressed flat by a decade of royal decree.

A woman near the dais fainted into her husband's arms. A lord dropped his wine goblet and didn't notice it shattering. Somewhere in the gallery above me, a child began to cry, and I remember thinking, with the strange clarity that comes when everything is about to go wrong: *good*. Cry, little one. That's the correct response. Everyone else is about a decade late.

Then the second second arrived, and with it the guards.

There were six of them between me and the doors, and they moved wrong—not like men following orders but like instruments being played, their boots striking the floor in perfect unison, their hands finding sword hilts at the same instant, as if some vast hand had plucked them all from the same string. Their eyes were open too wide. And when they blinked—and they blinked together, all six, like a single creature wearing six faces—I heard it again, faint under the chaos: singing behind their eyes.

"Tamsin." Sera's voice, low, at my shoulder. She'd come down from the gallery the moment the harmony broke, her Justiciar's cloak thrown back, her hand nowhere near her sword because she understood what I understood: steel was not going to settle this. "The king."

King Aldric stood at the center of the dais, and he was *moving*—his body jerking through gestures no living man would make, arms rising in benediction, head tilting at angles the neck permits only reluctantly. His mouth opened. What came out was not a voice. It was twelve voices braided into one, a chord wearing a man's throat like a stolen coat.

*WHO DISTURBS THE SONG?*

"Me," I said. My voice came out smaller than I'd have liked. Wry narration is difficult when your knees are negotiating their own surrender. "Sorry to interrupt the recital. I have requests."

The guards were twenty feet away. Fifteen. Sera put herself between me and them with her sword drawn now after all, and I loved her for it and hated myself for letting her.

"The bones," she said. "Tamsin. The names. *Now.*"

She was right. Of course she was right; she'd been right since an inn called Nine Bells, and I'd been stalling ever since, because here is the thing nobody tells you about sacrifice: you can carry it for weeks, turn it over, admire its shape, tell yourself stories about it. And then the moment comes, and it isn't noble at all. It's just cold, and it wants everything you have.

I opened the instrument case.

Twelve finger bones lay inside on velvet Maren Corvane had wrapped around them with her blind, careful hands, each one carved fine as frost ferns with a true name in the old script—the names the court had buried along with the singers, the names that were the only keys that fit these particular locks. I lifted the first bone, and the hall went very quiet around me, the way a forest goes quiet before something with teeth arrives.

*You cannot,* said the Choir, through Aldric's mouth, through the guards' mouths, through the mouths of three courtiers who had been bound longer than anyone knew. *We are the crown. We are the law. Strike us and the kingdom falls.*

"You're not the law," I said. "You're what the law was used to bury. There's a difference. I carve bones for a living. I know the difference between a thing and what people do with it."

The first guard reached Sera. Steel rang. I stopped watching, because there was nothing more I could do for her except the thing I had come to do, and half-doing it would kill us both.

I put the first bone to my lips like a whistle, which is essentially what it was—a whistle made of a murdered woman's finger, carved by a grieving man in a valley where magic couldn't follow. And I sang her name.

Her name was Lisseny Harrow.

I know that sounds simple. Four syllables. But a true name isn't a word; it's a whole person compressed until it sings, and when I gave it breath, the hall *heard* her—not the flattened instrument the Choir had made of her voice, but Lisseny herself, twenty-three years old, who had once sung lullabies to her younger brothers in a village that no longer appears on maps. Her voice rose out of the bone and out of the Choir both, tearing free like a netted bird, and where it passed, the false harmony frayed.

It cost me. That's the part I want written down somewhere, in case anyone ever thinks this was easy or clean or triumphant in the way songs make triumph sound. The first name took my breath—not metaphorically; I finished the syllables and couldn't draw air for three full seconds, bent double, seeing spots. Warmth drained out of my fingers as if I'd plunged them in snowmelt.

And underneath all of it, quieter than either, something in my hearing itself dimmed. Like a door closing in a house I'd lived in all my life.

Eleven names to go.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the next several minutes, arranged not in order but in weight:

Sera fighting. Not winning—there were six of them and they didn't tire, didn't bleed properly, didn't *stop*—but holding, giving ground foot by foot, using every dirty trick the Justiciar academy pretends it doesn't teach. At some point she killed one, and the body dropped and kept twitching, and from the slack mouth came a thread of song until the second name I sang silenced it, and she looked at me across the chaos with an expression I'll carry to whatever grave I get: gratitude and horror in equal measure, because she understood I wasn't just freeing the dead. I was spending myself like coin.

The nobility. Some fled. Most didn't—they stood transfixed, because each freed voice washed over them carrying its own memory, its own truth, and truth is a drug to people who've been breathing lies for a decade. When I sang the fourth name—Ovelin Duskwater, countertenor, who had loved the old king enough to forgive him, which somehow made his murder worse—the Lord Chancellor fell to his knees in front of everyone and began, horribly, to weep, and I knew he remembered signing the order that sent twelve singers into a crypt.

The king. With each name, Aldric's puppet-strings lost another strand. By the seventh he had stopped moving entirely and stood swaying like a man in high wind, and his face—the face beneath the Choir's grip—was the face of a drowning man who has just seen light on the surface. His eyes found mine. They were wet. They were *his*, whoever he had been before the thing wearing him swallowed that word.

*Please,* his mouth said, without sound. Or maybe the Choir said it, mocking. I chose to believe him. Belief was the only weapon I had left that didn't cost flesh.

The eighth name took my ability to feel my feet. The ninth turned the world silver at the edges. The tenth—

The tenth was Tam Vael, the youngest, sixteen years old, and his name came out of me as a sob because by then I couldn't separate my voice from grief anymore, and maybe that was right. Maybe that was always how it had to be sung. His freed voice was so bright it hurt, and somewhere in the gallery the crying child stopped crying and started humming along, and I thought: *there. That's what you were for, Tam. Not this. That.*

Two left.

The Choir knew it too. It stopped fighting Sera—all six guards froze mid-strike—and poured everything it had into me, a flood of offers and threats delivered directly into the hollowing space behind my eyes. My valley burning. My mother's grave desecrated. My tools snapped one by one. And then, softer, almost kind: *Stop, and we return your silence. Your valley. Your small life. You were happy there. We can let you be happy again.*

"That's the trouble with liars," I rasped. "They always offer you back the thing they already took."

I sang the eleventh name kneeling, because my legs had resigned without consulting me. Maren Corvane's voice was in my memory supplying the pronunciation, patient as she'd been in the archive, and I sang it correctly, and the eleventh voice went free, and the great chord that had worn a crown for ten years was down to a single thread.

One name. One bone in my hand, warm despite my frozen fingers, as if the dead man himself were holding on.

His name was Aldric Vane.

I hadn't known until Maren told me, three nights ago, why that name had sounded familiar the first time I read it. The old king's brother. The chorister who discovered what his brother had done—the massacres, the buried villages, the crimes the dynasty was built on—and who gathered eleven other singers to testify, and who was murdered alongside them for it, and whose name was given afterward to his brother's son, our current king, as if naming a boy after a murdered man could un-murder him.

The last name would free the last voice. And the last voice was the keystone. When it went, everything the Choir held up would come down—including, I suspected, the body it had animated from within for ten years.

Including, quite possibly, me. The dead had told me as much in the crypt: *a song needs a singer, carver, and we will take the nearest throat.* I'd decided, kneeling among twelve unnamed coffins, that it was a fair price. I'd had a good run. Better than most. Better than Tam Vael.

But the price turned out different than advertised, and I want to be honest about that too, because honesty is the whole point of what happened next.

I sang the twelfth name. And it did not take my life.

It took my *ear*.

* * *

The moment the final syllable left me, the Hollow Choir came apart.

Not violently—that's the thing I hadn't expected. After all the screaming and the strings and the terror of it, the ending was quiet. Twelve voices rose out of King Aldric like heat-shimmer off summer stone, and instead of vanishing they *lingered*, circling the hall once, twice, and every person present heard them clearly, unmistakably, each voice distinct: Lisseny's warmth, Ovelin's forgiveness, Tam's brightness, and nine others, each singing not accusations but simply *themselves*, their true sound, unbound and unowned, for the length of one long breath.

Witnessed. Remembered rightly. That was all they had asked for in the crypt, and it was all they needed now.

Then they rose—through the roof, through the banners, through ten years of silence—and were gone, and the bells of the capital, every tower at once, rang out a chord so flat and ragged and *human* that grown lords covered their ears, and I laughed, or tried to, and got about halfway there.

King Aldric fell.

Sera caught him—she was closest, having finally dropped the last guard, bleeding from a cut above her eye and breathing like a bellows. She lowered him to the marble with a care that contradicted everything her uniform stood for, and I dragged myself over on hands that no longer belonged to me, and we knelt together over the ruin of a king.

He was himself. Briefly, terribly, himself. Ten years of vacancy looked back at us out of his eyes, and behind it, faint as a lamp in fog, the man. He looked at Sera first—at her Justiciar grey—and something like shame crossed his face.

"I heard them," he whispered. "Every day. Under everything. I thought—" A cough. "I thought I was mad. I thought the singing was *me*."

"It wasn't," I said. My voice was nearly gone, scraped down to gravel and apology. "It was never you."

His gaze shifted to me, and he studied my face with the slow effort of a man remembering how faces worked. "Who are you?"

"A bone-carver," I said. "From Brightwater valley."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. "Of course," he breathed. "Of course it's a *carver*. The dead always send tradesmen. Kings send armies, and the dead send..." His hand twitched toward mine. I took it. His fingers were cold as the bones in my case. "Thank you. Tell them—I'm sorry. Tell them I listened. Every night, I—"

He didn't finish. The vacancy took him the way dusk takes a room—not suddenly, but completely, the light withdrawing degree by degree until what remained was only shape. His eyes stayed open and saw nothing, or rather saw the way walls see. Whatever had been King Aldric, the man, the father, the prisoner in his own skull, had gone wherever freed voices go, and what remained on the marble was a body that had been a throne for ten years and was now merely furniture.

"He's alive," Sera said quietly, fingers at his throat. "Barely. Heart's a metronome with no musician." She looked up at me, and her expression did something complicated. "Tamsin. Say something to me."

"What?"

"Anything. I want to hear—" She stopped. Started again, gentler, the way you talk to someone bleeding. "Can you still hear them? The dead. Can you hear anything?"

I listened.

I listened the way I had listened my whole life—to the spaces between sounds, to the pressure behind the air, to that faint tidal pull the dead exert on the living world, the whisper under every whisper. The sense that had made me strange in Brightwater and necessary in Brightwater and *mine* in Brightwater.

Silence. Total, bottomless, ordinary silence. The kind of silence everyone else had lived in all along.

"No," I said. And then, because wryness is a load-bearing wall in my character and I was running out of walls: "Well. On the bright side, I imagine funerals will be much less awkward from now on."

Sera laughed—one short, cracked sound, half sob—and then the doors burst open and the world came back in.

* * *

They came in ranks: palace guards, real ones, mortal and confused, weapons drawn at a scene none of them had words for. A king collapsed on the dais. A Justiciar-sergeant kneeling in blood that was mostly not hers. A stranger in road-dusted clothes clutching an empty instrument case, gray-lipped, frost-pale, looking for all the world like a corpse that had gotten up early.

And behind them, pushing through, High Justiciar Ossler, whose face performed shock the way bad actors perform grief—technically correct, emotionally vacant.

"Seize them," Ossler said. "Both of them. This man has attacked the king's person—"

"Your Grace." Sera rose. She put herself between Ossler and me, and I saw her hand rest on her sword—not drawing it, just resting, the way you touch a promise. Her voice carried across the hall the way voices only carry when they've stopped caring about consequences. "Before your men take another step, I'd suggest you explain to this court why the warrant you issued for the courier's map was sealed under harmonic authority. Why the archive undercroft was raided two nights ago. Why twelve choristers murdered a decade ago appear in no record anywhere in this kingdom—except the ones a blind archivist died protecting yesterday."

The hall had gone very still. Nobles were excellent at stillness; it was practically their trade.

Ossler's face did its work. "Sergeant Vane, you are confused and possibly concussed—"

"I'm neither." She pulled the folded testimony from inside her cloak—Maren's pages, copied in my handwriting during three sleepless nights, every name, every date, every signature Ossler had inked on orders he believed destroyed. "And I'm done reporting to you. Lords of the court!" Her voice rang off the vaulting, flat and human and utterly out of tune with everything this room had been for ten years. "This man"—she pointed at me, and forty pairs of eyes landed on the shaking bone-carver from nowhere—"walked into your king's throat and cut twelve people loose. Ask yourselves who benefits from arresting him. Ask yourselves what else has been singing to you, and for how long, and *why you never wondered*."

Silence. The terrible, fertile kind.

Then old Lady Casselaine—ninety if she was a day, the sort of dowager who had survived four reigns by missing nothing—rose from her seat, crossed the marble with her cane clicking like a slow clock, and stopped in front of me.

"Young man," she said. "That fourth voice. The one who forgave everyone." Her chin trembled, just slightly. "I knew him. Ovelin. He sang at my wedding." She looked back at the assembled lords, and her voice hardened to iron. "I move that the sergeant be heard. All of her. Now."

And the court, like a tide changing its mind, began to murmur yes.

Sera glanced down at me. I was sitting on the marble now, officially, dignity having filed for retirement somewhere around the ninth name. She extended her hand, and I took it, and her grip was steady and warm and alive, and I could hear it—her heartbeat, her breath, the small honest noises of a living person—more clearly than I had ever heard anything.

"Can you stand?" she asked.

"For a few minutes," I said. "After that, someone fetch me a chair and possibly a funeral I won't be able to overhear."

She hauled me up. Behind us, physicians clustered around the vacant king; ahead of us, a court waited to hear the truth with all its ugly harmonics intact. And far away—too far to matter, close enough to ache—I thought of a valley that refused magic, and a workbench, and a life I had traded for twelve names, and I found, to my surprise, that I did not regret it.

Though I confess the chair idea was growing on me by the second.

Because at the edge of the crowd, unnoticed by guards and lords alike, a girl in servant's grey was watching me—watching me with eyes that were dry, and clear, and utterly silent, and familiar in a way that made my new-deafened heart stumble.

Maren Corvane had been blind for thirty years.

These eyes were looking straight at me.
