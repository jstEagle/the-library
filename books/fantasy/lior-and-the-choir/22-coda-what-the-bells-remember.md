## 22. Coda: What the Bells Remember

The valley has a way of pretending nothing ever happened. That's its whole talent, really. Magic can't get in, and apparently neither can consequence. The ford runs the same, the wardens scowl the same, and old Marta still brings me bones to carve as though I hadn't spent half a year toppling a kingdom.

I'd been home three months when the bells started arriving.

Not actual bells—letters. Petitions, more like, though nobody in Brightwater had ever used that word for anything except church business. They came by courier, one every week or so, each one smelling faintly of ink and ambition, each one asking some version of the same question: *How did you do it?*

I burned the first four. Then I felt guilty about burning them, because Maren Corvane had died so that paper like this could survive, and burning it felt like a small betrayal. So I stacked them on the shelf where my grandmother's tuning fork used to live and let them gather dust. The dust seemed appropriate. It was the only thing in the valley that moved without my permission.

The dead were still gone. That hadn't changed.

I want to tell you I made peace with it quickly, that I woke one morning and thought, *well, that's that,* and went back to carving charms for grieving mothers with a light heart. But grief doesn't work like that, even when what you've lost is a sense you never asked for. For weeks I kept reaching for the silence the way you reach for a stair that isn't there. A woman came to me with her father's knucklebone and I held it to my ear out of pure habit, and there was nothing—not his voice, not his name, not even the faint static the valley always used to have, the hush beneath hushes. Just bone. Just the sound of my own blood being disappointed in me.

But here is the thing nobody warns you about losing a sense: the others get louder. Not metaphorically—I mean actually. I heard Marta's breathing change before she said her son's name. I heard the hesitation in the warden's boots when he came to ask if I was all right, which he did far too often, because I think he suspected I'd come back wrong. I heard the exact moment a lie starts in a person's throat, that little catch, like a latch lifting.

I had spent thirty years listening to the dead and calling it a gift. It took losing it to hear the living properly. There's a joke in there somewhere, and I'm sure the dead would have appreciated it. The living mostly just wanted their charms carved.

* * *

Sera Vane arrived on a Tuesday, which surprised me, because I'd have bet money she'd arrive during a storm. She had a knack for dramatic timing that she'd deny under oath.

She came up the valley road on a grey mare that had opinions about the ford, and she sat the animal badly enough that I knew she hadn't been riding long. Soldiers walk. It's in the knees. But she was dressed as a traveler now, no white-and-grey of the Justiciars, just brown wool and mud, and she looked—well. She looked like someone who had testified before an assembled court and then been thanked by having her career set on fire.

"You look terrible," I said, from the doorway of my workshop.

"You should see the other institution," she said. She swung down from the mare, winced, and handed me the reins as though I worked for her. Some things survive regency transitions. "Is that tea I smell, or are you boiling leather again?"

"Leather. Tea's for guests."

"Then I'll be a guest." She followed me inside, ducking under the lintel the way she always did, too tall for rooms built by short stubborn people, and stopped when she saw the shelf. The letters. The stack of them, dusty and patient. "You didn't answer any of these."

"I answered the important ones."

"There are no important ones."

"Exactly."

She smiled at that—the tired version, the one that cost something—and took off her gloves finger by finger. Her hands were scarred differently now than they'd been a year ago. New scars. I didn't ask. There's a courtesy between people who've seen each other at their worst: you don't inventory the damage.

"Maren's archive is restored," she said, when we were seated and the leather had been declared, after extensive testing, drinkable. "Every banned hymnal copied twice, once for the regency library and once—" she tapped the satchel at her hip "—for wherever you decide it belongs. She'd have wanted it here, I think. Or somewhere like here. Somewhere quiet, where songs go to be safe instead of useful."

"She'd have wanted it argued over," I said. "Loudly. In a room with bad lighting."

"That too. We compromised. The regency gets the argument; the valley gets the copies." She unslung the satchel and set it on the table between us, and I swear the wood creaked, though that might have been my imagination. Twelve hymnals' worth of suppressed song, hand-copied by clerks who wept while they wrote, or so Sera claimed. I believed her. I'd read enough of those letters to know what the names did to people now. *Tamsin sang twelve names and a king fell down* — that was how they told it in the capital, tidy as a ballad, all the breath and warmth and hearing stripped out of it.

"And the bells?" I asked. "Do they still ring flat?"

"Worse," she said, and her face did something complicated. "They ring true now. Out of tune, I mean—each tower its own tune, the way they were before. It sounds awful. Pilgrims complain constantly." She paused. "It's the best sound in the city. People have started walking farther to hear it. There's a bell-ringer in the Low Quarter who refuses to harmonize with anyone, and crowds gather just to listen to her be wrong on purpose."

I laughed, and it startled both of us. It had been a while.

* * *

She stayed two nights, which was one night longer than her errand required, and neither of us mentioned that. On the second evening she finally took out the summons.

I'd been expecting it since the ford. You don't carry a document wrapped in oilcloth and guarded like a relic unless it's the kind of thing that changes weather. She laid it on the table, seal intact—wax the color of old wine, pressed with the interregnum crest, the empty crown above crossed keys—and slid it across to me without ceremony. That was a kindness. Ceremony would have given me something to refuse.

"The regency council," she said. "What's left of it, which is seven lords, two judges, and a very frightened archivist who inherited Maren's chair. They voted unanimously. Well. Six voted yes. Lord Cassery abstained, but he abstains from everything; I think he considers it a spiritual practice."

I broke the seal and read. It was polite, which somehow made it worse. Politeness in official documents is like sugar on meat—you know something underneath is being hidden.

*The Council of Regency, acting in trust for the realm until such time as succession may be lawfully determined, requests the presence and counsel of Tamsin Brightwater, bone-carver, whose service to the crown—*

"They mean the new crown," Sera said. "The one that doesn't exist yet. They want you to help build it."

"I toppled the last one."

"Yes. They noticed." She leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for a moment she was the sergeant again, briefing me in a marsh with hunters' eyes singing at us through the reeds. "Listen. They're not fools, whatever else they are. They watched Aldric rule for ten years and never once wonder why his edicts all rhymed. Now they're terrified of the same thing happening again—some new voice getting into whoever wears the crown next, some new choir, some quieter version of the same theft. And there's exactly one man alive who knows what that sounds like from the inside."

"So they want a guard dog."

"They want a listener," she said. "There's a difference. Guard dogs bite. Listeners hear the latch lift before the door opens." She tapped the table, right where the summons lay. "You told me once—in the fens, the night after the ambush—that the worst part wasn't the hunters. It was realizing the obedience sounded almost like loyalty. Almost like love. If someone had taught you to hear that earlier, you said, you'd have known what Aldric was years before the courier died."

I had said that. I'd also been knee-deep in fen water and concussed at the time, which she conveniently failed to mention.

"They'll give you a workshop," she went on. "Access to the archive. Whatever materials you need. And they'll give you something else, though the letter won't say it plainly, because documents never do." She met my eyes. "They'll give you a reason to matter. Officially. On record. No more hiding in a valley waiting for the world to forget your name."

"The world forgetting my name was the entire point of the valley."

"Was it?" she asked, gently, and I hated her a little for it.

* * *

I walked out that night, alone, past the wardens' post where the fire was banked low, down to the ford. The water ran loud and silver under a half-moon, and I stood on the near bank like an idiot, looking at the far side as though the answer were written on the stones.

Here is what I knew about myself, standing there: I had spent my whole life believing the valley protected me. That was the story I told—the magic-dead hollow, the clean silence, the place where the dead spoke clearly and the living left well enough alone. Safe. Hidden. Small on purpose.

But Sera had said something on the road once, back when we were still deciding whether to trust each other, and it had lodged in me like a splinter: *You weren't hiding here, Tamsin. This place was keeping you.* At the time I'd dismissed it as soldier's poetry. Standing at the ford with the summons folded against my chest, I finally understood what she meant. The valley hadn't been my shield. It had been my keeper. And keepers, however kind, however necessary, decide what you're for.

My grandmother had left the valley once. Everyone said she couldn't have—everyone said the tuning fork proved it, the only object that ever went out and came back unchanged. But the fork came back unchanged because *she* had changed, and she'd carried the change home in her hands, and she'd taught me to carve with it. Leaving and returning weren't opposites. They were a single motion, like a bell swing. You go out, you come back, and the valley rings a little differently afterward.

The dead were silent on the far bank. Of course they were. But I found, standing there, that I could imagine them anyway—which is not the same as hearing them, and is maybe better. Memory is a kind of listening that nobody can take from you. Maren's voice, dry as paper, explaining the twelve murders in a whisper because even the archive walls had ears. The courier's ghost at dusk, begging me not to open the map, and opening it anyway. The choristers themselves in the crypt, not begging rescue but asking to be remembered rightly. *Remembered rightly.* That was the job. That had always been the job. The singing of the names was just the last verse of it.

And the job wasn't finished. A dozen letters on my shelf said so. A blind archivist's chair, newly filled by a frightened successor, said so. A country full of bell towers ringing honest and wrong, with crowds gathering to listen—that said so loudest of all.

I turned back toward the workshop, and the light in the window was gold and steady, and Sera's shadow moved across it, and I thought: *she waited.* She could have delivered the summons and ridden out at dawn, duty done, and instead she was sitting in my workshop drinking terrible leather-tea, waiting to see what I chose. Not what the regency commanded. What I chose. That was new. That was worth walking toward.

* * *

I began packing at dawn, which surprised her. I could tell it surprised her because she tried very hard not to show it, and failed completely, the way honest people fail.

"You're going," she said.

"I'm going." I wrapped the tuning fork in wool and set it in the case beside the carved bones—or rather, beside the casts of the carved bones, because the originals had gone into the crypt memorial where they belonged, and the regency's stonemasons had made copies so faithful I sometimes dreamed of them. Tools next. Chisels, rasps, the little hooked knife Grandmother called *the gossip* because it always found the flaw in anything. Maren's hymnals, wrapped in oilcloth, heavier than paper had any right to be.

"The valley will hold," I said. "That's what it does. It holds things. It held me for thirty years, and it'll hold the archive, and someday somebody will come here the way I came here—a stranger at the ford, bleeding, carrying something that matters—and the valley will keep them until they're ready." I closed the case. "But keeping isn't the same as living. I learned that from a king who forgot the difference."

Sera stood in the doorway with the morning behind her, and for a moment she was every road I'd ever refused to take, all at once.

"The capital's loud," she said. "Bells everywhere. Crowds. Politicians. You'll hate it."

"Probably."

"There's a workshop near the archive with bad lighting. Maren's old argument room. They've kept it exactly as she left it, on the theory that disturbing it might disturb her ghost, and honestly, nobody wants to test that theory."

"Then it's perfect." I picked up the case. It weighed what it had always weighed. It felt lighter anyway, which I understood now was not a property of objects but of choices.

We walked down to the ford together, leading the grey mare, who still had opinions and expressed them by trying to eat my sleeve. The water ran silver. The wardens watched from their post and didn't stop us, because the valley lets people leave—it always has. It just makes sure you know the way back.

Halfway across, Sera glanced sideways at me. "Any second thoughts?"

I listened. Not with the ear I'd lost—with the one I'd gained. I heard her breathing, steady, already halfway to the far bank in her mind. I heard the valley behind us, holding everything it held, patient as stone. And underneath it, faint as memory, I could almost swear I heard bells—distant, honest, gloriously out of tune, ringing from a city that had learned to love the sound of being wrong.

"No," I said. "For the first time in my life, I can't hear a single doubt."

She laughed, and the mare snorted, and the ford carried us across.

Behind us the valley kept its silence, keeping what it kept. Ahead, the road went on toward the bells—and this time, I was the one doing the walking.
