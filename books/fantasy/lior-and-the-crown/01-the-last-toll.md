## 1. The Last Toll

The pass had a voice, and Rosalind Vasquez knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat.

Wind through the Thornfell narrows sang low and constant, a note that never quite resolved, and beneath it ran the smaller music: water ticking over stone, the creak of ice in the high crags, the far-off groan of a pine bending under snow it would carry until spring. For eleven years she had moved through that music unseen. The guild had taught her that the mountains were not terrain but language, and she spoke it fluently—every switchback, every blind ravine, every shelf of scree that would hold a mule's weight or swallow it whole.

Tonight she carried nothing worth stealing. That was the grief of it.

Three bolts of Aldenmere wool, dyed madder-red. A crate of southern pepper gone soft with damp. Letters from mothers to sons who'd gone down-country for work and mostly stayed there. The last honest cargo the Thornfell route would ever see, because after tonight there would be no one left to run it.

*Four of us,* she thought, correcting herself with the old bitterness. *Three.*

Master Corvo had died in autumn, coughing his lungs out in a waystation above Greymist with nothing in his hands but a cold cup of willowbark tea and a ledger he'd kept for forty years—every debt owed to the guild, every favor banked, every name. He'd made her memorize the names before the end, all two hundred and six of them, and then he'd said, *Burn the book, little wren. Names are only safe in heads.* She had burned it. She still woke some nights reciting them like prayers, terrified of losing her place.

Now there was only her, old Tam Wicker whose knees had given out two winters past, and young Fen, who was nineteen and had never known the guild when the guild was something. Three smugglers keeping a fire alive in a house where every other room had gone dark.

She crested the shoulder of the pass as the moon came up fat and white behind the eastern peaks, and stopped, as she always did, at the cairn.

The cairn was older than the guild. Older than the road, maybe. Travelers' custom said you added a stone and named someone you were carrying home to. The stack stood taller than Rosalind now, generations of grief piled into a rough tower against the sky. Her own stones were somewhere in its middle: one for her mother, who hadn't been guild at all but had understood; one for Corvo; one for every runner lost to weather, to wardens, to the simple treachery of mountains.

She didn't add a stone tonight. There wasn't anyone new. That, too, was a kind of grief—the cairn growing no taller because the world had stopped giving her people worth mourning loudly.

"Last toll," she said aloud, to the wind, to the stone, to whatever listened. "Last toll, and then we're done."

The word *done* sat wrong in her mouth. She spat into the snow and kept moving.

* * *

The drop point was a hollow below Raven's Tooth, a fold of rock that hid a firepit and half a lean-to from everything except the stars. Fen was already there, hunched over a flame no bigger than a cupped hand, feeding it twigs one at a time like a man paying out coins he couldn't spare.

"You're late," he said, without looking up.

"I'm exactly on time. You're early. It's different." She shrugged off her pack and let it fall. "Wool's dry. Pepper's ruined. Tell Tam we eat well this month and poorly next."

Fen grinned—that quick, crooked grin that reminded her painfully of another boy, another decade, another fire on this same stone. "Tam says if the wool sells clean, he can fix the roof before thaw."

"The roof," Rosalind said, "has needed fixing since before you could walk."

"And yet here we are, still dry-ish." He fed the fire another twig. His grin faded by degrees, the way it did when he let himself think about what came after. "Roz. After tonight. What do we actually do?"

It was the question she'd been refusing to answer since Corvo died. The roads were closing—not just theirs, all of them. The Crown's new toll stations squatted at every valley mouth like gray toads, and the inquisitors rode with ledgers instead of swords now, which was worse, because a sword only killed you once. Trade had dried to a trickle. Smugglers starved honestly or turned bandit dishonestly, and the guild had never been bandits. That line mattered to her more than blood.

"We do what we've always done," she said. "We wait. Roads close. Roads open. Mountains don't care who sits the throne."

"And if they never open?"

"Then we're three people with a very large cellar and excellent taste in stolen wine."

He laughed, and for a moment the hollow below Raven's Tooth felt like it used to feel—warm, conspiratorial, a room with a hundred voices in it even when only two bodies sat there. She let herself have the moment. Grief was a tax you paid in installments, Corvo used to say, and tonight she could afford one small payment of joy.

They were loading the last of the wool onto the buyer's mule—a silent, nervous wool-merchant's factor who never met their eyes—when the dog started barking.

They didn't have a dog. The factor did, a shaggy yellow thing tied near the mule, and it went suddenly rigid, ears up, baying at the dark mouth of the ravine north of the hollow.

Rosalind's hand found her knife before her mind caught up. "Fen. Light."

He raised the lantern. Yellow light slid down into the ravine and found, first, blood on the rocks—too much, black and glistening—and then a man.

He was lying wedged between two boulders where he'd clearly crawled, dragging himself uphill toward the light with the last of whatever he had left. Courier's leathers, mud-caked, torn across the ribs. One boot missing. His hands were pressed to his belly as if holding himself shut, and even from ten feet away Rosalind could smell the wound underneath the cold air: copper and rot.

"Fen, get my kit. Now."

She slid down the scree without waiting, boots finding holds by instinct, and knelt in the frozen muck beside him. Up close he was younger than she'd first thought—mid-twenties, maybe, with a court courier's fine cloak ruined past recognition and a face gray as ash.

"Don't," he rasped, when she reached for him. "Don't waste—it. Listen."

"I'm listening while I work." She got his cloak open. The wound beneath was bad in the way wounds were bad when the person wearing them had been running for days. A crossbow bolt, snapped off short, buried deep. "Who shot you?"

"Inquisitors." The word came out flat, matter-of-fact, the way men said *weather* or *taxes*. "Not for me. For what I carry."

Fen arrived with the kit, took one look, and went whiter than the patient. "That's—Roz, that's a royal seal on his satchel."

The courier laughed, wet and terrible. "Was. Won't be by morning. They'll burn me and call it treason." He caught Rosalind's wrist with shocking strength, fingers like iron bands around her bones. "You're guild. Thornfell guild. I know the sign on your collar."

She hadn't known she was wearing it. A small stitched wren inside her hood, invisible unless you knew to look. Old habits. Old pride.

"My name is Aldous Ren," he said. "I carried dispatches for the Crown nine years. Nine years, and three days ago I saw something in the king's own corridor that I cannot—I don't have the words—" His breath hitched, rattled. "Doesn't matter. Words won't save anyone. This might."

His hand left her wrist and fumbled at the satchel's buckle. Fen looked at Rosalind. Rosalind looked at the ravine behind them, at the dark where the inquisitors presumably still hunted, and made the oldest decision in the guild's creed without knowing she was making it: *you don't leave a runner on the road.*

"Give it here," she said.

He pressed it into her hands—a map case, oilskin-wrapped, sealed with a blob of wax stamped with the crowned stag of Aldmere. But the wax had been broken and resealed, hastily, with a second mark pressed over the first: a physician's caduceus, or something like it, drawn freehand in a second layer of red wax.

"It goes to the palace," Aldous whispered. "Not to the palace—to *under* it. You understand? Under. The vaults. There's a door the maps don't show, and this—" his bloody fingers tapped the case, tap, tap, tap, like a dying clock, "—this shows it. Shows everything. Where the roads go. Where *he* goes."

"Where who goes?"

The courier's eyes fixed on hers, and what she saw in them frightened her more than the blood, more than the inquisitors, more than anything the mountains had ever shown her in eleven years of walking their dark places. It wasn't fear of death. It was fear of something he couldn't stop seeing.

"The king," Aldous Ren said. "The king of Aldmere has not been himself for three years. Not ill. Not mad. *Not himself.* Something else is wearing him, and it knows I know, and it sent them—" a spasm took him, arched his back, wrung a sound out of him that Fen flinched away from, "—it sent them to take this back. Don't let them. Whatever they offer you. Whatever they promise. Don't let them take it back."

"Aldous—"

"Promise me." His grip tightened. "You're the last of them. Corvo's guild. The road-keepers. If anyone can get it under the palace unseen, it's your kind. Promise me you'll finish the delivery."

Every instinct she owned said refuse. Say nothing. Take no burden, owe no debt, move unseen—the creed, the whole creed, the wall she'd built her life inside. Taking this case meant being seen. Being seen meant being hunted. Being hunted meant the last three members of the Thornfell guild would become zero, and the fire would go out for good, and the cairn would grow by three stones she'd never carve.

But he was dying in front of her, and he had crawled uphill toward her light, and he had called her *the last of them* like it was a title worth something.

"I promise," Rosalind Vasquez said.

Aldous Ren smiled. It transformed his face, made him briefly, terribly young. "Knew you would. Guild honor." His eyes drifted past her shoulder, up toward the cairn she couldn't see from down here in the ravine. "Add a stone for me, will you? Top of the pile. Be dramatic about it."

And then, between one breath and the next, he was gone—grip slackening, head lolling, the terrible tension of the dying simply switching off. The ravine got quieter. The wind picked up his blood-smell and carried it away north.

Fen made a small sound. Rosalind realized she was still holding the map case, both hands, hard enough to hurt.

* * *

They buried him shallow, under a cairn of their own making, because the ground was iron and neither of them had a shovel. Fen stacked the stones. Rosalind stood over them a long time, doing sums she didn't want to do.

One dead courier. One broken seal. One promise made to a stranger in a ravine, witnessed by a boy of nineteen and a dog that had finally stopped barking.

"They'll come looking for that," Fen said quietly. "Inquisitors. They'll trace the route. They'll find the hollow."

"Then we're not going back to the hollow." She weighed the case in her hands. Light. Absurdly light, for the weight of what it held. "Get the wool to Tam. Sell it, fix the roof, and then get him somewhere south where I can find you. Both of you."

"And you?"

Rosalind looked north, toward the capital she'd spent her whole life avoiding, the great smoky sprawl of Aldmere City where kings lived in palaces built over older, darker palaces. She had never wanted anything to do with crowns. Crowns were the reason the tolls existed. Crowns were why the guild was three people in a cellar.

But a dying man had put a title on her—*the last of them*—and guild honor was the one currency she'd never learned to counterfeit.

"I'm going to read a map," she said.

She turned the case over in her hands. In the moonlight, the double-sealed wax gleamed like a wound of its own—the crowned stag of Aldmere, and beneath it, pressed deep, that strange red mark she didn't recognize. A physician's sign, maybe. Or something older.

Whatever it was, it had gotten a nine-year courier killed on her mountain, in her pass, on the last night of the last route.

Rosalind tucked the case inside her coat, against her heart, where smugglers carried the things they couldn't afford to lose, and started walking south.

Behind her, the wind rose through the narrows, singing its one unresolved note, and for the first time in eleven years, Rosalind Vasquez heard it as a warning.
