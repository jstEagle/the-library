## 2. What the Map Refuses to Show

The cellar beneath the Drowned Bell smelled of tallow, mildew, and forty years of secrets, and Rosalind Vasquez had spent half her life learning every inch of it.

She'd lit three candles instead of one. A extravagance, by guild reckoning—Old Maren would have boxed her ears for wasting wax—but she needed light for this. She needed to see everything.

The courier's map lay unrolled across the barrelhead worktable, weighted at its corners with four smooth stones she'd collected from the Thornfell riverbed as a girl, back when the guild had numbered thirty and the passes had sung with their traffic. The parchment was fine stuff. Too fine. Smugglers drew maps on scraps of hide and stolen ledger paper; this was vellum, cream-colored, ruled with a precision that made her fingers itch.

And the seal—the broken stub of dark green wax still clinging to its edge—was pressed with a crown.

Not a merchant's mark. Not a guild signet. A crown.

"Stolen crown property," she murmured to the empty cellar, testing the words the dying man had used. *They'll say you stole it.* He hadn't said what it was. Only that she should burn it if she was caught, and that burning it would be a mercy.

Rosalind pulled her chair closer and began, properly, to read.

* * *

The first hour taught her that whoever had drawn this map knew roads. Not soldier's roads or surveyor's roads—*smuggler's* roads. The cartographer's hand marked the old ways with a familiarity that bordered on reverence: the Hollow Stair behind Vetch's Mill, the Sump, the long crawl beneath the Widow's Tooth where you had to exhale your whole breath and trust the stone. Routes Rosalind had bled to learn. Routes Maren had sworn only three living people knew, and two of them were dead.

Someone else had known them all along. Someone close enough to the Crown to draw on vellum and seal in wax—and hidden enough to know the smuggler's grammar of dashed lines and hollow circles, of tide-marks and toll-stones.

That thought alone would have kept her up for a week. But it was the second hour that stopped her heart.

The map didn't end at Thornfell. It continued east, past the last of the guild's territory, into country Rosalind had never walked—the lowlands, the river country, and then the capital itself, Aldmere-on-the-Spire, rendered in strokes so confident they could only come from memory. And threaded through the capital's tidy streets like veins through a wrist were passages.

Passages under the palace.

She counted them twice, then a third time, tracing each line with a fingertip gone cold. A drain-route from the tanneries that surfaced inside the outer ward. A stair beneath the chapel of Saint Verene that dropped past the crypts. And there—at the very center, circled once in ink so fresh it still gleamed—a descent marked with the guild-sign for *deep water* and beside it, in a small, careful hand, a single word:

**BINDING.**

"That's not treasure," she said aloud. Her voice sounded wrong in the cellar, too loud, as if the stones themselves were listening. "Nobody hides treasure under a palace."

Treasure got guarded loudly. Treasure wanted witnesses, wanted ledgers, wanted the whole kingdom to know the Crown was rich and strong. Whatever lay at the bottom of this route had been buried on purpose, sealed with a word that belonged to priests and necromancers and old wives' tales about the founding of Aldmere.

The courier's blood was still under her fingernails. She looked at them now, at the brown cracked crescents, and remembered his weight against her shoulder, the wet rattle of his breathing, the way his eyes had fixed on hers with the desperate clarity of a man spending his last coin.

*The king is not the king.*

Outside, above the cellar stairs, the Drowned Bell creaked in the wind. Ordinary sounds. The inn settling. Old Tam's cat scratching at the door of the taproom overhead. Everything exactly as it had been an hour ago, when the world had been small and simple and hers.

She bent back over the map because looking at it was easier than thinking about what the courier had said. There was cipher-work in the margins—numbers paired with letters, a repeating pattern of marks along the eastern routes. She copied them onto scrap paper, arranged and rearranged them, and got nowhere. The cipher wasn't guild-work. It was court-work, dense and formal, the kind of thing that wanted a scholar's patience rather than a thief's instinct.

Fine. She knew a scholar. Or the nearest thing Thornfell had left: Dorian Ashe, the physician who'd been spat out of the capital three years ago and never said why, who traded remedies for firewood at the edge of the village and drank alone at the Bell every Thirdday. A man with court hands and court manners worn down to the nub. If anyone south of the Spire could read this cipher—

Boots crossed the floorboards overhead.

Rosalind went still, one hand flat on the map, the other sliding to the knife at her belt. Boots were normal. The Bell had boots all day. But these were heavy, deliberate, several sets moving together in the synchronized rhythm of people who had drilled at marching until marching became their gait.

Soldiers didn't come to the Drowned Bell. Soldiers cost money, and Thornfell had none to spare.

Three knocks at the taproom door. A pause. Three more. Then Tam's voice, pitched high with the particular cheerfulness he wore like armor: "Gentlemen! Closed till sundown, but for friends of the Crown, well—"

"We're not friends of the Crown." The voice was dry as ash and carried through the floorboards with terrible ease. "We are the Crown's Inquisition. Open your cellar."

* * *

Later—years later, in another life—Rosalind would try to remember deciding to move, and fail. There was no decision. There was only the sound of the bar being lifted overhead and her body already in motion, folding the map along its creases, rolling it tight, thrusting it into the oilskin sleeve sewn inside her coat where the guild's tax-ledgers used to ride. Her hands did what forty years of guild training had built them to do while her mind stood frozen in the middle of the room screaming.

*They're here. They came for me. They know.*

The candles. She pinched them dead. Darkness swallowed the cellar, and she moved through it blind and sure, down the row of casks, past the false wall that hid the strongroom, to the third wine rack from the north wall. She hauled it open on its counterweighted pivot—Maren's masterpiece, a door disguised as inventory—and slipped into the gap before the mechanism whispered shut behind her.

The escape tunnel beyond was older than the guild, older than the Bell, cut by smugglers nobody had ever caught. It ran forty feet to a root-cellar beneath the tanner's yard, and from there the world opened up in a dozen directions—if you knew the ways. Rosalind knew the ways. She'd known them since she was seven years old.

So why were her hands shaking?

She stood in the black tunnel with her palm against cold earth and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what had frightened her. Not the soldiers. She'd dodged excise men since childhood; danger was the family trade. What frightened her was the words she'd heard through the floorboards, muffled but unmistakable:

*"A thief carrying stolen crown property. Small, dark-haired, answers to no registered name."*

*Answers to no registered name.* They hadn't even bothered to learn who she was. To the Inquisition she was a thing, a rumor, a hole in the world shaped like a woman. And some animal part of her had found comfort in that—invisible, unknowable, safe the way smoke is safe.

But they'd come to *her* cellar. They'd knocked on *her* door. For the first time in her life, the great machinery of the Crown had turned its attention toward Thornfell, and it had turned it toward her, and invisibility was suddenly not a shield but a countdown. Sooner or later, "no registered name" became a name. Sooner or later, someone in the village said *the Vasquez girl, the smuggler's brat, lives at the Bell,* and then she wasn't smoke anymore. She was a person with a face, and faces could be hunted.

Above her, muffled by forty feet of earth, the cellar door burst open. Wood splintered. Boots descended. A voice, dry as ash: "Search everything. She was seen on the mountain road with a dead courier. She has it."

*Seen.* The word lodged in her chest like a splinter. Someone had watched her kneel in the ravine. Someone had reported her. The passes themselves had eyes now.

Rosalind ran—not the panicked sprint of the hunted, but the controlled, breath-counting lope Maren had drilled into her, hand trailing the guide-rope strung along the tunnel wall, feet finding the two safe boards over the collapsed section without her eyes needing light. The tunnel bent, climbed, forked at the marker-stone carved with the guild's toll-sign, and she took the left branch on pure instinct, the branch that hadn't been walked in six years, the one even she only half-remembered.

It let her out through the root-cellar of the abandoned Kellam farmstead, into grey afternoon light and the smell of rotting apples. Behind her, faint as a heartbeat, the Drowned Bell's cellar was being torn apart plank by plank.

She crouched in the nettles, catching her breath, and forced herself to think like Maren instead of feeling like a child. Options. The village was burned—she couldn't go back for supplies, for coin, for the good boots in her room. Tam would deny knowing her; that was the arrangement, and Tam was good at denial. The southern routes were watched; the courier had died on the southern road. That left east. East through the high passes, down into the lowlands, toward the capital and the physician who owed the guild nothing and might be the only person alive who could read what she carried.

Dorian Ashe. Court hands. Court cipher. A man who had been exiled for asking questions, if the tavern gossip was true—and tavern gossip usually was.

"He'd better be worth it," she whispered to the nettles, and heard how thin her voice sounded, and hated it.

She moved along the hedgerow, keeping to the shadow-line where the farm met the wood, and paused once at the crest of the field to look back. Smoke rose thin from the Drowned Bell's chimney—Tam stoking the fire, playing the innocent, bless his frightened heart. Beyond it, Thornfell's peaks stood grey and indifferent against the sky, the passes that had raised her, the roads that had been her family longer than any blood kin.

*I'll come back,* she told them. *Whatever this costs, I'll come back.*

Then she turned east, put the map's weight against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and started walking—away from everything she knew, toward a city full of eyes, carrying the one thing in Aldmere that everyone with power seemed willing to kill for.

Behind her, at the treeline, something shifted. Not wind. Not deer.

She didn't stop to look. Looking was how the seen got caught.
