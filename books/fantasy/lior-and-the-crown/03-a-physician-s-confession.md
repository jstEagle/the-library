## 3. A Physician's Confession

The rain had followed Dorian Ashe all the way from the capital, as if the sky itself had been paid to remind him of home.

He sat in the back room of a miller's cottage in the village of Harrowmere, three days' ride from Aldmere City and a lifetime from the marble corridors where he had once been the youngest physician ever appointed to the royal household. The room smelled of flour dust and lamp oil and the bitter tinctures he still brewed out of habit, though there was no one left to prescribe them to. A single candle burned on the table between his mortar and a half-eaten loaf. Outside, the storm worried the eaves like a patient with a fever that would not break.

He had been in Harrowmere for eleven months. Long enough that the villagers had stopped calling him *sir* and started calling him *Doctor* again, which he supposed was its own small mercy. They brought him fevers and broken bones and the slow failures of age, and he treated them all with the same careful hands that had once touched the wrist of a king.

And every night, before sleep—if sleep came at all—he asked himself the question that had unmade him.

*Why did His Majesty stop sleeping?*

Not: why did he sicken. Not: why did he waste. Those were questions with answers, answers Dorian had written down in his neat clinical hand until the pages were taken from him and burned in a brazier while Lord Chancellor Merrick watched with eyes like coins. No—the question was simpler and far worse. Kings did not simply stop sleeping. Bodies did not simply forget how. In nineteen years of practice, Dorian had seen insomnia born of grief, of guilt, of laudanum and heart-failure and tumors pressing on the mind. He had never seen anything like what he'd witnessed in the king's private chambers on the night of the coronation anniversary, three years past.

He had been summoned because the king had not slept in nine days. Nine. By the fourth day the hallucinations should have begun; by the sixth, the tremors and the paranoia. Yet when Dorian entered the candlelit chamber, King Aldric IV had been sitting upright by the window, perfectly composed, watching the moon with an expression of mild interest—like a man observing weather he had no intention of going out into.

"Your Majesty," Dorian had said, "you must rest."

"I know," the king had replied pleasantly. "I have tried. Something else is using the hours."

Dorian had laughed, because it sounded like a joke. The king had not laughed. And then the king had turned his head—not quickly, not dramatically, just a smooth rotation, like a door swinging—and looked at Dorian with eyes that were open and aware and utterly vacant, the way a house looks through its windows when the family has gone.

Dorian had written his report anyway. *His Majesty presents signs consistent with profound somnolent disturbance of unknown etiology. Recommend observation.* He had used the word *etiology* forty times in six pages, circling the thing he could not name the way a tongue circles a broken tooth. Three weeks later, Lord Chancellor Merrick had invited him to tea, praised his service, and informed him that the court's medical needs would henceforth be handled by physicians of "more appropriate discretion." He had been given a purse, a horse, and one day to leave the city.

That had been two years ago. He had spent them asking the walls of Harrowmere the question no one would answer.

So when the knock came at his door on that rain-lashed night—three sharp raps, then two more, uneven, like someone fighting to stay upright—he rose without thinking, the way he always rose, and opened the door onto a stranger who would change everything he thought he knew about the question.

* * *

She was soaked to the bone, hood plastered to her face, and she moved like water finding cracks—sideways, first checking the lane behind her, then slipping inside before he had fully stepped aside. Under the hood she was young, mid-twenties perhaps, with dark hair escaping its braid and a knife already half-drawn at her hip, though her hand stayed loose on the hilt. Her eyes did the arithmetic of the room in a heartbeat: exits, weapons, the value of the candlesticks.

"You're the exiled physician," she said. Not a question. "Ashe."

"And you're robbing me, I think," Dorian said mildly, "though you've chosen a poor night for it. My wealth consists of leeches and a very good mortar."

Something flickered across her face—annoyance, or maybe reluctant amusement. She pulled back her hood. "My name doesn't matter. I need you to look at something." From inside her coat she drew a leather tube, oilcloth-wrapped, and set it on his table beside the bread. "A man died giving me this. I want to know what it is before anyone else dies for it."

Dorian looked at the tube and felt the hair rise along his arms.

He knew that wrapping. Oilcloth of the palace stores, folded in the field style the royal couriers used—double-folded corners, waxed thread. And when she unwound it, revealing a map rolled tight around a slender rod of black iron, he saw the seal holding the outer fold closed: a disc of deep green wax stamped with a crowned tower.

Not the royal seal. The *other* one. The one that appeared on perhaps a dozen documents a year, carried only by the king's most trusted messengers, the ones whose existence the chancery officially denied. During his years at court, Dorian had seen it exactly twice—once on a summons delivered to his predecessor at midnight, and once, memorably, on the letter that had ordered him gone.

"That seal," he said quietly, "is not stolen crown property. It's the kind of thing people are killed for *carrying*, let alone reading." He looked up at her. "Where did you get this?"

"A courier. Dying in a ravine in Thornfell." Her jaw tightened. "He said—" She stopped. Started again, and her voice came out lower, rougher. "He said the king hasn't been himself for three years. He said if I valued my soul I'd burn it. Then he said the vaults, and he said *the binding*, and then he stopped saying anything at all."

The candle flame leaned in the draft from the door, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

Then Dorian pulled out the chair opposite his own. "Sit down," he said. "You're dripping on my floor, and we are both about to be in a great deal of trouble."

* * *

She gave her name eventually—Rosalind, nothing more—and she drank the tea he made her with the wariness of someone who had learned never to accept anything without knowing its price. But she spread the map on his table, weighting the corners with his mortar, his pestle, the salt dish, the candlestick, and Dorian bent over it and forgot, for a while, to be afraid.

It was beautiful. That was his first thought, absurdly—a physician's appreciation of anatomy rendered in ink. Every passage of the capital's underbelly drawn in fine brown lines: the sewers, the cisterns, the old smuggler roads beneath the foundations, and threading among them, in a different hand, in silver-gray ink that caught the light like frost, corridors that had no business existing. Corridors that passed *through* the palace's outer wall. Corridors that descended.

Toward the vaults.

"This isn't a treasure map," Rosalind said. "Or—it is, but the treasure's a ritual. The courier called it a binding. Do you know what that means?"

Dorian did not answer immediately. His finger had stopped on a notation near the lowest level, a cluster of characters in cipher—except they weren't cipher, not entirely. They were Old Court script, the liturgical alphabet the founders had used for oaths and wards, and he recognized enough of it to feel cold. *Vinculum. Hostis. Memoria pretium.* The binding. The enemy. Memory as price.

"I know what it means," he said slowly, "in the way a man knows the word for a disease he's only read about. There are stories. Founding-era stories, the kind the church calls allegory so no one asks inconvenient questions." He traced the gray corridor downward. "Stories about a thing that was bound beneath the first palace. Fed, not freed. Kept asleep by ritual, by oath, by—" he hesitated— "*payment*. The old texts say bindings of that kind require an anchor. A person. Someone whose ties to the world are strong enough to hold the knot."

Rosalind had gone very still. "Payment of what?"

"Memory," Dorian said. "Belonging. Everything that makes a life worth staying in." He met her eyes. "Which is why, I imagine, no one has rushed to renew it."

The rain filled the silence. Rosalind looked down at the map, and Dorian watched her do the same arithmetic he was doing—the cost, the impossibility, the dead courier in a ravine who had thought this worth his last breath.

"Three years ago," she said at last. "You were at court then. You treated him. What happened to the king?"

And there it was. The question, arriving at his door in wet wool with a knife at her hip, after two years of asking it alone.

Dorian sat down heavily. He poured himself tea he didn't want, mostly to have something to hold.

"On the night of the third coronation anniversary," he said, "there was a ceremony in the crypts below the chapel. Renewal of the founding oaths—I attended in a medical capacity, in case the king's health failed during the rites. It was a formality. It had been a formality for two hundred years." He turned the cup in his hands. "Except that year, the officiant was new. A chaplain none of us knew, appointed by the Lord Chancellor directly. And during the recitation, the king stumbled over a line of the oath. Just one line. Everyone laughed politely. But I was close enough to see his face, and Your Majesty did not look embarrassed. He looked *surprised*. As if the words had been taken out of his mouth and replaced with others."

He looked up. "Nine days later, he stopped sleeping. Within a month, the man who had wept at his father's funeral and argued with his ministers and remembered the name of every servant in the palace began to smile at things that weren't there and sign orders he burned an hour later. I wrote reports. The reports disappeared. I asked why the king stopped sleeping, and the asking cost me everything." He smiled, and knew it was a thin, bitter thing. "They call it exile. It felt more like amputation."

Rosalind was watching him with an expression he couldn't name—until he realized he could name it, because he wore it in every mirror. Recognition. The look of one liar seeing another, one lonely creature identifying its own species across a dark room.

"The courier said the king hasn't been himself," she said quietly. "You're telling me he literally isn't. That something got into him, and the binding that should have kept it out was allowed to lapse."

"Or was *allowed* to lapse," Dorian said. "There's a difference, and I suspect Lord Chancellor Merrick knows which side of it he stands on." He stood, went to the window, and parted the curtain a finger's width. The lane was empty, rain hammering the mud, but somewhere beyond the village a dog was barking—not the idle bark of a dog chasing shadows, but the sharp, rhythmic alarm of a dog that had smelled strangers. "Inquisitors travel in threes," he said. "If they've tracked that map to Thornfell, they'll track it here next. We have until morning, perhaps less."

"*We*?" Rosalind said.

Dorian turned back to her. He was fifty-one years old, disgraced, afraid, and standing in a miller's cottage with a thief and a dead man's map—and for the first time in two years, the question in his chest had an answer taking shape around it.

"I spent two years asking why the king stopped sleeping," he said. "You're the first person to bring me evidence instead of pity. So yes—*we*. I can read the cipher. I know the palace's bones, which doors are guarded and which are forgotten. And you, I suspect, know how to move through the world without being seen." He held out his hand. "I make a poor ally and a worse liar, but I'm told I'm excellent company in a crisis."

Rosalind looked at his hand for a long moment. Then she took it, her grip dry and strong despite the rain.

Outside, the dog fell suddenly silent—which was somehow worse than the barking—and far off down the valley road, faint through the storm, came the sound of hooves. More than one horse. Moving fast.
