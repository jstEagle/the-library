## 4. The Hollow Court

The throne room of Aldmere smelled of lilies and old smoke, and Captain Sera Ives had learned, over three years of morning audiences, to hate both.

The lilies were the king's preference. He had them brought up from the glasshouses every second day, cartloads of white blooms that filled the hall with a sweetness so thick it sat on the tongue like syrup. The smoke was older—centuries of tallow and torch-oil soaked into the vaulted stone above the dais—and no amount of scrubbing had ever lifted it. Sera had stood her post beneath that stone since she was nineteen years old. She knew the room the way she knew her own hands: every draft, every shadow, every place where a man might hide a knife.

She did not know this king.

He sat beneath the antlered crown with his hands folded in his lap, listening to the petitioners shuffle forward one by one across the marble, and there was something in the stillness of him that had never been there before. King Aldric had once been a restless man—a fidgeter, a laugher, a king who drummed his fingers on the armrests and leaned out over the edge of the dais to hear the small voices better. That man was gone. What remained sat like a statue dressed in flesh, and when he moved, he moved all at once, smoothly, the way water moves when you tip a cup.

"Bring the prisoner forward," the king said.

His voice was the worst part. It was still Aldric's voice—the same low baritone Sera had sworn her oath to, the same voice that had once sung badly at midwinter feasts and laughed at his own mistakes. But it came out flat now, level as a frozen lake, without anything moving underneath it.

The guards hauled the prisoner up the aisle between the pillars. A poacher, by the look of him—lean, sunburnt, one eye swollen shut from the journey down from the northern marches. He'd been caught in the royal forest with a bow and two hares in his snare-sack. Under the old law, under any law Sera had ever enforced, that meant a fine and a flogging and a night in the cells. Perhaps the loss of a hand if the magistrate was feeling cruel.

"Your Majesty," the Lord Justiciar intoned, "the prisoner Tam Weller, taken red-handed in the Vertwood, guilty of theft against the crown's game."

The king looked at the poacher for a long moment. His eyes were the color of frost forming on a windowpane—pale blue shot through with white, and utterly, perfectly still. Sera had seen men go into shock and wear eyes like that. She had seen corpses wear them too.

"Kill him," the king said.

The word dropped into the hall like a stone into a well. Even the Justiciar blinked.

"Your Majesty," the Justiciar ventured carefully, "the law provides for—"

"I know what the law provides." The king's head turned, smooth as a weathervane swinging. "I said kill him. Hang him from the gate at noon. Let the forest people see what happens to thieves."

Sera felt her own spine go rigid. Around her, the honor guard kept their faces carved into neutrality, but she caught the flicker that passed between two of them, Bren and Oskar, veterans both, men who had followed Aldric through the border wars. That flicker said: *this is wrong.*

"Your Majesty," Sera heard herself say, before she could stop her own mouth. "The man has a family. Three children, by the magistrate's report."

The king looked at her.

For one heartbeat—one single, suspended heartbeat—something moved behind those frosted eyes. Something that looked, absurdly, like surprise. Like a thing that had forgotten it could be questioned.

Then it smoothed away, and the king smiled, and the smile was Aldric's smile, warm and crooked, and it made Sera's skin crawl worse than the stillness had.

"Captain Ives." Her name in his mouth sounded like a coin being placed on a table. "You have a soft heart. That is why I keep you." He turned back to the Justiciar. "Noon. The gate. See it done."

* * *

It was not done.

At half past eleven, Sera climbed the stairs to the king's private study with the warrant in her hand, intending to beg a reprieve—not mercy, exactly; she told herself it was procedure, the warrant lacked the countersignature of the Chancellor, and the paperwork would be a nightmare if they hanged a man on an unsigned order—but when she reached the door, she found it ajar, and stopped.

Through the gap she could see the king standing at the hearth.

He was holding the warrant. Her warrant. The one she'd left on his desk an hour ago for signature, because protocol demanded the king's own seal on an execution order, even—or especially—when the king himself had pronounced the sentence.

He was burning it.

He held the corner of the parchment to the candle flame and watched it catch, and as the fire crawled up toward his fingers, his shoulders began to shake. At first Sera thought it was laughter. Then the sound came through the door, low and broken, and she understood.

He was weeping.

Great, silent, helpless sobs, the kind that wrack a body from the belly, while the warrant curled to ash between his fingers and fell into the grate. He pressed his free hand against his mouth to smother the sound, and his whole frame shuddered with it, and he did not—Sera was certain of this, certain down to her bones—he did not know why he was crying. There was no grief in his face. There was nothing in his face at all except the tears, spilling out of a body that remembered how to mourn even though whatever lived behind those eyes had forgotten.

The last scrap of parchment blackened and died. The king straightened. He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, briskly, the way a man brushes off rain, and when he spoke aloud to the empty room, his voice was level as a frozen lake again.

"Send for the Justiciar. The sentence is commuted. Fine and flogging."

Sera backed away from the door before her shadow could shift across the gap. She walked back down the corridor with her heart hammering against her ribs like a fist on a cell door, and by the time she reached the guard post at the head of the stairs, she had already begun to lie to herself—to tell herself she'd seen grief, exhaustion, the weight of kingship pressing down on a tired man. Kings were strange. Power was strange. Three years of rule could hollow anyone.

But three years. That was the number that wouldn't leave her alone.

Three years ago—almost to the day—King Aldric had stopped sleeping.

She hadn't noticed it at first. No one had. It had begun as small things: the king appearing at dawn councils already dressed, already alert, when the chamber staff swore no one had woken him. The lamps in his study burning past midnight and yet never needing fresh oil. Physician Ashe asking his quiet, careful questions—*how does His Majesty rest, has anyone observed him sleeping*—and then being gone, exiled within the season, and everyone too relieved to be rid of the awkward questions to ask where he'd gone.

Sera had signed the order for his exile herself. She remembered the weight of the quill. She remembered thinking: *good riddance, let the court have one less worry.*

Now she stood at the head of the stairs and thought about Dorian Ashe's face as he'd been led out through the gate—pale, bewildered, mouthing something to her as he passed. She'd never worked out what he'd been trying to say. She thought about it now, turning it over like a coin worn smooth, and the shape of the words finally assembled themselves in her memory:

*I only asked why.*

* * *

That evening she went looking for answers the way she'd once gone looking for smugglers in the Vertwood: quietly, alone, without telling anyone where she was going.

The palace archive occupied the oldest wing, a warren of stone rooms where the kingdom's memory gathered dust in iron-bound chests. The archivist, a stooped woman named Pell who feared nothing on earth except mice, raised one eyebrow when Sera asked for the coronation records.

"Anniversary records, you mean," Pell said. "Three years back?"

"Everything," Sera said. "The feast, the guest lists, the household accounts. Everything from that week."

Pell studied her for a long moment. Whatever she saw in Sera's face made her decide not to ask. She lit a lamp and led the way into the stacks.

The records told Sera very little and one enormous thing.

Very little: the anniversary feast had proceeded normally. Two hundred guests, forty courses, the usual round of toasts. The king had retired at midnight. Nothing remarkable.

One enormous thing: the household accounts showed that on the morning after the feast, the royal apothecary had purchased—on a single order, paid from the privy purse—eleven pounds of white lilies. And every second day since, for three years, another order. Eleven pounds, every second day, without fail. Over four thousand pounds of flowers. Enough lilies to fill a chapel. Enough to fill a tomb.

Nobody bought flowers like that for love. Sera's mother had grown roses; she knew what cut blooms cost, and she knew what they were for. Flowers were for covering smells.

Or feeding something.

She sat back from the ledger, and the lamp flame steadied, and in its light she made herself look at the thought she'd been circling for six hours the way a tongue circles a cracked tooth.

*The king is not the king.*

Not mad. Not ill. Not burdened. *Replaced.* Some other thing wearing Aldric's face, sitting in his chair, signing his orders, smiling his smile—and weeping, sometimes, in empty rooms, because somewhere deep inside the stolen body the real man was still screaming.

It was madness. It had to be madness. She was a soldier, not a priest or a philosopher; she dealt in things she could see and count and arrest. And yet the frost-pale eyes rose behind her eyelids every time she blinked, and the sound of that weeping—grief with no mourner inside it—would not stop ringing in her ears.

There was a knock at the archive door. Pell went, exchanged low words with someone, and returned carrying a folded paper.

"For you, Captain. From the Lord Justiciar's office. Marked urgent."

Sera broke the seal. The Justiciar's handwriting was cramped and precise:

*By order of His Majesty: a thief has fled the Thornfell roads bearing stolen property of the crown—a map. The thief is to be found and the property recovered intact. You are assigned to lead the hunt. The king asks particularly that you succeed. He says you have a talent for finding what hides.*

She read it twice. The words were ordinary. Warrants were always ordinary; that was their power. But the last line sat wrong in her stomach, the way a stair sits wrong when you expect one more step and find air.

*He says you have a talent for finding what hides.*

The king had spoken to the Justiciar about her. Personally. Had chosen her, out of every officer in the realm, to hunt this thief—and had known enough to flatter her with the exact truth of her own skill.

Which meant the king knew her. Watched her. Had perhaps watched her standing outside his study door this afternoon, watching him burn a warrant and weep for reasons he didn't understand.

Sera folded the paper slowly. Outside the archive's narrow windows, the first snow of winter was coming down over the capital, softening the rooftops, erasing the roads, laying a white sheet over everything the city wanted hidden.

Somewhere out there, past the passes, a thief was running with a map that mattered enough to make a dead-eyed king afraid.

Sera decided, in that lamplit quiet, with snow falling on the world like a shroud, that she was going to find the thief first.

And she was going to find out what, exactly, the crown was so desperate to bury.
