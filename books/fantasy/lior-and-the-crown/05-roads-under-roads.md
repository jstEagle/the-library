## 5. Roads Under Roads

The old smuggler ways did not appear on any map of Aldmere, which was precisely why they still existed.

Rosalind led Dorian down a stair that had been carved into the mountain's root before the kingdom had a name, her lantern held low so the light pooled at their feet instead of spilling into the dark ahead. The steps were slick with a dampness that never dried, and the walls wept mineral tears in long white streaks, like the stone itself was grieving something.

"Mind the fourth step," she said, without turning. "It's a lie."

"A lie," Dorian repeated. His voice had the flat patience of a man deciding whether he'd made a terrible mistake three days ago, or whether the verdict was still pending.

"It looks solid. It isn't. Step on the edge instead." She heard him test it, heard the hollow knock of his boot on stone that would have dropped him into a drainage channel full of winter meltwater. "Every road I know has one lie in it. A false step, a false wall, a false turn. The guild built them in on purpose."

"Why?"

"Because maps get stolen." She glanced back at him. In the lantern light his face was all sharp angles and sleeplessness, the face of a man who had spent years in comfortable rooms and was only now learning what his body could survive. "A thief with a map knows where to go. A thief who's walked the road knows where *not* to. That's worth more."

"And the rest of it? This creed you keep mentioning?"

Rosalind faced forward again and kept walking. "Move unseen. Owe nothing. That's the whole of it. Everything else is commentary."

They descended for what felt like hours, through chambers where the ceiling opened into darkness too vast for the lantern to touch, past doorways bricked shut by hands two centuries dead. Once they crossed a gallery where the walls were carved with names—hundreds of them, thousands, cut in a dozen different hands across generations—and Rosalind touched two fingers to the stone as she passed, an old habit she didn't examine.

Dorian noticed anyway. He noticed everything; it was the physician in her. "Whose names are those?"

"People who used these roads when using them cost something." She kept walking. "Ask me again someday, if there is a someday."

* * *

They camped in a waystation the guild had maintained since before Rosalind was born—a chamber behind a false wall, dry and defensible, with a hearth vented so cleverly that no smoke ever reached the surface. There were stores cached here: hardtack wrapped in waxed cloth, a jar of pickled things gone soft with age, a bottle of plum brandy that Maren had laid down against a bad winter that had apparently outlasted her.

Rosalind spread the map on the floor between them and weighted its corners with stones while Dorian saw to the fire. Three days of travel had taught her something about him: he worked best with his hands busy. Give him a task and the fear went quiet in him.

"The cipher," she said. "You said you were close."

"I said I was *frustrated*." He sat down across from her, cross-legged, and pulled the map toward himself. "But yes. Close. Look here—the border ornamentation. It repeats, but not evenly. Every seventh motif is different. A keyhole where there should be a rose, a rose where there should be a keyhole." He traced the pattern with a fingertip, and Rosalind watched his eyes move with the same focused hunger she'd seen in him when he talked about the king's illness. "It's a counting cipher. The guild used something similar?"

"We used knots. Different knot for every kind of cargo, tied in sequence." She shrugged. "Same idea. Go on."

"So the motifs mark letters, or numbers, or both. And the sequence—" He stopped. Sat very still. Then, slowly, he turned the map ninety degrees and looked at it sideways, and Rosalind saw the color drain from his face in real time.

"What?" she said. "What is it?"

"This isn't a route to treasure." His voice came out strange, thin. "These coordinates—they're not surface features. They're depths. Levels. Look—" He stabbed his finger at a cluster of markings near the map's eastern edge, beneath where the capital sat like a spider at the center of its web. "This whole section is drawn upside down relative to everything else. I thought it was a mistake. It's not a mistake. It's a mirror. These aren't roads *to* the palace, Rosalind. They're roads *under* it."

The fire popped. Somewhere far above them, water dripped in the dark.

"There's nothing under the palace but vaults and bones," Rosalind said slowly.

"That's what everyone thinks." Dorian looked up at her, and there was something almost apologetic in his expression, as if he regretted being the bearer of the truth. "But I served in that palace for eleven years. And there was always one door in the undercrofts that no one had a key to—not the steward, not the captain of the guard. Old stone, older than the rest. The masons' records called it the Binding Vault, and the records stopped calling it anything at all about sixty years ago, because someone burned those pages." He exhaled. "Your dead courier wasn't carrying a map to the king's gold. He was carrying a map to whatever the crown has been hiding underneath itself."

Rosalind stared at the mirrored section of the map, at the elegant inverted geometry of it, and felt the weight of the courier's last breath against her ear all over again. *The king is not the king.*

"Well," she said finally. "Then we'd better hope the roads under roads are better kept than the ones above."

* * *

They traveled four more days, and somewhere in those days the strangest thing happened: they became companions.

It happened in increments. Dorian learned to read the lies in the road—the false step, the false wall—and stopped asking why. Rosalind learned that he hummed when he was thinking, some court tune worn nearly smooth, and that he could set a broken finger and stitch a wound with hands steadier than her own. He learned that she counted her steps in sevens, an old guild habit for keeping time in the dark, and that she slept facing the exit of every room they stopped in.

On the third night he asked her, quietly, over the last of Maren's brandy: "Why do you keep doing this? The guild is three people and a cellar. You could walk away. Start over somewhere the name Vasquez doesn't weigh anything."

Rosalind turned her cup in her hands. "Because when my mother died, the guild took me in. Fed me. Taught me the roads, the creed, the knots. Gave me a family when I had nothing but her body and her debts." She drank. "You don't abandon family because the house is falling down. You hold up the roof."

"And if holding it up kills you?"

"Then I'll have died owing nothing," she said, "which is more than most people manage."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then: "I asked why the king stopped sleeping. One question. That's all it was. Eleven years of service, and one question ended me." His laugh had no humor in it. "My whole life I believed that if you were competent and honest, the institution would protect you. It took me until exile to understand the institution protects itself. Everything else is inventory."

"Welcome to the guild," Rosalind said. "First lesson's free."

* * *

The ambush came on the fifth day, at a junction called the Widow's Throat, where three tunnels met in a chamber tall enough to swallow a church.

Rosalind smelled them before she saw them—tallow, cold iron, and under it all a faint chemical sharpness she'd learned to dread in the last week. Inquisitor's oil. They were already in the chamber when she caught the scent, four figures rising from behind a barricade of fallen rock, and the lead one lifted a lantern of his own and said, in a voice like a man reading charges:

"Rosalind Vasquez. By authority of the Crown, you are detained for theft of royal property. Surrender the document and your companion, and the Crown may yet be merciful."

She had time to register exactly three things: that they'd found the Widow's Throat, which meant someone had given them the road, which meant a leak in what remained of her world; that Dorian was between her and the second man, unarmed, frozen; and that the nearest tunnel mouth—the Sump, a flooded crawl she hadn't used since she was sixteen—was forty feet of open ground away.

"Owe nothing," she breathed, more prayer than creed. Then she grabbed Dorian's collar and ran.

The first crossbow bolt buried itself in the wall where her head had been. The second took their supply pack off Dorian's shoulder, tearing through canvas and salt pork and the spare lantern, scattering a week of survival across the stones. Rosalind didn't stop. She hit the Sump's entrance at a slide, shoved Dorian in ahead of her, and followed him into water that hit her chest like a fist, black and cold enough to stop her heart for a beat.

"Crawl," she gasped. "Don't stand up. The ceiling drops. If you stand, you drown."

They crawled. Behind them, boots splashed at the tunnel mouth, then voices—sharp, arguing—and then a splash and a scream as one of the inquisitors found the deep channel the hard way. Rosalind kept moving, hand over hand along a guide-rope so old it felt like touching a ancestor's ghost, dragging Dorian by the wrist whenever his breathing went ragged.

They came out at last through a spring-fed cleft into grey daylight and freezing rain, half-drowned, shaking, alive. Their supplies were gone. The map—rolled in oilcloth inside Rosalind's coat—was soaked but intact. Dorian sat on a wet rock and laughed, high and unsteady, the laugh of a man whose body had decided terror needed an exit.

"They knew the junction," he said. "How did they know the junction?"

Rosalind stood in the rain and let the cold settle the question she'd been avoiding for five days. Someone had sold the Widow's Throat. Which meant the guild's remaining secrets were leaking, which meant either one of her three surviving guildmates had broken—or someone had gotten to them.

"Dorian," she said. "How fast can you walk?"

He looked up at her, dripping, exhausted, and something in her face must have answered the question before she asked it, because he got off the rock.

"Faster than that," he said.

They walked. And behind them, far back down the dark of the Sump, Rosalind could have sworn she heard the sound of pursuit turning away—not retreating, but *redirecting*, flowing around them like water finding a new path, toward the one place all roads in Aldmere eventually led.

Toward the capital. Where something wearing the king's face was waiting to learn whether its map had survived the flood.
