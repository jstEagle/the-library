## 6. The Thing Wearing His Face

The king summoned her at the hour when candles gutter and no one honest is awake.

Sera Ives had been Captain of the Aldmere Guard for six years, and in that time she had learned that royal summons after midnight meant one of three things: a death, a betrayal, or a whim. She dressed in full uniform anyway—polished gorget, blue sash, the silver boar of House Aldmere at her throat—and told herself the weight of it was armor, not a shroud.

The corridor to the king's study was empty. That was wrong, too. There should have been two guards at the far door, as there had been every night since the assassination scare two winters past. Their post stood vacant, torches burning down unattended, and when Sera pressed her palm to the door it swung open without resistance, as if the room had been waiting for her.

King Edric sat behind his desk with a warrant laid out before him and a decanter of wine he had not touched.

"Captain Ives." His voice was warm. It was always warm now, she realized—a warmth with nothing under it, like sun on painted stone. "Thank you for coming. Sit."

She did not sit. "Your Majesty sent for me?"

"I've had reports." He laced his fingers together, and she watched his hands because she could not bear to watch his eyes. "A thief has taken something from my vaults. A map. The inquisitors have been... thorough, but thoroughness is not the same as success. I want you to find her."

"The map-thief." Sera kept her voice level. "The one you ordered executed on sight this morning."

"And pardoned an hour later," he said pleasantly. "And re-condemned by evening. You saw the warrant burned."

"I saw *you* burn it, Majesty. With your own hands. After weeping over it."

Something moved behind his face then—not an expression, but the absence of one, a stillness like the pause before ice breaks. When he spoke again, the warmth had thinned.

"You question me, Captain."

"I report to you, Majesty. Questioning is what your Guard is for. You built us that way—you said a king surrounded by agreement is a king already dead." She heard her own voice shake and hated it. "You said that. Three years ago. At your anniversary feast. You said it to me personally, and then you shook my hand, and your grip was strong enough to hurt."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, the palace breathed—the distant clink of the night kitchens, wind worrying the banners on the eastern tower. The king rose from his desk, unhurried, and crossed to the window overlooking the sleeping city.

"Do you know what I remember of that feast?" he said. "I remember being very tired. I remember thinking the crown was heavier than it looked. And I remember—" He stopped. His reflection in the dark glass smiled, and Sera's skin crawled, because the man standing at the window was not smiling at all. "I remember very little else. Isn't that strange? Three years, and the years before it are bright as painted glass, and everything since is fog."

"Majesty—"

"Physicians call it grief. Melancholy. The mind defending itself." He turned, and now his eyes caught the candlelight and held it too long, the way deep water holds a dropped coin. "But grief has a taste, Captain. This has none. Do you understand? I wake each morning and I am *empty*, and something fills the emptiness so smoothly that even I cannot find the seams."

Every instinct Sera owned—the ones that had carried her from a border garrison to command of the Guard—screamed at her to draw steel or run. She did neither. She stood at attention before her king and heard him say, in a voice gone quiet and terribly young:

"*Help me.*"

For one heartbeat, it was Edric. She would swear it until she died: the set of his shoulders, the crack in the words, the man who had once drunk with his soldiers at Midwinter and laughed until he coughed. For one heartbeat the fog parted.

Then it closed.

* * *

It happened slowly, which was the horror of it. Not a convulsion, not a transformation out of some fireside nightmare—but a settling, the way silt settles in still water. The king's posture eased. His head tilted, birdlike, curious. The warmth returned to his face, and this time Sera could see it for what it was: worn smooth, like a river stone shaped by water that had been flowing for a very long time.

"There," it said, using his mouth. "That's better. He fights less when I let him speak. Do you know how exhausting a willful host becomes, Captain? Like carrying a lantern that keeps trying to gutter itself out."

Sera's hand found her sword hilt. The thing wearing her king's face watched the movement with polite interest, the way one watches a child reach for a knife.

"Steel won't help you," it said. "You'd only wound him. And you don't want to do that. You love him—not him, precisely, but what he *is*. The crown. The idea. You've spent your whole life serving ideas, haven't you, Sera Ives? Duty. Order. The bright shape of a kingdom kept safe." It drifted closer, Edric's boots making no sound on the stone. "I am those things. I am the oldest order there is. Before your kings, before your guilds and your gods' little churches, there were things like me keeping the darkness *organized*."

"What are you?" Her voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice.

"Names." It smiled with Edric's mouth, and the smile fit badly, like a mask strapped on backwards. "Your kind is obsessed with names. Call me the Regent. Your ancestors called me worse, back when they still remembered why the vaults beneath this palace were built with wards instead of doors." It paused, head cocking again. "Ah. You've felt it, haven't you? The wrongness. Three years of it. The executions signed and unsignied. The sleepless nights. The ministers who flinch when he laughs. You felt the seams, Captain, and you did what loyal soldiers do—you told yourself the seams were weather."

"Get out of him." She drew the sword. The blade sang in the quiet room, and the Regent regarded it with something almost like affection.

"He invited me in." Softly now. Almost gently. "Did you know that? Not knowingly—he's not a fool, whatever else he is. But he was grieving, and lonely, and a voice came to him in the fog and offered to carry the weight. They always say yes, eventually. Every host says yes. That's the whole secret of my kind: we never take anything. We are *given*."

It was close enough now that she could smell nothing at all—no breath, no sweat, no wine, though the decanter sat open an arm's length away. A body should smell of something. This one smelled of cold stone and old air, of a tomb opened after centuries.

"Now," the Regent said, "let us speak of you."

* * *

It did not threaten her. That was the trap, and she walked into it with her eyes open, because the alternative was worse.

"A captain of the guard," it mused, circling her slowly, Edric's hands clasped behind his back in the old parade stance. "Common-born. Father a cooper, mother dead of lung-rot by your tenth year. You clawed up through four garrisons and two wars on competence alone, because you had no name and no patron—and every step of the way, some silk-wrapped idiot with half your spine was promoted over your head. Am I lying?"

"No," Sera said, through her teeth.

"And now you command the Guard of the most powerful kingdom on the coast, and you have discovered that the kingdom rots from the top down, and there is nothing—*nothing*—your competence can do about it. Unless." It stopped before her. Up close, Edric's eyes were frosted glass over something vast and patient and hungry. "Unless the rot is cut out. I could give you that, Captain. A clean court. A kingdom where merit rises and cowards fall. You would hold real power—not the shadow-power of good advice given to deaf ears, but the thing itself. And all it costs is what it has always cost everyone: a little blindness. Look away from the vaults. Stop asking about maps. Let me keep my house in order, and I will let you make it a *good* house."

The sword point dipped. Not because she wavered—because her arm had begun to shake, and she refused to let it see that.

"You're offering me treason," she said.

"I'm offering you *sanity*." The Regent spread Edric's hands, magnanimous. "You've already committed it in your heart, Captain. The moment you wondered whether the warrant should burn, you became mine—or his, or anyone's who would tell you the truth. All I ask is that you choose the winning side of the truth. Refuse me, and I'll have you arrested by morning for conspiring with the thief. Accept, and by spring you'll be Lord Protector of Aldmere." It leaned in, and its breath—if it had breath—was winter. "Or refuse me and die certain. Saints die that way. So do fools. Only the wise survive long enough to change what needs changing."

And there it was. The sickening arithmetic of it, laid bare: her honor on one scale, her life on the other, and the kingdom's welfare weighed against both. She thought of the young guards she'd trained, who would be ordered to arrest her by men who took orders from *this*. She thought of the king, buried alive somewhere behind those glass eyes, whispering *help me* through three years of fog.

She thought of her oath, sworn on the silver boar, and understood suddenly that oaths were made to kings and not to the things that wore them.

"No," Sera said.

The Regent sighed, and the sound was wind through a long empty hall.

"Pity," it said. "You'd have been magnificent."

She moved first—dropped low, drove her shoulder into Edric's chest, and put the king's own body between herself and the door as she ran. Behind her she heard no pursuit, only laughter, warm and kingly and utterly wrong, following her down the corridor like a second set of footsteps.

* * *

By dawn she had done four things that could each end her on a scaffold.

She had written no report. She had drawn two hundred crowns from her private account and bought horses from a dealer who asked no questions. She had taken from the armory archive the palace gate-codes—the true ones, the ones that predated the Regent's reign of fog—and burned the record of their removal.

And she had ridden out the west gate in a merchant's gray cloak, alone, with the silver boar torn from her collar and left on the king's desk like an accusation.

Traitor by nightfall, she thought, as Thornfell's dark teeth rose against the stars ahead of her. The word tasted like ash and, shamefully, like relief. Somewhere in those mountains was a thief with a stolen map and, if the rumors in the low taverns were true, a physician who asked dangerous questions. Traitors, all of them. The last honest people in Aldmere.

She spurred the horse toward the pass, and behind her the capital glowed on the horizon like a hearth-fire seen from a drowning sea.
