## 7. Three Thieves at the Gate

The snow came down in fat, silent flakes, the kind that muffled the world so completely Rosalind could almost pretend she wasn't being hunted. She crouched beneath a dead alder at the edge of the millpond, watching Dorian pick his way along the frozen bank with all the grace of a man who had spent his life on marble floors. Three days out from the ambush, three days of cold rations and colder silence, and he still flinched every time a branch cracked.

She was about to call him forward when she felt it—that prickle at the nape, the old guild instinct that had kept her alive through twenty years of Thornfell winters.

*Someone's behind you.*

She didn't turn. She let her hand drift to the knife at her hip and said, quietly, to the empty air, "You've been following us since the ford."

A pause. Then, from the shadow of the ruined mill: "Since the ford. Yes." A woman's voice, roughened by wind and something rawer. "I could have taken you at the ford."

"You could have *tried*."

"Ives," Dorian breathed beside her, gone gray. "Captain Sera Ives. Rosalind—she's the king's own huntress. She's been leading the search for us since Greywater."

"I know who she is." Rosalind turned slowly, keeping the alder's trunk between them. The woman who stepped into the open wore no colors, no crest—just a travel-stained cloak over armor stripped of its insignia, and a sword she carried like it had recently disappointed her. Her face was hollowed, her eyes rimmed red, and there was a look about her that Rosalind knew intimately: the look of someone whose whole world had broken open in a single night.

"You're alone," Rosalind said.

"I'm *worse* than alone." Sera Ives stopped ten paces off, hands loose at her sides, deliberately unthreatening. "I stood in front of my king six nights ago and watched something else look out of his face. It offered me a generalship. A title. Everything I've bled for since I was sixteen." Her jaw worked. "And then it smiled with his mouth, and I understood that everything I'd bled for was already eaten."

Dorian made a small sound. Rosalind kept her eyes on the captain.

"So you ran."

"So I ran," Sera agreed. "And now I'm standing in the snow offering my sword to a smuggler and a disgraced physician, because they're the only people in Aldmere who might actually do something." She reached into her coat—Rosalind's knife came free before she'd finished the motion—and drew out not a weapon but a strip of oiled leather, which she tossed to land at Rosalind's boots. "The palace service codes. Rotations, guard posts, the vault stair, the names of every officer still loyal enough to be dangerous. Bought with—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—with things I'm not proud of. With my honor, if I ever had any left after Greywater."

Rosalind looked down at the leather but didn't touch it. "Why should I trust you?"

"You shouldn't." Sera's voice cracked, and she let it. "Trust is for people with something left to lose. I'm asking you to *use* me. Point me at a wall and I'll hold it. That's all I am now—a wall that walks."

For a long moment the only sound was snow settling on the pond ice. Then Dorian, of all people, stepped forward and picked up the strip of leather. He unrolled it, scanned it with a physician's quick eye, and looked up at Rosalind.

"The cipher work is genuine. And the Midwinter rotation—it's marked. She's right. If we're going in, we go in during the feast."

Rosalind exhaled a long plume of breath. *Move unseen. Owe nothing.* The creed sat sour in her mouth. But the creed had never accounted for a kingdom wearing a stolen face, or for the exhausted woman standing in the snow with nothing left to sell.

"Pick up your sword, Captain," she said at last. "You walk behind me where I can see you. And if you're lying—"

"If I'm lying, you'll have plenty of time to kill me in the dungeons." Sera almost smiled. It was terrible and human and Rosalind found herself believing her anyway.

* * *

They came down to the capital on the night of the feast, when the city poured itself into celebration like wine into a cracked cup.

Rosalind had smuggled into Aldmere City a dozen times, but never like this—never with the streets this drunk, this loud, this *distracted*. Midwinter was the one night the watch doubled its numbers and halved its attention; every gate guard was thinking of the feast, of the king's toast, of the girl he'd meet after shift. Lantern light turned the snow to gold. Somewhere a fiddle band was playing badly enough to mean the taverns were full.

"Attention stretched thinnest," Dorian murmured as they passed beneath the walls in the wake of a grain cart, three shadows among fifty. "That's what you said."

"That's what the guild always said. Feast nights were how we moved anything worth stealing." She glanced back at Sera, hooded and hunched, walking like a woman carrying a corpse on her shoulders. "How did you get out of the palace?"

"I walked. The Regent doesn't chase what it thinks it's already caught." Her voice was flat. "It let me go because my betrayal amuses it. It wants to see what fear makes people do."

"Then tonight we disappoint it."

They crossed the city in the gaps between patrols, Rosalind reading the streets the way Dorian read symptoms—the sag of a tired sentry, the dark window that meant a bribed doorwarden, the alley that smelled of tannin and meant no one would follow them into it. Old roads under new ones. The guild had threaded this city for a century, and though the guild was three people and a memory, the threads still held.

At the foot of the palace hill, in the lee of a collapsed boathouse, they stopped to make final plans. Dorian spread the map on an upturned barrel, weighting its corners with stones, and traced the route one last time: the disused cistern stair, the servants' gallery, the long descent to the Binding Vault beneath the foundations.

"It's real," he said quietly. "All of it. I checked the architecture against what I remember of the old plans. There *is* a vault under the east foundation, older than the palace itself. The court forgot it. Or was made to forget it."

"And the ritual?" Sera asked.

Dorian hesitated. Rosalind saw the hesitation and felt dread pool cold in her stomach, because she had spent a lifetime learning to read hesitation, and this one was the worst kind—the kind that preceded bad news someone hoped to soften.

"There's a final passage," he said. "In the cipher margin. I translated it this morning and I've been trying to find another meaning in it all day." He looked up at her, and his eyes were kind, which made it worse. "The binding requires an anchor. Not blood. Not sacrifice of life. The ritual has to be *held* while the Regent is severed—and the only thing strong enough to hold it is a mind bound fast to something it loves. The binder pours those bonds into the working. All of them. Every attachment, every belonging, every name they cherish." He swallowed. "The Regent feeds on connection, Rosalind. So the binding starves it with the binder's own. Whoever performs it loses everyone they love—not their lives. Their *memory* of them. Permanently."

The snow ticked softly against the barrel. Somewhere above, the feast roared on, a thousand voices singing the king's health to a thing that wore his face.

"They'd forget," Sera said slowly. "Their family. Their—"

"Everyone. Forever. They'd remember *that* they loved, and never *whom*. The working leaves the shape of the loss and takes the substance." Dorian closed his eyes. "I volunteered already. Before we left the mill. I've been trying to find a way to tell you both without—"

"No." Rosalind heard her own voice come out flat and hard, a stranger's voice. "No. You don't get to decide that."

"Rosalind—"

"You're a physician. You're the only one who can keep the king alive once the thing's out of him. That's *need*, not sacrifice. And she—" she jerked her chin at Sera, "—is the only one who knows the corridors. Which leaves exactly one person who knows the vault ways, the cipher, and the guild rites for holding a working steady. One person who's done anchor-work before, in miniature, on the old routes." She hated how steady her voice sounded. She hated that some cold, practical corner of her had already known, since the ravine, since the courier died pressing the map into her hands, that the road ended here—with her.

"You've lost enough," Dorian said softly. "Your guild is dying. Your family—"

"My guild is *mine* to spend." The words came out before she could stop them, and they were true, and truth cut going down. "If the price of killing that thing is the memory of the people who made me, then it's mine to pay. Nobody else's."

Sera said nothing. She just looked at Rosalind with an expression that was worse than pity—recognition, soldier to soldier, of someone volunteering for the post no one comes back from.

Rosalind rolled up the map. Her hands were steady. She noticed that, and hated it.

"We go in through the cistern stair at the third bell, when the toast is drunk and every eye in the palace is on the great hall," she said. "We reach the vault before midnight. And whatever happens down there—" she met their eyes, one and then the other, "—whatever it offers me, whatever it shows me, you don't let me stop. Swear it."

Dorian's face crumpled and reassembled itself into something like resolve. Sera drew her sword an inch and slid it home again—an oath in the old style, blade instead of words.

"Third bell," Sera said. "Then we steal a kingdom back."

Above them, on the palace hill, the great hall blazed with light, and something ancient lifted the king's cup and smiled at a thousand guests who had no idea what they were toasting.
