## 8. The Binding Vault

The stairs went down farther than the palace had any right to be tall.

Rosalind counted them the way her masters had taught her—by feel, by rhythm, by the small betrayals of stone underfoot. Forty steps of dressed granite, then older work, then older still, until the walls stopped being built and started being *carved*, and the air took on the taste of deep places: cold iron, old dust, and something sweeter underneath, like flowers left too long in a sealed room.

Behind her, Dorian's breathing had gone shallow. Ahead, Sera's lantern threw their three shadows huge against the descending dark.

"You knew about this," Sera said quietly. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse than that—it was a question she was afraid to have answered.

"I knew there were roads under roads," Rosalind said. "I didn't know where they ended." She let her fingers trail the wall, reading it the way she'd been taught to read everything—by touch, by absence, by what wasn't there. "Nobody did. That was rather the point."

* * *

The map had led them true, as it turned out—but not the way any of them had guessed. The courier's wax-sealed lines weren't a route into the royal vaults at all. They were a route *around* them, beneath them, through foundations laid before Aldmere was Aldmere, through passages that appeared on no survey because the men who drew surveys had never been permitted to know they existed.

The last door was not locked. It was *sealed*, and the seal was not wax or iron but words—old ones, cut into the stone in a script that made Rosalind's eyes water to look at directly, arranged in rings that seemed to shift when she wasn't watching.

Dorian read them aloud before she could stop him.

"Don't—" she began.

But he'd already translated the first line, his physician's Latin sliding into something older, and the rings stopped moving, and the door exhaled a breath of air that smelled like a tomb that had been waiting patiently for company.

"Sorry," he said, not sounding sorry at all. "Habit. Things that are written down want to be read."

"That," Rosalind said, "is exactly the kind of thinking that gets people killed."

"Or saves kingdoms." He pushed the door. It swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, which was somehow more frightening than screaming ones would have been. "Come on."

* * *

The Binding Vault was round.

That was the first thing. Not square, not vaulted in the way of cellars and crypts, but perfectly, deliberately round, as if the space itself had been shaped around a single purpose. The ceiling arched overhead into darkness the lantern couldn't reach. The floor was a mosaic of white stone, and set into it, in concentric rings, were channels—grooves worn smooth, running from the outer edge inward toward a raised dais at the center.

On the dais stood a bier. Empty. Waiting.

And carved into every surface—walls, floor, ceiling, even the bier itself—were names.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Cut deep and filled with something that had once been gold and had gone black with age, spiraling outward from the center in great looping lines that were names and also sentences and also, when Rosalind tilted her head and let her eyes unfocus, something like music.

"Oh," Dorian said softly. He'd gone very still, the way he did when a diagnosis finally assembled itself in his mind. "Oh, you beautiful, terrible people."

"What is it?" Sera's hand had gone to her sword. She hadn't drawn it. There was nothing to draw against. "What are we looking at?"

"A ledger," Dorian said. "Or a cage. Both. The same thing, really." He stepped forward onto the white stone, and the sound of his boot was wrong—too loud, too present, as though the room wanted to make sure it heard him coming. "This is where they bound it. The Hollow Regent. This is where it was supposed to stay."

Rosalind moved to the nearest wall and put her palm flat against the carvings. The stone was cold, but beneath the cold was something else—a faint vibration, like a heart beating very slowly, very far away. "These names—"

"The anchors." Dorian had reached the dais now. He was reading the innermost ring, the oldest, his lips moving. "Every person who gave something to hold it down. Their names, their bonds, their—yes. Yes, look." He beckoned them over, and his finger traced a line of text that wound among the names. "'Let each who binds give up that which holds him to the world, for the Regent feeds on love as fire feeds on air, and only a hollow anchor can hold a hollow thing.'"

The silence after his voice died was very long.

"It eats memories," Rosalind said. "Of belonging. Of people. That's the price."

"That's the price." Dorian looked up at her, and in the lantern light his face was pale and set and terribly calm—the calm of a man who has spent years being afraid and has finally been given something specific to be afraid of. "And do you understand why the court broke it? Look at the dates. Look at the succession of names. Generation after generation, someone had to come down here and give up everyone they loved. Fathers. Mothers. Whole families, erased from the minds of the one person standing between the kingdom and that thing." He laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "Three hundred years of that. And then some frightened chancellor decided the price was too high, and the Regent was sleeping anyway, and surely it would keep sleeping—and they cracked the binding open like an egg and told themselves stories about progress."

"And it waited," Sera said. Her voice was flat. Rosalind recognized the tone; it was the voice of a woman holding herself together with discipline alone. "It waited three hundred years, and then it found its way back to the throne."

"To the throne, yes. Where else? A king is a man surrounded by fear, and fear is what seasons the meat." Dorian crouched beside the empty bier, studying the channels in the floor. "Blood, I think. Or something like it. The ritual runs through these grooves—the binder stands here, at the center, and everything they are flows outward along these lines, and the names take root, and the cage closes." He paused. "It can be reworked. The structure's intact. The words are intact. All it needs—"

"All it needs is someone willing to forget their own mother's face," Rosalind finished.

"Yes."

"No." Sera stepped forward. "No, there has to be another way. Some other anchor. Something else it will accept—"

"There isn't." Dorian rose. He said it gently, which was somehow crueler. "Believe me, Captain, I've spent the walk down hoping otherwise. But the whole mechanism depends on it. A hollow anchor for a hollow thing. If the binder keeps their bonds, the Regent simply drinks them instead—that's how it grew strong enough to wear a king. We'd be handing it a feast and calling it a prison."

"Then we find someone with no one," Sera said desperately. "Some hermit, some—"

"Someone with no bonds can't anchor anything. The names are the nails." Dorian shook his head slowly. "It has to be someone who loves deeply enough that the sacrifice means something. Someone whose loss will actually close the circuit." He turned, and Rosalind saw what was coming the way you see a cart crash in slow motion. "Which is why it should be me."

"No," Rosalind said.

"You haven't heard my reasoning yet."

"I don't need to. No."

"Rosalind." He said her name carefully, the way he said everything—like a man handling instruments that could cut. "Think about what each of us brings. You're a smuggler. Magnificent, but your skills end at the vault door. Sera is a soldier; her skills end at the corridor above. I'm the one who read the seal. I'm the one who understands the mechanism. And—" his voice caught, just slightly, the first crack in the calm, "—and I have comparatively little to lose. An estranged sister. A career already burned. A few colleagues who stopped writing to me years ago. I'm nearly hollow already. Let me finish the job."

"That's not how this works." Rosalind heard her own voice go low and hard, the voice she used on green runners about to panic in a checkpoint. "You don't get to decide you're worthless and call it arithmetic."

"It's not—"

"You just told us the names are the nails. Nails need wood, Dorian. You're offering splinters and calling yourself a beam." She stepped closer, close enough to see the exhaustion carved into him, the loneliness he wore like a second coat. "A few old letters. One sister. Is that going to hold a god-eating parasite for three hundred years? Or does the binding fail in fifty, and some other poor bastard comes down here to pay the bill we shorted?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. She watched the truth land behind his eyes, watched him hate it.

"Then who?" he asked quietly. "Who do you propose?"

* * *

The answer was in her mouth before she'd fully decided it, and it tasted like cold water and falling.

"Me."

Sera made a sound—not quite a word. Dorian went white.

"Rosalind—"

"Think it through." She was walking as she talked, circling the dais, because if she stood still she would stop. "The guild raised me. Guild-masters who fed me, taught me the roads, taught me the creed. Mara, who pulled me out of the Whitcombe flood when I was nine. Old Tam, who taught me locks. Joss, who—"

Her voice snagged. She kept walking.

"Everyone I love fits inside one cellar in Thornfell. Three people left, and half a dozen ghosts. That's the whole of it. Small enough to count. Deep enough to hold." She stopped and faced them both, and found, to her surprise, that she wasn't shaking. "You'd be spending scraps, Dorian. I'd be spending the entire treasury. And unlike you, I know how to carry a price without flinching. I've been saying goodbye to things my whole life. This is just the last one."

"This isn't a toll road," Dorian said hoarsely. "You don't get to pay and walk away clean. You won't remember why you fought. You won't remember *us*. You'll wake up in a room with two strangers and a king you saved for reasons you can't name."

"Then you'll have to be worth re-meeting." She smiled, and it hurt her face. "First impressions matter. Try not to bleed on anyone."

"Rosalind." Sera's voice had gone rough. "There has to be another—"

"There isn't. Your own physician said so." Rosalind turned back to the dais, to the empty bier, to the black-gold names spiraling away into the dark. Somewhere far above, muffled by thirty feet of stone, the Midwinter feast was still roaring—music and laughter and ten thousand people who didn't know what was being weighed in the basement. Somewhere above that, in a gilded room, a thing wearing a beloved face was smiling at courtiers and drinking their fear like wine.

She reached out and touched the innermost ring of names. The stone hummed against her fingertips, patient and hungry and old.

"When?" Dorian asked quietly. Behind the grief in his voice, she could hear him already planning—dosages, timing, contingencies. A physician to the last.

"Now," Rosalind said. "While it's still glutted on feast-day fear and slow to notice us. While the corridors are empty and Sera can hold the door." She rolled up her sleeves, and the cold of the vault settled against her bare arms like a hand. "Because I've thought about it, and I've realized something."

"What?"

"If I sleep on this decision, I'll find a reason to run. So I'm not going to sleep on it." She climbed onto the dais and lay down on the bier, and the stone accepted her weight with terrible gentleness, and the channels in the floor began, very faintly, to glow.

Above them, far above, a bell began to ring.

Not the feast bells. Something else—deeper, wrong, a sound like a struck coffin lid—and Rosalind felt the vibration of it come up through the stone and into her spine, and she knew, with a smuggler's certainty, that somewhere in the palace above, the Hollow Regent had just felt them reach for it.

"Hold the door, Captain," she said. "And Dorian—read me the words. Quickly."
