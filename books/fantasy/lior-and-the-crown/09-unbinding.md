## 9. Unbinding

**Rosalind**

The Binding Vault breathed cold.

Not the honest cold of Thornfell in deep winter, which Rosalind had known all her life and loved the way you love a scarred hand—familiar, useful, yours. This was a cold with intention. It pooled at the ankle like standing water, and it came from the walls themselves: black stone veined with silver script so old that half the letters had worn to suggestion. The original binding. The one a frightened court had broken three generations ago because it demanded too much of them.

The circle waited in the center of the floor, a ring of silver inlay wide enough for two people to lie down in, or one person to stand and be unmade.

Dorian knelt at its edge, chalk in hand, finishing the last of the reworked sigils from the map's cipher. His hands were steady. His voice was not. "Rosalind. Say it once more. Plainly."

"I stand in the circle. I speak the names. The Regent comes for what feeds it—love, belonging, memory of being held—and I let it feed until there's nothing left but me." She rolled her shoulders. "And then it starves mid-strike, because I am not enough. Because no one is ever enough for it. That's how the old binders beat it. They just had to survive being emptied."

"And if you flinch—"

"Then it takes everything anyway, and Aldmere gets a king who isn't a king, and we've bled for nothing." She looked at him. He was pale as tallow, and she found, absurdly, that she wanted to memorize his face—the crooked nose broken twice, the ink stain on his left thumb that never washed out. In case. In case this was the last time she got to know him. "You volunteered first. I'm stealing your turn. Be angry about it later."

"I intend to be furious about it for decades," Dorian said, and his voice cracked on *decades*, and he kissed her forehead once, quick, like sealing a letter, and stepped back out of the circle.

Above them, muffled through forty feet of palace stone, the Midwinter feast roared on. Somewhere up there, the king sat wearing a face that wasn't only his own, laughing at jokes made by men who would order their deaths by morning. Somewhere above them, Sera Ives stood alone in the corridor between the vault door and every inquisitor in the capital.

Rosalind stepped into the silver ring.

The stone was warm under her boots. That was the worst thing yet—that after all that cold, the circle itself was warm, like a held hand, like a hearth, like home. It was already learning what it could take from her.

She began to speak the names.

*Marisol Vasquez,* her mother, dead eleven years, and the vault shivered. *Tomas Vasquez,* her father, who taught her the passes before he taught her letters. The silver script brightened. Something vast turned over in the dark beneath the world, and began to listen.

---

**Sera**

The corridor outside the Binding Vault was thirty paces long, lit by four torches, and Sera Ives intended to die in it if that was the price of the next hour.

She heard them coming before she saw them—boots on marble, too many, moving in the disciplined silence of Crown inquisitors who had stopped pretending to be anything else. Six of them rounded the corner in grey lacquered armor, faces hidden behind masks of white porcelain, and at their center walked Inquisitor-General Corvane, unmasked, smiling the way a knife smiles.

"Captain Ives." His voice was gentle. It always was. That was how you knew. "The king requires the thief below brought to him alive. Stand aside."

"The king," Sera said, "is not here. I've seen what wears him."

Something flickered behind Corvane's eyes—not doubt. Hunger. "Three years you served him. Three years of honors, of rank, of a place at the high table. And you'd throw it away for a smuggler's rumor?"

"For a man weeping alone in his chambers at midnight," Sera said, drawing her sword, "burning his own warrants and not knowing why. For the boy I trained beside when we were both nineteen. Whatever's left of him deserves better than you."

Corvane sighed, genuinely regretful, and raised one white-gloved hand. "Then we do this the hard way, Captain. I always liked you. Remember that, in whatever comes after."

They came at her all at once, six blades in a narrow corridor, and Sera Ives did the only thing she had ever been truly great at, which was hold a line that should not have held.

---

**Rosalind**

*Wren Calloway.* Her first partner, lost in an avalanche on the Kestrel Pass when they were both twenty-two. The vault drank the name and the memory that came with it—the sound of Wren's laugh, the way he whistled off-key on night roads—and gave back nothing but the fact that she had once known someone by that name.

It didn't hurt. That was the horror of it. Each name simply *left*, the way warmth leaves a room through a cracked window, so gradually you don't notice until you're already cold.

The Regent rose through the floor like smoke through a keyhole.

It didn't have a shape at first. Then it had hers—a second Rosalind standing at the circle's edge, wearing her face, her guild leathers, her mother's locket, smiling with perfect kindness.

"You could stop," it said, in her own voice. "Right now. And I'll give them back. All of them. Marisol. Tomas. Wren. Every name you're feeding me—I keep them safe, little thief. I remember them *perfectly*. You'll never lose another thing again."

"You don't keep anything," Rosalind said. Her knees were shaking. She kept speaking. "*Old Maren*, who ran the toll-house at Thornfell's mouth—" and felt the smell of woodsmoke and dried fish slide out of her forever. "—who fed every hungry courier who ever passed her door."

"You're killing yourself to save a stranger," the Regent said. "A king. Kings have never once saved *you*. Your guild starved while his court feasted. Give me the names and take back your family, and I will leave Aldmere tomorrow. I swear it on the old stone."

"It lies," Dorian said from outside the circle, hoarse. "Rosalind—it lies, it has to lie—"

"I know," Rosalind whispered. And she did know, the way she knew the passes: not with her mind anymore, but with whatever was left underneath. "That's the trick of it. It can't offer truth. Truth doesn't feed it."

She spoke the next name. And the next.

---

**Sera**

The third inquisitor fell at her feet and the fourth nearly took her arm off at the elbow, and somewhere in the ringing chaos of it Sera understood that she was going to lose. There were too many. Corvane hadn't even drawn his blade; he stood watching her with the patient interest of a man observing weather.

"You fight beautifully," he said. "It's almost a shame."

"Almost," Sera gasped, and put her shoulder into the fifth one's sternum, and drove him into the wall hard enough to crack porcelain.

The sixth circled behind her. She knew it was over the moment she heard his boots change rhythm—

—and then the corridor filled with light and screaming, because the vault door burst open with a sound like a struck bell, and something poured out of it that was not Rosalind, that was not human, that was grief given weight, and the sixth inquisitor simply *stopped*, dropped his sword, and sat down on the marble and began to weep without knowing why.

Sera knew why. She'd seen it before, in a throne room, three years ago.

"It's working," she breathed. "Corvane—it's *working*—"

"No," Corvane said softly, and for the first time his smile was gone. "No, it can't be. The binding was *broken*. We broke it ourselves, we were promised—" He caught himself, horrified at his own mouth, and Sera laughed through blood and a split lip, because even now, even here, the truth was a weapon and she'd just watched it cut him.

"Promised by *whom*, General?" she asked, and charged him.

---

**Rosalind**

There were so few names left.

She was kneeling now. She couldn't remember when that had happened. The circle blazed around her, silver gone white-hot, and the Regent screamed in a voice that was every voice it had ever eaten—kings, courtiers, children—and tore at the walls of her.

*Give them back,* it shrieked. *I'll give you the guild entire! The passes! The toll-houses! The creed, the fires, the hands on your shoulders—you'll walk into any tavern in Thornfell and be KNOWN—*

"I know," Rosalind said.

And she found, sifting through what remained of herself, that she still knew the names. Not the faces. Not the voices. But the names, and the love that had no object anymore, vast and orphaned, like a river whose valley has vanished.

So she gave it the river.

*"I love my mother. I love my father. I love Wren, and Maren, and the guild, every soul of it, every name I've already forgotten and every name I never learned. I love Dorian Ashe, who kisses like sealing a letter. I love Sera Ives, who holds lines that shouldn't hold. Take all of it. Take it ALL—"*

The Regent lunged for the feast.

And starved.

Because love with no memory attached is a fire with no fuel, and the thing that wore the king's face had spent three thousand years eating only what people *held*, and Rosalind Vasquez, kneeling in a burning circle beneath the palace of Aldmere, was holding nothing at all.

It came apart mid-strike. Not dramatically. Quietly, the way frost leaves a window in spring—unraveling into threads of grey light that streamed upward through the vault ceiling, toward the feast, toward the throne room, toward the hollowed man sitting there who suddenly went rigid in his chair, gasped like a drowning man surfacing, and wept.

The king woke.

He was human. He was hollowed. He sat among his terrified court and wept for three years he could not account for, and far below him, Rosalind toppled sideways out of the circle into Dorian's arms, and the last thing she thought, before the dark took her, was that she couldn't remember why she was crying.

But she was crying. That much had survived.

* * *

**Sera**

Corvane lay breathing shallowly against the wall where she'd left him, disarmed, his porcelain mask shattered, his face finally just a tired middle-aged man's face. Above them, the palace rang with alarm bells—but different bells now, confused bells, bells rung by guards who had woken from something.

Sera pushed open the vault door and descended into silver light and chalk dust and the smell of scorched stone.

Rosalind was unconscious in Dorian's arms, alive, breathing, her eyes moving fast beneath closed lids as though chasing something she'd never catch. Dorian looked up at Sera, and his face was wrecked and radiant at once.

"She did it," he said. "She paid it. All of it."

Sera knelt beside them. Gently, carefully, she brushed the hair back from Rosalind's brow, the way you'd touch something rescued from a fire.

"What did it cost her?" Sera asked quietly. "Exactly?"

Dorian's jaw worked. "Ask her," he said, "when she wakes. And then be ready for her to look at us like strangers."

Above them, faintly, the bells kept ringing—and somewhere in the ringing, Sera thought she heard, impossibly, a single voice singing an old road-song down in the tunnels below the vault, a song no living smuggler should know.

The song of the guild Rosalind could no longer remember.

And it was getting closer.
