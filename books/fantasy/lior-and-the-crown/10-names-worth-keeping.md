## 10. Names Worth Keeping

The snow came early to Thornfell that year, and Rosalind was glad of it.

She stood at the mouth of the old toll cave where the guild had once collected its dues from every cart and courier foolish enough to cross the passes without paying, and she tried—again—to remember Master Corvin's face.

She knew she had loved him. That fact sat in her chest like a coal that refused to go cold: *I loved him. I loved them all.* But when she reached for his face the way she'd once reached for a hidden latch in the dark, her fingers closed on nothing. She remembered hands—broad, scarred, always warm even in winter. She remembered a voice like gravel rolled in honey, saying *move unseen, owe nothing, little wren*. But the face above those hands was a smudge, a watercolor left out in the rain.

Three years of memories, traded away in a vault beneath a palace, and this was the shape of the price: not pain, not absence, but this terrible certainty of love with nothing left to attach itself to.

"You're doing it again."

Dorian's voice came from behind her, gentle. He'd learned to read her stillness the way she'd once read weather. He came to stand beside her, his physician's cloak pulled tight against the wind, his breath steaming.

"Doing what?" she said, though they both knew.

"Reaching for something that isn't there." He didn't look at her; he'd learned that too. "You get this line between your brows. Like you're picking a lock."

"Maybe I am." She huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "The lock just happens to be my own head."

"And?"

"And it's the one lock I never learned to pick." She turned back toward the cave, where their small camp glowed—a fire, two bedrolls, Sera's armor hung on a stone hook like a shed skin. "Come on. The pass closes by dark, and we've got markers to set before it does."

* * *

They had been at it for six weeks now, the three of them, walking the old roads with hammer and chisel and lantern instead of contraband and lies. It had started as an apology, though none of them had said so aloud. The smugglers' roads were the kingdom's forgotten veins—tunnels under mountains, fords that appeared only in drought, stairways cut into cliff faces by hands three centuries dead—and after the Regent fell, after the king woke weeping in his own bed with no memory of the thing that had worn him, someone needed to decide what became of those roads.

The Crown's answer had been seals and garrisons. Lock everything. Own everything.

Rosalind's answer had been different.

"The roads belong to the people who walk them," she'd told the restored King Aldric, standing in a throne room she still found absurd—all gilt and marble, built by men who'd never once been hungry. "That was the first law of the guild, older than any of your charters. You can post your soldiers on the main passes if you like. But the other roads stay open. Free. For anyone who needs them."

The king had studied her for a long moment. He was thinner than a king should be, and there were days he flinched at shadows, days he woke screaming about frost and hunger and a voice like a drain emptying. But his eyes were his own now. That was the thing Rosalind noticed most, whenever she saw him: eyes that moved like a man's, not like frosted glass.

"You could have a place here," he'd said. "A title. Lands. You saved your kingdom, Rosalind Vasquez. Let it owe you something back."

And she had felt the pull of it—gods, she'd felt it—the warmth of walls, of a table that stayed put, of belonging somewhere permanent. For one heartbeat she'd wanted it so badly her teeth ached.

Then she'd thought of Master Corvin's face she couldn't remember, and the toll cave, and the creed beaten into her since she was seven years old, and she'd shaken her head.

"With respect, Your Majesty," she'd said, "your court is the last place in Aldmere I'd be able to breathe."

He'd laughed—actually laughed, a rusty sound like a gate opening after years shut—and said, "Then take the roads. All of them. Keep them open, keep them secret, and send me word when you find trouble moving through them before my spies do." A pause. "Consider it smuggling for the crown. Without the crown."

So that was the work now. Markers and maps, safehouses restocked, wells cleared, old wards renewed. Not a guild. Something else. Something without dues or oaths or masters—just three people who'd bled together in a vault beneath a palace, keeping doors open in a world that kept trying to close them.

* * *

Sera found the marker stone an hour before dusk, half-buried in drift, and dug it out with bare hands because gloves were for people with patience.

"Here." Her voice carried down the slope. "This is the one from the old charts. Corvin's mark."

Rosalind climbed up to her. The stone stood taller than a man, granite furred with lichen, and on its southern face—worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather—was carved the guild's sigil: a wren in flight over a keyhole. Below it, rows of names. Guild-masters, going back generations, each one chiseled by the hand of the one who followed.

Rosalind knelt and brushed snow from the lowest row.

*Corvin Ashe-Toll. Maren Quick. Odile Vasquez.*

Her mother's name. She traced it with a thumb and felt—nothing. Not grief, exactly. Grief needed a face, a voice, a memory to ache around. What she felt was more like standing outside a lit window on a winter night, knowing there was warmth inside, knowing it had once been hers, and being unable to recall a single detail of the room.

"I loved them," she said quietly. It wasn't the first time she'd said it. It might have been the hundredth. Saying it aloud was the only way she had left to make it true.

"I know," Sera said. No pity in it. Sera didn't do pity; she did presence. She crouched beside Rosalind and pulled the chisel and mallet from her pack. "So add to it. That's how stones like this work. They don't hold what's gone. They hold what comes next."

Dorian reached them then, breathing hard from the climb, and looked at the blank space below the old names. Then at Rosalind.

"You should do it," he said. "Your road. Your stone."

Rosalind took the chisel. It was heavier than she expected—or maybe her hands were lighter than they used to be, these days, unburdened by three years of ghosts she couldn't name.

"What do I carve?" she asked. "The guild's dead. I'm the last of it, and I don't even—" She stopped. Started again. "Whatever I write, it won't be a continuation. There's nothing left to continue."

"No," Dorian agreed. He lowered himself onto the stone beside her, careless of the cold seeping through his coat, and produced a flask of something that smelled like it could strip rust. "It'll be a beginning. Those are different things. Believe me—I spent ten years thinking I was continuing my father's practice, and it near killed me. The day I started over was the day I actually became a physician."

"Sentimental," Sera muttered, but she passed the flask to Rosalind first, which was Sera's way of agreeing.

Rosalind drank. The liquor burned all the way down and pooled warm behind the place where the coal of love-without-memory sat, and she thought: *that's as good a hearth as any.*

She set the chisel's edge against the granite below *Odile Vasquez*, and carved.

*Rosalind Vasquez.*

Then, because the stone deserved truth and not lineage:

*Dorian Ashe — who asks why.*
*Sera Ives — who chose.*

Three names. Not a dynasty. Not a guild roll. Just three people who happened to each other in the dark, and decided to keep happening.

"There," Sera said softly, when the last stroke was done. "Now the stone holds something true again."

Rosalind stepped back. The wind picked at her hair, and the light was going gold and then grey over the eastern peaks, and below them the whole of Thornfell spread out in white and shadow—every hidden road, every buried door, every secret the mountains had ever kept, now hers to give away freely.

She couldn't remember her mother's laugh. She couldn't remember Corvin's face, or Maren's voice, or the smell of the cellar safehouse where she'd grown up. The Regent had eaten all of it, and no ritual under heaven would bring it back.

But she could remember Dorian's terrible bedside manner. She could remember Sera humming off-key on night watch. She could remember the exact weight of the map in her hands the night a dying courier made her, against every instinct of her creed, responsible for something larger than herself.

Belonging rebuilt, not remembered. It wasn't the family she'd lost. It would never be. But it was a family, and it was hers, and she had chosen it with her eyes open—which, she suspected, was more than most people ever got to say.

"Move unseen," she murmured—an old habit, an old prayer, aimed at faces she could no longer see. Then, louder, turning to the others: "Owe nothing."

"Except to each other," Dorian said, and shouldered his pack.

"That part we're keeping," Sera said. "New creed. First law."

They started down the pass together, three sets of prints stitching across the fresh snow, and behind them the marker stone stood its watch with five names where there had been three, holding the door open for whoever came next.

They were halfway to the tree line when Sera stopped, head cocked, hand drifting to the sword she still wore out of habit.

"Smoke," she said. "Down-valley. And that's wrong—it's coming from the old shrine at Hollow Ford. Nobody's used that road in years."

Rosalind felt it before she understood it: the coal in her chest flaring bright, the old instinct waking like a dog that had only been sleeping. Someone was on her roads. Someone who might need them—or might mean to close them.

She smiled, and it wasn't a gentle smile.

"Well then," she said, already moving. "Let's go see who's knocking."
