## 1. The Valley That Forgets

There are three things you need to know about Hollowmere before you can understand anything else about me.

First: the valley forgets. Not the people—the land itself. Magic comes to the rim of Hollowmere the way a traveler comes to a river in flood, looks at the water, and decides to go around. Spells unravel at the ridgeline like bad knitting. Enchanted swords arrive as very expensive letter openers. Prophets who cross into the valley wake up with nothing but a headache and an inexplicable craving for turnips. The old folk say it's a blessing. The old folk say a lot of things.

Second: because magic cannot follow us down here, it also cannot find us. Which means that for two hundred years, Hollowmere has been where people come when they want to stop existing. Debtors. Deserters. Second sons with inconvenient faces. And once or twice a generation, someone like me.

Third: I have spent thirty-one years pretending to be exactly as boring as I look.

I'm told I succeed admirably.

* * *

The morning it all came apart—if you can call it coming apart when the crack had been there all along and I'd simply been standing on it—began the way my mornings always did: with a lame mare insulting my intelligence.

"Again," she said, which is what she always said, because horses are creatures of habit and this one had made a habit of doubting me.

"It's not again," I said, kneeling in the straw of Bram Hollis's back paddock with her near foreleg across my knees. "It's the same poultice, but I've changed the proportions. Comfrey, clay, and willow bark. Less clay."

"Less clay," she repeated, in the tone of a woman reading a menu she didn't believe in.

"You don't even know what clay is."

"I know what it does. It's cold and it drips." She shifted her weight, and I felt the tremor run down through her hoof into my hands—the deep ache of a tendon strained past its argument with gravity. "The tall one rides me too hard up the switchbacks. Tell him."

"The tall one is Bram Hollis, and Bram Hollis pays me four coppers a week to make his animals well, not to take riding advice from them."

"Then he pays for half a horse," she said, and turned her great dark head away with the particular dignity of the injured, which is to say: completely.

Her name was Wren, which was a stupid name for a horse and I told her so the first time we spoke, three weeks ago, when Bram brought her in favoring that leg and muttering about farriers. She'd looked at me over the stall door with an expression I can only describe as *finally*, and said, "You hear me."

Not a question. Horses don't ask questions they already know the answers to. It's one of the many ways they're smarter than people.

And I had done the thing I always do, the thing I have trained myself to do so thoroughly that it happens before thought, faster than breath: I glanced over my shoulder, checked the yard, checked the light in the house, and said, quietly, "Yes. But never again out loud where anyone can see your ears move."

Because that's the trick of it, you understand. In Hollowmere, nobody has heard a beast speak in living memory. There are no tales of beast-speakers here, no burned grandmothers, no whispered legends by the fire. The valley forgets, and what it forgets cannot be feared. So when a man stands in a paddock moving his lips at a horse, the villagers see a man talking to himself, which is sad but common, and the horse sees a man talking to her, which is rare but private. As long as I kept my voice low and my eyes elsewhere—as long as I performed the small theater of the absentminded animal-doctor—I was safe. I was invisible. I was *nothing*, which in my experience is the safest thing a person can be.

Wren lowered her head and breathed warm air against the back of my neck, which is how horses hug, if they're being honest about it.

"You're sad today," she observed.

"I'm not sad."

"You smell sad. Like wet stone."

"That's the clay."

"Mm," she said, in the tone of a woman reading a menu she didn't believe in.

* * *

I should explain what my life was, before, so you understand what I was protecting. Because from the outside it looked like almost nothing, and from the inside it was everything I had.

I lived in a low stone house at the edge of the village with my sister Maren, who baked bread for half of Hollowmere and gossiped for the other half, and who believed—because I had built the belief brick by brick over twenty years—that her brother was a decent enough animal-healer with a gift for quiet and a tragic inability to hold a conversation at market. Maren was loud where I was silent, quick where I was careful, and she loved me with the fierce, exasperated love of someone who has decided her little brother is slightly simple and must be protected from the world's sharper edges. I let her believe it. It was easier than letting her believe the truth, which would have required her to be afraid every day for the rest of her life, and Maren afraid was not a thing I could have lived inside.

Our parents were ten years gone—fever, the winter I was twenty-one—and they had known. My mother knew; mothers always know, they simply don't know what they know. She caught me once, I was nine, talking to our neighbor's dog about the dog's bad hip, and she never said a word about it, ever, but after that day she started sending me on long errands alone and telling everyone her boy liked his solitude. She built my invisibility alongside me. It was the only inheritance she had to give.

So: mornings with the animals. Afternoons grinding poultices and setting bones and lying, gently, constantly, to every farmer in the valley about how I knew their cow was carrying twins ("she carries wide"), their dog was going deaf ("he's just stubborn"), their plow-horse hated the new bit ("they're particular"). And evenings at Maren's table, eating bread, listening to her talk about Aldous Weaver's courting disaster or the price of wool, answering maybe one sentence in ten, and feeling—this is the part I've never said aloud, not to anyone, not even to the mare—

Feeling like a man warming his hands at a fire visible through a window. Present. Close. On the outside of the glass.

I loved my sister. I loved the valley. I loved the smell of bread and the sound of rain on the slate roof and the way the mist came down off the fells in October like something patient. And every single day of it cost me the same price: I could never once, not ever, be fully myself in the presence of anyone who loved me. The silence wasn't empty. It was full of everything I swallowed.

You get good at swallowing. You get so good you forget your throat was ever meant for anything else.

* * *

That afternoon, Maren found me in the yard splitting wood, which I did badly and often, mostly because swinging an axe at something is cheaper than prayer.

"Bram Hollis says his mare talked to you," she announced, arms crossed, flour on her cheekbone, wearing the expression she reserved for catching me in small crimes.

My hands did not stop moving. This is important. Twenty years of practice lives in the hands. "Bram Hollis says a lot of things. Last month he said the miller was poisoning his pigs."

"He says she follows you around the paddock like a duckling. He says she stopped limping a week early." Maren narrowed her eyes. "What do you do to these animals, Rafe?"

"What any healer does. Poultice. Rest. Patience."

"Hm." She watched me a moment longer, and I split another log with tremendous concentration, and whatever she saw—a brother, an axe, a dull and dependable man—was apparently enough, because she sighed and said, "Supper's at sundown. Aldous is coming. Try to say more than six words; he thinks you hate him."

"Aldous proposed to Widow Carrow twice last spring. He should be so lucky as my hatred."

"There! A whole sentence. See? You can do it." She went back inside, laughing, and I stood in the yard holding the axe and feeling the familiar ache settle into my chest, the one shaped exactly like a truth I couldn't set down anywhere.

Aldous came to supper. I managed eleven words, which Maren counted, which tells you everything about how carefully my family had learned to measure me without knowing why. The bread was good. The fire was warm. Through the window, out in the dark, I could hear Wren's stable-call drifting faint from Bram's place, half a mile off—not words, just the low huffing note mares use when they're content, and I found myself answering it under my breath, a sound too low for human ears, and felt, for one unguarded moment, entirely whole.

Then Maren asked me to pass the butter, and I became a boring man again, and the window glass slid back into place between me and the world.

This was my life. It was small, and it was safe, and I would have sworn to you—did swear to myself, nightly, like a litany—that it was enough.

Litany is just a word for the lie you repeat until it holds.

* * *

The stranger came at the ford two days later, but I'm getting ahead of myself, and besides, there was one more evening of the old life left, and I want to record it properly, because you don't know a thing is the last of its kind until afterward, and afterward I wanted to remember.

It rained that night. Real rain, the autumn kind, drumming on the slate and turning the lane to soup. Maren was at the Carrows' playing cards, and I sat up late with a cup of barley tea doing the accounts for my rounds, and around midnight I heard, through the rain, a sound at the gate.

Not a knock. A scramble. Claws on wet stone.

I opened the door expecting a stray dog and found instead a fox sitting on my step, soaked to the skin, looking up at me with an expression of such acute, intelligent impatience that I forgot every rule I had in a single heartbeat.

"Well?" it said. "Are you going to leave me out here all night? I've been traveling for eleven days and I have opinions about your weather."

I stood in the doorway with the rain blowing in and my tea going cold in my hand, and every instinct I owned—the ones my mother built, the ones I'd built on top of hers, the whole architecture of a hidden life—screamed at me to close the door, to tell myself I'd imagined it, to go back to bed and be boring forever.

Instead I heard myself say, "Foxes don't talk."

"And men don't stand in paddocks discussing poultice ratios with lame mares," said the fox, shaking rain off its ears with tremendous dignity, "and yet here we both are, disappointing the natural order. May I come in? There's a courier dying at the south ford, and you are going to want to be there for this, and I would rather walk than be carried."

I want to be clear that some part of me understood, even then, standing on my own threshold with a talking fox at my feet, that the door I was opening was not the one with the hinges.

But I stepped aside anyway.

The fox trotted past me into the warm, paused on the hearthstone, and looked back with eyes the color of banked coals.

"Good," it said. "My name is Lior. And Rafael Eriksen—you may want to sit down for the rest."
