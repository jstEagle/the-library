## 3. What the Fox Knew

I did not sleep.

This will surprise no one who has ever held a dead man's last words in their hands. *The Ember remembers.* Four syllables of dying man's breath, and I sat up all night with the leather tube on my kitchen table like it was a sleeping adder, which, as it turned out, was closer to the truth than I could have guessed.

The mare, Bramble, had wanted me to stay with her. She'd pressed her broad gray forehead against my chest in the dark of her stall and breathed at me the way horses do when they're trying to tell you something important and lack the words for it — a long, warm exhalation that said, roughly, *bad thing, go home, lock door.* Horses are not subtle. It's one of the things I love about them.

But I went home, because home was where the tube was, and because a man cannot hide in a stable forever no matter how much he might prefer to.

Hollowmere at night is the quietest place you will ever stand. No owls — or rather, owls, but owls that say nothing worth hearing, because nothing here says anything worth hearing. The valley eats sound the way it eats everything else strange. I used to find that peaceful. That night it felt like the silence of a house where someone has just stopped breathing.

I lit a candle. I set the tube on the table. I looked at it for a long time, the way you look at a letter from a creditor, hoping the act of staring will dissolve it.

It didn't dissolve.

The courier's blood had dried on the cap in a brown crescent. I wiped it off with my sleeve, which felt like a small desecration and also like the only decent thing left to do for him. Then I pulled the cap free.

Inside: a rolled map on vellum so fine it seemed to generate its own light, the way snow does at dusk. And under the map, a scrap of parchment with three words in a hand that shook:

*Find the speaker.*

"Well," I said to my empty kitchen, "you found him, and I'd very much like to be un-found."

The map did not answer. The map unrolled itself.

I want to be clear about what I mean. I did not unroll it. My hands were flat on the table on either side of it, nowhere near it, doing their level best to be part of the furniture. The vellum curled open across my table like a fern frond in fast-forward, and there it lay: a country I had never seen, drawn in ink the color of embers, mountains hatched in strokes like struck sparks, rivers rendered in lines that —

moved.

The rivers moved. Slowly, the way real rivers move, if you watched them from very high up and very patiently. A road crawled along a valley floor like a worm under a blanket. And in the upper corner of the map, where cartographers usually put a sea serpent or a compass rose, something was happening that made my scalp try to leave my skull entirely.

The margin was rippling.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about me before this next part, so you understand the scale of my cowardice properly: I have spoken with animals since I was six years old, and in thirty years I have never once told another human being. Not Maren. Not our father before he died. Not the priest whose sermons about abominations I sat through every restday with my hands folded and my soul folded smaller inside them. In Hollowmere, magic doesn't work — everyone knows it, everyone is quietly grateful, and everyone agrees never to wonder too loudly why. Whatever I was, the valley had smothered it down to something survivable: whispers to lame mares, gossip with crows, a lifetime of listening and never once being heard.

So when the margin of the map rippled and a fox stepped out of it onto my kitchen table, every instinct I owned screamed the same word, and the word was *no.*

The fox was red the way fire is red, which is to say it was not really any color at all but a decision light was making. Its eyes were the amber of old resin, and they were fixed on me with an expression I recognized instantly, because I'd seen it on the face of every animal I'd ever helped: hope, wearing armor made of suspicion.

"You took your time," the fox said.

Its voice was dry and precise, like pages turning. It came out of its mouth, which should not have been possible, and it came in words, which was worse, because animals talk to me in pictures and pressures and smells-that-mean-things, and here was a creature speaking the way men speak, and some deep animal part of me understood that this was the most dangerous kind of creature there is.

"No," I said, because it was the only word I had.

"Yes," said the fox. "You're the speaker. You heard the courier die. You've been talking to beasts since before you had the sense to hide it." It sat, curling its tail over its feet with the neatness of a cat and the patience of something much older. "You are Rafael Eriksen of Hollowmere, son of Tomas Eriksen, grandson of Ilse, who was granddaughter of a woman named Vess, who kept bees that told her when the king's riders were coming, and who was hanged for it in the year the covenant broke."

My mouth had gone dry enough to strike sparks. "How do you know my grandmother's grandmother's name?"

"I keep the map," said the fox. "The map keeps the names." It tilted its head. "And I know, Speaker, that you are standing four feet from the door and calculating whether you can reach it before I do."

"I'm not," I said, which was a lie, and the fox's ears flicked with something that was almost laughter.

"Animals don't lie," it said. "It's one of the reasons they'll like you. Sit down, Rafael Eriksen. What I have to tell you will take less time than running, and end better."

I sat. Cowardice, for the record, but also — and I have had thirty years to make peace with this — curiosity. It has always been my worst trait. It's the reason I talked to the crows when I was six instead of telling Mother about the voices. It's the reason I healed Bramble's leg instead of letting nature take its dull, lawful course. Every quiet catastrophe of my life began with wanting to know.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"Lior," said the fox. "I am the messenger of the Ember. I am bound to this map until the Ember is found, and I have been bound to it for two hundred and eleven years, during which I have been carried by forty-one bearers, six of whom died of natural causes, nine of whom died of unnatural causes, and twenty-six of whom turned back." Its tail-tip twitched. "You are bearer forty-two. Congratulations are apparently traditional, so: congratulations."

"The Ember," I said. "The courier said — he said it remembers. What is it?"

Lior was quiet for a moment, and in that moment the candle flame leaned toward the fox, the way flames lean toward things that are more interesting than candles.

"A fire," Lior said at last. "The first fire. Before your valley was dead, before the thrones, before the witch-hunters and their ash-colored cloaks, there was a fire that lived, and it made a bargain with the speaking-kind — the people who could hear beasts. The covenant. The speakers kept the Ember's secrets; the Ember kept the speakers' gifts alive. Every beast-speaker in every generation was a thread of that old weaving." Its amber eyes did not blink. Foxes blink. This was not blinking. "Then the kings decided a fire that listened to peasants was a fire that could not be trusted, and they tried to own it, and when it would not be owned, they tried to kill it, and when it could not be killed, they killed everyone who could hear it instead. Your gift, Speaker, is not an accident. It is an heirloom. You are the last thread."

The candle guttered. Outside, the valley held its breath, the way it always did, the way I had always thought of as peace.

"And Hollowmere?" I asked. My voice came out strange. "Magic doesn't work here. Everyone knows it. It's why —"

"It's why you're alive," Lior said. "Yes. I know. You think your valley's dead magic protected you." The fox rose, paced once around the map, and looked up at me with something that in a human face I would have called pity, and in a fox's face was worse. "It never protected you, Rafael. It hid you. There is a difference, and the difference is that protection keeps the wolf out, and hiding keeps you from ever knowing the wolf exists. Your valley is not a shield. It is a blindfold. Someone wove it — long ago, deliberately, over the whole hollow — so that the last threads of the covenant could grow up unseen. Unfound. Unburned." It paused. "The blindfold worked. For two hundred years, it worked. And then a burned man staggered into your ford carrying a map, and the blindfold tore, and now everything that hunts the Ember knows exactly where the last thread has been hiding."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My legs simply informed me we were done sitting.

"That's not possible," I said. "Nothing gets into the valley. Nothing strange. The mist on the ridgeline turns travelers around. We haven't had a stranger in — "

"In how long?" said Lior softly. "Think, Speaker. Think carefully. When was the last time someone new came to Hollowmere and stayed?"

I opened my mouth to answer, and found, to my horror, that I couldn't.

Not the peddler — the peddler came twice a year, but he was born in the next valley over, and everyone knew his mother. Not the surveyor, seven years back — he'd stayed one night and left complaining of headaches. Not the pilgrims, who never came at all, though the shrine road passed within a day's walk. I stood in my kitchen and counted backward through my whole life and found that the list of outsiders who had ever *stayed* was empty, and that I had never once wondered why, and that the not-wondering was the deepest magic of all.

"Oh," I said.

"Yes," said Lior.

"The mist. The headaches. The — we thought it was the valley. We thought it was just how the world was."

"It is how the world was *made*, for you. By hands that loved you, probably. Or feared for you. The two look similar from inside." The fox stepped delicately over the moving rivers of the map, leaving no prints, and sat again facing me. "And now the courier is dead on your ford, and the Ashguard's hunters are asking questions in villages within three days' ride, and the blindfold has a hole in it shaped exactly like you."

I looked at the map. The ember-ink roads crawled. Somewhere on it, I understood without being told, a fire waited in a high place, older than every throne, and every power in the world was already turning toward it like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"I'm a horse-doctor," I said. It sounded absurd even to me, and I heard the absurdity and hated it. "I pull foals. I set legs. I talk to cows about weather. I am not — whatever this is. Bearer forty-two. I'm not brave. I've spent my whole life making sure no one ever had a reason to look at me twice."

"Yes," said Lior. "That is precisely why it has to be you."

"What?"

"Forty-one bearers," the fox said. "Knights who wanted glory. Scholars who wanted knowledge. Priests who wanted God to notice them. Queens' agents who wanted leverage. Every one of them looked at the Ember and saw a thing they could use, or a thing they could give away, or a thing they could become. And every one of them failed — turned back, or died, or worse, tried to *keep* it." Its voice dropped, and the candle flame dropped with it. "The Ember does not need a brave bearer, Rafael. Bravery is common as rain. It needs someone who has spent a lifetime understanding that the safest place for a wild thing is not a cage. You have hidden your whole life. Now you must decide what hiding was *for*."

I sat back down. The chair complained. So did I, inwardly, comprehensively, in a language no animal needed to translate.

Outside, far off toward the ford, a dog began to bark — one of the miller's, a big stupid affectionate brute named Turnip who barked at moths and his own reflection. But this bark was different. Short. Alarmed. Aimed at something coming up the valley road in the dark.

Lior's ears swiveled toward the sound, and then, slowly, back to me.

"They're early," the fox said. "They usually give the grief three days. This one gave you one night." It stepped backward onto the map, and its paws sank into the ink like it was stepping into shallow water. "Decide quickly, Speaker. Hide again, and let them find you in a hole — or come out, and make them regret the looking."

The dog fell silent mid-bark, which was somehow worse than the barking.

I blew out the candle, because it seemed suddenly important that the darkness be mine by choice.

"All right," I said to the fox in the dark, and my voice only shook a little, which I count among the great victories of my life. "Tell me about the Ember."

Lior smiled. I could hear it.

"First," it said, "tell me what the dog saw."
