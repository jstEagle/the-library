## 4. Witch-Hunters Come to Hollowmere

The valley didn't want us.

I don't mean that in the poetic way the chaplains back at Ashkeep liked to put it—*the land itself resists corruption*, etc., usually right before they burned someone. I mean it practically. Hollowmere sat in its bowl of hills like a stone in a boot, and every road into it had been allowed to go bad on purpose, as if the valley had looked at the world's maps and asked, politely, to be left off them.

My horse disagreed with this arrangement most of all. She picked her way down the last mile of switchback with the expression of a duchess being led through a sewer, and I couldn't blame her. The air was wrong here. Not foul—wrong. Empty. Like a room where someone had just stopped playing music and you kept waiting for the note that never came.

"Cheer up," I told her. "We'll be gone by dark."

She flicked an ear at me, which from her was a full speech.

Behind me, my four riders came down the trail in their gray-and-ash cloaks, and I watched the village notice us before we'd even reached the palisade gate. That's the thing about witch-hunters: you never have to announce yourselves. Children stop playing. Dogs go quiet or go mad, depending on their temperaments. A woman pulling water from a well stood very still with the rope in both hands, watching us the way you watch weather.

Good. Fear saved time.

* * *

The courier had come through here—I was sure of it. Three days dead ahead of us, riding something lamed and desperate, bleeding through his bandages where our fire had kissed him at the ford upstream. Burned men leave traces. Not the kind you think—not blood, not cloth. They leave *fear*. Horses shy at fences they've ignored for years. Farmers find their dogs barking at empty road. A whole valley catches a fever of looking over its shoulder, and if you know how to read it, you can follow a man across a country by the wake of his terror alone.

Hollowmere's wake was strange, though. I'd been doing this work for nine years, since I was nineteen and still believed the Ashguard's motto meant something, and I had learned that frightened places all smell alike. This one didn't. There was fear in it, yes—the water-drawer, the children, the dogs—but it was old fear, settled fear, the kind that has made its peace with living indoors. Nobody here had looked over their shoulder recently.

Which meant either the courier hadn't come through—

—or he had, and no one had seen him, which was worse.

The reeve met us at the gate. A broad, sunburned man with the careful blankness of a peasant who has learned exactly how much intelligence is safe to display to armed strangers. "Riders," he said, as though we might be anything else. "Welcome to Hollowmere. What brings the Crown's service so far from the King's roads?"

Not *the King*. Interesting. Veylan had worn the crown for six years now and half the borderlands still said *the Crown* the way you'd say *the weather*—something that happened to you, not something you chose.

"A courier," I said. "Burned. Riding hard. He would have passed within the last three days."

The reeve's face did nothing at all, which was itself information. "We see few couriers. The post-road runs north of the ridge."

"The post-road runs north of the ridge because your grandfathers paid good silver to make it do so," I said pleasantly. "I've read the old charters. Your valley bought its privacy. I'm not here to end the arrangement. I'm here for one man, and then you'll never see me again."

That was a lie, and he probably knew it was a lie, and we both let it stand there between us like a vase neither of us wanted to be holding when it broke.

"You can stable your horses at the Wether's Rest," he said finally. "There's stew, such as it is."

"There always is," I said.

* * *

I ran the interviews myself. My riders were good men—good enough, anyway; in the Ashguard *good* is a relative term, like *dry* in a flood—and I trusted three of them to knock doors without breaking them. But questioning is a craft, and I did not delegate craft.

The innkeeper first. A widow, hands like tree roots, eyes that went to my cloak's ash-gray hem and away again quickly. "A burned man? No, ser knight." Everyone assumed the rank; I'd stopped correcting people years ago, mostly because the correction—"it's *sera*, actually"—started conversations I didn't want. "Though Tam Wilder's dog's been strange lately."

"Strange how?"

"Barking at the ford road. Nothing there." She shrugged. "Dogs are strange."

*Dogs are strange.* Four words that had ended a hundred trails, and might have ended this one, except that she said them too smoothly, like a line rehearsed in front of a mirror.

Then the miller, who told me about his grain. Then the smith, who told me about his iron. Then a shepherd who told me, at length and with genuine passion, about his sheep, and I began to understand that Hollowmere's grandfathers hadn't just bought privacy—they had built a culture of it, a whole civic architecture of boring. Every answer was true and useless. Every face was open and empty. It was like interrogating a field.

By late afternoon I had a list of what I *didn't* have: no burned man, no strange horses, no strangers at all in living memory, amen. My riders were getting that restless look, the one that ended with someone's barn on fire, and I was drafting the report in my head—*nothing found, valley clean, recommend continued observation*—when I saw him.

He came out of the far pasture leading a mare.

I noticed the mare before the man, because that's my trade. Lame near-hind, favoring it carefully, but not freshly lame—weeks lame, maybe more. And walking beside her, not behind, not ahead, *beside*, the way you walk with a person. He had a hand on her neck and his head bent toward her ear, and he was talking to her.

People talk to horses. I know that. Half the kingdom talks to its horses, its cows, its stubborn mules, and none of it means anything. But there's talking and there's *talking*, and this was the second kind. His lips moved. Her ears moved. And then—this is the part I keep coming back to, the part I have turned over every night since like a stone looking for what's underneath—she *answered*. Not a whicker, not a toss of the head. She stopped, turned her head toward him, and held it there, and whatever passed between them had the unmistakable texture of conversation. Two parties. Taking turns.

Then he saw us.

And here is the thing I wrote in my report later, in the careful coded hand we used for observations that would get a man laughed out of the order if anyone else read them: *Subject observed. Subject's animals exhibit anomalous responsiveness. Subject's demeanor under observation anomalous.*

Because he didn't flinch.

Everyone flinches. Peasants, merchants, priests, other knights—it doesn't matter. When five ash-cloaked riders materialize at the edge of your afternoon, some animal part of you jumps, and the jump shows, in the shoulders, in the step, in the way the hands suddenly need somewhere to be. Nine years of this work and I could count on one hand the people who hadn't flinched, and two of them had been other Ashguard and one had been dead already.

This man—tall, brown-haired, ordinary as bread—looked at us the way you look at rain. *Ah. That.* He finished whatever he was saying to the mare, patted her neck once, and only then turned to cross the yard toward the inn, unhurried, passing within ten feet of me.

"Evening," he said.

"Evening," I said. "Your mare's lame."

"She's healing," he said. "Slowly. She'll be sound by spring."

"You're treating her yourself?"

"I do what I can." And he smiled—a real smile, easy, unguarded—and walked into the inn, and I stood in the yard of a magic-dead valley feeling the hair rise on my arms like a cat's.

* * *

Supper at the Wether's Rest was stew, such as it was. I took the table by the door, because I always take the table by the door, and I put the beast-man's table in my sightline and watched him eat with his sister—a sharp-faced girl with ink-stained fingers, a clerk or a schoolmistress or something of the kind—and an older couple I took for their parents. A family. A whole soft, ordinary family, arranged around a table like candles around a shrine.

He laughed at something his sister said. He passed the bread. He refilled his father's cup without being asked. If I had seen him in any market in the kingdom I'd have forgotten him inside a minute.

But the mare. And the eyes.

It wasn't steadiness, exactly—that's what I wrote at first and then struck through. Steadiness is a soldier's virtue; I know it when I see it, and this wasn't it. What he had was more unsettling than courage. He looked at me the way the *animals* had looked at me all day—with attention, with assessment, with a complete absence of the awe that my cloak was designed to produce. As though the ash-gray and the badges and the reputation were things that applied to other people.

As though he had decided what I was, filed me away, and found me uninteresting.

Nobody finds me uninteresting. I have worked very hard to ensure that nobody ever finds me uninteresting.

"So," said Brannic, my second, leaning close over the stew. "Nothing here. Waste of a week?"

"Maybe."

"You felt it too, then." Brannic had been with me six years and could read my silences better than most men read proclamations. "The air. Like a cellar with the door shut."

"It's a dead valley. Dead valleys feel like cellars."

"This one feels *kept*," he said. "Like something's being stored."

I didn't answer, because the beast-man had gotten up to fetch more water for the table, and as he passed behind my chair he paused—just a half-step, barely a hesitation—and I felt him look at the sword hilt showing over my shoulder. Not with fear. With recognition. The way you look at a tool whose use you understand.

Then he moved on, and I ate my stew, and I told myself I was chasing shadows because the alternative was admitting that a burned courier carrying the most dangerous object in the world had vanished somewhere inside ten miles of this valley, and that the only anomaly in fifty households was a horse-doctor with steady eyes.

* * *

I interviewed him last. Of course I did. You save the interesting ones for last, when they've had all day to watch how you treat everyone else, and you've had all day to watch them watch.

He was splitting wood behind his house when I came around at dusk. Good wood-splitting, economical, no wasted motion. The lame mare watched from a paddock with the alertness of a sentry, and I noted that too.

"Ser knight," he said, laying the axe down before I'd said a word. "You're the one asking after the burned man."

"You've heard."

"Half the village heard by noon. We're a small place. News travels fast because there isn't much of it." He wiped his palms on his thighs. "I haven't seen him. I'd remember. A burned man on the roads here would be remembered by everyone."

"Would he? Your valley keeps to itself."

"We keep to ourselves, yes. But keeping to ourselves means noticing everything that isn't us. It's the whole hobby." That smile again, self-deprecating, disarming. "Ask anyone."

"I have. Ask anyone is exactly what I've been doing." I let the silence sit. Most people fill silences. They pour words into them like water into cracks, and you just wait for the level to rise above the lip. He didn't. He waited with me, comfortable, patient, and I thought: *this man has been silent his whole life. Silence doesn't frighten him. Silence is his native country.*

"What's your name?" I asked, though I'd had it since noon. Rafael Eriksen. Beast-keeper, horse-doctor, son of Aldous Eriksen, brother to Maren the clerk. Twenty-six years old. Never traveled farther than the ridge towns. Never married. Never in trouble. A life so quiet it was nearly a form of camouflage.

"Rafael. Rafael Eriksen."

"And you heal animals."

"I try. Mostly I keep them comfortable and wait. Time does most of the work; I just keep the work honest." He glanced at the paddock. "That mare came to me with a ruined tendon. The farrier said put her down. I said give her a season."

"And?"

"And spring will tell." He looked back at me, and for a moment—half a moment, less—the pleasantness dropped away, not into hostility but into something quieter and much more dangerous. Curiosity. He was studying *me*. The way I studied suspects. "Why does it matter to you? The mare."

"It doesn't. The courier matters."

"He matters to the Crown."

"He matters to the Crown," I agreed.

"But not to you." It wasn't a question, and it wasn't insolent, and I genuinely could not have told you why it landed the way it did—like a key turning in a lock I hadn't known was on my chest. "You ask about him like a woman doing sums she doesn't like. Forgive me, sera"—he'd caught the rank after all, or guessed it—"but you don't burn with the others. I've been watching you all day. You knock instead of kick. You thank the innkeeper. Whatever you're hunting, you hunt it like it grieves you."

For a moment I forgot to be the Inquisition's instrument. For a moment I was just a tired woman standing in a stranger's yard at dusk, wanting to say: *you're right, it grieves me, everything about this work grieves me, and I do it anyway because the alternative is worse, and I don't expect a horse-doctor in a hidden valley to understand the arithmetic of that.*

What I said was: "Watch fewer people, Eriksen. It's unhealthy."

"Probably," he said, and smiled, and the shutter came down again, and he was bread again, ordinary as bread.

* * *

I rode the perimeter that night. Alone, which broke protocol, and I didn't care. Moonrise over the eastern ridge, the valley below me a scatter of hearth-light and shadow, and everywhere that wrongness in the air—the held breath, the shut door, the music that had stopped.

Brannic was right. It felt *kept*.

I sat my horse on the high pasture and tried to assemble the day into sense. A burned courier, dying, carrying a map to the Ember—the Ember, the living flame, the thing the whole rotten war was secretly about beneath its talk of succession and legitimacy—had come south along the ford road. Had entered this valley, or had meant to. And then had simply ceased to exist. No wake of fear. No trace. As if the valley had swallowed him, or as if—

Or as if he'd found what he was looking for.

I thought about the mare answering him. I thought about the dogs that hadn't barked at us until we were inside the gates, closer than dogs should allow. I thought about a valley that had paid silver, generations ago, to be erased from the maps, and wondered what its founders had actually been buying. Privacy, the charters said. But charters are written by the survivors, and survivors lie.

Below me, one light still burned in the Eriksen house. Late. Past midnight. I watched it, and told myself I was watching it because a competent officer checks every thread twice.

The truth was simpler and I knew it. I was watching because for one moment in that yard, a stranger had looked at me—at the cloak, the sword, the nine years, the arithmetic of grief I carry like a second spine—and had *seen* it. Not feared it. Not respected it. Seen it. And I had spent nine years making sure no one could do that, and I did not know yet whether I wanted to arrest him for it or thank him.

The light went out.

In the morning we would ride the ford road one last time, and then I would write my report, and the report would say what reports say, and the Ashguard would file it, and Hollowmere would go back to being erased.

I gave the order to mount at dawn.

And if my eye went, one more time, to the pasture where a lame mare stood beside a man who talked to her like a person—if I marked those too-steady eyes one final time, the way you mark a coin you suspect is gold but can't prove—I told myself that was diligence.

It was diligence.

It was also the last morning either of us would ever be invisible, though neither of us knew it yet. Somewhere south, a burned man's map lay folded in a leather tube, and a fox made of margins was already awake, and the hunt I thought I was conducting was, in truth, conducting me.
