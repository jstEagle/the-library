## 5. Leaving

There is a particular smell to a lie told to family. It's not the smoke of the fire you're burning, though Hollowmere's hearths have plenty of that. It's something older, sweeter, like apples rotting in the cellar of your own chest. I stood in my father's study with an armful of his ledgers and thought: *this is what betrayal smells like.* Then I thought: *no, this is what it smells like when you feed a family's history to a stove to keep a fox-spirit from being found,* and decided both were true, and that truth was getting crowded.

The ledgers went back four generations. Eriksen farriers, Eriksen shepherds, Eriksen beekeepers who kept meticulous notes on which hives swarmed and which sulked. My grandfather had recorded rainfall for thirty years with the devotion of a man who trusted nothing he couldn't measure. None of it mentioned magic. That was the joke, really — we were so careful never to write down anything worth hiding that there was nothing here worth finding. Except me. Except the map now rolled up in my coat like a sleeping snake.

Lior sat on my father's desk, tail curled around its own feet, watching the fire with the expression of a creature who has seen many fires and been impressed by none.

"You're hesitating," it said. Foxes don't have eyebrows, but Lior managed anyway.

"I'm grieving," I said. "It takes time."

"You've been grieving for two days. The witch-hunters have been grieving for two days too, in their way. They grieve by asking questions at the mill."

"They asked questions at the mill because the miller talks. Everyone knows the miller talks."

"Everyone knows," Lior agreed, "which is why the knight with the steady hands bought flour she didn't need and listened for forty minutes." It hopped down, light as a dropped glove. "Burn the papers, Rafael. Grief keeps. Fear doesn't."

I fed my grandfather's rainfall records into the stove one page at a time, because feeding them in all at once felt like murder and one page at a time felt merely like cremation. Thirty years of weather curled and blackened. Somewhere in those pages was a note about the summer the bees refused to swarm, and how my grandmother had hummed to them through the fence, and how they'd settled. Nobody had ever wondered why the bees liked her humming. In Hollowmere, nobody wonders about anything twice. Wondering twice is how you find out your valley isn't quiet — it's deafened.

That was the thing I'd learned from the map and the fox and the dying man at the ford: the valley didn't protect us from magic. It hid us *from* it. A pocket sewn shut. And pockets get turned out eventually.

* * *

Maren found me in the barn at dusk, mucking stalls I no longer needed to muck, because a clean stall is what I do instead of screaming.

"You're doing Bramble's stall," she said. "Bramble died in spring."

"Habit."

"You've been full of habits lately. Burning-paper habits. Whispering-to-yourself habits. Ma says you talk to the horses more than usual, and Ma notices everything except the things right in front of her."

I leaned on the pitchfork. Maren had our mother's square jaw and our father's talent for standing in doorways looking like the last honest person in the world. She was twenty-three and ran the household better than I ever had, mostly by refusing to be lied to, which made the next five minutes the hardest work of my life.

"I'm going away for a while," I said.

Silence. The kind with weight.

"Away where?"

"West. There's a horse breeder near Thornwick who wants someone good with lame animals. It's good money, Rafe money. Real coin, not barter."

"You hate Thornwick. You said the people there have 'faces like closed doors.'"

"People change."

"Not you. You've lived your whole life in a valley that doesn't change, and you like it that way, and don't tell me otherwise because I've watched you turn down three offers of work beyond the pass and invent a different bad reason each time." She crossed her arms. "What's wrong?"

Here is what I should have said: *Someone died at the ford. He gave me a map that moves and a fox came out of it and there are soldiers hunting both, and if they find me they'll burn everything I love starting with the parts that talk.* Here is what I actually said:

"The pay's triple."

"The pay," Maren repeated, "is triple."

"And I'm tired of being the second-best farrier in a valley with one farrier."

She stared at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide — visibly, the way she decided whether to believe Da about the drinking, whether to sell the gray mare, whether to forgive me for missing harvest — that her brother was being an idiot rather than a stranger. Idiots she could handle. Strangers frightened her.

"When?" she said finally.

"Tomorrow night. Before dawn. I'll take the pack pony."

"Bramble's dead."

"The other pony, then."

"Coriander."

"I wasn't going to say Bramble."

"You were absolutely going to say Bramble." But some of the iron went out of her voice, and she stepped forward and did the thing she does instead of crying, which is fix my collar, hard, like she's punishing it. "You'll write."

"I'll write."

"You'll write *words*, Rafael, not one sentence and a drawing of a horse."

"No promises on the horse."

She laughed, one short wet note, and left, and I stood in the dark barn with my heart going like a hammer on a stuck gate, because I had just lied to the only person whose opinion of me I'd ever cared about keeping clean. Coriander, in her stall, snorted at me. Horses are honest the way weather is honest. *Liar,* she said, without words, the way she said everything. *Liar, liar, liar.*

"Yes," I told her. "But a useful one."

* * *

We left at moonrise, or close enough — the moon was a fingernail paring over the eastern ridge, which Lior informed me was ideal, since witch-hunters, like most predators, hunt best under fuller light. I took almost nothing. Spare shirt, flint, the knife Da gave me at fourteen, dried apples Maren had packed while believing every word of my lie, which somehow made them heavier than lead. The map rode against my ribs in its leather tube. The courier's blood was still on the outside of it. I hadn't cleaned it off. It seemed important not to.

At the edge of the yard I stopped and looked back once, which every story I've ever heard says is a mistake, so naturally I did it.

The house was dark except for the kitchen window, where a candle still burned. Maren always left a candle for late walkers. It had burned for my father on the nights he came home drunk from Bellharbor road; it had burned for me every night of my life, probably, and I'd never once noticed until now, standing in the cold with a spirit-fox at my heel and a stolen destiny in my coat. That candle was the whole valley in miniature: small, stubborn, lit for people who might never come back.

"Rafael," Lior said quietly. "The candle will gutter by morning either way."

"That's not why I'm looking."

"No. But it helps to know that."

I turned west and walked, and Coriander followed with the pack, and the ford came up on us faster than roads do when you're dreading them. The water was low and loud. On the far bank, the world stopped being shaped like home.

Here is the arithmetic I did while crossing, boots wet, breath smoking: somewhere past that ridge were men who could do things with fire that fire shouldn't do, and a knight with steady hands, and a queen-in-exile I hadn't met yet and already distrusted on principle, and a usurper with a treasury and a grudge. And somewhere behind me was a candle in a window and a sister who thought I was chasing triple pay, and a lame mare I'd been secretly healing who would now get worse before anyone understood why, and a valley full of people who would learn within the week that Rafael Eriksen had lied to them all, and would never know it was the kindest thing I ever did.

Grief and exhilaration, I discovered, are not opposites. They're the same animal wearing two coats. My chest was a stable at feeding time — everything shoving, everything hungry, nothing sure which trough was its.

On the far bank, Lior shook dew from its fur and looked up at me with eyes like struck coins.

"For what it's worth," the fox said, "every bearer before you hesitated at their own threshold. It's practically tradition."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to inform you. Comfort is above my station." It trotted three steps toward the dark tree line, then paused. "Also, the knight with the steady hands has reached the miller. We have perhaps an hour."

The exhilaration won, briefly. Then grief took its turn. Then I whistled Coriander forward, and we went up into the hills together, three liars and a candle behind us, toward whatever the world outside the pocket had been saving all these years.

Behind us, faint as a held breath, the valley closed its silence over the place where we'd been — and ahead, down in the black folds of the foothills, something answered a whistle I hadn't made yet.

A dog, maybe. Or something wearing a dog's shape, badly.

Lior's ears went flat. "Walk faster," it said, "and ask fewer questions."

For once, I intended to obey.
