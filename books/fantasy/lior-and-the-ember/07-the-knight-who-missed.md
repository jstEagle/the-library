## 7. The Knight Who Missed

I had killed eleven people before I ever missed one on purpose.

That is not a confession. In the Ashguard it is a résumé. We do not call them people, of course—we call them *marks*, or *assets of concern*, or, in the older ledgers that Dray still keeps in his own hand, *embers to be snuffed*. The vocabulary does the killing for you, half the time. It is very hard to murder a man. It is remarkably easy to extinguish an asset.

Rafael Eriksen was listed as an asset. The courier's trail had gone cold at the ford outside Hollowmere, and the trail had picked up again three days east, moving slow, moving badly, moving like a man who had never in his life slept outdoors and was determined to make up for it all at once. A beast-speaker with no training, no escort, no plan—just a leather tube and a fox that wasn't a fox, if my informants were to be believed, and my informants were usually dead by the time I checked their work.

I tracked him for six days. I want that understood: six days of cold river water, wet wool, and eating things I pretended were food, because the Ashguard pays for excellence and executes mediocrity, and I had decided long ago which of those two fates suited my complexion better.

On the sixth day I caught him at the crossing of the River Sennet, where the old bridge had rotted down to its ribs and travelers waded at the gravel shallows, and I learned something about myself that I have spent every day since trying to unlearn.

But let me do this properly. Let me tell it the way it happened, not the way I wish it had.

* * *

The morning of the crossing, the mist came off the Sennet like breath off a sleeping animal, and Rafael Eriksen stood in the shallows arguing with a heron.

I want to be precise here, because precision is the last professional habit I have left. He was not talking *near* the heron. He was not doing what madmen and lonely shepherds do, which is narrate their lives to anything with feathers. He was standing hip-deep in freezing water, map tube slung across his back, having what was unmistakably a negotiation. The heron stood on a stone at his elbow with the particular insolence herons reserve for the world, and when it tilted its head, he tilted his back, and when it made its dry rattling croak, he laughed—a short, startled laugh, the laugh of a man who has just lost an argument to a bird and knows it.

And then the heron looked at me.

I was flat against the far bank under a deadfall, sixty yards off, downwind, motionless, wrapped in gray wool in gray mist. I have lain in ambush for kings' assassins without being marked. I once waited out an entire church service behind an altar screen while the mark confessed forty feet away.

The heron turned its head, fixed one yellow eye on the exact spot where I lay, and screamed.

Not the fishing cry. The other one—the ragged, clattering alarm they raise when a fox comes into the reeds. And every bird along the bank took it up, and somewhere upstream a deer crashed away through brush that had been silent a moment before, and Rafael Eriksen went still in the water in a way that told me he understood exactly what the birds were saying.

He didn't run. That was the first strange thing. A guilty man runs. He turned slowly, scanning the bank, and said—not loudly, not to me, but clearly—"It's all right. She's just tired."

*I'm* tired? I thought, absurdly. I had slept four hours in three days. But that wasn't what stopped my heart. What stopped it was that he said *she*. Not "it." Not "that bird." *She.*

Protocol said kill him now, while he stood blind in open water with his weapon still strapped to a pack he hadn't opened. Protocol was very clear about beast-speakers. Beast-speakers were the reason Veylan held the throne and the reason he couldn't sleep in it. A beast-speaker who could read the land through ten thousand eyes was worth a legion; a beast-speaker loyal to the old covenant was worth a pyre. My orders, written in Dray's cramped iron hand, ran to one line: *Recover the map. Extinguish the bearer.*

So I did what I was trained to do. I rose out of the mist with my sword already clearing its sheath, closed the distance while he was still turning, and put myself between him and the far bank so there would be nowhere to go but the deep channel.

"Rafael Eriksen," I said. "By order of the Crown Regent, you are extinguished."

Yes. I said it exactly like that. Eleven kills, and I still sounded like a tax notice. There is a version of this story where I say something elegant, and I want you to know that version is a lie I tell at taverns.

He looked at me—at the sword, at the Ashguard gray, at the ash-leaf badge on my collar—and instead of terror, his face did something worse. It went *sad*. The way you look at a lame horse the vet is coming for.

"You're the one the heron saw," he said. "You've been following us since the willows. You should eat more. And sleep. Your hands shake."

"My hands," I said, "do not shake."

They shook. Slightly. From exhaustion, I told myself. From exhaustion alone.

Behind him, the fox sat down on a stone at the water's edge—an ordinary red fox, except that it sat like a magistrate, and it watched me with an expression I can only describe as *disappointed*, and I am a grown woman and a knight of the Ashguard and that fox made me feel like a novice caught stealing from the tithing box.

"Last chance," I said, because protocol required the offer even though we both knew the offer was scenery. "Give me the map and walk into the river."

"That's not a last chance," he said. "That's just a sentence with a question mark missing."

I lunged.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the next four seconds, and I have reviewed these four seconds more times than I have reviewed any battle of my career, including the one where I got the scar.

I remember the water exploding around him as he twisted away—clumsy, graceless, a farm boy's flailing, nothing a swordsman need respect. I remember my blade coming around on the clean line I have cut a thousand times, the line that opens the throat and ends the asset, and I remember the heron hitting me.

A heron weighs perhaps five pounds. It has hollow bones. It is not, by any measure known to warfare, a weapon.

It came off that stone like a thrown spear, wings cracking, and hit me across the eyes and helm-rim, and I staggered—one step, half a second—and in that half second the entire river changed sides.

I don't know how else to say it. The water itself seemed to thicken around my legs, dragging at me like hands, while it carried him—buoyed him, shoved him toward the shallows like a mother cat moving a kitten. Pike, I think—I saw later, three of them, big ones—held the deep channel in a way pike do not hold anything, arrayed across it like a picket line. The horses of the drovers camped upstream began screaming and breaking their tethers, though nothing had touched them. And the birds—the birds came off that bank in a shrieking gray storm, dive after dive, not hurting me, never drawing blood, simply *refusing to let me see*, and every one of them aimed its fury with a precision that meant only one thing.

Someone was telling them where to go.

I fought through it. Of course I fought through it; I am very good at fighting through things, it is most of what I am. I cleared the birds, I bulled through the water, and I found him again at the gravel bar, soaked, gasping, the map tube clutched to his chest like a child, and I had him—truly had him, sword point at his chest, the kill a matter of six inches and no argument.

And the animals went quiet.

All of them. At once. The heron landed on the bar between us and folded itself and stood there like a witness. The pike slid away. Even the wind dropped. The whole river held its breath, and Rafael Eriksen looked down the length of my blade and did not beg, and did not run, and did not do any of the eleven things I had watched eleven other people do in that position.

He said, quietly, "You could say no."

"What?"

"To whoever taught you that word. *Extinguished.* Somebody gave you that word and you carry it around like it's yours." His voice cracked on the last part—he was terrified, I realized; he was simply refusing to let the terror vote. "You're shaking again."

"I have a condition," I said.

"It's called being asked to do something wrong."

Six inches. Six inches of steel, and my arm—the arm that has never once failed an order, the arm they built me on, the arm Dray used to call his best pen—would not close the distance. I stood there in the shallows of the Sennet with death balanced on my fingertips and felt it turn to lead, and I want to tell you I thought of some great moral principle, some crisis of conscience worthy of a ballad.

What I actually thought was: *the mare.* Which makes no sense, because I'd never seen his mare, didn't yet know he had one, wouldn't hear about her until weeks later. But the look on his face was the look I had seen once, years ago, on a child in a burning village in the purge years, holding a scalded cat and looking up at my armor as if armor might mean help. I had been too late for the child. I had been exactly on time for everyone else.

My sword arm lowered. Not dropped—lowered, controlled, deliberate, because whatever else I was, I was going to be precise about my treason.

"On your knees," I said. "Hands behind your head. You're under arrest."

"I thought I was extinguished."

"The paperwork," I said, "has been delayed."

* * *

You cannot take a prisoner across open country alone without becoming a story, so I became one. I bound his wrists in front—kindness disguised as procedure, procedure disguised as kindness; in the Ashguard we are fluent in both directions—and I marched him north along the Sennet toward the garrison at Corven's Mill, where Dray's writ ran thick and where I could hand over the map and wash my hands of the whole affair and stop hearing herons in my sleep.

He talked the entire way. That is not an exaggeration. I have escorted silent fanatics, weeping mothers, and one archbishop who sang. Eriksen *talked*. To me, mostly, which was bad enough—but also to the fox, who trotted along at the end of no leash whatsoever and answered him in little barking coughs that I gradually, horribly, began to suspect were actual answers.

"You understand it," I said on the second day. "The fox."

"He understands me," Rafael said. "Understanding goes both ways or it isn't understanding. That's sort of the whole point."

"And what does he say?"

"He says you haven't eaten properly since the willows and your left boot is rubbing a blister and you check the treeline every forty seconds even though you're exhausted, and he thinks that last one is sad." The fox made its coughing sound. "He also says he could take your sword belt while you slept, but he won't, because I asked him not to, and because he thinks you're interesting."

"Interesting," I repeated.

"In the way a trap is interesting," Rafael said, "if you're the kind of creature that's been caught in a lot of them."

I should have gagged him. Instead I gave him half my bread, which I told myself was prisoner management, keeping the asset healthy for delivery. The fox watched me do it with that magistrate expression, and I swear on my commission—which, mind you, I still possessed at that hour—that the animal nodded.

Nights were worse than days. Nights, tied to the same tree as me for security, he would ask questions. Not clever interrogator questions. Worse ones. Personal ones.

"Why does the Ashguard burn speakers?" he asked the first night, when the fire had burned down to that orange hour when everything true comes loose.

"Because a speaker can raise the beasts against any army. Because the old covenant put power in the hands of—"

"—of whoever happened to be born listening," he finished. "Yes. Lior told me. And your answer to that is to kill the listeners."

"Our answer is order. Without it, you get warlords with dragon-fangs and plague-witches and—" I heard my own voice climbing and made myself stop. "You wouldn't understand. You grew up in a valley where none of it touches you."

Something crossed his face at that—something hurt and complicated—and he said, softly, "Is that what you think Hollowmere is?" and I did not answer, and the fire crackled, and the fox looked at me with its chin on its paws like a judge waiting for a plea.

The truth—the thing I did not say, lying awake with my back to him, counting his breathing because it was steadier than mine—is that I had begun to doubt the arithmetic of my whole life. Eleven extinguishings. I had done the first at nineteen, a speaker-girl of maybe thirty, and Dray had put his hand on my shoulder afterward and said, *You've kept the realm safe tonight,* and I had believed him, because believing him was warmer than the alternative, and I had gone on believing him through ten more, each easier, each colder, until the words *asset* and *ember* fit in my mouth without tasting of anything at all.

And then a heron had flown into my face for a man who fed his bread ration to his prisoner, and the arithmetic had come apart in my hands like wet paper.

* * *

On the third day, an hour out of Corven's Mill, I found the sign.

Two riders had passed on the mill road within the last day—Ashguard remounts, shod heavy, moving fast. And tied to the milestone at the crossroads, in Dray's cramped iron hand, a notice:

*To all officers of the Ashguard. Knight-Sera Vane is relieved of command pending inquiry. She is to be detained on sight regarding irregularities in the Eriksen recovery. —Inquisitor Dray*

Irregularities. Someone had reported the ford. A fisherman, a drover, one of the birds themselves for all I knew—someone had seen the knight of the Ashguard stand six inches from her mark and lower her sword, and had sent the word ahead, and Dray, who misses nothing and forgives less, had already moved.

I stood at that milestone a long time. Behind me, Rafael shifted his bound hands.

"That's your name," he said. "Sera."

"Don't wear it out."

"'Pending inquiry.' Is that bad? That sounds bad."

"It means," I said, "that in about an hour, every officer between here and the coast will be hunting both of us. Me for mercy. You for existing." I laughed—it came out wrong, cracked down the middle, nothing like my tavern laugh. "Congratulations. You're the first prisoner I've ever taken who outranks me."

He was quiet for a moment. Then, in the reasonable tone of a man discussing weather: "So the people who trained you are going to kill you for not killing me."

"Yes."

"And the people who raised me would kill me for leaving the valley, if they could find either of us." He looked at the milestone, at the fox, at the road north that led to Corven's Mill and safety-for-him and ruin-for-me, and then at the road south, which led nowhere at all. "Well," he said. "Since we're both already dead, I don't suppose you'd consider untying my hands."

Every instinct I owned said *garrison, writ, surrender, explain*. Every instinct I owned had been built by the same hands that wrote that notice.

I drew my knife and cut his bonds.

"We go south," I said, "away from the roads, until I decide what to do with you."

"Of course," he said solemnly. "You'll decide. Any day now." The fox barked once, and I would stake my life—it may yet come to that—that the sound was laughter.

We turned south together into the hills, fugitives both, and behind us on the milestone Dray's neat letters dried in the sun like a wound closing over. I told myself I still had a plan. I told myself I was still taking him in, just by another route, to another authority, someday, when my hands stopped shaking.

The fox glanced back at me over its shoulder, and I had the distinct feeling it was keeping count of how many days I would keep telling myself that.

It was right to keep count. By nightfall I had stopped.
