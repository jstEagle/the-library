## 8. Bargains in the Dark

*Rafael*

The rope around my wrists was, I had to admit, very professionally tied.

"You're staring at it," Sera said without turning around. She walked ahead of me along the riverbank like a woman out for a morning constitutional who happened to be towing a prisoner.

"I'm admiring the knotwork."

"Don't flatter me. It makes you harder to kill."

"That's the idea," I said. Lior trotted at my heel, tail up, radiating the smug serenity of a creature that had gotten exactly what it wanted. *This is going well,* the fox's posture said. *You've been captured by the one knight in the Ashguard with a conscience. I planned this.*

The fox had not planned this. The fox had been asleep in my pack when Sera cornered us at the crossing, which was either a failure of its legendary foresight or further proof that everything about my situation was improvised by idiots.

Three days, she'd said. Three days to the garrison at Thornmark, where I'd be handed over, questioned, and—she hadn't said killed, but she'd said it in the way people say nothing while meaning everything. Three days of walking behind a woman whose sword arm worked better than her conscience, trailed by a fox who kept making comments I could feel but not hear.

"Your animal keeps looking at me," she said on the first evening, as we made camp under an overhang of gray stone.

"He does that."

"What is he?"

"A fox."

"That's not what I asked."

"And yet it's what I'm answering." I sat down heavily against the rock. My legs ached. Rope chafes more than you'd think when you're not being dragged so much as escorted, which is somehow worse—it implies courtesy, and courtesy from your captor is its own kind of insult. "Ask me something I can survive answering."

She crouched across the small fire, feeding it twigs one at a time, precise as a surgeon. Firelight did things to her face that made interrogation seem almost companionable. Almost.

"Why didn't they run?" she said. "At the crossing. The deer, the herons, the otter. Animals don't shield men. They flee men. It's the oldest law there is."

"They liked me."

"Animals don't *like*, beast-speaker. They fear, they hunger, they breed, they die. Liking is a thing we invented so we could lie to ourselves about the rest."

"You must be wonderful at parties."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not a smile, more the memory of one, buried so deep it had fossilized. "I'm wonderful at executions," she said. "That's the family trade."

I should have let it go. A wise man, tied up beside a witch-hunter's fire, lets silence do the work. But I'd spent thirty years in a valley that taught me silence the way other places teach prayer, and I found I'd rather die talkative than live quiet one more day.

"Whose family?" I asked. "Yours? Or the Ashguard's? Because those aren't the same thing anymore, are they. You missed."

Her hand went still over the fire. One twig, half-burned, held between two fingers.

"I didn't miss."

"Sera." I said her name the way you'd set down a heavy thing, carefully, watching where it lands. "You're the finest killer I've ever seen, and I've watched a hawk take a hare at forty paces. You put an arrow into the mud six inches from my heart. That wasn't a miss. That was a letter."

She stood, dropped the twig into the flames, and looked at me for a long moment with those eyes that catalogued everything and confessed nothing.

"Sleep," she said. "We move at dawn."

* * *

*Sera*

He talked in his sleep. Not words—sounds. Little clicks and whirs and low huffing notes, like a man dreaming in a language made of birdsong and hoofbeats. I sat awake most of the first night listening to it and telling myself it was nothing. A tic. A madness. The gifted were always a little mad; every text agreed on that.

The texts also said the gifted died screaming, and this man slept like a child in a hayloft while a wolf watched him from the treeline and did not approach. Did not approach. Wolves do not decline opportunities. Wolves are opportunity given teeth.

I wrote in my report-log by moonlight, because procedure steadied me: *Prisoner secured. Transport proceeding. Anomaly noted: local fauna non-hostile.* Then I stopped, because the next line wanted to be *anomaly noted: prisoner kind,* and kind is not an anomaly, kind is just a thing I had forgotten the shape of.

On the second day we came through country burned two years past—a wildfire, black trunks standing like the ribs of some enormous dead animal—and he stopped talking entirely. He didn't need to. Every stump we passed, he touched. Just brushed his fingers over the char as we went, the way you'd touch a coffin lid. And I swear before any god still taking attendance that the ground under our feet felt less dead after he passed. I told myself that was imagination. Imagination is the first symptom. The texts said that too.

"Tell me about the courier," I said, mostly to hear his voice again, though I'd have denied that under torture.

"Burned. Dying. Scared." He walked with his bound hands loose in front of him, using them to push branches aside for me before I reached them, which was either habit or strategy and I couldn't decide which annoyed me more. "He said 'the Ember remembers.' Then he said—I think—he said 'don't let them own it.'"

"Them?"

"Anyone. Everyone. He wasn't choosy at the end. Dying men get democratic."

"The Ember is a weapon of succession. Whoever holds it ends the war in a week."

"Or starts one that never ends." He glanced back at me, and his eyes were steady in a way that unsettled me more than hatred would have. Hatred I knew how to carry. "You've read the histories. What happened the last time a throne held living fire?"

I had read the histories. I'd memorized them. The Cinder Kings, three centuries of ash, whole provinces rendered down to their skeletons. I said nothing, which he took, correctly, for agreement.

That night he asked me a question I still haven't answered.

"Why do you do it?" he said. We were sharing the fire because the nights had turned cold and even captors freeze. "Hunting the gifted. You're not stupid. You're not cruel—or you're crueler than you look, but I don't think so, I've met cruel, cruel doesn't feed prisoners first. So why?"

Because the order raised me from nothing, I thought. Because duty is the only parent I have. Because if I stop believing the work is righteous then the child's face comes back, the one from Vellin Cross, the small marked face, and I will follow it down into the dark and not come up.

"Because someone has to," I said.

"That's not a reason. That's a surrender with good posture."

I threw a pinecone at him. He caught it with his bound hands, which shouldn't have been possible, and grinned at me, and I lay down facing away from the fire so he couldn't see whatever my face was doing.

* * *

*Rafael*

By the third day I'd learned three things about Sera Vane.

One: she counted everything. Steps, rations, birdsong, my breaths when she thought I slept. A woman counting is a woman holding the world together by arithmetic.

Two: she talked to the horses. Not the way I did—no answers, no conversation—but low murmured nothings at the picket lines, apologies for the bit, thanks for the miles. She'd have denied it under oath. The horses knew better. Horses always know.

Three: she was going to miss again. I couldn't have told you how I knew. Maybe it was the way she checked my ropes each morning—tight enough to satisfy regulation, loose enough that my hands stayed warm. Maybe it was the second blanket she'd produced from her pack on the second night and tossed at my head without comment. Maybe it was just that mercy, once started, is like water finding a crack: it keeps going until something breaks.

Lior, meanwhile, had appointed itself our translator of silences. When Sera went rigid at some kindness of mine, the fox would sit between us and groom its tail with theatrical patience until she relaxed. When I got bitter—which I did, mornings especially, rope-burned and homesick—the fox would lean against my shin, warm as a coal, and the bitterness would lose its footing.

*What are you?* I asked it silently, for the hundredth time.

*Patient,* it answered, which was no answer at all, and both of us knew it.

We were fording a shallow stretch of the Thorn River on the third afternoon when the geese told me.

Geese are gossips. They'll tell you anything—weather, wolves, where the best grain is, which farmers shoot straight. These ones came down the valley in ragged formation, honking a single word over and over, and the word was *iron iron iron iron.*

I stopped mid-river. "Sera."

"Don't stop in the water."

"There are riders behind us. Six. Heavy armed. Moving fast." I turned to look at her. Water pulled at my knees. "They're not following us, Sera. They're following *you.*"

I watched her understand it. It moved across her face like weather—the tightening, the calculation, the cold arithmetic of a soldier realizing her own side has reclassified her. She'd reported the courier's death. She'd reported the capture. Somewhere between the crossing and here, someone had reported the *mercy*—the missed shot, or the soft ropes, or the second blanket, or all of it braided together into a word that gets knights burned alongside witches: *compromised.*

"How many did you say?" Her voice was perfectly level. I admired it and pitied it in equal measure.

"Six. Maybe seven. There's a seventh whose horse walks wrong—old injury, left hind."

"Lameness in the off-hind," she said automatically, then caught herself. "Stop doing that."

"Doing what?"

"Being *useful.*"

The riders crested the ridge behind us as the sun went down—six lances and a banner I recognized from her saddlecloth, the ash-gray chevron of the Inquisition. Sera waded to the bank, hauled me up by the rope, and stood there a moment with the firelight of sunset on her face, weighing her entire life in the balance of a few seconds.

Then she cut my hands free.

"Took you long enough," I said.

"Shut up and run."

* * *

*Sera*

We ran. There was no plan in it, only terrain—up the scree, into the pines, along a game trail only he seemed able to see, because the deer had told him, because of course they had. Behind us the riders split, two circling wide, four coming straight on, and Dray's voice carrying across the valley like a thrown knife:

*"SERAPHINE VANE. YOU ARE NAMED IN THE LIST OF THE FALLEN. DELIVER THE PRISONER AND BE JUDGED MERCIFULLY."*

Judged mercifully. I knew what Dray's mercy weighed. I'd helped calibrate the scales for eleven years.

An arrow took the bark off the pine beside my head. Rafael grabbed my sleeve and hauled me sideways—*the prisoner saving the captor*, Dray would have wept with vindication—and shoved me toward a hollow under a fallen trunk.

"In here. They won't come close."

"Why not?"

"Because," he said, and whistled.

It wasn't a whistle a man should be able to make. It went through registers, bird-high to bull-deep, and the forest answered. I heard boars moving in the dark—boars, plural, big ones—and something else, something with too many feet moving too quietly, and the pursuit crashed to a halt at the edge of the trees like a wave hitting a wall.

I pressed myself into the hollow beside him, shoulder to shoulder in the dark, both of us breathing hard, and listened to six trained killers of the Inquisition retreat from a pig.

"You could have gone with them," he whispered. "Walked out with your hands up. Said I forced you."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

Because Dray's mercy is a pit. Because I have seen the inside of an Inquisitor's judgment chamber and heard what confession sounds like when it's finished. Because somewhere between the crossing and tonight I had stopped being able to imagine the man's death, and a knight who cannot imagine the kill is already dead in her order's eyes.

"Because you'd tell them about the pinecone," I said.

In the dark, I felt him grin. It's a strange thing, feeling a stranger grin. Like hearing rain start on a roof you'd thought was solid.

* * *

*Rafael*

We struck the bargain at dawn, sitting on a cold hilltop with the smoke of our abandoned camp rising behind us and the whole world suddenly arranged against us.

Terms were hers first: I would not run. I would not use—"it"—on her. I would answer her questions honestly, and in exchange she would not deliver me to anyone wearing a chevron, or a crown, or, as she put it with grim precision, "any symbol of authority whatsoever, which at current count excludes everyone."

Terms were mine: she would stop calling Lior "it." She would stop pretending she hadn't missed on purpose. And when this was over—when the map had done whatever it was going to do—she would decide for herself what she believed, instead of letting dead men in books decide for her.

"That last one isn't a term," she said. "That's a sermon."

"Everything worth bargaining for is."

She looked east, where the road ran toward Bellharbor and the free council and whatever politics waited there, and west, where Thornmark's garrison would soon learn their quarry had escaped with a traitor, and south, where Veylan's armies sat like weather waiting to happen.

"If I help you," she said slowly, "and the Ember ends up in a queen's hands, or a king's, and the burning starts again—your burning, my kind of burning—then I will have done it. Me. With my own feet."

"And if you don't help me, and it ends up in Veylan's hands, same arithmetic. Worse, actually. He'll hold it longer."

"You're saying I'm damned either way."

"I'm saying we're all choosing, Sera. You've just been choosing by standing still, and standing still is starting to cost extra."

For a long moment she said nothing. Below us a kestrel hung in the wind, hunting, patient, utterly certain of the sky. She watched it the way she watched everything—as if it might testify against her.

Then she pulled the rope from her belt, the good professional rope, three days of knots and courtesy, and threw it off the hillside into the bracken.

"We go to Bellharbor," she said. "We find whoever's buying maps, and we make sure they buy nothing. And Rafael—" she said my name like she was testing whether it fit in her mouth, "—if you ever whistle like that at me, I'll gut you."

"Noted," I said. "It's really more of a request system."

Lior bounded ahead down the trail, tail high, insufferably pleased, and after a moment—for no reason I could defend, and every reason I couldn't say—we followed.

Behind us, far to the south, a beacon tower lit against the morning: word spreading, war waking, the hunt widening into something neither of us had a name for yet.

Ahead of us, Bellharbor. Where, as Lior had tried to warn me for two days, the law itself was about to become the thing we'd have to steal from.
