## 9. The Free City of Bellharbor

Bellharbor smelled of salt, tar, and money—the last of these the strongest, and the least honest of the three.

We came down off the coast road at dusk, when the city's ten thousand lamps were being lit one by one by lamplighters who moved along the seawall like a slow procession of fireflies. Below them the harbor itself was a forest of masts, and above everything, crowning the hill at the city's heart, stood the Councilhouse: a fat, white-domed building that looked less like a seat of government than an enormous egg laid by a very confident bird.

"That," said Lior, perched on my shoulder in its fox-shape, small enough now to pass for a peculiar pet, "is where freedom goes to be weighed and sold by the pound."

"You could try optimism once. Just to see how it fits."

"I tried it in 1143. It chafed."

I had learned not to ask Lior for dates. The answers were always worse than the question.

Sera walked a half-step behind me with her hood up and her sword wrapped in sacking on her back, because an Ashguard blade in Bellharbor was worth more than a confession in a courtroom—it would get you hanged before you finished explaining it. She hadn't spoken much since Grayfen. Since the songs. We'd come into the city expecting anonymity and found instead that we'd arrived as a rumor: *the beast-speaker of the fen*, who turned herons into spies and eels into picket lines and never spilled a drop of blood. Minstrels had already improved the story. In the version sung outside the Gate of Chains, I was seven feet tall and the fen itself had risen to fight beside me.

The truth was that I'd spent two days afterward unable to look at water without hearing every fish in it complaining about the cold.

* * *

We found lodging in the Tidesick Ward, which is the part of any free city where people go to be unremarkable, and paid our rent to a landlady named Odji who looked at Sera's wrapped sword, at Lior, at the map tube slung across my chest, and said only, "No fires after midnight, no questions ever." It was the most hospitality we'd been shown in weeks.

The plan—such as it was—was simple. Rest. Resupply. Learn the roads east toward the mountains while Veylan's witch-hunters combed the wrong provinces. The Ember's cradle lay nine days' hard ride from Bellharbor if you knew the passes, and nobody in this city knew the passes better than the caravan-masters who wintered here.

That was the plan. But plans are made by people, and people, given a free city and a hard choice, will always find a way to make things worse politely.

We heard about the council session the way you hear about weather: everyone talking about it at once. Veylan's envoys had arrived under a flag of parley. They carried an offer. And the Free Council of Bellharbor—the oldest continuous government on the coast, older than either throne, the institution every exile and smuggler and runaway gifted child had ever pointed to as proof that people could govern themselves without a crown—was going to debate whether to sell us.

Not *hunt* us. Not *betray* us, with all the ugly connotations. *Bargain.* The word did the work of the other two while keeping its hands clean.

"They'll vote to take the offer," Sera said flatly, the first evening, staring out the window at the harbor lights. "I sat in war councils for six years. I know the arithmetic they'll do."

"Then we leave tonight."

"And walk into Dray's net on the east road? He's not hunting the fen anymore, Rafael. He's hunting the roads around this city, because he knows exactly what I know—that this is the last safe harbor, and safe harbors are traps with better manners." She turned. In the lamplight her face was all angles and exhaustion. "We need the council to vote *no*. Or we need to be gone before they vote at all."

Lior, curled on the bed like a spill of orange ink, opened one eye. "Or," it said, with the relish of a creature about to suggest something terrible, "we attend the debate."

* * *

Getting into the public gallery of the Councilhouse turned out to be insultingly easy. Bellharbor prided itself on open sessions; any citizen could climb the gallery stairs and watch their governors deliberate. Nobody checked faces, because nobody in Bellharbor believed they had anything to fear from their own institutions. That, I came to understand, was precisely the problem.

So on the third morning after our arrival, Rafael Eriksen, wanted in two kingdoms, sat in a wooden gallery bench between a fishwife and a law student, and watched the free world decide what he was worth.

The chamber below was a horseshoe of desks beneath the great dome, forty-one councilors, and at the center a raised chair occupied by First Speaker Corvane Hale—a broad, gray-bearded man with a voice like a ship's bell, who had, according to the fishwife (who narrated everything), personally negotiated the treaty that kept Bellharbor neutral through the last border war. A hero of the old style. The kind of man whose portrait hung in taverns.

Veylan's envoy stood at the floor's center like a knife someone had left standing upright. His name was Master Quillon, and he was soft-spoken in the way of men who have never needed to raise their voices because other people raised theirs for them, eagerly, hoping to be spared.

"The Protectorate," Quillon was saying—I noticed he said *Protectorate*, never *usurper*, never *Veylan's rule*, as though the man himself had been dissolved into his own bureaucracy—"does not ask Bellharbor to take sides. The Protectorate asks Bellharbor to protect *itself*. There is a fugitive in your city carrying an artifact of immense destructive potential. There are forces converging on your walls who will not distinguish between the fugitive and the city that sheltered him. Hand over the map and the bearer, and the Protectorate guarantees your charters, your neutrality, and your shipping lanes for a hundred years."

A hundred years. Spoken like a man giving away something that belonged to him.

An old councilor near the horseshoe's curve—a woman with a cane propped against her desk, introduced as Councilor Vess—did not bother hiding her contempt. "Guarantees from a usurper. I was in this chamber when his soldiers burned the granaries at Cael Ford, Master Quillon. Shall I fetch the minutes?"

"Times change," Quillon said pleasantly. "So do granaries."

There was laughter—not much, but some—and I felt something cold move through my stomach, because the laughter wasn't cruel. It was *nervous*. It was the sound of good people testing whether it was safe yet to find this funny.

Corvane Hale rang his bell for order and called the debate properly open. And then, for four hours, I watched the free city of Bellharbor think out loud.

* * *

Here is what I learned about how a good institution rots: it doesn't rot from malice. Malice is too obvious; malice gets voted down. It rots from *care*. From responsibility. Speaker after speaker rose—not villains, I want to be clear about that, not one single villain among them—and spoke movingly about the thirty thousand souls of Bellharbor. About the famine winter of their grandparents. About the duty of a council to weigh one stranger's life against a city's peace.

"We are not cowards," said a young councilor named Pell, and I believed him, which was the worst part. "But courage that costs the city everything isn't courage. It's vanity wearing courage's coat."

"Handing over a man to be burned," Vess retorted, "is not prudence. It is arson with a ledger."

"He is one man."

"He is a *precedent*," she snapped. "Today the bearer of a map. Tomorrow the healer whose gift offends the Protectorate. The day after, anyone the envoys point at. You think Veylan's price stays bought? Men like him renegotiate."

And Quillon simply waited, patient as tide, and when the fury crested and broke, he said gently: "Councilor Vess speaks of what may happen. I speak of what *is*: two armies, one fugitive, and a city without walls thick enough for sentiment. The Protectorate's patience is genuine but finite. I regret the arithmetic. I did not write it."

He hadn't written it. That was the trick of it. Every man in that chamber could hate the arithmetic and still do the sum.

In the gallery, the fishwife muttered curses. The law student took notes with the feverish diligence of someone watching history and mistaking it for jurisprudence. And I sat very still, because I had learned in Hollowmere that stillness is what animals do when they've caught the scent of something hunting.

Beside me, Sera had gone white. Not afraid—furious, the old furious, the Ashguard furious, the fury of a woman watching the machine she used to be do its work in a new housing.

"This is how it happened to us," she said quietly. "This exact room. This exact tone. Reasonable men, real fears, and one person in the middle who becomes a line item." Her hand had found mine without either of us deciding it. "Rafael, they're going to vote yes. Not today—maybe not even this week. But yes."

Below, Corvane Hale called a recess until the morrow, and the horseshoe emptied, and the great egg-shaped dome filled with the shuffling echo of forty-one consciences going to lunch.

* * *

We spent the afternoon doing the things desperate people do: checking the east road through a carter Lior bribed with dried fish (the fox has opinions about bribery; chiefly that it is underrated), counting witch-hunter sign at the gates, arguing in circles. Sera wanted to run west and risk the sea. I kept seeing the east road, and Dray's grey riders, and Maren's face the night I lied to her, and I couldn't make my feet agree to any direction at all.

It was Lior who went quiet first, and Lior quiet is a weather warning.

"You should hear them," the fox said suddenly, ears swiveling toward the window. "Down by the fish market. The gulls."

"What are they saying?"

"Gulls mostly say *mine* and *give*. But there's a dog talking to them, a big mastiff with a dockworker's collar, and the dog says the harbormaster's clerks came through this morning asking after a tall traveler with a fox. Asking at the *Tidesick Ward*." Lior's eyes caught the lamplight, green as harbor water. "Someone on the council talked. Before the vote. Someone already sold the answer."

I stood very still. Outside, Bellharbor glittered, ten thousand lamps over ten thousand honest lives, and somewhere in that shine a clerk was walking door to door with my description in his pocket, doing his job, probably a decent man, certainly following orders, absolutely certain he was protecting his city.

"They haven't voted yet," Sera said. "Whatever the clerks are doing, it's not lawful seizure. Not yet. If we go to Hale—"

"With what?" I heard my own voice come out flat and strange. "A claim? We're the item being debated, Sera. You don't get to argue your own case when you're the agenda."

"Then we run. Tonight. Now."

"Into Dray." I pressed my palms against my eyes. "Every road. He knows. Everyone knows. Don't you see? There's nowhere left that's *outside* the decision. The whole coast is a chamber, and we're the vote."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, a drunk was singing the song about the fen, badly, the version where I'm seven feet tall.

It was Lior who said, softly, in the voice it used maybe once a month, the old voice, the covenant voice: "The map is in the council's strongroom. Quillon asked for it to be held 'in trust pending resolution.' Hale agreed—it seemed so reasonable, so orderly, custody rather than surrender. It's been there since yesterday noon."

"So?"

"So," said the fox, "you keep telling me the valley hid you. That your whole life was built on being unseen, unheard, unrecorded. Fine. Be unseen. The strongroom is under the east stair. Two guards at the door, one asleep by the second watch. The lock is a Bellharbor civic lock—they are proud of those locks, which means they have not changed the design in sixty years, because pride and habit wear the same coat." Its tail flicked. "I am telling you, beast-speaker, that the lawful thing has already decided what happens to you. What remains is to decide what happens to the map."

* * *

I want to tell you I wrestled with it longer than I did. I want to report a long dark night of the soul, angels and devils on either shoulder, a proper moral drama worthy of the minstrels.

What actually happened is that I sat on the edge of the bed for perhaps the length of two bells, listening to Sera pack in the next room with the controlled violence of a woman who has already lost one institution and cannot bear to watch another die, and I thought about Councilor Vess saying *he is a precedent*, and I thought about the mare in Hollowmere, lame and gentle, whom I had healed in secret because healing was against no law but was somehow still a thing that had to be hidden—and I understood, with a clarity that felt less like thought than like a bone setting, that the law in Bellharbor and the silence in Hollowmere were the same animal. Both meant well. Both kept me safe by making me small.

And both, in the end, would hand me over.

"There's a version of this where we're thieves," I said.

"There's a version of this where we're dead," said Lior. "I prefer ours."

Sera appeared in the doorway with her unwrapped sword already belted on, and I braced for the argument—the knight's argument, duty and process and *there must be another way*. Instead she looked at me for a long moment and said, "Strongrooms have a second exit or they're just tombs. Find out which."

That was all. That was her blessing. Later I would understand what it cost her, the woman who had executed a child for the crime I was about to commit myself: choosing justice over the law, alone, in the dark, with no banner to hold it up. But that night she just checked the street, and nodded, and we went out into the salt-smelling dark together.

* * *

The Councilhouse by night was a different creature than by day. By day it was an egg, a dome, a monument. By night it was an old body breathing—timbers ticking, the dome pooling moonlight, the smell of wax and paper and stone dust. We came over the wall from the chandlery row, Lior first, then me, then Sera, and crossed a courtyard where a cat watched us with the professional indifference of its kind.

The cat said, to my senses, *mice in the wall, nothing else, go away,* and I nearly laughed aloud at how ordinary the world's conspiracy was.

Two guards at the east stair, as promised. One awake, one—Lior had been precise—asleep. The awake one I did not fight. I crouched at the stairhead in the dark and reached out the way I'd learned to reach, past words, into that place where a man's attention lives when it's bored and cold and thinking about supper, and I found the dogs in the kennel yard, and I let one of them bark. Once. Twice. Distant, directionless, the sound of nothing.

The guard's head turned toward it. His lantern turned with him. And for eleven seconds—Lior counted, Lior counts everything—his back was to the stairwell door, and I was through it, and down.

The strongroom was iron-banded oak and a civic lock sixty years proud. Sera had brought tools; Sera, it turns out, can pick a lock faster than she can admit she knows how, and I filed that away with all the other things she didn't talk about. The mechanism gave a soft click like a knuckle cracking, and the door swung, and there it was.

Shelves. Ledgers. Sealed writs. The accumulated paper skeleton of a free city. And on the second shelf, in a padded tray, as casually catalogued as a disputed deed: the leather tube.

I lifted it and the map stirred inside like something waking, and warmth ran up my arm, and Lior—waiting at the door, dim, threadbare, spending itself still—made a sound I had never heard from the fox before. Small. Almost like relief.

"You feel it," the fox whispered.

"Yes."

"That pull. Toward the door, toward the road, toward *away*." Its green eyes were very bright. "Every bearer feels it. It is the Ember asking what you'll do with it. Remember, when the asking gets louder: the ones who failed all answered *for themselves*. The ones who might succeed—" it hesitated, and for once the ancient, weary creature sounded almost hopeful— "answer for everyone else."

Above us, a bell began to ring. Not the alarm—not yet. The watch bell, marking the hour, ordinary as bread. But somewhere in the building a door had slammed, and a voice was calling for the night clerk, and the eleven seconds were long over.

Sera's hand closed on my shoulder. "East stair's waking up. Roof?"

"Roof," I said, and slung the stolen map across my chest, and followed her up into the dark.

Behind us the free city slept on, dreaming its lawful dreams, and I ran across its rooftops with the law's own property warm against my heart, and for the first time in my life I was not hiding from anything.

I was running *toward* something. I just didn't yet know that by morning, every bell in Bellharbor would be ringing our names.
