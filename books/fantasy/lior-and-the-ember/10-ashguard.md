## 10. Ashguard

The summons came on a Tuesday, which should have been my first warning. Inquisitors do not work Tuesdays. Tuesdays are for ledgers, hangover confessions, and the slow administrative murder of anyone whose paperwork has fallen behind. A man like Dray appearing in person, on a Tuesday, in a border town with more goats than people, meant one of two things: either I was already dead and hadn't been informed, or he wanted something.

I was drinking bad ale at the Drowned Hen when the room went quiet in that particular way rooms go quiet around Ashguard grey. Not silence—Hollowmere had taught me what real silence sounded like, and this wasn't it. This was the sound of forty people deciding very loudly to mind their own business.

He sat down across from me without asking. Of course he did. Dray had never once asked permission for anything in his life, including his own promotion.

"Sera Vane." He said my name the way a man reads an inventory item he's about to write off. "You look terrible."

"You look exactly the same," I said. "Have you considered that one of us is aging correctly?"

Dray smiled. It was not a warm smile. Dray's smiles were the kind you found on tax documents—technically present, functionally hostile. He was a big man gone soft at the edges the way old boots go soft, all of it settled into his shoulders and jaw, and his eyes were pale as river ice and just as likely to drown you. Fifteen years ago he'd pulled me out of a gutter orphanage in Corvane and told me I had good instincts and bad blood, and that the Ashguard could fix the second if I let it use the first.

I'd believed him. That was the whole tragedy of my twenties, compressed into one sentence.

"Three weeks ago," he said, "a courier carrying something valuable to the Ember was killed near a valley called Hollowmere. Two days after that, Knight-Sergeant Vane, who was assigned to track said courier, reports that she lost him at the ford."

"I did lose him."

"You reported losing him *after* arriving in Hollowmere. You reported losing him after spending a night there. After questioning villagers. After—" he consulted a scrap of paper he didn't need, because Dray remembered everything and used paper only to watch people sweat, "—after speaking at length with a local healer named Rafael Eriksen."

"The courier came through the valley. Eriksen was the last person to see him alive. That's called investigation."

"That's called investigation," Dray agreed pleasantly, "when the investigator doesn't then let the suspect walk out of the valley under her nose while she files a report claiming the trail went cold." He folded the paper away. "Reinforcements arrived in Hollowmere four days after you left. They found ash in your hearth, a burned map case in the inn's stove, and a witness who remembers you buying rope."

"Rope is standard kit."

"It is. The quantity was not." He leaned back. The chair creaked its professional opinion. "Here is what happens now, Sera. You are going to tell me where Eriksen is going, and you're going to tell me tonight, and tomorrow morning you'll be reinstated with a commendation and a transfer somewhere with better ale. Or you're going to tell me nothing, and I'm going to do this the way I did it in Corvane, and we'll both be older by the end of it."

I looked at my ale. It really was terrible. Thin, sour, the color of regret.

"And if I say I don't know where he is?"

"Then I'll know you're lying, because you bought rope for three and provisions for three, and Lior the fox-spirit eats nothing but apparently requires consideration anyway." He paused, savoring it. "A fox-spirit, Sera. Bound to a moving map. Do you understand what that means? The Ember isn't a rumor anymore. It's *found*. And whoever brings it to His Radiance will want for nothing for the rest of their life. Their family's name restored. Their crimes—" another smile, "—forgiven."

*Their crimes.* He said it looking directly at me, and I understood that he knew about Grayfen. Not the rescue—that hadn't happened yet—but the child. The one from eleven years ago, the marked boy in the fishing village whose name I still couldn't say out loud without tasting copper. Dray had signed that order too. He'd handed me the sword himself, and watched, and called it a lesson in resolve.

"There's no crime to forgive," I said. "I lost a fugitive. It happens."

"It does happen," Dray said. "To mediocre knights. You were never mediocre. That's why this disappoints me." He stood, dropped a coin on the table—generous, ostentatious, a little performance for the room—and said, quietly enough that only I could hear: "You have until dawn. The Bellharbor road, the shrine at Kiln's Rest. You know where I'll be. Bring me the healer and the map, or bring me your commission, because those are the only two things walking out of that shrine tomorrow."

He left. The room exhaled. My ale had gotten worse somehow, sitting there absorbing the conversation.

I paid for it anyway. Habit. Then I went upstairs to the room where Rafael was pretending to sleep, and Lior was actually sleeping, curled on the windowsill like a scarf someone had abandoned, and I sat in the dark chair by the door and thought about choices.

*

Here is the thing they don't put in the recruiting ballads: the Ashguard doesn't raise soldiers. It raises tools that occasionally dream of being people. I know, because I was one of the best tools they ever made. Give me a warrant and a target and I would produce results with the emotional range of a well-thrown hammer. For eleven years that was fine. For eleven years I didn't let myself remember a fishing village, or a boy's face, or the specific sound a mother makes when—

Anyway. Tools don't remember. That's the point of them.

Rafael shifted in his sleep and murmured something to the horse stabled below us—I could hear it even through the floor, that low animal murmur of his, the one that made every beast within earshot lean toward him like plants toward sun. Even asleep, half-dreaming, he was talking to something. Listening to something. The man could not stop being what he was, and it was going to get him killed, and some cold efficient part of me—the part Dray built—noted that it would be cleaner for everyone if I did it myself rather than letting Dray's people do it messily.

That part of me had been getting quieter lately. I'd started noticing it the way you notice a toothache fading: with suspicion, then relief, then something dangerously close to grief.

Lior opened one eye. Foxes' eyes are amber; this one's were amber with a banked coal behind them, and it looked at me across the dark room with the patience of something much older than its shape.

"He offered you a bargain," Lior said. Its voice was dry leaves and embers.

"How do you—"

"I am a fragment of the fire that burns in every oath ever sworn. Lies have a smell, knight. Yours smelled like a woman standing at a door deciding whether to open it." The fox stretched, yawned enormously, showing teeth that were slightly too many. "What will you do?"

"Sleep," I said.

"Liar," said the fox, comfortably, and closed its eye.

*

I didn't sleep. I walked.

Bellharbor at night is a city pretending to be free, all lanterns and argument and the smell of fish money, and I walked its edges until the walls ran out and the road began, and then I kept walking, because my feet apparently had opinions my mouth wouldn't admit to. The shrine at Kiln's Rest sat two miles out—an old waystation to some god nobody remembered, roof fallen in, altar cracked, the kind of place travelers stopped at without knowing why. I'd passed it twice on the road south. I knew Dray would pick it. Dray picked ruins; they flattered him.

Dawn was a gray bruise on the hills when I arrived, and he was already there, alone, which meant his men weren't. Which meant either he trusted me completely or he had crossbows in the treeline, and I had known Dray fifteen years, so: crossbows.

"You came," he said. He sounded genuinely pleased. That was the worst part of Dray—he could be pleased, in his way, the way a knife is pleased.

"I came to give you an answer."

"Answers before sunrise are usually yes. Answers after are usually no. You're early." He spread his hands. "So. Where is he?"

And here is where I should tell you I gave some speech, some ringing declaration, some moment worthy of the songs that would eventually—annoyingly—get written about all this. I didn't. What I felt wasn't noble. It was tired, and angry, and underneath both, a small stubborn animal thing that had been growing in me since a lame mare in a dead-magic valley had looked at Rafael Eriksen like he was the first kind word in its whole life.

I thought about the boy in the fishing village. I thought about how Dray had watched. I thought about how he was watching now, with the same expression, waiting to see if the tool still worked.

"No," I said.

Dray sighed, and it was almost tender. "Sera. Girl. I pulled you out of the gutter. I taught you everything—"

"You taught me how to kill a child and call it resolve." My voice came out flat. Good. Flat was safe. "You taught me that obedience is honor and doubt is rot. And I believed you, because I was nine years old and you were the first person who fed me. But I've spent three weeks watching a man heal animals nobody else can see are hurting, and listening to a fox explain that power was never supposed to belong to thrones at all, and I have discovered, Inquisitor, that everything you built in me has a crack running straight through it, and the crack has a name, and I'm not going to tell you what it is because you'd hunt him harder."

Something moved behind Dray's eyes. Calculation, mostly. Underneath it, faintly, actual hurt—which surprised me, and then didn't. Men like Dray believe they love the things they own.

"The Ember goes to Veylan," he said. "That is the future. You can be part of it or you can be a footnote in it. Last chance, Sergeant."

"Knight," I corrected. "If I'm going to be a footnote, spell my rank properly."

I reached inside my coat and took out my commission—the gray parchment, the wax seal of the Ashguard, the words that had defined me since I was old enough to hold a sword. Eleven years of identity, folded into a rectangle. My hands were steady. I noted that with distant interest, the way you note weather.

There was a brazier by the ruined altar. Pilgrims' convenience, centuries dead. Someone had left coals in it that still held a dull life, and I knelt—it seemed right, kneeling, for once for the correct reason—and touched the parchment to them.

It caught slowly. Parchment always does, reluctant, curling, browning at the edges like something deciding. Then the seal went, the wax bubbling, the grey crest of the Ashguard softening into shapelessness, and the flame took the rest in a hurry as if it had been waiting years for permission.

Dray watched me burn it and did not move to stop me. That was the most frightening thing he could have done. Stopping me would have meant I mattered. Watching meant I was already filed, already sentenced, already a line item in some ledger I'd never see.

"Treason," he said, when the last corner crumbled to orange dust. "You understand that. There's no coming back from burning it. No pardon, no appeal. Veylan's writ covers every road from here to the sea."

"I know."

"Your family name dies with you."

"My family name died eleven years ago in a fishing village," I said, "and I've been carrying the corpse around ever since. It's lighter now."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. Wind moved through the broken shrine, stirring ash off the altar, off my hands, off everything. Somewhere in the treeline a crossbowman shifted his weight—careless, or deliberately audible, letting me know I was spared and why. Dray lifted two fingers and the sound stopped.

"Walk away, Sera," he said finally, and his voice had gone quiet and old and almost kind, which was how I knew he meant the opposite. "Walk away, and live a year, maybe two, in some town where nobody asks questions. Because if I see you again—if you're standing between me and that healer again—I won't offer. Next time there's no shrine, no choice, no speech. Just the work."

"I know that too."

"Do you?" He studied me, head tilted, and for one unguarded instant he looked less like an inquisitor and more like the man who'd once bandaged my hands after my first sword drill, who'd told me I was worth something, who'd lied so thoroughly and for so long that even he probably believed it. "Then you learned the wrong lessons slower than most. That's something."

He turned and walked out through the shrine's empty doorway into the gray morning, and the treeline emptied behind him like water finding a drain, and I stood alone among the ashes of my own name with soot on my fingers and my heart going like a war drum.

I laughed. Actually laughed—once, short, startled out of me. It was done. Irrevocable. There was a version of me that existed until this morning, and she was cinders on a dead god's floor, and the woman standing here now had no rank, no order, no warrant, no protection, and a price that would be on every board from here to Corvane by week's end.

She also, I realized, felt lighter than she had in eleven years.

*

Rafael was waiting at the edge of Bellharbor's fish market, doing a poor job of looking inconspicuous, which is to say he was talking to the seagulls and the seagulls were talking back, loudly, in front of everyone. Lior sat on his shoulder wearing the smug expression of a chaperone whose charges have finally behaved interestingly.

"Well?" Rafael said, when I reached him. He'd gone pale when he saw my face—or maybe when he saw the soot. Beast-speaker senses, probably; he could likely smell the smoke on me, read the whole night off my skin. "Sera. What happened?"

"I resigned," I said.

"You—resigned. From the Ashguard. You can't resign from the Ashguard."

"I burned my commission. It's a slightly more permanent form of paperwork." I flexed my blackened fingers. "As of dawn I am a traitor to the crown of Veylan, wanted, landless, and unemployed. So. If your covenant needs a sword with no master attached, the position's open."

He stared at me. Behind him the gulls screamed what I sincerely hoped was encouragement.

"Why?" he asked, quiet. "You could have gone back. He offered you everything."

"He offered me the chance to keep being what he made me." I looked at the harbor, the masts, the gray water, anywhere but his face, because his face made honesty easier than it should be. "I executed a child once, Rafael. Marked as gifted. I've never told anyone that. Dray ordered it and I did it and I told myself for eleven years it was duty." My voice did something unsteady; I hated it and let it happen. "This morning he offered me reinstatement if I delivered you. Same order. Different child. And I looked at him and I finally understood that it was never duty. It was just him, using my hands."

The gulls had gone quiet. Even the market noise seemed to have stepped back a pace.

"So I chose," I finished. "Badly, probably, by any sensible accounting. But I chose it myself. First thing in fifteen years I've chosen with my own judgment instead of a warrant." I finally met his eyes. "Don't make it weird."

"Too late," Rafael said, and smiled, and it was the smile of a man watching someone come back from somewhere far away. "Weird is sort of my entire gift."

Lior leapt from his shoulder to mine, which no one had ever dared, and settled there like a verdict. "Sentimental," the fox pronounced. "Excellent. Sentiment survives longer than oaths. Now—move. There is a man across the market who has been counting coins and glancing at your soot for six minutes, and I do not think he is a poet."

I followed the fox's nose to a wiry figure in a dockworker's coat, scribbling on a slate, and my new reality landed all at once: no colors, no badge, no order between me and the world anymore. Just a traitor, a beast-speaker, a fragment of living fire, and a bounty with our names on it, heading east toward a mountain where every army in the kingdom intended to arrive first.

"We need horses," I said. "Fast ones. And Rafael?"

"Yeah?"

"From now on, talk to the gulls *quietly*."
