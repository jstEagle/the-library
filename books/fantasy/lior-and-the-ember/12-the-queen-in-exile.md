## 12. The Queen in Exile

I have been recognized by a mule, a magpie, and a one-eyed badger who wanted to discuss the ethics of chicken theft at length. But I had never, until that gray morning on the road above the Vell, been recognized by a queen.

She didn't announce herself. That was the first thing I noticed, before the guards, before the banner folded up small as a handkerchief in a saddlebag. She came down the trail alone, mud to the knee, hair the color of old copper tied back with what looked suspiciously like a strip of tent canvas, and she said:

"You're the beast-speaker."

Not a question. Queens, I would learn, don't ask questions they've already paid to answer.

"I'm a horse doctor," I said, because old habits die hard and mine had died only recently enough to twitch.

"You're a horse doctor," she agreed, "who walked out of Hollowmere with a map no army has held for two hundred years, crossed the Road of Small Kings with an Ashguard deserter, and robbed the Free Council of Bellharbor in broad daylight." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile but had clearly heard of them. "You've been busy for a man who heals lame mares."

Behind me, Sera's hand had gone to her sword with the smooth, terrible calm of someone deciding how many people she'd need to kill and in what order. Lior sat on my shoulder — he'd taken to riding there when his strength ran low, a warm weight like a loaf of bread that occasionally made remarks — and went very still.

"Easy," I murmured. To all three of them, actually. It worked best on the mare.

* * *

Her name was Amara, and she was nothing like I expected, which is to say she was exactly like I expected and I hated it.

I'd built a picture of the exiled queen out of campfire rumors: a woman of forty hardened by ten years of running, bitter, gaunt, more title than person. The woman who spread her cloak on a fallen log and invited us to sit was perhaps thirty-five, laugh lines already carved deep around eyes the gray-green of river water, hands that were a working woman's hands — callused across the palm, one finger crooked from a badly set break. She poured tea from a dented tin kettle like she'd done it ten thousand times on ten thousand roads, and she handed me the first cup, which is either courtesy or strategy depending on how cynical you're feeling that day.

That day I was feeling moderately cynical. By evening I'd be recalibrating.

"Your fox is dying," she said, not unkindly, watching Lior curl himself against my neck. "Or something near it. Ember-fragments burn what they are to do what they do. You know that?"

"I know he sleeps more than he used."

"He's spending himself holding your path open." She said it plainly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Every ward turned aside, every hunter misdirected, every bridge that happened to be unflooded when you needed it. That's him. And when he's spent—" She shrugged, and left the rest of the sentence in the steam off her tea.

Lior's tail tightened once around my neck. *She's not wrong,* he said, too quiet for anyone else to hear. *Don't let her be right about the rest.*

The rest being, presumably, everything else she was about to say. My fox has a gift for prophecy and a terrible sense of timing.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Amara going in, gathered from tavern talk and Sera's flat soldier's briefings: daughter of the old king, smuggled out of the palace the night Veylan's Ashguard burned the nursery wing where her younger brother slept. Ten years moving between loyalist cells, foreign courts, and mountain camps, gathering an army the way a rolling stone gathers moss — slowly, patchily, mostly in places nobody important was looking. The legitimate heir. The rightful queen. Words that got thrown around so often they'd gone smooth as river stones, worn free of meaning.

What I learned over the next three days in her camp was different, and it unsettled me precisely because it was true.

She knew her soldiers' names. Not the officers' — everyone knows the officers'. The cooks'. The farriers'. A boy named Tam who'd joined at fifteen and still couldn't grow a proper beard; she asked after his mother's cough. She argued supply logistics with a quartermaster half her age and lost, gracefully, and changed the plan. When a scout came back with frostbitten feet she knelt in the mud herself to help pull his boots off, and if you think that kind of thing can't be performed, I'd have agreed with you before I watched her do it — except that afterward, away from the fires, I saw her flex her own hands like they ached, and I thought: either that's real or she's the finest actor ever to lose a kingdom.

And the beasts liked her. That's the part I keep coming back to. Animals are honest in ways courts never learn to be. The camp dogs didn't orbit her the way they orbited people with food; they *leaned* on her, heavy and boneless, the way dogs lean on someone they trust with their whole stupid hearts. A hawk on her falconer's glove kept turning its head to watch her when she spoke.

"She's genuine," I told Sera that night, miserably, because I'd been hoping to find the seam in her. "That's the problem. She means it. All of it."

"That's what worries me," Sera said. She was sharpening a knife she did not need sharpened, which is what Sera does instead of pacing. "Genuine people with armies scare me more than liars. Liars can be bought. Believers have to be *stopped*."

* * *

On the third night she came to find me at the edge of camp, where I'd gone to talk to the horses, because after three days among humans I always need to talk to something that doesn't want anything from me except oats.

The mare — a rangy bay named Sorrel who belonged to no one in particular and had opinions about everyone — was telling me about a wolf she'd known as a foal, and whether wolves dreamed, and I was explaining that I honestly didn't know, when Amara stepped out of the dark and said, "Do they? Dream?"

I should have heard her coming. I always hear people coming. But she moved like the camp itself moved, like someone the ground had agreed to carry quietly, and I spun around with my heart trying to exit through my ribs.

"Sorry," she said, and meant it, and waited until I'd nodded before she came closer. "May I ask you something strange?"

"Everyone does."

"Do they talk to you? The animals. Or do you talk *at* them, and call their answers whatever flatters you?"

It was such a good question that I forgot to be guarded. "They talk. Really talk. Though most of them mostly talk about food, fear, and other animals. The world looks simpler from four legs."

"And truer?" 

"Sometimes. Mostly just smaller and more urgent."

She laughed — a real laugh, quick and surprised out of her, and I felt it land somewhere under my sternum and take root, which alarmed me. Charisma, I was learning, isn't a performance. It's a weather system. You stand in it and things start growing in you that you didn't plant.

"The court I was born in," she said, looking out at the dark hills, "had a hundred languages and none of them honest. Everyone spoke to gain something. Then I fled, and for ten years I've spoken to soldiers, spies, and farmers, and do you know what I found? Farmers speak like your animals. Food, fear, and each other. Smaller and more urgent." She turned those river-water eyes on me. "You're the first person I've met in a decade whose gift is *listening*. Every crown in this world is full of men who command. Not one of them listens. Do you understand what you could be to a kingdom, Rafael Eriksen?"

"A novelty," I said. "A circus act. A very expensive parrot."

"A conscience," she said. "With ears."

* * *

She made the offer by the fire, formally, with her captains summoned and dismissed again inside a minute — she wanted witnesses to the offer, apparently, but not an audience for my answer, which I grudgingly respected.

"Swear the Ember to my cause," she said. "Bind it to my line, as the covenant bound it to my ancestors'. In return: legitimacy. Protection. Purpose. Your sister lives under my seal for the rest of her life, untouchable. Your valley becomes sanctuary ground, written into law no successor can repeal. And you—" she paused, and here it was, the hook under the bait, delivered gently, "—you would finally belong somewhere. I've watched you at the edges of my camp for three days, beast-speaker. You stand outside every circle. Even with your knight, even with your fox, there's a part of you keeping count of the exits. I could give you a place where you'd stop counting."

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind me, Sera had gone very still, the way she goes still before saying something dangerous.

And the worst part — the part I'll admit here because the horses already know and they're terrible gossips in the way that matters least — is that she was right. About all of it.

I counted exits. I'd counted them since I was six years old and first heard the mare in my father's stable say *hurts* and understood, with the cold certainty children reserve for disasters, that this was a thing to hide. Twenty years of hiding. Twenty years of a gift amputated down to a trick, a secret, a shame. And here was a woman offering to make it a *title*. To write into law that what I was wasn't aberration but office. To give Maren safety I couldn't buy with anything I had. To make the listening holy.

I felt my knees consider it. Actually physically consider it — a warmth in the joints, a lean forward, the body readying itself to kneel before the mind had signed anything. Belonging, it turns out, has a weight, and when it's offered after twenty years of weightlessness, your body grabs for it like a drowning man grabs a rope, and doesn't check who's holding the other end.

Lior stirred against my neck. He didn't say anything. He just pressed his forehead, briefly, against my jaw — the way a fox says *I'm here, and also, look at the trap.*

"Your ancestors," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "The covenant bound the Ember to your line. And then your line lost the throne anyway, and the Ember ended up in a leather tube in a dead courier's hands. How did that work out for the last binding?"

Something flickered across her face — fast, quickly governed, but I'd spent three days learning her face and I caught it. Not guilt. *Impatience.* The expression of a woman who has heard an objection before and considers it beneath the moment.

"The last binding failed because the world failed it," she said. "Veylan happened. Corruption happens. That's why it must be bound *again*, properly, to a line that will guard it—"

"Or," I said, "it fails because binding power to any single bloodline is how you get Veylans. Give it time. Give it heirs. Give it a bad winter and a hungry court, and watch what 'guardian' comes to mean."

"Then what would you do?" she asked, and her voice dropped its warmth for one bare sentence, and I saw — really saw — the hunger underneath, the ten years of it, the woman who had lost everything and intended to be repaid in kingdoms. "Wander? Scatter it? Hand miracles out like alms and pray the world grows gentle? Idealism is a luxury of people who've never had to *rebuild*, beast-speaker. Someone must hold the center or everything falls."

"Maybe," I said, "the center shouldn't be holdable."

Silence. The fire popped. Behind me I heard Sera exhale, slow, through her nose.

Amara looked at me for a long moment, and then — and this is why I'll never fully trust my own judgment where she's concerned — she smiled, tired and real, and said, "You're not ready to answer yet. Good. Men who kneel quickly rise quickly too." She rose, shook out her cloak, and paused. "But hear me, Rafael. I'm not the villain of this story. I've bled ten years for my people, and I will spend the Ember well. Whatever you decide — Veylan is coming for that map, and so is Dray, and neither of them will offer you tea first. Decide soon."

She walked back toward the fires, and the dogs leaned on her as she passed, and I sat there hating how much I'd wanted to say yes.

* * *

Sera found me later, out past the picket lines, where I'd gone to apologize to Sorrel for ignoring her wolf question.

"Well," she said. "You didn't kneel."

"Nearly."

"I saw. Your left knee has no discipline whatsoever." She settled onto the grass beside me, close, our shoulders touching — we'd gotten to the shoulders-touching stage somewhere on the road out of Bellharbor, neither of us announcing it, both of us pretending not to notice, which is how Sera does everything that frightens her.

"She's real," I said. "That's what I can't shake. The soldiers, the dogs, the frostbitten scout — it's not theater. She'd be a good queen. Maybe a great one."

"Mm."

"'Mm'? That's it? I deliver a nuanced moral crisis and you give me 'mm'?"

Sera was quiet long enough that I turned to look at her. In the starlight her face had gone somewhere I hadn't seen it go before — not closed, exactly. *Bracing.*

"There's something you should know," she said. "About me. Before you go making decisions about queens and oaths and what people deserve." Her hand found mine in the grass, gripped it, and didn't let go. "It's not a pleasant story. And after I tell it, you might not want my shoulder touching yours anymore. So I'd rather tell it now than after you've sworn yourself to somebody."

The wind moved through the grass. Down in the valley, the campfires of the rightful Queen of the Realm burned in neat, hopeful rows.

"Sera," I said, "whatever it is—"

"No." Steady. Terrible. Brave, in the way I was learning cost her the most. "Don't forgive it before you've heard it. Nobody's ever done that, and I'm not going to start letting you."

She took a breath, and began.
