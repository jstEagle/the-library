## 13. Sera's Truth

I have killed eleven people in my life, and I could give you a reason for each one. That is the part that should frighten you—not the number, but the reasons, which all sounded reasonable at the time and sound like coins dropped down a well now. *He was marked. She resisted. The order came.* Eleven coins, eleven wells, and at the bottom of every one the same dry echo: *you were following orders, Sera Vane, and orders are a kind of absolution if you never look up.*

The twelfth was not a person.

That is what I told myself for six years. *Not a person. A vessel. A spark of the old corruption wearing a child's face.* The Ashguard had a whole liturgy for it, polished smooth as a chapel floor from use. We did not kill children; we *quenched* them. We did not murder; we *cleansed*. Language is a merciful thing when you let it do your thinking for you.

Her name was Wren. She was nine years old. She could make moths land on her fingers like they'd been invited.

I have thought about those moths more times than I have thought about my own mother's face.

* * *

It happened in the foothills above Bellharbor, three days after we left the free city behind us with its council's shame still smoking behind our ears, and it happened because Rafael Eriksen cannot walk past a suffering animal any more than water can walk uphill.

We had stopped to rest by a shrine—some old roadside god with its face worn blank, a stone basin full of rainwater and pine needles—and there was a dog under the shrine. A shepherd mix, ribs like a harp, one eye clouded white, and a snare wound around its hind leg so old the wire had grown into the flesh. It should have bitten him. Any sane animal would have bitten him. Instead it watched Rafael crouch down with an expression I recognized, because I had been wearing it myself for weeks: the expression of something wild deciding, against all evidence, to trust.

"Hey," Rafael said softly. "Hey, friend. Let me see."

And the dog *let* him. Lifted its ruined leg into his hands like an offering.

Lior sat on the shrine's flat top with its tail curled around its feet, watching with those amber coin-eyes, and said nothing, which from Lior is a kind of commentary.

"You're going to carry that dog," I said. Not a question. We had a stolen map, two armies' worth of hunters on our trail, and a fox-spirit spending itself like a candle to keep the path open, and this man was going to add a limping stray to the column.

"I'm going to carry that dog," Rafael agreed. He didn't even look up. His hands moved over the wound with a gentleness that made my chest hurt in a way I didn't have time to examine. "What's your name, hmm? No? They never tell me their names. Just their hurts." He glanced up at me then, and his eyes did the thing they always did—the thing that had marked him to me from the first moment in Hollowmere, too steady, too present, like a man who has spent his whole life listening and therefore never learned to look away. "You could help. You're better at splints than you pretend to be."

"I'm not pretending anything about splints."

"You wrapped my ankle after Grayfen like you'd done it a hundred times. Field dressing, Ashguard pattern. You hum while you work. You don't know you hum."

"I do not hum."

"You hum. Very quietly. Something in a minor key." He turned back to the dog. "It's all right," he told it, though the dog hadn't growled. Or maybe it had, and only he heard. "She looks frightening but she's mostly scar tissue."

I should have been offended. I found, to my considerable irritation, that I was smiling instead. This was the trouble with Rafael Eriksen: he disarmed you by noticing things. An Ashguard blade comes at you fast and you can fight it. Kindness aimed with that much precision—you just stand there and take it.

We made camp early that night, in the lee of a fallen oak, because the dog couldn't go further and Rafael wouldn't hear of leaving it, and because—for reasons I declined to inspect—I didn't want to push on either. Lior curled into a ball on Rafael's bedroll, dimmer than it had been a week ago, its fur gone gray at the edges like frost on a window. It slept the way fire sleeps: reluctantly, with one ear burning.

Rafael fed the dog broth from our dwindling stores. I cleaned my blades, which is what I do instead of praying. And somewhere between the second whetstone pass and the third, he said, without any preamble at all:

"Why did you really miss?"

I kept working the stone along the edge. "Miss what?"

"The river crossing. Back before everything. You had me dead to rights. I've replayed it a hundred times. Your stroke was true—I felt it coming, I'd already said goodbye to the world—and then it wasn't. You hit the pack strap instead of my throat. A strap, Sera. You've killed eleven men, I know that now, I've seen how you move. You don't miss straps."

The whetstone stopped. I noticed it stopping the way you notice your own heartbeat during a fever—something automatic going wrong.

"There was a dog," I said finally. "At the crossing. A marsh hound with pups under the far bank, and they were screaming at you. Not barking. Screaming. Like they knew what you were." I set the blade down. "And you were talking back to them. Under your breath. And I thought—" I stopped.

"Thought what?"

"Thought that no one who listens like that should die by my hand." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too short. "Which is a stupid reason to disobey a direct order. Believe me, I've had six years to compile the list of stupid reasons, and that one ranks high."

"It's not stupid."

"It's the least stupid thing about me," I said, "which tells you the state of the rest."

The fire cracked. The dog sighed in its sleep, dreaming of whatever dogs dream of—herds, perhaps, or the absence of snares. And Rafael didn't push. He just waited, the way he waited with animals, with that terrible patient attention, until the silence itself became a hand held out flat, palm up, waiting to see if you'd put something in it.

I put something in it.

"Her name was Wren," I said.

* * *

I told it badly, at first. I want to be honest about that, because the story deserves honesty even from its villain. I gave him the official version first—the report version, the version I'd recited to my confessor-shaped bottle of ashwine a hundred times—*a gifted child in a quarantine village, marked by the census, order signed, execution carried out per protocol*—and Rafael listened to the whole bloodless parade of nouns and then said, very quietly:

"Sera. What was her name?"

And the wall went down. All of it. Six years of masonry, gone in four words, because he hadn't asked what the protocol was or whether I'd followed it correctly. He'd asked her name.

"Wren," I said. "Wren Halloway. Her father was a cooper. Good barrels, I'm told. I never met him—he'd been taken the month before, to the questioning houses in Carreth. She was hiding in the smokehouse when we came. Nine years old, and she hid like a child hides, which is to say she hid where a child would hide, behind the salt barrels, and she was crying, and the moths—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I pressed on. "There were moths in the smokehouse. Dead of winter, mind you, snow on the ground, and there were moths on her shoulders and in her hair, dozens of them, alive, like she was wearing a cloak of them. And my sergeant looked at me and said *Vane, you're junior, you do it, it's good practice,* and I drew my sword, and she looked up at me, and she wasn't afraid. That's the part. Everyone thinks the worst part would be fear. It wasn't. She looked at me like she understood. Like she pitied *me*. Nine years old, kneeling in the salt, covered in moths, and she pitied the woman holding the sword."

The fire had burned low. I stared into it because looking at Rafael was impossible.

"She said, 'It doesn't hurt them. The listening. It doesn't hurt anyone.' And I said, 'I know,' Sera Vane, knight of the Ashguard, said *I know*, and then I did it anyway. Because the order was signed. Because refusing meant the questioning houses for me and worse for my family. Because eleven reasons, all of them coins, all of them wells." I finally looked up. "So when you ask why I missed at the river—that's why. Because I had already decided, somewhere underneath the armor, underneath the oaths, underneath six years of telling myself she wasn't a person, that I would never again be the sword that comes down on someone gentle. And then you knelt down in the mud and talked to hounds, and my arm decided before I did."

Rafael didn't say anything for a long time. Long enough that I started composing exits—*forget I spoke, soldier's rambling, the wine at Bellharbor*—and then he moved, slow, the way you move around a wounded animal, and sat down beside me close enough that his shoulder touched mine.

"You didn't come to Hollowmere to kill me," he said. "After the river. You took me prisoner when your orders said otherwise. You've been choosing her ever since, haven't you? Every day since the smokehouse. Choosing her over the order."

"I don't know what I've been choosing."

"Yes you do." Gentle. Infuriatingly gentle. "You've been trying to become someone she wouldn't have pitied."

I laughed—a real one, broken open, ugly and clean at once. "Gods. You make it sound almost noble. It wasn't noble. It was cowardice stretched over six years."

"Sometimes those are the same animal," Rafael said. "Different pelts."

* * *

He told me about the mare that night. I think he decided we were past the point of trading secrets in halves, and besides, I had asked for it without asking—had given him my worst thing and left the space open for his.

"Her name was Bess," he said. We were lying side by side now, staring up through the bare branches at a sky rinsed with stars, the dog a warm weight against my boots, Lior a dim ember on the bedroll. "My sister Maren bought her as a foal, off a drover who was going to sell her for meat because she was born lame in the near foreleg. Maren has a soft heart and no head for economics. And Bess grew up crooked and sweet and she talked—well, she didn't talk, but she *spoke*, the way animals speak, pictures and pressures and smells-with-meanings. And I answered. That's all my gift is, really. Answering. Anyone could do it if they believed the conversation was real. That's the secret nobody wants: the covenant isn't magic. It's manners."

"Manners," I repeated.

"Courtesy," he said seriously. "Listening is courtesy. The valley forgot magic, but it never forgot manners, and so the animals stayed talkative there long after they went silent everywhere else. Did you know that? Everywhere else, the purges taught them silence. Generations of it. Beasts learned that humans who listen get their listeners burned, so they stopped speaking except to the rare ones, the safe ones. The world outside is full of refugees who won't say a word. But in Hollowmere—" He smiled at the stars. "In Hollowmere the horses gossip. The crows hold grudges with genealogies. There's a barn cat who has opinions about Maren's cooking that would curdle milk."

"And you listened to them your whole life. And told no one."

"Told no one." His voice changed, went quieter, and I understood we had arrived at the true country beneath the pleasant map. "Do you know what it's like, Sera, to live inside a miracle and never once be allowed to mention it? To sit at supper while my father complains about a horse that's 'stubborn,' knowing the horse is in pain, knowing exactly which tooth, knowing I could fix it in an afternoon if I could just say *she's not stubborn, she's hurting*—and instead nodding, passing the bread, being agreeable, being invisible?" He exhaled slowly. "I built a life out of silence the way you build a shelter out of stones. Careful. Load-bearing. One stone out of place and the whole thing comes down on everyone I love. So I stayed small. I healed Bess's lameness secretly, over months, little adjustments, and when she walked sound again Maren cried and called it a miracle of the valley saints, and I stood there smiling and dying."

"Bess," I said. "The mare. Where is she now?"

Something crossed his face—grief with an old scar over it. "Dead three winters now. Old age, peacefully, in the warm stall, with Maren's daughter asleep against her flank. She talked to me at the end. She said—" His voice caught, steadied. "She said I was the best listener she'd ever known, and that I shouldn't waste it on horses. That I was made for bigger conversations." He turned his head to look at me. In the firelight his eyes were the color of strong tea, and utterly unguarded. "I thought she meant the Ember. Now I think maybe she meant this. Sitting in the dark telling the truth to someone who needs to hear it."

The night was cold but I didn't feel it. "You were invisible in your own home," I said. "For thirty years."

"And you were visible everywhere and seen nowhere," he said. "Armor is just invisibility you wear outside. We're the same injury, Sera. Different sides of the same wound."

I wanted to argue. I was, professionally, excellent at arguing. Instead I lay there and let the truth of it settle over me like snowfall—weightless individually, accumulating into something that bent the branches.

* * *

Near dawn, the dog woke whimpering from a bad dream, and I was moving before I was awake—crawling the few feet to it, gathering its ugly scarred head into my lap, murmuring nonsense the way you'd soothe a child. It licked my wrist once, gravely, and subsided.

When I looked up, Rafael was watching me with an expression I will carry to whatever grave I eventually earn.

"What?" I said, defensive.

"Nothing," he said. "You hum when you do that, too."

"I do not—"

"In a major key, though," he said. "With the dog. Minor key for splints. Major for comfort." He crawled over and settled beside me, shoulder to shoulder, the dog between us breathing in slow bellows. "I notice things. It's the whole gift. Everything else is decoration."

The sky was going gray at the edges. Lior stirred on the bedroll, opened one amber eye, took in the two of us sitting together in the ruins of each other's secrets, and made a sound that was unmistakably, insufferably, a fox's chuckle.

"Oh, shut up," I told it.

"I didn't say anything," said Lior, smugly, and closed its eye.

But here is the thing I understood in that gray hour, with a stray dog's weight in my lap and a beast-speaker's warmth along my side: love doesn't arrive the way the songs claim. It doesn't thunder. It arrives the way a wild thing comes to your hand—not seized, not summoned, but after long stillness, after the showing of empty palms, after enough dawns pass without harm that the trusting simply becomes possible. It had arrived somewhere in the last weeks without my permission, between a missed stroke and a shared fire, and here in the cold morning light I finally turned my palm over and admitted it was there.

Rafael's hand found mine in the dark between us. Neither of us performed anything. Neither of us needed to.

We might have stayed that way till full sunrise, if Lior hadn't sat bolt upright, every hair on its spine lifted, ears swiveled hard south, and said:

"Riders. Many. Moving fast." Its ember-eyes flared. "And they're not following the map."

They were following us.
