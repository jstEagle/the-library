## 14. Dray's Net

*Rafael*

The crow found us at dawn, three days out of Bellharbor, sitting on the fence rail of an abandoned shepherd's hut like it had been waiting for someone to complain to.

It was a marsh crow, mud-colored, with a wing that healed crooked from some old break. It looked at me with one flat black eye and said, in the way crows say things—not words, but pictures pressed against the inside of your skull—*girl. valley girl. cage of iron. smell of ash.*

I dropped the tin cup I'd been holding.

"Sera." My voice came out wrong, thin as a reed. "Sera, wake up."

She woke up the way she did everything: instantly, silently, knife already in her hand, scanning the treeline before her eyes were fully open. Then she saw my face and lowered it by degrees.

"What is it?"

"The crow. There's a girl in a cage. Ash-smell." I swallowed. "Maren followed me. She followed me out of the valley, Sera, she must have—I thought I'd covered my tracks, I thought—" The words tangled. I started over. "Dray has my sister."

Sera crossed the camp in four strides and took the cup out of my hand, which was shaking badly enough to be embarrassing, and set it down on the fence post with a small deliberate clink, as if to say *look, the world still contains ordinary sounds.* "Tell me exactly what the crow showed you."

"A cage. Iron bars. A girl with Maren's hands—she has a scar across her left palm from when we were kids, she grabbed a scythe, I told her not to—and ash. Lots of ash. And a road." I closed my eyes and let the picture come again, because the crow was still there, patient, offering it up like a dog returning a stick. "A crossroads. A burned waystone. She's at a burned waystone."

"Which road?"

"I don't know. The stone had writing on it but crows don't read."

"Crows don't read," Sera repeated, and something flickered across her face that might have been the ghost of a smile under other circumstances. These were not other circumstances. "Rafael. Listen to me. This could be real, or it could be bait."

"It's my sister."

"It could be *both*." She said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Dray knows what you are now. Grayfen made sure of that—half the fen folk sing about you whether we like it or not. He knows your gift. Does he know your heart?"

"What does that mean?"

"It means he knows you'll walk into any trap in the world if there's a girl in a cage at the center of it. That's not an insult. It's arithmetic." She crouched by the cold fire pit and began raking ash with two fingers, thinking. "Inquisitors don't capture hostages they don't intend to display. If he has Maren, he wants you to come. He's counting on it."

"Then I'll go and disappoint him by winning."

"That's not how traps work."

"That's not how *I* work," I said, and heard how it sounded—young, stubborn, exactly the kind of thing a man says right before a trap closes on his leg. I didn't care. Somewhere behind me was a burned waystone and a girl with a scarred palm in an iron cage, and every hour I spent raking ash with a knight who thought in arithmetic was an hour they were deciding whether my sister was worth feeding.

Lior uncurled from the map tube where it slept these days, more often than not, its glow gone the color of old honey. It looked at me for a long moment with those eyes that had seen twenty bearers fail.

*The fox says:* it began, and then stopped, which Lior almost never did.

"Say it," I said.

*The fox says the pattern holds. Every bearer, when the ones they loved were threatened, ran straight down the road their enemies wanted. The Ember has been nearly captured six times this way. Twice it was captured.*

"And what happened when it was captured?"

Lior's ears flattened. *You do not want the history.*

"Wonderful." I turned back to Sera. "How fast can we move?"

Sera stood up slowly. And I watched something happen in her face—the thing I would think about later, in the bad hours, the way you pick at a splinter. Not disagreement. Something older than disagreement. Fear, maybe, wearing strategy's clothes.

"We don't move," she said. "Not yet. Not straight at him."

* * *

*Sera*

He looked at me like I'd slapped him.

I knew the look. I'd worn it myself once, years ago, standing in front of Commander Dray while he explained why the thing I'd been ordered to do was necessary, and sensible, and mine to carry without flinching. *Duty is doing the arithmetic when your heart refuses to count,* Dray used to say. I had believed him. I had built a whole hollow life inside that belief.

And here I was, handing Rafael the same arithmetic, watching him refuse to count.

"Walk me through it," he said, in a voice too controlled. "Slowly. Convince me."

"Fine." I knelt and drew in the dirt with a stick. "Here's the waystone—or wherever it is; we'll confirm with the birds. Here's us. Dray is an inquisitor of the Ashguard. He doesn't set ambushes with ten men. He sets them with fifty, and a second fifty behind the first in case the first fifty embarrass themselves. He will have picked ground that neutralizes your gift—open field, no beasts nearby, or worse, beasts already cleared out or killed. He knows what happened at Grayfen. He will have prepared for it."

"You don't know that."

"I know *him*. I served under him for nine years. He once burned a village's granary in winter to flush out one hedge-witch, then distributed bread to the survivors so they'd remember him kindly." I kept my voice level. "He is patient, thorough, and completely without cruelty in the personal sense—which makes him crueler than any sadist you'll ever meet. Sadists get sloppy. Dray never gets sloppy."

"So we go around. We find another way in."

"There is no around. That's the point of a net. You go in through the hole he leaves you, and the hole closes."

Rafael stood there with his fists half-clenched, and I could see the argument happening behind his eyes, the war between the man who'd learned patience healing animals in a hidden valley and the brother who'd just learned his sister was in a cage. I knew which one was winning. I'd have bet the farm on it.

"How long?" he asked finally. "If we scout first. If we do it your way. How long does Maren sit in an iron cage?"

"I don't know."

"Days?"

"Possibly."

"She's nineteen, Sera. She's never slept anywhere colder than our barn. She followed me because—" His voice cracked and he hated it, I could see him hating it. "Because of me. Because I lied to her and slipped out at night and she loves me enough to be stupid about it. Every hour in that cage is an hour I put her in."

"No," I said. "Every hour in that cage is an hour *Dray* put her in. That distinction matters. The moment you start owning his crimes, he wins something even if you die."

"Don't lecture me about winning." He was loud now, and the crow on the fence shifted its feet, uneasy. Even the birds went quiet when we fought, which should have told me something. "You want to know what I did before all this? I sat in a valley where nothing magical could follow, healing a lame mare nobody could know about, lying to everyone I loved, for *years*, because being invisible was safer than being seen. I am done with safe. I am done with careful. Careful is just another word for letting it happen somewhere else."

"And charging in is just another word for dying next to her," I snapped, and my own voice surprised me with its edge. Nine years of Ashguard discipline, undone by a beast-speaker with mud on his boots. "You think I don't understand wanting to burn the world down for someone? I defected. I'm a traitor with a price on my head because I couldn't watch anymore. But I learned the difference between a rescue and a suicide, Rafael, and the difference is *one day of patience*."

"One day." He laughed, and it wasn't a laugh at all. "You sound like them. Like the council at Bellharbor. *We must weigh, we must deliberate, we must be responsible*—while the wagonloads of gifted children roll toward Veylan's pyres. Responsibility is what cowards call fear when they've dressed it up."

That one landed. I felt it land. And the worst part, the part I couldn't say out loud, was that some frightened corner of me agreed with him—that every instinct I had was screaming to ride for that waystone with both blades out, consequences damned. That was the Ashguard in me, the part that solved problems with steel and speed. I'd spent nine years learning to distrust that voice. Now it was wearing love's face and I couldn't tell anymore which voice was which.

So I said the only true thing left.

"If you charge in there and die, who frees her?"

The words fell between us like a dropped blade.

"Think about it," I said, quieter. "Not as tactics. As the plain truth. Dray doesn't want you dead, Rafael. Dead bear-speakers are worthless. He wants you *delivered*—alive, broken, kneeling, with the map and whatever's left of Lior, presented to Veylan as proof the Ember can be owned. If you run at that net alone, the best possible outcome is you're captured and she's used as leverage forever. The worst outcome is you're both in cages. Me? I'm a loose end with a burned commission. He'll kill me on sight and consider it tidying up." I held his eyes. "So if you won't do it for strategy, do it for her. The fastest way to leave Maren in that cage forever is to get yourself caught trying to play the hero in one afternoon."

He stared at me for a long time. The crow made a low sound, almost like a warning, almost like grief.

Then he said, "One day. We scout today, we plan tonight, we move tomorrow at first light. One day, Sera. If the birds tell me she's being moved south—to Veylan, to a pyre, anywhere—one day becomes zero."

"One day," I agreed.

"And Sera?" His voice had gone quiet, which from Rafael was worse than shouting. "When this is done—if she has so much as a bruise Dray put on her—you don't get to talk me out of anything ever again. That's the bargain."

I should have argued. A good soldier would have argued, would have refused terms, would have explained that bargains struck in fear are chains. Instead I heard myself say, "Agreed," and understood that I had just promised to help him burn down anything he needed burned, and that some part of me had known I would from the moment the crow landed on that fence.

Love, I was learning, was just duty with the object changed. And I had never once in my life won an argument against my own duty.

* * *

*Rafael*

We spent the day gathering the way Sera gathered everything—methodically, quietly, like a woman building a wall one stone at a time while insisting she wasn't building anything.

The birds came. That was the one mercy of the day, the one thing that kept my hands steady: the birds came, dozens of them, drawn out of the grey morning like iron filings to a lodestone, because word travels among the winged ones faster than among any court. A kestrel with frost-nipped feathers. Two hooded crows and a raven who spoke in long, sardatic images. A barn owl, absurdly far from any barn, blinking at us from a hawthorn.

They told me what I'd feared and what I'd hoped, braided together.

*Maren lived.* She'd slipped out of Hollowmere two days after me, following my trail with the same stubbornness she'd used to follow me into trouble our whole childhood. She'd made it forty miles before the riders took her on the open road—taken alive, which told me Dray wanted something, and I knew what. The owl had seen the cage itself: iron-barred, wagon-mounted, ringed with ash-salt that stung the nostrils of anything living. Ash-salt. Dray had studied Grayfen too.

But the owl also gave me the shape of the camp, and the shape of the camp gave me hope, because Dray had made one mistake. He'd built his net on the ruins of an old drover's market at the crossroads—a place with a midden, a stable block, half-wild goats in the bracken, rats in the walls, and a colony of feral dogs denning under the collapsed granary. Open country, yes. Cleared of easy magic, yes. But he'd assumed that beasts near his camp meant nothing, because beasts meant nothing to the Ashguard. They were furniture. Vermin. Weather.

He had surrounded himself with fifty soldiers and a hundred pairs of eyes that belonged to me.

"They're not eyes," Sera said that night, when I laid it out for her in the dirt. "They're animals. They can't fight armed men."

"They don't have to fight. They have to *mislead*." I drew it as I talked, faster now, the plan assembling itself the way it had at Grayfen—assembling itself like something I'd always known how to do and only just remembered. "The dogs can drive the goat herd through the sentry lines at midnight. Panic in a camp at night is worth twenty men. The rats can chew bowstrings—no, wait, they'd need days for that. The rats can tell me where every guard stands when the alarm hits. The crows can carry false sounds—scream like a woman, scream like a horse, make the west perimeter think we're coming from the west while we come from the midden ditch. And the owl—" I paused. "The owl can find out which wagon the cage is on, and whether Maren's alive, tonight, before we commit to anything."

Sera studied the dirt for a long moment. When she looked up, something in her expression had shifted—not surrender, exactly. Recalculation. The look of a soldier realizing the strange weapon in front of her might actually be a weapon.

"This could work," she admitted. "It could also collapse into chaos, and chaos favors the side with armor."

"Chaos favored us at Grayfen."

"At Grayfen you had a fen full of creatures that had lived wild for generations. These are camp-following vermin and half-starved dogs, Rafael. They're brave, I believe you—but bravery isn't discipline."

"Neither is fear," I said, "and fear is all Dray's men have. Fifty men guarding one girl in a cage in the middle of nowhere, waiting for a man whose songs say he speaks with the mouths of beasts. What do you suppose they're imagining tonight? Every snap of a twig is me. Every shadow on the tent canvas." I met her eyes. "Their captain taught them to be afraid of exactly the thing I am. That's not a weakness I have to create, Sera. He built it himself."

For a moment neither of us spoke. The fire had burned down to a red eye. Lior lay with its chin on its paws, watching us both, and I had the odd sense of being weighed—of the fox measuring whether the bearer was finally beginning to understand what the covenant asked.

Sera reached over and put her hand over mine, calloused palm against my knuckles, and said, "First light tomorrow. But Rafael—if it goes wrong, if the net starts closing, you take Maren and you run, and you don't look back for me. Swear it."

"I don't make promises I intend to break."

"Swear it anyway."

"I swear I'll get her out," I said. "All of us out. I'm done with the part of the story where somebody stays behind."

She held my gaze, and I watched her decide not to fight it—watched her fold that argument away with all the others, the way she folded away everything, a woman carrying a lifetime of unsaid things in perfect military order.

It was the owl, returning an hour past midnight, wings silent as falling snow, who ended the argument for good. It landed on my shoulder and gave me the picture whole: the cage wagon, the ash-salt ring, Maren curled on the floor with her knees drawn up—alive, awake, glaring at her captors with an expression I recognized from thirty years of family dinners—and beside the wagon, giving orders in a low voice to a circle of lantern-lit officers, a tall man with a grey streak in his beard and a face like a ledger.

And the owl gave me one thing more. The wagons were being loaded. Harnesses going on. At dawn, the whole camp would move.

South. Toward the capital. Toward Veylan.

One day had just become zero.
