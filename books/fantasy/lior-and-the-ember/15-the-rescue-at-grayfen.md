## 15. The Rescue at Grayfen

The fen woke me before dawn, which was rude of it.

I'd been dreaming of Hollowmere—of the smell of hay and lamp oil in the stable, of Maren's terrible singing through the floorboards—and then a heron screamed somewhere out in the reeds and the dream tore like wet paper. I lay there in the cold under our lean-to of woven branches, listening to the marsh talk to itself, and I understood that I would never sleep properly again as long as I lived. The world had too many voices now. It was like being handed every conversation in a city at once and told to find your own name in the noise.

"Rafael." Sera's whisper from three feet away, because she still didn't trust me not to sleepwalk into Dray's camp. "Herons again?"

"Heron," I said. "One. But he's chatty."

"What's he saying?"

"That there are men on the causeway. Forty, maybe more. Torches banked low. And that they have a girl with them who bites."

Sera went very still in the way she did—not the stillness of fear but of a blade being set down carefully so it doesn't ring against anything. "Maren bites?"

"When she's frightened. When she's angry. Once, when she was bored." I sat up. "It runs in the family. Our mother bit a tax collector."

"I'm going to need to hear that story someday."

"If we live, you'll hear all of them. That's the deal I've decided we're making."

She didn't answer that, which was its own kind of answer. Three days since Grayfen's name had come to us on the wind—literally on the wind, carried by a crow with a taste for drama—and three days of watching Dray's net settle over the marsh like a lid on a pot. He had Maren. He had broadcast it the way a man broadcasts anything: loudly, cruelly, and with an eye toward exactly which ears needed to burn. *Come for the girl or let her hang as a conspirator.* He knew I wouldn't stay away. Mercy had been reported in my company; mercy was now a crime; and Dray, who understood arithmetic better than most inquisitors, had calculated that love makes people stupid.

He wasn't wrong. I just intended to be stupid in ways he hadn't priced in.

* * *

Grayfen itself was beautiful the way a knife is beautiful, if you're the sort of person who finds knives beautiful, which I apparently now was. Black water threaded between hummocks of sedge, mist lying on it like something sleeping. The causeway ran straight through the middle—a raised road of old stone, half-drowned, built by someone centuries ago who had either great ambition or no respect for water. Dray's witch-hunters held the causeway's midpoint: a ruined tollhouse with walls thick enough to stop arrows and a cage in the yard where they'd put my sister, because Dray wanted me to see her. Wanted me to look at what disobedience cost before I paid it.

Forty men. Crossbows. Dogs—and this was the part that made my stomach turn, because the dogs were mine to talk to whether I wanted to or not. Fen-hounds, big and gray and miserable, chained near the tollhouse. They'd been screaming at me in their blunt, hungry way since midnight: *meat, cold, chain, master-who-kicks.*

"You're doing the face," Lior said. The fox sat on the hummock beside me, tail wrapped around its feet, looking—as always—like the only creature in the marsh having a pleasant morning. "The one where you apologize to things that haven't been hurt yet."

"I'm apologizing in advance. It's efficient."

"To the dogs?"

"To everyone. This is going to be loud, Lior. Not sword-loud. Me-loud. Every bird, every fish, every eel in this fen is going to know my name by noon, and after that there's no valley small enough to hide in."

Lior's eyes caught the pre-dawn light and threw it back amber. "You said that about Bellharbor. You said it about the river crossing. You keep announcing the end of your invisibility and then remaining invisible."

"This time is different."

"Mm," said the fox, in the tone of a creature that had watched eleven bearers say *this time is different* and had opinions about the survival rate.

Sera crouched beside me, armor left behind, dressed in the drab wool of a fen-fisher, her hair muddied to match. Even disguised she moved like a knight, all economy and intent, and I caught myself—again—watching her hands. There was something wrong with me. Well. Something else.

"Say it again," she said. "All of it. If I'm going to bet my neck on a plan that involves asking frogs for help, I want to hear it sober, twice."

"Frogs are very reliable," I said. "Frogs have never once betrayed anyone."

"Rafael."

"Fine. The plan." I took a breath. "Dray expects me to come sneaking. Sneaking is what the map-bearer does; it's in his doctrine, probably written down somewhere with a diagram. So he's watching the dark edges, the reed beds, the water. Everything quiet. Which means we don't be quiet." I pointed out across the fen, east, where the sky was just beginning to consider the idea of light. "At first light, every bird in Grayfen rises at once. Not scattered—*all of them*, a wall of wings, heading west over the tollhouse. Geese, ducks, herons, everything. It'll sound like the sky tearing. Every crossbow goes up. Every eye goes up. And while forty men are staring at a miracle—" I tapped my own chest "—the eels take the chains off the dogs' stakes, the otters open the cage lock, and Maren walks into the water and swims. She's a farm girl. Farm girls swim like otters anyway. The otters will escort her. She won't even get her boots wet."

"And the dogs?"

"The dogs go mad. All at once, all directions, dragging their stakes out of the ground because the eels loosened them. Dray's line breaks trying to hold leashes that aren't attached to anything. Nobody gets cut. Nobody gets shot. By the time the birds land, we're gone north with Maren and there's nothing to chase but mud."

Silence. Mist. Somewhere an eel, listening, radiated what I can only describe as professional interest.

"That's the plan," Sera said flatly.

"Yes."

"It relies on every animal in a four-mile fen obeying you simultaneously."

"Yes."

"Otters opening a lock."

"Otters are *extremely* good with their hands. You'd know this if you talked to them."

"I don't talk to them, Rafael, I eat them—" She stopped. Rubbed the bridge of her nose. And then, quietly, in the voice she only used when something true was getting out past the guard: "My whole life, power meant ranks of men. Formations. Orders shouted down a line. And you're telling me you can do what an army does by *asking*."

"I'm telling you I can ask. Whether they say yes is up to them. That's the whole covenant, Lior says. Asking. It's slower than commanding. It's also—" I shrugged, embarrassed by my own sincerity. "It's the only version I trust."

Lior made a small sound that might have been approval or indigestion. With foxes, historically, same thing.

"All right," Sera said. "But if the otters fail, I'm going in after her myself, and then the no-blood part of the plan dies with my patience."

"Noted," I said. "Try to be gentle with the inquisitors."

"No promises."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and I opened myself to the fen.

People think speaking to beasts is like talking. It isn't. It's like standing in a doorway in a storm and letting the weather decide how much of you gets soaked. I reached out and Grayfen poured in: ten thousand small lives, each one a lit window. Frogs counting themselves smug in the shallows. Pike hanging in the black water like thoughts nobody finished. A marsh harrier riding the wind with the patience of a landlord. And underneath it all, the dogs—forty pounds of hunger and confusion each, eight of them, chained in the yard, dreaming dog dreams of running.

*I need you,* I said, to all of it. Not a command. I don't actually know how to command; maybe that's my defect, maybe it's the point. *A girl is in a cage. She bites, but she's kind. Help me bring her home, and no one bleeds today.*

And the fen—the fen *answered*. That's the part I will never manage to explain to anyone who wasn't there. It wasn't obedience. It was a crowd hearing music. The geese lifted first, a hundred strong off the eastern mere, honking like the end of the world, and the ducks went up with them, and the herons unfolded on legs like folded ladders, and the harrier screamed once, high and wild, and the whole sky over Grayfen became a single moving wall of feathers heading west across the causeway.

Below us, the tollhouse erupted. Horns. Shouting. "LOOSE! LOFT AND LOOSE!" Crossbow strings snapped in chorus and bolts went up into a sky suddenly full of targets that were mostly other birds' problem. I hated that part—I felt every near-miss like a spark on my own skin—but the geese were furious rather than afraid, wheeling and diving, and no hunter alive keeps his aim while a goose the size of a dog is expressing its opinion of him at speed.

"Now," Sera breathed.

The water moved. I felt it move—an eel the length of my arm slipping through the flooded foundations beneath the dog stakes, worrying the anchor-lines loose with the patient malice of its kind; two others at the cage, working the old padlock's hasp while an otter stood on the cage roof like a foreman offering encouragement. In the yard, the hounds came awake all at once, and I gave them the only gift I had: *run.* Eight gray shapes hit the ends of their chains, found the chains no longer anchored, and exploded into chaos—tangling legs, overturning a cookfire, scattering a picket line, generally achieving more mayhem per pound than any siege engine in history.

Through the smoke and feathers, in the yard, the cage door swung open.

And Maren—my sister, twenty years old, mud to the knees, hair like a haystack that had lost an argument with weather—stepped out, looked around at the apocalypse, looked directly at the nearest witch-hunter (a young one, frozen, crossbow half-raised), and *bit* him. On the hand. Then she ran for the water.

I have never been prouder of anyone in my life.

"She's swimming," I said, laughing, half-sobbing. "Sera, she's—she's swimming, the otters are with her, she's *riding* one, that's not necessary, otters, she knows how to swim—"

"Get down," Sera said, and hauled me flat as a bolt cracked through the reeds where my head had been.

Because here is the thing about miracles: they end. The birds spent themselves, settling onto the western meres in ragged, victorious clouds. The dogs, free, did what dogs do and vanished into the fens to found whatever new lives dogs found. And Dray's men—rattled, furious, forty of them—turned as one toward the reed bed where three fugitives crouched, and began to advance.

"Time to go," Lior said pleasantly. "North. Quickly. Bring the sister, leave the sentimentality, sentimentality drowns."

We ran. Through sedge that cut like razors, across channels where the pike—bless their cold hearts—rose to break our scent-trail in the water behind us. We reached the deep channel where Maren was wading out, soaked, wild-eyed, alive, and she saw me and made a sound I'll carry to my grave, half laugh and half cry, and then she hit me at a dead run and knocked us both into two feet of fen water, which is how my family hugs.

"You absolute idiot," she gasped into my shoulder. "You came. You came, you lunatic, they said you'd come, I told them you'd come and the mean one got so *angry*—"

"Which mean one? Tall? Sad eyes? Voice like a door hinge?"

"Inquisitor Dray."

"Then we should probably keep moving," Sera said dryly, "before the door hinge catches up," and Maren pulled back and stared at her, and stared at me, and I watched my sister arrive at approximately nine conclusions in the space of two seconds, several of which involved my love life, all of which were correct.

"Later," I said. "Everything, later. Run now."

* * *

We lost them in the deep fen by noon, and that was when I learned the second thing the day had come to teach me.

Because word travels faster than witches. By the time we reached the drover's track north of the marsh, the story had already grown legs. A carter heading south told us it without knowing who he told it to: how at Grayfen the birds had risen like a judgment, how the hounds had turned on their masters, how a girl had walked out of an iron cage untouched while the water itself conspired. How the beast-speaker of Hollowmere—the last heir of the old covenant, some were saying, though where they'd gotten *that* phrase I couldn't imagine—had freed his sister without shedding a drop of blood.

"They're singing it already," the carter said cheerfully, chewing his bread. "Got a song. *The Fen-Wife's Deliverance*, they're calling it in the villages. My wife sings it. Badly."

"Badly," Maren repeated faintly, once the carter had rattled on. "There's a song. Rafa. There's a *song* about you. About *us*. I'm in the song. Am I in the song?"

"You bite a man in the song," Lior said. "It's the best verse."

"There's a bounty too," the carter added helpfully, over his shoulder, already leaving. "Hundred crowns. Two hundred if taken breathing. Veylan wants the speaker alive, queen's loyalists want him breathing too, and the Ashguard—" he shrugged "—Ashguard pays either way. Everyone wants a piece of the fen-miracle!"

We stood in the road and watched him go, and the silence among the four of us had a specific flavor. Triumph, yes—Maren was alive, unchained, unbled, and I would do the whole mad morning again twice over for that alone. But underneath the triumph, cold water rising:

Everyone wanted a piece.

"Songs travel faster than horses," Sera said quietly. "Bounties travel faster than songs. Rafael—invisibility was never coming back after Bellharbor. But this—" she gestured back toward the fen, the invisible scene of the miracle "—this burned the last of it. Every ear in three kingdoms is pricked toward you now. Dray. Amara. Veylan himself, eventually. There is no valley, no road, no fen remote enough."

"I know," I said.

And I did know. What I knew, deeper than knowing, was that it didn't matter—that hiding had never actually saved anyone, it had only delayed the bill. The valley's silence hadn't protected the courier who died at the ford. My silence hadn't protected the beasts whose voices I'd swallowed for thirty years. There was no safe smallness left to buy. There was only forward, and the Ember ahead of us, and every throne in the world turning its head toward the sound of my name.

"Well," said Maren, wringing out her sleeve with the grim competence of a woman who had recently bitten an inquisitor's man and would clearly do it again, "if there's a song, we'd best make sure the ending's a good one. Where exactly are we walking, brother?"

Ahead of us the road climbed away from the fens, north and east, toward hills I could feel even from here—old hills, with something old sleeping under them. Lior stood at the roadside, ears forward, tail perfectly still, and when it spoke, the flippancy was gone from its voice entirely.

"Toward the mountain," the fox said. "First Fire. We're close enough now that I can feel it. And Rafael—so can everyone else. We are no longer the only ones who know the way."

Behind us, far off across the marsh, a horn sounded. Then another. Then, faint but unmistakable, carried on the wind like a promise and a threat at once—someone was singing.
