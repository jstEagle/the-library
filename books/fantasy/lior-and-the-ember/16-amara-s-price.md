## 16. Amara's Price

I have knelt before queens before. It is a skill, like swordwork—you learn where to put your weight, how to lower your eyes without lowering your guard. I learned it at fifteen in the Ashguard barracks, kneeling to a portrait of Veylan because the real man was too busy burning things to visit us.

Kneeling to Amara was different. That was the trap of her.

She didn't want the knee. She waved it away before I'd finished bending, caught my elbow, and lifted me like we were old friends sharing a joke about ceremony.

"Get up, Sera Vane. I've read your file. Kneeling is for people who haven't bled for me yet."

*For me.* Not for the crown. Not for the realm. For *her*. I noticed it the way you notice a hairline crack in a blade—small enough to ignore, deep enough to matter later.

We were in a hunting lodge in the hills above Grayfen, all dark beams and mounted stag heads and the particular smell of wealth pretending to be modesty. Rafael had been taken ahead of us an hour ago—taken *graciously*, which is its own kind of violence—and I stood in the great room while Amara poured wine with her own hands, because queens who pour their own wine want you to remember it forever.

"You look like a woman doing arithmetic," she said, handing me a cup.

"I'm deciding whether to drink it."

"Drink it. If I wanted you dead, you'd never see the cup coming." She smiled, and it was a good smile, warm as a hearth, and I thought: *Rafael knelt to this. Or almost did. Or wanted to.*

That was three days ago. I am writing this in my head because there is no paper I trust and no hand I would leave it in, and because the act of composing sentences keeps me from screaming. So let me set down what happened in that lodge, in order, plainly, the way you'd write a report for an officer who might court-martial you for it.

Which, in a sense, I am.

* * *

The first two days were almost pleasant. That's the thing about Amara they never tell you—the exiled queen in the ballads is gaunt and holy, eating roots in the snow. The real one laughed easily, remembered soldiers' names, ate stew from the common pot and made sure everyone saw her do it. She asked Rafael about the mare in Hollowmere. The actual mare, by name, having read the courier reports. Rafael lit up like a lamp, and I watched my own heart do something stupid and unsoldierly in my chest.

She gave him a room with a fire. She gave him maps and honest questions. She never once said *swear*—not on the road, not at supper, not when he told her about Lior dimming like a coal going out.

On the third day she said it.

Not to Rafael. To me. Alone, in the mews, where she'd asked me to meet her among the hawks. I should have known then. You don't discuss oaths in a room full of hooded birds unless you intend someone to be one.

"The Ember," she said, "will be bound to my bloodline. Sole heirship, sealed in the binding itself. No council, no succession war, no ambitious cousin with a private army. One flame, one line, one law." She said it the way she said everything—reasonably, warmly, as if the alternative were not merely wrong but rude.

"And if he refuses?"

"He won't. He's a good man, and good men want to be useful. But I'm not telling you this because of him." She turned, and the hawk-light made her eyes flat and bright. "I'm telling you because of you."

"Me."

"When the binding is done, loose ends get gathered. It's not cruelty, Sera. It's hygiene." She said the word *hygiene* the way other people say *mercy*. "You've defected once. You missed your killing stroke at the river—I know, don't make the face, Dray writes very thorough reports. You burned your commission. You share a bedroll with the bearer of the most dangerous object in the world and you're in love with him, which makes you either my greatest asset or my greatest liability, and assets can become liabilities overnight."

"So the order is—"

"There's no order yet. There's a conversation, happening now, between friends." She touched my arm. Her hands were soft. Queens' hands are always softer than you expect; you spend years imagining them calloused by justice, and then you hold one and it's like holding bread. "Give me something. A vow renewed, witnessed. Stand at his side when he kneels and swear the Ember to my line, and you're not a loose end anymore. You're a founder."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you'll be reassigned." She smiled again, that hearth-warmth, and beneath it, faint as a watermark: *or disposed of*. "I'd hate that. I genuinely would. I think you're remarkable."

Here is what I should tell you about being raised by an order: they teach you to read orders, terrain, faces. They do not teach you what to do when all three agree that the person smiling at you means to kill you, gently, eventually, for the good of everyone.

I said, "Let me speak with him first."

"Of course." Warm. Generous. And then, as I reached the door: "Sera. The guards will be doubled tonight. For his protection, naturally. He's very precious to the cause."

*Naturally.*

* * *

I found Rafael on the lodge roof, because of course he was on the roof. Three weeks on the road and the man still hadn't learned that roofs are for people with nothing better to hunt them than witch-hunters. Lior was curled in his lap, and even from the ladder I could see the fox had gone thin—not thin in body, thin in *substance*, edges gone slightly translucent, like a coin worn past reading.

He looked up and did the thing he does, the thing that ruined me: he saw me exactly, all at once, no performance required.

"What happened?" he said.

"Queening," I said. "It happened upstairs. It's happening everywhere. It's a plague."

He waited. He's good at waiting. Beasts taught him that, I think—that most creatures say what they mean if you give them silence enough to fill.

So I told him. All of it. The bloodline clause, the sole-heir binding, the word *hygiene*, the arithmetic of my usefulness. I told him about the doubled guard and the phrase *very precious to the cause*, and I watched him go through it the way he goes through everything—slowly, honestly, refusing the easy exits.

"She sounds like Veylan," he said finally.

"She *is* Veylan. That's the whole discovery. I spent six years thinking the difference between usurper and rightful queen was the difference between wolf and shepherd." I sat down against the chimney stack. Below us, the valley held its breath under early frost. "It's the difference between two wolves arguing over whose sheep they are."

"Amara fed the village at Thornford," he said quietly. "She paid reparations. She freed the press-ganged boys."

"Yes."

"She cried at the mass grave."

"Yes. And meant it. That's the worst part, Rafael. She's not lying. She believes every kind thing she does, and she believes the cruel ones are kindness too, and there is no daylight between those beliefs for her. She'll free every press-ganged boy in the kingdom and bind the Ember to her grandchildren's grandchildren, and both acts will feel, to her, like the same mercy."

Lior opened one eye. When the fox speaks these days it costs him; the voice comes out papery, small, a wind through reeds.

"I have seen eleven bearers," Lior said. "Two were tyrants. Nine were good people. The nine frightened me more."

"Why?" Rafael asked, though I think he already knew. I think he'd known since Bellharbor, maybe since the river.

"Because tyrants know they are taking. Good people believe they are giving. You cannot argue a gift out of someone's hands. They only tighten."

Rafael looked down at the fox a long moment. Then at me.

"We leave tonight," he said.

"Not tonight," I said. "Now. While the guard rotation changes. I watched them set it—there's a gap at the woodshed side, forty counts, maybe fifty."

He stared at me. "You've been planning our escape since the mews."

"I've been planning escapes since I was twelve. It's the one subject the Ashguard covered thoroughly." I stood, offered him my hand, and he took it, and for one moment on that cold roof with a dying fox-spirit in his lap, holding my hand, he smiled—a real one, tired and crooked—and I thought: *this. This is the thing worth being a traitor twice over for.*

Then the bell in the yard began to ring, and the moment went the way moments do.

* * *

They knew. Of course they knew. Amara had never intended to let me walk out of that mews conversation; the conversation itself had been the test, and I had failed it by asking to speak with him first. Love, reported accurately, is evidence.

What saved us was that she expected obedience to be slow. Queens surround themselves with people who take an hour to betray them—time to pack, time to pray, time to say goodbye. She budgeted for that hour. We used forty seconds of it.

Down the ladder, through the kitchen where a scullery boy looked up and then, deliberately, looked back down at his pots—bless him, whoever he is, may his bread always rise—out the woodshed door into the gap in the rotation, across frozen mud, into trees. Behind us the bell kept ringing, patient and furious, and torches bloomed along the lodge walls like a rash.

We ran. I ran the way I was trained, choosing ground, breaking line of sight, and Rafael ran the way beasts run, low and silent and utterly certain, and Lior—Lior burned. There's no other word for it. The fox poured himself out along the forest floor like a spill of embers, and every hound in Amara's kennels suddenly remembered urgent business elsewhere, and every horse in her pickets decided the night air disagreed with them, and the trackers lost our trail where the trail was plainest, because the trail itself had opinions now.

"He's killing himself," Rafael gasped, as we crouched in a streambed a mile out, listening to horns argue behind us.

"He's spending," I corrected. "There's a difference. Barely." I took stock: two knives, a bow with nine arrows, half a loaf, no map—the map was in Rafael's head now, had been since Bellharbor, which was the only reason stealing it back from the council had mattered. "We need distance and darkness and a story. In that order."

"What story?"

"The true one. It's the only kind I can tell without my face doing something treasonous."

* * *

We walked through the night and most of the next day, off every road, guided by Rafael's conversations with crows, who are gossips but reliable ones if you flatter them. Somewhere in the second afternoon, in the lee of a fallen oak, Rafael stopped walking and said, "Show me."

"Show you what?"

"Whatever you're carrying that's making you walk like you swallowed a stone. You've been carrying it since the lodge. Since before the lodge, maybe. Since the mews." He sat down on the oak root, pulled me down beside him, and waited, the way he waits.

So I showed him.

Not papers—I had no papers. What I had was memory, and memory is heavier. I told him about the war council at Thornford, before the lodge, when I'd still been deciding whether Amara was the answer to everything Veylan had broken. I'd stood at the back and heard her plan the Ember's use, and it wasn't torture chambers or fire armies—it was worse, it was *administration*. Grain quotas enforced by weather. Rebellions ended before they began, because every raven in the kingdom would whisper to her throne. A realm where nothing could hide, run by a woman who genuinely believed hiding was suffering and exposure was safety.

"A kinder tyranny," I said. "That's the proof I carry. Not a document. A pattern. Every choice she's made since I joined her bends the same direction—toward gentleness, and toward total control, and she cannot see that those are opposites. She thinks she's healing the world. She's planning to bandage its mouth."

Rafael was quiet for a long time. A crow sat overhead and, I swear, listened with more tact than most humans I've known.

"My sister used to say," he finally said, "that the most dangerous person in any village is the one who's right about everything. Because they stop checking." He rubbed his eyes. "I nearly knelt to her, Sera. At supper on the second night, she talked about Hollowmere—about making sure no valley ever has to be magic-dead again, no child ever has to hide a gift like mine—and I felt my knee want to bend. I wanted to belong somewhere so badly I could taste it."

"I know. I watched."

"And you didn't stop me."

"No," I admitted. "Because stopping you would've been another person deciding for you. I've had enough of that to last several lifetimes." I looked at him, at the frost in his hair, at the translucent fox asleep in his collar like a scarf made of dying light. "But I'm telling you now, as the woman who loves you and the knight who's betrayed two crowns: no throne deserves the fire. Not hers. Not his. Not any that's coming. The moment it sits on a throne it stops being a flame and starts being a law, and laws don't warm anyone—they just burn according to schedule."

He nodded slowly. Then, wry despite everything, despite the horns we could still sometimes hear: "You know this is the second time you've talked me out of a monarchy. People will talk."

"Let them. I've been talked about by professionals."

He laughed—short, cracked, real—and leaned his shoulder into mine, and for a minute we just breathed, two fugitives in the cold, rich beyond counting in the only currency that had ever mattered to either of us.

Then the crow on the branch above us said, in its rusted voice, words Rafael translated with his face going white:

*Riders. Many. Red cloaks. Coming fast along the river road. And they have a prisoner on a horse, and the prisoner has your chin, beast-speaker.*

Maren.

Dray hadn't been chasing us at all. He'd been herding us—straight toward the one thing we couldn't ride past.

Rafael was on his feet before the crow finished, and I was up beside him, and somewhere behind the fallen oak the last light of a dying fox flickered like a struck match deciding whether to catch.

"How many riders?" Rafael asked the sky.

The crow answered. It was not a number I liked.
