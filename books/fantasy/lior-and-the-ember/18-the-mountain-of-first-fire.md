## 18. The Mountain of First Fire

*Rafael*

The mountain did not want us there.

I want to be clear about that, because the songs later made it sound like a pilgrimage—brave souls ascending toward glory, trumpets, etc. The truth is that the Mountain of First Fire hated us with the patient, geological hatred of a thing that has been waiting ten thousand years for someone stupid enough to climb it.

The path was not a path so much as a grudge. It switchbacked up a face of black stone that drank the daylight and gave nothing back, and every step was slick with a frost that shouldn't have existed in high summer. Lior walked ahead of us, and where the fox stepped, the frost melted. That was the problem. That was the whole terrible arithmetic of the last three days: wherever Lior went, the way opened, and wherever Lior had been, the fox was smaller.

On the morning of the fourth day I finally said it out loud.

"You're dying."

Lior stopped on a ledge no wider than a coffin lid and turned back to look at me. A fox-spirit's face is not built for expression—it's built for inscrutability, the way a knife is built for cutting—but I'd learned to read it anyway. What I saw then was annoyance, mostly, and underneath it something that in a person I would have called fear.

"I am *spending*," Lior said. "There is a difference. Dying is what happens to things that end. I am being poured out. It is a much more dignified process."

"It looks identical from down here."

"Then look away." The fox flicked an ear toward the summit, hidden in cloud. "Two days more. Perhaps three. The cradle opens for the bearer, not for me. After that you may bury me under whatever rock has the best view."

"We're not burying you."

"No," Lior agreed, and started walking again. "You are going to do something far worse. You always were."

I didn't ask what that meant. I'd stopped asking Lior questions I wasn't sure I wanted answered somewhere around the Free City, when the answers had started arriving like bills.

Behind me, Sera climbed in silence. She'd been silent for two days—not cold, exactly, but coiled, the way she got before a fight she'd already decided to win. Every hour or so she'd stop, put two fingers to the black stone, and listen. On the third finger-to-stone she said, without turning around:

"They're below us. Dray's column. Half a day back, maybe less—they've found the goat track."

"And above?"

"Above is worse." She stood, brushed frost from her knee. "Amara's loyalists took the high pass yesterday. Which means we're between them, which means—" she glanced at me, and her mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling, one corner lifting like a door opening onto a room she didn't let people see, "—which means we should climb faster."

I looked at the ledge ahead, at the drop beneath it, at the cloud swallowing the summit. "Faster."

"Faster," Sera confirmed.

So we climbed. And all around us, in the cracks and hollows of the mountain, the small lives of the stone listened. Marmots in their burrows. A lammergeier riding thermals it could no longer feel warming. Snow hares gone white in a season that had forgotten them. They pressed against my awareness like children at a window, because word travels among beasts faster than among men, and somewhere below the tree line a wren had told a chough had told a raven that the Listener was climbing the First Fire, and the ones who could still feel anything—the ones the purges hadn't reached, the ones too small and too far from thrones to matter—had come to watch.

They didn't help. That's what I told myself, anyway, when I felt them gathering. They only watched. But the ledges held when they shouldn't have. The frost thinned along our route and thickened behind us, and once, when Sera's boot skidded on scree above a drop that would have ended our story early, a hundred small bodies shifted at once and the scree settled under her like a hand catching a cup before it falls.

Sera stared at the slope. Then at me.

"They like you," she said, in the tone of someone reporting a tactical liability.

"They're bored," I said. "It's been a quiet century."

* * *

*Sera*

I had killed men on better mountains than this one, and worse ones, and I want it recorded—I am writing this by firelight while Rafael sleeps, so it will be recorded—that none of those climbs had ever been like this. When the Ashguard takes a height, it takes it with ropes and iron and forty men who hate each other slightly less than they hate the enemy. We had a fox that was spending itself into nothing, a satchel containing either the salvation of the world or its final enslavement depending entirely on the next three days, and Rafael Eriksen, who climbed like a man who had learned his footing on a farmyard and refused to update his methods.

He also sang. Badly. Under his breath, some valley tune about a miller's daughter, and the marmots whistled along, and I decided that if we survived I was going to teach him actual songs, and then I caught myself planning a future and had to grip the rock until my knuckles went white.

Because here is the thing I had not said aloud, to him or to the dark or to the god I'd stopped believing in around the time I put a sword through a child: I loved him. I loved him the way you love a wound that taught you your own shape. And on this mountain, with Dray's column grinding up behind us and Amara's loyalists descending from the pass like judgment, love had become the most dangerous thing I carried. Heavier than the map. Heavier than the sword.

We made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, out of the wind, and Lior curled up between us and did not curl quite all the way. There was space around the fox now, a faintness, like a candle burned down inside its own glass. I had seen men spend themselves like that. I had been the reason for some of them.

"The loyalists will reach the saddle by midmorning," I said, feeding the tiny fire we allowed ourselves. "Dray will reach the treeline tonight. He moves fast when he's angry, and he's been angry since Bellharbor."

"Then we sleep fast too," Rafael said, and lay back on the stone with his hands behind his head as though we were picnicking, which is either courage or brain damage, and after four months of him I still hadn't determined which.

"Sera."

"Mm."

"Come here. You're sitting like a sentry and I'm tired of talking to your profile."

"I *am* a sentry."

"You're off duty. Dray's half a mountain away and everything else on this rock is a herbivore." He patted the stone beside him with the shameless confidence of a man who had never once considered rejection might apply to him. It was infuriating. It had been infuriating for months. I went and sat beside him, because I am apparently also brain-damaged.

For a while neither of us spoke. The wind worked at the overhang. Below us, faint as a bad memory, I could almost hear the Ashguard's drums—or imagined I could; Dray didn't use drums, he used silence, which was worse.

"When this is done," Rafael said quietly, "whatever happens up there—when it's done—I'm going back to Hollowmere. Or what's left of it. Maren will need—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm asking you to come with me. Not as a guard. Not as penance. As—"

"As what?" My voice came out harder than I meant. Old habit. "Rafael, I executed a child. Your beasts may have forgiven you for loving me, but I haven't—"

"I know what you did."

"—and Amara wants me dead, and Veylan wants me leashed, and every road out of this story ends with my name on a list—"

"I know," he said again, and took my hand, and turned it over, and looked at the calluses the way he looked at everything: like it was telling him the truth whether it wanted to or not. "I know all of it. I've known since Grayfen. I'm not asking the knight. I'm asking the woman who hums when she thinks no one's listening. She hums off-key, incidentally. Terribly. The hares have complained."

Despite everything—despite the drums I couldn't hear and the fox dimming between us and the morning coming up the mountain with knives in it—I laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, like a bird flushed from cover.

"Sera Winterbourne," Rafael said.

"That's not my name."

"It could be. If you wanted. I'm told the valley doesn't ask many questions." He lifted my hand, pressed his mouth to the knuckles, scarred and sworn and shaking, and said, simply, without any of his usual infuriating humor: "I love you. I've loved you since the river crossing, when you pointed a sword at me and apologized to the horse. I'll love you tomorrow whether we live or not, and I wanted you to have it before the top of the mountain, in case the top of the mountain has opinions."

The wind moved. Somewhere below, real this time, a horn sounded—one long note, Dray's note, the signal for *found them*.

I kissed him. It was not graceful. We were both exhausted and half-frozen and I knocked my elbow on stone, and it was the truest thing I have ever done, and I told him so, or tried to, in fragments, against his mouth: *yes, and yes, and yes.*

Above us, Lior opened one eye, observed us with the weary satisfaction of a matchmaker whose work is nearly finished, and closed it again.

* * *

*Rafael*

We reached the saddle at dawn, and that was where the mountain stopped pretending.

The path beyond the saddle was not carved. It was *burned*—a stairway of glassy fused stone spiraling up through a chimney in the peak, glowing faintly from within, warm underfoot after days of frost. The first fire had come down this way once, Lior said, in the world's childhood, and the rock had never forgotten the shape of it.

Lior could barely walk. The fox had gone translucent at the edges, light showing through like sun through a thin coin, and each step up the burning stair cost something I could see leaving. At the halfway rest—a shelf where the chimney widened—Lior sat down without being invited, which the fox had never once done, and did not get up quickly.

"How much is left?" I asked.

"Enough." The fox's voice had gone thin too, paper over embers. "Bearer. Listen. When we reach the chamber, there will be a moment—all factions converging, all voices speaking—and in that moment the Ember will ask you one question. It will not ask it in words. It will ask it the way the mare asked you whether she should keep living, the day you found her lame. Do you remember what you answered?"

I remembered. I'd knelt in the mud of my sister's paddock with a ruined leg in front of me and a farmer's pragmatism in my throat, and the mare had looked at me with those enormous liquid eyes, and I had said, *that's not mine to decide. But if it were mine, I'd want to see another summer.* And the mare had decided to see another summer.

"Yes," I said.

"Good." Lior laid its chin on its paws. "Every bearer before you heard the question and answered with themselves. *Give it to me. Give it to my queen. Give it to my god.* The answer it is waiting for has never once been spoken." The fox's tail curled, once, a gesture I had come to know as the closest thing it had to tenderness. "You are the last thread, Rafael. Threads exist to be pulled."

From below, faint but rising, came the sound of iron on stone. Dray's vanguard, entering the burning stair. From above, a thin whistle—Amara's loyalists signaling across the saddle.

Sera stood, rolled her shoulders, drew her sword, and looked at me with an expression I intended to spend the rest of my life learning to read.

"Together?" she said.

"Together," I said.

And Lior rose—dim, spent, luminous—took one step up the burning stair, and the whole mountain exhaled, and the way to the cradle opened like a throat about to speak.
