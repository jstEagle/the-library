## 20. Aftermath of a Common Miracle

I have killed men for less than what a milk cow did last spring.

That is not an exaggeration I'm proud of, but it is the truth, and Rafael has taught me that truth is cheaper than silence in the long run. The cow belonged to a widow outside Bellharbor—a gray, patient animal named Buttercup, which is the sort of name you give a cow when you intend to eat her eventually and don't want to feel bad about it. One morning she planted herself in front of the widow's hearth and refused to move, lowing like a dying saint, until the widow—cursing, confused—finally dragged her out by the halter and turned around to find her roof beam smoldering where a spark had lodged in the thatch.

The cow had known. Not guessed. *Known.*

The widow told everyone. Everyone told everyone else. And within a season, there were stories everywhere: geese that screamed before avalanches, dogs that could smell a lie souring on a man's breath, hearths in three villages that simply would not burn near children—flames leaning away from toddlers like courtiers avoiding a drunk lord. A miller's cat who led searchers to a lost child by walking a straight line through snow no one else could read.

Small things. Distributed things. Useless things, if what you wanted was an army.

Which is, I suspect, exactly why they worked.

* * *

The thrones did not take it well.

I had imagined—I think we all imagined—that when Rafael scattered the Ember, Veylan's claim would collapse like wet bread and Amara's loyalists would drift home, and the world would exhale. What actually happened was louder and stupider. Veylan raged from his capital that the flame had been *stolen from the crown*, as though fire could be property, as though he hadn't spent twenty years burning anyone who so much as hummed a spell. Amara issued a proclamation from exile calling it "the great betrayal of the rightful order," which was rich coming from a woman who'd ordered my death over breakfast. Both of them sent scholars scurrying into the mountain's ruins looking for remnants, fragments, embers of the Ember.

They found ash. Rafael had been thorough. He is a gentle man, but he has a farmer's understanding of seed—you don't hoard the harvest; you put it back in the ground.

The witch-hunters were the ones who took it worst, though it took me a while to understand why. You'd think men whose entire purpose was extinguishing magic would celebrate its dilution into milk cows and helpful cats. But Dray's Ashguard had never really been about extinguishing anything. They'd been about *owning* the fear of it. When magic lived only in marked people—in children with too-steady eyes, in women who talked to ravens—then fear had a face, and faces can be hunted, and hunters can be paid. But when the magic went into everything, into cows and chimneys and the common mud, fear lost its address. You cannot burn a village for harboring a storm-warning goose. Well. You can. Dray tried. But even his own men started asking what, precisely, they were protecting the realm from.

Irrelevance, it turns out, is a slow poison for institutions built on terror.

* * *

As for me: I was pardoned by nobody.

There was no decree, no seal, no formal restoration of my name. What happened instead was that the Ashguard stopped looking for me—not out of mercy but arithmetic. I was one defector among a hundred now. The order was hemorrhaging; half its knights had gone home to farms where their mothers' hearths refused to burn and their sisters' goats gave prophecy. Hunting me cost more than ignoring me. Somewhere in a ledger I will never see, someone decided Sera Vane was no longer worth the ink.

Rafael found this funny in a way I didn't appreciate at the time.

"You're free," he said, "because you're boring."

"I am not *boring*."

"You're statistically unremarkable." He was mending a bridle at the time, sitting on a fence rail in the foothills south of Grayfen, squinting against the light like he'd been born to it instead of a valley that forgot itself. "One defected knight among dozens. No bounty worth collecting. No story anyone tells twice."

"There are songs about us."

"There are songs about *him*," he said, nodding at Lior, who was asleep in a patch of sun with the profound commitment of a creature who had once been a fragment of divine fire and had since discovered naps. "You're just the woman standing next to him in the chorus."

I threw a apple core at his head. He caught it without looking up, which should have been endearing and was instead infuriating, and then he laughed—that quiet, surprised laugh he still seemed startled to own—and I let the argument die, because he wasn't wrong, and because being unremarkable next to him felt, for the first time in my life, like enough.

Here is what no one tells you about peace: it is terrifying. I had been a weapon since I was nine years old. Weapons do not wonder what to do with a Tuesday. For the first month after the mountain, after the chamber full of dying light and screaming lords, I woke every dawn reaching for a sword that I had deliberately left behind, and lay there in the grey listening to Rafael breathe, waiting for the panic to pass. Some mornings it didn't. On those mornings he didn't tell me I was safe—he knew better than to say that word to me—but he'd get up and make tea badly and talk about nothing, fence posts and weather and whether foxes could get fat, until the world reassembled itself around small stupid details and I could stand up without checking the windows.

Lior, for its part, adjusted to diminished divinity with insulting grace.

"It spent centuries as a fragment of the first fire," Rafael told me once, "and now its greatest ambition is to be fed by hand."

"That tracks," I said. "Power does that to people too. Give them everything and all they want is to be petted."

Lior opened one eye, considered me with the flat amber judgment only foxes and old sergeants can manage, and went back to sleep. It had lost most of its speech—or rather, it had chosen to spend it, pouring words into the path up the mountain until there was almost nothing left. Now it communicated in looks and tail-flicks and the occasional single word, deployed like a knife: *food*, *no*, *warm*, and once, memorably, when Rafael was being particularly self-pitying about his scarred hands, *drama*.

We were not, during those months, entirely idle. Word travels, and word of a beast-speaker who refused two crowns travels faster than most. People came. At first it was stragglers—a woman whose son heard voices in the wind, a shepherd whose flock had begun voting on directions. Then whole families. They came because the world had changed under them and no throne would explain how, and Rafael sat with them in farmyards and inn yards and explained what he understood, which was less than they hoped and more than they'd had: that the covenant was old, older than the kingdoms, that power had once been a conversation between all living things and some clever bastard had made it a monopoly, and that monopoly was broken now, and breaking things is easy but tending them is the work of a lifetime.

I watched him do it and thought: this is what I was trained to destroy, and it looks like a man kneeling in the mud showing a frightened boy how to listen to a horse.

* * *

In the autumn we went back toward Hollowmere. Not home—we weren't ready for that, and neither, I suspected, was the valley—but near enough to hear news of it. Maren wrote now, real letters carried by real couriers, and I learned to recognize her handwriting the way you learn a voice through a wall: sharp, slanted, impatient with margins.

*The valley isn't dead anymore,* she wrote. *The well tastes different. Old Tomas says the bees talk to him, which would have gotten him burned two years ago and now mostly gets him free honey. Mother's grave has wildflowers on it that don't grow anywhere else here. I don't know what to do with any of this. Come see for yourselves when you're done being famous.*

"We're not famous," Rafael said, reading over my shoulder.

"Your sister thinks you are."

"My sister thinks *everything* dramatically. She once staged a funeral for a hen."

But we went, in the way of people circling something they're afraid to touch. And on the road we saw the miracle doing its quiet work. A border town where the garrison had mutinied rather than enforce a purge order—their captain told us, sheepishly, that his horse had refused to carry him toward the marked quarter, and when your own horse files a moral objection, you start reconsidering your orders. A monastery where the monks had stopped copying anti-magic edicts because their ink kept fading from the page, which everyone agreed was either God or the Ember, and no one wanted to argue with either. Children everywhere—this was the thing that got me, the children—who had grown up in the purge years learning to hide anything strange, suddenly *showing off*. Whistling to crows. Asking questions. Being strange in daylight.

I had executed a child for that once. I have written about it nowhere and told Rafael everything, and both of those facts live side by side in me like cats who've agreed to a truce. In that border town I watched a girl make a candle flame dance for her friends, laughing, unafraid, and I had to walk away and stand by the horses for a while. Rafael came and stood with me. He didn't say it gets easier. He said, "She'll never know what it cost to make a world where she can laugh like that," and I said, "Good," and meant it, and he put his arm through mine and we went back in.

That is love, I think. Not the songs' version—all thunder and sacrifice on mountainsides. This version: standing by the horses. Making bad tea. Learning that someone's silences are not walls but rooms, and learning to knock.

* * *

Winter found us in the foothills above Grayfen, in a shepherd's cottage we rented off a woman who asked no questions because Rafael had healed her dog's hip and Lior had murdered her rat problem with medieval enthusiasm. We stayed longer than planned. That's how it goes now—days accumulate like snowfall, soft and unannounced.

It was there, in that cottage, that the letter came. Not Maren's handwriting. A seal I recognized: the free council of Bellharbor, requesting—requesting, mind you, the age of commanding us was apparently over—"the counsel of the Beast-Speaker" regarding "the regulation of distributed gifts." They wanted rules. Registries. A framework. All very reasonable, all very sensible, all very much the first bricks of a new monopoly being laid by well-meaning hands.

Rafael read it twice and set it on the table.

"They want to help," he said.

"They want to file you."

"Both can be true." He looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road. "Sera, if we say no, someone worse says yes. If we say yes—"

"You become the thing you scattered the Ember to prevent."

He was quiet a long time. Outside, the wind moved through the pines, and somewhere down the slope a ewe was complaining about the weather to anyone who would listen, which these days was everyone.

"What did you learn," I asked him, "from the mare? Back in the valley. Before all of this."

He smiled faintly. "That listening isn't the same as obeying. She told me what she needed. I decided what I could give. Neither of us owned the other."

"Then write them that."

So he did. It took him three days and four drafts, and what he finally sent was short enough to fit on one page: *Teach, don't register. Listen, don't legislate. Power that must be catalogued is power being collected. We will come speak to any council that asks, and we will leave when we choose.* And beneath it, in smaller script, the line I suspect history will remember better than anything else he ever wrote:

*A gift given to all belongs to none, and that is its protection.*

Whether Bellharbor listens—whether any of them listen—is a question for spring.

* * *

On the last night of the year, snow falling fat and silent past the window, Rafael sat across from me sharpening a kitchen knife that did not need sharpening, because his hands like work the way mine used to need a sword hilt, and Lior was a warm weight against my side on the bench, and I found myself doing arithmetic I'd never dared before.

Not how long until the next hunt. Not how many miles to the nearest garrison. Just: *how long has it been since I flinched at a knock?*

Three months. Maybe four.

"Sera." He'd noticed, of course. He always notices; it's the least martial talent he has and the most dangerous. "Where did you go?"

"Nowhere. I was counting."

"Counting what?"

"How long it's been since I was afraid."

He set the knife down. "And?"

"And it's been long enough that I'm now afraid of not being afraid, which I'm told is called healing and feels like a scam."

He laughed, and crossed the room, and sat beside me, and Lior grumbled and rearranged itself across both our laps with the magnanimity of a deposed king sharing a bench with peasants. Through the window the snow kept falling on a world that was quietly, stubbornly remaking itself—one cow, one hearth, one strange unashamed child at a time. Thrones were raging at power they couldn't own. Armies were dissolving into farms. Somewhere a widow was thanking a cow.

And here, in a rented cottage at the edge of the map, a knight with no banner and a speaker with no crown were learning, clumsily, day by day, how to be ordinary together—which may be the hardest discipline either of us ever attempted, and the only one I'd choose again.

"The council will write back," Rafael said into my hair. "And Maren wants us at midwinter planting. And there's a girl in the valley village who swears the river talks to her, and her parents don't know what to do."

"So we'll go," I said. "In spring. After the thaw."

"After the thaw," he agreed.

And then the knock came—two raps, polite, unfamiliar—and I felt the old cold start to rise in my chest, and then, remarkably, it didn't. Because Lior's head came up first, ears forward, and it looked at the door and then at us and said, clearly, in a voice it had supposedly spent on a mountainside:

"*Friend.*"

Rafael and I exchanged a look. We weren't expecting anyone. But the fox was smiling, insofar as foxes smile, and outside the snow was filling in the footprints of whoever stood on our step, and I reached for the latch thinking: irrelevance has its privileges, but curiosity, it turns out, is the one power that was never ours to scatter in the first place.

I opened the door.
