## 21. Homecoming

The valley announced itself the way it always had—not with a sign or a boundary stone, but with a change in the air, a thinning, like walking into a room where someone has just stopped talking.

Except this time the silence was different. This time it wasn't empty.

I felt it before I saw anything: a hundred small attentions turning toward me like flowers finding the sun. The hedgerow sparrows. A doe and her half-grown fawn in the barley stubble. Old Bramble, the miller's dog, who had spent eleven years barking at nothing and now, I realized with a start, had never been barking at nothing at all—he'd been trying to tell us about the moles under the millrace for a decade, and no one had listened.

*You're back,* said the doe, in the wordless way of deer, which is mostly a feeling of watchful patience. *The tall one is back.*

Not magic-dead anymore. That was the thing no one had warned me about—well, many people had warned me about many things, but not this particular ache. When I'd left Hollowmere, the valley had been a wall against everything I was. Now it was full of everything I was, and the wall was gone, and I stood on the near slope of it like a man returning to his childhood house to find the rooms rearranged and every door moved two inches to the left.

Home, but not home. Familiar, but speaking a language I'd only just learned.

Lior trotted ahead of me down the cart track, and I watched him carefully, because watching Lior had become a habit I didn't know how to break. He looked ordinary now. That was the strangest part of all of it—the fox who had stepped out of a map's margins, who had spoken in riddles that were somehow also instructions, who had burned himself down to a coal and then to an ember and then to almost nothing holding the path open on the mountain—that fox now looked like exactly what he appeared to be: a red fox with one white sock, a notch in his left ear, and the slightly disreputable air of an animal who has recently eaten something he shouldn't have.

He was clever. Genuinely, ordinarily clever—the kind of clever that let him open a latch if you showed him twice, and steal bread if you didn't. But the old knowing was gone. When I spoke to him now, he heard words, not meaning. He answered with a tilt of his head, a yip, the particular flat stare of a fox being asked a stupid question by a human.

I missed him. Which was absurd, because he was right there, and also because what I missed had never been a thing a person could keep.

"You could stay in Bellharbor," I'd told him, three days back. "They'd make you a monument. They're already arguing about where to put it."

He had looked at me with those amber eyes—just eyes now, just a fox's eyes—and then he had sat down on my boot, which was his way of ending conversations he considered beneath him.

So: homecoming.

* * *

Hollowmere had changed less than I expected and more than I feared.

The village proper was the same crooked collection of stone and thatch it had always been, huddled around its green like children around a fire. Smoke rose from chimneys. Someone was beating a rug over a fence rail with the rhythmic violence of the deeply contented. The forge hammer rang. If I squinted—which I did, deliberately, wanting to be wrong in the good direction—it might have been any evening of the eighteen years I'd lived here.

But there were small things. A girl of maybe seven sitting cross-legged in the grass, perfectly still, while a crow stood on her knee and they regarded each other with the mutual gravity of colleagues. An old man talking to his oxen not in the absent patter farmers use but in actual sentences, and the oxen—this was the part that made my throat tighten—actually leaning into their yokes, actually listening, working with him instead of despite him.

And everywhere, that hum. Not sound, exactly. More like the feeling of a house where many people are awake behind their doors.

"They'll know," Lior had said once, back when Lior could say things. "When the fire scattered, they'll all know what they are. Some of them will hate it. Most of them won't know what to do with it. All of them will need someone."

Someone. I'd thought he meant a teacher. Walking down into Hollowmere, I understood he'd meant something more tiring than that.

Word traveled ahead of me the way it does in villages—faster than a horse, slower than sense. By the time I reached the green, faces were appearing in doorways, and I got the full range of it: wonder from the children, suspicion from the elders, and from Tam Wicker, the blacksmith, a look of such profound vindication that I knew, before anyone said a word, that Tam had always suspected.

"Rafael Eriksen," he said, arms crossed, soot to the elbows. "Come back from the dead, have you?"

"Only mostly."

"Heard you broke the Ember." He said it the way you'd say *heard you kicked over the king's carriage*, which is to say with admiration and horror in equal parts. "Scattered it. Into animals. Into *people*." He shook his head slowly. "Half the kingdom wants your head and the other half wants your blessing, and you walk in here looking like you slept in a ditch."

"I did sleep in a ditch."

"Good," said Tam. "Keeps you humble." And then, gruffly, clapping my shoulder hard enough to rearrange it: "Your sister's been keeping your forge warm. Don't tell her I told you she cries at night. She'll deny it."

* * *

Maren was in the forge when I found her—or rather, Maren was standing in the doorway of the forge with a hammer in her hand and an expression on her face that I last saw on Sera's, the day she burned her commission: the expression of someone who has decided, several times, not to hope, and is furious at the world for making her decide again.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Hello to you too."

"The whole valley's been telling stories about you. Beast-speaker. Fire-scatterer. The man who refused a crown." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, and I watched her hate it. "Do you know what I've been doing? Keeping your accounts. Feeding your chickens. Telling myself you were alive because the alternative was—" She stopped. Set the hammer down very carefully, the way you do when the alternative is to throw it. "Eight months, Rafael."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I wrote to you. Through Bellharbor, through the river traders, through a pilgrim, through a man who claimed he knew a man. Nothing came back. Not even—" Her jaw worked. "Not even a *no*. Just nothing. Do you know what nothing does to a person?"

"It hollows them out," I said quietly. "Yes. I know exactly what nothing does."

She stared at me. And then, because we were Eriksens and therefore constitutionally incapable of a graceful moment, she punched me in the arm—hard, a smith's punch—and then grabbed a fistful of my shirt and pulled my forehead down against hers, and we stood there in the doorway of our parents' forge while the whole valley pretended very hard not to watch.

"You absolute idiot," she said into my collar. "You came back."

"Told you I would."

"You told me you were going to the market in Duncarrow. You said you'd be back by supper." She pulled away, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, glaring at me with red-rimmed eyes. "That was eight months ago. Supper was lamb and turnips. It went cold."

"I'm sorry about the lamb."

"You should be. It was a good lamb." But she was almost smiling now, the way she does—sideways, grudging, like the smile costs her money. Then her gaze slid past my shoulder and sharpened. "And what, precisely, is *that*?"

Lior was sitting in the middle of the lane, washing a paw, radiating the unbothered sovereignty of his kind.

"That's Lior."

"The spirit fox. From the stories." She narrowed her eyes. "It looks like a regular fox."

"He is a regular fox now. Mostly. He gave—" My voice caught, unexpectedly, on the truth of it. "He gave most of himself to get us up the mountain. What's left is... just him. Cleverness and appetite and opinions."

Maren studied the fox. The fox studied Maren. Somewhere in that exchange—I never learned what passed between them—a verdict was reached.

"He stays in the house," she said, "and if he gets in the hen coop again, I don't care whose fragment of divine fire he used to be. He's soup."

Lior yawned enormously, showing every tooth, which I chose to interpret as acceptance of terms.

* * *

Reconciliation, it turns out, is not a moment. It's a season.

Maren and I circled each other for weeks. There were things we couldn't say yet—her eight months of nothing, my eight months of guilt carried like a stone in the chest, the letter I'd finally sent from Grayfen that had arrived three days after Dray's men had come asking questions about her brother, which was why she'd packed a bag and started walking toward the war before the war came to find her. We'd saved each other badly and bravely and neither of us knew how to be grateful without it sounding like blame.

So we did it the way siblings do: through work. She taught me the forge again—my hands remembered, mercifully; hands are loyal that way—and I taught her what I could of listening, though Maren's gift, when it woke, turned out to be iron and flame rather than beasts. The forge-fire leaned toward her when she entered. The metal softened under her hammer a breath sooner than physics strictly allowed. She pretended not to notice. She noticed constantly.

"You scattered it," she said one night, sparks drifting up past her face like slow orange snow. "Into everyone. Everything. Why not keep some? Just a little. For us."

I thought about the chamber on the mountain. About the Ember offering—because it had offered, in its burning, patient way—Maren healed, her scars smoothed, the years given back. About how easy it would have been. About how the offer itself had been the test.

"Because power kept is power hoarded," I said. "And hoarded power always finds a throne eventually. Even a kind one. Even ours."

"That's a very pretty answer."

"It took me twenty years and a war to think of it. I'd appreciate some credit."

She snorted. But later, banked fire, two chairs, the quiet that siblings earn, she said: "The girl with the crow. Nettie Corrigan. Her mother wanted to send her away. Thought she was cursed." Maren turned her mug in her hands. "I told her about you. About the mare. About how you used to sneak out at night to talk to a lame horse because you couldn't bear anyone knowing what you were." She looked at me. "Nettie stays now. I'm teaching her the bellows."

The stone in my chest shifted—didn't lift, stones don't lift, but shifted, settled into a place I could carry it.

* * *

Sera stayed a month. Long enough for the valley to stop flinching at her Ashguard bearing, not long enough for her to stop checking sightlines out of habit. She and Maren got along the way a flood gets along with a riverbank—dramatically, and with lasting changes to the landscape.

She left on a gray morning in early autumn, and I walked her as far as the ford.

"This isn't goodbye," she said. It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. "There's work. The gifted are waking up all over the place, and half of them are waking up scared, and scared people with new power find bad teachers. Somebody has to be there first." She touched the scar at her collarbone—the one she'd carried since the child, since the confession, since the night two damaged people stopped performing strength. "Somebody who knows what fear makes people do."

"Come back when you can."

"I'll come back when I can." She kissed me—briefly, thoroughly, the way she did everything—and then walked across the ford without looking back, because looking back was a luxury she hadn't finished learning to afford.

I stood in the shallows until the reeds closed behind her. Lior sat beside me in the wet grass and, after a long moment, leaned his warm ordinary weight against my leg.

"Yeah," I said to him. "Me too."

* * *

Winter came, and with it the first real test of the thing we were building.

A traveler arrived half-frozen in December—a boy of fifteen with frostbitten ears and a talent that made lamps gutter and dogs howl, running from a village that had decided he was the cause of a bad harvest. His name was Pell. He was starving, terrified, and so saturated with untrained power that the horses wouldn't graze within thirty feet of him.

Maren wanted to feed him and point him at the road south. I understood why. Hollowmere was small. Small things break.

But I stood in our yard in the falling snow and listened—to the boy shaking, to the dogs complaining, to the valley itself humming its new, uncertain chord—and I heard Lior's voice, the old voice, the voice that lived in my memory now like a fossil lives in stone:

*All of them will need someone.*

"We'll take him," I said. "For the winter. We'll teach him what he is before someone else teaches him what to fear."

Maren looked at me for a long moment. Then she sighed the sigh of a woman whose brother refuses to learn the lesson that no good deed goes unpunished, and said, "He sleeps in the loft, he chops wood, and if he blows up my forge, he's rebuilding it himself."

Pell stayed. Then came a girl who dreamed true, and twins who could quiet pain with a touch, and a shepherd whose sheep had started answering back. By spring there were seven of them. By the following winter, twelve. Maren built a second forge. The valley, which had spent centuries forgetting, began—slowly, clumsily, with all the grace of a foal finding its legs—to remember.

* * *

On the first warm evening of the second spring, I climbed the hill above the forge where I'd buried our parents, and sat in the grass with Lior's chin hooked over my knee, and looked down at the lights coming on in the windows below.

Down in the yard, Nettie Corrigan was teaching Pell to listen to the bees. Maren's hammer rang late in the forge, and the fire leaned toward her like a flower. Somewhere a dog was explaining the mole situation to anyone who would listen. The valley hummed. The world beyond it hummed too—I could feel it now, faintly, endlessly, a thousand small fires where there had been one great one.

"You'd have hated this," I told Lior, scratching the notch in his ear. "All these amateurs. No sense of mystery. Nettie asked me last week if you were 'the vessel of primordial flame' and I said no, he's the vessel of stolen sausages, and she laughed at you."

Lior bit my sleeve gently, an ordinary fox's rebuke, and settled deeper against my leg.

Below, the forge door opened and Maren stepped out, wiping her hands, and paused, and looked up the hill at me. Even at this distance I could see her weighing whether to climb up and drag me down to supper or let me have my moment. She was still deciding when the light caught something else—

a rider on the far road, moving fast, raising dust.

Maren shaded her eyes. I felt the valley's attention swing toward the stranger like a field of wheat bending to wind, and I felt, underneath the hum, a note I recognized: a beast somewhere on that road, exhausted and afraid, calling out in the only language it had.

*Help. Help. Help.*

I stood up. Lior's ears went forward.

"Well," I said, to the fox, to the valley, to whoever was listening. "Supper can wait."
