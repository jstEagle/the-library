## 22. The Valley That Remembers

My brother used to be the quietest man in Hollowmere. Now he is the loudest thing in it, and he doesn't even raise his voice.

That's the trick of him, if you want to know. Rafael Eriksen, the beast-speaker, the man who broke the Ember and scattered it like seed corn across the world—that man sits in a hay barn of a schoolhouse with his boots in the straw and teaches children to shut up. Not rudely. Reverently. He says listening is mostly silence with your whole body, and forty children under twelve try to do it every morning, and every morning they fail magnificently, and every morning he laughs like it's the best thing he's ever seen.

I watch from the doorway because that's my job now. I'm the one who stands at doors. Someone has to make sure the world stays outside when it needs to, and comes in when it's asked.

* * *

You should understand what Hollowmere was, before. It was the valley that forgot. Magic couldn't live here—wouldn't, maybe—and we told ourselves that was a blessing. No witch-hunters came because there was nothing to hunt. No wonder ever visited because wonder had been uninvited generations ago, and we kept the house swept for its absence the way some widows keep dusting a room no one sleeps in.

We were proud of our emptiness. I was proud of mine. I baked bread, I kept ledgers for the mill, I married late and widowed earlier than anyone should, and I never once suspected that my own brother could talk to animals. He hid it from me for twenty years. Twenty years of suppers where he'd go still as a fence post while a lame mare's voice filled his head, and I thought he was just tired, or thinking about weather, or being Rafael.

When I found out—after everything, after Grayfen and Bellharbor and the mountain—I didn't speak to him for a month. Not because he'd lied. Because he'd needed to. Because I lived in a valley so starved for magic that my own brother decided starving me was safer than feeding me, and he was right, and being right made it worse.

He apologized with his hands full of flour. That's how he does apologies. He was kneading dough and he said, "Maren, I would have told you if telling you wouldn't have painted a target on your bread." And I said, "It's a good loaf," which in our family means all is forgiven, and he cried into the dough, and we ate the saltiest bread in the history of the valley, and that was that.

Families are strange little kingdoms. We don't need crowns. We need someone who knows what the bread means.

* * *

The school started by accident, the way most true things do.

After the scattering—after Rafael stood in the Mountain of First Fire and refused every throne that wanted the flame and gave it instead to every creature that could hear him—the world changed in small ways first. A milk cow in Tarnwick lowed three days before the flood and saved eleven families. A hearth in Bellharbor guttered itself flat whenever a child got too close, and the woman who owned it stopped beating her children, because the fire had opinions and she couldn't unhear them. Dogs began to testify. Crows ran errands for coin. The whole world woke up murmuring, and nobody—not Veylan in his tower, not Amara with her exile turned invasion turned exile again—could gather the murmuring into a fist. That was the point. That was always the point.

But the gifted children were another matter. They woke up loud, in villages that remembered witch-hunters, in towns where the Ashguard had burned lists into public memory. Mothers drowned kittens and feared their daughters. Fathers beat the song out of sons who whistled back at birds.

And the mothers started walking. Toward Hollowmere. Because travelers carried rumors, and the rumors said: there's a valley where a beast-speaker lives, and he refused a crown, and he takes no masters, and he asks no oaths, and the valley remembers how to hide people.

The first mother arrived in autumn with a girl of six who spoke only to goats. Then a boy who dreamed storms two days out. Then twins who could feel each other's pain across a room, which sounds tender until you've watched one of them stub a toe.

Rafael took them all. Sera drilled them in the mornings—not magic, never magic first, but running, falling, getting up, the grammar of a body that might someday need to flee. Lior napped in the sunbeam by the chalkboard and pretended not to be interested, which is fox for deeply interested.

And I kept the door. I learned which travelers were pilgrims and which were spies, which gifts were gifts and which were wounds wearing gifts' clothing. I wrote the letters to distant parents. I buried two children's old names at their request and planted rosemary over both graves.

Justice outside the law, Sera calls what we did on the road. But here's what I learned watching her teach a child to fall properly: justice outside the law, done long enough and gently enough, becomes a community inside one. We have laws now. We wrote them ourselves, on the back of my old ledger pages, and the first one is: *No gift shall be owned.* Not by a crown, not by a covenant, not by a teacher, not even by a parent. The second law is: *No one listens alone.* Rafael insisted on that one. He spent twenty years listening alone, and he will tell you, if you buy him enough cider, that it nearly killed him twice—once slowly, once at a ford with a dying man's blood on his hands.

* * *

This morning I watched him teach the listening lesson, and I'll tell you how it goes, because I have watched it perhaps four hundred times and it has never once gone stale.

"Close your eyes," he says. "No—don't reach. Everyone reaches first. Reaching is wanting, and wanting is loud. Just open the door and see who walks in."

Forty small faces scrunch in concentration. The barn creaks. Somewhere a goat named Duchess—who is, I am assured, a very serious person—chews thoughtfully near the window.

And then, usually, one child gasps.

Today it was Tam, the cooper's daughter, seven years old, born deaf and therefore, Rafael says, the finest listener in the valley. She gasped and signed too fast for most of us, and Rafael signed back, slow, grinning, and then translated aloud for the class: "Duchess says the rain will come tonight, and she'd like her blanket moved before it does."

The children erupted. Forty voices demanding to know what their animals were saying, whether the hens had opinions, whether the old dog by the gate remembered them from Tuesday. Rafael let the noise crest and break and settle, the way he lets everything crest and break and settle, and then he said the thing he says every time:

"You heard nothing yet. Most of you. And that's fine. Duchess talked to Tam because Tam has been sitting with her since midwinter without asking her for anything. Listening isn't a gift you're given. It's a debt you pay, in patience, until somebody trusts you enough to speak."

Then he caught my eye through the doorway—he always catches my eye through the doorway, as if checking I'm still standing guard—and winked, and went back to chaos.

* * *

Sera found me at the fence at noon. She does that. She has an unerring instinct for when I'm brooding, which she claims is knightly training and which I claim is guilt, because she still thinks she doesn't deserve the peace she helped build.

She leaned on the rail beside me. Her hair's gone grey at the temples and she wears it like a decoration she earned. "You've got your ledger face on," she said.

"I always have my ledger face on."

"You've got your ledger face on and your jaw set. Which means you were thinking about the years he hid it from you."

I hate her a little, sometimes. In the way you hate someone who has read your favorite book and can quote it back to you.

"He could have died," I said. "All those years. If the wrong person had seen him with that mare."

"He knew that better than anyone. That's why he did it." She watched the children spill out into the yard, shrieking, racing toward the paddocks to test Tam's weather report against actual clouds. "Mercy that costs nothing isn't mercy. His silence cost him everything, and he paid it daily, for you. For all of us. I executed a child for less than what he was, Maren. I know the arithmetic of hiding. He was better at it than I ever was, and kinder."

"You forgave yourself eventually," I said. "I heard you say so."

"I said I'd stopped punishing myself daily. Forgiveness is a longer campaign." She pushed off the rail. "Come inside. Lior stole a pie again and Rafael's negotiating reparations, and you're the only arbiter either of them respects."

"Lior is a fox."

"Lior is a former fragment of a god with the moral gravity of a pastry thief, yes. Come arbitrate."

* * *

Here is what the travelers say, when they come up the valley road. I collect their stories the way I used to collect debts—patiently, with interest.

They say there's a beast-speaker in Hollowmere who was offered the Ember entire and gave it away. They say the usurper's empire cracked the same season, not from war but from laughter—because when power belongs to everyone, thrones become punchlines. They say Queen Amara lives quietly now in the south, teaching history, and that she begins every lecture with the words *I was wrong*, and that the children believe her faster than the adults do. They say Veylan died alone in a tower full of wards that no longer worked, shouting orders to soldiers who had gone home to farms where the cows warned them of storms and the hearths loved their children.

They say the witch-hunters' order dissolved when its last inquisitor, Dray, reached for a flame that chose not to be held, and the reaching killed him, and no one avenged him, and no one needed to.

They say a knight burned her commission in a free city and followed a map-keeper into the mountains, and that when he scattered the fire she was the first ordinary creature it touched, and that she has heard animals ever since, faintly, like music through a wall—and that she refuses to call it a gift, and calls it wages instead.

They say the fox at the schoolhouse is just a fox now. Clever, ordinary, mortal. It stayed anyway. It sleeps on the chalkboard ledge and steals pies and once, when a child cried herself sick missing her mother, it curled on her chest all night, doing nothing magical at all, which was the most magical thing anyone in that room had ever seen.

And they say—if they stay long enough, if they eat enough of my bread—they say the strangest thing of all. They say the valley itself remembers now. That Hollowmere, which forgot magic for three hundred years, holds it loosely, like a valley-sized cupped hand. That things grow here that grow nowhere else. That the dead magic wasn't dead, only sleeping, waiting for someone to come home and tell it the war was over.

Rafael says that's sentimental. Rafael says valleys don't remember; people do, and people planted themselves here, and roots are just memory you can water.

But I've lived here my whole life, and I'll tell you what I know. I know the fog comes down gentler than it used to. I know the ford where a dying man pressed a leather tube into my brother's hands runs clear even in spring melt. I know that when the children practice listening in the old barn, the building seems to lean in.

Maybe that's memory. Maybe it's just wind. Out here, we've learned the difference matters less than you'd think.

* * *

Evening now. Rain arrived on schedule, right when Duchess said, and Tam walked the length of the yard in it, bareheaded, vindicated, glowing. Supper is over. The boarders are abed. Rafael is at the table across from me, mending a saddle strap by lamplight, and Lior is a warm weight against his hip, and through the window I can hear Sera walking the perimeter with the night dog, their footsteps falling into step the way hers and Rafael's fell into step on a road I only half-know the story of, because some chapters belong to other people.

"Tam's going to be trouble," Rafael says.

"Good trouble?"

"The best kind. She asked me today what happens when a listener hears something terrible." He pulls the strap tight, ties it off. "I told her the truth. You come get someone. You never carry it alone. That's the whole covenant, really. Everything else is technique."

"And what did she say?"

"She said, 'Then why did you wait twenty years?'" He laughs—my brother, the quietest man in Hollowmere, laughing at himself, easy as breathing. "Children. No respect for dramatic timing."

Outside, the rain settles in for the night, patient as a debt being paid. The valley drinks. Somewhere in the dark, a hundred creatures are saying small important things to whoever will listen, and someone always will, now. That was his doing. Ours, I suppose, if I'm feeling generous, and tonight I am.

Tomorrow a new family arrives—a boy who speaks to rivers, they wrote, and a mother who hasn't slept in a year. I'll stand at the door. I'll take their names. I'll put bread in front of them and wait for it to mean what it means.

The valley remembers. So do we.
