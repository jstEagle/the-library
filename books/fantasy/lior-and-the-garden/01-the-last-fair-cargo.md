## 1. The Last Fair Cargo

The crate began to sing on the second night.

Lior Dravenmoor had hauled stranger cargo through the Thornfell passes — caged nightingales for a duke who couldn't sleep, a coffin that hummed when it rained, once an entire wedding party's worth of contraband cheese — but he had never hauled anything that sang. It was a thin sound, barely there, threading through the wind like a hair pulled taut. A boy's voice, he realized, muffled by pine boards and tar and whatever else the Guild had used to seal the thing shut.

He stopped his mule on the switchback above the Greywater falls and stood very still in the falling snow.

"Hello?" he said, feeling immediately foolish. Smugglers did not talk to their cargo. Smugglers especially did not talk to cargo that answered.

The singing stopped. Then, small and careful, from somewhere inside the crate: "Are you the one taking me?"

Lior looked at the crate. The crate, being a crate, offered no further comment. He looked at the pass ahead of him, white and silent, three days from the delivery point at Hollowmere, where a crown courier would be waiting with papers and payment and, if the rumors were true, a company of the king's new hunters.

"I'm the one taking the crate," Lior said finally.

A pause. "There's holes," the voice said. "In the corners. I can see out a little."

*Well, that's a design flaw,* Lior thought, and then thought nothing at all for a moment, because the voice was young — younger than him, even, and Lior was nineteen and still got carded at the Drowned Rat whenever Vess sent him to collect debts there.

"What's your name?" he asked, because apparently tonight he was the kind of smuggler who talked to crates.

"Tam."

"Tam what?"

"Just Tam." A rustle against the wood, like someone shifting to get more comfortable, which seemed optimistic given the dimensions involved. "Are you going to open it?"

"No."

"Oh." Another pause. "I didn't think so. The man with the rings said nobody would."

Lior knew the man with the rings. Master Corvel, the Guild's broker of record, who wore silver on every finger and smiled with exactly half his mouth. Corvel had handed over the crate two days ago with the paperwork already signed and a look on his face that Lior had spent two days trying not to think about. It hadn't been the look of a man selling a barrel of brandy. It had been the look of a man selling something he hoped never to see again.

"Tam," Lior said, "what did they tell you was happening?"

"They said I was going to serve the court." Tam's voice had gone carefully flat, the way voices do when someone has practiced being brave. "They said it was an honor. My mother got money." Another pause, longer. "She cried anyway."

Lior leaned his forehead briefly against the cold strap of the mule's harness. *Do not ask,* he told himself. *You have never asked. That is the whole trick of this life. You carry the box, you don't shake the box.*

"What's in the winter court," he heard himself say instead, "that needs a boy?"

"I don't know. But I dream about it now." Tam's voice dropped, went strange. "I dream about doors under the mountain, and all of them are mine. And when I wake up, my mouth tastes like coins."

Lior had grown up on guild stories, the ones told low over cards in the waystations: the mountain court that kept the winter, the lords beneath Thornfell who paid in favors older than the crown, and — newer, uglier — the king's proclamation nailed to every chapel door from here to the coast. *All gifted persons to be registered. All unregistered gifts to be surrendered.* The hunt had started soft, with lists and oaths. Lists had a way of becoming bonfires. Everyone in the Guild knew it; everyone pretended not to.

And everyone in the Guild knew what a soul-scent was, too, though nobody said the word in daylight. Some people were born carrying more than themselves — a brightness under the skin that dogs couldn't smell but other things could. Lior had met maybe three gifted folk in his life, and all three had made the hair on his arms stand up, not from fear exactly, but from recognition. Like hearing a chord you didn't know you remembered.

The crate was humming again. Not singing now — just a faint resonance, like a struck bell that wouldn't finish fading. And underneath the tar and pine, Lior could smell it: something clean and green and utterly wrong for a frozen pass in late autumn, like a meadow in high summer folded up small and hidden inside a box.

Crown property. Registered, sealed, and sold through his Guild to the court beneath the mountain.

"Well," Lior said aloud, to nobody, to the snow. "That's not fair cargo at all."

* * *

Vess was waiting in the Lamplight waystation when he brought the crate down off the mountain, which was itself unusual enough to set Lior's teeth on edge. Master Vess Arden did not wait on deliveries. She sat in the Guildhall in Thornfell proper, behind her ledgers, running the family the way she ran everything: precisely, and slightly disappointed.

But there she was, in the waystation's back room, hands wrapped around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking, and the firelight caught the grey coming in at her temples that Lior had never noticed before. She looked tired. She looked, worse, old.

"You opened it," she said.

"I didn't." Lior set down his pack. "It talked first."

Something moved across her face — quick, quickly buried. "Lior."

"He's twelve, Vess. Maybe thirteen. He dreams about doors under the mountain and wakes up tasting coins. You want to tell me what that is?"

"It's registered cargo with a crown seal, and it reaches Hollowmere tomorrow, and you will deliver it." Her voice was level, the ledger-voice, the one that ended arguments before they started. "Sit down."

Lior didn't sit. "The hunt—"

"The hunt is exactly why we can't keep him." For the first time, her composure slipped, just a hairline crack, and Lior saw the fear underneath, and hated how much better that made him feel about her. "Corvel brokered this sale six weeks ago, before the proclamation even went public. Do you understand what that means? The crown knows things about us, Lior. Names. There are people sleeping under this roof whose names are on those lists, and the price of those names staying off the pyres is one boy, delivered quietly, to a court that pays better than the king does and asks fewer questions after."

"So we're buying our lives with him."

"We're buying *everyone's* lives with him." She set the cup down. It rattled, very slightly, against its saucer. "I found him a place, Lior. The mountain court doesn't burn what it keeps. Whatever happens to him down there, it isn't fire. I checked. I spent three weeks checking."

"That's not the same as safe."

"No," Vess said quietly. "It isn't. But it's the only arithmetic I have. One child against forty souls in this guild, and every road-company from here to the coast getting burned out one waystation at a time. You've seen the smoke over Miller's Crossing. Don't make me do this sum in front of you like you're simple."

Lior stood there and hated her, and loved her, and hated that he loved her. She had pulled him out of a ditch when he was seven, taught him stars and lockpicks and which guild stories were lies. She had never once lied to him, as far as he knew, and she wasn't lying now. That was the worst part. She was telling him the truth, and the truth was a cage with good ventilation.

"The courier at Hollowmere," Lior said. "What happens to him after handoff?"

"He becomes court property. Court property is kept." She paused. "Lior. Deliver the crate."

He delivered the crate.

* * *

Hollowmere at dawn was fog and frost and a crown courier with a face like a closed ledger. The handoff took eleven minutes. Papers signed, seals matched, coin weighed. The courier's men loaded the crate onto a flat wagon, and Lior stood there with his receipt and his payment pouch and watched them do it, and told himself the whole time: *this is done, this is done, this is how it works.*

As they lifted the crate, the singing started again. Thin, muffled, patient. Not a plea anymore — Tam had stopped pleading somewhere on the second day, and somehow that was infinitely worse.

Then, just before the wagon rolled, the voice came one last time through the knothole, quiet enough that only Lior could have heard it:

"You smelled like home. That's why I asked."

The wagon went into the fog toward the mountain. Lior stood in the frost until the sound faded, then longer, holding a payment pouch that felt heavier than any cargo he'd ever carried.

He was three miles back toward Thornfell before he admitted to himself that he'd memorized the route. Every switchback, every hidden door, every smugglers' road that ran down under the mountain where no crown map would ever follow.

He told himself he was just being thorough. He told himself that all the way home, past the burned-out waystation at Miller's Crossing, where the smoke was still rising.

At the Guildhall, Vess looked up from her ledgers and saw his face, and set down her pen.

"Lior," she said warily. "What did you do?"

"Nothing," Lior said. "Yet."
