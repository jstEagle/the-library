## 2. What the Guild Sold

The ledgers of the Thornfell Guild were kept in a room with no windows and one door, and the door was locked from the inside by a woman who trusted no one, least of all the people she'd raised.

Lior had picked that lock at fourteen as a dare and gotten a week of latrine duty for it. He picked it again now, at twenty-two, with no dare to excuse him — only a boy's voice still whispering in his ears: *you smell like roads. Take me back the way you came.*

The ledger room smelled of tallow and old paper. Vess kept her accounts in three books: the grey book for honest cargo, the black book for dishonest cargo, and the red book for cargo that had never officially existed. Lior found the red book where she always hid it, behind a loose stone that thought itself very clever, and he sat down on the cold floor with his back against the shelf and read until the candle burned low.

He found the entry on the third pass through. It wasn't hidden. That was the worst part. It sat there in Vess's neat, upright hand like any other transaction, dated nine days before the crown's proclamation:

*Received: writ of quiet, full and perpetual, covering all persons under Thornfell mark.*
*Given: one (1) soul, unclaimed minor, provenance gifted. Delivery to Undercourt broker, autumn fair.*
*Note: boy is small. Boy will not be missed.*

*Boy will not be missed.* Lior read the line four times. His hands were steady, which surprised him; he'd expected them to shake. Instead there was just a cold, clean clarity, the kind he usually felt only when a rope bridge started singing under his boots.

It hadn't been desperation. It hadn't been a knife at the guild's throat. Vess had shopped. She'd gone to the mountain court with a list of what the guild needed — silence, safety, the crown looking elsewhere while her smugglers moved gifted folk through the passes — and she'd paid with a child. Not even a child of the guild. A boy bought off a famine road for two coppers and a bowl of stew, carried into Thornfell, and entered into a ledger like a bolt of silk.

Lior closed the book. Sat in the dark a while. The candle guttered and he didn't relight it.

He thought about going to Vess. He thought about it honestly, the way you weigh a bridge before crossing. And he realized, sitting there in the dark with the red book in his lap, that he already knew how that conversation went. *We did what we had to do, little wren. The guild eats first. You know that. I taught you that.* She would say it kindly. She always said the terrible things kindly. That was the trick of her, the thing he'd loved about her since he was six years old and she'd fished him out of a snowdrift: she made cruelty feel like shelter.

He put the red book back behind its clever stone. He left the lock exactly as he'd found it. And he walked out into the guildhall's noise and lamplight and smiled at people he loved, and hated himself for how easily the smile came.

* * *

Tam sat on a bench in the hall beneath the mountain, where the ceiling was carved with faces and none of the faces had mouths.

He had been told the bench was an honor. Everything here was an honor. The soup was an honor. The small cell with the warm stones was an honor. The collar of woven silver they'd fastened around his throat, light as a necklace, was called a *cradle*, and the broker who fitted it had said, *you should feel proud, little one. Most souls rattle loose in ordinary vessels. Yours has been given a fine home.*

Tam touched the cradle sometimes, when no one watched. It didn't hurt. That was the strangest thing. It felt like nothing at all, like touching your own wrist. But at night, when the mountain hummed its long slow note through the walls, he could feel something in the silver leaning toward the sound — a softness in him, going somewhere without him. Like watching your own breath fog a window and drift away.

There was another boy in the next cell over, older, brought in during summer. His name was Pell and his gift was finding things, and he had spent four months finding every crack in the mountain's rules. "They can't make you forget," Pell whispered through the wall, most nights. "That's the one thing. They can take everything else, but the taking itself stays. So remember it. Remember it hard. Remembering is a kind of keeping."

So Tam remembered. He remembered his sister Brenna's laugh, which was too loud and started in her belly. He remembered the taste of stolen apples. He remembered the man with the road-smell who had carried his crate so gently, gentler than anyone had carried him since Brenna, and who had knelt in the dark and said *I'm sorry* like it cost him something.

Remembering was a kind of keeping. Tam kept all of it, and the mountain hummed, and the silver leaned, and he held on.

* * *

Three days later, the crown burned the Hollowford waystation.

Lior heard about it from a poultry farmer who traded eggs for passage and gossip in equal measure. By the time Lior reached the ridge above Hollowford, the roof was a black ribcage and the smoke smelled like burnt pine and something else underneath, something sweeter and worse.

Davy Osk was dead. Davy, who taught him cards. Davy, who cheated at cards so badly it was practically charity. Davy, whose name was written in the red book's protection list same as everyone's — *covered, covered, covered* — except the writ of quiet apparently had edges, and Davy had stepped outside them. He'd sheltered a gifted girl for a single night. One night. The hunters burned the station with him in it and left the girl alive, deliberately, walking her to the road and pointing her toward Thornfell like a message being delivered.

*We know where you keep them,* the message said. *Quiet has a price. Pay again, or burn.*

Lior stood on the ridge with the wind pulling at his coat and understood, finally, the shape of the bargain Vess had made. It wasn't a wall. It was a leash. And leashes only ever pulled one direction.

He went to find the others.

* * *

Sera Quill was mending nets in the fishing village at the fen's edge when Lior found her, though there were no fish worth catching within fifty miles and everyone knew it. She'd been a hedge-witch of good standing once, before the guild's protection failed her too and the village decided her gifts were the reason their well turned bitter. Now she mended nets and drank nettle tea and pretended her hands didn't itch for real work.

"You have the look," she said, not looking up. "The look of a man about to ask me for something stupid."

"I'm asking you for something suicidal," Lior said. "It's different."

She looked up then. He laid it out — the ledger, the sale, the burning, the leash — and watched her face go through anger and grief and land somewhere harder. When he finished she was quiet a long moment, then began rolling up her sleeves.

"I'll want my herbs," she said. "And someone sensible to talk me out of the worst ideas. Who else?"

"Wick."

Sera snorted. "The tracker? He doesn't talk."

"He doesn't need to. You'll see."

Wick was exactly where Lior knew he'd be, high on the Torren crags, watching the passes. He was a lean, weathered man of indeterminate age who had lost his tongue to a crown interrogator years ago and never let it slow him down. Lior didn't explain anything. He simply held up the red book. Wick read the open page, and his jaw tightened, and he held up one finger — *wait* — and vanished into his cave, and came back with a pack already strapped, as if he'd been waiting months for permission.

Last, Lior went to the millhouse on the Ash Road, where a girl named Brenna worked sixteen hours a day and slept four, because grief couldn't be outrun but exhaustion could hide it. She opened the door before he knocked.

"The crate-man," she said. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and hopeful all at once, which was a terrible combination on a person. "The one who carried my brother. I've been waiting for you. I didn't know I was waiting for you, but I've been standing at this door for nine days."

"They took him under the mountain," Lior said. "Vess sold him. I'm going to steal him back, and I need people, and I don't have anyone but—"

"Us." Brenna was already reaching for her cloak. "You have us. Say it right, though. Say we're coming whether you like it or not, so it's your idea and our choice both. That's how family works."

Behind her, Sera muttered something about *worst ideas*, and Wick tapped his chest twice — his way of saying *yes*, or maybe *finally* — and Lior stood on the millhouse step with three near-strangers and a stolen ledger's truth between them, and felt something shift in his chest that had been locked since the night he'd knelt beside a crate and apologized to a boy he couldn't save.

Not yet saved. But chosen. That counted for something.

"Undercourt's sealed by first snow," Sera said, squinting at the peaks, where the clouds were already stacking up white and heavy. "How many weeks do we have?"

Lior followed her gaze. The mountain wore its winter crown early this year, and beneath it, somewhere in the dark, a boy was holding on to the memory of an apple.

"Fewer than that," he said. "Pack light. We leave tonight."
