## 3. The Court Beneath

The smugglers' road into the mountain was called the Kneeler, because it bent you double before you'd walked a mile of it. Lior went first with a lantern hooded to a coin's worth of light, and behind him came Sera, muttering charms at the damp, and behind her the tracker Wren, who said nothing because Wren never did, and last of all Tam's sister, Nettie, carrying the boy himself on her back like he weighed nothing, which he nearly didn't anymore.

"Cheerful place," Sera said, for the fourth hour running.

"You could stop saying that."

"I could. But then the dark would win, wouldn't it."

Lior almost smiled. That was Sera's whole method, he'd learned: she talked at the dark until the dark got embarrassed and left. A hedge-witch with half her training and all her nerve, struck off the rolls for healing a tax collector's daughter without payment — the crime, as far as Lior could tell, had been the *without payment*.

The Kneeler went down in switchbacks that smelled of old smoke and older stone. Twice they passed doors set into the rock, guild-doors, marked with the Thornfell knot, and twice Lior touched them as he passed the way you'd touch a sleeping dog you weren't sure was friendly.

*You were raised on these roads,* he told himself. *They'll remember you.*

But roads remembered what you carried, too. And he was carrying a stolen boy toward a court that bought souls by the pound.

* * *

Sera felt the change before she saw anything: a hush in the air like the moment before snowfall, except going the wrong way — upward, inward, a hush being *drawn* somewhere ahead. She put out a hand and stopped Nettie by the shoulder.

"What," Nettie whispered.

"Winter," Sera said. "Real winter. The kind that's kept somewhere."

She'd read about it in books the Guild would have burned if the Guild had been literate about anything but profit. The Court Beneath did not endure winter; it *manufactured* it, bottled it, poured it down the passes every year so that the lowlands would need what the court sold. Warmth. Passage. Mercy. All priced.

Ahead, the tunnel opened its mouth.

They came out onto a gallery cut into a vast hollow, and Sera heard Nettie's breath catch against Tam's hair. Below them, lit by veins of blue fire in the walls, lay a city that was less a city than a counting-house wearing a city's clothes. Bridges of black ice arched over streets where figures moved in furs, and every figure, every single one, wore a small glass vessel at the throat or wrist or belt — some dull, some glowing faintly from within, like lamps with someone home.

"Candles," Sera murmured. "They call them candles here. A soul in glass burns steady." She looked at Tam, asleep against his sister's back, his chest rising too shallowly. "And when it gutters—"

"Don't," Nettie said.

"No," Sera agreed. "We won't let it."

Wren tapped Lior's arm and pointed down. At the foot of the gallery stood an archway hung with chains of tiny bells, and beneath it, waiting with the patience of furniture, a figure in grey.

"They know we're here," Lior said.

"Of course they do," Sera said. "We're carrying the loudest thing in the mountain."

* * *

The figure under the arch was a steward, or called itself one — a thin person whose face seemed assembled fresh each time you blinked. It did not ask their names. It asked what they had to trade.

"Passage to the vaults," Lior said. "We're brokers."

"Brokers." The steward's eyes moved to Tam. "Of what?"

"Livestock," Sera said smoothly, before Lior's face could do anything stupid. "A delivery that went astray. We're returning it. There's a fee owed to us, and your masters pay their fees."

It was a good lie. Sera told it warmly, with the little shrug of a woman who has done this a hundred times, and Lior watched the steward weigh it, and watched it begin to believe—

And then Tam woke, and looked at the steward, and screamed.

Not loudly. That was the terrible part. A small, scraped sound, like a door in a house where no one lives. But every vessel in the near street flickered at once, and the steward's face finished assembling itself into something like hunger.

"Ah," it said. "The runaway stock. How convenient. You will be paid a finder's fee."

"We'll be paid," Lior said, "a great deal more than that, and you'll take us to the vaults yourself, or my associate will explain what happens to vessels that are handled roughly in transit." He let his hand rest very casually on the boy. "Gifted stock is fragile. You want him delivered breathing."

The steward considered. Somewhere above, far up through miles of stone, wind began — the first breath of the court sealing itself for the long winter.

"Come," it said. "Quickly. The gates close tonight."

* * *

They did not go quickly enough.

The vaults were real — Lior saw them, rows upon rows of candles in niches, ten thousand small lights, ten thousand someones — and the steward led them past the public counters into a corridor where the blue fire ran brighter, and there it stopped, and smiled with a face that had finally settled on a shape Lior recognized: Vess's.

"That's not funny," Sera said.

"It isn't meant to be," said the steward. "Your mistress is already here. Your mistress is always here, this time of year. Someone must count the winter out."

Behind them, the corridor filled. Grey figures, unhurried, each holding a candle of their own — and Lior understood, with a lurch, that these were not guards. They were collateral. People who had traded themselves in installments.

"Nettie," he said quietly. "Run when I run."

"When you run where?"

"I'll tell you when I'm running."

He drew the only weapon the Guild had ever taught him to love: a knife, plain, honest, good for opening crates. The first grey figure reached for Tam and Lior opened its wrist instead, and the corridor stopped being quiet.

What followed was not a battle so much as a bad bargain conducted at speed. Sera threw light — real light, hedge-crafted, ugly and orange — and the grey figures flinched from it as if it were a bill come due. Wren moved through the crowd like a rumor, and things fell down around her without ever quite being pushed. Nettie ran with Tam pressed to her chest, following Lior's voice through corridors that narrowed and branched, and the blue fire in the walls guttered as they passed, as if the mountain itself were watching through a squint.

Then a side door opened, and the fighting stopped, because everyone in the corridor knew better than to be making noise in front of her.

Vess stood in the doorway with a lantern in one hand and a ledger under her arm, and she looked exactly as she had on the morning she'd found Lior stealing bread off her cart: tired, amused, and entirely unimpressed by the world's nonsense.

"Well," she said. "Look at this. My best runner, robbing me."

"Not robbing," Lior said. His voice shook and he hated it. "Returning. Like you should have done."

"The sale holds, Lior. It has held for two years. You think I wanted it?" She stepped forward, and the grey figures parted, and Sera raised her hands and did not throw anything, because even hedge-witches know when a room belongs to someone else. "I traded one soul to keep forty. Forty people who sleep under my roof, eat my bread, trust me. You were one of them. You still are, if you'd stop being sentimental long enough to notice."

"Tam is eleven."

"Tam was *currency*. I'm sorry for it. I've been sorry for two years, and sorry has kept forty people alive." Her eyes went to the boy, and for one moment — Lior would spend years deciding whether he'd imagined it — something in her face cracked like river ice. Then it closed. "Give him to the steward. Walk away. In spring I'll buy him back, I swear it on the Guild."

"You swore on the Guild when you sold him."

Above them, deep in the stone, a bell began to toll — slow, enormous, final. The steward smiled its borrowed smile.

"The gates," it said. "The long winter begins. No one leaves now."

Vess did not look away from Lior. "No one leaves," she agreed softly. "So you may as well put the knife down, boy, and—"

Lior ran.

Not away. Toward — down the corridor past her, shoulder checking hers hard enough to spin her into the wall, Wren and Sera flooding after him, Nettie's footsteps hammering behind, and the tolling bell swallowing everything, and ahead, at the end of the last passage, a door of black ice with a lock shaped like a closed eye.

Beyond it, he could feel it — the way you feel a fire in the next room. Ten thousand candles. And among them, one small flame burning low, guttering, waiting.

"Vess!" he shouted back over his shoulder, knife already rising toward the lock. "You can keep your forty! We're taking the forty-one!"

The eye-shaped lock shuddered under his blade — and somewhere below, in the dark of the vaults, ten thousand souls flickered at once, and the winter outside began, very slowly, to crack.
