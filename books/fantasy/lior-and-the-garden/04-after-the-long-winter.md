## 4. After the Long Winter

The vessel was smaller than Lior expected.

That was the cruelty of it, he thought. All the weight of what had been done — a boy's soul, sold like a bolt of silk, brokered by the woman who'd taught him to read the stars — and it fit in his two hands. A globe of pale glass no bigger than an apple, with something inside that moved like smoke and wept like weather. It was warm. That was the worst part. It was warm as a sleeping child.

Behind him, the court of the mountain was coming apart.

He could hear it — not crashing, nothing so honest as crashing, but a long unraveling sigh, like a great tapestry being picked apart thread by thread. The pillars of blue ice that had held the throne room's ceiling were dimming. The courtiers who had watched the fight with their beautiful, terrible faces were flickering at their edges, going translucent, going *less*. And through the high windows of the hall, for the first time in anyone's living memory, light was coming down the mountain instead of being swallowed by it.

"Boy," rasped the old courtier who had bargained with them, the one called Master of Ledgers, "you do not know what you unmake."

"I know exactly what I'm unmaking," Lior said. His voice shook. He let it. "I've been carrying your cargo since I was nine years old. I just never looked inside the crates before."

Sera stood at his shoulder, hedge-witch's staff planted on the marble, her burned hand steady on his arm. Wren the tracker stood silent as ever at the door, though her eyes said everything her voice never would: *do it*. And Tam — Tam was on his knees on the cold floor with his sister Maren wrapped around him, both of them crying without any grace whatsoever, and Lior thought that if he ever wrote this story down, that was the part he'd keep. Not the fighting. The crying.

He raised the vessel above his head.

"You might feel it," Sera murmured. "The boy. When it breaks. Like a door opening in a house you forgot you owned."

Lior brought the vessel down against the ice pillar.

It did not shatter so much as *exhale*. The glass came apart into a breath of silver, and the silver went into Tam like rain into cracked earth, and the sound he made was not a word in any language but every person in the hall understood it anyway. The winter wall over the passes — the one the court had maintained for three hundred years, the one that kept Thornfell sealed and secret and starving — groaned once, deep in the stone, and let go.

Lior felt it go. Everyone did. A pressure behind the eyes lifting, a held breath released, snow beginning somewhere far above to fall soft and ordinary instead of endless.

The Master of Ledgers faded to almost nothing and did not seem to mind. "The crown's claim," it said, sounding almost amused, "was written in our keeping. Without us, it is only paper." And then it was gone, and the throne room was just a cave with pretty ice, and the mountain was just a mountain.

* * *

They found Vess at the mouth of the court's great gate, standing alone in the first true sunlight to touch that threshold in centuries.

She had come down the smugglers' roads behind them, as she always could — Vess Nystrom had forgotten more hidden paths than the guild had ever mapped — and she had arrived too late to stop anything, which Lior suspected had been the point. She looked small in the daylight. She had never looked small before.

"Lior." Her voice was level. It always was. "The guild is dead, then."

"The guild sold a child," Lior said. "So yes."

"Not the guild. *I* sold the child. The others didn't know. Most of them." She looked out at the pass, where the snow was sliding off the heights in long white ribbons, revealing the road beneath, brown and real and open. "I told myself it was arithmetic. One boy's soul against forty lives. The hunters were burning waystations, Lior. They killed Doss. They'd have found the rest of us by spring. I bought time the only coin they accepted."

"You bought time with Tam."

"Yes." She did not flinch from it. She never had; that was the thing about Vess, the thing he had loved and hated in equal measure since he was small enough to ride her shoulders. She never lied to herself where witnesses could see. "And now the coin is spent, and the debt is called, and I am the last piece of the old guild still standing. So." She turned to face him fully. "What happens to me?"

Lior had imagined this moment a hundred times on the road down. In some versions he shouted. In some he walked away. In none of them, until now, did he notice how tired she looked — tired the way a wall is tired, holding up a roof long after the house has burned.

"The guild's ledgers are going in the ground," he said. "Every name, every bargain, every debt. Including yours."

"And me?"

"There's a valley south of here," Lior said. "Old orchards. Nobody's claimed it since the winter closed the roads. You could grow apples. Nobody in Thornfell grows apples. It'd be a scandal."

Something moved across Vess Nystrom's face that on any other person Lior would have called a laugh. "Exile to an orchard."

"Exile with cider," Sera offered helpfully.

Vess looked at them all — the witch, the mute tracker, the boy whose soul she'd sold and the sister who'd clawed her way up a mountain to get it back — and whatever she saw there, she nodded once, slow, like a ledger closing. "I'll take the valley," she said. "And Lior. When the apples come in. Send someone."

"We'll send Tam," Lior said. "He can tell you what sunlight tastes like. You owe him the description."

* * *

They buried the ledgers at the crossroads below Thornfell village, under a cairn of river stones, in a hole Maren insisted be dug properly deep because "if the crown ever digs them up I want them to work for it."

There were eleven books. Eleven books of names and prices and bargains struck in the dark, forty years of the Thornfell Guild's quiet commerce in everything the law forbade and conscience should have. Lior read a few pages aloud as they filled the grave — not the shameful parts, but the good ones. The year Vess had fed half the village through a blight winter. The tolls the guild had quietly waived for widows. The record of every smuggler lost on the roads, each name written in Vess's own careful hand, because she had never trusted anyone else to spell them right.

"That's the eulogy," Lior said, when the last spade of dirt went down. "Not the ledgers. Us. We're the eulogy."

Wren tapped his shoulder twice — her way of saying *well said, fool* — and put a sprig of rowan on the cairn, because trackers have their own rites and don't explain them to novelists.

* * *

They founded the new company in the back room of the Waystation Inn, over stew, which Sera declared was the only proper way to found anything.

They argued about the name for two hours. Maren wanted something fierce. Sera wanted something clever. Tam, who had been quiet all evening in the way of someone relearning the weight of their own thoughts, finally said, "Call it the Open Road Company. Because the passes are open. And because—" he hesitated, and Lior watched him find the words like a man finding stones in a river, "—because we're not hiding anymore. That's the whole point. We carry things in daylight now."

So it was the Open Road Company, and its charter — written on the back of an inn bill in Sera's handwriting — read: *We carry what people need, tell them what we carry, and charge fair rates.* Wren added, in gestures, a clause about never working for anyone who wore a crown, which passed unanimously.

Their first contract arrived before the stew was finished. A farmer's wife from the valley, breathless at the inn door: her daughter was gifted — small gift, a gift of mending, broken things knitting whole under her hands — and the crown's men had been asking questions in the low villages, and could the famous smugglers of Thornfell—

"We're not smugglers," Lior said, standing. "We're couriers. And yes. We'll take her south tonight, ahead of the thaw traffic. There's a school past the border, run by a hedge-witch Sera knows. They teach gifts there instead of selling them."

The woman stared. "You'll do it? For what price?"

Lior looked around the table — at Maren already packing, at Wren checking her bow strings, at Tam watching the door with new-come courage, at Sera grinning into her stew — and he thought of a crate he'd once carried through these same passes without looking inside, and the whisper that had followed him home, and every mile since.

"No charge," he said. "But when she's settled, send word. We like knowing how the stories end."

Outside, the snow was melting off the eaves in bright drops, and the road ran open all the way to the horizon, and for the first time in the long history of the Thornfell passes, nobody was waiting in the dark to collect on anything.
