## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trouble with goats, Casper Joubert had long ago decided, was that they had opinions.

Not opinions the way people had opinions — people kept theirs folded up neat and only brought them out at market or at funerals, where opinions belonged. Goats wore theirs on the outside, all the time, like a second coat. Her eldest nanny, a slate-grey creature named Widow, held the firm conviction that the fence between the lower pasture and the bramble hollow was a suggestion rather than a boundary, and she communicated this belief every single morning by standing at the weak spot and staring at Casper with the flat, patient insolence of a tax collector.

"No," Casper said.

Widow blinked.

"You know what I said. I know you know what I said. Don't give me that face."

Widow turned her head with tremendous dignity and began, very slowly, to lean her shoulder against the fence post.

Casper sighed, set down the milk pail on the flat stone, and trudged over to press her palm against the old girl's neck. "You've had your breakfast. You've had the good clover, the part by the creek that everyone else wanted. What more does a goat need?"

The answer came back the way it always did — not in words exactly, not the way a person would say it, but in a shape of feeling that Casper's head translated as best it could: *more is what a goat needs. Green on the other side of the fence is greener. This is known.*

"That's not wisdom, that's just greed with a beard."

Widow chewed, unbothered, and the warm dumb contentment of her rolled off her like heat off a stone in August, and Casper stood there with her hand on the goat's neck in the early gold light and felt, as she did every morning, that peculiar doubled thing she could never say aloud: that she was the richest woman in the Hollow, and the poorest, and that both of those things were her own fault.

Because Casper Joubert could understand goats.

And badgers. And crows, and the fat brown rats that lived under the mill, and the spider that had built a web across her kitchen window and complained — constantly, at length — about the quality of the flies in this valley. She could understand them the way you understand your mother's humming, or the creak of a stair that means someone's coming: not word for word, but true for true. The gift had been with her as long as she could remember. Her first word, according to her mother, had been *milk*, which everyone had assumed referred to the obvious. It had not. It had been addressed to the farm cat, a vast orange tyrant named Griddle, who had been demanding breakfast with increasing volume since dawn and had been deeply gratified, if smug, to be finally understood.

Her mother had figured it out by the time Casper was six. Casper remembered the afternoon of it — the kitchen, the smell of bread, and her mother going very still as Casper relayed, helpfully, that the sow was going to farrow early and they'd best move her to the dry pen. Her mother had knelt down, taken Casper's face in her flour-dusted hands, and said the words that built the walls of Casper's whole life:

"You will never, ever tell a living soul. Not your dearest friend. Not a priest. Not me, when I ask you straight out, because I may have to ask you straight out someday, and you'll need to lie to my face, and I will forgive you for it. Do you understand me, little bird?"

Casper had understood. Children in the Hollow learned early that the valley was safe in the way a locked room is safe — and the lock had a name, though nobody said it above a whisper. The Hollow was dead ground. Magic couldn't live here. The old stories disagreed about why. Some said a war had been fought here so long ago that the land itself had sworn off the whole business, the way a man who's been burned swears off the forge. Some said the valley was a wound that had healed over into scar tissue, and scar tissue doesn't feel anything. The elders said only: *the Hollow forgets*, and left it at that, and the saying did its work. Nothing strange took root here. No wise-women, no hedge-witches, no lights over the marsh. And no burnings, either — because there was nothing to burn. That was the bargain the valley had made with the world, or the world had made with the valley, and everyone kept their side of it by never, ever mentioning that the bargain had a price, and that the price had a face, and the face ate breakfast at her mother's table and milked goats and was, right now, being out-stubborned by a nanny named Widow.

"Fine," Casper told her. "But if you get into the brambles again I'm not combing you out. Last time you looked like something a witch would keep in a jar."

Widow butted her affectionately, nearly knocking her over, which in goat language was roughly a hug.

* * *

The morning passed in the way the Hollow's mornings had always passed, which was to say slowly, and in order. Casper milked the four nannies while Griddle supervised from the fence rail, offering commentary that was ninety percent grievance and ten percent, grudgingly, praise. She carried the pails up to the house, where her mother was already at the loom, weaving the good wool they sold at the autumn market. Her father had been dead nine years; the farm was the two of them and the animals now, and it was enough, and it was small, and some mornings it felt like both at once.

"Market today?" her mother asked, not looking up from the loom. Her mother had a way of asking questions that were not really questions.

"Market today."

"Take the eggs. And the wool from the south shelf, the grey. And Casper—" now she did look up, and her eyes did the thing they did, the thing that was not quite a question and not quite a warning, "—mind you're home before the light goes."

"Mother. I'm twenty-three."

"And the light still goes," her mother said, and turned back to the loom, and that was the end of it, because that was how conversations about the gift went in the Joubert house: they never started, and they never finished, they just hung in the air between them like smoke that had nowhere to go.

Casper took the basket and went down the lane toward the mill.

The village of Thistledown — for the valley had a village, three of them really, strung along the river like beads on a wet string — was waking in its usual unhurried way. Old Tam was sweeping his doorstep with the same broom he'd been ruining for a decade. The baker's boy was hauling the morning's first loaves out and burning his fingers on them, as was tradition, apparently, since his father had done the same. Two women stood by the well having one of those conversations that was entirely about the price of onions and entirely about something else.

"Morning, Casper."

"Morning, Mistress Hale. Morning, Annet."

"Your mother sending you to market? Good. Tell Marta I want first pick of the grey wool, and if that sister of hers gets there before me there'll be words."

"There'll be words," Casper agreed, and walked on, and everyone in Thistledown knew her — knew her walk, her basket, her mother's wool, her father's grave under the rowan on the hill. She belonged here the way the millstone belonged in the mill. And not one soul among them knew the first true thing about her. She could stand in the middle of the square and be surrounded by every person she'd ever known and be, completely, a stranger.

She'd made her peace with it. Mostly. On good days it felt less like loneliness and more like privacy, and she told herself the difference was a matter of seasoning.

The mill sat where the river bent, and past the mill, past the tumbled stone wall where the village ended and the wild began, was the badger's sett — which was where Casper went when she wanted, just for a little while, to stop being a stranger.

* * *

Bracken was dozing in the sun outside his sett when she arrived, flat on his back in the grass with his lame hind leg stuck up at an angle, like a gentleman who had been overcome by the quality of the afternoon. He was old — older than any badger had a right to be, though badgers were cagey about their ages and Bracken was cagey about everything. His muzzle had gone silver and his belly had gone wide and his left hind leg had been wrong since before Casper knew him, crushed in a trap in some year he refused to specify, and he had a way of speaking that made everything sound like either an insult or a legal complaint.

"You're late," he said, without opening his eyes.

"I'm not late. There's no appointed time."

"There's always an appointed time. It's the time I decide I want you here, and that was an hour ago, and you're late." He cracked one eye. "You bring anything?"

"You're a wild animal. You hunt your own food."

"I'm a *lame* wild animal with a bad back and no family, and society has obligations, girl, whether it acknowledges them or not." He rolled ponderously upright, an operation involving several distinct stages, like a ship coming about. "Also the worms by the creek this year are terrible. Sandy. I don't know what the river's doing but it's doing it to my worms."

"You complain about the worms every year."

"Because every year they're terrible. Consistency is not the same as repetition." He settled himself against the warm stone and looked her over with those small dark eyes that missed nothing. "You've got a look on you."

"I don't have a look."

"Everyone has a look. Yours today says the goats won again."

"Widow tried the fence again."

"Course she did. She's the only one in this valley with any ambition." Bracken scratched his ear with a hind foot, an operation of great violence. "What's the news, then? You've got market gossip, I can smell it on you. You always carry market gossip like other folk carry bread. Come on. I've been lying here since dawn with nothing but my thoughts, and my thoughts are all about worms. Give a poor cripple something to live for."

And this was the arrangement, and had been for eleven years, since the day twelve-year-old Casper had found a bleeding, trapped, furious badger behind the stone wall and — before she'd had time to think better of it — had told him, out loud, in words, that she'd help him if he stopped biting. And the badger had stopped biting and said, in words, *well. That's inconvenient for both of us, isn't it.*

So they traded. She brought him gossip — who was courting whom, whose pig had won at the fair, which of the miller's sons had come home from the lowlands with a fancy hat and a fancy manner and no money — and he brought her the valley's other news, the news from under things. Where the fox had denned. Which hawks were hunting the pasture this season. What the river meant by the way it had gone green in the bend below the ford. He was, she suspected, the closest thing to a wise man the Hollow would ever admit to having, and he was a badger, and he was her best friend, and no living person knew he existed.

"Right," she said, settling down beside him in the grass, close enough for the warmth of him. "So. Marta and her sister are fighting again—"

"Again? It's been a fortnight."

"It's been a fortnight since the *last* fight. This one's new. It's about a rooster."

"A rooster."

"A prize rooster. And here's the thing, Bracken — and I got this from the baker's boy, who got it from his cousin, so it may have grown in the telling — but the rooster in question is not entirely the rooster it's claimed to be."

Bracken's whiskers twitched, which was his version of leaning forward. "Go on."

"It's the same rooster that won last year. Painted."

"Painted." He said it with the deep satisfaction of a connoisseur. "Painted. Oh, that's *filthy*. That's the best thing I've heard since spring." He chittered, a rattling badger laugh. "Who painted it?"

"Nobody knows. That's the mystery. Marta swears it's the sister. The sister swears it's Marta, doing it to herself to make the sister look like a liar."

"But the rooster knows," Bracken said, with sudden gravity.

Casper blinked. "What?"

"The rooster, girl. The rooster knows exactly what happened to him. He's been paraded around all summer as a champion and he's been a fraud the whole time and nobody's asked him a single thing about it." His eyes glittered. "You could ask him."

"I am not interrogating a rooster over a county fair scandal."

"Why not? You interrogate me weekly."

"That's different. You *like* it."

"I do," Bracken admitted, without a trace of shame. "It's the only conversation I get. Do you know what it's like, talking to worms? The worms have nothing to say. The worms have *never* had anything to say. Fifty years I've listened to worms and I could count their total contributions to my life on one claw." He heaved a sigh that moved his whole wide body. "No. You ask the rooster. For science."

"I'll ask the rooster," she said, because she would, because she always did, because the truth was that she'd have asked the rooster, the sow, the spiders, and the whole congregation of crows if Bracken so much as raised an eyebrow at her. That was the other thing about the arrangement, the thing she didn't let herself look at straight on: he was the only creature in the world she could talk to *as herself*. Not as the quiet Joubert girl who kept her eyes down and her words few. With Bracken she was funny, and she was sharp, and she was known all the way through, and it cost her nothing but a walk past the mill.

The sun climbed. The gossip ran out, as it always did, into the older talk — the state of the burrows, the coming winter, the fox vixen who'd lost her mate to a snare and was hunting desperate along the tree line, which Bracken disapproved of on professional grounds.

"Snares," he said, spitting the word. "Man's invention. No craft to it. At least a hawk kills you honest, out of the sky, eye to eye. A snare kills you by *accident*, and then you lie there, and—" he stopped. He almost never talked about the leg. "Anyway. The vixen's got kits. Three of them, under the old beech. If the hunting stays bad she'll come into the village, and that'll be a tragedy with a short runway. Someone ought to leave the fish guts from the mill on the far side of the wall. Someone with thumbs."

"I'll see what I can do."

"You're all heart and no follow-through, but I appreciate the sentiment." He squinted up at the sky, where the light had begun to lean toward afternoon. "You'll be wanted home soon."

"Soon."

"Mm." He was quiet a moment, which was rare enough that she noticed it. His small dark eyes had gone to the north, to the far rim of the valley, where the hills stepped up and the world beyond began. "Quiet year," he said. "Too quiet, maybe. The crows have been flying the north ridge in circles for three days now. Nothing comes down. They just circle, like they're looking at something on the far side."

"Looking at what?"

"If I knew that, girl, I'd be a raven, and ravens are insufferable." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on the ridge a moment longer than a joke warranted. "The valley forgets," he said then, in the tone of a proverb, the tone everyone used. "That's the blessing of the place. Nothing strange can find you here."

"I know."

"Mm," Bracken said again. "Well." He heaved himself around, stages and all, and began the slow process of going back into his sett, pausing at the mouth of it to look back at her. "Eggs tomorrow. And if the rooster scandal develops, I want it first. I'm an old lame animal, Casper. Scandal is all I have."

"You have worms."

"Terrible worms," he agreed, and was gone into the dark of the sett, and Casper walked home through the long gold light with her empty basket, past the mill, past the well where the women still talked, up the lane to the farm where her mother's loom clacked its patient rhythm, and she was home before the light went, and the valley was quiet, and everything was fine.

It was three days later that the crier came to Thistledown, riding a horse the color of wet slate, with the king's seal on a scroll and a voice trained to carry across a square — and the first word out of his mouth was *Gifted*, and the second was *Hunt*, and the valley's long forgetting ended all at once, in broad daylight, in front of everyone.
