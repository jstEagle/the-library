## 2. Proclamation at the Mill

The trouble with living in a valley that forgets magic is that it also forgets to hurry.

Casper Joubert had learned this the way she learned most things — slowly, and from goats. The Hollow moved at the pace of weather and wool. News arrived when it arrived, usually late, usually wrong, and always carried by someone who wanted to stay for supper while telling it. A birth in Lowmarsh might take three days to reach Millbrook, by which time the child could walk, or so the teller would claim with a straight face.

So on the morning the king's crier came down the pass, nobody was prepared for him, least of all Casper, who was elbow-deep in a goat named Duchess and having what she would later describe as a philosophical disagreement about milking.

"You're being dramatic," she told Duchess, who was being dramatic. "It's one bucket. You've done this every day for six years. You know how it ends."

Duchess said — in the long, put-upon groan that Casper heard as clearly as speech, though no one else on God's green hills would have believed it — *I know how it ends. That's why I object.*

"That's not an objection, that's a routine."

*It's a principled stand.*

"It's a Tuesday." Casper leaned her forehead against the goat's warm flank and let her hands work without her. This was the part she never got tired of, even after all these years: the strange, easy conversation of it. Not words, exactly. More like the shape of words, poured straight into her head through her palms. Duchess's mind was a small, warm room that smelled of hay and grievance, and Casper had been welcome in it since she was seven years old.

She'd been welcome in Bracken's den longer than that.

The thought of the old badger made her glance up the slope toward the hedgerow where his sett opened under the roots of a dead elm. He'd be asleep this time of day. Badgers kept worse hours than drunkards and were twice as grumpy about being woken, so she didn't wake him. She just looked, the way you look at a thing to confirm the world still has it where you left it.

The elm stood. The hedge stood. The morning was ordinary gold, and the millrace below turned its slow wheel, and everything was fine.

Which is why, when the horn sounded from the mill bridge, Casper's hands went still inside the bucket before her head understood why.

*That's not our horn,* said Duchess, ears swiveling. *Ours is cracked. That one isn't.*

"I noticed."

*You're squeezing my teat like you mean it personally.*

"Sorry." She let go, wiped her hands on her apron, and stood very still in the middle of the pen, listening past the horn to what came under it: hooves. Several sets. Shod horses, which meant money, which meant the lowlands, which meant — her stomach did something slow and cold — *not us*. Nobody in the Hollow rode shod horses. Horses here were for plowing and were mostly named after vegetables.

She left Duchess with a pat and a promise she didn't keep about extra clover, and walked down the lane toward the mill with her heart climbing into her throat one rung at a time.

* * *

By the time she reached the bridge, half the valley had already gathered, because a royal horn was the one thing that could outrun gossip. They stood in their work clothes with their hats in their hands — Old Marta the weaver, the Tull brothers who quarried slate and disagreed about everything except slate, Widow Fen with her flour-white apron, and behind them a scatter of children who had been sent for water and would now be hours late getting back.

The crier stood on the mill bridge in tabard colors Casper had only ever seen on coins: deep blue and gold, the crowned stag of House Aldric stitched across his chest. Behind him, four riders sat their horses with the particular stillness of men paid to be watched. Their armor wasn't parade-bright. It was road-worn, practical, and there was a lot more leather than plate, which struck Casper as odd until she thought about what kind of hunting required quiet.

The crier unrolled a scroll with a snap that carried across the water.

"Hear ye! Hear ye! By word and seal of Aldric, King of the Ninefold Realm, Protector of the Marches, Keeper of the Crown Eternal—"

The titles went on for a while. Casper counted them out of habit; there were eleven, two more than the last proclamation anyone remembered, which had been about a tax on eels and had also been ignored successfully for a decade.

"—let it be known throughout his Majesty's dominions that a great and secret peril festers among his loyal subjects. There are those among you — neighbors, kin, tradesmen — who traffic in forbidden arts. Who whisper with beasts as a man whispers with his own heart. Who hold converse with creatures of field and forest, and by such converse open the gates of the realm to powers older and darker than the crown."

The millwheel turned. The water said nothing. Casper discovered she had stopped breathing and instructed herself, carefully, to start again.

"Let all such persons be named *the Gifted*, and let them be known by this sign: they speak, and beasts answer."

A murmur went through the crowd — not fear, not yet. The Hollow's murmurs ran to confusion first. Old Marta said, audibly, "Speak with *beasts?*" in the tone of a woman who had once spent an entire winter arguing with a mule and gotten nowhere.

"The Gifted are commanded to present themselves to his Majesty's officers within forty days," the crier read on, "for questioning and lawful assessment. Those who come freely shall be treated with the crown's mercy. Those who conceal themselves—" he paused, and Casper would remember afterward that he looked up from the scroll when he said it, and looked at the crowd, and let his eyes move across their faces like a man counting sheep, "—shall be sought out. His Majesty has authorized the formation of a special commission. The Gifted Hunt. Its hounds do not tire. Its writ runs everywhere. Even here."

He rolled the scroll shut.

The silence lasted three full turns of the millwheel. Casper knew because she counted, because counting was better than thinking, and thinking was better than screaming, and she intended to do neither.

Then Elder Hollis stepped forward.

Hollis was seventy-three years old, built like a fence post that other fence posts leaned on, and he had been running the valley council since before Casper was born. He wore his good coat, which meant he'd known — someone had ridden ahead in the night, someone always did — and he wore the expression he saved for funerals and tax assessors, which today were apparently the same thing.

"Honored crier," Hollis said. "The Hollow welcomes the king's word, as it always has."

Casper's stomach dropped through the bridge planks, through the water, and kept going.

"We are simple folk," Hollis continued, in the voice he used for selling wool, which was smooth as lanolin. "We have no truck with arts or mysteries. You'll find no witches here, no whisperers. Whatever this Hunt seeks, it won't find it in our valley. We pledge the crown our full cooperation. Ask anything. Search anywhere. We have nothing to hide."

*Nothing to hide.* Casper stood in the third row with a goat-stained apron and a secret fourteen years deep, and felt those three words land on her like a dropped roof beam.

One of the riders dismounted. He was younger than the others, with a captain's knot on his shoulder cord and a face that might have been handsome if it had ever been permitted to relax. He walked to the bridge rail and looked out over the gathered valley folk with pale, unhurried eyes.

"The crown appreciates cooperation," he said. His voice was quieter than the crier's, which somehow made it carry further. "Cooperation will be verified. The Hunt's hounds will pass through every parish in this valley within the month. They'll walk your fields, your barns, your cellars. Anyone harboring a gifted person — feeding one, sheltering one, failing to report one — shares their guilt."

Widow Fen made a small sound. Somewhere in the crowd a baby started crying and was hushed too fast.

"Names may be given in confidence," the captain added. "To me, or to any officer of the Hunt. There is a reward for accurate reporting. There is a penalty for the alternative." He let that sit the way you let a stone sit at the bottom of a well, so everyone could hear it land. Then he remounted, and the five of them — crier and riders — clattered off down the far side of the bridge toward Lowmarsh, taking the silence with them and leaving a worse one behind.

For a moment nobody moved. Then the crowd broke apart the way crowds do, into knots and whispers, and Casper found herself standing next to Old Marta, who gripped her arm with fingers like knitting needles.

"Beast-speakers," Marta said. "Can you imagine? Talking to animals. What sort of person does a thing like that?"

"No idea," said Casper, whose voice came out almost steady, which she counted as the finest acting of her life. "Sounds mad."

"Madder than a fish in a tree." Marta patted her arm and shuffled off toward the weaving house, already composing the retelling.

Casper stayed on the bridge until the crowd thinned. The millrace turned. The water kept saying nothing. Downstream, a heron stood in the shallows on one leg, doing its impression of a man pretending to be calm, and when Casper glanced at it, it glanced back, and she heard — faint, wry, unhurried — *Well. That was unpleasant.*

"Yes," Casper said, barely moving her lips. "It was."

*They'll be back,* said the heron. *Riders always come back. We fish the same waters.*

"I know."

*You should eat something,* the heron advised, which was the sum total of its philosophy and, most days, good advice. Then it stabbed at the water and lost interest in her entirely, and Casper walked home along the lane with the sun still shining and the whole valley smelling of bread and cut grass, and dread walking beside her in broad daylight like an old friend who'd come to stay.

* * *

She didn't go home. Her feet decided for her, and her feet took the sheep track up past the top pasture, over the stile with the broken rail, to the dead elm and the hedgerow and the dark mouth of the badger's sett underneath.

"Bracken."

Nothing.

She knelt and knocked on the packed earth beside the entrance, three times, the way you knock on a door. "Bracken, I know you're awake. I can hear you rearranging yourself to pretend you're asleep."

A long pause. Then, from the dark, gravel and grievance: *I was asleep.*

"You were not. You were listening to the horn same as everyone."

*I was resting my ears from listening to you.*

"Bracken." She sat down in the grass with her back against the elm root, close enough that she could smell the cool earth-smell of him, musk and clay and old leaves. "Riders came. From the lowlands. Royal ones."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

"There's a proclamation," she went on, staring at her own hands. "About beast-speakers. They're calling us 'the Gifted.' Like it's a compliment. They want us to turn ourselves in for questioning. And Hollis — Hollis stood right there on the bridge and told them the valley has nothing to hide. Full cooperation. He pledged it, Bracken. He pledged *us* away without blinking."

From the sett's mouth, two black-tipped ears emerged first, then the silver-striped face, then the whole heavy, grizzled bulk of him, moving with the deliberate dignity of someone who refuses to be hurried even by apocalypse. Bracken settled himself in the grass beside her with a sigh that seemed to come up from his toes. He was old — older than any badger had a right to be, though Casper had long ago stopped wondering why some animals lived past their seasons, because the answer frightened her — and his muzzle had gone white as frost, and his eyes were still quick as a weasel's.

*Hollis,* he said at last, *once sold me a lame ewe and swore on his mother she was sound.*

"He doesn't know about me."

*No. But he knows about the valley. And the valley knows how to survive. It's been practicing for three hundred years.* Bracken scratched his ear with a hind foot, thoroughly, the way he did when he was buying time. *Tell me the rest. The rider with the knot on his shoulder. What did he look like?*

"How did you know about—"

*The heron gossips worse than the millwives. Go on.*

Casper described the captain: young, pale-eyed, calm as deep water. Bracken listened with his whole body, ears forward, and when she finished he was quiet for so long she thought he'd fallen asleep sitting up, which he had done before, mid-sentence, at her mother's funeral, and refused ever to apologize for.

*Hounds,* he said finally. *Did they mention hounds?*

"The crier did. 'Its hounds do not tire.'" She tried to make it sound like a quote instead of a wound. "Badger poetry, probably. Criers love a flourish."

*Casper.* He said her name rarely enough that it landed every time, flat and heavy as a door closing. *I have been alive a disgracefully long time, and I have smelled many things on the wind, and I am telling you: there are hounds bred in the king's kennels that don't hunt rabbits. They hunt the gift itself. They smell it the way you smell rain coming. Distance, walls, weather — none of it matters to them. And this valley—*

"This valley is empty of magic," she said. "Everyone says so. That's the whole point of the Hollow. Magic can't follow you in. It slides off. Grandmother said—"

*Your grandmother,* said Bracken, *was describing the door, not the room. The valley keeps magic out. It does not necessarily keep out a creature that hunts the *absence* of magic. Think, girl. What does a hound smell when it smells a beast-speaker?*

Casper frowned. "Magic. The gift."

*Does it? Or does it smell the hole where the wild things go quiet around you? Every animal in earshot bends toward you whether you speak or not. Birds stop singing when you pass — no. No, they don't, do they. They sing *at* you. They lean. There's a shape in the world where you stand, girl, a shape made of attention, and a clever nose could follow that shape through a snowstorm.* He fixed her with his small dark eyes. *And you are the loudest quiet thing in this valley.*

The afternoon had gone on shining, indifferent. Bees worked the hedge. Somewhere downhill, Duchess was complaining to somebody about the clover situation. The world was carrying on exactly as if nothing had changed, and Casper sat against the elm root and understood that everything had.

"So I run," she said. It came out smaller than she meant it to.

*You run.*

"Over the pass. Tonight. Before the hounds get here. I could be in the low country by—"

*And leave me?*

The question wasn't sharp. That was the worst of it. Bracken asked it the way he asked everything, mildly, as if the answer hardly mattered, and it cut her to the bone anyway.

"You're a badger," she said. "You're a *lame* badger. They're hunting people who talk to animals. Nobody's going to burn a badger, Bracken. They won't even look at you."

*Mm,* said Bracken, which was what he said when he thought she was being stupid but had decided, out of charity, not to say so.

"They won't."

*Of course not.* He began, with tremendous ceremony, to groom his front paws. *Run, then. Over the pass. I'll stay here and dig worms and think fondly of you, and in twenty years some grandchild of the Tulls will swear there's a badger in this hedge older than the church, and they'll be right, and it will be a fine story for them.*

"That's not fair."

*Nothing about this is fair. Fairness is for markets.* He stopped grooming. When he looked at her again, all the mildness had gone out of his face, and underneath it was something old and hard and terribly fond. *Listen to me, Casper Joubert. Fourteen years ago a half-starved child wandered up to my sett talking nonsense to a wounded crow, and I could have chased her off, and I nearly did, because I was comfortable and old and I knew what company costs. And then she shared her bread with me, badly, because she couldn't cook then either, and she stayed, and she became the best thing in a long life that I have been too stubborn to end. So I know precisely what I'm asking you to weigh. I'm asking you to weigh it anyway.*

"Bracken—"

*Forty days,* he said. *The crier gave the valley forty days. The hounds will come well before that — they always arrive early, that's how you know a hunt is serious. So you have less than forty days, and I have a den, and a hedge, and thirty years of tunnels under this hill that no soldier on horseback will ever find. I am the single best-hidden creature in this entire valley, and I am telling you: one of us should use that. It doesn't have to be me.*

Casper looked at him. He looked back, insufferably serene, chewing on the whole argument like a gristly bit he intended to win.

"I'm not leaving you," she said.

*I haven't finished making my case.*

"You never finish making your case. You just repeat it until I give in out of exhaustion, and I'm telling you now, in advance, so we can skip ahead: I'm not leaving you. If the hounds come, we'll deal with the hounds. If the riders come, we'll deal with the riders. You dug me out of a snowslide when I was nine and you complain about it every winter, and I'm not repaying that by trotting off over the pass while you sit in a hole eating worms and calling it strategy."

Bracken regarded her for a long moment.

*Stubborn,* he said at last, and there was, buried under the disgust, unmistakable warmth. *Stupid, brave, stubborn. Your grandmother was the same. It must be something in the water.*

"It's the well water. Everyone says."

He huffed — the badger laugh, a double grunt through the nose — and settled his bulk against her side, warm as a hearthstone, and for a little while neither of them said anything, and the bees worked the hedge, and the light went long and gold across the top pasture, and it was almost possible to believe the world hadn't changed.

Almost.

*Forty days,* Bracken said quietly, into the grass. *We'll waste not one of them. First lesson starts tomorrow: you'll learn the difference between hiding and being overlooked, because you've only ever managed the second, and the second is luck, and luck is a currency that spends fast.*

"And tonight?"

*Tonight,* said Bracken, *you're going to bring me whatever Marta baked for the council meeting, because if the world is ending, I refuse to end it on an empty stomach, and you're going to tell me every single thing that happened on that bridge, again, slowly, starting with the color of the captain's horse.*

"Chestnut."

*Hm. Cavalry remount, then, not a hunter's stock. Interesting. Go on.*

And so they sat under the dead elm as the sun went down, the girl and the badger, planning a war they didn't yet understand, while somewhere beyond the pass — patient, leashed, and learning the scent — the hounds were already walking.
