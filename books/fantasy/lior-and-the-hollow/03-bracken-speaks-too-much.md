## 3. Bracken Speaks Too Much

The trouble with living in a valley that forgot magic, Casper had long ago decided, was that it forgot everything else too — including how to make a decent cheese press.

She said as much to the flat stone she was wedging under the oak beam, and the stone, being a stone, offered no opinion. The goats offered several. Juniper stood on her hind legs with her front hooves on the fence rail and delivered what sounded like a full parliamentary address on the subject of evening feed times, and Casper answered her honestly.

"Yes, I know you're hungry. You were hungry at dawn. You were hungry while you were eating. You are a hunger with legs, Juniper."

Juniper bleated, satisfied to have been understood, which was more than most people got from a conversation.

That was the thing nobody in the Hollow knew about her, and the thing she could never tell them: she didn't just talk *to* animals. She talked *with* them. Not in words, exactly — not the way she was thinking now, with grammar and sarcasm — but in meanings that arrived whole, like remembering something she'd never learned. The goats told her when their bellies hurt and where the best clover grew and, once, in alarming detail, about a fox they'd seen three valleys over. The chickens gossiped. The dog before him — old Rill, dead two winters now — had been a philosopher of the highest order and mostly opined about rain.

And then there was Bracken.

* * *

She found him where she always found him on market days, or rather he found her, materializing out of the bracken — hence the name, though he claimed he'd had it first and the plants were named after him, "an honor I never asked for" — at the edge of the alder stand behind the mill. He sat in his usual patch of late sun like an old gentleman who had paid off his mortgage and intended to enjoy it, gray-furred and grizzled, one foreleg held stiff from the injury that had lamed him years before Casper was born.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm early. Market doesn't start for an hour."

"For you it started an hour ago. You walked past the Hendersons' turnip cart and didn't stop to listen to them complain about the soil. That's not like you. That's the sort of thing that keeps a badger's spirits up through a long winter."

"The Hendersons' turnips are fine and they know it. They complain because complaining is their crop."

Bracken made a sound that in a human would have been a chuckle and in a badger was more of a pleased rumble deep in the chest. "Sit down. You've got flour on your sleeve and worry on your face, and only one of those is going to brush off."

Casper sat down in the dry leaves beside him. This was the arrangement, unspoken and ancient as far as she was concerned: every week, market day, the alder stand, the two of them trading news like a pair of old women who happened to be separated by species. She brought gossip from the village — who was courting whom, whose roof had leaked, what the miller's wife had said about the miller's other wife, which was a story Bracken had demanded updates on for three years running. He brought news from everywhere else. Badgers ran wide territories, and badgers talked to everything, and everything talked back to the one creature in the valley who could carry a message without laughing.

Except her. But he didn't know that. Or — she had sometimes wondered, watching his black eyes go thoughtful — maybe he did, and had the good manners of his kind, which was to say excellent manners, and simply never mentioned it.

"Your turnip people," Bracken said, "are the least of anyone's problems."

"Oh?"

"Mm." He scratched at his stiff leg with a hind claw, a habit that meant he was choosing his words, which meant she should probably be worried. "I've had visitors."

"Badger visitors?"

"Foxes. Two, from over the eastern ridge. Young ones, fast talkers, the kind who think every thought is a discovery." His voice had gone flat and careful. "They came through the pass by night. They said there are men camped up there. A lot of men. Horses, wagons, and dogs."

"Dogs," Casper repeated. "Hunting dogs? It's nearly autumn. The lords hunt this time of year, everyone knows—"

"Not deer hounds." Bracken turned his head and looked at her straight on, and there was nothing comfortable in it. "These dogs are bred special, the foxes said. Bred in the king's own kennels. They don't smell blood and they don't smell fear. They smell *magic*. Old bloodlines, fed on things I don't want described to me, trained since pups to cast toward any creature carrying the gift. And the men with them aren't hunting boar, girl. They're calling themselves the Gifted Hunt, and they're working their way valley to valley, and the foxes say—" he stopped.

"Say what?"

"They say the dogs cast toward the ridge above your farm twice yesterday. Twice. Then lost it." His eyes stayed on hers. "Lost it, Casper. Like a scent that's there and isn't. Like something *smothered*."

The leaves rustled overhead. Somewhere up the slope a jay made its opinion known, and even the jay's opinion sounded nervous.

"That'll be the valley," Casper said, and heard how thin her voice came out. "Everyone says it. Nothing strange can hold here. Magic slides right off the Hollow like water off a goose. That's why my gran came here, that's why all of them came—"

"And it's true," Bracken said. "It's true as bread. This valley eats magic for breakfast and asks for seconds. Whatever you've got tucked away inside you — whatever it is you think I don't know about — it goes quiet here. Quieter than quiet. That's why you've lived to be twenty-two years old instead of dying in a fire at seven, which is the national age for burning beast-speakers these days, or so the crows tell me, and crows are morbid but rarely wrong."

Casper's mouth went dry. "You—"

"Knew?" Bracken snorted. "Child, I've known since you were six years old and tried to explain to me, in front of your mother, that the Hendersons' cow was calving early and needed help. In words. Full sentences. Your mother laughed and said children make up stories, and I looked at you, and you looked at me, and we both agreed without saying so that we'd take this particular secret to our respective graves. Mine's coming sooner than yours, I expect. Badgers are honest about arithmetic."

"But you never said anything. All these years—"

"What was there to say? 'Hello, small child, you're carrying the crime that gets families burned out of house and home, do carry on'? You were a baby. You were careful. You *are* careful. You've spent your whole life being so careful you apologize to furniture." He shifted his weight, wincing at the lame leg. "Careful has worked. Careful is why we're sitting here having this pleasant conversation instead of you being in a cage. But careful, Casper, is about to stop being enough."

"Why? Because of some foxes' tall tale about—"

"Because the Hunt doesn't need proof to come looking. That's what the young ones said, and they were frightened, and foxes lie about many things but never about being frightened. The king's men ride valley to valley with those dogs, and they run the dogs along every treeline, and if a dog casts even once — even a whiff, even a ghost of a whiff — the riders stop. They ask questions. They set up camp. They stay until the dog stops casting, and dogs like that don't stop casting until they've found what they're smelling or died trying." He paused. "And yours is the only farm on that side of the valley."

"They lost the scent," Casper said stubbornly. "Twice, you said. The valley hides me. It's always hidden me."

"It hid your grandmother too, did it?" Bracken asked quietly.

Casper didn't answer. Her grandmother had lived in the Hollow sixty years and died in her bed at eighty-one, safe as stones, and Casper had built her whole understanding of safety on top of that fact like a house on a foundation. But there had been that one winter, the stories said, when Gran wouldn't go near the treeline for a month. When she kept the shutters closed. When she'd held little Casper by the shoulders — Casper couldn't have been more than four — and said, in a voice Casper still heard sometimes in dreams: *"If anyone ever comes asking, you've never heard an animal speak in your life. Promise me. Promise me, little wren."*

"Your grandmother was smarter than both of us put together," Bracken said, reading her silence the way he read everything. "She knew the valley's protection wasn't a wall. It was a blanket. Blankets keep you warm. They don't stop arrows."

"So what are you telling me to do?" Casper's voice cracked upward, sharper than she meant. "Leave? Just — pack up, walk out of the only home I've ever—"

"Yes," Bracken said. "Exactly that. Tonight, if you've any sense. Tomorrow at the latest. Take the south track over the shoulder of the mountain, down past the hot springs — the goats know the way, take Juniper, she's got a head for geography and a face for negotiations. Go east to the coast cities. Big places, Casper. Places where a thousand smells drown out one. Places where a girl with flour on her sleeves is nobody worth noticing. Find work. Find a room. Grow turnips, if you must, God help you. Live."

"Live," Casper repeated. "Alone. In a city. Where I don't know a soul."

"You'd know me."

"You're a badger, Bracken. You can't exactly write letters."

"I could dictate to a passing crow. Cows owe me favors. There are ways." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't match his voice, and that was the worst part — Bracken joking while his heart was breaking, which was, she realized suddenly, exactly what she did. Exactly what she'd learned from him.

"No," Casper said.

"Casper—"

"No. Listen to yourself. You're telling me to run away and leave you here — leave *everyone* here — with the dogs coming. If those hounds catch so much as a thread of me anywhere near this valley, who do you think they question first? Who do you think they burn first?" She was on her feet without remembering standing, hands shaking, flour drifting off her sleeve. "You've been my — you're the only person in the world I can actually *talk* to, do you understand that? Twenty-two years and one badger. And you want me to stroll off to the coast and grow turnips while they drag you out of your sett and put you in a cage because you're the strange animal that lives next to the strange girl?"

"They wouldn't hurt me. I'm a badger. I'm vermin with opinions. Worst they do is chase me out—"

"They'd hurt you because you'd *let* them, you great stubborn lump of fur, because that's what you do, you cover for me, you've been covering for me since I was six!" Her voice broke and she hated it. "If I run, I'm not saving myself. I'm just making sure there's a witness left over."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The sun had moved; the warm patch they'd been sharing slid off toward the alders, and the shade crept in cool across the leaves.

Then Bracken sighed, a huge, gusty, defeated sound, the sigh of a creature surrendering a position he'd fortified all morning.

"Well," he said. "There it is. Stubborn as your grandmother. She argued with me the same way, you know. Same spot, near enough. Fifty years ago, give or take."

Casper blinked. "You knew my grandmother? Knew her *knew* her? Talked-to-her knew her?"

"Who do you think taught her the south track past the hot springs?" Bracken settled back against a root, lamely, comfortably, an old soldier standing down. "Who do you think carried messages between her and her sister in the coast cities for thirty years? Who do you think sat with her the night before she died, because she sent for me — yes, sent, she knew too, we were a whole conspiracy of two before you were ever born — and told me, 'Someone will need to look after the girl when she starts talking to things. She'll be too proud to ask. Don't let her be alone with it.'"

Casper sat back down very slowly. The leaves were cold now. "You never told me any of this."

"There was never a reason. Now there's a reason, and I wish to God there weren't." He looked up at her, and his voice gentled into something she'd rarely heard from him — no irony, no armor. "So hear me, girl, because I'm only saying it once and then I'm going to insult your cheese-making to change the subject. Running isn't abandoning me. Running is doing the one thing that keeps all of this —" he gestured with his gray muzzle at the valley, the farm, the whole green bowl of the world they'd shared — "worth anything. A secret kept safe is worth ten secrets avenged. Go. Live. Be boring. Be *safe*. Let an old badger die in his bed like a gentleman, worrying about turnips instead of fires."

"No," Casper said again, but softer now, and she reached out and laid her hand on the broad flat of his head, between the ears, the way she'd done since she was small enough that he'd carried her on his back the length of the orchard wall. He allowed it, as he always allowed it, with the weary dignity of the much-loved. "We figure it out together. We always have. Maybe the dogs come and smell nothing, because the valley swallows it. Maybe they pass through in a week and it's a market-day story forever. But I'm not leaving you, Bracken. I'd sooner teach Juniper to sing."

"She'd be terrible at it."

"Appalling. All vibrato."

He rumbled his laugh, low and reluctant, and leaned into her hand. "Stubborn love," he muttered. "Worse than clever hate, every time. Your grandmother used to say that too."

"Did she?"

"No, I made it up just now. I'm a very wise badger." But he pressed his head harder against her palm, briefly, and then pulled away and became brisk and businesslike, because that was how he loved — sideways, disguised as logistics. "Right. If we're staying, we're staying *properly*. First: the midden heap goes. Today. You've been burying your goat remedies and your herb scraps and half of them are things no ordinary farmwife should own, and dogs dig. Second: the loft. Anything with a charm on it, anything Gran left you, comes down and goes in the river or the stove, I don't care which, weep later. Third—"

The jay stopped screaming.

Casper noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping — the absence arriving louder than the sound ever had. Up and down the valley, bird by bird, the afternoon went quiet. The goats lifted their heads at the fence line, all of them at once, ears forward, staring west toward the pass.

Bracken went rigid beneath her hand. Every hair along his spine rose like grass before a storm.

"They're not coming next week," he breathed. "They're not coming tomorrow. The foxes were three days behind them."

Far off, thin as a needle through the clear autumn air, came the sound of a horn. Then another, answering. And beneath them, faint but unmistakable, rolling down off the ridge and into the valley that forgot everything —

dogs, baying.
