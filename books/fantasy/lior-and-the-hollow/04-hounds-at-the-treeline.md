## 4. Hounds at the Treeline

The goats knew before the birds did.

Casper was mending the fence by the south pasture when Petunia stopped mid-chew and stared at the treeline with her whole body — ears forward, jaw frozen, a cudgel of half-chewed grass forgotten on her tongue. Then Marigold did the same, and then old Bramble, who had never in eleven years stopped eating for anything short of lightning.

Casper straightened, needle still between her teeth.

*What is it?* she asked, in the way that wasn't words.

*Men,* said Bramble, who was too old to be bothered with precision. *And dogs that smell wrong.*

She spat out the needle. "Wrong how?"

But Bramble had gone back to his grass, because he was eleven years old and had earned the right, and it was Petunia — young, dramatic, always hungry for catastrophe — who answered. *They smell like bells. Like iron bells ringing under their skin.*

Casper looked at the treeline. The morning was ordinary: mist burning off the birches, a wren scolding somewhere, the creek doing its patient work below the pasture. Nothing about it said *royal hunt*. But the proclamation at the mill three days past had said plenty, in the crier's brass voice while the elders stood there nodding like weathercocks, pledging the valley's full cooperation, and Casper had stood in the crowd with her hands folded and her heart going like a trapped sparrow, thinking only: *Bracken. They'll come for Bracken.*

And now here they came.

She counted them from behind the fence rail. Six riders where the track met the trees, moving at a walk, unhurried, the way men move when they own the ground they're standing on. Their horses were tall grey things, far too fine for the valley's mud, and they carried themselves with a stiffness that made even the mist seem to get out of their way. Behind them loped four hounds — big, black-and-tan, broad through the chest, moving not like dogs but like instruments being played.

Casper had grown up around dogs. Farm dogs, drovers' dogs, the miller's fat useless spaniel. She knew what a dog smelled like when it worked: joy, mostly, and hunger, and the bright single-mindedness of a creature doing the one thing it loved.

These hounds smelled like nothing at all. That was the wrongness. They were quiet inside, the way an empty house is quiet.

*Go to the barn,* she told the goats. *All of you. Now.*

Petunia opened her mouth to argue — Petunia argued with everything, including fences, including rain — but Bramble bumped her hip with his horns and they went, all three, trotting stiff-legged toward the barn with their ears pinned back.

Casper ran for the house.

* * *

Bracken was already there when she burst through the door, which shouldn't have surprised her and did anyway. He'd let himself in through the loose board by the woodpile as he always did, and he sat in the middle of her kitchen floor like a small striped magistrate, his lame hind leg stretched out before him, his black eyes fixed on the window.

"You felt it too," she said, breathless.

"Badgers feel most things first," he said. His voice was gravel poured slow, the voice of an animal who had been talking longer than she'd been alive. "Earthquakes. Floods. Weddings. It's the weddings I could do without." He turned his head. "Riders. Six. And those dogs aren't dogs."

"The crier said—"

"I heard what the crier said. Everyone in this valley heard it, and everyone in this valley will line up to hand over anyone the crown points at, because that's what valleys do. They keep their heads down and their ditches clean." He scratched at the scarred ear he'd lost half of to a fox some twenty years ago. "You should have run when I told you."

"Bracken—"

"Don't *Bracken* me in that voice. Go out the back, down the creek bed, up to the old quarry. Nobody's been up there since your grandfather's day. You can live on goat cheese and self-pity until spring."

"I'm not leaving you."

He looked at her then, and it was the look he gave her maybe twice a year, the one that made her feel eight years old again, sitting in the bracken — ha — with a scraped knee and a broken bird cupped in her hands, while a limping badger explained to her, with enormous patience, that the world was larger and crueler than her mother's kitchen and that she would need to be cleverer than both.

"Girl," he said, "they cannot smell you. Do you understand me? Three days those dogs have been at the valley's edge, and they've caught nothing, because this valley forgets magic the way other places forget snow in July. Whatever you are, the Hollow keeps it. But me?" He lifted his badger grin, all teeth, no comfort. "I talk. I've talked my whole life. To you, to the foxes, once to a very rude owl. If they've got hounds that smell gift, then I am a bonfire in a dark field, and every nose in that troop is pointed at me already."

"Then we both go," Casper said. "Now. Before they reach the farm. The quarry, together, and—"

"And what? You want a lame old badger trotting up a scree slope ahead of six riders?" He snorted, which in a badger is a genuinely alarming sound. "I've buried three of my nine lives in this valley, Casper. I wasn't planning to spend the rest running."

"They won't kill you. They said questioning. They said the crown wants beast-speakers taken *alive* for questioning—"

"Yes." Something moved behind his eyes, something old and cold. "And I know what questioning looks like when the questioner wears a crown. I saw it, long ago, in another valley that also kept its head down." He got to his feet, favoring the bad leg, and shook himself all over, fur to nose, the way he did before anything difficult. "Here's what happens. You go out the back and down the creek, and you don't stop until the quarry. And when they come sniffing after you — and they will, girl, because whatever else this valley hides, it can't hide a girl who runs — I give them something better to chase."

"No," Casper said. "No, absolutely not—"

"You'd argue with a landslide too, if it stood still long enough." He waddled toward the front door, and she grabbed for him and got a mouthful of coarse fur as he twisted away with surprising speed. "Listen to me. Once. Just once, listen. You are the only creature in this whole green world who has ever understood a word I said. Do you think I'd trade that for a long life in a cage? They can question me until kingdom come. I'll tell them the moon is made of cheese and the king owes me money. But you—" he paused at the door, silhouetted against the bright morning, small and square and utterly immovable, "—you get to be free. So be free. That's the whole of my asking."

Outside, faint but growing, came the jingle of harness and the creak of leather, and beneath it a sound that raised every hair on Casper's arms: the hounds, giving tongue. Not barking. *Singing.* A low, bell-toned cry, four voices in perfect unison, like a hymn sung by something that had never once been happy.

Bracken's ears flattened. "Ah," he said softly. "There's my cue."

"Bracken, please—"

"Down the creek, girl. Count to a hundred before you look back. And if you must look back—" the grin again, quick and crooked, "—make it a good view. I intend to be magnificent."

Then he shoved open the door with his head and was gone into the morning, a low dark shape flowing across the yard toward the road, toward the riders, toward the singing.

Casper stood in her kitchen with her heart breaking in real time, and then she did the hardest thing she had ever done in her life, which was exactly what he told her to do.

* * *

The creek bed was cold and loud and mercifully deep-walled, and she went down it like a frightened otter, boots slipping on wet stone, briars taking their toll of her sleeves and scalp. At forty she heard shouting behind her. At sixty she heard the hounds change key — the unison hymn splintering into chaos, snarling, a sound like a fight in a church. At eighty she heard a man scream, high and human, and something answered it that was not a scream at all but a roar, a huge and furious churring bellow that she had heard exactly once before, the night a fox got into the henhouse and learned why badgers are named what they're named.

At ninety-nine she broke her promise and looked back.

From the lip of the creek bank she could see the near end of her own yard. Two horses were down — one screaming, one very still. Three riders were off their mounts and backing in a wide circle, spears leveled at a small striped shape in the churned mud, and the hounds — the terrible bell-voiced hounds — were cowering behind the horses' legs, all four of them, tails down, refusing the order their handler screamed and screamed.

Bracken stood in the center of it all, bloodied along one shoulder, planted over the fallen rider like a fortress with legs, roaring his broken-toothed defiance at the entire apparatus of the crown.

And at the edge of the circle, apart from her men, sat a woman on a grey horse, watching.

Even at this distance Casper knew her for the captain. There was a way she held herself — not rigid like the others but *settled*, weight sunk deep in the saddle, a sword-hand resting easy on her thigh — that said she had done this a hundred times and hated perhaps forty of them. Dark hair cropped short. A scar through one eyebrow. Eyes that were doing something none of her soldiers' eyes were doing: they weren't angry. They were *counting*. Measuring the badger, the downed men, the cowering hounds, the geometry of the whole disaster, and finding in it some answer she didn't like.

Her voice carried across the yard, clear and flat. "Hold. HOLD, you idiots — nobody kills it. The writ says alive. Corwin, get that fool's horse out of range before it takes someone's face off."

Then, quieter, almost to herself, but the wind brought it up the bank to Casper's ear as if it were meant for no one and therefore meant for her:

"What in the nine hells is a badger doing defending empty ground?"

Empty ground. Casper's stomach dropped through the earth. Because the captain was right — the yard was empty, the house was empty, the whole farm showed nothing but goats shut in a barn and a mad badger guarding nothing at all — and any hunter worth her badge would ask exactly that question, and keep asking it, and start walking the creek bed with her eyes open.

Casper ran.

* * *

The quarry was a grey bowl in the hillside, half-swallowed by birch and gorse, its flooded floor green and still. Her grandfather had cut stone there; her father had let it go; the valley had done what the valley always did, folding it back into itself, forgetting. There was a tool shed against the north wall with a roof that mostly held, and Casper had stocked it weeks ago without letting herself admit why — oilcloth, matches, a sack of oats, hard cheese wrapped in wax, a water skin. Guilt-provisions. Fear made practical.

She sat in the doorway of the shed as the light went long and gold, and she watched the road.

The riders left the farm near dusk. She heard them before she saw them, the jingle and creak coming up the valley, and pressed herself flat among the gorse as they passed along the track below. The captain rode last. Her grey horse walked stiffly, favoring a foreleg, and one of the hounds limped at her stirrup with its head hung low, and on the lead rider's saddle, tied down like a sack of turnips, was a cage.

Iron-barred. Small. Far too small.

Inside it, a shape shifted, and a voice — gravel poured slow, pitched deliberately, lazily, insolently low so that only the wind and one hiding girl could possibly catch it — drifted up the hillside:

"—and ANOTHER thing, your majesty's dogs are cowards, and I've known braver turnips—"

A soldier cuffed the cage and it swung, and Bracken's voice cut off in a grunt, and Casper dug her nails into her palms so hard she drew blood, because if she made a sound she would die, and if she died he would have defended nothing at all.

The captain reined in. Casper froze. For one horrible moment Elara Voss turned in the saddle and looked directly up the hillside, directly at the gorse, directly at her — a long, level, unreadable stare, the stare of a woman adding two and two and getting an answer she hadn't reported to anyone yet.

Then she clicked her tongue, and the column moved on, westward, toward the capital, and the cage swung in time to the trotting, and the last thing Casper heard drifting back up the valley was Bracken's voice starting up again, undiminished, unbowed, cataloguing the personal failings of the entire royal hunt in alphabetical order.

She stayed in the gorse until the stars came out. Then she sat in the shed doorway with the cheese untouched in her lap and cried the way she hadn't cried since she was small — silently, furiously, uselessly — while the flooded quarry held the moon in its green water and the valley kept all her secrets, even the ones she wished it would shout.

He had traded himself for her. He had stood in the mud and roared at spears for her. And she had hidden in the bushes and counted to a hundred, just like he asked.

Somewhere down the valley, in an iron cage too small for him, an old badger was spending his fourth life so that she could keep her silence.

Casper wiped her face, wrapped the cheese, and began, in the dark, to plan.

Because the writ said the prisoners went to the capital. And the capital, whatever else it was, lay at the end of a road — and roads, unlike valleys, forgot nothing, and neither would she.
