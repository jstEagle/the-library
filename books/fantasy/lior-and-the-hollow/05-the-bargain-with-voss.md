## 5. The Bargain with Voss

Casper waited until the third watch of the night, when even the goats had stopped complaining about the smell of strangers on the wind.

She had spent the day doing what she did best: nothing that looked like anything. She'd mended a fence post with the same three nails her father had used before her, because in the Hollow you didn't waste nails and you didn't waste motion. She'd milked the goats. She'd scowled at the riders camped beyond her gate as if they were merely an inconvenient weather pattern. And all the while she had listened — to the sparrows in the eaves, to the miller's dog chained by the wheel, to the mice in the granary who gossiped worse than any widow.

What they told her had turned her blood to well water: cold, clear, and full of things she didn't want to see.

The badger was alive. That was the whole of the good news, and it came from a raven who had followed the soldiers' cook wagon out of sheer professional interest. *Old one fought like a landslide,* the raven reported, preening. *Bit a man's thumb near off. They've got him in a cage on a cart, and he's swearing at them in words nobody understands but me, and I'm not repeating the worst ones.*

But the hounds were still here. Three of them, big black-and-tan brutes quartering the valley with their noses down, working the treeline where Bracken had burst from cover. Working, slowly and methodically, toward her farm.

They couldn't find her scent — the Hollow saw to that, wrapping her in its magic-dead blankness like a blanket pulled over a child's head. But hounds didn't need magic to be patient, and the men holding their leads had orders, and orders in the Hollow now came with a crier's seal and the king's own crest burned into wax.

So Casper sat on her doorstep in the dark, whittling a stick into nothing, and made the decision she'd been circling since noon the way a moth circles a lamp.

If she ran, they would burn her farm looking for her, and then they would burn the neighbors' farms asking after her, and the elders who had pledged cooperation would pledge harder, and someone — old Marta, probably, who still called her "girl" and slipped her honey cakes — would remember something useful under pressure. The Hollow had survived four hundred years by forgetting. It could not survive being remembered.

And Bracken was in a cage because he had come out of the brush swinging for her sake, an arthritic badger against six armed men, which was either the bravest or stupidest thing anyone had ever done on her behalf, and possibly both.

"There's no version of this," she said aloud to the dark, "where I'm the kind of person who lets that stand."

The dark, politely, said nothing back. That was the trouble with the Hollow. Even your own voice sounded alone in it.

* * *

Captain Elara Voss did not sleep in tents.

This was not vanity, though her lieutenant had once suggested it was. It was habit: she slept wherever the map was, and the map was always spread on the captain's table, and tonight the table was the miller's kitchen, and the map was the Hollow itself, and she hated every inch of it.

She sat with a cup of the miller's cider going soft beside her elbow and studied the inked lines. Rivers. Roads. The treeline where the badger had ambushed her men — she'd walked it herself at dusk, reading the ground the way other people read letters. A fighting retreat. Deliberate. Loud on purpose. Something small and old had thrown itself at armed riders to buy time for something else to run.

*Something.* Not someone. The reports all said beast-speaker, and beast-speakers were people, but the badger had been the loud part, so what was the quiet part?

A knock. Not her sentry's knock — two knuckles, casual, wrong.

"Enter," she said, and put her hand flat on the knife beside the map.

The door opened on a young woman carrying a lantern and a basket, of all things. Plain wool dress, hair tied back with string, cheeks pink from the cold. She looked exactly like what she was pretending to be: a farm girl delivering eggs at an hour no sane person delivered eggs.

"You're the captain," the girl said. Not a question.

"I'm the captain."

"I brought bread." She set the basket on the table, next to the map, with the calm of someone placing a bet. "Because I thought this might take a while, and I didn't want you getting hungry and making a bad decision."

Elara looked at the bread. Then at the girl. Then at the door, where her sentry stood with the expression of a man who had been thoroughly talked at. "You're the quiet part," she said.

"I'm the quiet part." The girl sat down across from her without being asked, which Elara noted, and without fear, which she noted harder. "My name's Casper Joubert. I keep goats up past the south fence. I've lived in this valley my whole life, and until three days ago, the most interesting thing that ever happened here was when the Hendersons' cow got into the church."

"And now?"

"Now there's a badger in a cage on your cook wagon who took a sword-blow meant for me, and three hounds sniffing at my fence line, and a proclamation nailed to the mill that says the crown takes beast-speakers for questioning." She folded her hands on the table. Clean hands, work-worn. Steady. "I'm a beast-speaker, Captain. Have been since I was six. And I've come to make you a bargain."

It was, Elara thought later, the strangest arrest she never made. The girl had walked into her custody voluntarily, announced her own crime, and started negotiating before Elara had finished deciding whether to reach for the irons.

"You understand," Elara said carefully, "that I'm required to take you in regardless of what you offer."

"I understand you're required to try." Casper's eyes were pale gray and entirely unimpressed. "I also understand that your hounds have been at my fence for two days and haven't caught my scent, and that your men have searched my farm twice and found nothing but goats and bad poetry, and that if I hadn't walked through your front door tonight, you'd have left this valley with one badger and no answers. So let's skip the part where we pretend you're holding all the cards."

The sentry made a sound that might have been a swallowed laugh. Elara silenced him with a look that had ended careers.

"Talk," she said.

* * *

Here is what Casper offered, laid out on the miller's table between the bread and the map:

Names. Not many — the Hollow kept its secrets the way a stone keeps water — but some. The falconer's son two valleys over who screamed in hawk. The widow on the far ridge whose bees came when she hummed. Half-remembered stories from her grandmother about a family in the marsh country, gone now or gone quiet. Enough true detail, each piece verifiable, to prove she wasn't selling smoke.

"And in exchange," she said, "the badger goes free. Unharmed. Released at the valley border with his dignity intact, or as much of it as a creature who bit a royal soldier can retain."

"That's not how this works." Elara heard her own voice go flat and official, and hated it. "Prisoners taken under the Gifted Edict are remanded to the capital for—"

"For questioning. Yes. I read the proclamation. It's very grand." Casper leaned forward. "Captain, I watched your men load him onto that cart. He's twenty years old, which for a badger is older than old, and he's lame in the left foreleg, and he hasn't eaten since they took him because he won't touch food from their hands. Do you know what happens to him in the capital? Because I do. Everyone in the Hollow knows what happens to the ones the crown questions. They stop coming back, or they come back wrong."

"The edict says—"

"I know what the edict says." For the first time, something moved under the girl's steadiness — a crack of heat, quickly covered. "The edict says the gifted are taken for questioning. It doesn't say questioned by whom, or how, or why the questioning needs thumbscrews. My grandmother used to say a law that has to hide what it does isn't a law, it's a costume." She sat back. "You flinched just now. When I said thumbscrews. You knew already."

Elara said nothing. The cider had gone completely flat. Somewhere outside, one of the hounds bayed at nothing, and the sound went through her like a hook.

Three years she had served the Hunt. Three years of believing — needing to believe — that the crown's business was grim but necessary, that the interrogators in the capital were harsh the way surgeons are harsh, that the screams she tried not to hear through the wagon walls were the price of a kingdom's safety. She had built that belief plank by plank, and this goat-girl with flour on her sleeves had kicked the whole structure with one sentence.

"I don't have authority to release prisoners," she said at last. It came out quieter than she intended.

"No," Casper agreed. "But you have authority over the escort. Over the route. Over whether the cage gets tarps against the rain, and whether the guards are reminded that the prisoner is a national treasure to be handled gently, and whether the man whose thumb he bit gets assigned to guard duty or gets reassigned somewhere far away." She shrugged. "I'm not asking you to break your oath, Captain. I'm asking you to interpret it generously. There's a difference, and everyone who's ever worn a uniform knows exactly where the line is, because they've spent their whole career standing on it."

The audacity of it. Elara almost laughed. Almost.

"And what do I get," she asked, "if I interpret generously? Besides names I could shake out of you with less trouble?"

"You get me," Casper said simply. "Voluntary. Cooperative. Walking in, no irons needed, no scene at the farm, no neighbors watching and remembering your face with hate in it. You get a prisoner who'll answer questions instead of screaming through them. And—" she hesitated, and for a moment the negotiator vanished and something younger looked out, something tired, "—and you get to be the kind of officer who can sleep in whatever room the map is in. I'd guess that's getting harder to come by."

The silence stretched. Outside, the hound bayed again, closer to the fence line now, patient as winter.

Elara looked at the girl across the table and saw, with uncomfortable clarity, herself at nineteen — the self that had joined the Hunt because somebody had to enforce order, because law was the wall between the kingdom and the wild dark, because her father had died holding that wall and she would be damned before she let it crumble. She had wanted to be the shield. She had not expected the shield to be used as a lid.

"You could run," Elara said. "The valley hides you. Your beasts warned you, I assume — they always do. You'd have had days of lead on us. Why walk in here?"

"Because he stayed." Casper's voice did something complicated on the word *he*. "He's a badger. He weighs as much as a sack of turnips and he's got half a lung and he attacked six armed men so I could get clear. If I'd run, everything he bought with that would've been worth nothing. I don't know how to make that worth nothing." She met Elara's eyes. "You do understand that. Don't you? That's why you're sitting there looking like a woman chewing a wasp. Because you understand exactly what I'm asking, and you understand that it's right, and you're trying to find the crack in it where you're allowed to say yes."

Elara stood. Walked to the window. The camp fires glimmered among the wagons, and beyond them the dark bulk of the hills, and beyond those, three weeks' hard road to the capital, where a badger in a cage was waiting to learn what the crown's questions felt like.

"If I do this," she said, without turning around, "and anyone learns I traded a prisoner's treatment for intelligence — unofficially, off the record, in a miller's kitchen at midnight — I hang. Or worse. The tribunal doesn't forgive officers who go soft."

"Then we'd better make sure nobody learns it," Casper said. "That's generally how bargains work."

* * *

They shook on it, which surprised them both. Elara because she hadn't decided to; her hand had simply extended, acting on some verdict reached below the level of her rank. Casper because, she admitted later, she'd expected to be in irons by now and had planned her exit routes accordingly, and it was disorienting to have plans go unused.

"One condition of mine," Elara said, as the girl gathered her lantern. "The names you gave me. If I find out any of them were false — if you've sent my men chasing ghosts while someone real slips away — our bargain dies, and I will personally put you in the finest cage the crown owns."

"They're true." Casper paused at the door. "Every one. I don't sell ghosts, Captain. Ghosts are all anybody else seems to sell these days." Then, softer, almost to herself: "He likes honey, by the way. The badger. If the guards want the food touched, put honey on it. He can't resist honey. It's undignified and he knows it."

And she was gone into the dark, leaving behind a basket of bread, a list of names, and a captain standing alone in a miller's kitchen feeling as though she had just been outmaneuvered by someone armed only with honesty.

* * *

At dawn, Elara gave the orders.

The badger's cage was moved to the front of the column, under tarp against the rain, guarded by her two steadiest men with explicit instructions that the prisoner was evidence and evidence was not to be bruised. Corporal Fenwick — the thumb — found himself detailed to scout the rear, a posting he accepted with the sullen grace of a man who suspected he'd been managed and couldn't prove it.

The girl came down from her farm at first light with a pack on her shoulder and her goats penned and provisioned for a fortnight, having apparently arranged their care with a neighbor through means she declined to explain. She surrendered her knife handle-first, thanked the sentry for opening the gate, and complimented the hounds — at which all three brutes promptly sat down and stared at her with the rapt devotion of churchgoers, to the visible alarm of their handlers.

"What did you do to them?" one handler demanded.

"Nothing," said Casper. "They're sweet. They miss having their ears scratched properly. You scratch them like you're polishing boots."

Elara watched from horseback as the column formed up, and felt the strange double weight of the morning: the satisfaction of a bargain kept, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the first cold thread of doubt about everything the bargain was for. The girl climbed into the prison wagon without being told, settled herself beside the tarp-covered cage, and began, quietly, to talk to whatever was under the canvas in a low voice full of honey and home.

The badger answered. Nobody but the girl understood a word of it.

Elara turned her horse toward the capital road, toward three weeks of escort duty and a tribunal waiting at the end of it, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the cold.

Behind her, under the tarp, an old badger pressed his scarred face against the bars and asked his girl, for the fourth time, whether she had lost her mind — and she laughed, and told him she'd ask the captain, because the captain seemed to be wondering the same thing.

And ahead, three weeks down that road, in a palace where the king had not spoken his own words in three years, something ancient stirred in its borrowed crown and listened — patiently, hungrily — for a voice it had begun, very faintly, to hope for.
