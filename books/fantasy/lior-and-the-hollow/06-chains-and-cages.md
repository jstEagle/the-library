## 6. Chains and Cages

Elara Voss had marched prisoners before. She had told herself, in the early years, that it was simple logistics — bodies moved from one place to another, like grain or timber, and a captain who thought too hard about grain was a captain who starved her men.

She was finding it harder to think of people as grain lately.

The convoy had left the Hollow at dawn on the third day, forty riders and eleven prisoners in a rattling line of wagons that crawled east along the old trade road toward the capital. The valley's elders had watched them go from behind their shutters, faces pale and carefully blank, the way people watch a flood recede and pretend they never needed anything from the water.

Elara rode at the head because riding at the head meant she didn't have to ride at the back, where the wagons were.

"You're doing it again," said Dain, falling in beside her horse. Her lieutenant had a soldier's talent for reading her silences, which she'd once counted among his virtues and was beginning to reconsider.

"Doing what?"

"Thinking. Loudly."

"Then I'm doing my job twice," Elara said. "Ride on."

He grinned and dropped back, obedient in everything except the matter of leaving her alone, which he never managed. Twenty-two years old, earnest as a hound pup, promoted twice on her recommendation. She kept waiting for the day his earnestness curdled into something useful and cruel, the way it did in every other officer she'd served under. It hadn't happened yet. Some mornings she found herself almost proud of that. Most mornings it just made her tired.

The road climbed out of the valley's green throat and into hill country where the wind smelled of pine and distance. Behind her, the wagons creaked. In the first wagon rode three adults from the Hollow's edge-farms, sullen and frightened. In the second, two brothers who ran a ferry and had apparently been overheard talking to their mule in a manner the local reeve had found suspicious. In the third —

In the third rode the child.

* * *

Her name was Mirei. Elara knew this because she'd read the intake ledger, because knowing names was one of the last professional habits she still respected. Seven years old, maybe eight. Dark hair cut raggedly, the way children cut their own hair when no one is watching closely enough. She had been taken from a fishing village two valleys south of the Hollow when a neighbor reported that gulls brought the girl things. Shiny things. Gifts.

Elara had signed the transport order herself. She remembered the weight of the pen, absurdly heavy for such a small act.

The interrogators traveled with the convoy — two of them, Crown Warrant holders, soft-handed men in gray coats who did not ride and did not walk and did not sweat. Their name, collectively, was Master Quillon and Assistant Fenwick, though Elara suspected those were not so much names as job titles worn like coats.

On the fourth morning, at the rest halt outside the ruined chapel at Marrow Ford, Elara saw what the gray coats actually did.

She had been checking the picket lines when she heard it — a sound small enough to be dismissed as a bird, if a man wanted very badly to dismiss things. It came again. Not a bird.

The wagons were drawn up in the chapel's shadow. The third wagon's canvas flap was tied open, and inside, half in the shade, Master Quillon crouched beside Mirei with something in his hands — a tuning fork, Elara realized later, a silver thing that hummed at a pitch that made her own teeth ache from six yards away. He held it near the girl's ear and turned it, patiently, the way a man might turn a key in a lock he expected to open.

Mirei was making the small sound again. Over and over. A sound with no words in it.

Fenwick stood at the wagon's tail taking notes. "No response to auditory stimulus," he said. "Subject continues to deny communication with animal life."

"She's communicating with something," Quillon observed mildly. "Just not with us." He adjusted the fork. The hum climbed. Mirei pressed her hands flat over her ears, which were already bleeding thinly at the edges, and made the small sound again, faster now, like a heart trying to outrun its own chest.

Elara stood there in the morning light with her hand resting on her sword hilt and did absolutely nothing, and the nothing would stay with her longer than most of the somethings she'd ever done.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been taught: the Hunt existed to protect the realm. The realm's king commanded the Hunt. The king's warrant authorized questioning. Questioning required pressure, and pressure on the gifted required particular instruments, because the gifted could lie in ways ordinary tongues couldn't. Therefore the humming fork. Therefore the small sound. Therefore the ledger entry, in her own eventual hand: *transport conducted without incident.*

Without incident.

Mirei looked up then, past Quillon's shoulder, and her eyes found Elara across the yard with the terrible directness of children and animals — the ones who haven't learned yet that authority is supposed to be invisible. She looked at Elara the way you look at a door.

Then Quillon shifted, blocking the view, and hummed the fork again, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

Elara walked away. She checked horses that did not need checking. She counted saddle nails. And somewhere between the picket line and the far end of the camp, without deciding to, without permitting herself to decide to, she began committing small crimes.

* * *

The first crime was trivial. The convoy's orders specified water rations for prisoners at half measure — "to discourage excess conversation," per the appendix, as though thirst were an interrogation technique rather than cruelty wearing a clerk's collar. Elara reviewed the water stores, announced that the barrels had developed a leak, and redistributed the shortfall onto the guard rotation, doubling the prisoners' share while reducing everyone else's by a mouthful apiece. When Dain raised an eyebrow, she said, "Dehydrated guards make mistakes," and he nodded and wrote it down, because Dain believed his captain even when she gave him reasons that were slightly wrong-shaped.

The second crime was pettier and therefore more dangerous, because petty crimes are the ones a person stops noticing. Quillon requisitioned the prisoners' blankets for his instruments' padding. Elara discovered the requisition form improperly countersigned and returned it — twice — until the paperwork delayed the confiscation past the point of cold nights. Fenwick complained to her about bureaucratic obstruction. She agreed with him warmly and blamed a quartermaster in the capital who, as far as she knew, had died the previous winter.

"You're enjoying this," Dain said that evening, watching her forge a signature with more flourish than strictly necessary.

"I am performing my duty with exceptional thoroughness."

"You're hiding a knife in your sleeve and calling it a pen."

"Dain." She blew on the ink. "When you've served as long as I have, you learn that the empire runs on paper. Paper is how we fight. Paper is how we win."

"Win what?"

She didn't answer that. She wasn't sure she could yet.

* * *

It was on the sixth day that Casper Joubert noticed.

Elara had put the beast-speaker in the second wagon with the ferry brothers, chained at the ankle like the rest, mostly because keeping her close meant keeping her visible. Joubert was a problem Elara hadn't solved. The girl had surrendered herself voluntarily — walked into Elara's tent the night after the badger was taken, chin up, terrified, offering information on other gifted in exchange for the animal's life. There had been no other gifted to give up; Elara had checked, quietly, and found the girl's list consisted of a lame badger and, apparently, "most of the goats, but only when they're gossiping."

Which meant Joubert had traded herself for nothing. For an animal. Which meant either the girl was a fool, or she was the kind of honest that the crown's machinery had no slot for, and both possibilities sat in Elara's chest like swallowed stones.

So: second wagon, visible, alive. For now.

They halted at noon in a beech wood where the light came down green and dappled, and Elara was reviewing the day's route when she heard Quillon's voice rise — not angry, never angry, just *mild*, that terrible mildness — and turned to see the gray coats at the third wagon again, the fork out, Mirei's small sound starting up like a cracked bell.

And she saw Joubert watching.

The girl stood chained at the wagon's rail, and her face did something Elara had seen on battlefields and hoped never to see on a farm girl: it went from horror to understanding to decision, all in the space of a breath. Joubert tested her chain, found its length, and then — deliberately, loudly, in a voice pitched to carry — began to sing.

It was not a good song. It was, in fact, a goat-herding song from the western valleys, tuneless and repetitive, about a billy named Ambrose who ate a hat. But she sang it at full volume, off-key, clanking her chain in rhythm, and every head in the camp turned toward her, including Quillon's, including Fenwick's, including — Elara noted with a strange lurch — Mirei's.

"What is that noise?" Fenwick demanded, stalking over.

"That's Ambrose," Joubert said solemnly. "He ate a hat. It's a cautionary tale. Would you like the verse where he eats the second hat? It's structurally identical but emotionally devastating."

"This is a prisoner transport, not a music hall—"

"And yet the acoustics are excellent." She gestured at the beeches with her chained hands, gracious as a court performer. "You should hear the echo off the far ridge. Very flattering to the low notes, which, admittedly, are all I have."

Fenwick looked at Quillon. Quillon looked at Joubert with the expression of a man doing sums and disliking the total. Then, with a small shrug, he turned back to the wagon — and the fork came up again, and Mirei flinched, and the whole ugly arithmetic resumed beneath the cover of a girl singing about hats.

But something had changed, and Elara felt it change. Because Joubert hadn't stopped singing. She'd sung *louder*, and she'd angled herself so that her body blocked the guards' sightline to the child, and she kept singing — verse after verse, new verses, improvised verses in which Ambrose ate progressively larger hats, a bishop's mitre, eventually a small boat — until Quillon, deprived of quiet, deprived of focus, deprived of the silence his instrument needed, lowered the fork with a sigh of a craftsman denied his workshop.

"Tomorrow," he said to Fenwick, packing the tool away. "We'll need proper quiet tomorrow. Speak to the captain about segregating the loud one."

"Yes, Master Quillon."

Elara stood ten feet away, holding her route map, having heard every word, and met Joubert's eyes over the wagon rail.

The girl stopped singing mid-hat. For a moment neither of them said anything. Around them the camp bustled, oblivious, and in the third wagon Mirei had gone quiet too, her small sound finally stilled, her bleeding ears tipped toward the second wagon as though listening for whether the song would come back.

"You'll want to know," Joubert said quietly, "that Ambrose survived. The hat situation resolved itself. I can tell you how, if it helps."

"It doesn't help," Elara said.

"No," Joubert agreed. "I didn't think it would. I sang anyway." She shrugged, chains clinking. "Seemed like the sort of day that needed a song about hats."

Elara looked at her for a long moment — this farm girl with hay in her hair and a badger's life on her conscience and blood on her knuckles where she'd gripped the wagon rail too hard — and felt the fracture run through her own chest, clean as an axe-stroke, the place where the machine she'd served for fifteen years split into the part that worked and the part that simply hurt.

"I'll speak to the interrogators about the schedule," Elara heard herself say. "Segregation requires my authorization. I'll... review the request."

"Review it slowly," Joubert said.

"I always do."

Something passed between them then that Elara had no rank insignia for. Joubert's mouth twitched — not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might someday build a house.

* * *

That night, making her rounds under a sky bruised purple with coming rain, Elara paused at the third wagon. Mirei lay curled on the bare boards, awake, watching her with those door-looking eyes.

Elara crouched. From her coat she drew the extra blanket she'd "lost" from inventory that afternoon, and pushed it through the wagon slats without a word.

Mirei took it. Held it. Did not thank her — children who have learned to fear gray coats have also, sensibly, learned not to trust any coat — but she pulled it around her shoulders, and after a long moment she made a small motion with one hand. Fingers opening. Closing. Opening.

Not a wave. Elara stared at it, uncomprehending, until the meaning arrived sideways, the way meanings do: it was the way you'd move your hand to mimic a bird landing. Or a gull dropping something shiny into small waiting palms.

*I see you*, the gesture said. *I know what you did.*

Elara rose quickly and walked on, heart hammering like a recruit's, and told herself the whole way across the camp that she had done nothing worth noticing, that a blanket was a blanket, that a delayed requisition was a delayed requisition, that she was still — in every way that mattered to her pension, her commission, and her king — a loyal officer of the Gifted Hunt.

Behind her, in the second wagon, she heard Casper Joubert begin to hum.

And in the shadows between the wagons, unnoticed by anyone, a thin man with ink-stained fingers and the restless eyes of a scholar pretending to sleep opened one eye, and watched the captain walk by, and smiled faintly to himself as if he had just confirmed a very interesting theory.
