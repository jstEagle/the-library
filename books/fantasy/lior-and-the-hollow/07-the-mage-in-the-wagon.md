## 7. The Mage in the Wagon

The convoy moved at the pace of grief.

That was how Casper had come to think of it by the third day — not a march, not a procession, but something that dragged itself along the king's road the way a wounded animal drags itself toward water it will never reach. Cages on wheels. Ropes between riders. And threaded through all of it, the smell of fear, which was strange to her now, because for twenty-two years she had lived in a valley where magic couldn't follow and so neither could most of its consequences.

She rode in the third wagon, wrists bound with cord that had been dipped in something bitter — Sef would later tell her it was ashroot tincture, though at the time she only knew it made her skin itch and her nose wrinkle like a goat's when it smells rain coming. The wagon held four other prisoners: a fisherman from the low country who spoke to eels; a grandmother whose gift, as far as anyone could tell, was making hens lay in winter; and two brothers who communicated entirely in whistles and had been doing so since before the soldiers came, out of what appeared to be pure habit rather than any attempt at secrecy.

The grandmother's name was Odile. She had spent the first day telling Casper her whole life story in exhaustive detail — three husbands, one of them bigamous by accident, a recipe for plum brandy that Casper memorized mostly to have something other than dread to hold onto — and then, somewhere past the second milestone of the second day, she had simply stopped talking and started humming.

The humming was worse than the talking.

Casper leaned her head against the wagon's slatted side and watched the country go by. It was good country. That was the thing that kept catching in her chest like a fishbone. Green hills stitched with stone walls, orchards heavy enough that the branches needed crutches, a river flashing silver whenever the road bent toward it. A kingdom worth living in, if you didn't look too closely at the wagons rolling through it.

*Bracken,* she thought, for perhaps the ten-thousandth time. *Hold on.*

She had no way of knowing if he could hear her even if he were near, which he wasn't. The badger had been taken east with the first group of prisoners while she'd been marched west with this lot, which struck her as either incompetence or cruelty dressed up as logistics. Three days of road, and every mile took her further from the one creature in the world who had ever known her whole self and loved her anyway.

"You're doing the face again," said Odile, without opening her eyes. "The goat face."

"I don't have a goat face."

"Everyone has a goat face. Yours is just more honest than most." The old woman cracked one eye. "Eat your bread. Fainting in chains is undignified. I've seen it done."

Casper ate her bread. It was the kind of bread that had given up on being bread somewhere around dawn, but hunger is an excellent sauce, and she got it down.

It was near midday, with the sun high enough to make the wagon boards sweat pitch-smell, that she noticed the man in the corner.

He'd been there since the start, she realized — folded into the shadow behind the fisherman like a piece of the wagon itself. Thin. Young-ish, though the kind of young that exhaustion ages into something else. He wore a prisoner's gray like everyone else, but there was something wrong about the fit, the way there's something wrong about a scholar wearing a farmer's coat: the shoulders sat wrong, the hands emerged from sleeves that expected different work. His fingers kept moving. Not fidgeting — *tracing*. Little patterns on his knee, over and over, like a man practicing letters in a language with no alphabet.

And his eyes, when they flicked up and found hers, went very still.

Not afraid-still. *Recognizing*-still.

Casper knew that look. She'd spent her whole life learning to read faces the way other people read weather, because a girl with a secret learns early that faces are where secrets leak. She knew the look of someone who'd spotted the shape of her under her shape. She'd seen it once before, years ago, on a traveling peddler who'd watched her calm a spooked mule with nothing but a murmur, and she'd spent a week afterward convinced he'd sell her to the nearest magistrate. He hadn't. He'd sold her a comb instead and never come back. But the look — the look she remembered like a scar.

This man had it. Worse.

She looked away. Looked at the road. Counted fence posts. When she risked a glance back, he was still watching, and now he did something small and deliberate: he tapped his own chest twice, then pointed, very slightly, at the hedgerow they were passing — where a wren was scolding the convoy with the full fury of its tiny body.

*What's it saying?* the tap meant. Plain as speech.

Casper's stomach dropped through the wagon floor and kept going.

She shook her head, slow, once. *You're mistaken.*

His mouth twitched. Not a smile exactly. More like the ghost of patience. Then he closed his eyes and appeared to sleep, and did not open them again until the wagons halted for the night.

* * *

They camped in a fallow field ringed by elms, the cages drawn into a rough square with the soldiers' fires outside them. Casper learned the rhythm of it quickly: the clink of tack, the mutter of men too tired to be cruel, the particular silence of the gifted, who had all, without discussing it, decided to be as boring as possible. The whistle-brothers hadn't whistled since noon. The eel-fisherman stared at his hands. Even Odile's humming had gone quiet, replaced by a stillness that looked, from a distance, like peace and felt, up close, like a held breath.

Casper sat with her back against the cage bars and listened to the horses.

She couldn't help it. It was like being thirsty next to a well. The picket line stood forty feet off, eleven animals dozing on their feet, and their dreams drifted across the dark to her in fragments — green fields, the itch of flies, a memory of apples stolen from somebody's saddlebag (the bay mare, unrepentant), and underneath it all a low, continuous unease, the way horses are always uneasy near certain people. Near *him*, specifically. The thin man in the corner of her wagon had been transferred to the separate mage-wagon at dusk — she'd watched two soldiers walk him over with the exaggerated casualness of men handling something they'd been told was dangerous — and the horses had all turned their heads to watch him go, ears forward, in perfect unison, like a congregation tracking a preacher.

Horses didn't do that for ordinary prisoners.

The moon rose, half-full and indifferent. Somewhere beyond the firelight, a nightjar churred. Casper let her head tip back against the bars and did the thing she'd done every night since Bracken was taken: she sent her listening out anyway, uselessly, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. No answer. Of course no answer. Whatever badger-shaped hole existed in the world tonight, it was miles away and getting farther.

"You can stop pretending," said a voice at her ear.

She nearly jumped out of her skin. The thin man was crouched on the other side of the bars — he must have crossed the camp between watches, silent as spilled milk — with his fingers laced through the gaps and his face close, lit faintly blue by some light that wasn't firelight and wasn't moonlight either.

"Don't," Casper whispered. "Whatever you're about to say. Don't."

"The wren this afternoon," he said, ignoring her completely, voice pitched under the crackle of the fires. "It was complaining about the bell on the lead rider's horse. Said it rang wrong. Sour." His eyes were very bright. "You heard it too. I watched your face change before it changed its note."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "Of course not. And I'm not a runaway court mage sleeping in a cage I could leave whenever I liked, staying put because leaving means answering questions I'd rather not answer." He tilted his head. "We're both very committed to not knowing things. It's almost restful."

Casper stared at him. Up close, in the weird blue glow — which she now realized was coming from *him*, from somewhere under his skin, leaking out the way heat leaks from a stone at midnight — he looked less like a scholar and more like a fox that had been asked to impersonate one and was doing admirably well.

"Who are you?" she breathed.

"Sef." He said it lightly, a card tossed on a table. "Formerly of the Court College, Office of Resonances, third chair, which sounds grander than it was. Mostly I tuned things. Bells, wards, occasionally ministers' egos." A pause. "I ran eight months ago. They caught me Tuesday, outside Marrowford, because I stopped to argue with a cat about whether it had actually seen what it claimed to have seen, and it had, and by the time I finished apologizing to it, the riders were there."

Despite everything — despite the cords biting her wrists and the smell of ashroot and the ten thousand thoughts of Bracken — Casper felt her mouth twitch. "You apologized to a cat."

"It had *seen something*. You don't dismiss that." He said it with complete sincerity, and something in Casper's chest, wound tight for three days, loosened by one grudging notch. "Anyway. That's my confession. Here's yours, or the shape of it: you speak beast. Truly speak, not the hedge-cunning of knowing a dog's mood. And you've hidden it your whole life, which means you grew up somewhere the Hunt doesn't reach." His bright eyes narrowed with genuine curiosity. "Which interests me enormously, because there are only a handful of places in this kingdom where a gift can grow up unseen, and I've spent eight months studying all of them."

"The Hollow," Casper said, before her brain could stop her mouth.

Sef went very still. "I knew it," he whispered. "I *knew* it. Everyone says the Hollow is empty of magic. Dead ground. But dead ground isn't empty, is it — it's *muffled*. Something under that valley eats resonance. Do you know what that means? Do you have any idea—"

"Keep your voice down!"

"—do you know what lives in a place like that, untouched, unmeasured, for centuries—" He caught himself, physically pressed his lips together, exhaled through his nose. "Sorry. Sorry. Occupational hazard. Tuners get excited about acoustics."

From across the fire, a soldier laughed at something. Both of them froze. The moment stretched, snapped, passed.

"Why are you here?" Casper asked finally. "Really. If you could leave the cage, why stay in it?"

Something shifted in Sef's face — the fox-mask slipping, showing the tired young man underneath. He glanced back toward the mage-wagon, then at the distant tents where the interrogators slept, then at the child.

Casper followed his gaze and her stomach clenched. The child had been brought in that morning, found hiding in a root cellar two villages back — a girl, seven or eight maybe, with a scraped-shin scab and hair cut raggedly short, the way children cut it themselves when they're running. She hadn't spoken a word since capture. She didn't need to. Every animal within a mile of the camp had been telling Casper about her all day: the sparrows' agitation, the camp dogs' whining, the way the picket horses kept turning their hindquarters toward the interrogators' tent like a wall built of haunches.

"They questioned her today," Sef said quietly. "For an hour. She's eight. Her gift is mice — she talks to mice, that's all, mice and nothing else, and they put her hands in the ashroot cuffs and asked her questions until she cried herself silent." His jaw worked. "Tomorrow they'll try the other method. There's always another method. That's why I'm still in the cage. Because tomorrow, when they take her out, I'm going to be standing between her and whatever 'the other method' turns out to be, and a mage in a cage is a prisoner, and a prisoner interfering is a problem, whereas a mage who's *escaped* is a hunt, and hunts bring riders, and riders bring—" He stopped. Rubbed his eyes. "I'm explaining badly. I've been explaining badly for eight months. It's the isolation."

"You're going to get yourself killed," Casper said.

"Probably." He said it the way Odile might say *it'll rain*. "But listen — listen, because this is the part that matters, and I've been trying to say it to someone for eight months and everyone I tried to say it to was a cat." He gripped the bars. The blue light under his skin flared, and he smothered it with visible effort, like pinching out a candle. "Why do you think the Hunt exists? Really? You've heard the criers. *All beast-speakers to be taken for questioning.* Questioning about what?"

Casper frowned. "Rebellion. The elders said—"

"The elders said what they were told to say. There is no rebellion of beasts. There has never been a rebellion of beasts. Beasts don't rebel; they *remember*." His voice dropped to almost nothing, and the night seemed to lean in to hear it. "Three years ago, King Aldric stopped speaking his own words."

The sentence landed strangely, like a step missing at the bottom of a stair.

"What does that mean?"

"It means exactly what it says. I was there. Third chair, Office of Resonances — we tuned the throne room twice a year, every word the king spoke passed through our calibration. And three years ago, after the winter court, the king's voice changed. Not the sound of it — the *weight*. Words are resonances, girl; every voice has a signature, a grain, like wood. Aldric's grain was warm. Low. A little rough on the left side, from an old fever. And then one morning it was smooth. Perfectly smooth. Perfectly *even*. Like a voice sanded down." Sef's hands had gone white on the bars. "I filed a report. My master burned it. I filed another. I was reassigned to tuning granary wards in the provinces. That was my answer."

Casper's mouth had gone dry. "People change."

"People don't change their grain. And the animals—" He laughed, but there was no humor in it, only a kind of horror worn smooth by repetition. "You want to know why the crown fears beast-speakers? Ask yourself what beasts notice that people don't. Horses won't enter the throne room anymore. Did you know that? They re-shod the ceremonial team six times last year trying to fix it. The palace ravens left — all of them, overnight, three years ago, the rookery stands empty. Dogs howl at the palace walls on certain nights. The king hasn't spoken his own words in three years, and every animal in the capital knows it, and the only creatures who could ever *tell* us are the ones the Hunt is dragging away in cages."

The nightjar had stopped calling. Casper became aware of her own heartbeat, loud and stupid in her ears.

"That's madness," she said, but weakly, because her mind was already turning it over, testing the joints, finding none loose. The horses' unease. The dogs' whining. The way the whole camp's animals had organized themselves, wordless, around the child's suffering — beasts *remembering*, keeping accounts.

"Is it?" Sef said softly. "You speak to beasts. Tell me — in all your life, has an animal ever lied to you?"

She thought of Bracken. Of his gruff, unhurried voice in her head like a hearth-fire given words. *No,* she thought. *Never once.*

"No," she whispered.

"There it is." Sef settled back on his heels, and for a moment the blue light steadied, and he looked at her with an expression she'd learn to know well in the months ahead — the look of a man handing someone a weight and apologizing for it. "The Hunt isn't hunting the gifted, Casper. That's the terrible joke of it. It's searching. Casting a net across the whole kingdom, dragging in every creature who can hear what the animals hear — looking for someone. Or something. A voice, maybe. An answer." He paused. "And the binding under the crown is failing. I've felt it in the bells. Every full moon, a little thinner. A little angrier."

"Binding," Casper repeated. "Under the crown. What binding? What's *under*—"

Boots on grass. Sef moved like water — one heartbeat crouched at her bars, the next flattened in the shadow between wagons, the blue light snuffed as if it had never been. A soldier strolled past the cage on his rounds, yawned, scratched, wandered on.

When Casper looked again, Sef was gone, melted back toward the mage-wagon, and she was left alone with the stars and the horses' uneasy dreams and one sentence circling her skull like a bird that wouldn't land:

*The king hasn't spoken his own words in three years.*

She thought of the proclamation at the mill. The crier's fat, rehearsed voice. *By order of the crown.* She thought of the elders bowing. She thought of Bracken's paw on her hand that last morning, and his warning — *the wind's changed, girl, something's walking in the high places* — which she'd laughed off, because Bracken was dramatic about weather.

Something's walking in the high places. Wearing a crown. Speaking with a sanded-down voice.

Across the camp, the interrogation tent's lamp guttered and went out. In the child's cage, very faintly, Casper heard mice moving — dozens of them, a soft rustling congregation, gathered close around a sleeping girl as if standing guard.

Beasts remember, Sef had said.

Casper closed her eyes and, for the first time since the treeline, prayed — not to anything in particular, just to the general idea of morning — that whatever was failing under the crown would keep failing slowly.

It did not keep failing slowly.

Because at dawn, the convoy turned east, toward the capital — and the horses, all eleven of them, began to sing.
