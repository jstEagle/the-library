## 8. Night Escape

The storm came up the valley of the Sere like a debt collector — no warning, no courtesy, just a wall of black cloud that ate the moon whole.

Elara Voss stood at the edge of the prisoner camp and let the first fat drops hit her face, and for once she was grateful for the weather. Rain meant sentries huddled under oilcloth. Rain meant the interrogators' braziers guttered and the screams from the question-tents went briefly unheard, or at least unheard by her, which was not the same thing but was all she had.

Three days on the road to Corvale. Three days of listening to Warden Hesk's men joke about the child in the third wagon who wouldn't cry anymore because crying took strength she needed for breathing.

Elara had served the crown for nineteen years. She had believed — she still half-believed, in the stubborn way a woman believes in a house that's burning around her — that the Hunt was necessary. That the gifted were dangerous. That cages were kinder than pyres.

Then she'd watched them strap a girl of maybe eight years to a chair and ask her, over and over, what the crows said, and the girl had looked at Elara with eyes gone old and flat as river stones, and something in Elara's chest had quietly, permanently broken.

The rain thickened. Somewhere behind her, a horse screamed at the lightning.

"Captain." Dain appeared at her shoulder, hood up, rain streaming off the leather. "Hesk wants the convoy moving before dawn. Says we'll make the ford by midday."

"The ford will be flooded."

"Hesk says—"

"I heard what Hesk says." She turned to look at her lieutenant. Nineteen years old when she'd found him starving behind a stable, twenty-six now, loyal as a hound and twice as honest. It made what she was about to do feel like drowning a puppy. "Dain. Go check the picket lines. All of them. Take your time."

He hesitated. He was not a stupid man, which was the tragedy of him. "Captain. What are you doing?"

"An officer's business. Go."

* * *

Casper Joubert had learned three things about cages in three days, and none of them were kind.

First: cages smelled. Of fear, of sickness, of the particular sourness of people who had given up pretending they'd ever be free. The badger cage in her memory had never smelled like this. Bracken's den had smelled of earth and honey and his own gruff musk, and she would have given a year of her life to smell it again.

Second: the guards talked more than the prisoners. She'd mapped half the convoy's routine from the wagon next to hers — shift changes, the warden's drinking, the fact that the man called Hesk kept the wagon keys on a ring inside his coat, against regulation, because he didn't trust his own soldiers.

Third: the child in the third wagon hadn't spoken since the question-tent, and every hour of silence coming off that wagon felt like a hand pressing down on Casper's chest.

She sat with her wrists chained to the wagon frame, rain running off her chin, and listened to the storm the way other people prayed. There were no beasts close enough to reach — the convoy's horses were drugged dull, the ravens driven off by the guards' crossbows — but the rain itself spoke, if you knew how to listen. Rain on canvas said *shelter*. Rain on iron said *cold*. And rain on mud, rising, rising, said *river*, said *flood*, said *tonight nothing will be where it was this morning*.

Which was when the wagon cover lifted and Captain Elara Voss climbed in out of the dark, holding a knife.

Casper didn't flinch. She was proud of that later. "Come to do it yourself? Save Hesk the paperwork?"

"Do you ever stop talking?"

"You've met me. No."

Voss knelt, took Casper's chained wrists, and set the knife to the links — and paused. In the lightning-flash through the canvas gap, Casper saw her face properly for the first time without a helmet between them: tired, wet, and terrified in a way she was clearly furious about.

"If I do this," Voss said quietly, "you understand what it costs me. Not you. Me. My rank. My name. Probably my neck. You'll be free and I'll be a traitor, and I need you to understand that before I cut, because after, there's no taking it back for either of us."

Casper studied her. This was the woman who'd taken Bracken. This was the woman whose hounds had circled her farm. Every selfish bone in Casper's body said *let her hang*.

But she'd also watched this woman, two nights ago, stand between Hesk's men and the third wagon and say, in a voice like a drawn blade: *"Not the child. Not tonight. We're short on water; dehydration is slower and I'm short on patience."* And she'd seen the look Hesk gave her — the look a man gives a door he intends to come back for.

"You could have let them have the girl," Casper said. "You didn't."

"Don't mistake one decent hour for a character."

"Too late. I'm a badger person. We decide fast and hold grudges longer." She held out her wrists. "Cut."

The chains parted. Voss worked fast — a spare key from her boot for the ankle irons, a bundle of dry clothes, a hooded cloak. "There's a mage in the wagon behind yours. Sef. He's marked for the tower, not the tents, which means he's worth more alive, which means he's guarded better, which means—" she exhaled through her teeth, "—which means I'm going to need your help, gods help me."

"With what?"

"Lying. Convincingly. To a guard. Right now. Can you do that?"

"Captain," Casper said, pulling the cloak over her head, "I have spent my entire life lying convincingly to an entire valley. Ask me something hard."

* * *

Sef had counted the hours of his captivity the way he'd once counted the seconds of ritual incantations — precisely, obsessively, because precision was the last thing they couldn't take from him.

So he knew it was the fourth bell of night, and he knew the storm had covered the sound of the outer sentry changing post eleven minutes late, and he knew — because he'd spent thirty years reading the small betrayals of faces — that the young guard currently peering into his wagon was about to be lied to by a farm girl and a hunt-captain, and was going to believe every word.

"—Hesk's orders," Casper was saying, her voice pitched into a nasal whine that Sef found genuinely impressive. "Prisoner transfer, special interrogation, the tall one with the gray in his beard. That's this wagon, yeah?"

The guard frowned. "This is the tower wagon. The mage."

"That's what I said. Tall. Gray beard." She gestured vaguely at Sef, who had neither quality, being slight and thirty-nine and clean-shaven. "You saying Hesk can't read his own list?"

"No, I—"

"Because I'm not going back to tell him he's wrong. Are you? Last man who told Hesk he was wrong carried the latrine buckets for a week."

The guard looked at Sef. Sef did the only thing a sensible man in his position could do: he looked confused, elderly, and mildly offended, and said, "I was told there would be a scribe. There is always a scribe. If there is no scribe, I am not moving, and you may inform the warden that my testimony regarding the western garrisons will wait upon a scribe or upon nothing."

It was beautiful. It was the most beautifully weaponized bureaucratic pettiness Sef had ever produced on such short notice, and it worked exactly as intended, because no soldier alive wants to be the reason a prisoner's paperwork goes sideways.

"Fine. Fine! Move him. But the captain signs for it."

"She's right here," Voss said, and signed Hesk's name on Hesk's own transfer chit with handwriting so good it should have been illegal, and then they were walking — Sef between them, hood up, chains rattling authentically — past the fire, past the picket line, past the third wagon.

Casper stopped.

"Casper." Voss's voice, low and sharp.

"He's watching us," Sef murmured. "Keep walking."

But Casper had turned, and through the rain-slick gap in the wagon cover she could see the child — Mirei, someone had said her name was, though no one had used it kindly — curled against the boards with her eyes open. Watching. Old stone eyes.

Casper did the stupid thing. She put her fingers to her lips, then drew them slowly apart — the sign the marsh herons used for *follow, safe, come* — and held the child's gaze until something flickered there. Not hope. Hope was too big a word. But maybe the space where hope might someday build.

Then Voss's hand closed on her arm and hauled her into the dark, and the storm swallowed them.

* * *

They nearly died twice before the treeline.

The first time was the flood. The ford wasn't flooded; the ford was *gone*, replaced by a brown roaring thing that had eaten the crossing stones and was working on the far bank. Voss stood on the muddy rise and stared at it with the expression of a woman recalculating her entire future.

"We can't cross," Sef said.

"We can't stay. Hesk finds that empty wagon by dawn bell and every hound in the company is on our scent by midmorning."

"So we go north along the bank, find the gorge narrows, and pray the high rocks bridge it."

"Pray," Voss repeated flatly. "Right. The mage suggests prayer. Wonderful. Everything's fine."

The second time was the dog.

One hound, left behind at the camp — old, half-lame, chained near the wagons — and it caught their scent at the treeline and came bellowing through the rain with its chain snapping behind it like a whip. Voss had her sword half-drawn before Casper stepped past her.

"What are you doing—"

"*Wait.*"

Casper knelt in the mud, rain hammering her shoulders, and opened herself the way she hadn't dared open herself in days, in front of witnesses, in front of *her* — and spoke.

Not words. Not exactly. Something older, that lived in the chest and the hands and the tilt of the head. *Stop. Not prey. Not enemy. Hurt? Yes. Hurt. Old. Tired. Left behind too.*

The hound skidded to a halt ten feet away, hackles up, rain steaming off its back. Its chain lay taut in the mud. It looked at her — really looked, the way animals look when they realize they've been *heard* — and its tail moved, once, uncertainly.

*Yes,* Casper told it. *I know. Go home. Good dog.*

It turned and limped back toward the fires, and Casper stayed kneeling a moment longer, feeling the shape of the conversation fade out of her like warmth leaving a cup.

When she looked up, Voss was staring at her with an expression Casper couldn't read at all.

"Well," Sef said into the silence, cheerful as a funeral bell. "That settles the question of whether the rumors were true. Shall we run now? I vote we run now."

* * *

Elara had killed men in storms before. She had buried friends in storms. She had once held a bridge for six hours in weather like this with fourteen soldiers and a prayer.

She had never, in nineteen years, done anything as terrifying as what she was doing now, which was nothing. Walking. Putting one boot in front of the other through the black hills with a fugitive beast-speaker and a runaway court mage, while everything she had built burned behind her with every step.

Her career was a specific, countable thing: nineteen years, two commendations, a captaincy earned over the bodies of bandits and worse. She could feel it dissolving with every mile, and what frightened her was how little it hurt. What frightened her was that it felt like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was carrying.

They reached the gorge narrows near dawn, and the flood had indeed bridged itself with fallen pines, and they crossed it shaking and soaked and laughing — actually laughing, all three of them, the unhinged laughter of people who cannot believe they are still alive — and on the far side, in the grey first light, Elara looked at the two of them and understood that something irreversible had happened.

Not the treason. The treason had happened at the wagon. This was different. This was the moment strangers stop being strangers, and she had no defense prepared for it whatsoever.

"The child," Casper said suddenly.

Elara blinked rain out of her eyes. "What?"

"Mirei. Back at the camp. I told her to follow. I *showed* her. She might actually try, Elara, she's eight years old and the camp is a day behind us and if Hesk finds her trying to slip the picket line—"

Elara closed her eyes. She had known this was coming. She had known it from the moment she'd seen the girl's face in the question-tent, known it the way you know a wound is deeper than it looks.

"She won't follow," she said. "Not yet. She's too frightened. Which means we have time — a little. Enough to get clear, find shelter, and then..." She exhaled. "And then I suppose we steal a child from a royal convoy. Because apparently that's who I am now."

"Yes," Casper said simply. "Apparently."

"Don't smile at me like that."

"Like what?"

"Like I'm a *good person*. I took your badger. I dragged you here in chains."

"And then you cut the chains," Casper said. "Badger people keep score, Captain. You're ahead."

* * *

They holed up at midday in a shepherd's bothy, long abandoned, its roof mostly honest. Sef conjured heat with a scrap of charm-work he claimed was harmless and Elara chose to believe him, and for a while the three of them simply existed — drying, shivering, eating the hard bread and dried plums from Voss's emergency kit.

It was Sef who broke the quiet, in the careful voice of a man setting down something heavy.

"You should know what you've bought, Captain. Both of you. This isn't a simple rescue you've stumbled out of." He fed a twig to the little heat-charm and watched it curl. "The king hasn't spoken his own words in three years. I was at court. I heard the change happen. Whatever wears Aldric's crown now, it isn't entirely Aldric — and the Gifted Hunt, the interrogations, the questions about voices and beasts..." His eyes came up, and they were older than his face. "They're not hunting the gifted. They're searching for something. Someone. And I suspect — I *deeply* suspect — that the two of you are sitting closer to the answer than either of you knows."

The rain drummed on the roof. Casper had gone very still, the way she went still when she was listening to something no one else could hear.

"And Bracken?" she said quietly. "The badger. Where do they take the ones worth keeping?"

Elara looked into the fire and told the truth, because these two had earned it and lies were the currency of the life she'd just burned down.

"To the capital," she said. "To the royal menagerie. They keep the strong ones alive." She hesitated, and then finished it, because finishing it was part of the price. "And the king visits them at night. Alone. Nobody knows why."

Outside, the storm rolled on eastward toward Corvale, and somewhere behind them a child named Mirei waited in a cage for a sign she was brave enough to follow, and somewhere ahead a badger sat in the dark of a golden cage, listening to footsteps in the night, waiting for an answer to give.
