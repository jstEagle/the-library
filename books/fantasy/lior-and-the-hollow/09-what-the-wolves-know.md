## 9. What the Wolves Know

Three days out from the burning of the convoy, Casper had learned that freedom smelled like wet wool and fear.

Not her own fear, mostly. Elara's horse, a rangy bay gelding named Turnip — a name so profoundly un-military that Casper had privately decided to like the man on that basis alone — spent every night radiating anxiety through his hooves and into the ground, where Casper could feel it even without speaking to him directly. He was worried about wolves. He was worried about the storm-swollen river they'd forded. He was, most of all, worried about Elara, who had not slept more than two hours at a stretch since she'd put a torch to the prison wagons and walked away from everything she'd been.

"You should tell him he's doing fine," Casper said on the third morning, watching Elara cinch the saddle with hands that moved too fast, too tight. "Turnip. He thinks you're angry at him."

Elara's head came up sharply. "I don't talk to my horse."

"I know. That's rather the point."

Something flickered across the hunt-captain's face — the old reflexive disgust, maybe, or just exhaustion wearing its mask — but she didn't argue. She finished with the girth strap, then, in a movement so small Casper almost missed it, laid one palm flat against Turnip's neck. The gelding's ears swiveled back. His worry didn't vanish, exactly, but it settled, the way a kettle settles when you take it off the flame.

Casper turned away to give them their privacy. Some conversations weren't hers to overhear, even when they were conducted entirely in silence.

Behind her, Sef was trying once more to coax Mirei into eating porridge. The child sat with her knees drawn up, staring at nothing, and let the spoon hover near her mouth without ever quite committing to it. Three days since the storm, three days since Elara had cut her cage open in the lightning-dark, and Mirei still hadn't spoken. Not a word. Not a sound. She communicated in the smallest possible gestures: a shake of the head, a pointing finger, and once — memorably — a flat refusal to walk another step that involved sitting down in the middle of a stream crossing until Sef carried her.

"She ate half," Sef reported, catching up beside Casper as they set off again into the hills. He said it with the tone of a man announcing a military victory. "Half a bowl. We're practically feasting."

"Practically," Casper agreed.

They were climbing into country that grew wilder by the hour — the kind of land the maps called *waste* and the people who lived there called home. Gorse and bracken, grey stone, wind coming off the high moors with the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet. No roads. No farms. No criers proclaiming anything to anyone.

It should have felt like safety. Instead, Casper kept catching herself listening — really listening — to the landscape around them, and what she heard troubled her.

The birds were quiet. Not absent; there were larks overhead, and a kestrel hanging on the wind above the ridge. But quiet in the way a room goes quiet when someone important walks into it. Watchful. As if the whole moor were holding its breath.

"They know we're here," she murmured to Sef.

"Who does?"

"Everyone." She nodded at the heather, at the stones, at the sky. "We've been watched since yesterday morning. Politely. From a distance." She hesitated. "Like something's deciding about us."

Sef glanced back at Elara, then lowered his voice. "Wolves?"

"Wolves would be simpler."

* * *

The wolves came at dusk, and they did not come to attack.

Casper knew it before the others did — felt the change in the land's attention, the way every rabbit and grouse within a mile went suddenly, deliberately still, the way the wind itself seemed to carry a new scent-thread of musk and cold fur. She stopped walking. Turnip stopped with her, blowing softly through his nostrils, and Elara's hand went to her sword on pure reflex.

"Don't," Casper said. "Please. Let me."

She walked forward alone, ten paces ahead of the others, and stood in the last gold light with her empty hands loose at her sides, and waited.

They came out of the rocks the way weather comes — gradually, then all at once. Six of them, lean and grey-brown, moving with the unhurried confidence of creatures who owned every stone they touched. The pack leader was a female, big through the shoulders, one ear tattered from some old fight, and she came right up to Casper and sat down in front of her with the composure of a magistrate.

*Small-voice,* the wolf said. Her mind-voice was cool and dry, like water over slate. *You smell of the valley that forgets.*

"That's me," Casper said aloud, because the others needed to hear something, even if they couldn't hear the rest. Behind her she heard Elara's sharp intake of breath and Sef's low curse, and ignored both. "I'm sorry to cross your country without asking."

*Asking is rare,* the wolf observed. *Manners are rarer.* She tilted her head, studying Casper with amber eyes gone pale with age around the edges. *You run from the two-legs who smell of iron and bells.*

"The Hunt. Yes."

*Mm.* The wolf looked past Casper, at Elara, and her lip lifted just slightly off one long canine. *One of those runs with you. Smells of kennels and bad decisions.*

"That's — actually very accurate," Casper admitted, and heard Sef choke behind her.

The wolf rose, circled Casper once, nose working along her sleeve, her hair, the hem of her coat. Then she sat again, and this time there was something different in her posture. Something heavier.

*You are the one,* she said.

Casper's mouth went dry. "The one what?"

*The one they seek.* The pack leader's ears flattened, not at Casper but at some memory. *The iron-and-bells two-legs came through our country in the green season. Many of them. They did not hunt us — we are beneath their interest, and we made sure they regretted testing that. They asked questions instead. To the ravens. To the foxes. To the old blind mole under the eastern tor, who tells truths for worms.* Her eyes fixed on Casper's. *They asked for a creature whose voice can reach the king. A speaker of speakers. They described a smell. Your smell.*

The wind moved through the gorse. Somewhere behind Casper, Turnip shifted his weight nervously.

"That doesn't make sense," Casper said slowly. "The Hunt takes beast-speakers. That's the whole — they proclaimed it. All gifted to be taken for questioning."

*Yes.* The wolf's tail flicked once, irritation or emphasis. *And do you know why, small-voice? Have you asked?*

"To find the gifted. To—"

*To sift.* The wolf stood, pacing now, restless. *A hundred speakers taken, and none of them the right one. The two-legs in the crown are not gathering your kind out of hatred, though there is hatred enough among them. They are searching. Dragging a net through the world and feeling every fish that touches it, waiting for one shape.* She stopped, and looked back over her shoulder, and her pale-rimmed eyes were almost — almost — pitying. *They took a child from the wagons. Did they ask the child questions? Or did they make the child speak to things, and listen to how the speaking sounded?*

Casper thought of Mirei, small and silent in the wagon, and of the interrogators' tent, and of the instruments she'd tried not to look at. Of how no one had ever explained what the questioning was *for*.

"They're listening for something specific," she whispered.

*They are listening for a voice that can reach a king,* the wolf said simply. *And now I have heard it myself, standing in front of it, and I know they will not stop until they find it. You run well, small-voice. But you cannot outrun being the answer.*

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang of rock, no fire because Elara wouldn't allow one, and Casper told them everything.

She told it plainly, the way Bracken had taught her to tell things — start at the beginning, don't decorate, don't apologize. The wolves. The Hunt passing through in spring. The questions asked of ravens and foxes and one truth-telling mole. The description of a smell. *A voice that can reach the king.*

When she finished, the silence lasted long enough that she could hear Turnip cropping grass at the edge of the hollow, and Mirei breathing in her sleep against Sef's shoulder.

"All right," Elara said at last. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice she used for reports. "Let's assume the wolf is telling the truth."

"Why would she lie?" Casper asked.

"Wolves lie constantly. About territory, about food, about which direction the deer went." Elara held up a hand before Casper could object. "But not about this. This has the shape of truth, which is worse. Because if it's true—" She stopped. Started again. "I've served the Gifted Hunt for six years. I've read the orders. I've executed the orders. And the orders never made sense. Why question the gifted at all? Speakers are useful, yes, but the crown has mages. If the goal were simply to control beast-speech, you'd bind them or kill them, quietly, the way you handle any other dangerous talent. Instead we transport them to the capital. Alive. Every single one. Do you know what happens to them there?"

"No," Casper said. "Do you?"

"No." Elara's jaw tightened. "That's the point. Nobody below a certain rank knows. Prisoners go into the palace and they don't come out. I told myself it was interrogation, imprisonment, whatever ugly thing governments do. But if the wolf is right—" She looked at the dark beyond the overhang, at the invisible moor. "Then I've been a courier. Delivering fish to a net."

"You couldn't have known," Sef said quietly.

"I signed the manifests, Sef. My name is on the papers that moved that child toward whatever waits for her in the palace." Elara's voice cracked, just barely, and she pressed it flat again. "Don't tell me I couldn't have known. Tell me what we do now."

"We run farther," Sef said. "South, maybe. Over the mountains. There are places—"

"And then what?" Elara rounded on him. "They sift every valley, every forest, every farmstead in the kingdom until they find her. You heard the wolf. They have her *smell*. How long do you think a farm girl, a disgraced captain, a runaway mage, and a mute child stay hidden from the full resources of a crown that knows exactly what it's looking for?"

"So we fight," Sef said. "There are four of us and one sword."

"There's also her." Elara nodded at Casper. "If the Hunt wants a voice that can reach the king, then either they want to use that voice, or they're afraid of it. And I've seen enough of the crown's work lately to bet on afraid." She turned to Casper, and in the starlight her face was unreadable, but her voice wasn't. "Why would they be afraid of you? What can you do that frightens a king?"

"I don't know," Casper said. And it was true, and it wasn't the whole truth, and she knew it wasn't, because there was a thing Bracken had said to her once, back in the valley, in one of his long twilight ramblings about old badger memories passed down from grandmother to grandmother: *Beasts remember voices, girl. It's the first thing we learn and the last thing we lose. Your mother's voice, the storm that killed your den, the hand that fed you. We keep them all. Kings are just voices too, to us. Louder than most.*

She hadn't thought about it in years. Now it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"I need to ask the wolves more," she said instead. "The pack leader — she said the Hunt spoke to a mole under the eastern tor. Moles remember everything; it's a failing of theirs. If anyone knows what the Hunt is truly after, it'll be her."

"And if the Hunt comes back through wolf country while you're sitting underground chatting?" Elara asked.

"Then I'll be very polite," Casper said, "and hope the wolves remember I complimented their manners."

Elara stared at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut. "Gods help me. I burned my career to rescue a comedian."

"Somebody had to," Casper said. "You were busy feeling guilty."

* * *

The mole's name was Yarrow, and she received them the following evening in a burrow beneath the eastern tor that smelled of earthworm and patience.

Getting to her required Casper to negotiate with the wolf pack — formally, which involved the pack leader walking a slow circle around all four of them while the younger wolves watched from the rocks, and ended with the pack leader pressing her forehead briefly against Casper's knee. *For manners,* she said. *And because old ones should be visited. It is a debt the young owe.* Then two of the younger wolves escorted them across the moor at a respectful distance, which Elara found deeply unnerving and Turnip found mortifying, being convinced the entire time that he was being herded.

Yarrow herself was nearly blind, nearly deaf by human standards, and ancient beyond any animal Casper had ever spoken with. Her mind-voice came slow and deep, like words rising through soil.

*A visitor,* the mole said, when Casper introduced herself. *From the valley that forgets. And a wolf-escort. And two-legs who smell of iron and ink and fear.* A long pause, filled with the sound of the mole eating something that Casper decided not to inquire about. *Ask, small-voice. Old ones grow tired of guessing games.*

"The Hunt," Casper said. "In the green season. They asked you questions. About a voice that can reach the king."

*They did.* Yarrow's claws rasped in the dark. *Strange two-legs. Polite, in their way. They brought worms — good worms, fat ones — and they sat at my mound and they asked: have you heard a voice that carries to the palace? Have you felt it pass beneath your halls? Voices travel in the ground, you know. Better than air. Air breaks them. Earth keeps them.*

Casper went very still. "Voices travel in the ground."

*Everything does,* Yarrow said, as if explaining rain to a duckling. *Footsteps. Cartwheels. Weeping. The ground remembers what the air forgets. And three winters past, something passed beneath this whole country that made the earth itself flinch. A great voice, buried deep under the capital, screaming through the stone. Not the king's voice. The king's voice was underneath it — small, and tired, and calling a name. Calling and calling. And the loud voice laughed and stepped on it, the way a boot steps on a beetle, and the small voice kept calling anyway.*

"What name?" Casper whispered. "What was the king calling?"

*Names in the ground get worn smooth,* Yarrow said apologetically. *Like pebbles. I could not hold it. But the wolves' grandsire heard it in the open air, before it was buried — a name-song, the kind mothers give children. Half of it, anyway. He sang it to his pack before he died, and they sing it to their pups, and the pups think it is only a song about the moon.* She paused. *You would have to ask the pack leader. She was a pup then. She will say she does not remember. She will be lying, because wolves are sentimental about their grandsires and embarrassed about it.*

Above ground, dusk was falling. Casper climbed out of the burrow with dirt in her hair and her heart hammering against her ribs, and found the pack leader waiting for her on the tor's shoulder, silhouetted against a sky going bruise-purple and gold.

"You knew," Casper said. "About the song."

The wolf's ear twitched. *Grandsire songs are not for strangers.*

"I'm not asking you to give it to me. Not yet. I'm asking you to tell me what it is."

A long silence. Wind moved the heather. Below, Elara and Sef were making camp, and Mirei was showing Sef — with immense solemnity and zero words — how to braid grass, and Turnip was pretending not to watch.

*My grandsire was young when the loud voice came to the capital,* the wolf said at last. *He ran messages for the court stables, before the sickness of crowns. He heard the king laugh, small-voice. Really laugh. And he heard the king sing to his horses in the mornings, badly, in a voice that loved them. And then one winter the singing stopped, and the laughing stopped, and a voice came out of the palace that made the birds fall silent for a day's flight in every direction, and my grandsire carried that silence in his chest until he died.* Her pale eyes found Casper's. *He left us one thing. Half a name-song. He said: keep it until a small-voice comes who asks the right question. I have waited nine years to learn what the right question was.*

Casper's throat was tight. "Was that it? Just now — was that the right question?"

*No,* the wolf said. *The right question is the one you have not asked yet. But you are close. I can smell it on you, the way the Hunt smells you. You are close, and they are close, and now everyone is running toward the same buried thing.* She rose, shook herself, and looked east, toward the distant smudge of the capital on the horizon. *Rest tonight, small-voice. Tomorrow I teach my pups' song to a two-legs, and my grandsire can finally stop waiting. And then you must decide what you are. Because the hunters think you are their quarry.*

Her eyes came back to Casper, and there was nothing soft in them at all.

*But quarry is only prey that hasn't chosen yet.*
