## 10. Sef's Confession

Sef had been a liar for eleven years, and he had gotten very good at it — good enough that the lies had begun to feel like furniture in his mouth, worn smooth where his tongue passed over them daily. *I left court because I quarreled with my master. I am a minor talent, a hedge-mage, nothing worth hunting. I don't know anything about the king.*

He told himself these things while walking beside a wagon full of chained children, and they sat in his chest like stones.

The convoy had stopped at midday in a hollow between hills, the kind of gray green place where rain went to think about falling. Elara Voss had ordered water distributed — an order that had surprised everyone, including, Sef suspected, Elara Voss herself. The interrogators grumbled but complied, because she still outranked them, though Sef had watched that authority eroding all week like a riverbank.

Casper Joubert sat apart from the others, as she always did, her back against a wheel of the supply wagon, watching him.

She had been watching him for two days now. Not with suspicion exactly — with patience, which was worse. She watched him the way she seemed to watch everything: like something that might speak, if you waited long enough and were quiet enough to hear it.

He was beginning to understand why the hounds couldn't smell her. It wasn't only the valley's deadness to magic. It was that she made herself small in a way no spell could teach. A stone among stones. He had spent his whole career learning to read the shape of power in people, and Casper Joubert read like a woman carrying nothing at all, which meant she was carrying everything.

"You keep looking at me," she said, without turning her head. "If you've got something to say, say it. If not, stop staring. It's rude."

"I'm sorry." He crossed the mud and sat down beside her, an arm's length away, close enough to talk low and far enough that no one would mark it as conspiracy. "I'm trying to decide how much of the truth you can survive."

"That's a strange way to start a conversation."

"It's the only honest way I know how."

She turned then and looked at him properly, and up close her eyes were the color of creek water over slate, and there was nothing soft in them at all. "You're the one who whispered to me in the wagon," she said. "*The king hasn't spoken his own words in three years.* You said it like a man confessing. So confess."

Around them the camp murmured — the clink of cups, a child crying quietly two wagons down (Mirei, who hadn't spoken since the interrogation tent, whose silence Sef could not look at directly without feeling something crack), the interrogator Corvane laughing at some joke of his own making. Rain finally committed itself, a fine cold drizzle that beaded on Sef's hood.

"Before I tell you anything," Sef said, "I need you to understand what I was. Not what I am. What I was."

"A mage."

"A court mage. Third circle, which sounds impressive until you learn there are nine circles, and the ninth can pull lightning out of a clear sky. I was third circle, which meant I was clever enough to be useful and not important enough to be dangerous. I served under Magister Ilvane." He said the name carefully, the way you'd set down a blade. "First circle. Keeper of the King's Voice."

"His voice," Casper repeated flatly. "Kings have secretaries for that sort of thing."

"Not that kind of voice." Sef pulled his cloak tighter. The drizzle was turning to real rain, and the camp was contracting under it, soldiers huddling beneath canvas, which suited him fine. Smaller audience. "You know how every living thing has a — call it a song? You'd know better than I do. You hear it plain. We mages hear it muffled, through wool, so to speak. But the king — Aldric, when he was crowned, twenty-two years ago — his song was loud. Loud enough that beasts came out of the forests to stand outside the palace walls during his coronation. Deer in the streets. Wolves sitting in the square like dogs waiting for a scrap. The old chronicles say the horses wept."

Casper had gone very still. "I've heard of that," she said slowly. "Bracken told me a story once. An old story. About a king the animals loved." Her eyes narrowed. "That's not an old story, is it. That's just a story from before."

"From before," Sef agreed. "And here is the part nobody outside the tower knows, and the part I have carried in my gut for three years like swallowed glass." He looked down at his hands. Ink-stained, once. Now cracked and dirty like any fugitive's. "The binding was our work. Mine included. I helped make the knot."

* * *

The rain thickened. Nobody was near enough to hear; Corvane's tent leaked and he was inside it swearing at it, and the sentries had found the lee side of the hill and their own misery. Sef spoke anyway in the low, level voice he'd used for years to deliver unwelcome truths to powerful men — the voice that kept your head attached to your shoulders.

"There was going to be a war," he said. "Not the kind fought with armies. Worse. The king's song was so strong that it was waking things. Old things. Things that sleep under mountains and in deep water and hadn't been sung to since before the kingdom had a name. They were answering him. And not all of them answered kindly. Three villages in the north drowned in a single night when something in the lake decided the king's voice was a summons. The border clans' shamans started hearing their gods argue. And underneath it all—" he hesitated.

"Underneath it all, what?"

"Underneath it all, something had already noticed him. Something that liked the sound." Sef's mouth was dry. "Ilvane called it the Crowned Shadow. We never learned its true name. It wasn't a demon the way the pulpit means it — it was older and more patient than that. It had been wearing kings for centuries, in other lands, in other ages. It finds a voice that carries and it moves in, the way rot moves into a great tree — from the heart outward, so the bark still looks sound for years."

"And the binding?"

"Ilvane designed it. I held the third anchor point, which mostly meant I stood in a freezing circle for six hours holding a silver rod and trying not to vomit." He laughed, briefly, and there was no humor in it at all. "We didn't kill the thing. You can't kill it — Ilvane believed killing it would only free it to find another throat. So we bound it *inside* the king. Caged it behind his own voice. The king keeps his crown and his throne and his mind, but his true voice — the song — is folded away somewhere even he can't reach. And the Crowned Shadow sits in the cage with it, muzzled by the same binding, unable to use the king's mouth for anything but ordinary words."

"So the king has been—"

"Prison and prison-keeper both. Yes. For three years. And it was supposed to be temporary." Sef pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "That's the part I need you to hear. Ilvane swore it was temporary. A season, maybe two, until we found another way — until we taught the king to hold his own song safely, or found some other vessel, or the thing starved. Temporary. I was twenty-six years old and I believed him because believing him was easier than not."

Casper was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the wagon wheel above them. Down the line, Mirei had stopped crying, which somehow felt worse.

"What changed?" Casper asked. "Three years ago. Why bind him then, if it was always meant to be short?"

"Because three years ago the binding almost wasn't possible anymore. The Shadow had grown roots. Another year and we couldn't have caged it at all — we'd have had to kill the king to kill it, and the army wouldn't have stood for regicide, and half the realm would have burned in the succession war after." He dropped his hands. "So we did it fast and ugly and we told ourselves we'd fix it properly later. That's what cowards say, Casper. *Later.* Later is where honor goes to die."

"And now?"

"Now the binding is failing." He said it plainly, because dressing it up would have been a kindness neither of them deserved. "It was built to hold for a year. It's held three. Every full moon, the cage thins. I can feel it from miles away — did you wonder why I flinched at the last moonrise? The Shadow pushes against the binding like a man pushing against a door, and each moon the door gives a little more. Two months ago the king dismissed his entire council in one night and appointed strangers. Last month he had four servants flogged for whispering in a corridor. The court calls it ill temper. It isn't ill temper. It's the cage bending."

Casper had drawn her knees up, arms wrapped around them, making herself small again — but her eyes hadn't gone small at all. "And the Hunt," she said. "The Gifted Hunt. The crier at the mill. All this — dragging beast-speakers out of their homes, chaining children—" her voice caught on the word *children*, and she mastered it with visible effort, "—this is about the binding?"

"The Hunt was real, once," Sef said. "A census, truly. Ilvane wanted to find the gifted because the gifted are the only ones who might hear what's happening to the king. Beasts remember his true voice, Casper. Beasts remember what he sounded like *before*. If anyone in this kingdom can tell whether the man on the throne is still the man, it's someone like you." He shook his head. "But Ilvane died eight months ago. Fell down a staircase in the tower, very conveniently, and the king's new favorites took over the Hunt and bent it into what it is now. Not a search. A harvest. They're not looking for answers anymore."

"Then what are they looking for?"

"They're looking for a voice." Sef met her eyes and hated himself for what he was about to hand her. "The binding can be undone — Ilvane's notes say so, in cipher, and I've spent eight months breaking the cipher. But it can't be undone from outside. It needs a voice the king trusts, a voice that can reach past the Shadow and remind him of his own name. And the new masters of the Hunt aren't searching for the gifted to question them. They're listening to every beast-speaker they catch, one by one, hoping to find the one whose voice fits the lock."

The rain drummed. Somewhere down the column a horse stamped and blew.

Casper stared at him. Then, horribly, she began to laugh — a small, cracked sound with no joy anywhere in it.

"They took Bracken," she said. "They took a lame old badger who cheats at riddles and steals my onions. Because they're fishing. And I was going to trade myself for him like a coin." She pressed her fist against her mouth. "I'm not a coin, Sef. I'm the fish they're trawling for."

"You might be," Sef said gently. "Or you might be nothing special to them at all. There are dozens of gifted in chains already. But yes. If any voice in this realm can reach a king buried alive inside his own crown—" he spread his hands, helpless, "—it would be one the beasts answer. One the valley hid. One that walked into a royal convoy to save a badger."

"Don't," she said sharply. "Don't make it noble. I did it because I love him. That's all. Love, not destiny. If you're about to tell me I'm chosen, I'll walk into the rain and keep walking."

"I'm not going to tell you you're chosen." Sef pulled his hood lower. "Chosen is a word priests and tyrants use to avoid saying *used*. I helped bind a living man because I believed I was serving the greater good, and I will regret it until I die. I won't dress you in the same lie."

For the first time since he'd known her — all five days of knowing her — something in Casper's face eased. Not softened. Eased, like a bow unstrung.

"Good," she said. "Then here's what I want to know, and don't lie to me, because I've spent my whole life around creatures who can smell a lie, and I've gotten good at it too." She leaned closer. "Why are you telling me this *now*? You've ridden with me for days. You whispered one sentence in the wagon and then shut up. What changed?"

Sef looked down the column, at the wagons, at the small shapes huddled under wet canvas. At Mirei, nine years old, wrists scarred where the questioning-cords had bitten, staring at nothing.

"Because yesterday," he said, "Corvane reported to Captain Voss that the prisoners are being taken to the capital not for questioning but for *testing*. There's a device in the palace — Ilvane built its grandfather — that measures how far a voice reaches. And the ones who reach farthest don't get released afterward, Casper. They get kept. Fed to the ritual on the thirteenth moon, when the new masters renew the binding — permanently, this time, with the Shadow fully awake and grateful to its keepers. They're going to spend children like coin to weld a monster onto a throne forever." His voice cracked, and he let it. "I helped build the first version of that cage. I will not help build the second. That's what changed."

Casper was silent. Rain ran off the wagon wheel in a thin bright thread between them, a little curtain of water, and beyond it her face was unreadable and then suddenly, entirely readable.

"You're afraid of me," she said at last. Not accusing. Observing, the way she observed everything.

"Yes."

"Why? I'm chained like everyone else."

"Because you're the most dangerous person in this convoy and you don't know it yet," Sef said. "And because I've just handed you every reason to hate me, and I need you not to. I need you to be angry at the right people. I need—" he stopped, surprised by his own honesty, "—I need you to let me help fix what I broke. That's all. That's the whole confession. I was a coward, and I'm asking for a chance to be something else, and I know exactly what that makes me worth, which is nothing, unless you say otherwise."

Casper looked at him for a long time. Rain dripped. A guard coughed. Somewhere beyond the hills, thunder muttered like an old dog dreaming.

Then she said, "The badger I told you about — Bracken. First time I ever spoke to him, I was seven, and I'd just realized I was a freak who'd burn for it, and I cried, and he said—" her voice wobbled, steadied, "—he said, *the gift isn't the wound, girl. What you do with it decides if it scars.*" She wiped her face with the back of her hand, rough, impatient. "So. You helped chain a king. Fine. Help me unchain him. And if you ever lie to me again—"

"I won't."

"—I'll know. Everyone I've ever listened to says humans are easy. Your hearts are loud."

Despite everything — despite the rain and the chains and the child who wouldn't speak and the moon counting itself down toward the thirteenth — Sef laughed. It startled him, the sound coming out of him like a bird flushed from cover.

"Heaven help me," he said. "You actually believe that."

"I *know* it," said Casper Joubert, and for a moment, just a moment, she smiled, and Sef thought: *there it is. There's the voice that reaches.*

Then the smile died, because down the column, Captain Elara Voss had risen from the interrogators' fire and was looking directly at the two of them — at Sef, at Casper, at the space between where secrets had clearly been traded — and her expression was the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"She knows," Sef breathed.

"Of course she knows," Casper said, standing, shaking rain from her cloak. "She's known for days. The question is what she's going to do about it." She glanced back at him, creek-water eyes gone hard and bright. "And Sef? Whatever you saw in her face just now — pray it's doubt. Because if it isn't, come the next full moon, we're all of us going to be ingredients."

Thunder rolled again, closer now, and the first fat drops of a true storm began to fall.
