## 11. The Thirteenth Moon

Elara Voss had spent eleven years learning to read military orders the way other people read weather: by what they *didn't* say. A requisition for extra rope meant a hanging. A sudden interest in cartographers meant a border war. And a courier who rode through the night without changing horses meant someone very high up was frightened.

So when she found the sealed dispatch tube sewn into the lining of Dain's old saddlebag — the one he'd pressed into her hands at the last crossing, saying only *"You'll want this when you're ready to be angry"* — she did not open it right away. She made tea first. She banked the fire. She waited until Casper and Sef were asleep in the lean-to, and Mirei was curled against the badger-sized hole in her heart that Bracken's absence had left, and then, and only then, did she crack the wax seal with her thumbnail and unroll three pages of cipher.

The firelight moved. The cipher did not.

It was the old Hunt cipher, the one they'd taught her in her first year, before she'd been trusted with anything worth sealing. Simple substitution, keyed to the captain's name — *her* name, once, because Dain had never updated his key after her disgrace. That was either sentiment or sloth, and knowing Dain it was both.

She worked through it with a charred stick and the flat of her knife, scratching letters onto a strip of birchbark, and the words came up out of the nonsense like drowned things rising.

*By order of the Lord Protector, acting under seal of the Crown: all assets of the Gifted Hunt to be concentrated at the capital no later than the twelfth full moon following the Renewal Proclamation. On the thirteenth full moon, the Coronation Renewal will be performed in the Cathedral of the Ascendant Sun. Attendance of the Hunt is mandatory. Attendance of the gifted is mandatory.*

*Note well:* — and here the handwriting shifted, tighter, as if the clerk had been told to write it twice to be sure — *the Renewal is not ceremonial. Failure to deliver sufficient vessels will be treated as treason against the Crown and against the realm's continued prosperity.*

Elara read it four times.

The first time, she read it as a soldier, and her stomach did what soldiers' stomachs do, which was nothing, because she'd trained it not to. The second time, she read it as a woman who had marched chained children through the rain, and her hands began to shake. The third time, she read it as a traitor, which was what she was now, officially and on paper, and she thought: *vessels.* They were calling people *vessels* now. Somewhere between the third page and the second reading, the word had changed from *prisoners* to *vessels*, and nobody in the chancery had thought that was strange.

The fourth time, she read it as a mathematician, because the numbers were wrong.

She counted back. The Renewal Proclamation had gone out — she remembered the day, she'd stood at attention while it was read to her garrison — fourteen full moons ago. Fourteen. But the orders said *no later than the twelfth full moon following*, and the ceremony itself on the thirteenth.

Which meant the orders had been written *before* the proclamation. Which meant the proclamation hadn't announced a policy. It had been a countdown.

"Thirteenth moon," she said aloud, to the fire, because saying things aloud helped her think and there was no one awake to mock her for it. "They've known since before they started."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, an owl asked its one question and got no answer, and Elara felt an absurd, traitorous urge to ask Casper what the owl had actually said.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat by the fire and did what she'd done every night since the storm, which was inventory.

One disgraced hunt-captain, forty-one years old, with a bad knee, a burned career, and a price on her head that had gone up twice since the posters went out. She'd checked. Vanity was a weakness; she'd simply wanted to know if she'd broken the record for fastest fall from favor. She had. Old Commander Brecht still held the record for highest bounty, but he'd murdered a magistrate, so she considered herself ahead on style.

One runaway court mage, currently snoring in a way that suggested he'd been handsome once and had decided to stop trying around the same time he'd stopped being loyal to anything except his own guilt.

One rescued child, mute, who had begun — and this was the thing that undid Elara more than any cipher — leaving little arrangements of stones outside Elara's bedroll in the mornings. Elara didn't know what they meant. She suspected they meant *I see you*, which from Mirei was worth more than a knighthood.

One beast-speaker farm girl who talked to goats and wolves and, apparently, owls, and who had looked at Elara across a campfire two nights ago and said, with the terrible directness of someone who had grown up with no one to lie to, *"You're going to get yourself killed doing something stupid and noble, and I'd like to register my objection in advance."*

And one old badger, caged somewhere in the capital, alive — the raven's memory had shown them that much — kept as bait in a menagerie, aging in a box because he'd thrown himself at armed riders to buy a girl time to run.

Elara fed another stick to the fire and looked at the birchbark strip with its charcoal letters, and let herself do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

Fourteen moons ago, the proclamation. Thirteen moons until permanence. Which meant the clock had started long before any of them knew there was a clock, and it had been running through every mile of the convoy, every night in the wagon, every day of their flight. They weren't racing toward a deadline.

They were already late.

* * *

Casper found her at dawn, which was not surprising, because Casper found everything at dawn. The girl rose with the light like something agricultural.

"You didn't sleep," Casper said. It wasn't a question. She dropped down onto the log beside Elara, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and held out half a loaf of yesterday's bread, buttered, which meant she'd raided Sef's pack, because Sef guarded his butter like a dragon.

"Dain's saddlebag," Elara said, and handed her the birchbark instead of the bread. "Read it."

Casper read. Elara watched her face do the same journey hers had done — confusion, comprehension, horror — compressed into about six seconds, because Casper processed grief faster than anyone Elara had ever met and then got angry about it immediately, which was probably healthier.

"Vessels," Casper said. "They're calling us *vessels*."

"I noticed."

"And the thirteenth moon." Casper turned the bark over, as if the back might contain better news. It contained nothing. Bark was honest that way. "How far off is that?"

Elara had done this sum too, lying awake. "Two moons. Perhaps a little less. The next full is in twenty-six days, and then—" She stopped. Did it again. Hated it again. "Twenty-six days, and then twenty-nine after that. Fifty-five days, give or take, until the thirteenth."

"Fifty-five days," Casper repeated. "To walk to the capital, break into a palace, free a badger, unbind a king, and defeat whatever's wearing him."

"When you list it like that it sounds ambitious."

"It sounds *insane*." But Casper was smiling, a small fierce thing, and Elara had learned by now that the smile meant the fear had finished arriving and been shown the door. "Good. Insane plans are the only kind that work. Sensible plans are the ones the enemy expects."

"That is not wisdom, that is a goat-herder's superstition."

"My goats are extremely wise," Casper said with dignity. "You should hear what they think about you."

"I'd rather not."

"They think you're a large suspicious dog who has decided to guard the flock anyway. It's meant as a compliment."

Elara laughed — actually laughed, short and rusty, the sound surprising them both — and then the morning caught up with the joke and took it away, the way mornings did. She tapped the birchbark. "There's more. Read the part about attendance."

Casper read it again, slower. "'Attendance of the gifted is mandatory.'" Her voice flattened. "They're not hunting us anymore, are they. Not really. They're *collecting*. For the ceremony."

"No. And here's the part that kept me up." Elara pulled the original cipher pages from her coat and laid them out on her knee. "These orders predate the proclamation. Whoever wrote them knew the timeline before the timeline existed. This isn't a king responding to a crisis, Casper. This is a schedule. Something in that palace has been counting down to a specific moon for over a year, and the whole Gifted Hunt — every rider, every hound, every cage we filled — was just procurement."

The silence stretched. Down the slope, Sef emerged from the lean-to, saw the two of them huddled over papers, and came over with the wariness of a man who had learned that whenever women conferred quietly, his life got more complicated.

"What's that?" he said.

"The end of the world, apparently," said Casper, "on a schedule."

Sef took the birchbark. He read it once, and Elara watched something worse than horror cross his face — recognition. "This is the Renewal," he said quietly. "I've heard the term. My master used it. I thought it was liturgy. Coronation anniversaries, blessing the crown, that sort of theater." His thumb moved along the line about vessels. "It's not theater."

"You knew the phrase and didn't mention it?" Elara kept her voice level. Mostly.

"I knew a *word*." Sef's jaw tightened. "Elara, the tower library has forty thousand documents. I have read perhaps a tenth of them, and most of those were inventories of candles. If I'd known—"

"You'd have done what? Warned us sooner?" Casper said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. Then she sighed and bumped her shoulder against his arm. "I know. There's always another document you haven't read. That's how they win — not with armies, with paperwork."

"Paperwork and bells," Elara murmured, thinking of the bell that screamed, thinking of Dain somewhere behind them with it, and pushed the thought away before it could settle in and pay rent.

"So," Sef said, sitting down heavily on the log, making it creak. "Fifty-five days. What do we actually know?"

Elara laid it out the way she'd lay out a battle map, because that was the only language she had left. "We know the king hasn't spoken his own words in three years — Sef's information. We know the binding is failing and something inside him grows stronger each moon — also Sef, and corroborated by every animal within fifty miles of the capital road, according to our resident translator. We know the Hunt is collecting the gifted for a ceremony on the thirteenth moon that will make the possession permanent. And we know—" she hesitated, then said it, because they deserved the whole ugly shape of it, "we know they're searching for someone whose voice can reach the king. Someone specific. Which means whatever happens at that ceremony, our girl here is either the guest of honor or the sacrifice, and I've seen enough crown functions to know the difference is mostly seating."

"Gee," said Casper. "Thanks."

"Don't thank me yet. Here's the worst part." Elara picked up the cipher pages and folded them away. "If they want her specifically, then every step we take toward the capital makes their job easier. And every step away from it leaves that thing on the throne forever, with a kingdom of collected vessels and fifty-five days of runway. There is no good option. There is only the option where we choose what we lose."

Casper was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, she was looking east, toward the capital none of them could see, in the direction where a badger sat in a cage.

"Then we go," she said. "Before the thirteenth moon. We go *early*."

* * *

They broke camp by midmorning, and Elara spent the walk doing the thing she hated most, which was planning honestly.

Because here was the truth she hadn't said aloud: she had decoded those orders in an hour. An hour, with a charred stick and a knife, using a cipher she'd learned as a recruit. Which meant the Hunt's security was a wet paper wall, which meant the court mages — real ones, competent ones, the ones Sef had fled rather than serve — already knew everything in those pages and had signed off on it anyway. This wasn't a conspiracy she could expose. Exposure required someone left to be scandalized.

No. What it required was different. It required getting to the king himself, past the guards, past the court, past the thing wearing him — and doing it before the thirteenth moon rang out over the cathedral bells.

Fifty-five days. She'd planned sieges with less time and worse odds, but never one where the fortress was also the prisoner, and the prisoner was also the objective, and the key was a girl who talked to animals walking into the loudest, most watched city in the realm.

At noon they stopped at a stream, and Casper crouched at the bank talking quietly to a heron, and came back frowning.

"What?" Elara asked.

"The heron says the fish have been moving downstream for weeks. All of them. Same direction." Casper looked east. "Away from the capital."

Elara felt the cold arrive, patient and certain, like winter. Even the fish knew. The whole living world was reading the same countdown she'd scratched onto birchbark, and it was voting with its feet.

"Fifty-five days," she said.

"Fifty-three," said Casper. "We lost two arguing."

And they walked on, three fugitives and a sleeping child carried in a sling on Sef's back, toward a city of bells that was counting down to something, with a strip of birchbark in Elara's coat that weighed, she was quite sure, more than her sword.
