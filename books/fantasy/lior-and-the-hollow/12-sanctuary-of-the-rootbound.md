## 12. Sanctuary of the Rootbound

The forest found them before they found it.

Casper had been walking for three days on feet that felt like someone else's swollen, blistered property, following a fox that Sef swore was leading them somewhere, when the trees simply changed. One moment they were in ordinary woodland — birch and alder, thin and nervous — and the next the trunks went vast and dark around them, oaks so old their bark had folded into faces, or what Casper's tired mind insisted were faces. The air changed too. It grew thick, sweet, almost drinkable, like the air inside a bakery or a church.

"Stop," Elara said quietly.

She had her sword half-drawn. Casper noticed she'd been keeping it half-drawn for three days now, ever since the wolves told her about the hunters searching for *her*, specifically, by name and gift. Half-drawn seemed to be where a hunt-captain lived these days.

"They're not going to hurt us," Casper said. "The fox says—"

"The fox," Elara said, "is a fox."

"She's very insistent."

Sef, limping along behind with Mirei's small hand wrapped in his, made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been rationed out of him years ago. "Listen to her, Captain. She speaks fluent fox. That's more than the entire court mage corps managed in two hundred years."

"I'm not a captain anymore."

"You'll always be a captain. It's baked into you, like stubbornness into a mule." Sef squinted up at the great trees. "Though I'll admit this is strange. This forest shouldn't be here. I've studied the maps of this whole region, and there's nothing but logged wasteland marked from here to the river."

"Maybe your maps are wrong."

"My maps are never wrong. Which means the forest is hiding." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. "Which means something in it doesn't want to be found. Which means, historically speaking, we're either about to be saved or eaten."

Mirei tugged his sleeve. She hadn't spoken since the wagon — not a word, not a sound, except once in her sleep when she cried out for someone named Nana — but she had developed an elaborate language of tugs. This tug meant *I'm scared*.

Casper crouched down to her level. "It's all right," she said. "Whatever lives here, the animals aren't afraid of it. And animals are better judges of character than people any day. A badger taught me that."

Mirei looked at her with those enormous dark eyes, then did a new gesture — one Casper hadn't seen before. She pointed at Casper, then pressed her own small fist against her chest.

*I trust you.*

Casper's throat did something complicated. She stood up quickly before it could show on her face.

"All right," she said to the forest at large. "We're coming in. Please don't eat us. Especially not Sef. He's mostly gristle."

The fox trotted ahead, tail high, entirely unbothered by being slandered.

They walked under the great oaks for what felt like an hour, though Casper couldn't have sworn to the direction afterward. The light came down green and honeyed. Somewhere overhead a wood pigeon was calling, and Casper caught snatches of its conversation — *safe, safe, come home, safe* — which was odd, because wood pigeons usually only talked about food and other pigeons' business.

Then the path opened onto a clearing, and Casper stopped so suddenly that Elara walked into her back.

There were houses in the trees.

Not treehouses, exactly — not platforms nailed on after the fact — but homes that had been *grown*, it seemed, branches woven and grafted into walls and stairways and round windows hung with drying herbs. Lanterns glowed warm in the late afternoon. There was a garden below, riotous and untidy, where a woman with a hoe paused mid-swing to stare at them. A child on a rope swing froze mid-arc. Somewhere a kettle was whistling, and the whistle cut off abruptly as if someone had clapped a hand over it.

And the animals. Casper had never seen anything like it. Deer grazed between the garden rows like livestock who'd simply decided farming was easier than foraging. A badger — her heart lurched stupidly at the sight, even knowing it wasn't Bracken, even knowing no badger would ever be Bracken again — waddled across the path with a rabbit, both of them moving with the easy familiarity of old neighbors. On a fence rail sat a crow, a magpie, and a jay, lined up like judges at a fair.

Every one of them turned to look at her.

Not at the group. At *her*.

"Oh," Casper said faintly. "Oh, they know."

"They know what?" Elara's hand was fully on her sword hilt now.

"What I am." Casper swallowed. "Elara, they all know what I am."

A door opened in the largest of the grown houses, and a woman came out.

She was old — older than Bracken had been, in human terms, though it was hard to say how Casper knew that; something in the way she moved, unhurried, like weather. Her eyes were white as milk, blind, and yet she walked down the garden path without a stick or a hand on the wall, straight as an arrow, and stopped three paces from Casper.

"You're loud," the woman said.

Casper blinked. "I— sorry?"

"Not your mouth. Your *gift*. It's been ringing through the roots for three days like a dinner bell. Every root-bound thing from here to the mountains has been asking me who's coming." The blind woman tilted her head, and her face softened into something between amusement and wonder. "A speaker. A true open speaker. I thought you were all dead or burned."

"We're not looking for trouble," Elara said, stepping forward, every inch the soldier despite the rags and the mud. "We'll pass through. We need water and a night's shelter, nothing more."

The woman ignored her completely, which Casper suspected took considerable practice and nerve. She reached out and took Casper's chin in a hand rough as tree bark, turning her face left, then right, as though reading it.

"Mm. Valley-raised. Hiding long. Lonely." She clicked her tongue. "And carrying grief like a stone in the belly. Whose?"

Casper's eyes stung. "His name was Bracken. A badger. They took him."

Something moved across the old woman's face — not pity, exactly. Recognition. "The menagerie," she said. "Yes. The trees heard about that too. Cages in the capital, and a king who walks among them at night talking to things that cannot answer." She let go of Casper's chin. "I am Wren. I keep this place. You will stay. Not because you asked. Because you're asked-for."

"Asked-for by whom?" Elara demanded.

Wren smiled, and it was a smile full of secrets and gentle mischief. "By everyone," she said, and gestured at the clearing — the deer, the crows, the children peering from doorways, the woman with the hoe. "Ask them yourself, speaker. You're the only one here who can."

* * *

They stayed one night. Then another. Then Casper lost count entirely, which frightened her a little, and comforted her more.

The community called itself the Rootbound, and it was unlike anything Casper had imagined existed outside of stories. There were perhaps forty people, and nearly every one of them carried some gift the world outside would burn them for. There was a man whose touch coaxed seeds to sprout weeks early. Twin sisters who could call birds down out of the sky. A boy of maybe ten who could hold his breath underwater for a quarter hour and claimed, with great seriousness, that the fish told him gossip. An old fellow who could find water with a forked stick and had strong opinions about where the well should have been dug twenty years ago.

Nobody hid here. That was the thing Casper kept stumbling over, again and again, like a root in a path. Nobody flinched. Nobody lowered their voice. The twins called hawks in broad daylight and nobody checked the treeline first. The seed-man worked his garden singing to the beans, out loud, and the worst anyone said was that his singing was terrible.

"It's genuinely awful," the fish-boy confided to Casper on her second morning, as they shelled peas together on a doorstep. "Old Tam sings to his beans every day. The worms have complained. Through me. Officially."

"The worms filed a complaint."

"A formal one. They want him to stop or they're relocating to the north bed." The boy shrugged with the gravity of a magistrate. "I told them Tam's hearing isn't what it was and he gets lonely. They agreed to give him until harvest."

Casper laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the wagons, since the chains, since Bracken's paws disappearing into a cage — and the laugh startled her so much she dropped a pea pod.

That evening, Wren found her sitting alone on a root-bench at the clearing's edge, watching the lanterns come alive one by one in the trees.

"You keep sitting apart," Wren said, settling beside her with a groan of old joints. "At supper. At the fire. Like a cat that's decided the house isn't real yet."

"I keep waiting for it to be taken away."

"Ah." Wren nodded slowly. "Valley girl. Yes. You learned early that good things get confiscated." She was quiet a moment. In the branches above, the wood pigeon murmured its endless *safe, safe, come home*. "Do you know how I keep this place? Everyone asks. They think it's some great working of magic, wards and veils."

"Isn't it?"

"The trees hide us, yes, but the trees would hide anyone who asked kindly. No. What keeps the Rootbound is smaller than magic." Wren tapped her own chest. "We remember each other. Every person here, every animal, every bird — we know each other's names and troubles and favorite foods. When the jay doesn't show at morning feed, someone goes looking. When the fish-boy goes quiet, his sister notices. That's all sanctuary is, girl. Attention. Kept up daily, like a fire."

Casper looked down at her hands. "I had that," she said. "One badger. One lame old badger on a hillside, and I thought I was rich."

"And now you've got forty souls and a forest, and you don't know what to do with it."

"How do you—"

"You sit apart," Wren said simply. "You listen to everything and belong to nothing. I know the posture. I wore it for thirty years before I founded this place." She rose, joints crackling like green firewood. "Supper's at sundown. Sit in the middle tonight. The stew won't kill you. Probably."

* * *

She sat in the middle.

It was harder than she expected. Supper at the Rootbound happened at three long tables under the biggest oak, and there was no order to it — soldiers of fortune and grandmothers, deer browsing the scraps, the crow-magpie-jay tribunal holding court on a beam overhead and demanding tribute. Casper ended up wedged between the fish-boy (introducing himself finally as Perch, "because it's funny, my sister doesn't think so") and one of the hawk-twins, who spent the entire meal narrating, sotto voce, the romantic entanglements of the local owl population.

"—and the barn owl, right, he's been courting this tawny for a month, bringing her mice, very traditional, and last night my sister sees him leaving the *saw-whet's* hollow at dawn—"

"That's scandalous."

"It's the talk of the canopy." The twin leaned in. "Anyway. Wren says you can hear everything. Is it loud? All of us together? I've always wondered what it's like for you, being able to hear everyone. For us it's just birds."

Casper considered the question honestly, looking around the table — Perch stealing bread off her plate with the shamelessness of a seagull, Elara at the far end actually smiling at something Sef said, Mirei sitting close against Casper's side, allowed, wanting to be close.

"It's like this," Casper said. "Like this table. Everything talking at once and most of it nonsense and all of it worth hearing."

The twin beamed and stole her bread too.

Afterward, Mirei wouldn't go to the pallet assigned to her. She stood gripping Casper's sleeve with that iron grip of hers, and when Casper knelt and asked — in words, in gestures, in the small sign-language they'd been building between them, brick by brick — the girl pointed at Casper, then at the ground beside her pallet, then lay down on the floor next to it, arms crossed, immovable as a badger.

So Casper slept with a child curled against her back like a hot coal, and lay awake listening to the forest breathe, and thought: *this is what it would have been like. If I'd been born here. If the valley hadn't needed me silent. If I'd had forty voices instead of one lame old badger and a hillside.*

The thought should have been sweet. Mostly it ached, like pressing on a bruise to check it still hurts.

* * *

On the fourth morning, Elara found her at the training yard — a flat stretch of moss where the Rootbound's few fighters drilled with wooden staves, badly, cheerfully. Elara had been drawn into teaching within a day, because Elara could no more watch bad swordwork than Casper could hear a distressed animal and walk past.

"You're leaving," Elara said. Not a question.

Casper kept her eyes on the drills. "Sef's been studying Wren's maps. There's a route south, along the river, deep country. The Hunt won't think to search it for months."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Casper admitted. "It isn't."

Elara leaned on the fence beside her. Up close, Casper could see the new lines the last weeks had carved into her face, the gray coming in early at her temples. "I've watched you for four days," Elara said. "You laugh here. Did you know that? You laugh, and then you catch yourself, like you owe someone an apology for it." She was quiet a moment. "You're thinking about staying."

"Would that be so wrong?" It came out sharper than Casper intended. She gestured at the clearing — the tables, the gardens, the deer, the children. "Look at it, Elara. They're *safe*. Safe and hidden and nobody burning anybody. I could have Mirei grow up here. I could—" Her voice caught. "Bracken used to tell me I belonged everywhere and nowhere. Maybe this is the everywhere part. Maybe I finally found it and I'd be a fool to walk back out."

Elara didn't argue. That was the worst part. She just looked out at the clearing with her soldier's eyes, taking its measure.

"There's a king in the capital," she said at last, "with something ancient wearing him like a coat. There's a Hunt with my name still on its rolls, burning children in wagons. There's a thirteenth moon coming, and a binding failing, and a voice — yours — that might be the only thing in the world that can reach him." She turned to Casper. "And there's a badger in a cage who talks too much, who attacked armed riders to buy you three minutes of running. Does any of that stay true if you stay here?"

Casper's jaw tightened. "You sound like him."

"Good. Someone has to."

"I hate that you're right."

"You'll live." But Elara's voice gentled, and she touched Casper's shoulder — briefly, awkwardly, the way she did everything tender, as if tenderness were a weapon she hadn't been issued training for. "For what it's worth — I understand the temptation. I spent twenty years believing duty was a cage. Now I believe it's a door. Same bars. Depends which side you're standing on."

* * *

Wren knew, of course. Wren always knew. Casper found her that evening in the heart-grove, a ring of the oldest oaks where the blind woman went to listen, and sat down beside her without being asked.

"The trees say you're leaving," Wren said.

"The trees talk too much."

"They get it from me." Wren's white eyes turned toward her, seeing everything anyway. "Stay, if you want. I mean that. There's room. There's work. Mirei would grow up whole. You'd grow old here, and it would be a good old age, and no one would ever make you afraid again." She paused. "But you'd hear it. Every night, through the roots. The cages in the capital. The king walking the menagerie, whispering. The beasts crying out for a voice that never comes. You'd hear it, and you'd lie awake, and one morning you'd pack a bag. Better to do it now, while your legs are young and your friends are willing."

Casper's throat was tight. "How do you bear it? Hearing everything?"

"Badly, some nights." Wren smiled. "But here's the secret, girl. Sanctuary isn't a place you hide in. It's a place you fill back up from. You go out, you spend yourself, you come back empty — and the ones who love you pour you full again. That's the whole trick. That's why we remember each other." She reached over and took Casper's hand, bark-rough and warm. "Go save your badger. Come back after. This forest isn't going anywhere. Neither are we."

Above them, the great oak stirred without wind, and the wood pigeon called down its one word, over and over, patient as rain:

*Safe. Safe. Come home.*

* * *

They left at dawn, four days after arriving — Casper, Elara, Sef, and Mirei riding double on two borrowed ponies who volunteered with suspicious enthusiasm and kept making jokes about it that only Casper could hear.

The Rootbound turned out to line the path. The hawk-twins brought them hardtack and, in the case of one twin, a lengthy and tearful farewell to the owls. Perch gave Mirei a river-smoothed stone ("it's a listening stone, the fish blessed it, probably") and made her promise to come back. Old Tam pressed a sack of beans on Sef and sang a farewell to them that was, objectively, an atrocity; the departing worms sent their regards.

Wren stood at the treeline, blind eyes toward the sun.

"Speaker," she called, as Casper passed. "When you stand before the king — and you will — remember what keeps this place. Not wards. Not veils." She tapped her chest again. "Attention. Remembered daily. Give him that, and even a crown of shadows will have nowhere to grip."

Cisper promised she would. Then the forest closed behind them like a held breath released, and the ordinary grey world took them back — the thin woods, the muddy road, the far-off smoke of a kingdom that didn't know yet what was coming for it.

Mirei rode pressed against Casper's back, and halfway through the morning, without warning, the girl began to hum. Softly. Tunelessly. Some half-remembered lullaby, offered to the road like a flower set on a grave.

Casper didn't turn around. She didn't dare. She just reached back and squeezed the small knee against her hip, and listened, and let the sound carry them south — toward the river, toward the capital, toward a cage with a badger in it and a moon that was growing fuller every night.

Behind them, far behind, the forest kept its vigil, and the trees remembered them daily, as promised.
