## 13. The Child Who Wouldn't Speak

The girl had not made a sound in nine days.

Casper knew this the way she knew most things now — by counting. Nine days since Elara had carried Mirei out of the prison wagon and into the rain, nine days since the child had watched the interrogator's tent burn with eyes that did not blink, nine days of silence so complete it had weight, like a stone the whole sanctuary carried in its pocket.

The Rootbound didn't mind silence. That was one of the reasons Casper loved them, though she'd have bitten her own tongue before saying so aloud. The hidden folk of the deep forest spoke when they had something worth saying and let the trees do the rest of the talking. Old Wren, their blind matriarch, could sit for an entire afternoon listening to a birch grow, and come away from it looking better informed than most priests.

But a silent child was different from a quiet forest. A quiet forest was at peace. A silent child was holding something down, and Casper knew exactly how heavy that something got, because she had spent twenty-two years holding her own.

"She eats," Sef observed, watching Mirei from across the long table in the root-hall. He said it the way he said everything, as if filing it in some vast internal ledger. "She sleeps. She follows Elara around like a duckling with a grievance. But she hasn't spoken."

"Maybe she has nothing to say to you," Casper said.

"Everyone has something to say to me. I'm fascinating." Sef turned a page in his notes without looking up. "It's you she watches."

That was true, and Casper wished it weren't. Wherever Mirei went in the sanctuary — and she went everywhere, small and quick-footed as a wren herself, always at the edge of things — her eyes found Casper eventually. Not with hope. Casper knew hope when she saw it; she'd seen it in Bracken's face a hundred times, that terrible patient optimism of old animals. No, what Mirei looked at Casper with was closer to *recognition*. The flat, assessing stare of one creature checking another for wounds.

*You're broken too,* those eyes said. *I can tell.*

"I'm not good with children," Casper told the fire that evening, when Elara sat down beside her with two bowls of barley stew and the particular expression of someone about to hand you a problem.

"You're good with everything that talks," Elara said. "You just don't like admitting it counts."

"That's different. Goats have simple needs. Grass, gossip, an occasional scratch behind the ears. Children are—" She gestured vaguely at the whole concept. "Complicated. They want things you can't give them. Like their mothers back. Like the bad men unmade."

Elara set the second bowl down in front of her anyway, which was its own kind of argument. "I carried her out of that wagon," she said quietly. "I've been carrying her ever since, in every way I know how. But I'm the one who led the Hunt that took her, Casper. Whatever face I make at her, she remembers my hands were on the cage door." She paused. "Yours were never on anything."

"My hands were on a lot of cage doors metaphorically."

"Casper."

"I'm eating my stew," Casper said, with dignity. "This is important work."

But later, when the root-hall had gone dark and the only sounds were the forest breathing and Sef's quill scratching somewhere above, Casper climbed to the loft where the children slept and found Mirei awake. Of course she was awake. Children who had heard the things Mirei had heard did not sleep easy, no matter how soft the moss.

The girl was sitting up, knees drawn to her chest, staring at the round window where moonlight came through. When Casper's head appeared over the edge of the loft ladder, Mirei didn't startle. She just looked, with that same flat accounting stare, and waited.

Casper considered her options. She could go back down. She could pretend she'd come up for a forgotten sock. She could attempt conversation, which seemed cruel — talking *at* a child who couldn't talk back felt like showing off a limb in front of someone who'd lost theirs.

So instead, she did the thing her body had been doing her whole life without asking permission.

She raised one hand, slow and deliberate, and made the sign.

Every valley child learned the beast-signs young, or the clever ones did — the ones who spent too much time alone with the flocks and needed someone to talk to. It wasn't magic. That was the joke of it, the beautiful stupid joke: the signs were just gestures, plain human gestures, the ones shepherds and drovers had used for centuries because shouting spooked the flocks. A flat hand bobbing meant *fox*. Two fingers walking meant *weasel*. A fist pressed to the chest meant *afraid*, borrowed from how a bird went still before it flushed.

Mirei's breath caught. Casper heard it — the first sound the girl had made in nine days, small as a mouse's heartbeat.

Casper kept going, keeping her face carefully bored, as if this were the most ordinary thing in the world. Fist to chest: *afraid*. Then both hands opening slowly, palms up, like something unfolding: the sign she'd invented herself, years ago, for the way a frightened ewe finally lay down in the straw. There was no official word for it. In her private vocabulary it meant *safe enough to rest*.

Mirei stared at her hands for a long time.

Then, slowly, stiffly, like someone remembering a language from a dream, the girl lifted her own small fist and pressed it to her chest. *Afraid.* And then — her hands shaking — she opened them, palms up, unfolding.

*Safe enough to rest.*

Neither of them slept much after that, but neither of them needed to explain why.

* * *

Word traveled through the sanctuary the way word traveled everywhere — badly, and with embellishments. By morning, half the Rootbound believed Casper had healed the child through the grace of the old forest, and Wren had to be talked out of holding some kind of ceremony.

"There will be no ceremony," Casper said, cornering the blind woman by the herb-drying racks. "There was no healing. I made hand shapes at her. Goats get the same treatment."

"Mm," said Wren, who had the unnerving habit of looking directly at you despite having no eyes to look with, and who heard lies the way other people heard bells. "And how do the goats respond?"

"With contempt, mostly."

"And the child?"

Casper opened her mouth. Closed it. Because the truth was that Mirei had responded like a person dying of thirst responds to water — greedily, desperately, with both hands. All day the girl had trailed Casper at a careful distance, and whenever Casper glanced back, there were the small hands already moving: *bird? tree? hungry? afraid?* A floodgate of questions held back for nine days, all of them pouring out in silence, and Casper found herself answering in kind while her own hands grew tired in a way they never did from work.

"It's not healing," Casper muttered. "It's just... talking. The way I've always talked. With the volume turned off."

Wren smiled, which rearranged her whole weathered face into something like sun through leaves. "In my experience," she said, "that is what healing looks like. Loudly, most people expect trumpets. It almost never comes to trumpets." She patted Casper's arm with uncanny accuracy. "Go on. Your student is waiting by the goat pen, pretending she isn't."

"I don't have students."

"You have a student."

The sanctuary kept three goats, which struck Casper as a scandalously small number for a community this size, but the Rootbound explained that the forest provided and goats were mostly for company and cheese. The goats themselves — a sour elderly doe named Turnip and two yearlings — had taken one sniff of Casper on arrival and declared her family, which was flattering and also meant she could never sneak anywhere again.

Mirei was indeed by the pen, sitting very straight on a stump, hands folded, wearing the expression of a scholar awaiting a lecture. When Casper approached, the girl signed one word:

*More.*

Casper sighed and sat down on the grass, because apparently she did have a student, and the student had already learned the most powerful word in any language.

"All right," she said aloud, because Mirei could hear even if she wouldn't answer, and hearing was half of everything. "Lesson one. Watch Turnip. Everything you need to know about honest living is in that goat."

Turnip, sensing herself discussed, turned her ancient yellow eyes upon them both with an expression of profound grievance.

Casper signed: *goat angry.*

Mirei giggled.

It was a tiny sound, rusty, half-swallowed — but it was a sound, and it hit Casper somewhere under the ribs with the force of a falling branch. She kept her face steady through sheer professional discipline, the same discipline that let her chat with a suspicious ewe without letting the ewe know she understood every bleat.

*Yes,* she signed back, solemn. *Goat very angry. Goat always angry. This is wisdom: some creatures are angry the way rivers are wet. You don't fix it. You bring grass.*

Mirei's hands moved hesitantly, shaping the question: *you talk to animals?*

And there it was. The question Casper had spent her whole life making sure nobody asked, arriving now from a seven-year-old in silence, and finding her undefended.

She thought about lying. It was reflex, older than thought — the valley had trained the lie into her bones until it sat there like marrow. *Magic cannot follow into the Hollow*, the elders said, and so nothing in Casper had ever been allowed to simply *be*. Her gift was a secret, then a shame, then a death sentence, and now here was this small broken mirror of herself asking the one question that mattered, and every instinct screamed at her to deflect, diminish, deny.

Instead she looked at Mirei — at the faded bruise-yellow still ghosting the girl's wrists where the bindings had been, at the way she sat ready to flinch — and Casper understood, with the clarity that sometimes comes in quiet moments, that lying to this child would be like kicking a bird with a broken wing.

*Yes,* she signed. *I talk to animals. It's called being gifted. It's why the bad men hunt us. It's why we're here.*

Mirei's hands trembled. Then, slowly: *me too?*

Casper's chest hurt. "Not yet," she said gently, aloud. "Your gift is asleep. Sometimes it sleeps when terrible things happen. It's protecting you." She signed it as she spoke, so the words would land twice. *Asleep. Safe. Waiting.*

Something in the girl's shoulders loosened — a degree, maybe two, but Casper had spent a lifetime reading bodies smaller than words, and she knew a fortress lowering one drawbridge chain when she saw it.

Mirei looked at the goats for a long moment. Then she slid off the stump, approached Turnip with the exaggerated care of someone approaching a sleeping bear, and held out a handful of grass.

Turnip, who had terrorized three generations of Rootbound children, regarded the offering. Regarded the child. And then, in an act of betrayal so complete it would have gotten a lesser goat exiled, lowered her great gray head and ate daintily from Mirei's palm.

*She likes me,* Mirei signed, and her whole face was lit up like the first morning after a long winter.

*Don't let it go to your head,* Casper signed. *She likes anyone holding food. It's not character, it's policy.*

But she was smiling. She could feel it happening and couldn't stop it, and for the first time in weeks — maybe for the first time since the crier had come to the mill square and read the king's proclamation in his bright carrying voice — the smile didn't feel like a betrayal of anything.

* * *

The days took on a shape after that, and Casper, who had spent her life resisting shape, resisted this one too, and lost, and mostly didn't mind.

Mornings were lessons, though Casper refused to call them lessons. They were, officially, "goat-related activities." Mirei learned the full shepherd's vocabulary of signs — *wolf, storm, run, hide, lamb, lame, good dog, bad dog, come, stay* — and invented new ones when the old ones failed, which was how Casper ended up with a gesture meaning *Sef is being annoying* (two fingers flicked dismissively off the nose) entering permanent circulation in the sanctuary. Sef pretended not to know about it. Sef was a terrible pretender.

Afternoons, Mirei followed Casper on her rounds, and Casper narrated the world to her the way she'd once narrated it to Bracken — which plants sulked, which birds gossiped, which of the Rootbound elders secretly fed bacon to the sanctuary's pig despite the pig being theoretically sacred. Mirei absorbed it all with the ferocious appetite of a child rebuilding a world from rubble, and every evening her hands moved a little faster, her signs a little surer, her silences a little less like walls and a little more like rooms she simply preferred.

On the sixth day, she spoke her first word aloud. It was, inevitably, "goat."

On the eighth day, she laughed — really laughed, doubled over, when one of the yearlings ate Sef's notes off the table and Sef chased it around the hall with what little dignity remained to him.

And on the ninth day, she asked the question Casper had been dreading.

They were sitting on the hillside above the sanctuary, sharing a stolen apple, watching the light go gold through the trees. Mirei had grown chattier by the day, her voice returning in fragments like ice breaking up on a spring river — single words, then phrases, then, suddenly, sentences, as if the dam hadn't broken so much as dissolved. Now she chewed her apple share and studied Casper with those grave dark eyes, and signed and said at once, the way she did when something mattered:

*"The badger. Your badger. Will we get him back?"*

Casper's hands went still.

She had known the question was coming. Bracken was woven through every story she'd told, every anecdote — *my friend Bracken says, old Bracken once told me* — until the child must have built him whole in her imagination: a wise gray uncle, waiting somewhere. She deserved the truth. Children always deserved the truth; adults just found it inconvenient.

"He was taken to the capital," Casper said. "To the royal menagerie. Caged." Her throat worked. "He attacked the hunters so I could run. He traded himself for me, and I've been—"

*Running,* Mirei signed.

"Yes."

*From him?*

"No!" The word came out sharper than intended, and Mirei flinched, and Casper hated herself for it. She gentled. "No, sweetheart. From the men who took him. Running to keep everyone safe. That's what I told myself." She looked out at the forest, at the deep green dark between the trees where the Rootbound lived safe and hidden and *small*, and heard her own words come back to her in Bracken's gravel voice: *A burrow's just a grave you decorate, girl.*

"But running isn't the same as saving," she said slowly. "And he's getting older. And the king's men—" She stopped, because the next part was the part she hadn't let herself think in full sentences: the thirteenth moon, Sef's failing binding, the clock ticking under everything like a beetle in a wall. If the thing wearing the crown finished whatever it was doing, no rescue would ever come for anyone, badger or child or kingdom.

Mirei tugged her sleeve. When Casper looked down, the girl's hands were moving, careful and clear:

*We go get him. After. When you're strong.*

"We?"

*You, me, Elara, Sef.* A pause, and then, with the devastating simplicity of childhood: *Family goes together.*

Casper stared at her. Something cracked open in her chest — not painfully, or not only painfully. More like ice going out of a river, like ground thawing, like all the frozen things shifting at once and finding they could move.

She had spent her whole life believing she belonged nowhere, that her gift made her a stranger in her own valley, that love was a debt she paid in secrecy. And now a mute child was sitting beside her on a hillside casually redefining the word *family* to include a disgraced hunt-captain, a runaway mage, a dead man's best friend, and herself, and the terrifying thing — the thing that made Casper's eyes sting in the golden light — was that it fit. It fit like a key.

"When I'm strong," Casper agreed, and signed it too, so it would be a promise in both languages.

Mirei nodded, satisfied, and leaned against her side, small and warm and trusting, and Casper put her arm around her and watched the sun go down and understood that she had just stopped running. Not toward the capital yet — the body would take a few more days to agree — but in every way that counted, the running was over. You couldn't run from a promise you'd made in two languages to a child who'd learned to speak again just to hold you to it.

Below them, the sanctuary lamps were coming alight one by one, and Elara stood at the edge of the clearing talking with Wren, and Sef was arguing with a goat over the remains of his notes, and it was, absurdly, almost home.

Then Casper's smile faded.

Because down in the valley below the ridge — far off, at the treeline where the sanctuary's safety ended — the birds had gone quiet. All of them, all at once, the way they did when something large moved through the forest that wasn't a deer.

Mirei felt her tense and looked up, questioning.

Casper didn't answer. She was watching the treeline, counting heartbeats, listening with every sense she owned — and beneath the hush of the birds, faint but unmistakable, came a sound she knew from nightmares.

A bell. Far away, thin as a wire, ringing a note that no human ear should find wrong — but every beast in the forest had gone still, and somewhere in Casper's chest, the place where her gift lived curled tight and hid.

Someone had brought the Hunt to the edge of the sanctuary.

And they were ringing the bell.
