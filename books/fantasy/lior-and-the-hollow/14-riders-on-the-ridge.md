## 14. Riders on the Ridge

Elara Voss had spent eleven years learning to read a battlefield, and in all that time she had never once been wrong about the sound of hoofbeats.

She was wrong now.

It wasn't the hoofbeats themselves — those were unmistakable, a low drumroll rolling down from the northern ridge like thunder deciding whether to commit. It was what they meant. For three days she had let herself believe the Sanctuary of the Rootbound was safe. Three days of Mirei chasing fireflies with the rootbound children, of Casper laughing — actually laughing, an unguarded sound that did something inconvenient to Elara's chest — of Sef complaining about the tea as though complaint were a form of prayer.

Three days of borrowed peace. And now the bill had come due, riding down the ridge at a canter.

"Elara." Wren, the blind woman who led the sanctuary, stood at the edge of the clearing with her face tilted toward the north, her hands loose at her sides. She hadn't needed to be told. The trees had told her; they always told her first. "How many?"

Elara counted by ear, an old habit. "Twenty riders. Maybe twenty-five. Light armor. They're not hurrying."

"They don't need to hurry," Wren said softly.

No. They didn't. Because Dain — her Dain, the boy she'd trained from a raw recruit into the finest tracker the Hunt had ever produced — wasn't tracking them anymore. He didn't have to track anything. He'd brought the bell.

* * *

She'd heard about the bells only in fragments, rumors passed between disgraced officers and sympathetic quartermasters: the crown's artificers, desperate after months of failed hunts, had built something new. A bell cast in layered bronze and something worse, something mined from the deep places where magic pooled like groundwater. When it rang, it didn't make sound so much as it made *wrongness* — a scream pitched to the frequency of the gift itself. Anyone carrying magic within half a mile heard it inside their own bones. It didn't hurt, exactly. The reports said it was worse than hurting. It said *I know where you are* in a language no one could pretend not to understand.

Sef had gone gray when she'd described it to him two nights ago. "That's not artifice," he'd said. "That's theft. They're stealing the voice of magic itself and using it against the people who speak it." Then he'd drunk his terrible tea and stared into the fire for a long time, and Elara had understood that somewhere in the mage tower, someone she used to respect had helped build it.

Now the bell was coming up the valley road, and Elara had perhaps a quarter hour before it rang.

She moved through the sanctuary the way she'd once moved through camps — quick, quiet, certain — except that this time every person she touched on the shoulder was someone she'd failed first. The rootbound scattered with the ease of long practice; they had hidden from the world for generations, and hiding was a language they spoke fluently. Mothers gathered children. Old men pulled supply caches from hollow trees. A boy of maybe ten paused in his running to grab a terrified hen under one arm, which struck Elara as either very practical or very foolish, and she decided she liked him.

Casper found her at the center of it all, Mirei's small hand locked in hers.

"How many?" Casper asked. No panic in her voice. That was the thing about Casper Joubert that kept catching Elara off guard: dread rolled off her in waves you could almost see, and yet when the moment came she went still as deep water.

"Twenty-five. Quarter hour, less if they spur."

"And the bell?"

"On the ridge. I can hear the mule carrying it."

"You can hear a *mule* over twenty-five horses?"

"I can hear that mule," Elara said grimly. "It walks like it hates its job."

Something flickered across Casper's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the reflex of a woman who coped with terror by finding the absurdity in it. "Then we run."

"No," Wren said.

They both turned. The blind woman stood with her head cocked, listening to something in the leaves above them. "You run," she said. "The sanctuary scatters. That is how we have always survived — we are not a place, we are a people. But you three cannot run east along the river. They will expect the river."

"The mountains, then," Elara said.

"The mountains," Wren agreed. "And you must go *now*, because when the bell rings, my people cannot help you. Do you understand? When it rings, every gift in this forest will be screaming, and we will be deaf and blind and stumbling while soldiers walk through us. The bell does not find you, Captain. It makes sure no one can hide you."

Elara felt the truth of it settle cold in her stomach. Safety was always borrowed. She'd known that since the day she'd burned her own career in a storm to free three prisoners, and she'd known it again every morning since, waking in the sanctuary with birdsong instead of bugles. She had simply let herself forget, because forgetting was warm and remembering was not.

"Casper," she said. "Take Mirei. North slope, the goat track, then west into the high passes. Don't wait for us."

"We're not splitting up," Casper said flatly.

"Sef and I will draw them—"

"*We're not splitting up.*" The stillness in her cracked, just slightly, and underneath it Elara saw the girl from the Hollow, the one who had refused to leave a lame old badger even when staying meant burning. "I've lost one family to these people already. I'm not doing arithmetic with this one. We go together or not at all."

From anyone else it would have been insubordination. From Casper it was simply a fact, stated the way one states that water is wet. Elara opened her mouth to argue — it was her duty to argue, she was still, in whatever remained of her soul, a soldier — and then closed it again.

"Together," she said. "West pass. Move."

* * *

They were halfway up the goat track when the bell rang.

Elara had braced for it. She had told herself it would be like artillery, like the war-drums of the border campaigns, like any loud terrible thing she'd survived before. It was none of those things.

It was a sound that happened *inside* her. Not in her ears — behind them, beneath them, in the marrow. A single brazen note that climbed and climbed without ever getting louder, a note shaped exactly like the feeling of being *seen*. Beside her, Sef dropped to one knee with a strangled cry, hands clamped over his ears though there was nothing to block out. Mirei folded up like a paper flower, and Casper caught her before she hit the stones, wrapping the child against her chest, murmuring into her hair words Elara couldn't hear over the ringing.

And below them, the forest screamed back.

Not the people — the *forest*. Every gift in the sanctuary flared at once, involuntary, dragged into the open by the bell's cruel frequency. Elara felt it through her boots: the ground-hearers crying out, the tree-speakers losing their thread, the whole hidden web of the Rootbound lit up like a signal fire. Wren had been right. They were blind now. All of them.

"There!" A voice from the ridge, young and hard and horribly familiar. "Movement on the goat track! Three adults, a child — *that's them*, that's Voss!"

Dain.

Elara turned, despite everything, despite the screaming in her skull, and looked back down the slope. Twenty-five riders were spilling off the ridge into the trees, and at their head rode a man she had last seen across a tribunal table, saluting her with eyes full of hurt and worship in equal measure. Lieutenant Dain Ferro. Her protégé. The best of them, she'd always said. The best of them, following the worst orders, carrying a weapon that made children collapse, and calling it duty.

He saw her looking. Even at four hundred yards, she saw him see her — saw the half-second hitch in his rein hand, the way his chin lifted as if he'd been struck. Good, some bitter part of her thought. *Feel it.*

"Go," she said hoarsely. "GO."

They ran.

* * *

The high passes were cruel country — scree and switchback and wind that came off the snowfields with knives in it. They climbed until the bell's note faded to a whisper and then, mercifully, to nothing, though Elara noticed Sef kept rubbing his jaw long after, as if the sound had left a residue, like smoke in cloth.

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a boulder the size of a chapel, and Mirei slept the instant she stopped moving, curled against Casper's side with the boneless trust of the truly exhausted. Casper didn't sleep. She sat stroking the girl's hair and staring west, and Elara sat down beside her because sitting beside her had apparently become a thing Elara's body did without consulting her.

"That bell," Casper said quietly. "It found us in a valley full of gifted people who've hidden for centuries. Hidden from kings. Hidden from everything."

"It didn't find us," Elara said. "It flushed us. There's a difference. Finding takes patience. Flushing just needs volume." She flexed her sword-hand, working feeling back into fingers that still remembered the wrongness. "Dain won't lose the trail now. He doesn't need hounds anymore. He'll follow the passes methodically, sweep every shelter, post watchers at every spring. He's thorough. I taught him to be thorough."

"You talk about him like you're proud and grieving at the same time."

"I am proud and grieving at the same time. Most feelings about people you love come in pairs if you carry them long enough."

Casper glanced at her then, and in the dying light her expression did something complicated. "Is that so, Captain."

"It is so," Elara said, with great dignity, and looked firmly at the fire.

Sef saved them both from whatever that was becoming by arriving with the evening's rations — hard bread, dried apples, and a face like a man delivering a eulogy for the apples. He lowered himself onto a stone with a groan that suggested the stone had personally wronged him.

"I've been thinking about the bell," he announced.

"You've been thinking about the bell since it rang," Elara said. "You've done nothing *but* think about the bell."

"Yes, well, some of us process trauma intellectually." He bit his bread with unnecessary violence. "Here's the thing. That frequency — the one it screams at? It isn't arbitrary. You can't just *make* a sound that resonates with the gift. You need a source. A living sample. Something with magic in it, close enough to study, for months." He paused, letting it land. "The artificers didn't invent that note, Elara. They *copied* it. Off someone."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, a night bird called once and fell silent, as if it too were listening.

"A prisoner," Casper said. Her voice had gone very quiet, and her hand had stilled on Mirei's hair.

"A prisoner," Sef agreed. "Or several. Someone the Hunt has kept alive specifically because their gift was useful. Which means—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the stars, at the whole rotten architecture of the kingdom — "somewhere in the capital right now, there's a person whose magic has been wrung out note by note to build that thing. And knowing the crown's accounting habits, they're keeping the source alive. Sources are expensive."

Casper was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was steady in the way Elara had learned to distrust — steady the way ice over a river is steady.

"Bracken," she said. "The menagerie. They took him to the royal menagerie. If any beast in this kingdom has been studied note by note—" She stopped. Swallowed. "A badger's speech is old magic. Older than most. If they wanted a frequency to ring against *every* gift..."

"Don't," Elara said gently. "Don't build the gallows before the trial."

"He's an old badger with a bad leg, Elara. He tells terrible jokes and steals my gloves. If they're using him as a tuning fork—" Her breath came out ragged. Mirei stirred against her, and Casper mastered herself with visible effort, smoothing the girl's blanket, tucking it with exaggerated care. "If they're using him, then every day we run is a day they ring him."

Elara reached over and put her hand over Casper's fist where it gripped the blanket. She didn't say anything clever. There wasn't anything clever to say. After a moment Casper's fist unclenched, just slightly, under hers.

"We'll get him back," Elara said. "After the thirteenth moon. One debt at a time."

"And until then?"

"Until then, we stay ahead of Dain Ferro." She looked west, where the passes descended into dark country she knew only by reputation — the Shrike Moors, the drowned forests, the long lawless stretch where the king's writ frayed into rumor. "Which means tomorrow we stop climbing and start descending, and we do it fast, and we do it quiet."

Sef raised his hand like a schoolboy. "Question. Does 'quiet' include the part where our resident beast-speaker negotiates passage with every crow between here and the moors? Because I love her dearly, but she has the subtlety of a festival."

"I heard that," Casper said.

"You were meant to."

Despite everything — the bell still echoing faintly in her teeth, Dain's face burned into her memory, the sanctuary scattered and deaf behind them — Elara felt her mouth twitch. This was the other thing she'd learned since burning her life down: fear traveled lighter when you carried it in company.

She took first watch. The others slept. And near midnight, as she sat sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening, she heard it — far off, down in the black folds of the valley, thin as a memory.

The bell. Ringing again. Not searching.

*Celebrating.* They'd found the empty sanctuary, and Dain was ringing it anyway, ringing it over and over into the deserted trees, and Elara understood with cold clarity that he wasn't trying to flush anyone out.

He was telling her he could keep this up forever.

She set down the knife, woke the others, and by moonrise they were moving west, four shadows and a sleeping child, into the last wild country between them and the sea of the king's patience.
