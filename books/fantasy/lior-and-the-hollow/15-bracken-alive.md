## 15. Bracken, Alive

The raven came at dusk, dropping out of a sky the color of old pewter like a piece of that sky had decided to fall.

Casper was mending a fence post with more stubbornness than skill when it landed on the gate beside her and said, in the flat, gravelly way ravens have of saying anything: *Black coat. Black coat. Shiny thing. Dead man's field.*

She'd been talking to birds since she could talk at all, so she knew most of what they said was nonsense strung together — weather reports, complaints about hawks, grievances against crows, who deserved every one of them. But this raven wasn't chattering. It was looking at her. Ravens did look at you when they had something to say worth hearing, which was more than she could say for most people she'd met lately.

"Shiny thing," she repeated. "What kind of shiny thing?"

*Shiny thing,* the raven agreed, pleased to be understood. Then it hopped closer along the gate rail, head cocking, one eye — black as a well — fixed on her with an attention that made the hair on her arms lift.

Because here was the thing about ravens, and about her gift, and about the particular trouble of being Casper Joubert: beasts remembered. Not the way people remembered, in words and stories, but the way water remembers the shape of the stone it ran over. A raven carried its memories like a crop of stones in its gizzard, and if you asked kindly — or if the raven decided, for reasons known only to itself and possibly the dead, to offer — you could feel those stones turn over.

This raven had come from the east. From the city.

Casper set down her mallet very slowly.

"You've been somewhere," she said softly. "Somewhere with a dead man's field and a shiny thing."

*Dead men,* the raven said, with relish. *Many. Fat worms.*

"That's grim."

*Worms are not grim. Worms are breakfast.*

Fair enough. She crouched down, bringing herself level with the bird, and did the thing she'd done ten thousand times in the Hollow and never once outside it: she opened the quiet door inside herself and let the animal's memory lean against hers.

It was like falling into cold water. Raven-memory didn't come with explanations. It came as images, sharp-edged and sideways, all beak-angle and wingbeat:

— A city of gray stone and ringing bells, bells that hurt, bells that screamed in a register she felt in her teeth even secondhand —

— A tower, and on the tower a cage, and in the cage —

Casper's breath went out of her.

— In the cage, a badger. Old. Gray-muzzled. One forepaw held wrong, stiff, the way Bracken's had gone lame three winters ago when he'd argued with a cart wheel and lost. He was pressed into the far corner of a cage too small for him, and there were iron bands on it, and his eyes — his eyes were open, and they were *looking*, the way Bracken always looked, like the whole world was a slightly disappointing relative who might still surprise him —

— And below the cage, men in royal livery. And one of them holding up something small and glinting, letting the light catch it, turning it so the whole courtyard could see. A badger's tooth. Old, yellowed, cracked down the middle.

Bracken's tooth. The one he'd lost to the same cart wheel, the one he'd given Casper as a joke years ago — *"Keep it, girl. Proof I once bit something harder than myself"* — except he hadn't. She had found it by his sett after the Hunt took him, and she'd carried it ever since, wrapped in cloth in her pocket, and it had never once occurred to her until this exact moment that a tooth tells stories too. That a tooth could be *read*. That the crown's mages might know how to read them.

The memory shuddered and ended. Casper came back to herself kneeling in the mud of her own half-mended fence, hands shaking, the raven watching her with what she would have sworn, in any other mood, was sympathy.

"They're using him," she said. Her voice came out strange and flat. "They're keeping him alive and putting him where I can see."

*Shiny thing,* the raven confirmed, and then, because ravens are ravens: *Bread?*

---

She didn't tell anyone for six hours, which she was not proud of.

She told herself it was because she needed to be sure. She told herself it was because Sef would only ask questions, and Elara would only do arithmetic — *a cage in a courtyard is a trap, traps need two things, bait and a hunter, guess which one you are* — and Mirei would only look at her with those enormous dark eyes that made Casper want to promise things no sensible person should promise.

All true. But underneath the truth was a smaller, meaner truth: some part of her wanted to keep Bracken alive a little longer in the only place he was safe, which was inside her own chest, unspoken, where no one could use him.

That lasted until supper.

They were camped in the shell of a shepherd's cot in the hills south of the river — Elara had insisted on high ground and two exits, because Elara insisted on those things the way other people insisted on breathing. Sef had conjured, from a pouch of dried beans and sheer professional shame, something almost resembling stew. Mirei sat close to the fire with a stick, drawing shapes in the ash. Lately she'd been drawing animals. Tonight it was a badger, round-bodied and fierce-eyed, and Casper looked at it and felt the ground tilt under her.

"I have to tell you something," she said.

Elara's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. Of course it did. The woman had instincts like a sprung trap.

Casper told them. All of it — the raven, the cage, the tooth, the courtyard full of livery arranged just so, the whole stage-set of it. She heard her own voice go steady in the telling, which surprised her; grief usually made her wobble, but this wasn't quite grief. This was rage wearing grief's coat.

When she finished, nobody spoke. The fire popped. Mirei had stopped drawing.

"So," Sef said at last, carefully, the way he handled everything lately, as though the world were made of eggshell. "They know what you are. They've known since the convoy, probably since Voss's report, and now they're — what, advertising?"

"They're fishing," Elara said. She hadn't touched her stew since Casper started. "You don't keep a prisoner alive and display him unless you want someone to come. They read the tooth. They know it matters to her." Her jaw worked. "It's a good trap. I'd have designed it the same way, back when I was someone who designed traps."

"Charming," said Sef.

"It's honest work," said Elara, in a voice that made it clear she knew exactly how that sounded.

Mirei tugged Casper's sleeve. When Casper turned, the girl held up the stick and pointed at the ash-badger, then at Casper, then made a shape with both hands — the sign they'd built between them over the long weeks, the one that meant *yours*, the one that meant *family*. Then she pointed east, toward the city, and her chin trembled but her eyes didn't.

"No," Casper said immediately. "No, absolutely not, nobody is saying what you're all clearly saying—"

"Casper." Sef set down his bowl. "I'm going to say the sensible thing, because apparently that's my role in this company, God help me. It's a trap. We know it's a trap. They know we know it's a trap, which means it's a trap they've built twice — once for your body and once for your judgment. Walking into the capital before the thirteenth moon, with the bell-weapons loose and Dain hunting us, to rescue one badger—"

"He's not *one badger*."

"—to rescue one badger," Sef continued, relentless, gentle, infuriating, "who, I will remind you, ordered you to run and got himself taken covering your escape precisely so that you would *not* do this. If Bracken could hear you planning this rescue, what do you think he'd say?"

Casper thought about it. She thought about it honestly, which was the worst part.

Bracken would call her a fool. He'd do it in that dry rumble of his, the voice like a landslide considering its options, and he'd list her failings in order of severity, and then he'd say something like, *Well. Since you're going to be a fool either way, girl, at least be a fool with a plan.* Because that was Bracken. He'd spent three years teaching her that love wasn't staying safe in the valley; love was knowing exactly how much trouble someone was worth and paying it without flinching.

He was worth a kingdom's worth of trouble. He'd have said she was worth the same.

"He'd say I'm an idiot," Casper said. "And then he'd tell me to bring rope."

Sef closed his eyes. "I knew it. I knew when I met you that you were going to be like this."

"Like what?"

"Like *her*." He gestured at Elara with his spoon. "You've caught honor off her like it's fleas."

Elara, to her credit, had the grace to look mildly ashamed. Mildly. It was about as much humility as her face could physically produce.

"The trap's real," she said. "But here's what they haven't accounted for. Every trap they build assumes you'll come alone, or nearly alone, and desperate. Desperate people take the front door." She leaned forward, firelight catching the scar along her jaw — the one she'd never explained and Casper had never asked about. "So we don't take the front door. And we don't go alone. And we don't go yet."

"Yet," Casper said.

"Yet." Elara held her gaze. "There are four weeks until the thirteenth moon. Four weeks to learn that courtyard, learn the guard rotations, learn which of my old colleagues still has a conscience and which ones sold it for a pension. You want him back? Then we do this the way that gets him *back*, not the way that gets you both in cages." A pause, and her voice dropped into something quieter, rougher. "I left him behind once already. On the road, in the storm, because the convoy schedule gave me no choice. I have carried that for two months. I will not carry it twice. So believe me when I tell you: we get him. But we do it right."

Casper looked at her for a long moment. Somewhere in the last few months, without her noticing the exact day it happened, Elara Voss had stopped being the woman who put chains on her and started being the woman who planned around her — and Casper still didn't entirely trust it, but she trusted it more than she trusted her own judgment right now, which was saying something, because her own judgment currently consisted of *run east and bite people*.

"Right," Casper said. "We do it right." She looked down at Mirei and the ash-badger. "But Mirei stays out of it. Whatever 'right' looks like, that part doesn't move."

Mirei scowled at her with the full force of a nine-year-old denied a war, which was considerable.

"And you," Casper added, turning to the fence line, where the raven still sat hunched against the dark like a small disapproving elder. "You're going to earn every scrap of bread you've ever stolen. I need eyes in that city. Can you find the cage again?"

The raven shuffled its feathers. *Shiny thing,* it said. *Bread first.*

"Bread first," Casper agreed. "Bread forever. You drive a hard bargain, and I respect it."

---

That night she couldn't sleep, so she climbed the hill behind the cot and sat with her knees drawn up under the thirteenth-moon-to-be — a fat silver sliver short of full, counting down like a debt collector.

Below her, the fire had burned low. Elara sat awake beside it, sharpening a knife that was already sharp, which was how Elara prayed. Sef slept in the manner of a man who had made peace with his nightmares by inviting them in for tea. Mirei was a small lump of blankets, and curled against the lump, having defied every standing order about wild animals near the child, was a fox kit Mirei had befriended in defiance of nature, sense, and Casper specifically.

Casper dug the wrapped tooth out of her pocket and turned it over in her palm.

*Proof I once bit something harder than myself.*

"Well," she murmured to it. "Looks like it's my turn."

The wind moved through the grass, and far off — so far it might have been imagination, but she didn't think it was — she felt the faint, vast stirring of the land's animals turning their heads east. Wolves pausing mid-hunt. Horses lifting their heads in stable stalls. Ten thousand small attentions swinging toward the capital like compass needles, waiting for a word from her.

Not yet, she told them. *Soon. And when I call, I'm going to need every one of you.*

Something answered — not in words, never in words, but in a pressure, a warmth, a tide leaning in. *Yes. Yes. Yes.*

Casper closed her hand around the tooth and stood up.

Four weeks. A trap built for her, in a city full of bells that screamed. An old badger in a cage, kept alive as bait by people who thought love was a weakness you could fish with.

They were about to learn what the Hollow had taught her all her life: that silence isn't emptiness. It's everything, holding its breath.

"We're coming, old man," she said to the east. "Bring rope."

Behind her, unnoticed, the raven lifted off from the fence post and flew into the dark — east, toward the city, carrying one message in its beak-shaped memory that Casper had never asked it to carry, and would have given anything to know:

that the men in the courtyard had not only read the tooth.

They had read the *raven*, too.
