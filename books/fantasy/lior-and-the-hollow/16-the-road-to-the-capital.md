## 16. The Road to the Capital

The safehouse was a shepherd's hut with pretensions of grandeur — one room, a hearth that smoked when the wind came from the west, and a roof that leaked directly over Sef's bedroll, which everyone had agreed was fair, since Sef complained the most.

Casper sat cross-legged on the floor with Mirei asleep against her shoulder and watched the fire argue with itself. Outside, rain came down in long grey sheets, and somewhere on the ridge above them, she could feel the sheep huddled together, grumbling about it in the wordless way sheep had. Even without speaking to them properly — her gift was a thin thread these days, stretched by fear and distance — she could feel them. Like hearing a conversation through a wall.

Elara crouched by the window, watching the dark. She'd been watching the dark for three days. Casper suspected if you cut her open you'd find watchfulness where other people kept blood.

"We can't stay," Elara said, for the fourth time.

"We know," said Casper, for the fourth time.

"I'm saying it again because nobody's decided anything."

"Because there's nothing to decide," Sef said from his damp corner. "We run. South, past the Marrowfen, into the free cantons. The Hunt's writ ends at the border. I've told you this. I've drawn maps." He held up a map, which was soggy at one corner. "This is a map."

"It's a napkin with lines on it."

"It's a *map*, Captain Voss, and it took me two hours—"

"You drew it during dinner."

"Multitasking is the soul of scholarship."

Mirei stirred against Casper's shoulder and went still again. Casper lowered her voice without deciding to; it happened around the girl now, the way quiet happened around sleeping animals. "The thirteenth moon is forty-one days away," she said. "If we run south, we're alive. And the king stays whatever he is forever. And every gifted person in this kingdom spends the rest of their life running from Dain and his bell." She paused. "And Bracken stays in a cage until he dies in it."

Nobody said anything to that. The fire popped. The roof dripped once, precisely onto Sef's bedroll, as though the house itself had opinions.

* * *

Elara couldn't sleep, so she did what she always did instead: she counted things.

Twelve days of hard riding to the capital, if they pushed and weren't caught. Forty-one minus twelve left twenty-nine days to do something no one had ever done, inside a city whose bells screamed magic into silence, guarded by an army she used to command. Against that: the road south, which was eleven days of riding to safety, and then nothing. Safety, forever, like a warm room with no doors.

She had spent thirty years choosing the warm room. It had gotten a child tortured in front of her.

She got up quietly, took her cloak, and stepped out into the rain, because some thoughts needed weather.

Casper was already outside, sitting under the eaves with her knees pulled up, watching the storm walk across the valley below. Of course she was. The girl treated bad weather like company.

"You'll catch your death," Elara said.

"I've been informed," Casper said. "By three people and a goat."

"The goat's right."

"The goat's usually right. Goats have excellent judgment. It's why they're so unpleasant — being right all the time curdles you." She scooted sideways, making room on the dry strip of stone. Elara sat. Rain came off the eaves in a beaded curtain between them and the world.

"You're not going south," Elara said.

"No."

"Sef will call it suicide."

"Sef calls everything suicide. He called my cooking suicide, and he ate three helpings."

"That's because he's a coward with a good appetite." Elara turned her face up. Rain found the creases around her eyes. Thirty years of soldiering lived in those creases, and lately something else was moving in behind them, something she hadn't invited and couldn't evict. Hope, possibly. She'd forgotten its face. "I've been counting," she said. "Twelve days' ride. Twenty-nine to work with. It's impossible."

"Mm."

"But it's *countable*. That's more than most impossible things get." She looked at Casper. In the dark, with the rain coming down, the girl looked exactly like what she was: a goatherd from a valley that forgot magic, sitting on a wet rock, carrying the weight of a kingdom because she was too stubborn to put it down. "You understand what you're asking us to walk into."

"I understand what I'm asking *myself* to walk into. You're all welcome to go south. I mean it. I won't think less—" 

"Don't do that," Elara said, sharper than she meant. "Don't hand out permission like we need it. We're past permission. We're a family, and families don't vote on whether they love each other, they just show up and complain about the food."

Casper blinked at her. Then she laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, the kind that made Mirei stir inside the hut. "Did you just say we're a family?"

"I said we're a tactical unit with emotional complications."

"You said family."

"I will deny it before a tribunal."

* * *

Sef woke to find the fire rebuilt and both women gone, which meant either disaster or a decision, and in his experience the two were indistinguishable. He padded to the doorway, blanket around his shoulders like a disapproving bat, and found them sitting side by side in the rain-shadow of the eaves, close enough that their shoulders touched, pretending they didn't notice.

"Oh no," he said. "You've reached an understanding. Those are never binding until witnessed. I refuse to witness."

"Sit down, Sef," Casper said.

"I'm standing. Standing is how I maintain boundaries."

"Your bedroll is leaking."

Sef looked back into the hut, where water was dripping with metronomic patience onto his belongings. He sighed the sigh of a man who had once commanded wards that could hold back armies and now lost arguments with roofing, and sat down between them.

"Here's where we are," he said, before either of them could arrange him. "I've served the court since I was nineteen. I helped bind the king's voice — I handed my master the chalk, I held the circle steady, I *swept up after*. Everything I've done since has been trying to outrun that. And now you two want to march into the one city where I am known by face, name, and outstanding warrant, to attempt a counter-ritual I only half remember, against an entity that eats kings, in less than six weeks." He held up a finger. "It is, categorically, the worst plan I have ever heard, and I once watched a man try to cure his own lycanthropy with soup."

"Then come south with us," Elara said evenly. "No one's chaining you to this."

"That's the problem!" Sef threw up his hands, and the blanket slid off, and he was too committed to the gesture to retrieve it. "Don't you see? If you leave me a way out, I'll take it. I always take it. I took it three years ago, and a child got interrogated, and an old badger is sitting in a menagerie, and a king is screaming silently inside his own skull. There is no version of me that runs south and lives with it. So stop offering me exits. It's cruel." He snatched the blanket back up. "Build me a cage instead. Give me a job so specific I can't slither out of it."

Casper was quiet a moment. Then, gently: "Unravel the binding. You're the only one who can."

"Yes. Thank you. See? A cage." He exhaled. "Also I want it noted that the counter-ritual requires the mare's name-song delivered through a voice the king trusts, sustained across a full moonrise, and that the last mage who channeled anything at that scale bled from the ears for a week and retired to grow turnips. Possibly related events. So when we do this, someone should stand near Casper with a cloth."

"I'll stand near Casper with a cloth," Elara said.

"This is the worst family," Sef said. "I want it minuted."

* * *

They woke Mirei for the decision, because it was hers too.

That had been Casper's insistence, and Elara had agreed before Casper finished saying it, which surprised them both. The girl sat wrapped in a quilt by the fire, small and solemn, her eyes going from face to face as Casper laid it out plainly — the road south, the road to the capital, the moon, the choice. No dressing it up. Casper had learned that much from animals: you didn't lie to the young ones. They smelled it.

"So," Casper finished. "Safe and sorry, or dangerous and maybe. Nobody here gets to decide alone. Not even you, sprout. Especially not you."

Mirei considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she raised one hand and made the sign — the one Casper had taught her, the flat palm circling, wing-shape. *Raven.*

"Raven?" Sef said. "What about ravens?"

Mirei pointed at Casper. Made the raven sign again. Then pointed east, toward the capital, and pressed her fist to her chest.

Casper felt it land in her ribs. *Bracken.* The raven's memory, weeks old now, but carried like a coal: Bracken alive, Bracken caged, Bracken bait. The girl remembered everything. Trauma had taken her words but kept her ledger.

"She's saying we can't leave him," Casper translated quietly. "She's right."

"She's eight," Sef protested weakly.

"She's eight and she's correct. It happens. Ask any goat."

Elara knelt so she was level with Mirei. "If we go," she said, "it will be frightening. Loud. Soldiers. Bells that hurt. And I will keep you beside me every single minute, and if it goes wrong, I will carry you out myself, and I have carried heavier things than you out of worse places. Do you believe me?"

Mirei studied her the way she studied everything — completely, without blinking, the way a wren decides whether your garden is safe. Then she nodded once, and leaned over, and put her small hand flat on Elara's breastplate, right over the badge-shaped scar where her insignia used to sit.

Elara went very still.

"Well," Sef said into the silence, his voice suspiciously thick. "That's settled, then. Outvoted by a mute child and a goat-adjacent woman. My master would weep."

"Your master bound a king," Casper said. "Let him weep."

* * *

They left two hours before dawn, in that blue-grey hour when even the rain takes a breath.

Elara rode point with Mirei bundled against her chest in a sling she'd improvised and pretended was standard military equipment. Sef brought up the rear on a borrowed mule he'd named Chancellor, "so that something in this operation answers to authority." Casper ranged alongside, and everywhere she passed, the waking country noticed her — hedge sparrows lifting to follow, a fox trotting the fence line parallel, an old horse at a crossing gate stretching its neck after her and blowing a soft, wondering breath.

They were gathering. Slowly, in ones and twos, without knowing why, the way birds know a storm before the sky does. She could feel it like pressure behind her teeth.

At the top of the first ridge, Elara reined in and looked back once at the safehouse, the smoking chimney, the whole shape of the life they might have run to. Then she faced east, toward the capital, where forty-one days from now the moon would rise full and thirteenth, and something ancient waited under a crown to be answered.

"Hold on back there, Chancellor," she said. "We're going to make history or die of it."

From the rear, Sef's voice floated forward: "Statistically, Captain, it's usually both."

Casper laughed, and the fox on the fence line laughed too, in its own fashion, and the road unrolled ahead of them toward the city of bells — where, unknown to any of them, a lieutenant named Dain was already reading reports of four travelers, and reaching for a bell that screamed.
