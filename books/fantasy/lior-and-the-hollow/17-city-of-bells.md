## 17. City of Bells

The capital announced itself long before we saw it.

We had been riding for two days through country that grew steadily more tamed — hedgerows instead of wild thickets, milestones instead of mossy cairns, and always, always the roads full of carts and soldiers and people who did not look at each other. Sef rode in the wagon bed with Mirei tucked under an oilcloth among the turnip sacks, because a runaway court mage with a face known to half the palace guard does not sit up front where eyes might find him. Elara drove, wearing her old captain's coat with its buttons polished to a shine that hurt my teeth, and I sat beside her in a collar.

Not a real one. A clever fake, leather-padded, that Sef had stitched with a hidden clasp so it would come off me faster than a lie leaves a courtier's mouth. But it *looked* real, and it sat against my throat like a hand, and every time the cart jolted I felt it remind me what I was pretending to be: a captured beast-speaker, hauled to the capital as Elara Voss's great prize, the fugitive who had slipped through three provinces until her former captain ran her down at last.

"You're scowling," Elara said quietly, eyes on the road.

"I'm practicing," I said. "Captured people scowl."

"Captured people keep their heads down. Scowling is for people with opinions, and nobody wants your opinions, Casper. That's rather the point of the collar."

"That's the most depressing thing anyone's ever said to me, and I once spent a winter listening to Bracken complain about his hip."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth — the ghost of the smile she kept locked away on this road like contraband. "Save the humor for when we're inside. Inside you'll need it worse."

"Why's that?"

She didn't answer right away. The road crested a hill, and there it was.

I had imagined the capital as a big version of a market town — more houses, more mud, a bigger wall. What rose out of the morning haze was nothing like that. It was a mountain that someone had decided should be a city, all grey stone and slate roofs stacked upon each other like scales, and rising out of the middle of it, towers — dozens of them — and from every tower hung bells. Great green-black bells, some of them taller than our barn back home, their mouths open toward the sky as if they were mid-shout and had been for a hundred years.

"City of Bells," Elara said. "They ring at every hour. They say it's to honor the Founding. They actually—" she paused, choosing the words carefully, "—they dampen things. Magic doesn't like the ringing. It makes the gifted dull-headed, slow. The court calls it a blessing. Order out of chaos."

"And everyone just lives under that?" I asked. "All that noise?"

"You get used to it." She said it flatly, the way people say things they've had to make themselves believe.

But here is what I noticed, sitting on that cart bench with a false collar at my throat: the bells weren't the only thing humming. As we rolled down toward the gates, I began to feel something underneath the noise of the wheels and the wind and Elara's steady breathing. A hum. Faint at first, like the far-off drone of bees in a summer field, except it wasn't bees, and it wasn't a field.

It was animals. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

* * *

I had grown up in a valley where magic couldn't follow, where the beasts around me were just beasts — well, no. Not just beasts. Bracken was never *just* anything. But the valley was quiet in the way of small places: a badger, some goats, the crows on the fence posts, the mice in the walls. My whole life, my gift had been a candle flame cupped in two hands, one voice at a time, one creature at a time, and even that had been dangerous enough to hide.

The capital was not a candle. The capital was a forest fire.

It came over me by degrees as we passed through the outer districts, and then all at once, like walking into cold water. First the carthorses — I felt them before I saw them, a weary gray patience, dozens of them standing in shafts along the street, and threaded through their tiredness a low muttering current of thought: *heavy load, heavy load, hill soon, rest after.* Then the dogs — every doorway seemed to have one, chained or leashed or lounging, and their attention snapped onto our cart like fish turning in a pond. Then the birds, oh, the birds — pigeons on every roof ridge, starlings in the gutters, sparrows in such numbers that the eaves seemed to ripple, and beneath it all, deeper down, something vast and slow and sad that took me a moment to place.

Stables. Enormous ones. Whole streets of them, and every stall occupied.

I gripped the edge of the bench. Elara glanced sideways. "Casper?"

"It's loud," I whispered. "It's so *loud*. How do they stand it? How do the animals stand it?"

"They don't hear each other," she said. "That's the thing about the bells. It keeps them — us — separate. Dampened. Why do you think the Hunt uses hounds trained outside the walls?"

But they weren't silent to *me*. That was the horror of it, and the wonder too, and I couldn't untangle which was which. Every animal in that city was talking — not in words, exactly, but in the constant, murmuring stream of want and fear and habit that is how creatures think — and none of them could hear any of the others. A thousand voices in a thousand sealed rooms. A chorus where every singer had been given a soundproof cell.

And underneath the ordinary murmur — the *feed me*, the *cold nose*, the *where is my person* — there was something else. Something that made my stomach drop.

Fear. A specific fear, repeated everywhere, in every species, in every street. I heard it in a dray horse as we passed the granary. I heard it in a terrier tied outside a butcher's shop. I heard it in the pigeons wheeling over the bell tower, in the fat stable cats, in a mule standing listlessly in a courtyard with its head hanging.

*The men take some away,* the fear said, in whatever wordless way fear says itself. *The men in grey come and some do not come back.*

"The Hunt," I breathed. "They've been taking animals too?"

Elara's jaw tightened. "Questioning them. The gifted can be found through their beasts — a speaker's animals react to their capture. And some of the interrogators believe the beasts themselves know things." Her hands were very still on the reins, which is how I'd learned to tell when she was angriest. "I signed the requisition orders, Casper. Two years of them. I told myself it was necessary."

The cart jolted over a cobblestone. In a doorway, a one-eared cat watched us pass, and its thoughts brushed mine like a cold finger: *cart smells of wolf and wrongness, look away, look away.*

For the first time in my life, I understood what Bracken had meant when he told me, in his grumbling way, that my gift was not a trick but a *debt*. "Every voice you're given," he'd said, "is a voice you owe something to." I had owed the valley's goats their gossip and their scratches behind the ears. I owed these thousands of animals something, and I didn't even know their names.

* * *

The gates of the inner city swallowed us at midday.

There was a line of carts, and guards, and Elara showing papers with a name on them that made the guards stand straighter and look at me with a mixture of pity and disgust. One of them reached out and touched my collar, testing it, and I flinched — genuinely, because even a fake collar carries the memory of what it imitates — and the guard smiled like I'd confirmed something for him.

"Voss," he said. "Heard you'd gone soft. Guess the reports were wrong."

"The reports were wrong," Elara said, in a voice like a drawn blade being sheathed very slowly.

Inside the walls, the hum became a roar.

I don't know how else to describe it. Imagine every goat you've ever met, every dog, every bird, every mouse in every wall, all speaking at once, and then imagine you are the only one who can hear them, and you must walk through them smiling with your head bowed like proper captured goods. The warhorses in the palace paddocks screamed silently in their boredom and pain — bits, spurs, the endless drilling. The ravens on the cathedral roosted in ranks like soldiers and thought in sharp black fragments: *shiny, order, order, the tall one feeds us, watch the tall one.* The palace hounds — the Hunt's own beasts, huge and scarred and disciplined — lay in kennels that smelled of blood and disinfectant, and their dreams leaked out into the yard like smoke, and their dreams were of running, always running, and of a smell they could never quite catch.

My smell. I realized it with a lurch. Their dreams were of my smell.

"Keep your breathing even," Elara murmured. We were walking now, the cart left at a livery, and I was on a lead — a *lead*, like a goat at market, though the clasp Sef built meant one twist of my wrist would free it. "You look like you're about to be sick."

"I am about to be sick. There are four hundred horses in that stable alone and every single one of them is lonely, and the pigeons have organized themselves into a military formation, and the hounds dream about me, Elara. They dream about me *by name of scent*."

"Four hundred and twelve," she said absently. "In the main stable. I used to exercise there." Then she caught herself, hearing what she'd said, hearing how easily the numbers came, and her face did something complicated and closed.

We crossed a square where a fountain stood dry, and around the fountain — cages. Small ones, ornamental, bolted to the stone like lamps. In them: songbirds. Dozens of songbirds, finches and larks and one bedraggled nightingale, and they were singing, because that is what songbirds do, but their song had a thread in it I will hear until I die. It was the sound of a creature singing to prove it was still alive, singing because the last bird that stopped singing was taken away, singing because the cage door has never once opened on its own.

I stopped walking. The lead went taut. Elara turned.

"Casper."

"They're singing to be let out," I said. My voice came out strange and thin. "They sing *please* and everyone thinks it's pretty."

Elara looked at the cages for a long moment. Around us, the square went on — merchants, guards, children chasing a hoop, a woman selling roasted chestnuts — all of it utterly indifferent to the small desperate music. This was the part that frightened me most, I think. Not the cruelty. Cruelty I had imagined. It was how *normal* it all looked. The whole city was built on top of this hum the way a coffin lid sits on a body, and everyone walked across it without hearing a thing.

"We'll come back for them," Elara said quietly. "When this is done. All of them."

"Do you mean that?"

"I'm standing in the city I served for eleven years, leading my— leading a prisoner I falsified papers to deliver, planning treason against a king I swore oaths to." She looked at the nightingale. "Yes, Casper. I mean it."

* * *

They put me in a holding cell in the Hunt's barracks, which was the plan, and the plan worked, which somehow made it worse. Sef had forged documents enough to buy us a night and a day before the transfer to the palace proper — one night in which I was supposed to rest while Elara attended the evening muster and learned what she could about the menagerie rotations. Rest. In a stone room with a barred window, three floors up, overlooking the barracks yard.

I rested the way a kettle rests.

Because the yard below was full of animals awaiting questioning. I could feel them through the floor, through the walls, through my teeth: a cartload of farm dogs from the eastern provinces, a gyrfalcon with a hooded head and a heart like a hammer, a shaggy hill pony whose thoughts were nothing but *home home home home* repeated until it frayed. And in the corner of the yard, in a cage too small for him, a young fox with a foreleg caught wrong in the bars, thinking, in the simple arithmetic of wild things: *hurt, trapped, hurt, trapped, if I am very still maybe the world will forget me.*

I pressed my forehead against the cool stone and listened to all of it, and I understood — really understood, for the first time since Bracken was taken — why we had come. Not for the king, not at first, not in the beginning. Sef talked of bindings and rituals; Elara talked of duty and treason; but underneath all our grand plans was this: a city full of voices sealed in rooms, waiting for someone to open the doors.

A chorus waiting for a voice.

And the terrible, dizzying thought that arrived with it, uninvited, lying on a cell bunk in the belly of the machine that hunted my kind:

*I can hear every one of them. What happens on the thirteenth moon if I can hear every one of them at once?*

The thought should have terrified me. Part of it did. But another part — the part that had spent twenty years talking to a lame badger because he was the only soul in the valley who knew me — that part leaned toward the hum like a plant toward light. All those voices. All that loneliness, stacked street upon street. If I could reach them, they wouldn't be alone, and neither, gods forgive me, would I.

Somewhere below, the young fox shifted its broken leg and went very still again, hoping the world would forget it.

I knew that hope. I had lived inside it my whole life, in a valley built to forget.

Footsteps in the corridor — Elara's stride, unmistakable, and a second set of boots beside hers, heavier, unhurried. A key turned. The door swung open, and Elara stood in the torchlight with a face carefully arranged into official blankness, and behind her loomed a broad-shouldered man in a grey uniform I hadn't seen before, with a ring of keys at his belt and eyes like coins.

"This is the prisoner?" the man said. "Voss, the Menagerie Master wants her moved tonight. The king's orders." He smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "His Majesty likes to visit the animals after dark. Seems he's expecting company."

Elara's hand, resting on her sword hilt, went very slightly white at the knuckle.

"The king," she said evenly, "wants to see her *now*?"
