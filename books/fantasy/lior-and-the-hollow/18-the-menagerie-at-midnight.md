## 18. The Menagerie at Midnight

The menagerie slept the way a city sleeps — badly, and in layers.

Casper heard it before she saw it. From three streets away, through Elara's borrowed guard cloak and Sef's nervous muttering, she felt it: a low, restless hum of animal misery that pressed against her gift like a hand against a window. Horses. Hounds. Hawks. Things she had no names for, brought from provinces she'd never see, all stacked in gilded cages behind the palace's eastern wall.

"They call it the Royal Collection," Sef whispered as they crouched in the shadow of an aqueduct arch. He'd insisted on coming this far, though his hands shook so badly the lockpicks chattered in their leather roll like teeth. "I catalogued half of it, back when I still thought service to the crown was a form of virtue. Twelve acres. Four hundred cages. The king added most of them himself."

"The king collects animals?"

"The king *visits* animals." Sef's voice did something complicated on the word *visits*. "No one knows why. The stable staff draw lots to avoid the night shift."

Above them, the thirteenth moon was six nights away — a fat silver coin with a bite already taken from its edge. Casper could feel it too, a tightening behind her ribs, like a rope being coiled. Six nights to find Bracken. Six nights to learn whatever he'd learned in his cage about the thing wearing the crown.

"Gate's there," Elara said. She hadn't spoken much since they'd entered the capital; the city sat on her like a debt. Her lieutenant's insignia — Dain's insignia now, gods rot him — got them past two checkpoints on forged orders, but the menagerie gate would need more than paper. It would need Casper.

She closed her eyes and let the gift open.

The night came alive. Rats along the aqueduct, going about their small commerce. A cat on a rooftop, watching them with professional disinterest. And inside the walls, layered and layered: the breathing of hundreds of beasts, each one a small warm note in a chord that should have been music and was instead a wound.

*Little sister,* said a voice near the gate, dry and amused. A raven perched on the arch above them, feathers slicked dark as spilled ink. *You're the one they whisper about. The valley-voice.*

*I'm looking for a badger,* Casper answered silently, the way she'd spoken to Bracken ten thousand evenings under the Hollow's grey sky. *Old. Lame in the left foreleg. Grumpy enough to curdle milk at forty paces.*

The raven made a sound that was almost laughter. *Grumpy is the whole building, little sister. But a badger who bites the keeper's son and tells fortunes — yes. I know him. East row, third from the fountain, the iron cage they think is clever because it has no bars he can see through.* The raven cocked its head. *He asks about you every night, you know. In his way. He asks if the moon is full yet.*

Casper's throat closed. She managed, after a moment: *Tell him—*

*Tell him yourself,* the raven said. *I'll keep the watchman's dog busy. He owes me for a sausage incident I will not describe.* And it lifted off the arch into the dark, and somewhere below, a hound began barking at absolutely nothing with tremendous commitment.

Elara looked at her. "In?"

"In."

* * *

The menagerie at midnight was worse than the hum had promised.

Lanterns burned low along gravel paths, and between them stood the cages — some of wood and wire, some of stone, some, for the dangerous or the precious, of iron worked into shapes meant to be beautiful. A snow leopard from the northern ranges paced a circuit exactly eleven steps long, turning at each end with mechanical precision, its eyes fixed on nothing. A herd of red deer stood so close together in their pen that they seemed to be trying to become one deer. Monkeys had torn out their own fur in patches. Everything smelled of straw gone sour and disinfectant and despair, and beneath it all ran the thing that made Casper's hands shake: silence. Not one animal made a sound. Four hundred creatures, and the night was quiet as a held breath.

They'd been taught quiet. Or something had.

"Gods," Sef breathed beside her. He had his hood up and his eyes down, and he walked like a man moving through his own sins. "I inventoried this place. I wrote the acquisition ledgers. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "You spend your life around mages, you learn to call cruelty *necessity*. It's such a tidy word."

"Don't drown," Elara murmured. "Not yet. Drown after."

Casper barely heard them. She was walking faster now, past the foxes and the cranes and a bear so old its muzzle had gone white, following the thread of her own heart eastward toward the fountain she couldn't see yet but could feel — and then the raven's directions resolved, and there it was.

An iron cage, third from the fountain, built with no bars facing the path — just smoked glass, so the animal inside could see out and no visitor could be troubled by seeing in. Clever. Cruel in the way that called itself kindness.

Inside, on a shelf of straw, lay a badger.

He was smaller than she remembered. That was the first obscenity of it — Bracken had always seemed vast, a boulder with opinions, and now he fit in a cage built for something prouder. His fur had gone grey at the muzzle and thin along his spine. His left foreleg was wrapped in a stained bandage. He lay facing the wall.

Casper pressed her palm flat against the cold glass.

*Bracken.*

Nothing. The shape in the straw didn't move.

*Bracken, you great stubborn lump of a creature, it's me. It's Casper. You absolute menace, you bit a keeper's son, what were you thinking—*

The badger's ear twitched.

Then he rolled over, and Casper saw his face, and she had to put her forehead against the glass because the relief hurt worse than anything the road had given her. His eyes were clouded now, milky at the edges, and they moved without finding things — but when they found the general direction of her palm, they stopped.

*Well,* Bracken said. His mind-voice was frayed at the edges, like a blanket washed too many times, but underneath it, unmistakably, it was him. *Either I've finally gone properly mad, which the food here suggests is overdue, or my girl has done something catastrophically stupid.*

"Catastrophically," Casper whispered aloud, because some things deserved air. "Hi, old man."

*Hi yourself.* A pause. She felt him gathering himself, sorting the world back into shapes. *You look taller. Road-fed. Someone's been feeding you actual meat instead of the sorrow I taught you to cook.*

"Sef cooks now. Badly. There's a hunt-captain who threatens him about it."

*A hunt-captain.* Bracken's whiskers twitched, and even frayed, his skepticism arrived fully formed. *You're traveling with a hunt-captain. My girl. The one who wouldn't steal an egg from a nest with no bird in it.*

"She freed us from a prison convoy. She's getting branded for it soon, probably."

*Mm.* He considered this with the gravity he'd once reserved for judging whether a storm was worth complaining about. *Then she'll do. Bring her around sometime. I'll bite her gently, as a welcome.*

Casper laughed, and it came out wet, and she didn't care. Behind her, Elara stood with her arms crossed and her face turned politely away, and Sef was examining the fountain as if fountains required intense study.

"I'm getting you out," Casper said. "That's why we're here. Well — not only why. But mostly why. Tonight, ideally. Sef's good with locks now, aren't you, Sef?"

"I'm adequate with locks," Sef said faintly, "under ideal conditions, which this is not—"

*No.*

The word landed like a stone dropped down a well. Casper went still.

*No, you don't,* Bracken said, and now his mind-voice had found its old iron underneath the fraying. *Listen to me, girl, and listen with the ears I know you have. This cage is bait. We both know it. They put me where I am the way you put out a snare with fresh meat in it. If you spring the trap tonight, they learn your face, your friends, your tricks — six nights before the moon. You'll trade my one mangy life for the kingdom's one chance, and I will haunt you, and I am *extremely* good at haunting. I once haunted a farmer for a full summer over a fence post.*

"You don't get a vote," Casper said, and her voice cracked in the middle. "You don't get to decide you're not worth saving. That's not how family works."

Something moved behind the clouded eyes. *Family,* he repeated softly. *Huh. Took you long enough to say it.*

"Years. You were very annoying about it."

*I was thorough about it. There's a difference.* He shifted then, wincing at the bandaged leg, and dragged himself closer to the glass until his nose nearly touched where her palm rested. When he spoke again, the gruffness fell away, and what was left was the voice that had raised her, in its way — patient, grave, older than the valley. *Then hear me, daughter-of-my-heart, because I've had months in this cage to think, and madness gives a badger time. The king comes here. At night. Did the mage tell you?*

Sef stepped closer. "The staff avoid the night shift. We assumed—"

*Assumptions are for people with better information.* Bracken settled himself, and Casper felt the other animals nearby go still — the whole east row leaning in, the way beasts do when an elder speaks. *Three nights ago. Two nights ago. Every night since they brought me here, near the hour when even the rats sleep. He walks these paths alone. No guards — they wait at the gate. No lantern. He doesn't need one, which should tell you everything. And he stops at every cage, one by one, and he whispers.*

"Whispers what?" Elara asked quietly.

*Questions.* Bracken's claws clicked once against the iron floor. *He leans close to the mesh, or the glass, or the bars, and he whispers questions. What do you remember. Who taught you your name. Have you heard the voice — have you heard the true voice — does any creature living remember the true voice of Aldric of the House of—* He broke off, and his breath came rough. *Forgive me. Some nights it gets tangled.*

"It's all right."

*It is not all right, but proceed regardless.* He gathered himself again. *And here is the thing that has kept me awake, girl, here is the thing I'd shout from the valley top if the valley weren't full of people who burn beasts that talk. No animal answers him. Do you understand? Four hundred creatures, and not one of us answers. The leopard pretends to sleep. The ravens study their feet. An old bear who knew the king's grandfather when he was a cub in the western woods — he lies down and plays dead rather than answer. Because we can all hear it, you see. Under the whisper. Under the kind, tired, lonely human voice asking its questions, there's the *other* voice. The one that wears him. And if you answer — if any beast answers — the other voice hears you. Knows you. Marks you.*

The night seemed to lean closer. Somewhere far off in the menagerie, the snow leopard had stopped pacing.

"He's searching," Sef whispered. His face had gone the color of the moonlight. "All this time — the Hunt, the interrogations, the collections — he's not hunting the gifted. He's *listening* for one. For a voice strong enough to reach through. To reach *him*."

*Now the mage does arithmetic,* Bracken said dryly. *Yes. And every night he leaves here having heard nothing, and every night the thing in him grows hungrier, and every night the questions come a little less human. Last night, girl, last night he stood at my cage — mine, the lame old badger who can't even see straight — and he asked me if I remembered the sound of bells before they learned to scream. And I played mad. I drooled and muttered and scratched my ear like a flea-bitten fool, and he watched me with those eyes that are two colors, girl, one blue and one the color of a winter sky at dusk, and he smiled, and he said, 'Another night, then,' and walked on.*

Casper realized she was gripping the glass so hard her fingers ached. "Six nights," she said. "On the thirteenth moon, they make it permanent. Sef found the ritual. If it finishes—"

*Then no voice reaches him ever again, and the questions stop being questions.* Bracken nodded slowly, the motion stiff, an old joint working. *So. Now you understand why you cannot take me tonight. Look at me, Casper. I'm half-blind, three-legged, and mad as a March hare on the wrong week. I'm no use to you on the road. But here — here I'm a spy. Here I'm the one creature in this palace who has stood nose-to-nose with the thing that wears the crown and lived, because I'm too broken to be interesting. Every night he comes, and every night I listen, and every night I learn. Let me be your ears, girl. One last job. Let an old badger do what badgers do best.*

"Which is what?" Casper asked, though she feared the answer.

*Dig in,* Bracken said, *and hold.*

* * *

They argued in the shadow of the fountain afterward, in voices barely louder than the water. Elara wanted to take him anyway — "We didn't come all this way to leave him in a cage" — and Sef argued logistics, and Casper stood apart with her hand still remembering the cold of the glass, listening to both sides and hearing, underneath, the truth she didn't want.

He was right. That was the worst of it. He was right the way he'd always been right about weather and fences and which mushrooms lied.

*Girl.* Bracken's voice reached her alone, soft, private, a channel worn smooth by years. *Come here. One more thing, and then you go, and you go quickly, because the watch changes at the second bell and the raven's sausage diversion won't hold forever.*

She came back to the glass. He pressed his ruined old nose against it, and she pressed her fingers opposite, and between them was half an inch of iron-smelted cold that felt like the whole distance of the world.

*When he comes tomorrow night,* Bracken said, *and the next night, and the next — I'll listen for you. Not for him. For you. Whatever you do on the thirteenth moon, wherever you stand, however loud it gets — I'll hear it. And I want you to know something, so you carry it with you into that square.* His mind-voice steadied, and became, for one moment, exactly the voice that had told her bedtime stories about the valley before the valley existed. *I have listened to every creature in this rotten collection, and the wolves, and the birds, and the fish through the pond water, and I tell you this: none of them are afraid of you, little sister. They're waiting for you. The way the field waits for rain. Whatever it costs you — and it will cost, I know you've guessed — it will not be spent on strangers. It will be spent on family. All of them. Ours.*

Casper wiped her face with her sleeve, the guard's cloak be damned. "You're supposed to be the cynical one."

*I'm flexible.* The whiskers twitched. *Now go. And girl — the mare. In the royal stables. The old grey mare they keep apart from the others, in the end stall, because the king won't let them put her down. He visits her too. He sits with her for hours. And she — * his voice caught, frayed again — *she remembers him laughing, Casper. Before. If anyone holds the thread of who he was, it's her. Follow that thread.*

"I will."

*Casper.* He said her name plainly, no title, no joke, the way he'd said it a thousand times across a goat pasture. *Whatever happens — you were the best thing the valley ever grew, and the valley never knew it. Go home to your family. All of them.*

She made herself let go of the glass. She made herself walk away, one step, then another, while behind her the smoked panes held their secret and the old badger settled back onto his straw and turned, deliberately, to face the wall — playing mad again, playing bait again, digging in, holding.

At the fountain, Elara touched her shoulder once, briefly, which from Elara was a speech. Sef was already counting bells under his breath. Above them, the raven wheeled down from the dark and folded itself onto Casper's shoulder like a returned debt.

*East row's clear,* it said. *Also, the watchman's dog sends regards and demands another sausage. Also also — little sister — the king's early tonight. His lantern just came through the west gate.*

Casper froze. "He's not due for hours."

*Tell that to him,* the raven said, *because he's walking the rows right now, and he's stopping at cages, and he's whispering — and he's headed east.*

Toward the fountain. Toward the iron cage with no visible bars. Toward Bracken.

And between the king and the badger, with nowhere left to hide, stood Casper — guard's cloak, stolen insignia, and a gift that the thing wearing the crown had spent three years starving to hear.
