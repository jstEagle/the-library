## 19. Audience with the Hollow King

They caught her in the third corridor, which Casper would later maintain was unfair, because the first two corridors had been completely empty and had given her a dangerous amount of confidence.

She had been doing so well, too. Dressed in a scullery maid's borrowed smock, hair pinned under a cap, carrying a basket of turnips as though she had somewhere legitimate to be with them. She'd even practiced a servant's shuffle — head down, shoulders rounded, the walk of someone whose entire ambition was to reach the end of the day without being noticed. She'd learned it from watching the geese at the sanctuary, actually, who were masters of looking like they belonged absolutely nowhere and everywhere at once.

The trouble was the dogs.

Two of them, grey-muzzled palace hounds, had come around the corner with a pair of guards, and before Casper could arrange her face into proper servile blankness, both dogs stopped. Sat. Stared at her with their heads tipped, ears forward, tails giving one slow, hopeful wag.

*You smell like the field,* said the older one, wonderingly. *You smell like open sky.*

*I don't know what you mean,* Casper thought at them desperately. *I am a turnip girl. I love walls.*

The younger hound's tail thumped twice against the flagstones. *Turnip girl,* it repeated, delighted, filing the name away forever, because dogs never forgot anything and never let anything go.

"Oi," said one of the guards, following his dog's gaze. "You there."

Casper considered running. She genuinely did. But the corridor was long, the guards were between her and the servants' stair, and something in her — some tired, stubborn root of her — was done with running. She'd been running since the treeline. Since Bracken's cage. Since the valley.

So instead she set down the basket of turnips very carefully, straightened up out of her servile crouch, and said, "Took you long enough."

Which was, in retrospect, not the move of an innocent scullery maid.

* * *

They took the turnips. That stung more than the manacles, honestly. Sef had risked his neck procuring those turnips — well, "procured" implied effort; he'd charmed a market vendor into giving them away by convincing the man they were for the royal kitchens, which was technically true now — and Casper hated thinking of them sitting abandoned on the flagstones, going soft in the dark.

The guards marched her through corridors that grew progressively grander and progressively quieter. The palace at night was a strange animal: all its daytime noise swallowed, leaving only the sound of boots, breath, and somewhere far off, the low tolling of the great bells marking the hour. The bells here didn't just ring, Casper realized. They *pressed*. Every toll came with a pressure behind the ears, a flattening sensation, like walking underwater. The bells that suppressed magic. Sef had told her about them, and Dain had drawn their locations on a scrap of cloth, and knowing about them was nothing like feeling them settle over her skin like wet wool.

Her gift didn't go silent, exactly. It went *muffled*. Like shouting into a quilt.

But not entirely silent. That was the thing that kept her heart steady as they walked. Because even under the bells, even muffled to a whisper, she could hear the palace breathing around her. Horses in the distant stables, restless. Rats in the walls, going about their nightly business with the brisk professionalism rats brought to everything. And birds — hundreds of birds roosting in the eaves and bell towers, doves and starlings and jackdaws, all of them awake, all of them *watching*, because word travels fast among birds when a new human enters the great stone nest.

*Small loud one,* a jackdaw called from a ledge as she passed. *They're taking you UP. Nobody goes UP after dark except—*

It didn't finish. It ruffled its feathers and fell silent, and every other bird along the corridor went silent too, in a wave, like wind moving through wheat.

That frightened her more than the guards did.

*Except what?* she wanted to shout. *Except WHO?*

But the guards' grip on her arms was firm, and the stairs were going up, and the air was getting warmer, and there was a smell she couldn't place — something old, something like a cellar and a thunderstorm having a disagreement.

* * *

The throne room doors were taller than her house had been. Probably taller than her house and the goat barn combined. They were dark wood banded with iron, carved over with scenes she didn't have time to study — though she caught a glimpse of antlers, of a crown, of small figures kneeling — and when the guards pushed them open, the sound echoed for a full three seconds afterward, rolling around the vast space beyond like a stone dropped down a well.

The room was lit by perhaps two dozen candles. For a room that size, that was less light than a firefly convention. Most of it lay in shadow, and the shadows pooled along the walls like standing water.

King Aldric sat on his throne at the far end, alone.

No courtiers. No guards inside the doors — the ones who'd brought her withdrew without a word, and Casper heard the doors boom shut behind her and felt the click of some mechanism turning, a lock the size of her fist. No herald announcing her. Just the king, seated straight-backed on a throne of black stone, hands resting on its arms, watching her come.

Well. Watching was maybe the wrong word. His eyes were open, and they were pointed at her, but there was a quality to his gaze that Casper recognized from the farm — from the way a snake watches, unblinking, not so much seeing as *waiting*. He was a big man gone gaunt, silver-haired, wearing robes of deep blue stitched with constellations that had probably cost more than the entire valley earned in a decade. The crown on his head was old iron, plain and heavy-looking, nothing like the gold monstrosity she'd expected.

And he was *humming*.

Not aloud. Not with his throat. It was a resonance she felt in her back teeth, in the bones behind her ears, a low sustained note like the biggest bell in the world struck once and never allowed to stop ringing. The crown-thing. Sef had described it as a voice under the voice, a second heartbeat. What Sef hadn't managed to describe was how much it filled the room — how the whole cathedral-sized space felt less like a hall and more like the inside of a throat.

Casper walked. Her legs decided this on their own, apparently having concluded that freezing would be undignified. Halfway down the aisle of shadow she became aware that she could hear other things again — the muffle lifting slightly, or perhaps the thing on the throne *parting* it, making room. Mice in the wainscoting, frozen mid-step. A cat somewhere high in the rafters, pressed flat, eyes wide. Even the rats had stopped working. The whole hidden population of the palace was holding its breath.

*Everyone's listening,* she thought. *Good. Let them hear me be brave.*

Then, more honestly: *Please let me be brave.*

Twenty feet from the throne, a guard's instinct made her stop. There was no line on the floor, no rope, but everything about the architecture said *this far and no farther*, and her legs, having gotten her this far, agreed.

For a long moment, nothing happened.

The king looked at her. The humming filled the silence. Candle flames leaned, all together, all in the same direction, as if a door had opened somewhere.

And then the thing inside him went *still*.

Casper didn't know how she knew — there was no motion to stop, no sound to cease. But the quality of the room changed, the way a forest changes when the deer catch your scent. The constant hungry pressure of the humming pulled inward, gathered itself, focused. She felt it the way you feel a hand hover an inch from the back of your neck.

It was tasting her.

There was no better word for it. Something ancient and enormous was passing over her the way a tongue passes over a strange food, patient, thorough, curious. She felt it find the edges of her gift — the beast-speech she'd spent her whole life hiding — and circle it, and press gently against it, the way Bracken used to nose at a closed hand to see what was inside.

She did the only thing she could think of. She closed her hand around it, metaphorically speaking, and held still, and thought about turnips.

*Interesting,* said the king.

His voice was wrong. That was the first thing. It was a fine voice, deep and cultured, a voice built for throne rooms — but it came out with a doubled edge, a harmonic underneath, like two singers a half-step apart. And it was *kind*. That was the second thing, and somehow the worse one. Nothing about him snarled or threatened. He sounded like a grandfather asking about your day.

"You've come a long way," he said. "Through my city, past my bells, into my house." He tilted his head, and the candlelight moved across his face, and for half a heartbeat Casper saw — or thought she saw — something flicker behind his eyes. Something tired. Something *frightened*. Then it was gone, smoothed away like a footprint in sand. "I imagine you didn't come for the architecture."

"I've heard the architecture is overrated," Casper said.

Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. Somewhere in the rafters, the cat made a tiny sound of approval.

The king smiled. It was a good smile. It reached his eyes, crinkled them warmly, did everything a smile should do — and Casper's skin crawled anyway, because she'd grown up reading faces that couldn't lie to her, and this smile was *worn*. It fit the way a stolen coat fits. Close enough to pass. Never quite right at the shoulders.

"There she is," he said softly. "Do you know, I wondered if I'd imagined you. Little voices in the valley, little whispers in the grass. Animals don't gossip, you know. Did you know that? People assume they do, but they don't. They *remember*. They carry things. And for three years they've been carrying fragments of a girl who talks to beasts, and I confess I began to think my hunger had invented you." He rose from the throne.

He was tall. Of course he was tall. Everything about tonight was committed to being imposing, and the king rising to his full height while candles guttered was simply the evening keeping up its standards.

But it wasn't the height that made Casper's mouth go dry. It was that as he descended the three steps from the dais, the animals of the palace began — quietly, one by one — to *whimper*. The mice. The cat. Somewhere outside, the hounds that had caught her, whining at their kennels across the courtyard. Not at the king. At the *note* under his voice, the one only they could hear clearly, the one that had been ringing for three years without pause.

"The Gifted Hunt was crude," the king continued, conversational, hands clasped behind his back as he circled her slowly. "I'll grant you that. My captains enjoy their work too much, and cruelty is such an inefficient tool. It makes the quarry stupid with fear, and stupid quarry runs in circles." He paused behind her. She felt the humming bend around her like water around a stone. "You haven't run in circles. You ran *here*. Straight to me. Do you want to know why?"

"Because I'm an idiot," Casper said. "It's been suggested."

The laugh that came out of him startled her — because for one instant, one flickering candle-beat, it was a *real* laugh. Round and warm and human, a laugh that belonged to somebody else entirely, somebody who might have been pleasant at a harvest festival. Then the harmonic slid back in and smoothed it flat, and the king's face settled into gentle amusement again.

"Oh, I like you," he said. "Genuinely. Do you know how long it's been since anyone spoke to me without calculating what it would cost them? Decades. Courtiers calculate. Priests calculate. My own reflection calculates, most days." He came around to face her again, and his eyes — grey, human, bloodshot at the edges — searched her face with what looked unbearably like loneliness. "You just talk. Like the animals do. Say the true thing and let it fall where it falls."

"That's not—" Casper started.

"It's a compliment. Take it." He raised a hand, and the pressure in the room eased, the humming receding to something almost tolerable, almost background. Almost a fireplace rather than a furnace. "I'm going to make you an offer, Casper Joubert. Yes, I know your name. Names are the easiest things in the world to learn, when everything that crawls and flies will tell you theirs."

Her stomach dropped. "What do you want?"

"What I've always wanted." He spread his hands, and the candlelight caught the iron crown, and she noticed for the first time that the crown was *fused* — the metal grown into the skin at his temples, thin tendrils of black iron threading back into his hairline like roots into soil. Her gift, muffled as it was, flinched away from the sight. "An ending to the noise. This kingdom screams, child. Day and night, it screams — grief and greed and fear, ten million throats, and I feel all of it. Every hour. Every prayer. Every lie sworn on every altar." His voice softened, and the awful thing was that it sounded *sincere*. "I am so tired. And then — a girl in a forgotten valley who can speak to every living thing. Who could gather them. Who could make them *quiet*, or make them sing, whichever the moment required. Do you understand what you are?"

"A goat farmer," Casper said.

"You are a *key*," the king said, "that has spent twenty years believing itself a rock. Stand beside me. Be my voice to the wild things and their voice to me. You would want for nothing. Your badger would live out his years in comfort — I keep my menagerie well, whatever else I am. Your friends would be pardoned. The Hunt would end tomorrow, by my own decree, and no gifted child would ever again see the inside of a wagon." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped to something almost tender, and the worst part — the part Casper knew she'd be replaying for weeks — was that she believed every word. "I am not offering you power, Casper. Power is what I have, and look how it's worn on me. I'm offering you *rest*. An end to hiding. A home where the thing you are is the reason you're cherished instead of the reason you burn."

Silence. The candles breathed. Somewhere above, the cat crept one careful inch closer to the edge of the rafter, watching.

And Casper stood there in her stolen smock with hay still in her hair, twenty-two years old, lonely in a way she'd never once said out loud, and let herself — just for a moment, just honestly — feel the pull of it. A home where she didn't have to hide. Bracken safe. Mirei safe. Elara and Sef free. All of it, handed to her, no thirteenth moon, no burning out of her soul, no terrible arithmetic.

It would be so easy.

That was how she knew, finally and completely, what he was. Not because of the fused crown or the doubled voice or the whimpering mice. Because the offer was tailored to her like a dress cut by someone who'd been reading her letters. Kindness that precise isn't kindness. It's bait.

"No," Casper said.

The temperature of the room did not change. The candles did not flare. Nothing dramatic happened at all — and yet every animal in the palace flinched at once, and Casper understood that she had just declined something that the thing in the crown had not been declined in a very, very long time.

"No?" the king said pleasantly.

"No," Casper said, and found, to her surprise, that her chin had come up. "You want quiet? You want the screaming to stop? Then take the crown off. Whatever's under it — whoever's under it — I think they'd like a word."

Something moved behind the king's eyes.

Not the flicker from before. This was different — slower, deeper, like a fish surfacing in a dark pond. His smile stayed fixed on his face, but his eyes stopped tracking hers, drifted, *focused inward*, and his lips parted, and for one impossible half-second the doubled voice collapsed into a single thread, thin and cracked and utterly human—

"—*can't hold it, it knows, run, girl, RUN*—"

—and then the harmonic crashed back in like a door slamming, and King Aldric blinked, and smiled, and said in his warm, kind, terrible voice:

"Well. We'll continue this conversation another time."

Behind Casper, with a boom that shook dust from the rafters, the throne room doors began to open.
