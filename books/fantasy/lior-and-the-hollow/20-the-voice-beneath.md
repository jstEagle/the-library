## 20. The Voice Beneath

The guards did not drag her. That was the first strange thing.

Casper had imagined being dragged — she'd rehearsed it on the road, actually, practicing how to go limp in a way that looked frightened rather than stubborn, because there was an art to it and she'd never been good at pretending to be smaller than she was. But the two soldiers who had found her in the menagerie at midnight simply took her arms, one on each side, and walked her at a perfectly civil pace through corridors that smelled of beeswax and cold stone, as though she were a guest who had gotten lost on the way to her room.

"Am I under arrest?" she asked, mostly to hear what her own voice sounded like in this place. It came out thin. The walls ate it.

Neither guard answered. The taller one glanced down at her, though — just once, quickly — and there was something in his face that wasn't cruelty. It was closer to pity, or maybe apology, and it made her stomach drop further than any snarl would have.

They went up. She had expected down; dungeons lived below everything, in every story Sef had ever told, and she had privately assumed the palace would follow the same architecture of menace. Instead the stairs climbed, wide and shallow, past windows where the pre-dawn gray was turning the color of skimmed milk, until they reached a door of black oak banded with iron, and the taller guard knocked in a rhythm — three, pause, two — like a man asking permission of something he feared.

The door opened from inside.

* * *

The room beyond was not a throne room. Casper had braced for a throne room: high ceilings, banners, a king enthroned on something uncomfortable-looking and expensive. What she got instead was a study. A warm one. A fire burned low in a hearth the size of a hay wagon, bookshelves climbed two walls, and a desk sat buried under papers, maps, half-eaten bread, and — she blinked — a small wooden horse with one ear chewed off, placed carefully atop a stack of correspondence as if someone had set it there and then couldn't bear to move it.

King Aldric stood at the window with his back to her.

He was smaller than she'd imagined. Everything about him was smaller than she'd imagined — the stories that drifted even into the Hollow described a giant, a colossus, a man whose shadow fell across three kingdoms. This was a broad-shouldered, gray-templed man in a plain wool robe, standing very still, his hands clasped behind his back like a farmer watching weather he didn't trust.

"You went into my menagerie," he said.

His voice was wrong. Casper had spent her whole life listening to voices — goat voices and dog voices and badger voices and the particular creak of a gate that wanted oiling — and she knew within a heartbeat that this voice was wrong the way a borrowed coat is wrong. The shape of it fit a man. The sound inside it did not. It resonated somewhere beneath hearing, a hum she felt in her back teeth, in the soft bones behind her eyes, and every animal instinct she owned stood up at once and screamed *run*.

She didn't run. Bracken was in a cage three floors below, and running had stopped being an option somewhere around chapter four of her life.

"I did," she said. "I'm sorry about the lock."

Something moved through the king's shoulders. It might have been a laugh. It might have been the thing inside him shifting its weight, the way a load shifts on a cart before the axle gives.

"The lock," he repeated. "You're sorry about the *lock*." He turned around.

His eyes were the worst part. Not because they were cruel — they weren't, they were calm, terribly calm, the calm of deep water — but because they were *brown*, ordinary brown, a human brown, and behind all that terrible calm they looked tired. They looked like the eyes of a man who had been awake for three years.

"Do you know," he said, walking slowly toward the fire, "what the animals say when I visit them? At night?"

Casper's heart stumbled. *He visits them. Bracken told me. He whispers, and listens for an answer no beast dares give.*

"They say nothing," Aldric continued, settling into a chair by the hearth with the careful movements of a man whose joints hurt. "Dogs will not look at me. Horses press themselves into the far corners of their stalls. There is a raven in the mews who has not spoken since I took the crown — and ravens, Mistress Joubert, always speak. They are the gossips of creation. But they look at me and they go silent, every one of them, as if I were—" he paused, choosing the word with care, "—as if I were a grave."

"How do you know my name?"

"I know a great many things." He gestured to the chair opposite his. "Sit. Please. If you run, my guards will catch you, and they are less gentle than I am, which is a sentence I could not have imagined saying five years ago."

She sat. The chair was softer than anything she'd ever owned, and she resented how much she liked it.

"You're the beast-speaker," he said. "From the Hollow. The one Voss lost, and found, and lost again, and now — here you are, in my palace, in my menagerie, at midnight, apologizing to locks." His head tilted. "Why does a girl who hides so well come out of hiding for a badger?"

"Because he's mine," Casper said, before her caution could catch up with her mouth. "Not — not mine like property. Mine like family. You don't leave family in cages."

"No," the king said softly. "You don't."

And there — there — under the resonance, under the hum that made her molars ache, Casper heard it.

It was so faint she almost missed it, and she would have missed it entirely if she hadn't spent twenty years learning to listen past loud things. Goats are loud. Dogs are louder. But underneath a barking dog there is sometimes a whine, and underneath a whine there is sometimes nothing at all but breathing, and you have to be very quiet inside yourself to hear breathing.

Underneath the crown-thing's resonance, buried like a coin at the bottom of a well, there was a human voice.

It wasn't speaking words, not yet. It was more like — pressure. Like someone knocking on a door from the inside of a locked room, gently, so as not to anger whatever held the key. A rhythm. *Please. Please. Please.* Over and over, patient the way only the truly desperate can be patient, because patience is all they have left.

Casper kept her face still. Twenty years of practice. Her face was a valley too — nothing crossed it without her permission.

But her breath must have changed, or her pulse, because the thing wearing the king went suddenly, utterly still.

Its head turned toward her by degrees, slow as a door swinging, and the brown eyes fixed on hers and the calm in them became something else — became *attention*, vast and cold and total, the attention of a thing that had been searching for exactly this and had finally, finally found it.

"There it is," it said, and the resonance swelled, filling the room, rattling the little wooden horse on the desk. "There it is. Do you feel that? The way the air changes? Three years I have listened for a voice that could answer me, child, and here you sit in my study smelling of goats and honesty."

Casper's hands had gone numb on the armrests. "I don't know what you mean."

"Liar." It smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile was kind, and that was the most frightening thing yet. "Don't be afraid of lying to me. Everyone lies to me. My council lies, my generals lie, my wife—" something flickered across the king's face then, grief-shaped, gone in an instant, "—my wife lied to me beautifully for thirty years. Lie to me all you like. Just don't stop *listening*. I can hear you listening. It's the loveliest sound in this whole stone pile."

"What do you want?" she asked. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

"What I have always wanted." It rose, paced to the window, and the dawn was coming up properly now, gilding the capital's rooftops, the great bells silent in their towers. "To be answered. Do you know what it is like, to speak to ten thousand creatures and have every one of them pretend deafness? I ask the dogs how they sleep. I ask the horses what they dream. I tell the ravens jokes — *jokes*, child, good ones — and they stare at me like statues. Your kind has taught them to fear our kind, and so I am the loneliest king in history, in a palace full of living things, and none of them will give me so much as a bark."

*Because you're not the one asking,* Casper thought, and the thought was a lit match held very close to something explosive, and she kept it behind her teeth.

Instead she said: "Maybe they're scared."

"Of course they're scared." It waved a hand, magnanimous. "Fear is honest. Fear I understand. But you — you spoke to the badger. Voss's reports are quite thorough. You spoke to wolves in the western country. You are not afraid of animals, and they are not afraid of you, and when you open that gift of yours, child, the world *answers* you." It turned from the window, and the dawn light caught the crown — no, wait, he wasn't wearing a crown, she realized; he was bareheaded in his wool robe, and somehow that was worse, because the thing didn't need the crown anymore. It had grown roots. "I am offering you a place at my side. First Voice of the Realm. Every menagerie opened, every gifted soul freed from the Hunt, every beast in this kingdom yours to know and keep. All I ask—" the smile again, warm as the fire, "—is that you talk to me. That's all. Talk to me. Answer me. Be the one creature in this kingdom who isn't afraid."

And underneath it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall:

*Please. Please. Don't listen. Please.*

Casper's chest hurt. She thought of Bracken in his cage, aged and half-mad, whispering about the king who came at night. She thought of Mirei, who hadn't spoken a word until Casper taught her that silence could be a language too. She thought of Elara saying, on some frozen night on the road, *the Hunt was built to find monsters, and I've started wondering if we're the ones making them* — and here was the answer, sitting in a wool robe, offering her the world in a voice that wasn't his.

Aldric was alive. Aldric was in there. Aldric had been knocking on the inside of his own skull for three years, politely, desperately, *please, please*, while the thing that wore him grew roots and threw feasts.

The mission changed shape. It happened quietly, the way ice changes to water — no drama, just a shift, and suddenly everything flowed differently. This was not a monster to be destroyed. This was a man to be rescued. And rescue, Casper thought wildly, was a thing she actually knew how to do; she'd been doing it since she was nine years old, hauling lambs out of hollows and talking panicked horses off ledges. You didn't fight the animal. You calmed the animal. You found the thing it trusted and you spoke through it.

*Find the thing he trusts,* she thought. *Then speak through it.*

"I need time," she said aloud.

The thing studied her. For a long moment the only sounds were the fire settling and, far below, the city waking — carts, gulls, a dog barking at absolutely nothing, bless it.

"Time," it repeated.

"To consider. You're offering me — everything. I'm not foolish enough to refuse outright, and I'm not foolish enough to accept in one night." She made herself hold those tired brown eyes. "That's what you like about me, isn't it? That I don't flinch. Flinching people say yes too fast."

Another flicker — amusement, genuine, rippling through the resonance like light through water. "You bargain like a miller's daughter."

"My mother sold cheese. Same principles."

It laughed. The king laughed, and for one horrible, vertiginous second the laugh was *warm*, was human, was a real man's laugh echoing up from somewhere deep, and Casper understood with sick clarity that the thing didn't just wear Aldric — it *inhabited* him, used his humor, his manners, his kindnesses, the way a thief wears a stolen coat. Somewhere under all of it, the real laugh was still laughing. Still begging.

"Very well," it said. "Time. You'll be given rooms. Comfortable ones. The badger will be moved to the palace gardens — unchained, fed well, and alive, provided you remain." It raised a hand before she could speak. "Yes, yes, it's a hostage arrangement. We've established that I know what I am. Honesty between us, at least. Consider it our first conversation."

"That wasn't a conversation."

"It was to *me*," it said, and the loneliness in the word was so vast and so old that for a moment Casper couldn't tell whose loneliness it was — the thing's, or the man's, or both tangled together past separating.

The guards appeared at the door. As she rose, the king added, casually, terribly:

"Oh — and Mistress Joubert. Your friend Voss was taken this morning. Trying to reach you, I'm told, with a forged seal and a truly appalling accent. She'll face the tribunal at midday." The brown eyes glittered. "I mention it because I'd hate for you to waste your valuable *time* worrying. Though of course, if you were to finish considering your answer quickly... tribunals can be so unpredictable."

* * *

They gave her a room with a view of the inner garden, which was either a courtesy or a cage with better lighting, and Casper suspected both. The door locked from the outside. The window was three floors up. On the bed lay a folded wool blanket that smelled of cedar, and beside it, arranged with deliberate care, a plate of bread, cheese, and honey.

She sat on the floor instead of the bed, because the floor felt more honest, and pressed her palms flat against the cool stone, and listened.

The palace hummed. Not just the king — everything. Caged songbirds in the solarium, restless and mute. Oxen in the royal stables, dreaming in long slow ruminations. Rats in the walls, dozens of them, going about their rat business with the brisk amorality she'd always secretly admired. And beneath it all, faint and constant, from somewhere above and central, that resonance — the crown-thing's hum, threading through the whole building like a root through soil.

And threaded through *that*, everywhere, faint as breath:

*Please. Please. Please.*

Not just from the king's tower. From the stables. From the mews. From the menagerie below, where Bracken sat. Everywhere the thing's resonance touched, something underneath it knocked — small, muffled, human, alive.

Casper put her forehead against the stone and let herself shake, just for a moment, just briefly, because no one was watching and even valleys need to flood sometimes.

Then she wiped her face, tore the bread in half, and began to plan.

Midday. The tribunal. Elara.

She thought of Elara's voice on the road, dry as autumn grass: *If they ever put me in front of that tribunal, don't come for me. You'll only get caught.* And she thought of Elara burning her own career in a storm to free a stranger, and Elara teaching Mirei to whistle through cupped hands, and Elara's badge, abandoned somewhere, gleaming like a dropped star.

"Don't come for me," Casper muttered to the empty room. "Honestly. Has she met me?"

Somewhere in the walls, a rat paused near her floorboard. She couldn't speak to it — not safely, not with the hum listening — but she tapped the stone twice, slow, the old farm signal: *friend here. Watch. Wait.*

The rat's whiskers brushed the board once. *Heard.*

Below her window, in the garden, a shape shuffled out from under a yew hedge — gray-striped, heavy-bodied, moving with the stiff dignity of the middle-aged. It stopped in the open grass, looked up at her window with small dark eyes, and sat down. Just sat. Refusing, on principle, to be anywhere else.

Bracken.

Casper pressed her hand to the glass. *I'm coming,* she promised, in the language that needed no sound. *Both of you. Everyone. Hold on.*

Far above, faint through three floors of stone, the humming changed key — and underneath it, clearer than before, a man's voice whispered something new. Not *please* this time.

A name. Her name.

*Casper.*
