## 21. Elara's Reckoning

They took her boots first.

Elara Voss had been a soldier long enough to know that the small indignities always came before the large ones. The boots were good leather, Hunt-issue, and the sergeant who confiscated them wouldn't meet her eyes — which told her he'd been briefed on who she was, and what she'd done, and that some part of him was ashamed to be standing there doing it.

"Mind the floor," she said pleasantly. "It's cold in here."

He flushed and left without answering.

The cell they gave her in the Hall of the Hunt was, ironically, nicer than any quarters she'd occupied in six years of field command. A proper bed. A window — barred, but a window nonetheless, looking out over the training yards where she had once drilled recruits in the art of not flinching when the hounds sang. Someone wanted her comfortable. Comfort, in Elara's experience, was never free.

She counted the hours by the light through the bars and spent them doing what she did best: planning for conversations she didn't want to have.

There would be three of them at the tribunal. She knew the composition before she was told, because tribunals of the Hunt were always three: one voice of the crown, one voice of the hounds, one voice of the field. The crown's seat would go to Lord Chancellor Merrick or one of his grey-faced clerks. The hounds' seat belonged to Master of Kennels Odile Brannigan, who had once held Elara's hair back after a funeral and now would not look at her across a table. And the field's seat —

The field's seat would be Dain.

That was the one that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

They came for her at dusk, two guards and a woman with a ledger who read her rights in a voice like someone reciting a shopping list. *Elara Voss, formerly Captain of the Third Hunt Company, stands accused of treason against the crown, abandonment of sworn duty, theft of crown property — "the property being people," Elara said, and the ledger-woman paused, made a small note, and continued — assault upon fellow officers, and arson.*

"The barn was already on fire," Elara said. "I just failed to put it out."

"That's not what the report says."

"No," Elara agreed. "Reports rarely say what happened. That's rather the point of reports."

The woman's mouth twitched. It was the smallest victory Elara had won in weeks, and she treasured it.

They walked her through corridors she knew better than her own heartbeat. Past the map room where she'd planned the sweep of the Hollow valley. Past the kennels, where the hounds began to sing as she passed — that high, wrong frequency that hurt something behind the eyes — and she stopped, despite the guards' hands on her arms, and listened.

They weren't singing at her.

They were singing toward the east wall, all of them, forty throats raised in the same direction, like compass needles. Toward the capital's eastern quarter. Toward wherever Casper Joubert was hiding, if she was still hiding, if she was still alive.

"They've caught the scent," one guard said, with the dull pride of a man reporting good weather.

"Mm," said Elara, and thought: *run east, you impossible girl, run east and keep running,* and then thought, immediately after, with the weary certainty of someone who had spent eleven days in the girl's company: *she won't. She'll do something stupid and brave and I will have to live with it.*

The tribunal chamber had been the chapel of the old fort, back before the Hunt had needed a hall instead of a god. The altars were gone; in their place stood a long table of black oak beneath the thirteenth-moon sigil of the crown — a silver disc ringed by twelve smaller ones, though everyone knew the thirteenth moon was the one that mattered now, the one the court astrologers had begun marking on every official document like a countdown.

Three chairs behind the table. Two filled.

The third was Dain's.

He looked terrible. That was her first thought, and her second, and her third. Lieutenant Dain Corvane had entered her service at nineteen with cheekbones and confidence and an unshakeable conviction that the world was a list of tasks that could be completed correctly if one simply worked hard enough. He had been her shadow, her scribe, her second-in-command, the closest thing to a son her life had permitted. Now he sat in the field commander's chair with a captain's insignia pinned crookedly to his collar — newly promoted, she noted, and hated whoever had done the promoting for the cruelty of it — and his eyes had the look of a man who had slept badly for exactly as long as she had been gone.

"Captain Voss." The clerk of the tribunal, a dry stick named Halloway, struck a little bell. "You are brought before this tribunal to answer charges of—"

"I can read," Elara said. "You sent me the list. I have notes."

Halloway blinked. From the crown's chair, Chancellor Merrick — grey-faced, grey-voiced, a man assembled entirely from committee minutes — said, "Your notes will be of limited use, Captain. The evidence is not in dispute."

"Then let's skip to sentencing and save everyone the evening."

"The evidence is not in dispute," Merrick repeated, with the patience of a man who enjoyed repeating things, "because it was provided by your own officers. Your own company, Captain. Eleven witnesses."

Elara turned her head slowly and looked at Dain.

Dain looked at the table.

"You freed prisoners," Merrick continued. "You burned a supply wagon. You struck Sergeant Hale — who required nine stitches, incidentally, and has asked personally that we mention it."

"He was holding a child's arm behind her back at the time," Elara said. "I'd strike him again. I'd strike him more efficiently this time; I've had eleven days to think about my technique."

"Captain Voss—"

"And the supply wagon," she went on, because if she was going to hang she intended to be annoying about it, "was full of interrogation equipment, which is a polite word for what was in those crates, and I have seen what your interrogators do with it, so yes. Guilty. Enthusiastically. Do you want a confession? Here it is: I did it, I'd do it again, and the only thing I regret is that I didn't do it sooner."

The silence that followed had texture. Somewhere above, a raven muttered on the roof beam — the Hall of the Hunt kept ravens for messages, and this one sounded, to Elara's ear, distinctly judgmental. She sympathized.

Merrick folded his hands. "Master Corvane. As the ranking field officer present, you were closest to the accused. The tribunal would hear your account."

And there it was. The stone in her chest turned over.

Dain lifted his head. For a moment — one unguarded moment — his face was nineteen years old again, sitting across from her in a tavern in Millbrook, saying, *Captain, why do the hounds hate what they're bred to find?* and her saying, *Because they're given no choice, lad, same as anyone,* and both of them laughing at how much heavier the answer was than the question.

Then the moment closed, and he was a lieutenant wearing a stolen captain's rank, and he said:

"She planned it for three days. I found the roster changes myself. I reported them." His voice was steady. Too steady, the way ice is steady. "She disabled two guards. She took the prisoner Joubert, the mage Sefrin Vale, and the child Mirei of Thornwick. She gave them horses, supplies, and the border codes. I pursued. I lost them in the Whitefell passes during the storm."

"Lost them," Merrick repeated.

"The storm was severe."

"You had the hounds."

"The hounds refused the trail." Dain's jaw tightened. "All of them. Every animal in the company went silent and sat down in the snow and would not move another step. I have filed a report on the phenomenon. It is under review."

Elara kept her face still, but inside her, something laughed — a wild, exhausted, delighted thing. *Good dogs. Good, good dogs.* Whatever Casper had said to them in that pass — whatever bargain the girl had struck with animals who owed her nothing — it had held.

"Refused," Merrick said flatly.

"Animals cannot be court-martialed, Chancellor. Believe me, I checked."

Something very close to a smile flickered at the corner of Dain's mouth and died there, executed on arrival. Halloway's bell rang for order even though no one had spoken out of turn, because Halloway believed bells solved most problems.

Merrick leaned back. "The tribunal finds the evidence sufficient. What remains is disposition. Captain Voss, you served the Hunt for fourteen years. Your record prior to these events is exemplary. The crown is not, despite popular belief, in the business of wasting assets." He produced a document from a folder with the ceremony of a magician producing a dove. "The charges may be set aside. Entirely. In exchange for services rendered."

Elara said nothing. Beside Merrick, Odile Brannigan — who had not yet spoken, who had been studying her hands this whole time like they belonged to a stranger — finally raised her head, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious, and Elara understood suddenly that Odile had argued for her, had fought for her in rooms Elara would never see, and had lost.

"Services rendered," Elara repeated carefully.

"You know the girl," Merrick said. "Joubert. You traveled with her, fed her, defended her — the witnesses are unanimous that you developed a rapport. You know how she thinks. Where she'd go. Who she'd trust." He slid the document down the table. It stopped precisely halfway between them, equidistant, a border neither of them crossed. "Deliver her to the Hall within thirty days, and this tribunal will record your actions as a deep-cover operation. Full reinstatement. Full honors. Your company restored. Your name cleared."

The chamber was very quiet. Even the raven had stopped muttering.

"And the others?" Elara asked. "The mage. The child."

"The mage is a fugitive. The child is a ward of the crown." Merrick's tone did not change, which was itself an answer. "The girl is the prize. The king's own interest. You've heard the rumors, I assume."

Everyone had heard the rumors. That the king walked the menagerie at night. That the hunts had doubled, tripled, that the Gifted Hunt had stopped being a census and become a search — that somewhere in the capital, behind the throne, there was a list of qualities being checked off one by one, and every gifted soul dragged in chains to the Hall was being questioned less about their crimes than about their *voice*. Elara had assumed it was palace paranoia. Sitting here, watching Merrick's careful face, she revised the assumption downward into something colder.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

"Then the charges stand. Treason carries forfeiture of rank, property, and—" Merrick consulted nothing, needing no document for this part, "—in wartime, the traditional penalty."

"It isn't wartime."

"The proclamation says otherwise."

"The proclamation," Elara said, "was written by the same hand that wrote your offer, and I notice both documents treat human beings as inventory. Forgive me if I don't find either authoritative."

"Captain." Dain spoke, and his voice cracked on the word — just slightly, just enough. "May I?"

Merrick inclined his head with the magnanimity of a man delegating unpleasantness.

Dain rose. He came around the table, and the guards tensed, and Halloway's bell-hand hovered, but Dain only walked until he stood at the edge of Elara's reach — close enough to speak quietly, far enough to preserve the fiction of procedure — and looked down at her with those tired young eyes.

"Take it," he said, low. "Elara. Take the offer."

"Dain—"

"I'm serious. Listen to me." His voice dropped further, urgent now, the procedural mask slipping off entirely. "Merrick doesn't care whether you mean it. Deliver the girl or don't — walk out of here with your rank and your name and *then decide what you do with them.* You can't help anyone from a cell. You can't help anyone dead. Take the paper, take the thirty days, and be smart about the rest."

It was, Elara reflected, exactly the argument she herself would have made, four months ago, to a younger officer in exactly his position. It was sound strategy. It was the arithmetic she had lived by her whole career: survive the compromise today so you can fight the war tomorrow.

It had taken one badger, one storm, one mute child asleep against her shoulder in a wet wagon, and one girl who talked to goats to teach her that the arithmetic was a lie — that the compromises *were* the war, fought daily, won daily, by the people who kept making them.

"Dain," she said gently. "Look at me."

He looked. His eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"When you were nineteen, you asked me why the hounds hate what they're bred to find."

His breath caught. "That's not—"

"You asked me, and I told you it was because they're given no choice. And you laughed, and you said, *well, I'm glad somebody gets a choice.* Do you remember?"

"This isn't—"

"Do you remember?"

A long silence. Then, barely audible: "Yes."

"So do I." She rose, slowly, so the guards wouldn't lunge, and stood facing him at her full height, which had never once intimidated him and visibly terrified him now. "I have been given a choice, lieutenant. First one in fourteen years. I am not going to spit on it by dressing it up as obedience."

"Elara." His voice broke properly this time. "They'll kill you."

"Probably."

"*Elara.*"

"But here's what I need you to understand, and I need you to carry it out of this room, wherever you go, whatever you end up doing." She kept her voice low, for him alone, while the tribunal watched like men observing weather. "What's happening in this kingdom — the hunt, the cages, the questions about voices — it isn't justice and it isn't security. Something is wrong with the king. Deeply, badly wrong, in a way I have seen with my own eyes, and everything the Hunt is doing — everything *we* built, you and I, all those years of good work — is feeding it. We're not protecting the realm, Dain. We're harvesting it."

"You can't know that."

"I know it the way the hounds knew not to follow me into that pass." She smiled, and it cost her. "The animals know, love. They've known for three years. Maybe it's time the rest of us started listening to them."

She stepped past him, back toward the guards, and raised her voice to fill the chapel that no longer held a god.

"Chancellor. My answer."

Merrick waited, pen poised over the document that would record her fate in whichever direction it fell. Behind him, Odile Brannigan had closed her eyes. Halloway's bell hung silent. On the roof beam, the raven shifted its feet and made a sound that was almost, almost a word.

Elara Voss, traitor, arsonist, rescuer of children, drew a breath that tasted of cold stone and old incense, and prepared to lose everything except the parts that mattered.

"My answer," she said, "is no."

* * *

Later — much later, in the dark of her cell, with her confiscated boots gone and her future forfeit and the moon rising thin and silver over the training yards — Elara lay on the too-comfortable bed and allowed herself, at last, to think about the look on Dain's face when she said it.

Not the fury. Not the grief. Those she had expected, had budgeted for.

What she hadn't expected was the flicker that came *after* — half a heartbeat, quickly buried, but unmistakable to a woman who had taught him everything he knew about hiding his face.

Relief.

He had offered her the rope hoping she wouldn't take it. He had sat in that chair and lied about the roster and the pursuit and the stitches, smoothing her file where he could, and when she refused the bargain he had looked — for one unguarded instant — like a man setting down something heavy.

Which meant her protégé was not lost. Which meant her protégé was *waiting*, the way she had once waited, for someone to show him the door out of the arithmetic.

Elara stared at the barred window, at the fat white face of the moon climbing over the eastern wall — toward the eastern quarter, toward wherever a goat-girl and a mage and a small mute child were hiding, planning gods-knew-what — and she began, very quietly, to laugh.

Because she had just thrown away her rank, her name, and quite possibly her neck in front of the entire tribunal, and the plan she'd shouted down in that chapel — *deliver the girl* — was, she realized, exactly the plan she intended to pursue anyway.

Not to deliver Casper Joubert to the crown.

But to reach her. To warn her. To stand beside her, however many days remained on whatever clock was ticking behind that throne — because Dain had handed her the key without knowing it: thirty days of freedom, a reinstated name, and a captain's authority to move through the city unquestioned.

Thirty days. One moon shy of thirteen.

Elara rolled onto her side, winced at her bruised ribs, and grinned into the dark like the traitor she officially now was.

"Well," she murmured to the empty cell, to the raven stirring on some distant roof, to the girl she was absolutely going to strangle and then hug in roughly that order, "let's see what a disgraced captain can do with a stolen month."

Somewhere beyond the wall, faint and far away, a dog began to sing — and this time, Elara could have sworn it sounded like laughter.
