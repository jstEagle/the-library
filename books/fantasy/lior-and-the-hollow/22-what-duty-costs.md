## 22. What Duty Costs

The tribunal chamber smelled of tallow and old incense, the way Elara remembered it from her swearing-in eleven years ago. Back then she had stood on the same worn flagstones with her hand pressed to a silver badge and her heart full of certainty, and High Justiciar Corvane had smiled at her like a father welcoming a daughter into the family trade.

Now he sat behind the long oak bench with two lesser officers flanking him, and he did not smile at all.

"Captain Voss." He used her rank the way a man uses a knife he intends to clean afterward. "You are accused of treason in the highest degree: the freeing of four prisoners of the Gifted Hunt, the destruction of crown property, and the abandonment of your command."

"Abandonment," Elara repeated. Her wrists were bound in front of her — iron, not rope, because they suspected what she was now, or feared it, which amounted to the same thing. "I walked into this room myself, Justiciar. I surrendered at the cathedral gates with my sword unbelted and my hands open. You may call many things by their names today, but you will not call that abandonment."

Corvane's jaw tightened. Behind him, in the gallery reserved for officers of the Hunt, sat Dain.

Her protégé. Her boy, practically — she'd found him at sixteen, half-starved and furious, sharpening a kitchen knife against a gravestone because his village had burned a hedge-witch they later learned had only been curing fevers. She had taught him to ride, to read tracks, to tell the difference between guilt and fear in a prisoner's eyes. He sat very straight in his new captain's uniform — *her* uniform's cut, tailored for broader shoulders — and he did not look at her.

That was the worst part. Not the irons. The not-looking.

"The charges also include," Corvane continued, consulting a parchment with theatrical slowness, "the abduction of one Mirei Solenne, a gifted child remanded to the custody of the Hunt for questioning."

Something went cold and hard in Elara's chest. "Questioning." She let the word sit there, naked, until some of the younger officers in the gallery looked away. "Is that what we're calling it now? When I joined the Hunt, we questioned people, Justiciar. We asked them things. We listened to answers. Somewhere between then and now, we started asking with hot irons, and calling the screaming testimony."

"You will speak when addressed—"

"I was addressed." Her voice carried further than she intended; the chamber's acoustics were built for confessions, and hers was going to be a loud one. "I have been addressed every night since I left that convoy. I hear a child crying in her sleep and asking why the king's men hurt her, and I have no answer that doesn't begin with *because I obeyed*, and I am finished with sentences that begin that way."

She drew a breath. This was the part she had rehearsed on the walk from the cells, chained between two guards who couldn't meet her eyes either. Everyone in this building was avoiding eye contact today. It told her something hopeful about the state of their souls and something grim about the state of their spines.

"So here is my confession, since we've gathered for one." She turned to face the gallery, so the whole Hunt could watch her do it. "Elara Voss denounces the Gifted Hunt. All of it. Its methods, its orders, its justifications, and its master. I freed those prisoners because they should never have been taken. I burned the convoy records because they were lies written in a child's blood. And if the crown calls that treason, then the crown has forgotten what it is for, and someone ought to remind it."

The silence afterward had texture. She could feel it pressing against her ears.

Corvane rose slowly, and for a moment — just a moment — she thought she saw grief move behind his face before duty shut the door on it. "Elara Voss," he said formally, "you are stripped of rank, title, and protection. You are branded traitor to the realm. Sentence is death, to be carried out at dawn."

"Dawn," Elara said. "Generous of you. I'd have expected you to skip straight to the courtyard."

"The king," Corvane said carefully, "has expressed interest in your cooperation first. There are questions about your associates. Their location. Their plans." His voice dropped, and here, finally, was the man she'd known for eleven years, tired under all the ceremony. "Give them to us, Elara. Give us the girl, and I can make the rest quick. I can't save you. But I can save you from *this*."

*This* being the lower cells, where the interrogators worked, where Mirei had spent three days before Elara carried her out through the storm.

Elara looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed — short, unlovely, surprised out of her.

"Corvane. You sweet, stubborn old mule. You think I walked in here without knowing exactly how it ends?" She leaned forward as far as the irons allowed. "You want to know where my people are? Here's what I know. They're somewhere you'll never look, doing something you wouldn't believe, and they don't need me to protect them anymore. They need me to buy them time. So that's what I'm doing. That's the whole job now. Write it down."

* * *

They took her to a holding cell near the tribunal offices rather than the deep dungeons — a courtesy, or an oversight, or perhaps Corvane's quiet rebellion, though she suspected the third option least. The cell had a barred window high on the wall, and through it she could see a slice of courtyard and, beyond that, the grey shoulder of the menagerie tower.

She thought about Bracken up there. The old badger Casper spoke of like a grandfather, caged among peacocks and hunting cats for the crime of being loved by the wrong person. She thought about Casper hearing that news and what it would do to her face — that particular way she had of going still, like a held breath, before the stubbornness came flooding back in.

"Hold on, old man," she murmured to the window, to the tower, to nobody. "We're coming. It's a terrible plan and we're coming anyway."

Footsteps in the corridor. She knew the stride before she saw him — Dain had always walked like the floor owed him money.

He stopped outside the bars. Up close, in the cell's dim light, she could see what the tribunal gallery had hidden: he looked terrible. Unshaved. A tremor in his sword hand he kept curling into a fist to hide.

"You could have just run," he said.

"Yes."

"You could be over the border by now. Nobody would have blamed—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "I would have blamed you."

"There he is," Elara said softly. "There's my boy."

"Don't." The word cracked down the middle. "Don't call me that. You don't get to—" He pressed his forehead briefly against the cold iron, and when he pulled back his eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "They gave me your company, Captain. Your company. Do you know what they're having us do with it?"

"I can guess."

"They gave me a list of names yesterday. Gifted. To be collected before the renewal ceremony." His fist tightened until the knuckles went white. "Half of them are children, Elara. There's a girl on it, nine years old, gift of making bread rise. *Bread.* And I signed the collection order because my hand moved before my head caught up, because that's what you trained into me, and I've been sick ever since."

Elara crossed to the bars. "Then unsign it."

"It's not that simple."

"It never is. That's the whole trick of it, Dain. They make every cruelty a paperwork problem so decent people drown in procedure before they ever have to decide anything." She kept her voice low, gentle, the voice she'd used on him at sixteen when the fury got too big for his body. "But underneath all the paper, it's always just one question. What do you do *right now*, with the choice in front of you? Right now, the choice is a locked door and a guard rotation you helped write. Right now, the choice is me."

He stared at her. "You want me to break you out. In the middle of the capital. Three days before the thirteenth moon."

"I want nothing," Elara said. "I'm a traitor. Traitors want things; captains assess situations." A ghost of the old grin. "Though if a captain were to note that the east corridor guard changes at the fourth bell, and that the armory key hangs on a hook beside a very sleepy man named Oskar who owes me dice debts..."

Dain made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "You're impossible."

"I'm *yours*," she corrected. "Whatever else happens tomorrow, whatever they write in the histories — remember that you were mine first, and everything good in you, I got from watching you choose kindness when it cost you. So go choose something."

He stood there a moment longer. Then, without a word, he unpinned the small iron key from his belt — the holding-cell key, the one that opened doors like this one all over the palace — and set it on the floor inside the bars, within reach of her bound hands.

"For the record," he said hoarsely, "you broke out. Overpowered me. Very embarrassing. I'll be disciplined for weeks."

"For the record," Elara agreed, closing her fingers around the key, "you fought bravely."

* * *

The fourth bell found the east corridor empty, exactly as promised. Elara went through it barefoot, boots in hand, wearing the guard's cloak Dain had left folded on a supply shelf with a soldier's practical tenderness. Her side ached where a tribunal escort's elbow had found it during her arrest — bruised ribs, probably cracked, certainly complaining — and she catalogued the pain and filed it under *later*, the way she'd learned to do in twenty years of soldiering.

Getting out of the tribunal wing was the easy part. Getting across the palace grounds was harder. Getting to the kennels, where they kept the children pending transport to the interrogation blocks, was hardest of all, and she did it anyway, because she had made a promise in a storm three weeks ago with rain running down her face and a small shaking girl in her arms: *Nobody touches you again. Not while I'm breathing.*

Mirei was in the second kennel cell, sitting with her back to the door, arms around her knees. They had cut her hair short — punishment, or convenience, or spite — and she didn't turn around when the lock clicked. She had stopped turning around for locks. That was the kind of thing the Hunt did to children, Elara thought, and her hands shook with rage so pure it was almost peaceful.

"Mirei." She kept her voice at stable-pitch, the low even tone you used on frightened horses. "It's Elara. I'm going to open the door now. No rush. Whenever you're ready."

A long pause. Then the small figure unfolded, turned, and looked at her — and Elara saw that the girl's silence had changed. Before, it had been the silence of terror. Now there was something else in it, something watchful and deliberate, like a decision being maintained.

Mirei raised one hand and tapped her own chest twice, then pointed at Elara, then made a walking motion with two fingers.

*I'm coming with you,* in the sign language Casper had been teaching her. The animals' language, borrowed for human hands.

"That's right," Elara whispered, throat tight. "We're walking. Both of us."

The girl took her hand. Her grip was fierce.

They made it through the kennel yard, past the sleeping hounds — who, Elara noted with a strange pang, lifted their heads and watched them pass and did not bark, not one of them, ears forward and eyes bright, as if they'd been waiting for exactly this — and through the postern gate whose lock Dain had reported broken three days ago and then quietly failed to fix.

The city beyond was dressed for festival. Lanterns everywhere, garlands on the eaves, workmen hauling the great bells' scaffolding higher for the renewal ceremony. Three days. The whole capital glittering like a knife being polished.

Elara kept to the shadowed side of the streets, Mirei's hand in hers, counting turns toward the safehouse in the tanners' quarter. Two streets from safety, a patrol rounded the corner ahead — six soldiers, lantern-bright, laughing about something.

No time to retreat. Elara pushed Mirei into a doorway alcove, stepped out into the lantern light, and did the last thing a branded traitor should do: she stood up straight, threw back the hood, and let them see her face.

"Sergeant Halloway," she called, naming the tallest of them, a man she'd personally promoted two winters ago. "Report."

It worked for exactly four seconds — long enough for the squad to snap toward her on pure drilled reflex, long enough for Mirei to slip from the doorway and vanish down the alley mouth behind them, exactly as rehearsed — and then Halloway's eyes widened and his hand went to his sword and the night tore open into shouting.

Elara ran.

Not toward the alley — toward the *other* alley, drawing them, buying the girl her head start, ribs screaming with every stride. Boots behind her, more than six now, whistles shrilling. She vaulted a cart, took a corner too fast, felt something in her side shift from *complaining* to *formal protest*, and kept going because stopping meant dawn and dawn meant the block and the block meant Mirei growing up learning that promises were things adults died breaking.

A crossbow bolt buried itself in the wall beside her ear. Another took her through the meat of her left shoulder, spinning her halfway around, and she went down hard on cobblestones slick with evening wash-water.

They closed in. She counted five, six, seven shapes, swords drawn, and somewhere above and behind, faintly, she heard a sound that made her grin through the blood: a chorus of dogs, all across the district, barking in perfect unison — not alarm, but *direction*, a rolling wave of noise pulling every ear in the neighborhood away from her, toward the river, toward nothing.

Casper's sparrows. Casper's strays. The intelligence corps, earning their keep.

*Good girl,* Elara thought, at the sky, at the whole ridiculous kingdom. *Good girl.*

She got one knee under herself. Seven soldiers. Bad odds. But she'd taught Dain that odds were just arithmetic wearing armor, and arithmetic could be embarrassed.

Then the nearest soldier dropped his sword, yelped, and started swatting wildly at his own helmet — and Elara saw, darting out of the dark, a shape of striped fur and absolute moral conviction, a badger the size of a wine barrel with murder in its heart and, unmistakably, *kinship* in its gait.

"Oh," Elara breathed. "Oh, you beautiful creature."

What followed was less a battle than a public nuisance. The badger went through the soldiers like weather. Elara, one-armed and bleeding, tripped the sergeant into a rain barrel. Somewhere a cat yowled encouragement from a windowsill. By the time the district watch arrived, summoned by the same misleading chorus of dogs, they found a trampled patrol, a discarded crossbow, and no traitor at all — just a large badger waddling with tremendous dignity into the storm drains, and, if anyone had thought to look, a smear of blood leading the same direction.

* * *

The safehouse cellar smelled of tannin and lamp oil, and it had never smelled so much like home.

Sef was pacing — Sef was always pacing, a thin anxious metronome of a man — and Casper was on her feet before Elara cleared the bottom stair, catching her as her legs finally filed their formal resignation.

"You're shot," Casper said. It wasn't a question. Her hands were already moving, steady, tearing the cloak open around the wound, and her face had gone into that held-breath stillness Elara knew so well.

"Bolt. Left shoulder. Missed everything important, which tells you something about where the important things keep." Elara hissed as Casper probed the entry. "Mirei?"

"Here." The girl materialized out of the shadows by the far wall, whole, unmarked, and crossed the room in three strides to throw her arms around Elara's uninjured side with a force that nearly undid her more than the bolt had. Then Mirei pulled back, took Elara's hand, and signed, rapid and fierce: *You came back. You promised. You came back.*

"Told you," Elara managed. "While I'm breathing."

"She denounced the Hunt," Casper said to the room, and there was something in her voice Elara couldn't quite name — pride and fear braided together. "Word's already in the taverns. They're saying she stood up in front of the whole tribunal and called the king's justice a lie to its face."

"They're exaggerating," Elara said. "It was half the tribunal. The other half was asleep."

Sef had stopped pacing. He was looking at her with an expression she'd last seen on scholars describing miracles. "Do you understand what you've done? Every gifted person in this city has heard by morning. Every servant, every soldier with a conscience, every fence-sitter in the palace. You didn't escape tonight, Captain. You *recruited*."

"I lost my rank, my name, my pension, and a truly excellent sword," Elara said, settling onto the cot while Casper worked needle and thread through her shoulder with a gentleness that contradicted her scowl. "If I also accidentally started something, I'd like it noted that I was aiming for a quiet life."

"You were never once aiming for a quiet life," Casper said. "I've known you four months. You aim for trouble and apologize to whoever's standing behind it."

Their eyes met. Something passed between them that neither of them had words for yet — a vow-shaped thing, still warm from the forge — and Casper looked away first, ears pink, tying off the stitch with unnecessary firmness.

From the shadows near the stairs, the big badger chuffed once, deeply satisfied, and began eating the emergency sausage ration.

Elara lay back on the cot, shoulder burning, side aching, everything she'd built in twenty years of service burned to ash behind her in a single night of testimony. Stripped, branded, hunted. Dawn would bring the whole machinery of the crown down on the tanners' quarter; she'd bought hours, maybe a day.

And in the lamplight, Mirei sat cross-legged beside the cot, keeping watch with a wooden soldier's seriousness, and Casper checked her bandage knots twice, and a badger guarded the sausages, and Sef was already scratching out plans on scrap leather, muttering about bells and moons.

Everything lost, Elara thought, except the things that mattered. Which was, when she actually counted, most of it.

"Casper," she said quietly, as the lamp guttered low. "Three days. Tell me the plan's ready."

Casper looked at her across the little circle of lamplight, and the held-breath stillness broke into something bright and terrible and certain.

"No," she said. "But the animals are already moving, and they don't need it to be ready. Neither do we."

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, the great bells tolled midnight — and beneath their bronze voices, so faint it might have been imagination, Elara could have sworn she heard ten thousand throats answering.
