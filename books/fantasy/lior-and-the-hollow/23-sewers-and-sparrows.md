## 23. Sewers and Sparrows

The undercity smelled like a wet dog's regrets, which was fitting, because Casper was living with a wet dog's regrets.

His name was Turnip — a name he'd given himself, proudly, in the way of dogs who had once belonged to someone and now belonged to themselves. He was a brindled lurcher with one ear that stood up and one that had been chewed into a sort of leather flag, and he had appointed himself her guide, bodyguard, and conscience within an hour of her arrival in the tunnels beneath the capital.

*You smell like the river,* he told her on that first night, tail thumping against a broken barrel she'd claimed as a bed. *But you listen proper. Most folk up-top talk at you. You listen.*

"I'm told it's my best quality," Casper said.

*It's your only quality,* Turnip said cheerfully, and put his chin on her knee.

Three weeks underground, and this was her kingdom: a stretch of storm-drain wide enough to walk upright in, lit by candles Sef smuggled down in his coat pockets along with bread, ink, and an increasingly alarming quantity of paper. Elara had found them a bolt-hole through a wine merchant's cellar whose owner owed her a debt from a war nobody talked about anymore. Mirei slept in the alcove behind the false wall, curled around a hot brick, breathing the small even breaths of a child who had finally stopped flinching at footsteps.

And above them — up through forty feet of brick and mud and the accumulated weight of a city that believed itself safe — sat the palace, glowing like a lamp made of teeth.

Casper sat cross-legged in the candlelight with a charcoal stub and a sheet of paper, and did what she had spent her whole life being careful never to do.

She gathered an audience.

* * *

It started with the sparrows, because everything in the capital started with the sparrows. There were thousands of them. They lived in the eaves of the cathedral, the rafters of the granaries, the ornamental dead trees the palace gardener refused to admit were dead. They were nosy, chattery, easily bribed, and possessed of a collective memory that would have shamed most royal archivists.

Her first recruit was a hen-sparrow with a torn wing feather whom she'd found sheltering from rain in the drain mouth, looking profoundly sorry for herself.

"You want crumbs," Casper had said, "and I want to know how many guards stand on the east wall at moonrise."

*All of them are boring,* the sparrow said. *But yes to crumbs.*

Her name, as near as Casper could render it, was Tchik-Tchik-Feather-Dust, which Casper shortened to Dust for everyone's sanity. Within two days Dust had brought eleven friends. Within a week, thirty. Sparrows, it turned out, had a culture of gossip so dense and self-referential that they'd essentially invented rumor as an art form, and the only thing they loved more than gossip was being the first to deliver it.

By the end of the second week, Casper had a morning routine. She'd sit by the drain grate at first light with a sack of stolen bakery ends — Elara's contact at the bakery thought they were feeding pigeons for luck, which was technically true — and the reports would come down to her in a bright, overlapping tumble of wings and voices.

*The grey gate has four men, but one is always asleep after the mid-bell,* someone reported. *He dreams about a woman named Odile. It is very sad. We have listened many times.*

*There is a dog in the inner yard who hates the captain of the gate. He pees on the captain's boot every morning. The captain thinks it is a different dog each time. It is always Biscuit.*

*Biscuit says he will pee on any boot we require,* Dust added solemnly. *Biscuit is a patriot.*

Casper wrote it all down. Guards' rotations, shift changes, which bells meant what, which windows were barred and which merely looked barred, where the kitchen scraps went (the palace hounds, all nine of them, who had opinions), when the king's own guard doubled (never on the nights of the full moon, which had made Elara go quiet for a long moment when Casper told her).

"Of course," Elara had said, staring at the map. "The thing wearing him gets stronger on the moons. It doesn't need guarding then. *He* needs guarding." She'd tapped the paper. "Or it does."

The rats came next, and the rats were a different education entirely.

* * *

You could bribe a sparrow with bread. A rat required respect.

The rat who negotiated on behalf of the undercity's rodents arrived on the fourth evening, descending the drain wall with the unhurried confidence of a landlord. He was enormous, grey-shot, missing half a tail, and he sat before Casper and groomed his paws while she waited, because making people wait was clearly part of his method.

*They say you speak,* he said at last. His voice was dry as old paper. *They say you spoke to the wolves beyond the walls and they did not eat you. The wolves do not spare anything. Even us. Especially us.*

"They didn't eat me because we were talking," Casper said. "That's generally how it works."

*Mm.* He examined her with small black eyes that missed nothing, including, she suspected, the exact state of her boots. *I am called Old Ledger. My mother was called Old Ledger before me. It is not a name. It is a position.*

"Ledger," Casper repeated. "Because you keep accounts?"

*Because I remember debts.* He folded his paws. *Now. The birds tell me you pay in bread. Bread is nothing. Birds are fools with good eyesight. What will you pay my people?*

Casper had thought about this. She'd thought about it a lot, lying awake listening to Mirei breathe and the city groan overhead.

"Safety," she said. "The crown's hunters set poison and traps in the grain stores and the tenements. I know where the hunt-captains get their orders. If your people bring me word of trap-lines and poison drops before they're laid, I'll pass it to the people who can warn the streets. Fewer of yours die. In exchange, mine watch the palace."

Old Ledger was silent for so long that Casper began composing apologies. Then he said:

*You would spend your bread on rats.*

"On anyone keeping me alive."

*Hm.* He stood, shook himself, and regarded her with something that in a human face she'd have called grudging approval. *The birds will tell you what they see. That is worth little; birds see everything and understand nothing. My people will tell you what things mean. Where the guards are bored. Where they are afraid. Which doors open for which words. Fear is the true map of a building, girl-beast-speaker. Stone lies. Fear doesn't.*

He turned to go, then paused on the drain wall, half-tail curling.

*One more thing. The palace keeps cats. Eleven of them. They are arrogant, useless, and deeply bored. Bored cats make excellent allies if approached correctly. Approach them incorrectly and you will be a lesson taught to other spies.* A pause. *I can teach you the correct approach. It will cost you. Everything costs. I am Old Ledger.*

* * *

The map grew.

Sef pinned the sheets to the tunnel wall with wax, correcting her sketches with the fussy precision of a man who had once walked those corridors daily and still dreamed about them. Here was the king's wing, its approaches, the servants' stair the sparrows used as a highway. Here the menagerie — Bracken's prison — marked with a small, careful circle that Casper couldn't look at directly without her chest doing something structural.

Here, in red, the cathedral square, where in twenty days the thirteenth full moon would rise over a coronation renewal that would seal a monster into a man forever.

"The east postern," Sef said, tapping. "Two guards, and both take wine from the same steward, and the steward waters it. I know because I was the one watering it, for three years, on the master's orders. Everyone watches the gates. Nobody watches the door that's embarrassing to admit exists."

"And the ritual itself?"

"In the cathedral undercroft. Below the crypts." Sef's finger hovered there without touching. "Which we won't reach through any door, because the undercroft is warded six ways to Sunday and the wards know my handwriting." He smiled without humor. "We'll need another way in. Or no way in, and a miracle instead."

"We've got twenty days to find one," Casper said. "Talk to the rats. Rats know every hole in this city. It's practically their profession."

* * *

Elara came down most evenings, smelling of wine cellars and other people's secrets, and reviewed the intelligence with a soldier's economy. She'd taken to calling the network "your little army," and Casper had taken to pretending not to be pleased by it.

Tonight she crouched over the newest sheets, frowning.

"This is too much," she said. "Do you understand that? Three weeks ago we were five fugitives in a hole. Now you've got eyes on every wall of that palace, ears in the kitchens, and — what is this?" She pointed at a notation shaped like a paw print.

"Biscuit's patrol route. He's a palace dog. He hates the gate captain. He's agreed to engineer distractions on request."

"A dog. On the payroll."

"He asked for payment in sausages and dignity. I'm providing the sausages."

Elara rubbed her face, but Casper caught the smile underneath. "When Dain finds out he's being outmaneuvered by a dog named Biscuit, he's going to resign."

"Dain's going to find out a lot of things," Casper said quietly. "That's rather the point."

Because here was the truth she hadn't said aloud yet, the one that sat in her ribs like a swallowed stone: she knew what it was to be hunted for what you were. She'd spent her whole life in the Hollow learning that the safest thing was silence, invisibility, being no one. And now she was doing the opposite — gathering every small overlooked creature in this city and telling them, in effect: *you see everything, you matter, and together we are enormous.*

The hunted, becoming the hunters' mapmakers.

Dust arrived at dusk with the evening report, landing on Casper's shoulder with the proprietary air of a general reviewing troops. Behind her came a dozen others, settling along the drain ledge like a row of small judgmental gargoyles.

*Big news,* Dust announced. *Big big news. The tall sad mage-man's old tower — the white one with the angry windows — there were lights last night. All night. Green lights. And a carriage came with no markings, and men carried boxes in, and the boxes clinked.*

*Clinking boxes,* another sparrow confirmed gravely. *Many clinks.*

Elara went very still. "Green lights. At the tower." She looked at Sef, who had gone the color of old porridge.

"That's the master's working light," Sef said. "That's — he only uses that when he's preparing something large. Something binding-grade." He pressed his knuckles to his mouth. "He knows we're in the city. Or he suspects. And if he's preparing counter-wards for the unbinding—"

"Then we need to know what's in those boxes," Elara said. "Before he finishes whatever he's starting."

Everyone looked at Casper.

Casper looked at the sparrows, who looked back with the frank greed of creatures who had heard the word *boxes* and were already calculating crumb-per-risk ratios.

"Well," she said, reaching for the bread sack. "Dust? How do your people feel about a job inside a wizard's tower?"

Dust ruffled her feathers, and her answer — bright, reckless, and entirely too delighted — was already lifting toward the dark slot of the drain grate before Casper finished asking.
