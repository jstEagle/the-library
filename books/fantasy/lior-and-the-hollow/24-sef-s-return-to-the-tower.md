## 24. Sef's Return to the Tower

The mage tower of Vael Corin had not changed in the three years since Sef had fled it, which was the first insult. He had changed enormously — he had become a fugitive, a liar of international caliber, a man who could pick a lock with a fish bone and had done so twice — and his former home hadn't even bothered to repaint the door.

It was still that same green. Sage green, Master Ondrel had called it, though everyone else called it "the color of old peas." Sef stood in the shadow of the tanner's wall across the lane, hood up, hands in his sleeves, and felt something absurd rise in his chest. Homesickness, possibly. Or indigestion. The two had always been difficult to tell apart where this building was concerned.

"You're staring," said the rat on his shoulder.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring *nostalgically*. I can feel it coming off you like steam off a pudding."

Sef exhaled through his nose. Her name was Wick, and she was a sewer rat he'd recruited four days ago along with roughly two hundred of her cousins, and she had opinions the way rivers have water — constantly, and with great force.

"The wards haven't been renewed," he said. "Look at the lintel. The sigil-work is faded to gray. When I left, Ondrel re-inked them every new moon without fail."

"Maybe he's dead."

"Ondrel is not dead. Ondrel will outlive the mountains out of sheer stubbornness and spite for anyone who has ever been late to a lecture."

"Then maybe he's *sad*," Wick said, and there was something almost gentle under the sarcasm, which was the most unsettling thing she'd ever done. "You did run away from him."

Sef didn't answer that. He crossed the lane instead, quick and quiet, and pressed his palm flat against the tower's side door — the servants' entrance, the one the apprentices used when sneaking back from the taverns, the one whose lock he had picked so many times at nineteen that his fingers could have done it asleep, and indeed once had.

The lock opened like an old friend shaking hands.

Inside smelled exactly as it always had: chalk dust, lamp oil, dried yarrow, and underneath it all the faint mineral tang of concentrated magic, like the air after lightning. Sef stood in the dark stairwell and let it wash over him and hated how much it felt like being held.

"Second floor," he murmured. "Archive room. The ritual codex is kept in the restricted case behind the almanacs, because Ondrel believes no one has ever wanted to read an almanac."

"Has anyone?"

"No. That's the genius of it."

They went up together, Sef's boots silent on stone worn smooth by two centuries of student feet. The tower was quiet in the way towers are quiet at night — not empty, but *listening*. Somewhere above, a familiar creak: the third-floor landing board that Ondrel had never fixed because, he claimed, it told him who was wandering after hours, and a master should be allowed his small tyrannies.

The archive room door was unlocked.

That stopped Sef cold, hand on the handle. Three years ago it would have taken him an hour and a stolen key to get in here. Now it swung open at a touch, and inside, the lamps were even lit — one lamp, low, on the reading table, beside a pot of tea.

Still steaming.

"Oh no," said Wick.

"Yes," said Sef.

"He knows you're here."

"He knew before I crossed the lane," Sef said grimly. "He probably knew before I crossed the *city*. There is a reason I ran rather than resigned."

At the far end of the archive, in the high-backed chair by the window, someone cleared their throat.

Master Ondrel had grown smaller. That was the second shock. In Sef's memory the man had been a monolith — tall, dry, angular as a folded letter, with eyebrows that could end arguments. The man in the chair was shrunken into his robes, white-haired now, hands knotted around a cup of tea like the cup might make a break for freedom. But the eyes were the same: pale, precise, and currently fixed on Sef with an expression that managed to convey *I am disappointed in you*, *I expected this*, and *you've lost weight, have some bread*, all at once.

"Wick," Sef whispered, "go."

"I'm not leaving you alone with—"

"*Go.* Tell Casper: the binding was meant to be temporary. If I don't come out, tell her the rest doesn't matter anyway."

Wick hesitated — rats are loyal creatures, whatever the poets say about them — then flowed down his arm and vanished under the door. Sef heard her claws tick-tick-tick away down the corridor, and then it was just him and the lamp and the old man.

"Master Ondrel," Sef said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "You're looking well."

"I am dying, actually," Ondrel said, in the tone other men used for discussing weather. "The heart. Slowly. I have perhaps a year, and I intend to spend it being insufferable, so do sit down and stop looming by the door like a debt collector."

Sef sat. It seemed unwise not to.

"You knew I was coming."

"I have known you were coming for eleven days." Ondrel sipped his tea. "Since the bells were tampered with. Only three people alive understand the harmonic suppression lattice well enough to sabotage it, and two of them are me and a corpse. So. Either you had returned, or the corpse had gotten ambitious." He set down the cup. "Hello, Sefren."

"Hello, Master."

"You look terrible."

"Thank you. I've been living in sewers."

"That explains the smell. I assumed it was a philosophical choice."

And there it was — the terrible, treacherous warmth, rising in Sef's chest despite everything. Three years of resentment, and the old man dismantled it in four sentences with tea and insults. It wasn't fair. Nothing about Ondrel had ever been fair; that was practically his school of magic.

"I came for the codex," Sef said. "The original binding ritual. Aldric's binding. I'm going to undo it."

Something moved behind Ondrel's pale eyes — not surprise. Grief, maybe. Or relief wearing grief's coat.

"I know," Ondrel said. "That's why I left the door unlocked."

* * *

The restricted case stood open when they reached it, its warded glass swung wide, and the codex lay on top of the almanacs like a patient animal. Sef stared at it, then at his master.

"For three years," Sef said slowly, "I believed if I ever came back here, you'd turn me over to the Hunt before I got both feet in the door."

"I considered it. Briefly. Around year two, when you blew up a granary in Dossel Vale."

"That was an accident."

"You blew up a granary in Dossel Vale *on purpose*, Sefren, I read the reports, I have friends everywhere, it's my only hobby." Ondrel lowered himself into the chair by the case with a grunt. "But no. I didn't turn you in, because turning you in would have been admitting what we did, and I find that at my age I would rather die than lie, which is inconvenient, because lying is most of what I've done lately."

Sef pulled the codex toward him. It was heavier than he remembered — or he was weaker than he remembered, which was likelier. He opened it to the marked page, and there it was: the binding. *The Rite of the Quieted Crown.* He'd copied it out himself, three years ago, ink-stained and sweating, while the king screamed in the tower above and the court mage Merrow shouted for speed.

He'd never read past the operative verses. You didn't, in those days. You did what you were told and you didn't ask what the words *meant*, because asking was how apprentices disappeared.

Now he read.

His stomach dropped somewhere around his knees.

"This says—" He stopped. Started again. "This says the binding decays. By design. Look — 'the seal shall hold seven years and no more, for a voice long silenced grows desperate, and desperation finds doors.'" He looked up. "It was *temporary*. From the beginning. Merrow built it to fail."

"Merrow built it to fail *slowly*," Ondrel corrected. "There is a difference, and the difference was supposed to matter. Sit. Listen. You've earned the whole story, and I'm too tired to ration it anymore."

Sef sat. Outside the window, the moon was climbing — fat, silver, twelve days from full. Twelve days from the thirteenth moon. He found himself counting without meaning to, the way you count a wound's pulse.

"When the thing came out of the deep forest and took the king," Ondrel said, "we had hours. Perhaps less. Merrow understood it better than any of us — she'd studied the old crown-lore, the entities that ride rulers. She knew we couldn't kill it without killing Aldric. She knew we couldn't drive it out; it had rooted too deep, woven itself into his voice, his mind, his *name*. So she did the only thing left. She buried his true voice beneath its resonance. Caged the man inside his own mouth, so the thing would have nothing to steer."

"And the cage was temporary."

"Everything is temporary. That is the first lesson of magic and the last comfort of the dying." Ondrel's knotted hands tightened around each other. "Seven years, the rite promised. Time enough to find a true cure. Time enough for the entity to starve, weaken, grow careless. Merrow planned to unbind him herself, at leisure, with the full resources of the tower. And then—"

"Then she died."

"Then she died," Ondrel agreed quietly, "in a manner the official record calls regrettable and I call suspicious, and the tower fell into the hands of men who read the first page of her notes and not the rest. They saw a king who was calm, obedient, manageable. They decided the binding was not a bridge but a destination." He looked up, and for a moment the old monolith flickered through the shrunken frame, all the old iron showing. "They have spent three years strengthening it, Sefren. Layering it. And every layer they add makes the eventual unbinding harder — and makes the thing beneath more *hungry*. A caged voice doesn't fade. It sharpens. It learns to beg in ways that carry."

Sef thought of the mare in the royal stables, of Casper sitting in the straw with her hand on the old horse's neck, listening to a laugh three years dead. He thought of the thirteenth moon, and Elara walking into a cathedral to buy minutes with her own body.

"So it can still be undone," he said. "Even with the extra layers."

"It can be undone by exactly one method. Merrow wrote it in the margin — the part nobody read." Ondrel tapped the codex with one crooked finger, and Sef looked down at the cramped annotation in faded brown ink:

*A binding of the voice answers only to the voice. No rite, no circle, no borrowed power can loose it. It must be unwoven by speech the bound heart trusts — freely given, truly known. Find the voice he loved, or all of this was for nothing.*

"I found it," Sef said. "Or — Casper found it. The mare. She remembers his real laugh. Casper learned his name-song from her."

For the first time that night, Ondrel smiled. It transformed his face entirely — made him look, briefly, like the man who had once smuggled honey cakes to homesick first-years and denied it under oath.

"A horse," he said. "Of course. Of *course* it was a horse. Kings trust crowns and courtiers least of all. An animal asks nothing and lies never." He shook his head. "Your girl — the beast-speaker. Voss's fugitive. The one the whole Hunt is hunting."

"She's not mine," Sef said, and then heard how it sounded, and didn't correct it.

"Mm," said Ondrel, in the tone of a man filing something away forever.

"Master." Sef leaned forward. "The layers. The ones the tower added. If Casper speaks the name-song on the thirteenth moon, will they hold her back? Will they smother the unbinding?"

"They will try." Ondrel rose, joints crackling like green firewood, and shuffled to the window. Below, the city sprawled under the pre-moon light, and somewhere in its heart the cathedral bells hung silent — sabotaged, thanks to Sef, thanks to a rat named Wick and two hundred cousins. "Which brings us to the narrow part of the road, boy, so attend me. The binding's anchor isn't in the king. It never was. Merrow anchored it in the *bells* — the great harmonic lattice of the capital, the same lattice your friends damaged. Every bell in Vael Corin is a knot in the cage. As long as the lattice stands, the binding holds against anything short of a god."

"But we broke the bells."

"You *dented* the bells." Ondrel turned, and his eyes were grim. "The tower has already begun repairs. I signed the work orders myself, because the alternative was letting Dain's people sign them, and I am still, God help me, faster. You have bought days, not victory. The lattice will be whole again well before the thirteenth moon unless—" he paused, and the pause had weight, "—unless someone completes what you started. Properly. From inside the belfry of the cathedral itself, on the night, with the tower's own counter-harmonies. Which requires the tower's counter-harmonies. Which are written down in exactly one place."

Sef felt the plan contract around him like a fist closing. "Where."

Ondrel walked to his writing desk, drew out a slim folio bound in gray leather, and held it out.

"My life's correction," he said. "Every flaw in Merrow's lattice, every seam, every note where the cage can be pried. I've spent three years pretending to serve them while writing the instruction manual for their defeat. It's why I haven't slept. It's also, incidentally, why my heart is failing, but I choose to blame the tea."

Sef took the folio. It was lighter than the codex and weighed ten times as much.

"Why?" he asked. It came out rougher than he intended. "Why help us? You stayed. You served them. You let me believe—"

"You ran, and I let you believe running was betrayal, yes." Ondrel settled back into his chair, suddenly looking every hour of his age. "Because someone had to stay inside and keep the cage honest, and because I am a coward in the specific way clever old men are cowards: I would risk anything except being disliked. You were braver at nineteen than I have ever been, Sefren. Don't argue. It's unbecoming and you'll lose."

Sef argued anyway, silently, and lost.

"There's one more thing," Ondrel said. "The narrow part. Listen carefully, because I will only say it once, and then I will pretend I never said it, and so will you." He waited until Sef nodded. "A binding of the voice answers only to the voice the bound heart trusts. Trust, Sefren. Not love. Not need. *Trust.* The mare gives you the song — good, vital, necessary. But a song carried by a stranger is just noise. On the thirteenth moon, in that square, with that thing listening — the king must hear the name-song from a voice he *knows*. A voice with no mask on it. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Sef did, and the understanding was cold water down his spine.

Casper couldn't hide. Couldn't stand shielded in the crowd, couldn't borrow Elara's cover or Sef's misdirection. To unbind the king, she would have to stand in front of him — in front of the thing wearing him — open, visible, and speak in her own true voice, with every layer of protection stripped away, while the entire apparatus of the Hunt watched.

"It's a trap-shaped plan," Sef said hoarsely.

"All good plans are trap-shaped. Traps are simply plans the enemy hasn't read yet." Ondrel waved a dismissive hand. "Make sure your girl reads it first. That's the difference."

Above them, faint through the stone, the third-floor landing board creaked.

Ondrel's face went still. "Boots," he said softly. "Two sets. Heavy. Armored." He looked at Sef, and there was no time left for anything, and somehow he still found room for one raised eyebrow. "Back stairs, past the stillroom, out through the herb garden — the gate sticks, lift and push. Take the folio. Leave the guilt; it's heavy and you'll need your hands."

"Master—"

"If you say thank you I will call the guards myself." But his voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and Sef — who had run from this man once without looking back — crossed the room and hugged him, brief and fierce, feeling how small the old shoulders had become.

"Year one of seven," Ondrel muttered into his ear. "We're late. Go be loud somewhere useful."

Sef went. Down the back stairs, through the stillroom thick with the smell of valerian, out into the herb garden where the gate stuck exactly as advertised. Behind him, through the walls, he heard Ondrel's voice rise, theatrical and carrying, addressing the guards with magnificent irritation about students, trespassers, and the general decline of youth.

Sef ran.

* * *

Wick found him an hour later, in the drainage culvert east of the tanners' quarter, where the resistance's map said safe and the smell said otherwise. She climbed his sleeve and sat on his shoulder, breathing hard in the way rats breathe hard, which is to say dramatically.

"You lived," she observed.

"Barely. Did you reach Casper?"

"Reached her sparrow. Sparrow reached her. Message delivered." Wick shifted her weight. "She sent one back."

Sef closed his eyes. "Let me guess. 'Tell Sef I'm not hiding behind anyone.'"

"Worse," said Wick. "She said: 'Good. Then he can stand next to me.'"

Sef laughed — a cracked, exhausted, helpless sound that echoed off the culvert stones — and clutched the gray folio to his chest, and above the city the moon climbed steadily toward full, twelve days out, patient as a held breath.
