## 25. A Voice the King Trusts

The sparrows found me before dawn.

They came in ones and twos through the broken slat of the tannery loft — a gray one first, then three brown ones, then a scruffy fellow missing half his tail who I'd privately named Lieutenant because he always arrived last and acted like it had been his idea. They settled on the beam above my head in a loose, shivering row, feathers puffed against the river damp, and they brought me the city the way other people brought news: in crumbs.

*A cart of oats going up Palace Hill. A dog kicked by a guard at the east gate — angry, angry, remembers faces. The stable cats are fat this winter. The horses are restless.*

That last one made me sit up on my bedroll.

*Restless how?*

The gray cocked her head, and I felt the answer arrive not as words but as weather: a picture of the great stable doors standing open at odd hours, grooms hurrying, and beneath it all a low, sour hum like a hive gone wrong. *The old mare won't eat. The young ones stand near her wall all night and don't sleep. The whole barn is holding its breath.*

I was pulling my boots on before the thought finished forming.

Sef had come back from the mage tower two nights ago looking like a man who'd aged a decade in an afternoon and couldn't decide whether to be terrified or relieved about it. He'd spread his stolen pages across the floor of our hideout — the original binding ritual, ink faded to the color of weak tea — and read us the sentence that had rearranged everything:

*The binding holds so long as the bound does not hear his own name spoken truly, by a voice he trusts.*

"Which is useless," Sef had said, rubbing his eyes, "because Aldric trusted no one. Not his councilors. Not his generals. Not his wife, gods rest her, and certainly not me or my master. We spent three years trying to find the thread and there wasn't one. The man was a fortress."

"He loved something," Elara had said flatly. She'd been cleaning her sword, which she did when she was thinking, which meant the blade had never been cleaner in its life. "Everyone loves something."

"A king?" Sef had laughed, short and bitter. "Kings love power and lineage and—"

"The mare," I'd said.

And the room had gone quiet, because once I said it, everyone could see it. The stable records Sef had copied listed a bay mare, thirty years old, bought as a foal the same year Aldric was crowned. *Emberlight.* She'd carried him through two wars and a famine winter. The records showed he'd visited her every week for eleven years — and then stopped, abruptly, three years ago. Three years ago, when the thing in the crown had finished settling into him like frost into mortar.

The king hadn't stopped loving her. The king hadn't been allowed near anything that remembered what he used to sound like.

* * *

Getting into the palace stables was, absurdly, easier than getting out of the tannery without waking Mirei.

Elara wanted to come. Of course she did; she'd argued for it with the particular stubbornness of a woman who had already lost one career and saw no reason to lose a friend on top of it. But the guards knew her face better than they knew their own mothers', and the plan required someone small, quiet, and officially nonexistent. That left me, which suited me fine, though I'd have preferred not to admit how much I liked being needed.

"You come back," Elara said, gripping my shoulder at the drain mouth that the rats swore led under the outer wall. Her hand was heavier than it looked. "Casper. You come back, or I will come find you, and I will be extremely embarrassing about it in front of the guards."

"That's a threat?"

"That's a promise. I'll tell them you're my sweetheart. I'll do voices."

"I'm going now," I said, and dropped into the dark before she could demonstrate.

The rats had undersold the smell and oversold the walking room, but an hour later I came up through a coal chute into the sweet, deep breath of the stable yard — hay and leather and horse and, underneath it, that wrongness the sparrows had described. It tasted like a struck bell that wouldn't stop ringing. Like the crown-thing's attention, stretched thin over the whole hill, listening even here.

I kept to the shadows between wagons and let the sparrows walk me in. The stable itself was enormous — forty stalls, a riding hall attached, rafters full of pigeons who gave me lazy, incurious glances. Court animals. They were fed and brushed and bored half to death, and their minds moved slow and round like fat bees. None of them paid me any mind except a dapple gelding who watched me pass with wide, intelligent eyes and thought, very clearly, *You smell like the valley. Why do you smell like the valley,* and then decided it wasn't his problem.

Emberlight was in the last stall, the big corner one with a window facing east.

She was lying down.

Horses don't lie down much, and old horses lie down meaningfully. She was folded onto the straw like a ship run gently aground, her legs tucked under her, her head low. Thirty years sat on her the way winter sits on a field. Her coat had gone dull along the neck, and her breathing had a rattle in it that I recognized from Bracken's last bad winter, back before everything — the sound of a body quietly negotiating its exit terms.

But her ears came up when I stopped at her door.

Her ears came up, and her head lifted, and she looked at me with one large brown eye, and what came off her was not fear, not alarm, but a kind of stunned, trembling hope that hit me harder than any scream would have.

*You,* she said. Or not said — poured. Old horses speak slowly, deeply, like wells filling. *You hear. After all these years, someone comes who hears.*

"I hear," I whispered, and crouched at the stall door. Up close I could see how thin she'd gotten, the ribs showing under the dull coat. "I'm sorry it took so long. I didn't know anyone was waiting."

*Everyone waits,* Emberlight said. *That is most of what horses do. But I waited for a voice, little one. And yours is not the voice I waited for, and yet—* she breathed out through her nose, a long shudder *—and yet it is close to it. Close enough that my heart is behaving foolishly. Come in. My legs are done pretending, but my manners are not.*

I slipped the latch and went in and sat down in the straw beside her enormous head, and she sighed and laid her chin across my knees, sixty stone of dying trust, and I put my hands on her cheek and tried not to cry, because I had a job to do, and because crying would make it true that I was about to ask a dying animal to give me the last thing she had left.

*You are sad,* she observed. *Sad, and frightened, and carrying something heavy wrapped in cloth inside your chest. You are also rude, arriving at midnight and making an old mare feel things.*

"I've been told I'm rude."

*By whom?*

"A badger. He's — he's in the menagerie. He's why I'm here."

Something moved behind her eye at that. *The menagerie,* she said slowly. *There is a badger there who talks in his sleep. The night creatures say he calls a name. Casper. Casper. Like a prayer said wrong.* Her nostrils flared against my knee. *Is that your name?*

"Yes."

*Then he waits for you too,* she said simply. *Go to him after. Promise me. Old animals know how these stories go, and we hate unfinished endings worse than pain.*

"I promise."

*Good.* She shifted, wincing, resettling her weight. *Now. You did not climb a coal chute at midnight to hold an old horse's head. Ask.*

So I asked. I told her — carefully, watching her face the way you watch a frozen pond — about the king. About the thing wearing him. About the binding, and the riddle, and the voice he trusted. I told her we believed such a voice existed, and that we thought it might be hers, and that if it was, I needed something from her that I had no right to ask.

Emberlight was quiet for a long time. Outside, a guard coughed; somewhere in the riding hall a pigeon muttered in its sleep. Her breathing rattled on, patient as tide.

*They told him I died,* she said at last.

My stomach dropped. "What?"

*Three winters ago. The new groom — the quiet one with the ring under his glove — came to my stall and spoke a word over me, a sleeping word, and I dreamed a long gray dream, and when I woke the whole stable believed I was dead. There was a false grave dug behind the far paddock. I heard them fill it. The king came — no. Something wearing the king came. It stood at my empty stall and it laughed, and the laugh was wrong, wrong the way a bell is wrong when the clapper is cracked, and every horse in this barn pretended to be asleep.* Her eye had gone very still. *It did not want him near me. Because I remember.*

"Remember what?" I whispered, though I knew. My heart was pounding so hard I was sure she could feel it through her jaw where it rested on my legs.

*His laugh,* she said. *His real laugh. Before the crown, when he was only a prince with mud on his boots who snuck out to ride me bareback through the barley fields and got thrashed for it and did it again the next week. He would laugh with his whole body, that boy. Horses know real laughter — it loosens the rider's seat, you understand, it makes the balance honest. His laughter was the most honest thing about him. And then one day he stopped coming, and the thing that wore his face came instead, and it sat on my back like a sack of wet sand, and it smelled of cold iron and old hunger, and I bit it.* She paused. *I regret nothing.*

Despite everything, I laughed — a small, wet, startled sound — and her ear flicked toward me with satisfaction.

*Good,* she said. *Laugh. That is the first lesson, actually. Are you ready to learn?*

"Learn what?"

*His name-song. Every creature has one. Yours is green and quick, like water over stones, with a crooked note in it that I suspect you got from the badger. The king's — the true king's — I have carried for thirty years, because a name-song is not a secret you keep, it is a song you keep, and songs are meant to be sung. It was only ever waiting for someone who could carry it to him.* Her breath caught, steadied. *But hear me, girl. This will cost me. A name-song lives in the deepest stall of the heart, and opening that door at my age — well. Some things leave and do not come back.*

I pulled my hands off her. "No. No, I can't — there has to be another way, I'll find—"

*Little one.* Gentle, and amused, and immovable. *Look at me. Look at these legs. I am leaving regardless. The only question is whether I leave as a horse who was comfortable, or a horse whose song gets home.* She nudged my knee, nearly toppling me. *Besides. You think I have lain here three winters listening to that cracked-bell laugh walk my halls and not prayed daily for exactly this? For a listener? The gods are slow, but they are not deaf.*

I was crying now, properly, the ugly kind, and she let me for exactly as long as it took me to run out of breath, and then she said, *Done? Good. Pay attention, because I am only singing this once, and I refuse to sing it twice for a girl who blubbers through the good part.*

* * *

A name-song is not words.

I need you to understand that, because everything I write down afterward will be a lie of sorts — a net thrown over water. What Emberlight gave me was more like the shape heat leaves in a room after a fire. It began in her chest, below the rattle, a vibration I felt through my knees before I heard anything, and then it rose through her throat and became — not melody, not quite. Becoming.

It was barley fields in late summer. It was a boy's bare feet on a horse's warm back. It was the specific quality of light through a stable door at dawn, and the taste of stolen apples, and the feeling of being young and certain that thrashings were worth it. It was loyalty like bedrock. It was grief held so long it had become part of the body's architecture. And underneath all of it, threaded through it like gold wire through dark cloth, was the laugh — the real one, whole-bodied and unguarded, the laugh of a person who had never yet learned what it cost to trust.

And it had a name in it. Not *Aldric*, the way the criers said it, the way the coins said it. The name-song held the name the way a riverbed holds a river — his mother's name for him, I think, or the name he'd been before he was ever a title. A small, bright, human sound. I caught it the way you catch a falling cup: without thinking, entirely, forever.

When she finished, the stable was utterly silent. Even the pigeons had stopped muttering. In the stalls around us, I realized, horses were standing with their heads over their doors, ears forward, faces soft. They had all heard. Half of them had probably known him themselves.

*There,* Emberlight said, and her voice was thinner now, I noticed, like a candle burned down to the last inch. *It is yours. Carry it carefully. Do not sing it in the rain. Do not sing it angry. And above all—* her eye found mine, fierce and fading *—do not sing it until the moment is right, because a name-song sung truly cannot be unsung, and that thing in the crown must hear it exactly once, at the exact moment it can least bear to.*

"I will. I swear it."

*Mm. Swearing. You valley folk and your swearing.* But her chin pressed a little heavier into my lap, and her breath came slower. *Girl. Casper-with-the-green-quick-song. Will you do an old mare one last kindness?*

"Anything."

*The window. Open it wider. I should like to see the sky while I finish my nap, and I should like it very much if you stayed until I was done napping, and I should like it most of all if you talked to me while I drift. Tell me about the valley. Tell me about the badger. Tell me anything true. Truths are the only fodder worth chewing at the end.*

So I opened the window, and the cold clean air of almost-dawn came in, and I sat back down in the straw and told her about the Hollow — about goats named after saints and a lame badger who cheated at riddles and a valley so magic-dead that even my secrets slept safe there. I told her about Elara doing voices. About Mirei's first sentence. About the sparrows and Lieutenant's delusions of command. She chuckled — an actual chuckle, a deep rumble that moved us both — and her breathing slowed, and slowed, and somewhere in the middle of a story about Bracken stealing an entire wheel of cheese through sheer insolence, the rattle in her chest went quiet, and the great warm weight of her head on my knees grew still.

I sat with her until the sun came over the hills and lit the straw gold, because I had promised.

* * *

I climbed back out through the coal chute with the name-song folded inside me like a coal I was afraid to touch, and I made it four steps into the tannery loft before Elara was on her feet with a knife drawn and relief warring openly with fury on her face.

"Twelve hours," she said. "You said the stables. The stables are twenty minutes away. I had a plan. The plan involved bribing a laundry cart."

"There was a complication."

"Casper."

"She was dying." My voice came out rougher than I expected. "She'd been shut in a lie for three years, Elara. They buried an empty grave for her. And she gave me—" I pressed my hand flat against my sternum, where the song sat humming. "She gave me the key. The actual key. And then she made me stay and talk to her while she went, because she didn't want to nap alone, and I couldn't—"

Elara crossed the loft in three strides and pulled me in, knife and all, which is a very specific way to be hugged. Over her shoulder I saw Sef sitting up among his papers, awake, watching me with an expression I'd learned to read as *the plan just changed, doesn't it.*

"It worked?" he asked quietly.

"It worked. I have it. All of it." I stepped back and wiped my face with my sleeve, and something else surfaced — something Emberlight had planted in me alongside the song, a seed of a thought that had been growing the whole walk home. "But Sef. The binding. You said it needs the voice the king trusts, sung truly, once, at the moment it can least bear it."

"Yes. Ideally during the ritual, when the crown-thing is at its most exposed — the coronation renewal, the thirteenth moon. Which means getting into the cathedral square, past the bells, past the entire royal guard, in front of the whole court." He rubbed his eyes. "Which is impossible."

"Mm," I said. "About that."

Because Emberlight had told me one more thing, in the last hour, in the drifting half-voice of a horse letting go: that the king — the true king, the boy in the song — had never stopped visiting her either. That some nights, when the crown-thing slept deepest, something in him fought to the surface of his own eyes, and his feet carried him down to the stables on their own, and he stood at her stall in the dark, silent, unable to speak, unable to leave. That the grooms called him the ghost of Palace Hill and had orders to report it, and never did, because the ghost looked so lost.

"He still comes to her stall," I said. "Empty as it is now. Nearly every night. There's something left in him that knows the way."

The loft went very still. Outside, the city bells rang the hour, and even they sounded, to my newly tuned ears, like they were holding their breath.

"So we don't break into the cathedral," I said slowly, the plan assembling itself out of grief and audacity the way plans do. "We let the king come to *us*. We use her stall. We use the song. And on the thirteenth moon, when the thing in him is busiest sealing its own crown—"

"—we make him remember his own name," Elara finished.

From the corner, where she'd been awake the whole time pretending not to be, Mirei sat up in her blankets, looked at the three of us, and signed one small, deliberate word into the lamplight:

*Soon?*

"Soon," I promised her.

And far across the sleeping city, in a stable on Palace Hill, I knew — the way you know a song you can't unsing — that a hollow-eyed figure was standing at an empty stall even now, listening for a voice that had finally, finally stopped waiting.
