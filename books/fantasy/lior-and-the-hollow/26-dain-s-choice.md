## 26. Dain's Choice

The order came at dawn, written in the king's own hand, which was how Dain knew it had not come from the king at all.

He knew this because he had spent eleven years learning to read Captain Voss's face, and before that, six years learning to read the old captain's, and before that, a childhood spent reading the moods of his father's plow horses — and in all that time he had learned one thing above all others: cruelty writes itself differently than duty. Duty is cramped and apologetic. It uses small words and leaves room for mercy in the margins. This order filled the page edge to edge, its letters tall and gleaming like wet teeth, and it used the word *cleansing* twice.

*All Gifted taken in the western sweeps are to be cleansed by week's end,* it read. *No exceptions for age. The purity of the realm admits no half-measures.*

Dain stood in the command tent holding the paper while the camp woke up around him — the clink of harness, the grumble of soldiers who hadn't yet found their coffee, someone's hound dreaming loudly about rabbits. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of men doing a job. He had told himself for three years that it was a job, that the Hunt was a hard necessity in a kingdom gone strange, that Elara Voss had been weak when she ran, that loyalty meant carrying out orders you didn't like because the alternative was chaos.

He had gotten very good at telling himself things.

"Sir?" said Corporal Hesk, poking his head in. He was twenty-two, freckled, and had joined the Hunt because his mother said it would give him structure. "The, ah. The prisoners. The wagon master wants to know if we're moving them to the yard this morning or—"

"The yard," Dain said, and heard his own voice go flat as slate.

Hesk hesitated. He'd been there at the western sweeps. He'd seen what came out of the wagons afterward, on the nights the interrogators worked late. "Sir, are they — I mean, the little one, the girl with the sparrows, she can't be more than—"

"I know how old she is, Corporal."

"Yes sir." Hesk didn't leave. He stood there working his jaw like a man chewing a sentence he couldn't quite swallow. Finally he said, "My ma always said structure was important. But she also said a man who kicks dogs ends up alone, and I've been thinking maybe those two sayings don't fit together proper."

"That's a lot of thinking before coffee, Hesk."

"Yes sir." He left.

Dain looked down at the order again. Then he folded it, put it in his breast pocket over his heart, where he kept the things he intended to be ashamed of later, and went to see the prisoners.

* * *

They were held in a stockade of new-cut pine, four children and an old woman, and the smell of the place hit him before the sight did — straw gone sour, unwashed bodies, fear, which has a smell all its own, sharp as vinegar. The guards outside snapped to attention. Dain waved them off and ducked through the gate.

The old woman sat nearest the entrance, knitting. She had no yarn. Her hands simply moved, looping nothing, pulling invisible stitches tight, and her eyes were fixed on the middle distance with the calm of someone who had already done all her crying years ago and found the well dry.

"You're the one they call the hound-master," she said without looking up.

"They call me lieutenant."

"Mm. Same thing. You point, the dogs bite." Her needles clicked. "Which are you today? Pointer or biter?"

Dain didn't answer. He walked the line of the stockade instead, past the children. A boy of perhaps ten with a bandaged hand — the interrogators had burned him, testing whether pain made his gift flare. A pair of sisters, seven and nine, asleep tangled together like kittens, and even now, even asleep, sparrows perched on the stockade pole above them, keeping watch. And at the far end, smallest of all, a girl sitting perfectly straight with her hands in her lap, watching him with eyes too old for her face.

She didn't speak. None of them spoke to him. They'd learned that lesson fast; speaking got you taken to the tent with the lamps.

But the sparrows followed him along the fence line, hopping from post to post, and when he stopped, they stopped, and they tilted their heads at him in unison, and Dain had the sudden, lunatic certainty that they were discussing him.

"What do you want?" he asked the girl quietly.

Her mouth pressed thin. She glanced at the sleeping sisters, then back at him, and then — deliberately, slowly — she raised one small hand and pointed at the ground beside her, where someone had scratched marks into the dirt with a nail or a stone. Not letters. Pictures. A bird. A cage. A bell.

And under the bell, drawn smaller, almost hidden: a crack.

Dain crouched down. His knees cracked. Somewhere behind him the old woman's needles clicked, clicked, clicked, patient as rain.

"She drew that yesterday," the old woman said. "Been drawing it every day since they brought that cursed bell through camp. Hurts them, you know. That thing. Like a knife between the ears for anything with the gift. The little ones scream in their sleep now, not from dreams. From the ringing."

"It's a weapon," Dain said. "It's supposed to hurt."

"It's supposed to hurt *them*," the old woman agreed. "Funny thing about weapons, though. They never stay pointed where you aim them." She finally looked up, and her eyes were pale and clear and terribly kind. "You've been standing at a crossroads a long while, lieutenant. I can smell it on you. Like a horse that won't take the bit. How long do you mean to stand there?"

Dain rose. His mouth was dry. "I have orders."

"You have a piece of paper," the old woman said. "Orders are what you make of them. My husband had orders once, at the ford at Marren. Told to hold the bridge. Held it all night, brave as brass, and in the morning it turned out the war had moved on and everyone he'd died holding the bridge against had already gone home." She resumed her invisible knitting. "Check your orders, boy. See if the war has moved on."

* * *

That evening, the quartermaster brought him the prisoners' effects for cataloguing and disposal. It was procedure. Everything confiscated went into a ledger: one wooden bird-whistle, one knotted cord, one boot (left), one boot (right), and so on, and then everything was burned, because the crown did not want relics of the Gifted circulating among the superstitious.

Dain sat in his tent with the ledger open and a small heap of sad belongings in front of him, and he felt like a grave-robber at a child's funeral.

There was the bird-whistle, carved from alder wood, worn smooth by small thumbs. There was a knotted cord — he recognized the knotwork now, having seen Sef's kind of magic described in reports; it was a charm, probably, something to keep nightmares away, and it had failed, and here it was in his ledger. There was a hair ribbon. A river stone with a hole through it, the kind children find and treasure for reasons adults forget. A single sock, darned twice.

And underneath the sock, wrapped in a scrap of oilcloth:

A badge.

Dain went very still.

He knew that badge. Every soldier in the Hunt knew that badge. Silver, stamped with the antlered stag of the Crown Hunt, the maker's mark of Vellum & Sons of the capital on the reverse, and — he turned it over with fingers that had begun, without his permission, to shake — a dent in the lower rim, shaped exactly like the cobblestone it had struck the night Captain Elara Voss tore it from her own chest and threw it at the tribunal's feet, the night she became a traitor, the night Dain had stood in the ranks and watched and said nothing.

It was among a child's effects. Which meant either the child had found it — impossible, unless it had been given —

Or someone had given it to the child. Someone who had passed through the prisoner convoy. Someone who had been captured and recaptured, or had surrendered herself, or—

Casper Joubert. The beast-speaker. The one Elara had burned her whole life to free. The one whose trail Dain had chased across half the kingdom with a screaming bell and a growing sickness in his stomach, because every report of her said the same strange thing: that she was twenty-some years old and frightened and gentle, that she talked to animals the way other people talked to old friends, that she had surrendered herself once to save a badger.

A badger. She'd given herself up for a badger.

And somewhere between capture and escape, she had pressed her captain's badge into the hands of a captured child, the way you'd press a coin into a beggar's palm, or a torch into the hand of someone standing in the dark. *Here. Keep this. Someone's coming.*

Dain sat for a long time with the badge in his palm, and the tent around him filled with dusk, and outside, the great bell in its iron wagon began its evening cycle — a low, grinding tone that rolled through the camp and made the horses stamp and the dogs whine and, he now understood, made four children in a pine stockade clutch their heads and scream silently into their sleeves.

The old woman's voice, in his memory: *Funny thing about weapons. They never stay pointed where you aim them.*

He thought about the crack the child had drawn beneath the bell.

He thought about Elara, age nineteen, teaching him to read sign on the northern trails, laughing at his terrible tracking until he got better than hers, and then laughing harder, because she'd never minded being surpassed; she'd only ever minded cruelty. *The day the Hunt becomes a thing I'm ashamed of, Dain,* she'd told him once, sharpening her knife by firelight, *I'll burn it down myself. Promise me you'll at least step out of the way.*

He had promised. He'd been twenty-three and certain the day would never come.

The bell tolled on, and under its grinding note Dain heard — or imagined he heard, but no, he was fairly sure he actually heard — the sparrows in the stockade singing. Small voices, threading through the great bronze roar like grass through pavement. They shouldn't have been able to sing through it. The bell was designed to smother the gift entirely, to silence anything magical within a mile.

Unless the note was wrong. Unless the bell was flawed. Cracked, maybe, or mistuned, so that it crushed and crushed and never quite finished the job — leaving the gift bruised but breathing, the way a boot two sizes too small lets you walk, badly, painfully, but walk.

The child had known. Children always know. They live close to the ground where the truth grows.

Dain picked up his pen. In the ledger, next to *one silver badge*, he wrote: *unidentified scrap metal. Burned.* Then he closed the book, pocketed the badge, and went to find the bell-wagon's keeper, a drunk old artificer named Petch who owed Dain money and talked too much when he was nervous, which was always.

* * *

Petch's wagon smelled of lamp oil, solder, and regret. He was halfway through a bottle of plum brandy and greeted Dain with the wary cheer of a man calculating bribes.

"Lieutenant! Come to admire the crown's finest? She's a beauty, isn't she. Cast in the capital, blessed by three archmages, cost more than your entire—"

"Petch. Who tuned her?"

"Tuned her?" Petch blinked. "Nobody tunes her, she was cast true, she was cast—"

"Petch." Dain set the brandy bottle down, gently, the way you'd set down something you might later need as evidence. "I've stood next to that bell every day for a month. I've watched horses go deaf near it. I've watched it fail to fully silence a flock of sparrows the size of my fist. Either your masterpiece is perfect, or it's cracked, and if it's cracked, I need to know before someone else finds out, because right now the only person who gets blamed is the man who built it. Understood?"

The calculation behind Petch's eyes took visible shape: lie, get caught, hang; or confess, maybe get blamed anyway, but maybe not. He took a long pull of brandy and sagged.

"There's a flaw," he muttered. "Hairline. In the third ring-band, near the yoke-mount. Found it after casting. If I reported it, they'd melt her down and start again, eighteen months' work, and Master Corvelin would have my hide for the delay. So I... compensated. Adjusted the strike-weight. She still works. Mostly. She just—" he waved a hand vaguely, "—leaks. A little. At the edges. Any gift strong enough, or stubborn enough, slips through the gap in the tone. It's like..." he searched for the image, "...like a net with one finger-width hole. Catches most fish."

"And the ones it doesn't catch?"

"Get angrier," Petch said, and drank.

Dain thanked him, walked out into the cold, and stood looking at the bell-wagon silhouetted against the rising moon — the twelfth moon, fat and white and only one turn from the thirteenth, whatever that meant, though lately the word *thirteenth* had started appearing in sealed dispatches, and sealed dispatches appearing in a hunt-camp usually meant someone important was scared.

One finger-width hole in the net.

Enough for sparrows to sing through. Enough, maybe, for a woman with a voice that could reach every beast in the realm — if anyone could get that bell out of her way. And the bell would be at the capital by the thirteenth moon. He'd seen the marching orders. Everything was being gathered there. The bell, the prisoners, the Gifted, all of it flowing toward the city like blood toward a wound.

Like fish toward a net.

Dain walked back to his tent, took out the order with the tall gleaming letters, and read it one last time. *No exceptions for age.* The boy with the burned hand. The sisters asleep in a tangle. The small straight-backed girl who drew pictures in the dirt because speaking got you killed.

*Step out of the way,* Elara had said.

He fed the order to his cookfire and watched the letters curl and go gold and vanish. Then he took out a fresh sheet of paper and wrote, in his own cramped apologetic hand, the first order he had ever issued himself:

*At second watch, open the stockade. Take the children east along the mill road. Burn the ledger. Trust the birds.*

* * *

Second watch came down gray and drizzling. Dain moved through the camp with his heart going like a drum corps, past sentries he'd shared fires with for years, past Hesk, who looked at him once — just once, a long look — and then turned his back very conspicuously to study the empty road, which was either negligence or the bravest thing anyone had ever done for him.

The lock on the stockade gate was old iron and older habits; it opened to a whisper. Inside, the old woman was awake, knitting her nothing, and she smiled at him like she'd been expecting him for years.

"Took you long enough," she whispered.

"I had orders," Dain whispered back, and was surprised to find he was smiling too, wild and terrified and lighter than he'd been in three years.

The children woke without sound — gifted children learned to wake quietly — and the girl with the old eyes looked up at him, and then at the badge he held out to her, Elara's badge, dented and disgraceful and the truest thing in the whole camp.

"He says thank you," the girl said softly. Not to Dain. To the air above his shoulder, where a sparrow had landed without him noticing, feathers puffed against the drizzle. "For coming. She said someone would. She said the loyal ones turn slowest, but hardest."

"Did she," Dain managed.

The sparrow chirped. It sounded, frankly, smug.

They went out through the mud and the rain, five children and an old woman and a traitor, east along the mill road with the bell grinding its broken note behind them — and through the hole in the net, faint and fierce, the sparrows sang them all the way to the trees.

By dawn they'd need to be miles gone. By the thirteenth moon, they'd need to be in the capital, inside the walls, close enough to reach a bell with a hairline flaw and a kingdom full of animals waiting for a voice.

Dain pulled his collar up against the rain and lengthened his stride.

Somewhere ahead, he was almost certain, Elara Voss was going to laugh at him.
