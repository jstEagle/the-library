## 27. The Bell That Screams

The plan, as Dain explained it for the third time, involved a great deal of climbing and a truly insulting amount of pigeon droppings.

"The bell tower of Saint Verren," he said, tapping the map with one scarred finger. "That's the anchor. There are seven suppression bells in the capital, but they're tuned to each other — like a choir. Kill the lead voice and the rest go sour on their own within a day."

"And the lead voice lives up there," Sef said, "in a tower full of bell-wardens, guarded by soldiers, ringed by streets where every third person is an informer."

"Yes."

"Wonderful. I was worried it might be difficult."

Casper leaned over the map in the lamplight of the undercity hideout — a disused tannery cellar that smelled, according to Mirei, "like old boots crying." The rats had confirmed the layout weeks ago; now she cross-checked it against Dain's soldier's memory and found the two accounts agreed, which was more than she could say for any two humans she'd ever met.

"Tell them about the bell itself," Elara said from the doorway, arms crossed, wearing the expression she wore when she wanted everyone to know she thought the plan was madness but had already decided to help.

Dain grimaced. "It's not a normal bell. It doesn't ring sound, exactly. It rings *magic* — or rather, it screams at magic until the magic goes deaf and dumb. That's how they keep the gifted docile in the city. You feel it?" He looked at Casper. "You'd feel it worse than any of us."

She nodded slowly. She *did* feel it. Ever since they'd come underground to the capital's roots, her gift had been muffled, like hearing through wool. The sparrows still came to her, but their voices arrived thin and far away, as if they were calling across a frozen river. Somewhere above them, day and night, the great bell of Saint Verren was screaming, and everything magical in the city flinched beneath it.

"So we don't just climb up and crack it with a hammer," she said.

"You can't crack it with a hammer. Believe me." Dain's mouth twisted. "I watched a man try. The bell screamed back and he forgot his own mother's face. Walked around for a week asking strangers if they'd seen her."

A small silence followed. Mirei, curled in the corner with the three-legged dog she'd adopted and named Turnip, pulled the dog closer.

"So what do we do?" Casper asked.

"We do what you do," Dain said, and there was something almost shy in it, this former hunter admitting it. "We ask nicely."

* * *

The asking nicely took four days.

The problem was getting close enough to ask. The bell tower stood in the heart of the Temple Quarter, and its lower floors swarmed with wardens of the Order of the Clear Voice — men and women whose whole sacred duty was keeping the bell fed and oiled and screaming. They went up in shifts. They slept in the tower. They even ate there, in a refectory that smelled of candle wax and self-satisfaction, if the mice were to be believed.

The mice were, in fact, extremely forthcoming on the subject of the refectory. Casper spent the first evening of those four days crouched behind a flour barrel in the temple undercroft with a mouse the size of her thumb standing on her knee, chattering away in its quick bright way while Sef kept watch and pretended not to be amazed, because he had decided years ago that being amazed was undignified.

"They waste bread," the mouse reported indignantly. "Whole crusts! They cut the soft middles out and leave the rest like it's nothing. And there is a cat, but the cat is fat and stupid and owes me a debt."

"What kind of debt?"

"I saved her kitten from a drain. She pretends it didn't happen, but she knows." The mouse groomed its whiskers with the air of a creditor. "Anyway. The wardens' quarters are on the third floor. The bell is above. There is a stair, and a door with a lock, and the lock has a key that hangs on the neck of the fat warden who snores like a landslide."

"Thank you," Casper said. "You've been very helpful."

"Bring bread crusts tomorrow," said the mouse. "The soft middles. We know what they discard. We will take payment in what they value least."

There was a lesson in that, Casper thought, climbing back down through the drains to the tannery, that the crown would never learn.

* * *

The key was the easy part. The fat warden — Brother Odo, per the mice, who knew all the names and held strong opinions about several — snored precisely as advertised. Sef lifted the key off his neck with a cantrip so old and simple it predated the suppression bells themselves: a little slumber-deepening charm, the kind taught to apprentices before they were trusted with anything interesting. The Order of the Clear Voice had suppressed a great deal of magic in the capital, but apparently not the magic of picking a lock.

The hard part was the bell.

They came up the inner stair past midnight, Casper and Sef and Elara, with Dain below keeping watch on the warden's door and Mirei — over everyone's objections — waiting in the belfry's shadowed corner because she refused to be left behind and because, as she put it with the terrible logic of children, "the bell screams at magic and I'm not magic yet, so I'm the safest one here."

Elara opened her mouth to argue. Casper caught her eye and shook her head. Some battles weren't worth having, and besides, the girl wasn't wrong.

Then they stepped out onto the bell deck, and Casper forgot every argument she'd ever won.

The bell of Saint Verren filled the tower like a captive god. It was vast — thirty feet of dark bronze hanging from a beam thick as a mature oak — and it was wrong in a way her eyes understood before her mind did. Bells were made to be beautiful; even war-bells had a shape that promised music. This thing had been cast crooked on purpose, its lip jagged, its surface covered in spiraling grooves that hurt to look at, like a shell carved by something that hated the sea. And it hummed. Not loudly. Constantly. A thin, needle-fine note that Casper felt in her teeth, in the backs of her eyes, in the place behind her ribs where the beast-speech lived.

Up here, so close, her gift wasn't muffled. It was *pressed*. Like a hand flat against her chest, holding her down.

"That's why the animals hate this quarter," she whispered. "It's not the noise. It's — it's standing on your chest."

Sef had gone pale. He was staring up at the bell with an expression she'd learned to recognize: the look of a man doing arithmetic he wished came out differently. "I helped design the tuning forks for these," he said quietly. "Twenty years ago. I told myself it was acoustics. Pure theory." He laughed, and it wasn't a funny laugh. "You spend enough years in a tower, you can rationalize anything."

"Save the confession for after," Elara said, but gently, for her. "How do we kill it?"

"You don't kill it." Sef circled the great beam, reading the grooves. "You un-tune it. The seven bells are bound in resonance — a chord. Break one note cleanly and the chord collapses. But if you damage it crudely—" he touched the bronze and snatched his fingers back as if bitten, "—it screams. All at once, at full voice. Every gifted thing in the city gets seared. Including our friend here." He nodded at Casper. "Including, I suspect, Mirei, whatever she does or doesn't have yet. So: clean break, or nothing."

"How?"

Sef looked at her. "That part," he said apologetically, "is yours."

* * *

Here is what Casper knew, that no mage and no soldier did: the bell was alive.

Not alive like a badger or a sparrow. Alive the way a river is alive, or a storm — a made thing so old and so soaked in purpose that purpose had curdled into something like hunger. The Order fed it. She'd felt it in the undercroft, in the wardens' careful rituals, in the way the mice spoke of the tower with a shiver in their whiskers. The bell screamed at magic because it *wanted* to. Someone, centuries ago, had poured a hunger into bronze, and the bronze had been hungry ever since.

So she climbed the beam — Elara anchoring the rope, swearing steadily under her breath in hunt-captain fashion — and sat astride the oak beside the bell's crown, and put both palms flat against the cold metal, and listened.

What she heard made her throat close.

Under the scream, deep down, buried the way Bracken had once told her the king's true voice was buried — there was a note. One pure note, wound tight at the bell's heart, the note it had been cast to sing before someone bent it crooked. It sounded, faintly, like joy. Like a festival morning. Like the first bell of a wedding, rung by hands that loved ringing.

"Oh," Casper breathed. "Oh, you poor thing."

"Casper?" Elara's voice, sharp with alarm. "Talk to me."

"It's not evil." Her own voice sounded strange to her, half-drowned. "It's been *made* cruel. There's a real bell under all this. It remembers ringing properly." She pressed her forehead to the bronze. "I'm going to try to wake it up."

"You're going to *what*—"

But Casper had already closed her eyes and done the thing she'd done her whole life in the Hollow, the thing that would have gotten her burned: she reached out with the listening, past the scream, past the hunger, down to that one wound-tight note, and she spoke to it.

Not words. Beasts didn't need words, and neither, it turned out, did bells. She gave it what she'd given a thousand frightened creatures: *I see you. I know what you were. You don't have to do this.*

The bell shuddered.

The whole tower shuddered. Dust sifted from the rafters. Below, Dain's voice rose in a strangled hiss, and somewhere a warden shouted in his sleep. The scream in Casper's skull spiked — white, furious, the hunger feeling itself threatened — and she gasped and nearly lost her grip, and then, underneath, the small clear note turned toward her like a sunflower toward light.

*Sing*, she told it. *Sing the way you were made to.*

The bell rang.

Once. Softly. Without being struck.

And the sound was — Casper had no word for it and suspected no language did. It was the sound the valley made at dawn. It was Bracken's laugh. It was every good morning she had ever heard gathered into one brazen peal that rolled out over the sleeping capital, and in its wake the crooked scream simply... unraveled. The hunger didn't die; it *let go*, like a fist opening, like a child finally setting down a weight it had carried so long it had forgotten it wasn't theirs.

Across the city, six other bells faltered, soured, and fell silent.

Magic came flooding back into the capital like water into a dry channel. Casper felt it hit her — the sparrows suddenly *loud*, the rats triumphant, the stray dogs of three districts lifting their voices in a ragged chorus that was absolutely, unmistakably singing. Even the tannery cellar's resident moths seemed brighter.

In the belfry shadow, Mirei laughed out loud — a real laugh, full-throated, the first Casper had ever heard from her — and clapped her hands over her mouth too late.

"Well," said Sef weakly, from the beam. "That was supposed to require three days of ritual preparation and possibly a blood sacrifice. Instead you *asked it politely*."

"That's her whole method," Elara said. There was something in her voice Casper couldn't name — pride, maybe, or the beginning of it. "God help us all, it keeps working."

* * *

They should have gone straight home. That was the agreement; Elara said it twice on the way down the tower stairs, and Dain said it once more in the temple undercroft, and everyone agreed, and then Casper stood in the moonlit square outside Saint Verren and listened to the city waking up.

Because the city *was* waking up. Not the people — not yet — but everything else. Horses in the guard stables tossing their heads and blowing. Oxen lowing in the market pens. And overhead, wheeling against the stars, hundreds upon hundreds of birds, starlings and pigeons and gulls, circling the silent tower in a slow spiral, crying out to each other in voices no longer pressed flat.

"They haven't heard themselves in years," Casper whispered. "Some of these birds were hatched under the bell. They've never sung at full voice."

Mirei stood beside her with Turnip leaning on her leg, watching the spiral of birds with her chin tipped back, and said, very seriously, "They're saying thank you."

"Are they?"

"Mm. Mostly. One pigeon is complaining about the price of breadcrumbs, but mostly thank you."

Casper laughed — really laughed, the first time in longer than she could measure — and for one shining moment, standing in the square with the girl and the dog and the wheeling sky, she let herself believe they might actually win.

Which was, of course, when the temple doors banged open.

* * *

Brother Odo came out first, still in his nightshirt, the key swinging from his neck where Sef had considerately returned it, and behind him poured the wardens of the Clear Voice — a dozen men and women in grey, faces slack with horror, staring up at their silent bell as if at a corpse. Then the shouting started. Then the lanterns. Then, from the barracks street, the drums.

"Move," Elara said, very quietly, and they moved.

They went the way the rats had mapped for them — down through the tanner's alley, along the drainage cut, doubling twice — but the city was awake now in the wrong way, doors opening, watchmen whistling, and somewhere behind them a horn sounding the long note that meant *hunt*. Dain led them through a gap in the wall he'd known from his soldiering days, and they ran the frozen towpath along the canal with their breath smoking, and it was only when they dropped down into the tannery cellar, hearts slamming, that Casper realized what was wrong.

The others realized it a heartbeat later. The cellar was cold. Colder than they'd left it. The banked fire in the old copper vat had been raked apart — not burned down. *Raked.* Deliberately.

Mirei's hand found Casper's sleeve.

On the floor, weighted with a button, lay a scrap of paper. Elara picked it up and read it by the light of Sef's small flame, and Casper watched her face go from blank to stone.

"What is it?" Dain demanded. "Who—"

Elara held it out. Four words, in a neat clerk's hand:

**THEY KNOW WHERE YOU SLEEP.**

Above them, faint through the earth, the horns were changing direction.
