## 28. Betrayal in the Rookery

The trouble with hiding in a city of bells, Sef liked to say, was that bells were made to be heard.

Casper thought about that saying on the morning everything went wrong, while she sat in the rookery loft with straw dust in her hair and a pigeon named Turnip standing on her knee like he owned it. Which, in fairness, he did. He'd been there first.

"Tell me again," she murmured to him, "why you won't roost with the others."

Turnip cooed at her, which meant nothing and everything, the way pigeons did. Around her the loft was full of them — three hundred birds packed into the rafters above an abandoned tannery, each one a set of eyes she could borrow, each one a small feathered spy who worked for bread crumbs and the occasional scratch under the wing. The rats had their own network in the drains below. The stray dogs ran the streets. In eleven days since they'd come down into the capital's bones, Casper had built something out of nothing but listening: a map of the palace drawn in animal memory, guard rotations learned from sparrows who perched on windowsills nobody checked.

Eleven days. And somewhere in those eleven days, someone had sold them.

She didn't know that yet. What she knew was that Bracken had come up the ladder slowly, the way he always did now, his badger body stiff and his breath whistling through old scars. They'd smuggled him out of the menagerie four nights ago — Dain's doing, mostly, with a forged transfer order and a cart of fish for the palace cats — and he'd spent every hour since sleeping in the straw like a creature determined to make up for two months of cages.

"You're supposed to be resting," Casper said.

"I *was* resting." He lowered himself onto the hay with a groan that would have embarrassed a much older animal. "Then I smelled rain coming and decided I preferred being awake for it. Rain smells different here. Wrong. Like the sky's been boiled."

"That's the tanneries."

"I know what tanneries smell like, girl. I'm disagreeing with them."

She laughed despite herself, and Turnip startled off her knee, offended. Below them, through gaps in the floorboards, came the sounds of the hideout waking up: Mirei's quick footsteps as she raced Elara to the wash basin and lost, as she did every morning; Sef muttering over the stolen binding ritual, which he'd copied onto scraps of paper because he didn't trust his own memory anymore; the quiet clatter of Dain cleaning a weapon he still wasn't sure he was allowed to clean.

Twenty-three of them altogether. Refugees from the Rootbound sanctuary, scattered when Dain's bell had screamed across the ridge, gathered again here in the undercity because there had been nowhere else. A moth-speaker named Hollis who could call insects into patterns. Twin sisters who talked to river fish. An old man whose gift was so small he could only soothe animals' pain, never take it away, and who spent his days doing exactly that anyway, going from creature to creature like a man bailing a flood with a thimble.

They were loud, for people in hiding. They were hopeful. That should have warned her. Hopeful people got careless, and careless people got found.

* * *

It happened at dusk.

Casper was in the loft again — she spent too many hours there, Elara said, talking to birds instead of sleeping — when the sparrows went wrong.

Not one sparrow. All of them. Every bird within half a mile of the tannery lifted off the rooftops at once, a dark ragged sheet of wings, and the message that came flooding through them wasn't words so much as pure animal alarm: *many-feet, iron-smell, coming-coming-coming.*

She was down the ladder before her feet finished deciding.

"Sef!" Her voice cracked across the common room. "Sef, they're coming — soldiers, a lot of them, from the north and east both—"

He looked up from his papers, and she watched three years of guilt rearrange themselves into something harder. "How long?"

"Minutes. The dogs say minutes." She was already moving, kicking open the trapdoor to the cellar where the old drainage tunnel ran — the one the rats had shown her the second week, the one they'd kept clear "just in case," because Elara insisted on just-in-cases the way other people insisted on breathing. "Everyone up! Now! We're leaving!"

The hideout erupted. Not panic — Elara wouldn't have allowed panic if the building were already on fire — but the fast, grim efficiency of people who had run before. Blankets abandoned. Sef sweeping his papers into a oilcloth roll. Mirei appearing at Casper's side without being called, her hand finding Casper's sleeve the way she did when words were too far away.

Elara came through the door with her sword already belted. "Which way?"

"The drain tunnel, south, toward the river. It comes up behind the rope-walk."

"How far?"

"Half a mile. Maybe more. It's narrow."

"Narrow is good. Narrow means they can't ride us down in it." She turned and started counting heads, touching each shoulder as she counted, and Casper saw her lips move: *twenty-one, twenty-two, twenty-three.* "Dain! Rear guard. You know their formations better than anyone alive — make us expensive to follow."

Dain was already gone, sword out, face gray.

And then, in the middle of all of it, Casper heard the sound that stopped her heart: a low, rumbling growl from the corner by the grain store, where Bracken had hauled himself upright and was staring at the ceiling with his black eyes narrowed.

"They're not coming for the whole building," he said. "Listen. The boots. They're spreading around it. Surrounding, not storming."

Elara froze. "You can hear boots through a floor?"

"I can hear *boots,*" Bracken said, with the dignity of an animal who had once bitten a hunt-captain's horse and stood by the decision. "Someone taught these soldiers to walk quietly, and they're bad at it. They think we're rats in a barrel. They want to close the lid slow."

"Then we go out the bottom before they finish the barrel." Elara's voice was flat and fast. "Move. Everyone. *Now.*"

* * *

The tunnel swallowed them in ones and twos.

It was older than the city's oldest street, this drain — brick throat slick with moss, running water ankle-deep and cold enough to bite. Casper went third, behind Elara and Mirei, with the sparrows streaming along the ceiling above her like a nervous gray river, feeding her fragments: *soldiers at the north door, soldiers at the east wall, a lantern swinging in the loft, a lantern in the common room.* Above them, muffled through brick and earth, came the crash of the raid beginning — doors kicked, shouts, the crackle of something burning.

"They'll find the loft empty," Sef panted behind her. "Empty except for pigeons. They'll know."

"They already know," Casper said. "The question is whether they know about the tunnel."

The rats answered that one. Their message came sharp and simple: *blocked. blocked. blocked.* Both ends? No — the south end, the river end, clear. But the tunnel had branches, and into the branches, where the darkness pooled, soldiers were pouring something. Oil. She could smell it now, seeping down the main passage, thin and chemical-sweet.

"They're going to fire it," she said aloud, and hated how steady her voice came out. "Not chase us. Burn us out. Or burn us in."

"Then we stop being slow," Elara said.

They weren't slow. They ran bent-double through the dark, twenty-three people splashing through filth with a badger trotting grimly at the rear beside Dain, and Casper carried Mirei on her back for the last stretch because the child's legs were short and terror makes children heavy. Behind them the oil smell thickened. Ahead, blessedly, a gray thread of evening light marked the grate.

Hollis reached it first — the moth-speaker, thin and trembling — and wrenched at the rusted bars. They didn't move. He wrenched again, making a small hopeless sound, and Casper felt the whole column compress behind her like a fist closing.

"Stand aside." Dain shouldered forward, wrapped his cloak around his hands, and threw himself at the grate with a grunt that Casper felt in her own ribs. Once. Twice. On the third the ancient mortar gave with a crunch and the bars swung out into the dusk like a door opening onto the rest of their lives.

"Out," Elara ordered. "Fast and quiet. Rope-walk, then the cooper's yard, then scatter to the safehouses. *Go.*"

They went. One by one they squeezed through — the twins, the old pain-soother, Sef clutching his papers, Mirei sliding off Casper's back and through with a whimper she strangled halfway. Casper counted them out the way Elara counted them in. Twenty. Twenty-one. Twenty-two.

Bracken didn't come.

She turned. He was standing ten feet back up the tunnel, in the dark, facing the direction of the soldiers, and even in that gloom she could see the set of his shoulders — the same set he'd had the day the hounds came to the valley, the day he'd charged a line of armed men to buy her three seconds of running room.

"No," she said.

"Casper." His voice was low and rough and terribly calm. "Something's coming up the tunnel. A lot of somebodies. And they've got torches, and the oil's already down, and this grate is the only door." He glanced back at her, and his black eyes caught the gray light. "Somebody has to be the door."

"Dain—" she started.

"Dain is holding the rope-walk. And Dain is *slow,* girl, bless him, he walks like a cabinet falling downstairs. They'd hear him coming from the palace." Bracken took a step back into the dark, away from her, and it was the easiest movement she'd seen him make in weeks. "I'm fast in the dark. Always have been. It's the one thing age hasn't taxed yet."

"Bracken, *no.*" Her voice broke on it. "We go together or—"

"Together." He huffed, almost a laugh. "You said that to me in the valley, do you remember? When I told you to run and you planted your feet like a stump. Stubbornest creature I ever met, and I've met goats." The torchlight was growing behind him now, orange flickers crawling up the wet brick, and beneath it she could hear boots — dozens, closing. "You told me family doesn't leave family. So listen to me, Casper Joubert, because I'm about to be very family at you: *go.*"

"There has to be another way—"

"There's always another way. This one's mine." And then, softer, in the voice he'd used over goat-fences on summer evenings, telling her gossip about the valley's rabbits as though it were court intrigue: "You gave an old badger two good years, girl. You talked to me when the whole world had decided I was vermin. Do you know what that was worth? Everything. It was worth everything, and now I get to spend it, and there's no price I'd rather pay."

The torches rounded the bend.

What happened next, Casper saw only in pieces, because Elara's arms closed around her from behind and hauled her backward toward the light, and because some part of her mind — the part that had survived a lifetime in the Hollow by knowing when watching would break her — simply refused.

She saw Bracken charge.

She saw him hit the front rank of soldiers like a landslide in miniature, all the fury of a creature who had once held a valley's borders against wolves, and she heard men shout and stumble and fall. She heard Dain roaring somewhere, coming back too late. She heard the first soldier scream — not in pain, she realized later, but in sheer astonishment, because no one expects a badger.

And she heard Bracken's voice one last time, thrown back down the tunnel to her, clear as a bell over the chaos:

"*Run, you stubborn stump!*"

Then Elara dragged her through the grate, and the world became running.

* * *

They scattered through the cooper's yard and the alleys beyond, twenty-two ghosts in the gathering dark, and Casper ran because Bracken had told her to and because stopping would mean thinking and thinking would mean dying in a doorway like a fool. Elara kept a grip on her arm the whole way — not gentle, not cruel, just present, an anchor with a sword.

Behind them, faint and then fainter, the tunnel burned. She could hear it: a rush and roar, the collapse of something, shouts. She did not let herself imagine what the shouts meant. She would have years, she suspected, in which imagining was unavoidable.

They regrouped near midnight in a disused smokehouse that smelled of old salt, and Elara counted them again, twice, touching each shoulder. Twenty-two. All of them. Even Hollis, who sat in the corner shaking and would not meet anyone's eyes.

Casper noticed that. Filed it away. Set it down next to the grief, in the place where things waited to be dealt with later.

Mirei found her in the darkest corner and climbed into her lap without asking, the way she hadn't done since the first week, and Casper held her and stared at the wall and felt absolutely nothing, which she understood was the mind's mercy, and which she also understood would not last.

"He bought us the tunnel," Elara said quietly, crouching beside her. "All of it. Every life in this room is standing on his back right now."

"I know."

"He'd want you to know it wasn't a sacrifice. Badgers don't think in sacrifices. He'd say it was a *trade*, and he got the better end."

"I know," Casper said again, and her voice cracked clean through on the second word, and Elara put a hand on her shoulder and left it there, and for a long time nobody spoke, and the smokehouse filled up with twenty-two kinds of silence.

It was Mirei who finally broke it. She had been very still in Casper's lap, and then she stirred, and lifted her head, and looked up with those wide dark eyes, and said — in a whisper, but a real one, aimed and deliberate:

"He isn't gone."

Casper went cold. "Mirei—"

"The ravens." The girl's hand rose, trembling, and pointed west, toward the palace hill, toward the royal menagerie with its iron cages. "The ravens are saying... they're saying the king keeps him. The king wouldn't let them burn him. The king wants him *alive.*"

The words landed in the smokehouse like a stone into still water. Elara straightened slowly. Across the room, Sef looked up from his papers with an expression Casper couldn't read — hope and horror fighting for the same ground.

Because they all understood it at once, the same terrible arithmetic. If Bracken lived, then the enemy still had their bait. If Bracken lived, then the crown still had its leash on Casper's heart. And if Bracken lived —

— then there was still, buried inside the palace on the hill, one stubborn old badger waiting for his family to come and be family at him.

Casper looked at the western wall, at the darkness beyond it where the palace burned its thousand candles, and somewhere in the city a raven called once, long and low, like a name being read from a list.

"He's alive," she said. Her voice didn't shake. Later it would; tonight it didn't. "Then we're going in after him. Before the thirteenth moon. Whatever it costs."

Outside, the bells began to toll the hour, and every bird in the capital went silent to listen.
