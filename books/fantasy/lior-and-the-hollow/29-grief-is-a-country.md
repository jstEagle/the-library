## 29. Grief Is a Country

The world after Bracken died was a country Casper had never visited before, and she did not like the customs.

In this country, mornings still came. That was the first cruelty of it. She had half expected the sun to take one look at the smoke rising from the collapsed tunnel and decide it wasn't worth the trip, but no — dawn arrived on schedule, gray and indifferent, sliding through the cracks in the abandoned mill where they'd sheltered since the rookery fell. In this country, people still breathed. Elara checked the perimeter with her sword drawn and her jaw set. Sef muttered over his stolen ritual pages as though ink could save anyone. Dain sat apart from all of them, sharpening a knife he had no intention of using, because that was what his hands needed to do while his heart caught up.

And in this country, Casper Joubert lay on her back in the mill's dusty loft and listened to nothing.

That was the worst part. Not the silence itself — she'd grown up in silence, silence was her oldest friend — but the *shape* of it. For months now her gift had been a constant low music underneath everything: sparrows gossiping about crumbs, rats complaining about cats, the horses in whatever stable they passed murmuring their opinions of the weather. Even in flight, even hunted, she had never been alone. Now the world was a room with everyone gone out of it, and she kept turning her head toward doors that would not open.

Bracken was dead. She had felt it happen — or thought she had — through the last thread of her burning gift, a badger-sized absence opening in the world like a tooth pulled. He had held the tunnel open so the children could run. He had held it open knowing what the collapsing stone meant for an old animal with lame legs and a heart three years past its warranty. He had held it open because she asked him to survive long enough to be rescued, and being Bracken, he had interpreted this in the most literal, most infuriating, most *him* way possible: he had survived exactly until the others were safe, and not one heartbeat longer.

She wanted to quit.

Not die — she'd moved past wanting to die somewhere around the third hour of weeping into Elara's shoulder, which the hunt-captain had endured with the stiff, bewildered patience of a woman who had never been cried on by anyone she couldn't court-martial. No, she wanted to quit the way you quit a job or a war: put down the tools, walk away, let someone else carry it. Let the thirteenth moon come and do its worst. Let the thing wearing King Aldric's face keep the kingdom; it seemed to be managing well enough. She was a goatherd. Goatherds were not meant to unbind kings. Goatherds were meant to sit on hillsides and complain about the price of salt, and she had a hillside waiting for her, and every bone in her body ached for it the way a missing limb aches.

"You haven't eaten," Elara said from the ladder, without looking up at the loft. She said it the way she reported troop movements. Flat, factual, and meaning *I am worried about you and will express it through logistics.*

"I'm not hungry."

"You haven't eaten since yesterday. That's a tactical liability."

"Then court-martial me."

A pause. "I've seen your file. It wouldn't stick." And then, quieter, almost human: "Casper. Come down. Please."

The *please* got her, the way it always did. Elara Voss spent pleases like a miser spends gold, and each one cost her visibly. Casper hauled herself up, went down the ladder, and accepted the bowl of barley porridge that tasted like wet paper and grief. She ate half of it out of spite. Elara watched her do it and pretended not to, which was its own kind of tenderness.

Sef looked up from his papers. "We should talk about—"

"No."

"Casper, the moon is in eleven days. The ritual requires—"

"*No.*" Her voice cracked across the mill like a whip, and Mirei flinched where she sat by the window, and Casper immediately hated herself for it. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, Mirei. I just—" She set down the bowl. "I can't talk about the ritual today. I can't talk about moons. Today I want to sit here and miss my friend, and I want all of you to let me."

Nobody argued. That was the trouble with found families: they learned your tells too fast, and they gave you room at exactly the moments you wanted them to fight you, because fighting you would mean admitting how badly you were needed.

Dain spoke from his corner, not looking up from his knife. "He knew."

"What?"

"The badger. Bracken." The knife whispered along the whetstone. "I saw him, at the tunnel. Before the stone came down. He looked at me — right at me, like he understood exactly what I was holding and why I hesitated — and he *nodded*. Like we'd come to terms. Animals don't do that." He finally raised his eyes. "Unless they're talking to someone who taught them that people are worth terms. That was you. He died the way he lived because of what you made him believe about us." A pause. "So if you quit, you don't just quit on yourself. You quit on his arithmetic."

Casper stared at him. "That is the cruelest thing anyone has ever said to me, and I once heard a goat describe another goat as 'a waste of good grass.'"

"He's right, though," Elara said, insufferably.

"I hate all of you," Casper announced, to general agreement, and went back upstairs to lie down and miss her friend in private.

* * *

Grief, she discovered, was not one country but many, and she traveled through all of them in a single afternoon.

There was the province of Anger, where she raged at Bracken for dying, at herself for asking him to hold the tunnel, at Sef for having a master who wrote bindings, at the crown-thing for existing, at the universe generally for constructing a world in which old badgers had to be brave. There was the province of Bargaining, where she lay awake composing letters to powers she didn't believe in: *give him back and I'll give up the gift gladly, I'll give up anything, I'll give up Elara's terrible cooking.* There was the vast gray province of Numbness, which took up most of the map, and in which she stared at the rafters and counted the woodworm holes and thought about absolutely nothing, magnificently, for hours.

It was evening when Mirei climbed the ladder.

The girl moved quietly — she moved quietly everywhere, a habit the interrogators had built into her that Casper would spend years, if she was given years, trying to gently dismantle. She settled beside Casper without asking, the way Bracken used to, and for a moment the resemblance hurt so much Casper had to look at the rafters and breathe.

Mirei hadn't spoken since the wagon. Not a word, not a sound, except once in her sleep when she'd whimpered something in a language that made Sef go pale and leave the room. She communicated in signs — Casper's signs, the ones she'd taught her through the bars of a prison cart, hand-shapes for *safe*, for *hungry*, for *bad dream*, for *stay*. The other two fingers of her vocabulary were expressions: a furrowed brow that meant *why*, a flat stare that meant *Elara is being ridiculous again*, which both women found deeply unfair and entirely accurate.

Now the girl sat beside her in the failing light and studied Casper's face with those grave dark eyes, the way she studied everything, cataloguing it, deciding whether it was trustworthy.

Then she reached out and touched Casper's cheek, where the tears had dried in tracks.

Casper signed, slowly: *I'm sad.*

Mirei nodded. She knew sad. Sad was an old acquaintance of hers.

*I lost my friend,* Casper signed. *His name was Bracken. He was a badger. He was old and rude and he loved me, and he's gone, and I don't know how to do any of this without him.*

Mirei considered this for a long time. Then she did something she had never done. She took Casper's hands in her small ones — not signing *at* her, but holding them, folding the fingers closed, and when she spoke, her voice came out rusty and small and cracked from disuse, like a door in a house nobody had lived in for a year.

"You have to get up," Mirei said.

Casper went very still.

"They're coming," the girl said. "They're already coming. I hear them."

"Mirei—"

"The birds told me." Her voice wobbled but held, word after word, each one hauled up from somewhere deep like a bucket from a well. "At dawn. And again at noon. They're leaving the fields and the forests and flying toward the city. Starlings in rivers. Geese off course. A hawk landed on our roof this morning and looked at me, and it said—" she frowned, searching, "—it didn't say. It *asked*. With its eyes. It asked if this was the way, and I pointed, and it went." Her grip tightened on Casper's hands. "They think you called them. You didn't call them, but they think you did, because of the bells, because everyone heard the bells stop screaming, and they're coming anyway. They're coming because they believe in you."

"Mirei, sweetheart, I can't hear them anymore. My gift is—"

"I know." And the girl's chin came up, and there was something in her face that Casper recognized with a jolt — something stubborn, something familiar, something with grey fur and a limp and zero respect for self-pity. "You don't need to hear them. I can hear them *for* you. Until you can hear again. That's what family does."

The word landed like a stone dropped in still water.

Family. Bracken had been her family before she knew the word applied to anything but blood — a lame old badger who'd decided, somewhere in her ninth winter, that the silent girl on the hillside was his to look after, and had looked after her for fifteen years with insults and stolen pies and a ferocity of devotion that no human relative had ever matched. And now here was this small broken-brave girl offering to stand in the gap he'd left, and offering it in her first full sentence in months, and Casper understood suddenly that grief was a country, yes — but countries had borders, and borders could be crossed, and on the far side of even the worst country there were people waiting with lamps lit.

"Oh," Casper said, uselessly.

"And also," Mirei added, with the merciless timing of the very young, "the hawk is still waiting on the ridge. It has been waiting for two hours. It is very annoyed. I think it might be related to Bracken."

Something broke open in Casper's chest — not the wound, the wound stayed wounded, wounds like this stay wounded forever — but something next to the wound. A laugh. Small and wrecked and real.

"That," she managed, "is the most Bracken sentence I've ever heard."

* * *

She went up the ridge at dusk, because the hawk deserved an answer and because, though she'd never have admitted it under torture, Mirei was right.

The bird sat on the leaning fence post at the top of the rise, silhouetted against a sky going bruise-colored over the capital's distant glow. It was a big female, scarred at the breast, feathers rough with long travel. When Casper crested the hill it turned its head and fixed her with one golden eye, and even without her gift — especially without her gift — Casper could read the whole conversation in that eye, because fifteen years of listening had taught her a language deeper than magic.

*You're late,* said the eye.

"I know," Casper said aloud, her voice strange in her own ears, speaking to a wild thing with no magic carrying between them, just air and attention. "I'm sorry. Someone I loved died, and I lay in a loft feeling sorry for myself, and a seven-year-old had to kick me back onto my feet. It's been a whole day."

The hawk roused her feathers, unimpressed.

"Yes, I know it's ridiculous. He'd have said worse." Casper sat down in the grass below the fence post, close enough to touch, not touching. Below them the valley darkened, and far off, the capital's towers stood black against the last light, crowned with the thirteenth moon's coming. "Tell me what you've seen. Tell me everything. I'll listen the old way — the slow way, the way I did before I ever had the gift. Eyes and patience and guessing." She swallowed. "It turns out I remember how."

The hawk regarded her for a long moment. Then, deliberately, she dropped from the post and landed on Casper's knee, talons biting through cloth, heavy and warm and alive, and began — in the old language of posture and pitch and feather-angle that no binding could ever take from her — to speak.

Rivers of starlings. Roads full of moving shadows. Wolves pacing the treelines facing south. Cattle breaking fences. The whole kingdom, stirring like a sleeper before waking, turning toward the city, toward the bells, toward her.

Toward the thirteenth moon.

Casper listened until full dark, and the hawk talked until full dark, and when it finally lifted off into the night it left a single feather on her knee, which she took as either a blessing or a bill.

At the bottom of the ridge, Mirei was waiting with a lantern, and behind Mirei stood Elara and Sef and Dain, all pretending they hadn't been watching, all watching.

"Well," Casper said, and her voice only shook a little. "Somebody tell me everything Sef knows about that ritual. We've got eleven days, a kingdom's worth of animals already on the road, and apparently a hawk who thinks I owe her an apology." She looked at them — her impossible, borrowed, hard-won family — and felt Bracken's absence and presence in the same breath, the way you feel a missing tooth with the tongue, over and over, learning the new shape of things. "Let's go unbind a king."

From the darkness beyond the lantern light, faint but unmistakable, came the cry of geese — heading north, all of them, heading for the capital, heading for her.

Mirei smiled for the first time since the wagon.
