## 30. The Gathering

The wolves came down from the high passes three nights after the twelfth moon began to fatten.

Casper felt them before she saw them — a pressure at the edge of her gift, like a hand resting on a door. She was sitting on the rootbound sanctuary's oldest stump, watching Mirei practice her signs with a very patient hedgehog, when the forest went quiet in a way that wasn't fear.

"They're here," she said.

The pack emerged from the treeline in single file, gray and lean and travel-worn, the big scarred male at their head. He had run with her once through wild country, back when she'd been nothing but a runaway goat-girl with a secret, and he had told her then what the wind was telling every beast in the kingdom: *something is wrong with the two-legs who wears the crown.*

Now he stopped ten paces away and lowered his head — not submission, never that, but the particular courtesy of one courier acknowledging another.

*You called,* he said. *We heard.*

"I didn't call yet."

*You will.* His amber eyes were steady. *The old ones remember. My grandmother's grandmother ran beside the first king of this land when he still spoke true. We do not come because you asked. We come because we know what is coming, and we would rather stand in front of it than behind it.*

Behind him, the pack settled onto the frozen ground as if they intended to stay for winter. One young wolf immediately began chewing a stick. The scarred male sighed the long-suffering sigh of a father.

"How many?" Casper asked.

*More than the two-legs can count without taking off their boots,* he said. *Wolves from the north ranges. The hill dogs are coming too — they say they are wolves, which is charming, and we have decided not to correct them.*

Casper laughed, and it came out wet, and she didn't care.

*Rest now,* the old wolf said. *Run later. And girl —* he paused, ears forward — *when you speak on the thirteenth moon, speak loudly. Some of us are old, and our hearing isn't what it was.*

* * *

Two hundred miles south, in the king's own stables, the horses woke before dawn and would not settle.

The stable master, a round man named Petch whose entire philosophy of life was that horses were simpler than people and therefore better, stood in the aisle with a lantern and watched his charges press against their stall doors, all facing the same direction. North. Toward the capital's great square.

"Easy," he murmured. "Easy, my lovelies. What's got into you?"

He got no answer he could understand, but the old mare in the end stall — the gray, the ancient one, the one the king himself had ridden before his voice went strange — turned her head and looked at him with an expression Petch had only ever seen on grieving widows.

She knew where she was going. She could not go there. Her legs were done; the farrier had said so kindly last spring, the way you say things to animals you love. But her ears were pricked north, and her nostrils flared, and when the first wild horses thundered past the city walls that evening — dun-colored, unshod, manes full of burrs, moving like a river deciding its course — the old mare screamed.

Petch had worked horses for forty years. He had never heard a horse scream like that. It was not pain. It was a name, called out over and over to someone too far away to hear.

He did the only thing his heart allowed. He opened her stall door, and led her out into the yard, and pointed her north, and took off her halter.

"Go on, then," he said gruffly. "Tell him whatever it is you're telling him. He'll listen to you if he won't listen to anyone."

The old mare walked. Slowly, stiffly, one deliberate hoof at a time, out the gate and onto the north road, joining at the edge of the wild herd like a grandmother falling into step with grandchildren. Petch watched until the dark took her, then went back inside and sat down among his unsettled horses and, for reasons he couldn't have explained to the tribunal or the criers or his own wife, began quietly planning how many stalls he could clear by week's end.

* * *

The ravens came first to the towers, because ravens always come first.

They arrived in ones and twos, then dozens, lining the cathedral spires and the palace gutters until the rooftops looked tarred with birds. The city's raven-keeper, a sour old woman named Hesk who had fed the tower birds for thirty years and trusted exactly four people in her life, stood in her doorway and counted, and recounted, and went pale.

"Word's gone out," said her neighbor, a fishwife with a basket of herring. "Some new proclamation?"

"No proclamation moves birds like this." Hesk threw a handful of scraps and watched them ignore it entirely. Ravens ignoring food. That was the part that frightened her. "This is older than proclamations."

Overhead, the flock wheeled, split, reformed — and flew north in a ribbon of black, calling in voices that sounded almost like words. Behind them rose the crows from the fields, the jackdaws from the chimneys, the magpies in their thieving hundreds, all streaming toward the capital like iron filings toward a lodestone.

And below, on every road that fed the city, the cattle came.

They did not stampede. That was the uncanny thing, the thing the farmers would tell their grandchildren: the herds simply *left*. A dairy farmer outside Millbrook woke to find his forty cows standing at the gate in an orderly line, looking at him with mild brown patience, waiting for him to open it. When he didn't, they waited. When he finally did — because what else could a man do, faced with forty patient mothers — they walked north at a steady amble, calves trotting at their sides, as though summoned to market by a buyer they trusted more than any buyer alive.

"They're going to the city," his daughter said, watching from the fence.

"They're going somewhere," her father said. "Best hope it's not a slaughterhouse."

His daughter, who was eleven and paid closer attention than he gave her credit for, shook her head. "Da, look at them. Nobody walks like that to a slaughterhouse. They walk like that to church."

* * *

Elara Voss found the soldiers in the taverns, because that was where disgraced captains always find soldiers: wherever the ale is cheapest and the complaints are loudest.

She had expected to work alone. She had planned for alone — alone with Casper and Sef and a traumatized child and a plan so thin Sef had described it as "less a plan than a strong opinion." What she had not planned for was Dain.

Her former lieutenant sat across from her in the back room of the Drowned Gull, out of uniform, beard growing in patchy, looking like a man who had been sleeping badly for a month. Between them on the table lay his badge — her badge, really, the one she'd abandoned in the effects of captured children, the one that had turned him.

"You're insane," Dain said. It was not an accusation. It was almost fond. "You want sympathetic soldiers to desert en masse on the night of the coronation renewal. You want me to walk into the garrison I've served eight years and ask men to commit treason against a king who can have us all hanged before breakfast."

"Yes," Elara said.

"And your alternative plan?"

"There isn't one."

Dain rubbed his face. "Right. Good. Just checking we're being honest about it." He leaned forward. "There are thirty-one of them. Soldiers who've seen what the interrogators do and haven't managed to stop feeling sick about it. Thirty-one, Voss. I counted twice because I didn't believe it the first time."

"That's more than I hoped."

"It's fewer than you need."

"It's more than deserve to be asked," Elara said. "I'm asking anyway. Every one of them gets the choice, clean and private. No shaming if they say no. This isn't duty anymore, Dain. Duty's what got us here. This is just doing right, and nobody has to do right."

Dain was quiet for a while. Outside, through the shuttered window, came a sound that made them both turn: geese. Hundreds of geese, flying north over the city at midnight, honking in a rhythm that was almost — Elara tilted her head — almost like drums.

"The whole kingdom's lost its mind," Dain said.

"Or found it." Elara stood. "Thirty-one. Get me their names. And Dain—" she paused at the door— "thank you for picking up the badge."

"Don't thank me." He grimaced. "It's been ruining my sleep ever since. I figure handing you thirty-one traitors is the only way I get a full night's rest again."

* * *

Sef prepared the counter-ritual in a cellar, because all the important magic in his life had happened in cellars and he saw no reason to break tradition.

The room beneath the safehouse was cold and smelled of turnips, which Sef maintained was excellent for concentration, though no one else had ever verified this. Around him, arranged on the floor in careful chalk, lay the components: the stolen binding text, its pages soft as cloth from three hundred years of hands; seven candles he had bribed, begged, and mildly threatened to obtain; and a circle of salt that Mirei had helped pour and which was, Sef noted with professional approval, the most even salt circle he had seen from an apprentice in twenty years.

"Again," he said.

Mirei stood at the circle's edge, small and solemn, and recited the opening phrases. Not the old court-tongue — Casper had insisted on translating everything into plain speech, on the grounds that a ritual you don't understand is just expensive theater — but the meaning underneath it, word by word:

*"What was bound may be loosened. What was silenced may be sung. What was stolen may be returned — but only by the hand it trusts."*

"Good. Again."

By the fourth repetition she was stumbling, and by the sixth she sat down on a turnip crate and glared at him with an expression so exactly like her badger friend Bracken that Sef had to look away and fuss with the candles.

"In the tower," he said, settling beside her, "we drilled apprentices fourteen hours a day. If they faltered, they were flogged. If they excelled, they were used. I spent thirty years believing that was rigor." He handed her a dried apple slice, which she accepted with the gravity of a duchess receiving a title. "It turns out it was just cruelty wearing a robe. So. We rest when you're tired. We eat apples when apples are offered. And we go again tomorrow."

Mirei chewed thoughtfully. Then she held up her hands and signed — slow, careful, the vocabulary Casper had taught her: *Will it hurt her?*

Sef considered lying. He was, historically, quite good at it.

"Some," he signed back. "But she will not be alone. That is the whole design of it. Ten thousand voices sharing one weight. No single throat carries it all."

Mirei studied him for a long moment, the way children study adults when deciding whether to believe them, then signed one more phrase — a new one, one Sef hadn't taught her, one she must have invented herself from pieces:

*Then I will be one of the ten thousand.*

"You don't have a gift, little one. Not yet. Maybe not ever."

She shrugged, the universal gesture of children informing adults that adults are missing the point, and signed: *Neither does half the kingdom. They're coming anyway.*

Sef looked at her. Then he blew out the candles, because some lessons arrive finished, and there was nothing left to prepare that day except dinner.

* * *

On the ninth night of the moon's fattening, Casper climbed the ridge above the sanctuary and stopped breathing.

Roads. Every road she could see, glowing faintly under the stars with movement. To the west, a river of cattle, lowing softly, patient as tide. To the east, wild horses, manes flying, keeping pace with something that gleamed — and when her gift brushed it she gasped, because it was the old gray mare from the royal stables, limping gloriously, refusing every offer of rest, carrying her old bones north on legs that should have failed her miles ago. Overhead, the sky itself moved: ravens and crows and geese and owls, a weather system of wings. And threading between it all, on the ridgelines and in the treelines, the wolves and the hill dogs and the foxes and the shy, unlikely volunteers — deer, of all things, deer who had never in their history done anything together — all flowing toward the capital like blood returning to a heart.

Beside her, Elara stood in silence for a long time.

"I spent my career hunting the gifted," she said finally. "I told myself it was order. Keeping the wild things contained." She shook her head slowly. "Look at it. It was never contained. It was just waiting to be asked."

"Not asked," Casper said. Her voice cracked on the word. "Invited. There's a difference. Ask a beast to die for you and it'll think about it. Invite one to stand beside you—" she wiped her eyes with her sleeve, laughing at herself— "and apparently it brings friends."

Below them, Mirei stood in the yard with the hedgehog on her shoulder and the wolves' young one circling her ankles, and the blind matriarch of the rootbound lifted her face to the ridge as if she could see the roads, and perhaps she could, through her trees.

"They're coming," Casper whispered. "All of them. They actually came."

Elara put a hand on her shoulder. "One problem, though."

"One? Just one?"

"The capital has gates, walls, twelve thousand soldiers, and a bell that screams." Elara nodded north, where the city's towers caught the moonlight. "Whatever's coming — it can't get in. And neither can we. Not unless someone opens those gates from the inside."

Casper followed her gaze, and her stomach dropped, because she already knew the answer, had known it since the cellar, since the badge, since the beginning: someone had to be on the other side of that wall. Someone with a face the guards still saluted. Someone willing to walk through the front door of the cathedral and surrender.

Tomorrow, Elara would start writing her farewell letter. Tomorrow, the clock would begin its final turn.

But tonight, the kingdom moved, and the moon fattened, and somewhere in the royal menagerie an old badger's kin had already begun the long walk north.
