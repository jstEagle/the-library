## 31. Twelfth Moon Vigil

The twelfth moon rose fat and gold over the capital's eastern wall, and the whole city seemed to hold its breath beneath it.

Casper felt it in the animals first. The sparrows had gone quiet at dusk — not the ordinary quiet of sleep, but a listening quiet, hundreds of small bodies packed into the eaves of the tannery where they'd made their hideout, all facing the same direction. Toward the cathedral. Toward the bells.

*Tomorrow,* the oldest sparrow said, in the way sparrows say things, which is less a word than a weight. *Tomorrow-tomorrow.*

"Yes," Casper murmured back, and the sparrow ruffled its feathers, satisfied, and tucked its head under its wing.

She sat on the flat roof of the tannery with her knees drawn up, watching the moon climb. Below her, muffled through the floorboards, she could hear Sef muttering over his ritual diagrams for the hundredth time, and Dain sharpening a sword he probably wouldn't use, because the sound of it calmed him, and Mirei humming to the rats.

And somewhere behind her, boots on the ladder rungs. She didn't need magic to know whose. Elara Voss climbed ladders like she was breaching a wall — quick, economical, slightly annoyed at the ladder for existing.

"You're supposed to be sleeping," Elara said, settling beside her. "Sef says tomorrow will take everything you have."

"Sef also said the counter-ritual needed a circle of pure silver and a virgin mage of the seventh rank, and then he found out we had neither and revised it down to chalk and himself." Casper shrugged. "I trust Sef's desperation more than his predictions."

Elara huffed something that was almost a laugh. In the month since they'd come down out of the hills, since the rookery betrayal and Bracken—

Since Bracken. Casper still couldn't say his name inside her own head without the grief turning over like a stone in a streambed, showing a new sharp face. She was getting better at walking with it. Some days.

"Here." Elara held out a tin cup. Steam curled off it. "It's tea. Mostly. The good leaves ran out three days ago, so this is mostly the second brewing of the bad ones."

"Generous."

"I'm told generosity is my defining trait."

Casper took the cup. It was terrible. She drank it anyway, and Elara drank hers, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was its own kind of conversation. They'd gotten fluent in it, these past months. Silence with Elara was never empty; it was full of things both of them were choosing not to say yet.

The moon cleared the cathedral spires. Its light caught the great bells hanging dark and silent in their tower — the bells they'd sabotaged two weeks ago, the victory that had cost them their hideout by the river and very nearly cost them everything else. From here they looked harmless. Casper knew better. Tomorrow, if Sef was right, those bells would ring again — but rung backward, unrung, their suppression-song unwound thread by thread while ten thousand beasts and one small woman from the Hollow stood in the square below.

Her hands shook a little. She put the cup down.

"They're gathering," she said. "You know that, don't you? I can feel them at the edges. Wolves coming down out of the Grayreach. Cattle breaking fences. There's an old cart horse in a stable on Copper Lane who's been standing facing north for six hours, and when I asked him why, he said—" she stopped, and laughed despite herself, wet-eyed. "He said *the road remembers us.*"

Elara was quiet a moment. "I've got forty-one soldiers," she said. "Forty-one. Half of them Dain talked around, half came to me after the tribunal records leaked. Forty-one soldiers against the crown garrison, the Hunt remnant, whatever's left of Dain's bell-corps, and the thing wearing the king." She turned the cup in her scarred hands. "When I was hunt-captain, I could have mustered four hundred before breakfast. Funny what you lose when you grow a conscience."

"Funny what you gain."

"That too." Elara glanced sideways at her. "Mostly that."

* *

Below them, faintly, Mirei started to laugh.

Casper looked down through the roof hatch. The girl was sitting cross-legged on the floor surrounded by rats — seven of them, arranged in what appeared to be a semicircle, which meant Mirei had organized them, which meant Mirei was teaching them something. Her voice drifted up, thin and clear: she was telling them a story. Casper caught fragments. A fox. A lie about cheese. The rats were riveted.

Three months ago, Mirei hadn't spoken at all. Three months ago she'd flinched from Casper's shadow. Now she held court for rodents and fell asleep mid-sentence most nights, safe, mid-story, with someone always sitting near.

"She asked me today if you were going to die tomorrow," Elara said quietly.

Casper's chest tightened. "What did you tell her?"

"The truth. That I didn't know." Elara's jaw worked. "She looked at me the way she looks at things she's deciding whether to be afraid of, and then she said, 'Then somebody should say the important things tonight, in case there isn't a morning for it.' And then she went back to her rats." Elara exhaled slowly. "Eight years old. The interrogators broke her voice for half a year, and she comes out of it handing out wisdom like sweets."

"What are the important things, Captain?"

"Don't call me Captain."

"Why not? You'll be a general by winter if we live."

"If we live, I'm retiring to a farm and raising goats, and you can laugh at me about it forever." But she didn't smile when she said it. She set her cup down with deliberate care, the way she did when she was about to do something that frightened her — Casper had catalogued that gesture months ago, the way she'd once catalogued the warning-stamps of a nervous mare. Elara picked up her cup again. Put it down. Gave up on the cup entirely.

"Casper."

"Mm."

"I've been composing a speech for about eleven days. It's very good. It has structure. There's a bit in the middle where I acknowledge my failings as a person, and a bit near the end where I make clear this doesn't have to change anything between us, and there's a graceful exit built into the last line in case you want to take it." She stared hard at the moon. "I've delivered harder speeches to tribunals. I don't know why this one won't come out."

"Maybe skip to the end," Casper said. Her heart was doing something unhelpful. "The exit's the part I least want to hear."

That startled a real laugh out of Elara — short, rough, surprised out of her like a sneeze. Then she went still again, and turned, and looked at Casper properly, the way she did almost never: without the captain's assessment in it, without measuring threat or distance or angle of retreat. Just looking.

"When I took the convoy south," Elara said, "with you in chains, I told myself I was doing it because you were useful. Intelligence asset. Leverage. All the words that let me not look at what I was actually doing." Her voice stayed level, soldier-flat, but her hands didn't; they'd closed around each other like something drowning. "Somewhere on that road I stopped lying to myself, and it was your fault, and I have resented you for it every day since, and I would burn every bridge I have left rather than go back to being the woman who didn't know you."

The words hung in the cold air. Somewhere below, a rat squeaked commentary on Mirei's story.

"I'm not good at this," Elara went on, faster now, the speech finally tearing free of its structure. "I'm not — I was married once, did you know that? Two years, before the Hunt took everything soft in me. He said loving me was like loving a drawn bow. I thought he was being cruel. He was being accurate. And I don't— I can't promise softness, Casper. What I am is steady. What I am is the person who will be standing where she said she'd stand, at the hour she said she'd stand there, no matter what's coming. That's all I've got. I used to think it wasn't enough for anyone. Lately I think maybe it's exactly enough for someone who grew up belonging everywhere and known by no one, because—"

"Because it means being known," Casper finished softly.

Elara stopped.

Casper hadn't planned to speak. The words just came, the way true things do, plain as water. "You've been known by me since the convoy. Since the night you cut my chains and burned your career and didn't even hesitate, and I lay in the mud thinking, *oh no, oh no, this is the worst possible person to love, she's honorable, she'll get herself killed.*" She laughed, shaky. "I've been composing my own speech for eleven days too. Mine has worse structure. Mine is mostly just your name repeated at intervals."

"*Casper.*"

"There it is. That's the whole speech, really."

For a moment neither of them moved. The moonlight lay across the rooftop like a blade laid down — not raised, not sheathed. Set aside.

Then Elara reached over and took Casper's hand. Not gently; Elara didn't really do gentle. Firmly. The way you hold on to something you've decided not to lose.

"Not quite love," she said. "Or — more than. I don't have the word. I've read a lot of books and none of them have the word."

"It doesn't need a word," Casper said. "It needs a vow. Words are what Sef does. Vows are what we do."

"A vow." Elara tested the shape of it. "All right. Hear mine, then. Tomorrow, whatever happens in that square — if the binding works, if it fails, if the thing in the king looks at you the way I know it might — I will be at your shoulder. Not behind you. Not guarding you from a distance like some order I can hide behind. At your shoulder. And if it goes wrong, I will spend my last breath making sure it costs them dearly, and if it goes right, I will spend the rest of my life learning how to be a person instead of a weapon, and you're going to help me, and I apologize in advance."

Casper was crying now, quietly, the way she'd learned to in the Hollow — tears that made no sound, that no one could report. "Hear mine. Tomorrow I open the gift all the way. Every beast at once. Sef thinks it might burn the gift out of me even if nothing else goes wrong. Maybe it kills me. Probably it unmakes the only thing I ever thought I was." She turned her hand over and laced her fingers through Elara's. "But I vow I will not be afraid of it. Because fear is for people with something to protect, and I finally have that, and it's sitting right here holding my hand and pretending she isn't terrified too."

"I'm not pretending," Elara said. "I'm managing it. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Marginally."

They leaned together, shoulder to shoulder, two women watching the last full moon before the thirteenth. Below them the city slept badly, dreaming under a stolen king. Above them the stars wheeled, indifferent and ancient. And along every road in the kingdom, in ditches and hedgerows and dark forests, eyes were opening, ears were pricking, and old instincts older than language were pulling beasts toward the capital like a tide toward shore.

* * *

They didn't sleep, in the end. Around midnight Mirei climbed the ladder with her blanket dragging and wedged herself between them without asking, the way children and cats do, and neither of them had the heart to send her down. She fell asleep with her head on Casper's knee and her feet against Elara's thigh, and Elara — the terror of three kingdoms, the woman who'd once made a tribunal of hardened witch-hunters flinch — sat absolutely motionless for two hours so as not to wake her.

Around the third watch, Sef came up with the last of the tea and a face like a man carrying bad news he'd promised not to spill until morning. He looked at the three of them — the fugitive, the traitor, the child — and something in his expression softened and soured at once.

"I need to check the ritual anchors one more time," he said. "Which means I need Casper. And Elara — a rider just passed on the east road. Royal colors. Moving fast toward the palace."

Elara was on her feet before he finished. "How fast?"

"Fast enough that they weren't riding for routine. Fast enough that they were riding for *tomorrow*." Sef pushed his hair out of his eyes, and for the first time since Casper had known him, the runaway court mage looked genuinely frightened. "There's something else. The sparrows brought word from the square. They've been setting up since dusk — scaffolds, braziers, a platform before the cathedral steps. And Casper — they're building cages. Dozens of them. Big ones."

Casper went cold. "Big ones for what?"

Sef met her eyes, and she saw he already knew, and hated knowing.

"For the gathering," he said. "They know the beasts are coming. Someone told them — or something did. They're not preparing a coronation renewal, Casper. They're preparing a trap. And I don't think it's for the wolves."

Down in the dark of the tannery, unnoticed by any of them, the oldest sparrow lifted its head from under its wing and looked toward the cathedral, and did not sleep either.
