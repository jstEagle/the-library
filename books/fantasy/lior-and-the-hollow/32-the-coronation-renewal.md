## 32. The Coronation Renewal

Elara Voss had been arrested twice in her life. The first time she'd been eleven years old and had stolen a pie off a windowsill in her home village, and the constable had laughed so hard at her furious dignity that he'd let her go with a warning and half the pie.

The second time was now, and nobody was laughing.

She stood in the great square before the Cathedral of the Ascension with her wrists bound in iron that hummed against her skin — not ordinary iron, she noted. Warded. Someone in the armory had finally read a book. Four soldiers flanked her, and Dain stood at her shoulder wearing his captain's sash like it had been cut for someone else's shoulders, which, to be fair, it had. It had been cut for hers.

"You don't have to do this," he'd said, an hour ago, when she'd walked out of the alley mouth with her hands raised and her sword left behind on purpose. "You could run. We'd never—"

"Dain." She'd looked at him then, and something in her face must have reached him, because he'd gone quiet. "You know exactly what happens tonight. You've read the orders. I'm going to be inside that cathedral when it does. The only question is whether I walk in as a prisoner or as a ghost."

He hadn't understood. He still didn't, probably. But he'd bound her wrists gently, which told her more than any speech could have, and now here they were.

The thirteenth moon rose over the capital like a coin held up to the light — huge, white, and utterly indifferent to what was about to happen beneath it. Elara had served under this moon for twenty years. She had marched under it, bled under it, buried friends under it. Tonight it looked less like a moon and more like an eye.

*Good,* she thought. *Look closely. You're going to miss.*

The square was packed. Not with the usual festival crowds — with soldiers, three rings of them, pikes angled outward, and beyond them the common people pressed against barricades, craning to see. Word had spread through the city the way word always did: the coronation renewal was not a renewal at all but a sealing, and whatever sat on the throne intended to sit there forever. Sef's sparrows had done their work well. Half the rumors were wrong, but wrong in useful directions, and the crowd's murmur had a sound Elara recognized from border towns on the edge of revolt.

Above them all, the cathedral doors stood open, spilling gold light onto the steps.

"Captain Voss." Dain's voice was low. "The tribunal wants you processed through the holding cells first. Standard procedure."

"The tribunal," Elara said, "wants me where I can't see anything. Tell them the prisoner requested to witness the sacred rite as an act of penance. Quote scripture at them. They love that."

"That's manipulation."

"Yes. It's also how you got your sash. Go on."

He hesitated — one heartbeat, two — and then turned and gave the order, and Elara felt the small, absurd satisfaction of a plan surviving its first contact with bureaucracy.

* * *

They marched her up the steps between ranks of the Honor Guard, and Elara used every step of it. She counted guards. She counted archers — forty on the roofline, fewer than expected; someone had pulled men to search the undercity, thanks to the false trail Mirei's sparrows had laid through the sewers, bless that child and her terrifying aptitude for misdirection. She counted the bells hanging silent in the tower above, their clappers bound since the sabotage three nights ago, and felt a flicker of pride that she was careful to keep entirely off her face. Casper's crew had done that. *Casper's crew.* When had Elara started thinking of herself as part of a crew?

Somewhere around the third step, she stopped counting and started simply looking, because there, at the top of the stairs, framed in the doorway, stood the king.

Aldric wore full ceremonial regalia — the crown of nine points, the mantle stitched with the sigils of every province, the scepter that no living hand had lifted in three years because the thing inside him didn't need props. He stood very straight. Too straight. There was a stillness to him that human bodies didn't have, the stillness of a puppet whose strings had been drawn taut, and yet —

And yet his eyes moved. They swept the crowd, the guards, the sky, and when they passed over Elara they paused, just slightly, the way a man pauses when he hears his name called from another room.

*He's in there,* she thought, and her chest ached. *All this time, he's been in there, watching through his own eyes.*

The crown-thing's gaze settled on her and stayed. And Elara Voss, traitor, disgraced hunt-captain, woman who had burned her entire life down in a rainstorm eight months ago, did the bravest stupid thing available to her.

She smiled at it. Warmly. The way you smile at a colleague who once stole credit for your work.

Something behind the king's eyes flickered. Recognition, maybe. Or appetite.

"Ah," said the king, and his voice carried across the square without effort, smooth as poured honey over something with teeth. "The prodigal captain. We wondered when you would come home to us."

"I was never yours," Elara said clearly, letting the whole square hear it. "But I'm here now. Isn't that what matters?"

The crowd murmured. The guards shifted. The thing wearing Aldric studied her for a long moment, and Elara kept her breathing even and her heart rate boring, because she knew — Sef had drilled it into her for weeks — that it could taste strong emotion the way a shark tastes blood in water. Fear, fury, hope: all of it carried. So she gave it nothing but the flat, pleasant calm of a soldier reporting for inspection.

"Bring her," said the king. "Let her watch what devotion looks like."

*Oh, I intend to,* Elara thought, and let herself be led inside.

* * *

The cathedral interior was worse than she remembered, and she remembered it being bad.

She'd attended services here as a young officer, back when the vaulted ceiling had seemed inspiring rather than oppressive, back before she'd learned what the murals actually depicted — the Ascension of the First King, they called it, a radiant figure crowned by descending light. Now, knowing what she knew, the mural read differently. A light descending on a crowned head. A voice pouring into an open mouth. The artists had painted the possession four hundred years ago and called it holy, and everyone had applauded.

Prisoners knelt along the side aisles — gifted ones, taken in the last sweeps, their wrists warded, their faces hollow. Elara counted eleven. Her stomach turned. Among them, near the end, a girl of perhaps fifteen with a sparrow perched impossibly calm on her shoulder, and Elara caught the bird's eye and gave the smallest nod. The sparrow preened. Message received: *we see you. Hold on.*

At the front, below the altar, the ritual space had been prepared. A circle of white chalk and silver inlay, old and deep — older than the cathedral itself, Sef had said, cut into the foundation stones when this had been something else entirely. Court mages stood at its points, seven of them, robed in white, and Elara searched their faces for Sef among them and found him.

He looked terrible. He always looked terrible, but tonight he had achieved new heights: pale, sweating, hands trembling as he arranged the ritual instruments. To anyone watching, he was a broken man performing his duty under duress. Only Elara knew that the trembling was real and the duty was theater, that beneath his robes were the counter-sigils inked onto his skin in a paste made of ash and mare's-hair and sheer stubbornness, and that the instrument he was adjusting so nervously was the binding-anchor itself, and that he had spent three nights loosening it by fractions so small that only a mage who had helped build the original ritual would ever notice.

*Steady, old man,* she thought at him. He didn't look up. His hands shook harder. Perfect.

The choir began to sing, and Elara's blood went cold, because she recognized the melody. Everyone did. It was the Coronation Hymn, sung at every crowning for four centuries — except tonight the words had been changed, subtly, syllables shifted, and where the hymn should have risen toward joy it coiled downward instead, into a register that made the hair on her arms stand up. It wasn't a hymn. It was a summons. It had probably always been a summons, and the congregation had been singing the entity's welcome for four hundred years without knowing it.

*Four hundred years of choir practice,* she thought, with a flicker of gallows humor that Casper would have appreciated, *and it still sounds like a cat in a sack. Some things even ancient evil can't fix.*

They seated her in a chair at the front, of all things — the king's order, apparently. "Let the traitor have the best view of her own defeat." The guards flanked her. The warded irons stayed on. And directly across the nave, elevated on the dais, the throne waited, empty, with the crown of nine points resting on a cushion beside it like a sleeping animal.

The ceremony began.

Elara had planned to spend the ritual cataloguing exits. Instead she spent the first hour watching the king, because the king was watching the door.

Every time the great doors opened — late nobles slipping in, guards rotating posts — Aldric's head turned. Every time, the same small pause. The same listening stillness. He wasn't watching for assassins. Elara had watched men watch for assassins; it looked like tension. This looked like longing.

*It's waiting for her,* she realized. *It knows she has to come. It's been waiting for weeks, standing at the window of its own skull, listening for a voice.*

Which meant, she thought with grim satisfaction, that the most powerful entity in the kingdom had spent the evening doing exactly what she wanted it to do: looking at the wrong door.

Because Casper Joubert was not coming through the doors.

* * *

An hour in, the pain started.

Elara had expected it eventually — Sef had warned her that the counter-sigils would begin to burn as the ritual built, that the entity's power gathering in the circle would press against them like floodwater against a door. What she hadn't expected was the discipline required to sit through it with a pleasant expression while two guards watched her for exactly the flinching that would get her dragged out of the room.

It came in waves, timed to the choir. Each verse, the pressure climbed. The inked lines on her forearms, hidden beneath her sleeves, heated like wires in a fire, and sweat ran down her spine, and Elara Voss fixed her eyes on the mural of the First King and thought about pie.

It was a strange thing to think about, under the circumstances. But her mind went there anyway — the windowsill, the warm crust, the constable laughing, eleven years old and certain the world was simple. Before the Hunt. Before the oaths. Before she'd learned that the crown she'd served was a mouth.

*You did the best you could with what you knew,* Casper had told her once, in the sanctuary, when Elara had lain awake listing her sins like a merchant counting debt. *And then you learned better, and you did better. That's the whole trick. Nobody gets a clean ledger. They just get a chance to add to it.*

Tonight, Elara supposed, she was adding a very large entry.

The choir reached the verse where the congregation rose, and the whole cathedral stood — hundreds of voices, the nobility in their finery, the guards, even the prisoners hauled to their feet by their warders. Elara stood too. Across the nave, Sef's hands had stopped shaking. That frightened her more than the burning arms. Steady meant ready, and ready meant soon, and soon meant everything after this moment would happen very fast.

The king descended from the dais.

He walked slowly down the nave, and the crowd parted before him like water, and Elara saw what none of the courtiers could see: the way his shadow moved a half-beat behind his body, the way the candle flames leaned toward him as he passed, hungry. He stopped at the ritual circle's edge. The seven mages bowed. Sef bowed lowest, and Elara marveled, not for the first time, at the courage it took for a man to grovel beautifully at the thing he was about to betray.

"The vessel is prepared," Sef said, and his voice only cracked a little. "The binding will hold forever."

"Forever," the king repeated, tasting the word. And then, softly, in a voice pitched for no one — but Elara had spent twenty years reading lips across battlefields — the thing added: "*No more hunger. No more listening. No more waiting for a voice that will not come.*"

It thought she wasn't coming. It thought the girl had fled, or died, or lost her nerve. Every hour of delay Elara had bought with her surrender — every procedural objection, every scripture quote, every slow march up the steps — had been spent convincing it that the threat had dissolved.

The king turned. His gaze found Elara again across the nave, and this time he smiled, and it was Aldric's smile, the real one, worn like a borrowed coat, and somehow that was the most horrifying thing yet.

"Captain Voss," the entity said pleasantly, loudly enough for the court to hear. "You surrendered to witness our triumph. Speak, then. Any last words before the renewal? The court loves a confession."

The cathedral went silent. Hundreds of faces turned to her. Dain stood at the far wall, rigid, his hand nowhere near his sword and his eyes screaming *don't*. In the side aisle, the sparrow-girl had gone white. Above, through the high windows, the thirteenth moon had cleared the rooftops and hung directly over the altar, full and white and close enough to touch.

Elara rose from her chair. The warded irons clinked. Her arms burned like brands. And she smiled the smile she'd given it on the steps, warm and easy, and said:

"Just one. Your Majesty — you might want to check the roof."

Confusion rippled through the court. Guards craned upward. The entity's smile faltered for the first time in three years, its attention snapping skyward, to the bell tower, to the silenced bells, to the shadows along the roofline where — Elara saw it land, saw the whole cathedral see it — a single goat stood silhouetted against the enormous moon, chewing thoughtfully, wearing what appeared to be a garland of ravens.

And beneath the floor, beneath the flagstones, beneath four hundred years of chalk and silver, the city's animals began to arrive.

Elara sat back down, folded her bound hands in her lap, and waited for the loudest voice in the world.
