## 33. Every Beast at Once

The square had never held so many hearts.

Casper stood on the cathedral steps with her back to the great doors and felt them all — not heard, exactly, not yet, but felt, the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth. The square of Vael Corren was packed shoulder to shoulder with courtiers in their finery, soldiers in their grey, priests swinging censers that smelled of burnt myrrh, and above them all the thirteenth full moon rising fat and white as a peeled egg.

And beneath all of it, under the cobbles, under the city itself, the animals were coming.

She could feel them the way Bracken had once described feeling rain three valleys off: a pressure, a gathering, a tide with no shore yet. Wolves from the northern passes. Cattle walking out of their pastures with ropes still dragging. Ravens in numbers that made the sky look bruised. Horses breaking fences. Rats pouring up through the sewers in silver rivers. And sparrows — Mirei's sparrows, ten thousand small brown hearts beating in unison over the rooftops like a second pulse for the whole kingdom.

"They're coming," Mirei said beside her. The girl's hand was cold in hers. "All of them. I can feel them wanting to be brave."

"You don't have to be here," Casper said, for the hundredth time.

"Yes I do." Mirei's chin came up, stubborn as a goat refusing a gate. "You told me once that being scared and doing it anyway is just what courage looks like from the inside. You don't get to take it back now."

Casper almost laughed. Almost. Her mouth was too dry, and her heart was hammering somewhere up around her collarbone, and below her, filling the square like water fills a bowl, the coronation renewal was beginning.

* * *

It had gone wrong almost immediately, which was Elara's fault, or her gift, depending on how you looked at it.

The plan had been simple, which should have been their first warning. Sef would slip beneath the cathedral into the old binding vaults with Dain's stolen key-ring and begin unraveling the ritual from underneath. Elara would walk in openly — surrender herself, loudly, spectacularly — and buy time by making herself too valuable to kill until the moment came. And Casper would stand on the steps, in front of everyone, and when the moon cleared the cathedral spire, she would open her gift all the way for the first time in her life.

"Simple," Sef had said, rolling his eyes. "You've described a plan with four separate ways to die and called it simple."

"That's what makes it simple," Elara had said. "Only one thing has to go right."

Three things had already gone wrong instead. The bells they'd sabotaged two nights ago had been repaired faster than expected — half of them were ringing again, that low suppressive hum crawling under Casper's skin like insects. The entity inside King Aldric had grown suspicious; its eyes, pale as skimmed milk, kept sweeping the crowd as though tasting it. And Elara —

Elara was standing in chains in the middle of the square, having walked in through the west gate at sundown with her hands raised and her former badge of office pinned deliberately upside-down on her breast, announcing to every guard within earshot that she was Captain Elara Voss, traitor to the Hunt, and she demanded to speak to whoever was wearing the king.

That had gotten everyone's attention. That had gotten the square packed twice as fast as any coronation renewal would have managed on its own. And it had gotten her dragged before the dais, where she now knelt in her chains with her spine straight as a lance while the crown-thing looked down at her with Aldric's ruined face and smiled.

"Captain Voss," it said, and its voice was the worst thing in the square — Aldric's warm baritone stretched over something that moved underneath like eels under ice. "We wondered when you would come home."

"I'm not home," Elara said clearly. "I'm early."

A ripple of confused laughter went through the crowd and died quickly. Courtiers knew better than to laugh at the king, even when the king made jokes. Especially then.

Casper pressed back against the cathedral doors, hood up, one of a dozen prisoners-in-waiting flanked by guards who thought she was the Hunt's prize catch, delivered for the ceremony. That part of the plan had worked beautifully. She was close enough to see the sweat on Elara's jaw. Close enough to see the king's hands resting on the arms of the throne, fingers too long, nails slightly wrong, tapping tapping tapping in a rhythm that wasn't human.

*Come on, Sef,* she thought. *Whatever you're doing down there, do it faster.*

* * *

The ceremony unfolded around her like a slow fever dream.

Priests in white carried the relic-crown on a cushion of cloth-of-gold. A choir sang the old hymn of renewal, the one about the king's voice being the voice of the realm, and Casper had to bite the inside of her cheek not to laugh bitterly at the irony — if only they knew whose voice it actually was. Incense smoke curled upward and hung strangely in the air, as though even the smoke was waiting for something.

And the animals kept coming.

She felt the first wolves reach the city outskirts as the choir reached its second verse — a pack of nine, moving fast and silent along the river road, their minds arriving in her awareness like lanterns being lit one by one down a dark street. Then cattle, a whole herd of them, plodding patiently through the south gate while the gate-guards stared and, mercifully, did nothing, because what do you do about cows. Ravens began settling on every rooftop and statue and gibbet in the plaza, hundreds of them, thousands, silent as held breath.

The crowd noticed. Of course they noticed. A murmur ran through the square like wind through wheat. Someone screamed when a stag stepped out of an alleyway, calm as Sunday, and stood looking at the cathedral.

"Hold," the crown-thing said, and its voice cracked across the square like a whip, and the crowd froze. It rose from the throne, and Casper saw the courtiers nearest the dais lean away without seeming to know they were doing it, the way flowers lean from frost. "The beasts gather because the renewal draws near. They sense the majesty of the crown. This is fitting. This is *right*."

It's lying, Casper thought. It doesn't know why they're coming. It can't hear them at all.

That was the thing's blind spot, the one crack in all its terrible patience: it wore a king's voice but it had no gift for beast-speech. It could command men. It could not hear anything else. Everything alive in this square that wasn't human was invisible to it.

Everything except her.

Its milk-pale eyes found her across the crowd, and stopped.

Casper's blood turned to well-water, cold and still. The thing tilted Aldric's head, slowly, like a bird considering something shiny, and for three heartbeats the entire square seemed to hold its breath around her.

Then a priest stumbled, dropped the relic-cushion, and the moment shattered into commotion, and the eyes moved on.

*Breathe,* she told herself. *Breathe, you absolute goat.*

* * *

The moon cleared the spire.

She felt it happen more than saw it — a change in the weight of the air, a silvery pressure against her skin, and beneath her feet, far below, faint but unmistakable, a vibration: Sef, in the vaults, beginning to sing the counter-ritual. Old words. True words. Words his own master had taught him and he had spent a lifetime regretting.

The crown-thing lifted the relic-crown high. The choir swelled. The bells began their great suppressive peal, and Casper felt the hum claw at the edges of her gift like fingers at a shuttered window.

"Now," Elara breathed, kneeling in her chains, not looking at Casper at all, her lips barely moving. "Casper. Now."

Casper stepped forward.

Out of the line of prisoners. Past the guard who grabbed for her arm and missed because Mirei, bless her sharp little elbows, trod on his instep with all forty pounds of determined child. Up onto the low balustrade at the edge of the steps, where the whole square could see her — a small, thin girl in a borrowed cloak, standing against the moon.

"Hey!" someone shouted. "Guards! There's a—"

Casper opened her gift.

* * *

Later, she would try to describe it, and fail, and try again, and fail better.

It was like this: all her life she had lived in a house with one window, and through that window she had seen one field, one badger, one valley. Now every wall fell away at once and she was standing in the open sky.

Ten thousand minds arrived.

No — more. Far more. The wolves at the gates, yes, and the cattle in the square, and the ravens blackening the roofs, and the sparrows wheeling overhead in their vast living cloud. But also horses in the royal stables turning their heads toward the square as one. Also dogs in courtyards across the city lifting their voices. Also rats in the walls and mice in the granaries and moths drunk on lamplight and fish holding still in the cold river, all of them, everything with breath and blood in the whole sprawl of the capital and the roads beyond it and the wild country beyond that, the deer in the deep forest and the rootbound in their sanctuary and — impossibly, achingly far away — a thread of familiar gruff warmth from the north country, badgers, Bracken's cousins three generations removed, turning their blunt faces toward the moon.

They all spoke at once.

And it didn't deafen her. That was the miracle of it, the thing no one could have warned her about: it didn't deafen her, it *enlarged* her. Each mind was a single note and together they were not noise but chord, vast and layered and harmonizing against every law she knew, and she was not listening to the music, she was the space the music happened in.

*Casper.* The wolves' greeting, fierce and glad. *Casper-Casper-Casper,* the sparrows, quick as rain. *Little speaker,* rumbled the old lead cow, calm as a grandmother. *Speaker. Speaker. SPEAKER,* said ten thousand throats, and the sound of it was not sound at all, it was meaning poured directly into her chest, and she laughed out loud in the middle of the frozen square because it was the most beautiful thing that had ever happened to anyone, anywhere, ever.

The square had gone utterly silent. Every human face was turned up to her. Guards with drawn swords, forgetting to advance. Priests with their hymns dying in their throats. The crown-thing on the dais, rigid, its head snapping side to side, trying to find the source of a disturbance it could feel but not hear.

"What is this?" it snarled. "What is—"

And every animal in the square answered it at once.

The roar was not a roar. It was a bellow and a howl and a shriek and a chitter and the deep subsonic groan of cattle and the whinny of horses three streets away, all of it carrying the same word, the same meaning, shaped by Casper's will into something no beast had ever said aloud in a human square in the history of the kingdom:

***NO.***

Courtiers screamed and fainted. Soldiers staggered. The incense smoke scattered as though slapped. And Casper stood on the balustrade with tears streaming down her face and her arms spread wide, holding all of it, channeling all of it, the loudest voice alive, and she said:

"He is not your king. He is not your voice. He stole a man's throat and he cannot even hear yours. But I can. I have always been able to. And tonight, so help me, the whole realm speaks with one tongue — and it says *give him back*."

* * *

The thing wearing the crown looked at her then, truly looked, and Casper felt its attention like a searchlight.

"There you are," it said softly, and the kindness in its voice was the most frightening thing yet. "The little hollow-valley girl. Do you know, we have wanted to meet you for years? We felt you once, far away, through our hounds' dreams. Such a rare instrument. Such a *waste*, buried in dirt and goats." It descended the dais steps slowly, courtiers fleeing from its path. "Join us. Speak for us. Every beast in the realm at your call, every ear in the kingdom bent to your voice — you have tasted it now, haven't you? How it feels to be heard by everything. We could give you that forever. No burning. No hiding. A throne beside a throne."

And the awful truth, the truth she would carry privately all her life, was that for one half of one heartbeat, she wanted it.

Then she felt Mirei's small hand find hers. And she felt the wolves' steady patience, and the sparrows' bright courage, and the old cow's grandmotherly calm, and somewhere very far away a thread of gruff badger love that death had not managed to make quiet, and the half-heartbeat passed.

"No," Casper said, gently. "But thank you. That's the difference between us, you see. You collect voices because you have none. I've got more than I know what to do with."

She squeezed Mirei's hand. Below the earth, Sef's song rose to a crescendo. Above her, the thirteenth moon burned like an open door.

"But I'll tell you what I *will* do," she said, and opened herself wider than she had ever dared, wider than safe, wider than wise, taking hold of the mare's name-song in her memory — *Aldric, Aldric, laughing boy, apple-thief, kind hands* — and pouring it into ten thousand waiting throats. "I'm going to say his name."

The crown-thing lunged for her.

Every beast in the realm moved at once.
