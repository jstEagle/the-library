## 34. The Unbinding

The square was full of beasts.

That was the thought Casper kept returning to, absurdly, as the thirteenth moon climbed over the cathedral spires — that if you had told the girl on the hillside with her goats that one day she would stand in a capital city with ten thousand animals pressed shoulder to shoulder around her, she would have laughed, and then checked her fences twice.

They filled the great square and spilled down every street that fed it: wolves from the northern passes standing oddly patient beside plow horses; cattle lowing in long, rolling waves; ravens so thick on the rooftops that the slate looked like it had grown feathers; rats in their thousands along the gutters, which should have been horrifying and instead felt like a tide coming in. Somewhere near the fountain, a bear. Nobody had invited the bear. The bear had simply decided.

And above them all, the moon — full, enormous, thirteenth of its name, hanging over the cathedral where King Aldric sat upon his throne of white stone while the court performed its coronation renewal, and the thing wearing his crown drank deeper.

Casper stood on the cathedral steps because Elara had put her there.

"Right here," Elara had said, an hour ago, pressing her back against the cold stone as the bells began to toll. "Where everyone can see you. That's the whole point. You're not sneaking anywhere tonight."

"I've never done anything *not* sneaking in my life," Casper had said. "It feels indecent."

"Good. Be indecent loudly."

Elara had then walked down those same steps, alone, unarmed except for her old captain's badge, and surrendered herself to the guards at the doors — buying time, buying attention, buying Casper the minutes she needed. Casper had watched her go and felt something in her chest crack open in a way she suspected would never fully close again.

Now Sef's voice came to her through the sparrows — he'd spent three weeks teaching them to carry single words, and they were terrible at it, but *below* and *ready* and *now-ish* were within their abilities.

*Below. Ready. Now-ish.*

"Now-ish," Casper muttered. "Very reassuring, Sef."

The mare's song rose in her memory like water finding its level. Old Blossom, dying in the royal stables, her breath rattling, who had given Casper the last gift a dying creature can give: the king's true name-song, learned across thirty years of quiet mornings, the sound Aldric had made when he was young and whole and laughed at his own jokes before anyone could stop him. It wasn't words. It was better than words. It was the shape of a man's soul sung by someone who had loved him without wanting anything back.

Casper closed her eyes. She let the valley rise in her — the goats, the mill, Bracken grumbling about bad knees and superior badger wisdom — and then she opened the door she had kept locked her entire life.

She spoke to every beast in the realm at once.

---

There is no honest way to describe it. Language is a rope bridge built for one traveler, and what came through her was a flood.

Ten thousand minds touched hers simultaneously — not merging, thank every merciful star, but leaning in, the way a room leans toward a storyteller. She felt the wolves' cold patience and the cattle's slow warmth and the ravens' bright, greedy curiosity and the rats' fierce underground loyalty and the bear's vast, unhurried opinion that all of this was probably fine. They were waiting. They had been gathering for days on instinct older than language, and now they turned their ten thousand faces toward the small woman on the steps, and waited for her voice.

Her nose was bleeding. She noticed this distantly, the way you notice weather in another country.

*Blossom's song,* she told them. *I need you to sing it with me. All of you. I can't carry it alone.*

And the flood answered — not in words, never in words, but in assent, in the way a herd turns together, in the way a flock lifts off all at once as if pulled by a single string. Ten thousand throats opened. Ten thousand voices took up a dead mare's lullaby for a living king.

The sound had no air in it. It traveled through the ground and the stone and the marrow of every living thing in the square, and it went up the cathedral walls like ivy, and it found the crown.

---

Inside the cathedral, the renewal ritual stopped.

Casper felt it happen — felt the crown-thing notice her the way a spider notices the vibration of its web. It had been drinking from Aldric for three years, patient and deep, and now something was singing to the part of him it had buried, and it did not like it. It did not like it at all.

It pushed back.

The pressure hit her like a wall of cold — ancient, hungry, *arrogant*, a presence that had worn kings before Aldric and expected to wear them after. It poured contempt into her mind in a voice like frost forming: *little beast-monger, little valley rat, you are a candle arguing with winter.*

"Yeah," Casper said aloud, blood streaming freely from her nose now, gripping the stone balustrade with both hands. "But there's rather a lot of us candles."

She fed the song harder. Through the wolves' throats it gained teeth. Through the horses', endurance. Through the ravens', a wildness no cage had ever taught out of them. Through the rats — the glorious, despised, indispensable rats — it gained the patience of things that live underneath everything and outlast everything. The name-song swelled from a lullaby into a gale, and beneath it, faint and flickering, Casper heard what she had come to hear:

A human voice. Begging.

*Aldric.* Alive. Drowned but alive, reaching up through three years of dark toward a sound he knew — the sound of his own laughter, sung back to him by the only creature in the palace who had ever loved him plainly.

*Hold on,* Casper told him, the way she'd tell a frightened goat in a storm. *I'm coming. Don't make me chase you. I hate chasing.*

Something that might have been a laugh, or a sob, moved in the drowning dark.

---

Below, in the crypts where the original binding had been woven, Sef worked.

She couldn't see him. She didn't need to. The sparrows reported in fragments — *Sef. Candles. Many candles. Sef swearing. Big word* — and she felt the binding begin to loosen the way you feel a knot start to give: a small, wrong tension in the world easing thread by thread. His old master had built the binding to be temporary, reversible only by a voice the king trusted, and Sef had spent twenty years carrying the guilt of helping build it. Tonight he unraveled his own work stitch by stitch, and Casper sent him, through a breathless sparrow, the only message that mattered:

*Tell him I said thank you. And tell him if he blows himself up I will be extremely annoyed.*

The sparrow delivered this as: *Sef. Annoyed. Extremely.* Which Sef later swore made him laugh at the worst possible moment, nearly costing them everything, so really the birds deserved some of the credit.

---

The crown-thing stopped being subtle.

It tore itself free of pretense and came at her with everything — a flood of cold pouring down the cathedral steps, snuffing torches, making guards drop their pikes and press their hands over their ears. The gathered beasts cried out; the song wavered; somewhere in the crowd a horse went down screaming, and Casper felt every blow land in her own chest as though the ten thousand throats were her throat, the ten thousand hearts her heart.

This was the price. She had known it since the Rootbound elder first read the old rite aloud. To unbind the king, the voice must give itself entirely — every beast-speech burned away in the speaking, magic stripped to the root, silence forever after.

*Fine,* Casper thought, and meant it, and was surprised how much she meant it. *Take it. Take all of it. I know what silence costs. I lived there my whole life. But I'm not leaving him in yours.*

She stopped holding anything back.

She gave the song her mother's lost lullabies. Her father's whistled work tunes. Bracken's grumbling, which was honestly half her vocabulary. Mirei's small hand-signs. Elara saying *be indecent loudly*. Every conversation she had ever stolen with the secret half of her heart, she poured into ten thousand open mouths, and the name-song became a pillar of sound that had nothing left of caution in it, and it struck the crown like dawn striking glass.

The binding below snapped — she felt Sef feel it, felt his cry of triumph and terror both.

And the crown-thing *shrieked*.

Not through the air. Through everything. A sound like a kingdom's worth of nails dragged down a kingdom's worth of slate, a sound that bent the moonlight, a sound the beasts answered not with fear but with fury — ten thousand voices closing around it like a fist, like a pack, like a family that has decided, finally and forever, that the thing hurting their people stops tonight.

It ripped out of Aldric.

Casper saw it leave — saw it with eyes that were no longer entirely only hers: a shape of cold and hunger tearing loose from a slumped figure on a white throne, shrieking, thrashing, suddenly *small*, suddenly starving, suddenly with nowhere to wear. It screamed once more, a sound of pure offended disbelief, as if the universe had broken a promise to it.

Then it fled — a streak of darkness pouring off the cathedral roof and into the night, toward the trees, toward anywhere, and the gathered beasts surged after it with a roar like the sea.

The song ended.

---

Casper came back to herself on her hands and knees on the cathedral steps, and the world was quiet.

Not the good kind of quiet. Not the valley's warm, listening hush. Empty quiet. She reached for the goats of her childhood and found no answer. She reached for the sparrows wheeling overhead — her spies, her friends, her ridiculous feathered lieutenants — and heard nothing at all. One landed two feet from her, cocked its head, chirped something small and ordinary, and flew off, and it was like watching someone she loved walk past her without recognition.

"Oh," she said. Her voice sounded very far away. "Oh, that's — right. Yes. That was the deal."

She was crying, she realized. Also her nose was still bleeding, also her hands wouldn't stop shaking, also somewhere a king was waking up for the first time in three years. There was a great deal happening and she had, apparently, spent the last of herself on all of it.

Footsteps on the stairs. Guards, running — toward the throne room, past her, paying the bloody, trembling woman on the steps no mind at all. Then slower footsteps, uneven ones, and Elara dropped to her knees beside her, badgeless, bloodied at the temple, alive, *there*, and caught her before she tipped sideways.

"You did it," Elara said. "You impossible, stubborn, goat-herding miracle. You did it."

"Did I?" Casper asked. "I can't hear anything anymore. Everything's gone quiet. Elara, everything's—"

"I know." Elara's arms tightened around her. "I know. We'll figure it out. That's what we do now. We figure things out *together*, loudly, indecently."

"That's not how the phrase goes."

"It is tonight."

Above them, the cathedral doors burst open and light spilled out, and a voice Casper had only ever heard drowned and begging now spoke hoarse and human and awake, asking — of all the possible questions a newly freed king might ask — "*Where is she? Where is the girl who sang?*"

Elara started to laugh, wet and disbelieving. Casper tried to stand, failed, tried again.

And then the night, which had been celebrating, went wrong.

The beasts at the edges of the square — the outriders, the wolves and ravens chasing the fleeing dark — suddenly wheeled as one, and a new sound cut through the streets behind them: a child's scream, high and terrified, from the direction of the sanctuary wagons where Mirei waited with Dain.

The entity had nowhere left to wear a crown.

So it had gone looking for something smaller.

Casper was running before she finished deciding to, Elara's hand locked in hers, both of them stumbling down the steps into the dark — and behind them the whole gathered host of the realm's beasts turned as one body and followed.
