## 35. The Thing Without a Crown

**Elara**

The shriek did not come through the ears.

That was the first thing Elara understood, standing in the cathedral square with her hands still raised in surrender and her heart hammering against her ribs like a prisoner at a door. The sound came through the *bones*. Through the teeth. Through the old scar on her shoulder that had ached every winter since Vellmark. It was a sound like a fortress door being torn off its hinges by something that had never once been told *no*.

Above the cathedral steps, King Aldric — hollow-eyed, gold-crowned, three years a stranger in his own face — arched backward as if strung on an invisible rack. The crown-thing came out of him the way smoke comes out of a burning house: reluctantly, in coils, screaming.

And around the square, ten thousand throats answered.

Wolves from the northern passes stood shoulder to shoulder with cart-horses and milk-cows and palace hounds gone mutinous. Ravens blackened the rooftops like a second storm. Somewhere near the fountain, Elara could see Casper Joubert, small and mud-splattered and swaying on her feet, her mouth open, her eyes rolled white — speaking through all of them at once, wearing their voices like a cloak made of the whole living world.

The thing tore free.

It had no shape anyone's eyes could hold. It was a *pressure*, a cold spot in the air the size of a man, and where it passed, torches guttered and dogs whimpered and even the pigeons fell silent mid-coo. It hung above the square for one long moment, and Elara felt it *look* — felt that ancient, starving attention sweep across the crowd the way a beggar's gaze sweeps a banquet table.

Then it dove.

Not at Casper. Not at Sef, who was sprinting up the cathedral steps with the binding ritual clutched to his chest like a stolen loaf of bread.

At Mirei.

"No," Elara breathed, and then she was running, shoving through beasts who parted for her without being asked, because apparently she had been claimed by the animal kingdom sometime in the last month and no one had thought to inform her.

Mirei stood near the fountain where they'd left her — too small, too thin, seven years old and mute since the interrogators' room, holding a sparrow in her cupped hands because Casper had told her the birds were friends now. The cold pressure dropped toward her like a hawk on a hare.

The sparrow screamed.

Every bird in the capital screamed with it.

* * *

**Casper**

Casper had never been so many places at once, and she was fairly sure she was dying, and she was absolutely sure she didn't care.

She was the wolves' hunger and the horses' terror and the oxen's patient stubbornness and the rats' quick bright cunning, all braided together into one enormous voice that filled her chest past bursting. The unbinding was tearing her gift out of her by the roots — she could feel it going, feel each thread snap like harp strings — but there was no time to grieve, because the crown-thing had turned from the king, and it was falling toward Mirei like winter falling on a garden.

*MINE,* said the thing, in a voice that wasn't a voice. *SMALL. OPEN. UNFILLED.*

Because that was what it did. That was all it had ever done, Casper realized — found empty rooms and moved into them. A lonely king. A grieving mind. And now a child whose voice had been frightened out of her, whose silence must have looked, to a thing that ate silence, like a doorway.

Casper threw everything she had left down the connection.

Ten thousand animals moved as one body. The sparrow in Mirei's hands bit the air where the pressure touched it — a tiny absurd act of defiance, a sparrow against a god, and Casper loved it more than she had ever loved anything. The palace hounds hit the space around Mirei in a ring of bared teeth. A dray horse named Buttercup, whom Casper had spoken with exactly once outside the city gates and who had mostly complained about her harness, planted herself over the girl like a wall.

But the thing was cold, and cold does not care about teeth.

It slid between them. Casper felt it touch the edges of Mirei's mind and she felt the child flinch inward, folding small, folding *silent*, trying to be nothing —

— and then Mirei opened her mouth and spoke.

Later, Casper would try to remember the exact words and fail, because she heard them through a thousand ears at once and they came out different in each. But the shape of it stayed with her forever:

*"You can't have me. I'm already full."*

Full of sparrows. Full of a badger's gruff stories retold at bedtime. Full of a woman who had knelt in the dirt and taught her that quiet was not emptiness, that listening was its own kind of voice. The thing had mistaken Mirei's silence for a vacant room, and Mirei's silence was not vacant at all. It was furnished floor to ceiling with everyone who loved her.

The crown-thing recoiled. And in that moment of recoil, Dain hit it with a bell.

* * *

**Dain**

Dain had spent eleven years learning how to hurt things with sound. He had carried the screaming bell through four provinces. He had told himself it was duty, right up until the morning he'd found Elara's badge in a child's confiscated bundle and understood that duty was just a word men hid behind when they didn't want to look at what their hands were doing.

So there was a certain poetry in it, he supposed, standing in the cathedral square with the bell he'd been ordered to use on gifted children, swinging it instead at a god.

He couldn't hear the bell properly — nobody human could; it rang in the frequency of magic itself — but he could see its effect. The crown-thing, already wounded by a child's refusal, struck by the bell's discordant scream, *shuddered*. Its cold attention snapped toward Dain with something that might have been fury if fury weren't too warm a word for it.

*DAIN OF THE HUNT,* it said, and his own name in its mouth felt like ice water poured down his spine. *YOU RANG FOR ME ONCE.*

"I know," Dain said aloud, and his voice cracked, and he found he didn't care about that either. "I'm sorry. This is me ringing for you again."

He swung the bell in a wide arc, walking backward, drawing the thing after him the way you'd draw a wasp from a sleeping child's crib. Behind him he heard Elara shouting orders — actual orders, to soldiers, to beasts, to both at once, and the astonishing thing was that both obeyed. Ahead of him, the great north gate of the capital stood open, and beyond it the road ran dark toward the deep forest, the old forest, the forest the maps marked with a single word: *ROOTBOUND.*

Sef had explained it that morning, breathless, ink-stained, waving the original ritual like a madman: *the entity cannot be killed, it has no body to kill, but the rootbound — the old ones, the tree-speakers — they can give it a body. Wood remembers. Wood holds. You don't destroy it, you plant it.*

"You want us to *garden* it?" Elara had said.

"Essentially, yes," Sef had said.

So Dain walked backward through the gate with a god chasing him, and behind the god came the entire animal kingdom, and Dain thought, with a laugh that came out slightly unhinged, that this was the strangest hunt he had ever led, and the first one he'd ever been proud of.

* * *

**Elara**

They drove it north through the night.

It was not a battle. Battles have lines and banners and moments where you can stop and breathe. This was a *herding*, and Elara — who had spent her whole life hunting things — discovered that she knew exactly how to do it in reverse. She split her forces the way she'd split them a hundred times: riders on the flanks, hounds on the scent, the bell-bearer at the center drawing the quarry forward. Except her riders included a wolf-pack that needed no horses, and her hounds included foxes and feral cats and one extremely committed goose, and her quarry was a bodiless hunger that had worn a king like a coat for three years.

The thing fought. Of course it fought. It lashed out with cold, with despair, with whispers — Elara heard it trying every voice it had learned in the palace. It spoke to her in her father's tone. It spoke to the soldiers in the voices of dead comrades. It spoke to the horses in the soft croon of stable-hands, promising rest, promising warmth, promising *home*.

The horses did not slow.

Because ahead of them, faint on the wind, came the thread of Casper's voice — thinner now, fraying, but still there, still woven through every throat in the column — and the animals trusted that voice the way they had never trusted any human voice before. Trust, Elara was learning, was the one weapon the thing had no answer for. It could imitate anyone. It could not make anyone believe it.

Near midnight, at the edge of the deep forest, the trees began to move.

Not in the wind. There was no wind. The trees moved the way old people shift in chairs — slowly, deliberately, with intent. Roots lifted from the dark soil like the fingers of buried giants. Branches leaned together overhead, closing the canopy into a roof. And from somewhere deep in the wood came a sound like a choir humming in their sleep, low and green and patient beyond all human patience.

The rootbound had been waiting.

"They say it comes," murmured a voice at Elara's elbow, and she nearly drew her sword before she saw it was only a blind woman, barefoot among the roots, her face tilted toward the cold spot in the air. "Three hundred years we've waited. Everything hungry comes to the forest eventually. The forest is very good at feeding things." She smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. "Or at keeping them."

The crown-thing stopped at the treeline.

For a long moment it simply hung there, and Elara felt its attention turn back the way it had come — back toward the city, toward the square, toward the small mud-splattered girl who had unmade it. Elara put herself squarely in its path, sword drawn, knowing the sword was useless, knowing it didn't matter.

"It ends here," she said. "You've worn your last crown."

*NOT ENDS,* said the thing, and there was something almost petulant in it now, something small and mean and diminished, a beggar denied at the last door. *WAITS. WAITS. WAITS.*

"Yes," Elara said. "I imagine it does."

The forest took it.

* * *

**Casper**

Casper woke on the cathedral steps with her head in Sef's lap and no magic left in her at all.

The silence inside her was total. Where her gift had lived — that constant, crowded, murmuring country she had inhabited since childhood, the gossip of goats and the complaints of chickens and Bracken's dry commentary threading through every day of her life — there was now nothing. Just quiet. Just Casper, alone in her own skull for the first time since she was four years old.

It should have been a relief. Everyone in the Hollow would have called it a relief.

She lay there and wept anyway, quietly, while Sef stroked her hair and pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for her all day.

Around the square, the animals were dispersing. Wolves trotting north. Horses plodding home to stables they'd abandoned in the night, no doubt with a great deal to say about it. The ravens lifting off the rooftops in ragged spirals. As each creature passed her, it paused — a touch of a nose, a brush of feathers, a long look — and though she could no longer hear a word they said, she found she could still read them, plain as print. *Well done, small one.* *Rest now.* *We will remember.*

A sparrow landed on her chest and stayed there, and she let it.

"Mirei?" she managed.

"Sleeping," Sef said. "Between the horse and the hounds. She gave the horse a name, apparently. Buttercup's quite pleased about it." He paused. "She hasn't stopped talking since the square. Elara says she's making up for lost time. Elara says it loudly, but she's smiling when she says it, which frightens everyone."

Casper laughed, and it hurt, and it helped.

"And the thing?"

"In the forest." Sef's face went carefully neutral, the way it did when he was deciding how much truth a person could carry. "The rootbound have it. Growing it into an oak, from what I understand. A very large oak. In several centuries it may be a very impressive oak." He shrugged. "No throne. No crown. No drama. Just... wood. Honestly, for a creature that spent three years demanding worship, I think being a tree is the cruelest fate imaginable."

Somewhere across the square, a church bell rang the hour — a real bell, a human bell, ordinary and sweet — and Casper closed her eyes and listened to it the way she used to listen to everything, with her whole self.

The silence where her song had been was vast.

But it was not, she was beginning to suspect, empty.

* * *

**Dain**

Dain sat his horse at the forest's edge until dawn, watching the trees close over the place where a god had been planted.

Behind him, the road to the capital lay littered with the debris of a kingdom's long nightmare — discarded proclamations, a broken bell, the scattered papers of a Hunt that no longer had anyone's permission to exist. Ahead of him, the forest hummed its low green hymn, patient as stone.

His lieutenant's badge felt heavy in his pocket. He'd taken it off somewhere outside the city gates and hadn't been able to explain to himself why he was still carrying it. He thought maybe he'd bury it. Or maybe he'd keep it, the way you keep a scar — proof that you were there, proof that it cost something, proof that you came out the other side a different shape.

A fox trotted out of the treeline, sat down in front of his horse, and regarded him with an expression of frank assessment.

"Don't start," Dain said. "I've had enough of talking animals for one lifetime."

The fox yawned, unimpressed, and Dain got the distinct feeling — no magic in it, just the plain old animal arithmetic he'd spent his life ignoring — that he was being weighed, and measured, and, against all odds, found acceptable.

From the direction of the capital, far off, a horn sounded. Once. Twice. Then a third time — the old signal, the one every soldier knew, the one that meant the king was awake.

Dain turned his horse south and rode toward whatever came next.

* * *

In the deep forest, in a clearing no map had ever bothered with, a new oak stood where nothing had stood the night before. It was already taller than a man. Its bark was dark and folded in on itself, like a fist that had decided, after very long consideration, never to open.

The blind woman visited it at sunrise, barefoot in the frost, and laid her palm against the trunk.

"Hush now," she told it, kindly. "Grow."

Deep in the wood, something screamed without a mouth, forever.

The trees did not mind. Trees are very good at waiting — and they had, after all, been promised a very long time to listen.
