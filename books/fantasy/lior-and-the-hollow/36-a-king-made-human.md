## 36. A King Made Human

He did not wake all at once. That was the first thing Casper noticed, watching from the edge of the square as the healers swarmed him — he woke the way a fire does when it's been rained on for three years: reluctantly, in patches, with a great deal of smoke.

The crown-thing had gone out of him like a scream going out of a bell, and what was left lying on the cathedral steps was thinner than any king ought to be. His hands were old men's hands. His hair had gone white at the temples in a way that no portrait in the palace gallery would have admitted to. And his eyes — when they finally opened, blinking against the morning light like a creature dragged out of a very long burrow — were just eyes. Tired, human, and utterly lost.

Casper knew that look. She'd seen it on lambs born in a late frost, standing on legs that didn't yet believe in them.

"He's awake," Mirei whispered beside her, gripping her sleeve with both hands. The girl hadn't let go of her since the unbinding, not once, not even to sleep. "Casper. He's *awake*."

"I see that."

"Is he going to be horrible?"

Casper considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "Hard to say. He's been sharing a skull with something ancient and hungry for three years. I'd give him a day or two before judging his manners."

Mirei giggled — a small, startled sound, as if she'd surprised herself with it — and Casper felt it land somewhere under her ribs and stay there.

Down in the square, the crowd had gone quiet in that particular way crowds do when they're afraid to hope. Ten thousand people who had watched their king's face change by degrees over three years, who had learned to lower their voices around the palace, to keep their children indoors on certain nights, to flinch at bells. They stood shoulder to shoulder among the gathered beasts — wolves sitting oddly patient between farmers, the royal stables' horses with their ears all turned inward toward the steps, ravens packed onto every ledge like an audience waiting for a play to start — and they waited to find out what kind of man climbed out of that hollow.

Aldric sat up. A healer tried to ease him back down; he waved her off with a hand that shook, and then, slowly, he looked at his kingdom.

Casper saw the moment it landed. It moved across his face like weather — confusion first, then horror, then something worse than either, which was understanding. His gaze went from the wolves to the burned barricades at the square's edge, to the gallows still standing near the north gate that nobody had gotten around to taking down, to Elara Voss kneeling in the mud in her torn hunt-captain's coat with her wrists bound, because she had walked into the cathedral as a prisoner and nobody had thought to change the paperwork in the middle of a miracle.

His mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"What," King Aldric said, and his voice came out cracked and small, a voice that hadn't been used in years except by something else, "*have I done?*"

Nobody answered. The wind moved through the square, stirring ash.

He looked down at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger, which, in fairness, they recently had. Then he looked at the bound woman in the mud, and Casper watched a man begin to do arithmetic he would be doing for the rest of his life.

"Captain," he said. "Voss. Is that — you were reported dead. Twice."

"Reports were optimistic, Your Majesty," Elara said. Her voice was steady, but Casper could see the cost of it in her jaw. Three days ago this man's crown had ordered her branded traitor. This morning she knelt in front of him in chains while ten thousand witnesses decided whether to cheer her or hang her.

"Unbind her," Aldric said.

The guards hesitated. One of them glanced toward the tribunal dais where Dain stood — Dain, who had come into the square that morning carrying the bell-weapon's broken clapper like a confession, and who now, after a long moment, gave the smallest nod ever recorded in military history.

They cut Elara's wrists free.

Aldric tried to stand. Failed. Tried again, using a healer's shoulder, and made it upright by sheer stubbornness, which Casper privately approved of. She knew stubbornness when she saw it. She'd raised goats.

Then the king of the realm did something no one in that square — perhaps no one alive — had ever seen a king do.

He knelt.

Not a bow. Not the shallow court bend that meant *I acknowledge you while remaining above you*. He went down on both knees on the cold stone steps, in front of the whole assembled city, in his ruined coronation robes, and he bowed his head until his forehead nearly touched the ground.

"I don't know everything it did," he said, and his voice carried strangely well, the way quiet voices do in enormous silences. "I remember pieces. Waking sometimes, far inside myself, hearing my own mouth say things — orders — and being unable to stop them. I remember screaming and no one hearing it." He lifted his head. His eyes found the crowd, moving over faces, over beasts, over the child pressed against Casper's side. "You heard it. All of you. Every animal in this kingdom heard me screaming for three years, and I never knew, and you stayed anyway. Some of you came when called by a girl with no army and no title." His voice broke on the last of it. "I owe this kingdom a debt I cannot pay. But I can start paying it today, publicly, where everyone can watch me fail at it honestly."

He rose — with help, and without shame about needing it — and turned to face the tribunal dais.

"The Gifted Hunt," Aldric said, "is ended. Now. This hour. Every warrant burned, every prisoner released, every hound retired to a warm fireside whether it likes it or not." A ripple went through the square — disbelief first, then a sound building underneath it, half sob and half cheer. "Every person taken under its authority will be returned to their homes or given new ones if they have none to return to, at the crown's expense, which is fitting, since it was the crown that emptied them."

He paused. His gaze dropped to the gallows by the north gate.

"And take that down," he said quietly. "Take it down and burn it, and let the wood be used for something useful. A schoolhouse, perhaps. I'm told we'll need several."

* * *

The rest of the day happened to Casper somewhat at a distance, the way big days do.

There was a great deal of noise. There were clerks — clerks appeared out of the walls of the palace within minutes, as if they'd been growing there in the dark like mushrooms, waiting for someone to give them permission to exist — and the clerks wrote things down and stamped things and argued about seals, and somewhere in the middle of it the Hunt officially stopped having ever been allowed.

Elara was restored, then promoted, then offered things with titles so long that Sef started keeping score from the sidelines ("General-Commander of the Reformed Wardens, Defender of the Marches, *and* Keeper of the King's Peace — that's four nouns, she should negotiate for five"). She accepted none of it yet. She spent most of the afternoon instead standing very close to Casper in the palace courtyard, saying nothing, which between the two of them had become a complete conversation.

"You're staring at my bruises," Casper said finally.

"I'm inventorying your bruises. There's a difference. It's a professional habit."

"And?"

"And I'm going to need a larger inventory book." Elara's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling — a twitch at one corner, quickly arrested. "You nearly died out there. Sef says your heart stopped for the length of four bell-tolls."

"Sef exaggerates."

"Sef counted."

Casper had no answer to that, so she leaned sideways until her shoulder rested against Elara's armored one, and Elara allowed it, which from Elara was roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

Across the courtyard, the old mare stood in the shade of the stable arch, breathing slow and shallow. She had not much time left — everyone could see it, horse and human alike — but she had insisted on being present for all of it, and the grooms had made a bed of straw for her right there where she could watch. When Aldric passed her on his way back into the palace, he stopped.

Casper was close enough to hear. Everyone was close enough to hear; the whole courtyard had gone still.

"I rode you the day my father died," the king said to the mare. "Do you remember? You were the only one who didn't look at me differently afterward."

The mare blew a soft breath through her nostrils and lipped at his sleeve, gently, the way horses correct the people they forgive.

"I'm told you know my laugh," Aldric said. "The real one. I confess I've lost track of it myself." His hand rested on her neck. "When you go — and I'm sorry, I know you're tired — I'll try to find it again. It seems only fair that you should get to hear it first."

The mare sighed, deeply, the way old creatures sigh when a long job is finally done, and Casper had to look away and blink at the sky for a moment, because some things were too large to watch straight-on.

* * *

Evening found Casper where she least expected to be: summoned, formally, by a nervous page, to a small room in the palace with a fire and too many chairs, where King Aldric sat looking like a man wearing his own body for the first time in years and not entirely sure of the fit.

He dismissed the guards. He poured wine with his own hands, badly, spilling some, and handed her a cup.

"You're the goat girl," he said. "From the Hollow."

"Casper Joubert."

"Casper Joubert." He said it carefully, like a man learning a word in a foreign language. "Sef told me what the unbinding cost you. What it costs *every* gifted who spends themselves that way. Your magic is gone."

It wasn't a question, so she didn't answer it. She looked into her wine instead. The silence where the song had been was loudest in rooms like this one — warm, safe, full of people — because those were the rooms where she'd most want to reach for it.

"I want to tell you what I remember," the king said. "Because you're the only one who was inside it with me, in a way. At the end. You heard what was under the — under the thing." He swallowed. "Did I beg?"

Casper set down her cup. "Yes."

"Good," Aldric said, and Casper blinked. "Begging means I was still in there. Sef's master wasn't sure. For three years, no one was sure whether anything of me survived, and the court learned to stop wondering, and I—" He stopped. Started again. "I heard the animals, at the end. Through you. Everything, all at once. Wolves and sparrows and cattle and — a badger." His voice caught. "An old badger, telling me to hold on, that my kingdom was coming for me. Was that—"

"That was Bracken." Her throat tightened around the name. "He didn't make it out of the rookery. He held the tunnel open so the rest of us could run."

Aldric closed his eyes. "Then I owe a badger more than I owe any lord in this roomless hall of mine. Write down his name for me. Full honors. Whatever a badger's honors are, he'll have them, and if the heralds object they can take it up with me and my newly recovered temper."

Despite everything, Casper laughed — a wet, uneven sound, but a laugh. "He'd have hated that. He'd have demanded cheese instead."

"Cheese *and* honors," the king said. "I'm told I was a generous man before. I intend to find out if it's true."

He was quiet a moment, turning his cup. When he spoke again, the lightness had gone out of his voice, and what remained was heavier and more honest.

"I signed the Hunt's first warrant. Did you know that? Before it wore me — I mean, before I understood what was happening, in those early months when I thought the dreams were dreams — the council brought papers and I signed them, because signing papers is what kings do, and I never read past the second line." He looked up at her. "Three years of cages, and the door was opened by my own hand before the thing ever touched the lock. That's mine, Casper Joubert. Whatever it did to me, that part is mine, and I won't hide behind it."

"No," Casper agreed quietly. "You shouldn't."

"So I won't." He set the cup down. "There will be a reckoning — a true one, public, with testimony from the gifted and the hunted and anyone else the crown wronged, including, I expect, a great deal of testimony about me. I won't preside over it. I'll sit in the dock like anyone else and answer for what I signed." He almost smiled. "My council tells me no king has ever done such a thing. I told them that was rather the point."

Casper studied him — this hollowed, mortal, stubborn old man in a young man's crown — and thought of Bracken, who had always said that power was just weight, and the only question was who was strong enough to carry it without crushing what was under them.

"They'll call you weak," she said.

"They called me strong for three years," Aldric said. "Look how that went. I'd rather be weak and awake."

* * *

She left the palace near midnight, because Mirei was waiting and because palaces, however reformed, still made her skin itch. Elara walked her out through the courtyards, past the stables where the old mare slept standing up, past the north gate where workmen were already prying apart the gallows by torchlight, stacking the wood with the careful reverence of people building something out of what they'd torn down.

The city was loud tonight — genuinely loud, bells ringing off-schedule just because they could, dogs barking from every rooftop, people singing badly in taverns. Above it all the moon hung fat and white, one turn shy of full again, already climbing toward next year's thirteenth.

"One year," Elara said, following her gaze. "That's what Sef says. The moons will line up again in a year and a day, and the rootbound want the entity bound deeper before then, and they've asked — politely, in the way trees ask — for the kingdom's help."

"The kingdom," Casper said, "has a lot of apologizing to do to its forests."

"The kingdom," Elara corrected, bumping her shoulder, "has a lot of apologizing to do, full stop. Starting with a certain valley that it sent armed men into." She paused at the gate where the road west began, dark and long and, for the first time in Casper's memory, open. "The king wants to send an envoy to the Hollow. Formally. To lift whatever old law made your people forget magic in the first place." A beat. "He asked if I'd lead it. He also asked if I thought you'd come."

Casper looked down the west road, toward hills she could almost smell from here — rain and goats and Bracken's sett under the old oak — and felt something in her chest turn over, painful and hopeful, like a lamb finding its feet.

"What did you tell him?" she asked.

Elara smiled — the full one, this time, rare as a comet. "I told him I'd go wherever you went. So." She held out her hand, palm up, in the torchlight. "Where are we going, Casper Joubert?"

Behind them the bells rang on, and ahead the road waited, and Casper took her hand.
