## 37. Silence Where the Song Was

Casper woke to silence, and for a long, drifting moment she thought she had gone deaf.

The world was there — she could hear it. The creak of the infirmary cot beneath her. Wind worrying at the shutters of the palace guest wing, where someone had decided a girl who had unbound a king deserved a bed with actual linens instead of straw. Somewhere below, a cart rattled over cobblestones, and a woman was singing to herself in a voice that wandered off-key and didn't care.

All of that reached her fine.

What didn't reach her was everything else.

She lay still and listened the way she had listened her whole life, reaching out with the sense that had always lived just under her skin — the hum of the stable cats two floors down, the drowsy mutter of the kitchen mice, the great slow dreaming of the palace horses. She reached for it the way you reach for a stair in the dark, expecting it to be where it has always been.

Her foot found nothing. There was no stair. There was no dark.

There was only Casper, alone inside her own skull, for the first time since she was small enough to fit in a bread basket.

"Oh," she said aloud, to nobody, because there was no longer anybody else in the room with her. "Oh, that's what I did."

* * *

They had warned her. That was the bitter joke of it. Sef had explained it three times, drawing diagrams in spilled wine on the tavern table, and Elara had made him explain it a fourth time because she hadn't liked his face while he did it.

"The binding wove itself into your gift," Sef had said, tapping the wine-stain shaped like a knot. "It's been sitting in every beast-speaker like a splinter for three years, waiting for a hand big enough to pull it. You're the hand. But the splinter comes out with flesh."

"And afterward?" Casper had asked.

"Afterward," Sef said gently, "you'll be an ordinary woman."

Elara had snorted. "She's never been ordinary in her life."

And Casper had laughed, and said something brave and stupid like *it's worth it*, and meant it, and still meant it now, lying in the good bed with the good linens, meaning it so hard it felt like breaking.

Because here was the thing nobody told you about sacrifice: it doesn't feel noble when it's done. It feels like waking up missing a limb that isn't visibly gone. It feels like turning your head to say something to Bracken and remembering, all over again, each time, that the voice that would have answered is ash in a tunnel under the rookery. It is grief with excellent manners. It sits quietly. It waits until you're holding a cup of tea or tying your boot, and then it puts its cold hand on the back of your neck.

Casper got up, wrapped herself in the too-large robe some helpful servant had left, and went to the window.

The city was mending. Three days since the square, since ten thousand throats had spoken through her and torn a god-thing off a king, and already the scaffolding was going up on the cathedral's scorched east face, and children were playing some new game involving pretending to be ravens, which she suspected she'd started and would never be credited for. Down in the street, a dray horse stood patiently while its driver argued with a fishmonger, and the horse —

The horse was just a horse. A big brown patient animal flicking its ears at flies, thinking whatever horses think, which was now, permanently, none of her business.

She had spent twenty-four years knowing what horses think. It was mostly complaints about flies and strong opinions about other horses' hindquarters. It had been the background music of her entire existence, and now it was gone, and the silence where it had been was so vast she could have shouted into it and heard nothing come back.

"Right," she said to the horse, who did not answer, because it couldn't, because she couldn't, because that was the whole arrangement. "Right."

Her voice cracked on the second one anyway.

* * *

Mirei found her there an hour later, still at the window, having progressed from standing to sitting on the floor with her knees up, watching the brown horse with the fixed attention of a woman trying to memorize something she'd never actually learned by eye alone.

"You're supposed to eat," Mirei said. She was carrying a tray with the grim competence of a child who had appointed herself to the position. Toast, honey, an apple cut into slices. "Sef says people who save kingdoms are statistically likely to forget meals."

"Sef says a lot of things."

"He also cries when he reads his old master's letters, but he thinks nobody knows." Mirei set the tray down with a clack and sat down across from her, cross-legged, the way she used to sit across from frightened animals back when Casper could tell her what they were frightened *of*. "Are you sad?"

Casper considered lying. Mirei had been mute from terror once; Casper had coaxed her back with patience and animal signs, and the girl had a nose for falsehood that rivaled any hound's.

"Yes," Casper said. "But not the kind that means anything's wrong. The kind that means something was real."

Mirei nodded slowly, absorbing this. Then, with the terrifying directness of nine-year-olds: "Does it hurt?"

"Like a tooth that got pulled. It doesn't hurt anymore. There's just a hole where it was, and my tongue keeps finding it."

Mirei considered this. Then she reached out and took Casper's hand and turned it palm-up, and traced something in it with one finger — the old sign they'd invented, back in the sanctuary, back before Mirei had words. *Horse.* Then another. *Sad.* Then a third one Casper hadn't taught her, a little looping motion that was new, and when Casper looked up questioningly, Mirei shrugged.

"That one means 'still yours.' I made it up. For things you keep even after they're gone."

Casper looked at her — this scrap of a girl the Hunt had chained in a wagon, whom interrogators had starved into silence, who had come out the other side of it with more grace than half the court above them — and felt the grief shift its weight, the way it does when love leans against it.

"Still yours," Casper repeated, and signed it back, and ate the toast, because Mirei was watching, and some orders you take from queens and some from nine-year-olds with trays.

* * *

Elara came at midday, in civilian clothes that fit her like an apology. Without the captain's badge she moved differently — less like a drawn blade, more like a woman perpetually surprised to be somewhere. She'd been offered a generalship. Casper knew she'd spend the rest of her life being offered things by people hoping to pin the guilt of the Hunt on someone honorable enough to carry it.

"You're on the floor," Elara observed.

"I'm communing with the floor."

"How's that going?"

"It's very quiet," Casper said, and heard how it landed, and watched Elara's jaw tighten, because Elara had carried her own silence for years — the silence of a woman who'd known what the Hunt was and obeyed it anyway, and lived with the listening she'd done instead of speaking.

Elara sat down on the floor beside her, which for Elara was roughly equivalent to weeping openly.

"In the north garrisons," she said, after a while, "when a soldier loses an arm, they give them leave and a pension and everyone pretends the worst part is the arm. It isn't. The worst part is the ghost weight. You reach for things with a hand that isn't there. Everyone knows it. Nobody says it."

"How long does the ghost weight last?"

"Honestly? Years. Maybe forever. But it gets lighter." She paused. "You did the reaching-for-things thing this morning, didn't you."

"Three times. Once at a cat."

"What did the cat do?"

"Nothing. It was just a cat, walking past. It didn't even look at me." Casper laughed, wetly. "I spent my whole life being looked at by animals, Elara. Goats, badgers, crows, one deeply judgmental goose. And now I'm furniture. I'm weather. I'm a thing that happens near animals and registers not at all."

Elara was quiet for a moment. Then: "Bracken registered you."

The name went through her like a hook. Of course it did. Elara had never learned to soften a blow; she'd only learned to choose which ones to throw.

"He did," Casper said finally. "He looked at me like I was the whole sky, and he was a lame old badger with worms, and he chose me anyway. Without magic. So maybe—" Her voice did something embarrassing. She pushed through it. "Maybe looking is the part that mattered. Maybe the talking was just how I noticed the looking."

"That," said Elara, "is either very wise or very tired."

"Both. It's both."

Elara put her arm around her shoulders, awkward as a plank fence learning tenderness, and they sat on the floor together while the brown horse below finished its hay and the city hammered itself back together, and neither of them said anything else, because they were both fluent in silence by now, and had finally found a use for it.

* * *

In the evening, Sef knocked, entered without waiting — he'd picked up court manners and discarded them in the same week — and spread a scroll on the table with the air of a man delivering terrible news he'd rehearsed.

"So," he said. "The mare."

Casper's chest clenched. Old Blossom, the royal stables' grey mare, the last creature alive who remembered King Aldric's true laugh, whose memory had given Casper the name-song that unbound him. Casper had held the mare's velvet nose in her hands three days ago and sung a dying king's childhood back to him through her own throat.

"She's failing," Sef said. "The stable master says days, perhaps hours. She held on — the vet thinks she held on *for* something. To see it done." He hesitated. "She's asking for you. Well. Not asking. Looking at the door. Every time it opens."

Casper stood. Her legs were steady. Her eyes were not.

"I can't talk to her," she said. "I can't tell her — she held on for a thank-you, Sef, and I can't—"

"You don't need to talk," Sef said quietly. "You never needed magic for that. Ask anyone who ever loved you. Ask a badger."

So Casper went down through the palace, past guards who bowed to her now, which she hated, down the familiar stone stairs to the warm dark of the royal stables, smelling of hay and leather and horse, and into the last stall, where an ancient grey mare lay on her side, breathing in long, laborious pulls.

Blossom's ear turned toward the door before Casper's footsteps reached it. Not magic. Just a horse who knew her person's tread, the way horses have known their people's treads since before kings, before crowns, before anything wore anything.

Casper knelt in the straw and took the great soft head into her lap, and there was no song underneath the skin of the world, no hum, no chorus, no voice but her own — just her voice, just her hands, just a girl from a valley that forgot magic kneeling in the dirt with a dying friend.

"Thank you," she whispered, into the grey ear. "Thank you for keeping his laugh safe. Thank you for holding on. You can go now. It's done. He's free. He laughs sometimes, did you know? Badly. Like a door hinge. But it's his."

Blossom sighed — a huge, whole-body sound, the sigh of a creature setting down a burden carried for years — and her breathing slowed, and slowed, and stopped, gentle as a lamp going out.

And in the silence after, Casper realized she was crying, and that the crying was not only grief. Because she had understood that sigh perfectly. Had read it in the fall of the ribs, the softening of the eye, the angle of the ear — plain as speech, no gift required, nothing but twenty-four years of paying attention.

The magic was gone.

The listening wasn't.

She stayed until the stable master came with a lantern, and then she walked back up through the palace, past the bowing guards, past the hammering city, up to her room where Mirei was asleep on the cot she'd refused to vacate, and Elara dozed in the chair, and Sef had fallen asleep sitting up with letters on his chest.

Casper stood in the doorway a long time, watching her strange, improbable family breathe.

Then, on the table, she saw it: a folded note in a clerk's precise hand, sealed with the king's new modest seal. Beside it, a second item that made her heart stop and start again —

A raven feather, and beneath it, in scratched, hurried talon-marks pressed into wax, a message no beast should have been able to write:

*HE ASKS FOR THE GIRL FROM THE HOLLOW. HE REMEMBERS YOUR VOICE. COME TO THE DEEP FOREST WHEN THE MOON TURNS.*

The rootbound had sent word.

Something in the deep forest wanted to speak with her — and for the first time in her life, Casper had no idea what it was saying.
