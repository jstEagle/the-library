## 38. Learning to Listen Again

The silence had stopped being a wound and started being a room.

That was how Casper thought of it, in the weeks after the unbinding — not as an absence, not as the amputated thing she had wept over into Elara's shoulder on that first terrible morning, but as a room she was learning to live in. A quiet house with good light. She still caught herself reaching for it, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be. She would look at a goat and *reach*, out of habit, toward that warm channel where meaning used to flow like water from a spring — and find nothing there, just air, just her own small human mind rattling around inside her skull like a pebble in a jar.

But here was the thing nobody had warned her about, because nobody had ever survived to come back and say it:

She could still read them.

Not hear them. Not speak with them. But read them — the way you might read a letter written in a hand you knew intimately, or a face across a crowded market. Years of listening had taught her things about animals that no magic had ever bothered to teach her, because magic did the work for her, the way a cart does the work of walking. Take away the cart, and it turned out she had legs.

She discovered it on an ordinary morning, three weeks after waking to a world gone quiet, when she went out to check on the goats she'd half-adopted from the palace stables' overflow — creatures the crown had no more use for now that the king who'd whispered to them was only a man again.

The brown one, Sorrel, stood apart from the others at the fence line, facing the wrong way, chewing nothing.

Old Casper would have asked her what was wrong. New Casper watched instead. Watched the set of the ears — one forward, one swiveling back toward the barn, which meant the trouble wasn't where she was looking but where she'd come from. Watched the weight shifted off the left forefoot, favoring it, but not the dramatic favoring of real pain; the polite favoring of something that had been hurting long enough to become boring. Watched her chew at nothing, jaw working empty, which in a goat was the equivalent of a person drumming their fingers — worry with nowhere to go.

Casper followed the ear's direction to the barn and found a fox denned under the floorboards, thin and desperate, drawn by the smell of feed. It hadn't gotten near the goats yet. It wouldn't have. But Sorrel had been standing guard all night on a sore foot, too responsible to rest, too polite to make a fuss.

"There's easier meals than us," Casper told the fox, crouching at the barn door, "and I'll show you where they are if you promise to stop stressing my girl out."

The fox looked at her with the flat amber patience of its kind, and Casper realized she was doing it again — talking to animals as though they understood words. They didn't. They never really had, she suspected, even through the gift. What the gift had carried wasn't language. It was everything underneath language: intent, fear, appetite, affection. And all of that was still legible, if you paid attention. If you *listened* with your eyes and your patience instead of your blood.

She showed the fox the midden behind the tannery road, where scraps were reliable and dogs were few. The fox took the offer the way foxes take everything — as though it had been its own idea all along — and by evening Sorrel was grazing with the herd, and Casper stood in the field with her hands dirty and her heart doing something complicated in her chest.

*I didn't need it,* she thought, and then corrected herself, because that wasn't quite true either. *I needed it to learn this.*

* * *

Elara found her there, or came looking — with Elara it was always hard to tell the difference, since she moved through the world like someone who had never once been lost and resented the implication.

"You're talking to goats," Elara said, leaning on the fence.

"I'm negotiating with goats. There's a difference."

"With you there's never been a difference." But she said it softly, and she looked at Casper a moment longer than the joke required, the way she'd been doing lately — checking, Casper knew, for cracks. Elara checked for cracks the way other people breathed. It was what years of commanding soldiers taught you: everyone breaks somewhere, eventually, and it's better to know where before it matters.

"I'm fine," Casper said.

"You said that exact sentence yesterday while reorganizing the same shelf of jars four times."

"The jars were badly organized."

"Casper." Elara came around the fence and stood beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, both of them watching the herd. "You don't have to be fine for me. I've seen you scream at a king. I've seen you hold ten thousand voices in your head without splitting open. You're allowed to have a bad day about losing the whole song."

Casper considered this. Below them, Sorrel glanced up, ears tilting toward them both, then went back to grazing with the deep unbothered calm of an animal whose world had been put right.

"It's strange," Casper said finally. "I keep waiting for it to hurt the way it did at first, and it doesn't, and then I feel guilty that it doesn't, like I'm betraying it somehow. Like grieving it properly is the last duty I owe it."

"And?"

"And then this morning I read Sorrel's whole night off her ears and her feet, and I thought—" She laughed, short and surprised, even now. "I thought, oh. Oh, it wasn't the magic. The magic was just the door. I already knew how to walk through rooms."

Elara was quiet a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful evenness she used when something mattered too much to say carelessly. "My mother was deaf in one ear. Did I ever tell you that? Lost it to fever as a girl. And she used to say the strangest thing — that she heard better after. Because she'd stopped assuming she knew what people meant, and started actually watching them." She shrugged, awkward with tenderness the way she was awkward with most soft things, carrying them like live coals. "I think about that more lately."

"Your mother sounds like she'd have liked me."

"She'd have found you exhausting," Elara said, "and defended you to the death," and Casper laughed, and the laugh didn't catch on anything on the way out, and that felt like progress.

* * *

Mirei came to live with them officially in the fourth week, though unofficially she had been theirs since the night Elara carried her out of the convoy burning behind them, wrapped in a hunt-captain's cloak that smelled of smoke and rain.

The paperwork — Sef had insisted on paperwork, gleefully, the way only a man who'd spent years forging court documents under a possessed king could enjoy paperwork — named Casper as ward. Mirei had signed her own name beneath Casper's, slowly, tongue between her teeth, the letters enormous and slightly drunken. Then she'd looked up to see if it was right, and Casper had said it was the finest signature in the kingdom, and Mirei had believed her, because that was the arrangement they had: Casper said true things dressed as kindnesses, and Mirei pretended not to notice the dressing.

The muteness had loosened by degrees, the way frost leaves a window. First it was only animal signs — the little vocabulary of hands she and Casper had built on the road, *food*, *sleep*, *danger*, *the white mare*, *again*. Then whispered words to the animals, who required no volume. Then, one evening, a full sentence spoken aloud to Casper over supper, so casually it nearly stopped Casper's heart: *"Pass the bread, please."*

As though she'd been speaking all along. As though the silence had been a coat she'd simply decided to take off.

Now she chattered the way children do who have been rationed speech and suddenly own the whole supply — questions, observations, running commentary on the moral failures of chickens. She had opinions about everything. She had especially strong opinions about the hens, whom she accused daily of hoarding.

It was on a grey Tuesday, in the yard, that Casper saw it.

Mirei was crouched by the henhouse, mid-argument, holding out a crust with the stern patience of a magistrate. The lead hen — a tyrant named Duchess — was performing her usual theater of suspicion, circling the offering, complaining loudly to her constituents.

And then Mirei went still.

Not frightened-still. Listening-still. Her head tipped, very slightly, the way a dog tips its head at a sound too high for human ears. Her eyes unfocused, turned inward, and her lips moved without sound, shaping something — answering something.

Casper's whole body went cold and then hot.

"Mirei."

The girl blinked, came back, looked up at her with those grave dark eyes. "Duchess says the big one takes her eggs."

Casper sat down slowly on the step. "The... big one."

"The speckled one. She hides them under the water barrel and blames the rats." Mirei said it plainly, the way you'd report the weather, and then held out the crust again, business concluded, conversation apparently over.

Under the water barrel, Casper later confirmed, were eleven eggs. The speckled hen watched her find them with an expression of magnificent unconcern.

Casper sat on the step for a long time afterward, holding eleven eggs and feeling the ground shift gently under her life. *It runs in families,* Sef had told her once, in another life, in a wagon full of cages. *Gift doesn't care about blood lines. It cares about — openings.* People who had listened hard enough, long enough, in the places where listening was survival.

A mute child in a cage full of terrified animals, with nothing to do but listen.

"Oh, sweetheart," Casper murmured, to no one, to the yard, to whatever patient old power arranged these things. "What am I supposed to do with you?"

The answer arrived the next morning, as answers did, wearing work clothes.

* * *

Sef arrived with the post and a basket of oranges he absolutely had not paid for, and found Casper staring into the middle distance while Mirei taught the cat to sit, which the cat was permitting purely for its own amusement.

"She talks to them," Casper said, without preamble. "Really talks. Duchess told her about the eggs. In actual— she hears them, Sef. Words. Or near enough."

Sef set down the basket. He had gone carefully still, the way he did when his mind started moving faster than his mouth — a rare event, and therefore ominous. "How old is she?"

"Nine. Maybe. We think."

"And she was in the cages for how long?"

"Six days." Six days. Casper had counted every one of them from the outside.

Sef lowered himself onto the step beside her, and for once he didn't perform. He just sat, turning an orange in his clever scarred fingers. "When I was apprenticed," he said, "my master kept records. Gifted children, recovered by the tower before the Hunt got to them. Do you know what he wrote about the ones who'd been silenced? Beaten, or cursed, or simply frightened into never speaking?" He rolled the orange between finger and thumb. "He wrote that they made the strongest listeners he'd ever seen. That silence, imposed early enough, teaches a person to hear everything else. The body. The air. The intention underneath." He glanced at her sideways. "Sound familiar?"

Casper thought of the Hollow. Of a lifetime of swallowing every word that mattered, of living so quietly inside herself that the quiet became a country she governed fluently. Of hearing everything because she was allowed to say nothing.

"Yes," she said. "It sounds familiar."

"So the question isn't whether she has the gift." Sef tossed her the orange. "The question is what kind, and whether anyone teaches her before the world does it badly. And since the world's current curriculum involves bells and cages, I'd suggest we get ahead of it." He paused, and his voice gentled into something rare and unarmored. "You learned yours alone in a valley that forgot magic existed. Alone, Casper. You turned out magnificently, but you shouldn't have had to. She doesn't have to."

That night Casper lay awake next to Elara, listening to her breathe, and tried to imagine teaching. Not the gift itself — that came or it didn't, like weather — but the shape of it. The ethics of it. *When to speak and when to keep silent. How to carry what you hear without letting it carry you. How to be the valley and not the flood.*

Everything Bracken had taught her, she realized, without ever once calling it a lesson. The old badger had been her first teacher all along, grumbling philosophy through a mouthful of beetles: *Listen longer than you want to. Answer honestly. Keep your promises small and keep every one of them.*

She could do that. For Mirei, she could do that.

She slept, finally, and dreamed of a hillside, and of a low gray shape settling against her side, saying nothing, needing nothing said.

* * *

They built the lessons the way you build anything honest — slowly, out of what was at hand.

There was no curriculum for raising a beast-speaker; the tower's archives had burned, or been burned, and Sef mourned them weekly. So Casper invented one, and mostly it consisted of walks.

They walked the lanes at dawn, when the animals were busiest telling each other about the night. Casper taught Mirei to stop walking when a horse pinned its ears — *what changed just before? what did it see?* — and to wait for a nervous dog to decide about her rather than deciding for it. She taught her that fear in an animal smells like nothing and looks like everything: the whites of eyes, the tail clamped, the sudden stillness that isn't calm. She taught her the difference between an animal that won't speak and an animal that can't, and that both deserve the same courtesy.

"And people?" Mirei signed one morning, then said it aloud, practicing: "Do people count too?"

"People are just animals who learned to lie about it," Casper said. "Same ears. Same eyes. Watch a man's hands, not his mouth. Hands tell the truth late but they tell it whole."

Mirei took this with the terrifying literalism of nine-year-olds, and thereafter stared at the hands of every adult in the village until several of them complained to Elara, who laughed for a full minute, which Casper privately noted was the first time she'd heard her do that since the war.

The lessons worked both ways, which Casper hadn't expected. Mirei, who had lived six days among caged animals and a lifetime among silences, knew things Casper had never thought to ask. She knew that hens grieved. She knew that horses dreamed out loud, little snorts and twitches, and that you should never wake one mid-dream. She knew that some cats loved you and some cats merely tolerated you and that the difference was whether they brought you things they could have eaten themselves.

"Bracken used to bring me dead moles," Casper said, throat tight. "Worst gifts in the world. Best gifts in the world."

"What happened to him?" Mirei asked, and it wasn't careless — she had learned, in her careful way, exactly when to ask about the holes in people.

"He stayed behind so the rest of us could run," Casper said. "And then he stayed behind again, at the end, so the tunnel stayed open. He was very good at staying behind so other people could go forward." She smiled, and it mostly worked. "Badgers are like that. Stubborn all the way through."

Mirei considered this with great seriousness. Then she went to the garden, dug a hole at the corner where the light fell longest, and buried an acorn there, patting the earth flat with both hands.

"For him?" Casper asked.

"For badgers," Mirei said. "So when his family comes by, there'll be oak trees. Badgers like edges. Oaks make edges."

Casper had to walk out to the goat field and stand there for a while, pretending to count heads, until her face agreed to behave.

* * *

Autumn came down the valley in stages, gold first, then copper, then the deep red-brown of things settling in for sleep. The acorn sent up a shoot no bigger than a thumb, absurdly determined. Mirei visited it daily and reported its progress to the household as though it were a sibling.

And Casper learned her new country, the quiet one, and found it richer than she'd feared.

She couldn't call the hawk down from the sky anymore. But she could watch it hunt and know, from the angle of its circle, that the vole population in the east meadow was failing, and mention it to Farmer Ottley, whose winter grain stores thanked her. She couldn't ask the pack of semi-feral dogs at the crossroads why they barked at the tinker. But she could watch them do it, night after night, and notice they only barked when he wore the gray hat, never the brown one, and leave it to Elara to discover that the gray hat concealed a brand from a debtor's prison and a history of stealing dogs. She couldn't hear Sorrel's worries anymore. But Sorrel had learned that pressing her forehead to Casper's chest communicated everything necessary, and had apparently decided this method was superior anyway, being more efficient and involving scratching.

The gift had been a river. This was learning to read the ripples.

Some nights, lying awake, she missed it so badly it was like hunger. Other nights — more of them now, week over week — she lay awake marveling. All those years in the Hollow, she had thought her silence was a hiding place. It had been a school. Every swallowed word had sharpened her eyes. Every secret had taught her the weight of what goes unsaid, in beasts and in kings and in tired soldiers who loved her and said so by fixing fences at dawn without being asked.

Loss transmuted. Not gone — changed, the way ore changes in fire. She would not have chosen it. She would not, now, give it back.

* * *

On the last mild evening of the season, the three of them sat on the porch steps — Casper, Elara, and Mirei wedged between them with the proprietary comfort of a child who has thoroughly annexed two adults — watching the sun go down over the fields.

The cat was on the rail. The goats were shadows at the fence line. Somewhere down the lane, a dog was making its opinionated rounds.

"Duchess says the speckled one apologized," Mirei announced, apropos of nothing. "Sort of. Chickens apologize weird. She gave her a worm."

"Justice at last," Elara said gravely.

"And the dog down the lane — Biscuit — he says—" Mirei stopped. Tilted her head. Frowned, the small frown she got when something came through fuzzy, like a voice through a wall.

Casper went very still.

"He says what?" she prompted, gentle, casual, her heart hammering like a bird in a box.

Mirei tilted her head further. Her eyes had gone that inward place, that listening place, and her lips moved faintly, shaping words that weren't hers.

Then she blinked, and the frown cleared, and she grinned up at Casper with the uncomplicated joy of a child reporting good news.

"He says somebody's coming up the road," she said. "Somebody slow. Somebody with a funny walk." She squinted toward the darkening lane. "He says they visit the oak tree sometimes. The one I planted."

Casper's breath stopped in her chest.

Down the lane, where the dusk pooled blue and deep, something low and gray was coming up the track at its own unhurried pace — a waddling, deliberate, stubborn sort of pace, the pace of a creature that had never once hurried for anybody and did not intend to start now.

Elara's hand found Casper's and gripped it.

Mirei scrambled up, pointing, delighted. "It's a badger!"
