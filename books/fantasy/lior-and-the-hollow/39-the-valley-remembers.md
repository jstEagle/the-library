## 39. The Valley Remembers

### Elara

The letter arrived on the third morning of their journey home, carried by a courier who rode hard and looked terrified of her.

Elara read it twice by the side of the road while Casper pretended not to watch and Sef made a great show of fussing with the mule's harness. The seal was the crown's — the true crown now, whatever that meant for a king who woke up hollowed out and weeping in his own bedchamber. The handwriting was chancellery-neat.

*Captain-General Elara Voss is hereby offered command of all royal forces, with authority to restructure the Hunt as she sees fit.*

As she saw fit. Which was to say: dissolve it, they meant. They wanted her name on the dissolution so that when the old families grumbled about wasted gold and lost prestige, the grumbling would land on her shoulders instead of the throne's.

She had to admit it was clever. She'd have respected it more if she hadn't spent two years watching clever men feed children into interrogator's wagons.

"You're making the face," Casper said. She was sitting on a fence rail, legs swinging, a goat kid asleep across her lap like a scarf. Even without her gift, animals still trusted her. Elara suspected they always would. Some things weren't magic; they were just Casper.

"What face?"

"The one where you're being polite to someone who deserves to be thrown in a pond."

"It's an offer of generalship," Elara said. "Highest honor the crown can bestow."

"And?"

Elara folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, the way she did everything. "And I've spent my whole life learning that the highest honors are usually the heaviest things they'll ask you to carry." She held the page over the cookfire they'd built against the morning damp and let it catch. "I'm done carrying."

Sef looked up from the mule. "You know they'll offer again."

"Then I'll burn nicer paper."

Casper grinned at her — that crooked, unguarded grin she still seemed surprised by every time it happened, as if smiling were a language she'd only recently been granted permission to speak. "What will you do instead?"

Elara considered the road ahead of them, the long green climb toward the pass, toward the valley beyond it where the air itself had once refused to hold any magic at all. The valley that forgot. She wondered what a valley did with its memory once it got it back.

"I'm going home," she said, and heard how strange and easy the word sounded in her mouth. "Wherever that turns out to be."

### Sef

The mage tower had burned from the inside.

That was what Sef told himself, standing in the courtyard three weeks later, looking up at the black-gaped windows of the place where he'd learned his craft. It hadn't literally burned. But something in it had died — the thing his master had served, the quiet rot that had let a binding fester for years while everyone upstairs polished their credentials. The tower stood, but it was a ruin wearing good stonework.

They'd given him the keys anyway. That was the part that kept astonishing him. *Rebuild it honestly,* the new king had said, in a voice still rough as an unhealed scar, *or don't rebuild it at all.* Aldric had no patience left for ceremony. He'd spent three years as a puppet and come out of it with a horror of anyone who spoke in paragraphs.

So Sef swept out the interrogation records first. All of them. He built a bonfire in the courtyard that smoked for two days, and apprentices — new ones, frightened ones, kids from villages that had hidden their gifted for generations — watched him do it and began, slowly, to understand what kind of tower this was going to be.

He kept exactly one document. The original binding ritual, annotated now in his own hand with everything they'd learned too late: *temporary by design. Reversible by trust. Never meant to become a cage.* He pinned it above his desk where he'd have to look at it every day, a reminder that the most dangerous magic in the world wasn't the kind that shouted. It was the kind that whispered *this is necessary* until you believed it.

A raven landed on his windowsill one evening, cocked its head, and stared at him with an expression he'd learned to read during a year of traveling beside Casper Joubert.

"No, I haven't written to her yet," he said. "Yes, I know what you'll tell her."

The raven waited.

"She won't care about the tower. She'll want to know if I'm eating properly." He sighed. "Tell her yes. Tell her I sleep badly but honestly. Tell her—" He stopped, because the truth was embarrassing in its smallness. "Tell her the first class starts next month. Six students. Two of them can already talk to cats."

The raven took this under advisement and flew off, presumably to file its report.

### Dain

Dain Harrow planted turnips.

There was a version of this story, he knew, where he'd have ridden off to some frontier posting and drunk himself respectable and died with a sword in reach, and there would have been a certain grim poetry to it. Instead he had forty rows of turnips, a fence that needed mending, and a neighbor woman named Tolla who came by every few days ostensibly to borrow things she clearly owned herself, just to see whether he'd talk to her.

He was getting better at talking. It was slow work, harder than swordwork, and nobody saluted you at the end of it.

The farm had belonged to a family named Ostwick, before. Dain didn't like to think too hard about what the Hunt had done with the Ostwicks. So he fixed their fences and tended their fields and left the wildflowers growing thick around the well, because Mirei had once mentioned — in one of her rare, careful sentences — that bees needed flowers, and if he was going to be a farmer he was going to be a proper one.

Mirei wrote to him sometimes. Short letters, in careful schoolroom script, always beginning *Dear Captain Dain* even though he'd told her twice that he wasn't a captain anymore. He'd decided to stop correcting her. Some titles you earned by keeping your word, and he had kept his to her, and maybe that was worth more than the ones stamped on brass.

His last order as a soldier had been to execute captured gifted children. His first act as a farmer had been to plant something. He thought about that arithmetic a lot, out here, with the dirt under his nails and the sun coming up whether or not he deserved it.

Tolla appeared at the gate one afternoon holding a basket and no pretense whatsoever.

"Bread," she said. "You look terrible."

"I've been fighting bindweed."

"Who's winning?"

"The bindweed." He took the bread anyway. "I'm riding west next week. A wedding."

Tolla's eyebrows went up. "Yours?"

"Gods, no." Then, because honesty had gotten easier lately: "Well. Not mine exactly. There are two people I used to serve with. A hunt-captain and a beast-speaker. It's not a wedding yet. But it's going to be, and I want to be there when it happens, and I want to see the valley."

"The valley that ate the Hunt," Tolla said, with the particular relish country people reserved for stories about institutions getting their comeuppance.

"The same." Dain looked down the long rows of green, at the work that would keep, at the life that had somehow grown up around him while he wasn't paying attention. "I hear it remembers people now."

### Casper

The valley smelled different.

That was the first thing. Casper noticed it from the ridge, a full hour before they reached the border stones — the air coming up at them green and layered, thyme and wet stone and woodsmoke and underneath it all something she didn't have a word for anymore, because the word had always been *absence*, and the absence was gone.

For twenty-two years the Hollow had been a hole in the world's hearing. Magic slid off it like rain off slate. Now, standing on the ridge with Elara's hand warm in hers, Casper could feel nothing at all — her gift was a year dead and buried — but she could *see* it. Faint lights moving through the grass below, threads of color where the valley's old silence had finally let the world's magic seep back in like water finding an old riverbed.

"They say the wildflowers came back wrong," Elara said. "In a good way. Colors that shouldn't grow here."

"Mirei will love that."

"Mirei will try to catalog it."

They laughed together, easily, the way they'd learned to over a year of shared fires and shared silences. Casper leaned into her shoulder. Below them, the valley lay open under late-afternoon light, and the farms looked smaller than she remembered, and the mill looked exactly the same, and the whole of it looked — she searched for the word and found it embarrassingly simple — *welcoming*.

It hadn't, the last time she'd crested this ridge. The last time, she'd been running from hounds, and the valley had hidden her the way a mother hides a child behind her skirts: completely, and at terrible cost.

Now the elders met them at the border stones themselves. Old Marta, who had pledged cooperation with the Hunt with her chin high and her eyes anywhere else. The miller, whose son had once called Casper *goat-girl* for eleven straight years. Half the village behind them, awkward and shuffling, hats in hands.

"Casper Joubert," Marta said, and then seemed to run out of prepared remarks.

"We heard about the badger," the miller blurted, and went red. "Bracken. We heard. We should have — we should have done something, back when—"

"You couldn't have," Casper said. It didn't cost her much to say it anymore. Grief was a country she'd lived in long enough to know its roads. "None of us could. That's what the Hollow *was*. That's why it's not that anymore."

She'd expected it to be harder than this. She'd rehearsed coldness on the whole ride home, little speeches about how forgiveness wasn't owed. But the village had hung garlands on the mill, of all ridiculous places, and someone had put flowers on Bracken's grave marker — they'd given him a grave marker, a real one, carved stone, *BRAKKEN, WHO SPOKE TRUE* — and Marta was crying openly, and the miller's son, grown tall now, was holding his hat like it might escape.

"There's a house," Marta managed. "The Ostwick place is taken, but the shepherd's cottage on the south slope stands empty, and it's yours if you want it. All of yours." She gestured at the strange parade of them: a disgraced captain-general, a defrocked court mage, a former hunt lieutenant with dirt permanently under his nails, a girl of twelve who signed to the sparrows, and Casper. "We voted."

"You *voted*," Elara repeated flatly.

"Valley tradition," said the miller. "We vote on everything. Took us four ballots to agree on the garlands."

Casper looked at Elara. Elara looked at Casper. Something passed between them that needed no vote at all.

"We'll take it," Casper said.

### Mirei

The cottage had a loft, and the loft had a window, and the window faced the hillside where Casper said the badgers lived.

Mirei moved in on a Tuesday and had catalogued the entire household by Thursday. Casper: talks to goats even though the goats can't answer anymore, laughs more than she used to, hums when she bakes. Elara: sharpens things when she's thinking, pretends not to like the cat (there was a cat; it had adopted them within a day and appointed itself to Elara specifically). Sef: writes letters he doesn't send, eats standing up, teaches Mirei letters in exchange for her teaching him sparrow-sign, which he is bad at. Dain: visiting soon, bringing turnips, why turnips, she would ask him.

And the valley itself: louder every week. Not with sound — with the other thing, the shimmer under the grass that Mirei could feel in her teeth when she paid attention. Her gift was small so far. She could make a sparrow tilt its head. She could ask a moth to land on her finger and mostly it agreed. Casper said that was how all of it started, small, and that the size of the gift never mattered as much as what you did with the listening.

Mirei believed her. Casper had spoken with every creature in the realm at once, and unmade a king's prison, and gone deaf to all of it forever after — and she still listened better than anyone Mirei had ever met. With her eyes. With her whole face. Like listening was a thing you could give someone.

On the first warm evening of spring, Mirei sat in the loft window and practiced. A sparrow on the sill. *Hello,* she signed, small and careful. *My name is Mirei. This is my home now.*

The sparrow tilted its head.

And somewhere far off across the darkening hillside, from under a gorse bank where a clan of badgers had dug their sett in soil that remembered an old friend, something stirred, and turned its striped face toward the lighted window, and listened.

### Elara

She found Casper on the south slope at dusk, sitting on the flat stone they'd started calling the porch, watching the light go amber over the valley that had once refused to know her.

"Letter from the capital," Elara said, dropping down beside her. "They're offering the generalship again. Third time. The chancellor has begun including flattery."

"Does it work?"

"He called me *the savior of the realm's conscience*." She handed it over. Casper skimmed it, snorted, and fed it to the little cookfire they kept on the stone for exactly this purpose.

"That's four," Casper said. "You're building quite a collection."

"I'm going to send them a turnip next time. From Dain. Let them figure out the message." Elara stretched her legs out, boots nearly touching the fire ring, and let the quiet settle around them the way it had learned to — comfortable, unhurried, a quiet with people in it. Down in the cottage, lamplight glowed in the loft window where Mirei was supposed to be asleep and was almost certainly practicing sparrow-sign at the moths. Sef's letters from the tower arrived weekly now, longer each time, less guarded. Dain would ride over tomorrow with his absurd turnips and stay three days and pretend it was about the fence.

A year ago she'd commanded a hundred riders and answered to a crown. Tonight her whole jurisdiction was a hillside, a fire, and a woman who hummed when she baked.

Best posting she'd ever held.

"They asked me something else, actually," Elara said. "At the bottom. After the flattery." She hesitated, which was rare enough that Casper turned to look at her. "Next month. The anniversary. The king means to hold a remembrance in the capital square — for the gifted who were taken, for Bracken, for all of it. He wants us there. He wants—" She stopped, and tried again, and Elara Voss, who had faced tribunals and traitors' brands without blinking, found the sentence difficult. "He wants to kneel to you publicly. Again. And he wants the beasts honored. He says the ravens have been asking after you."

Casper was quiet a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere down the slope, a fox barked once — just a fox, just a fox sound, and still Casper's head turned toward it with the old attention, the listening that had outlived the magic.

"A year," she said finally. "Thirteenth moon come round again."

"Full moon," Elara confirmed. "The first one since."

Casper looked out at the darkening hills, at the gorse banks where badgers dug, at the valley that had learned her name. When she spoke, she was smiling.

"Well," she said. "We'd better bring the turnips."
