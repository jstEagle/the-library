## 40. Thirteenth Turn Complete

The moon came up over the Hollow the color of a fresh-laid egg, and Casper Joubert, who had once been the loudest voice in the kingdom and was now only a woman with sore knees and a crooked hat, sat down on her hillside to watch it rise.

She had claimed this hill the way she claimed most things in her life: by accident, then by stubbornness. It overlooked her farm — the same farm, the same sagging fence, the same goats who had never known she could speak to them and now never would, and who had adjusted to this information not at all, because as far as they were concerned she had always been a slightly disappointing human who occasionally produced apples. The fence still leaned east where Bracken used to dig under it. She had never fixed it. Some holes you leave alone.

Below her, lamplight moved through the windows of the farmhouse. Someone was arguing about bread.

She could hear it perfectly, of course. That was the joke the world had played on her, or maybe the gift it had left behind when it took the other thing away: she had spent twenty years learning to listen past words, and when the magic burned out of her on the thirteenth moon, all that listening stayed. Elara said it wasn't fair. Sef said it was the most interesting magical residue he'd ever documented and had filled two notebooks on the subject before Elara confiscated the notebooks for being smug. Mirei just said, with the devastating simplicity of an eleven-year-old, "You were always going to be like this anyway."

Casper had decided to take it as a compliment.

The argument below resolved itself — it usually did, around the time someone threatened to bake the disputed loaf into a doorstop — and the door banged open, and Dain came up the hill carrying two mugs and the particular walk of a man who has been sent out of his own kitchen.

"Elara says I'm impossible," he reported, handing her a mug. Cider. Warm. He'd remembered the honey.

"You are impossible."

"I made one comment about the kneading."

"One comment. About her kneading. In her kitchen. On the anniversary." Casper blew on the cider. "You're lucky she likes you."

"She doesn't like me."

"She let you build the south barn."

Dain considered this. The south barn had taken him four months, three redesigns, and one spectacular collapse that the goats still brought up at every opportunity, which is to say constantly, because goats hold grudges the way rivers hold water. Elara had stood in the wreckage with her arms crossed and said, "Try again," which from Elara was roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

"She let me rebuild the south barn," he corrected.

"That's what I said."

He settled onto the grass beside her with the careful grunt of a man whose farming back was no longer a soldier's back, and for a while they drank their cider and watched the moon climb. Down in the valley the Hollow lay silvered and quiet, the millpond flat as a coin, the orchards Sef had charmed into early bearing standing in neat dark rows. No fog tonight. The valley didn't need to forget anymore.

"Do you ever miss it?" Dain asked. He asked it every year, on this night, in exactly this tone — gentle, and braced for the answer.

Casper took her time. She took her time with most things now; hurry had been the old life's currency and she was out of it.

"I miss it like I miss my mother's voice," she said finally. "I couldn't tell you what it sounded like anymore. But I know it loved me."

Dain nodded slowly, and looked at the moon, and didn't say anything else, because he was a man who had learned — mostly from her, mostly the hard way — that some silences are full up.

* * *

They came up the hill in ones and twos, the way family does when nobody has to call them.

Sef arrived first, breathless, having run from the road because he'd seen the moon and refused to miss even a minute of the evening, and also because he had never once in his life entered a room calmly. He dropped onto the grass, produced a wrapped parcel from inside his coat, and presented it to Casper with the ceremony of a man unveiling a national treasure.

"What is it?"

"A gift."

"Sef."

"A book."

"Sef."

"It's *about* you," he said, wounded. "Well. Partly. It's about the unbinding. I finished the treatise. Master Corvel wrote the foreword — yes, *that* Corvel, we've made our peace, mostly, he's still insufferable but he's insufferable in a tower I rebuilt honestly, so." He pushed the parcel into her hands. "Chapter nine is yours. All of it. What you did in that square. I tried to write it plainly and I couldn't, so I wrote it the way it felt, and if the Academy rejects it for sentimentality then the Academy can go sit in a pond."

Casper unwrapped it. The leather cover was soft with handling already — Sef had clearly carried it around for weeks, rereading his own work, which was so purely Sef that she laughed. Chapter nine began: *There is no word in any court language for what happened next, which is fitting, because the girl who caused it had never needed court language to say anything true.*

"Oh, stop making that face," Sef said. "You'll wrinkle."

"I'm forty-one, Sef. The wrinkles are structural."

Mirei came up last, the way she always did, unhurried, with something alive in her cupped hands. This was new — the something-alive part. A year ago she had carried nothing but silence, and before that, the memory of a cage and a man with questions. Now she walked the hills like she owned the deed, and things came to her hands: beetles, fledglings fallen from nests, once — memorably — a very indignant duck.

Tonight it was a hedgehog.

"He followed me from the creek," she said, sitting down cross-legged and opening her hands so the hedgehog could decide its own business, which it did, unrolling and trundling off into the grass without so much as a thank-you. Mirei watched it go with deep satisfaction. "He's got a burrow under the third apple tree. He told me—" She stopped. Corrected herself, the way she'd learned to, the way they'd all learned to, translating the old fluency into plain speech. "*Showed* me. With the walking. They show me where things are now. I don't hear them like you did. But I see where they look."

"And where's he looking?" Casper asked.

"The cider," said Mirei. "Everyone's looking at the cider."

They passed the jug around. The moon climbed. And Casper sat in the middle of it — the disgraced hunt-captain who had burned her career to free a stranger and now couldn't bake bread without starting a war; the runaway mage who had helped bind a king and spent a year unbinding himself instead; the farmhand who had once been a lieutenant and had chosen children over orders and never once regretted it aloud; the small grave-quiet girl who spoke in glances and animal directions and was, everyone agreed, going to be either a great healer or a menace, possibly both — and felt the old ache turn over in her chest, familiar as a scar, and settle.

Belonging everywhere, known by no one, she had written once, in the long ago, describing her valley. She'd been wrong, in the end, about both halves. She belonged here, specifically, to these people, embarrassingly and permanently. And they knew her — all of her, the beast-speaker and the silence after, the cowardice and the courage, the night she'd wanted to quit and the morning Mirei had said her first full sentence (*the animals are already coming*) and Casper had gotten up off the floor and kept going.

Known by everyone. It should have terrified her. It had terrified her, for about a month, and then Elara had caught her crying over a goat and said, "This is what it costs. You knew the price when you paid it," and Casper had laughed until she couldn't breathe, because it was true, and because Elara was holding the goat, and because love, it turned out, was just duty that had come home.

* * *

It was nearly full dark when the badger came.

Casper heard him before she saw him — not with magic, never again with magic, but with the plain attention she'd spent a lifetime sharpening: the particular shuffle-and-stop of a heavy body moving through dry grass, the pause, the sniffing. The others heard it too and went still, because everyone on this hill knew what night this was, and everyone on this hill knew what came with it.

He came out of the bracken at the hill's crest, gray-striped and broad as a footstool, and stopped at the edge of the firelight to inspect them. Old, this one. Gray about the muzzle. One ear tattered from some long-settled argument with a fox.

Bracken's kin. Not Bracken — Casper had buried Bracken under the leaning fence two winters ago, with Elara's sword driven at the head of the grave the way soldiers honor their own, because Elara had insisted a badger who died holding a tunnel open against armed men deserved a soldier's honors, and there had been no argument worth having. But kin. The valley's badgers had known Bracken, and Bracken's people remembered their dead the way badgers remember everything: thoroughly, and along bloodlines.

The others held still. Even Sef, who had never in his life held still, held still.

The badger shuffled forward, ignored the entire assembled company of heroes, and settled himself against Casper's side with the weight and confidence of a creature taking possession of furniture he had owned for years. His warmth soaked through her cloak. He smelled of earth and apples and the creek.

"Hello," Casper said softly.

The badger said nothing, because he was a badger, and she was only a woman, and no word was needed — none had ever been needed, not really, not between her and Bracken either, though it had taken her a lifetime and a war to understand that. The talking had never been the gift. The listening had been the gift, and the listening was hers forever, bought and paid for, and it worked on badgers and kings and sisters and the sound of bread being argued over in a warm kitchen below.

She put her hand on his back, in the thick fur behind the ears, where Bracken had liked it.

"Yeah," she said, to him, to all of them, to the moon. "Me too."

* * *

Later — much later, when Dain had dozed off mid-sentence and Sef was reciting chapter nine from memory to an audience of one deeply asleep hunt-captain and one wide-eyed eleven-year-old — Casper stayed awake and watched the thirteenth moon make its slow way across the sky.

One year since the square. One year since ten thousand throats had opened at once and the crown-thing had shrieked out of a king like smoke out of a chimney, and the binding had unraveled, and her own song had gone out of her like a candle pinched between wet fingers. One year since Aldric, hollowed and mortal and horrified, had knelt in the mud of his own capital and repealed the Hunt with a voice that shook, and meant it, and had spent the year since proving he meant it, which was harder and better.

One year since she'd woken in a strange bed in a silent world and grieved like a limb was gone.

It was gone. She checked sometimes, quietly, hopefully, the way you check a tooth. She leaned over the creek fence and listened for the fish. Nothing. She stood in the pasture at dawn and waited for the goats' names to arrive in her head. Nothing — only the goats themselves, blunt and opinionated, wanting apples. The silence where the song had been was permanent, and honest, and hers.

But here was the thing she'd learned, the thing she suspected she'd been learning her whole life in the Hollow without knowing it: the beasts had never needed her voice. They'd needed someone willing to stand in a square and speak *for* them, and the speaking had cost everything except the one thing that mattered, which was that she had meant it. And meaning it didn't require magic. Meaning it required showing up, on hillsides, on anniversaries, with warm cider and a crooked hat, and staying.

The badger breathed slow and heavy against her side. Below, the farmhouse lamp went out. Mirei had fallen asleep against Elara's shoulder, and Elara — who had once ordered prisoners marched in chains and had never forgiven herself, and had been forgiven anyway, which was the whole miracle — sat unmoving so as not to wake her, staring at the moon with the expression of a woman counting blessings and finding the arithmetic embarrassing.

Casper reached over and squeezed her hand. Elara squeezed back. Neither of them said anything. None was needed.

The moon crossed the top of the sky and began, slowly, to come down the far side, and the thirteenth turn completed itself the way all turns do — quietly, whether or not anyone is watching — and Casper Joubert sat on her hill with her hand in a badger's fur and her family scattered asleep around her, and thought: *this*. Not the square, not the song, not the terrible bright moment when every beast in the realm had spoken through her at once. This. The ordinary, unmagical, hard-won after.

The badger grumbled in his sleep and pressed closer.

She stayed until the stars faded, and then, carefully, she woke her family, and they went down together toward the lamplit house — all of them, laughing softly about the bread, leaving the fence hole open behind them for whoever came next.
