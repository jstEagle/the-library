## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The goats always stopped at the tree line.

Casper had watched them do it a hundred mornings and a hundred evenings—the herd flowing down the high pasture like grey water, unhurried, content, until the first birch threw its shadow across the path. Then the lead doe, old Marigold with her crooked ear, would plant her hooves as if the ground itself had turned to glass, and the whole flock would mill and bleat until Casper walked ahead of them through the trees, and the spell of their fear broke, and they followed.

They were right to be afraid, in their animal way. Casper had long ago stopped being afraid, which was perhaps the stranger thing.

The valley had no name on any map that mattered. The crown's cartographers called it the Hollow Reach, a smear of green between two shoulders of mountain, useful for nothing but sheep and silence. The people who lived there—four hundred souls in eleven villages strung along the river like beads on a wet cord—called it simply home, and said it the way you say a word you have never thought to question.

But Casper knew what the valley was. Knew it the way you know a scar: not by looking at it but by feeling where the skin goes numb.

Magic died at the tree line.

Not faded. Not thinned. Died—struck down at the boundary of leaf and shadow as cleanly as a blade taken to a rope. Casper had tested it once, years ago, in the reckless season of their seventeenth year, when the hum under their breastbone still felt like a promise instead of a debt. They had stood at the edge of the birches with a single word held behind their teeth—a small word, a warm word, one of the first their grandmother's grandmother's voice had taught them—and stepped forward into the shade.

The word had gone out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

And something else had gone out too, some bright thread of memory, though for the life of them Casper could never say what. That was the cruelty of the valley. It did not only kill magic. It kept accounts. Every time the gift crossed the line and guttered, the valley took its tithe—not in blood, not in coin, but in remembering. Casper had come away from that experiment with a hole in their mind shaped like something loved, and no name to give the hole, and a strange conviction, arriving like weather, that they had deserved it.

So they stayed inside the wards. Everyone stayed inside the wards. That was the arrangement, though no one spoke of it as an arrangement. The valley kept them safe from the world, and the world, presumably, forgot them, and the gift slept in the blood like a stone at the bottom of a well, humming sometimes in dreams, and otherwise quiet.

Casper rose before dawn because the goats demanded it and because the hour suited them. In the blue-grey light the village of Aldermist looked soft-edged, smoke already rising from the baker's chimney, the river running loud with snowmelt from the peaks. Casper filled the pails, counted heads—thirty-one, all present, Marigold complaining as usual about the state of the world—and led the flock up the switchback path toward the high pasture, staff in hand, boots finding the ruts they had found ten thousand times before.

It was a good life. Casper told themselves this most mornings, the way you press a thumb against a bruise to check whether it still hurts.

* * *

The sword-forms came after the goats were turned out to graze.

There was a flat shelf of granite above the pasture where the wind came clean off the snowline, and there, alone, Casper practiced. Not fencing—no one in the valley fenced, and a swordspeaker did not fence anyway—but forms. Sequences of movement married to sequences of sound, passed down mouth to mouth through nine generations of an order that officially had never existed.

Casper knew forty-one forms. They had been taught twenty-two. The rest they had reconstructed from fragments: a phrase here, half a stance there, gleaned from the old woman Sef who had died when Casper was twelve, from a water-stained book hidden in the floor of the root cellar, from dreams so vivid they left the taste of iron in the mouth upon waking.

The forms were beautiful even silent. Casper moved through them barefoot on the cold stone, breath steaming, the practice blade—an honest length of ash wood, worn glossy at the grip—turning and turning. First form: *the river parts*. Second: *the reed remembers wind*. Third through ninth, the foundation set, the grammar of the art. And then the ones that required words.

Casper spoke them quietly. Always quietly. A whisper was enough inside the wards; the words did not need force here, only shape.

"*Vael.*"

The syllable left their lips and fell dead, as it always did, as it was meant to. But beneath the breastbone, deep down where the gift coiled like a sleeping animal, something stirred. The hum. Casper felt it rise through the ribs and settle in the wrists, a vibration like a struck bell heard through a wall, and for one suspended moment the morning seemed to hold its breath—the wind pausing in the grass, Marigold lifting her crooked head, the light on the snowfield going strange and attentive—

And then it passed. The wind resumed. The goat grazed. The light was only light.

Casper lowered the wooden blade and stood breathing hard, heart hammering, cheeks flushed with a pleasure that felt dangerously close to joy. This was the closest the valley allowed them to come to what they were. A bell that could not ring, rung anyway, softly, every morning, so the shape of ringing would not be forgotten.

Because that was the other thing the valley took, if you let it. Not just memories of magic. Memories of *why*. Casper had learned to write things down early—names, dates, the few facts of their inheritance—in a cramped hand in a book kept wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose hearthstone. *You are a swordspeaker. The words are real. Do not cross the tree line. Do not speak the deep forms aloud. You swore an oath. Keep it.*

The book did not say what the oath was. Casper had written that much honestly, years ago: *I do not remember what I promised, or to whom, or what it cost. I remember only that I meant it.*

Some mornings that sentence was the truest thing in their life.

* * *

Midday Casper spent in the village, as they did on market days, trading cheese for salt and flour and the small gossip that passed for news in a place the world had agreed to overlook. Aldermist was a huddle of stone and timber around a squat church whose bell hadn't rung in living memory—there being, the elders said, nothing worth ringing it for. The elders said a great many things in that tone. Nothing worth knowing lay beyond the pass. Nothing worth wanting grew beyond the tree line. Nothing worth grieving had ever been lost, because how could you grieve what you could not name?

Old Maren, who kept the candle shop and remembered more than she admitted, caught Casper's sleeve outside the well.

"You look pale," she said. Her eyes, milky with cataract, nonetheless fixed on Casper with uncomfortable precision. "Bad night?"

"Bad dream," Casper admitted.

"The same one?"

Casper hesitated. Maren was the only person in the valley who knew anything—knew that Casper's family had been different, knew that certain books existed, knew to look away at certain moments. She had never once asked a direct question in nineteen years, and Casper had never once answered one directly, and between those two courtesies a kind of friendship had grown.

"The same," Casper said.

Maren nodded slowly, working her jaw as if chewing on something bitter. "My mother used to say a dream that comes back is a letter that keeps arriving. Somebody wants to tell you something." She released Casper's sleeve and patted it instead, twice, like sealing a letter of her own. "Or warn you."

Then she shuffled off toward her shop, and Casper stood by the well with the market noise washing over them, feeling the truth of it settle cold in the stomach.

Because the dream was always the same, and it was not really a dream at all. It was too orderly for dreaming, too patient. In it, Casper stood in darkness that was not empty—it was *architectural*, dark with weight and purpose, the dark of somewhere built deep underground—and far below, or perhaps very near, a voice was calling a name.

Their name. But spoken in a way no one in the waking valley ever said it: softly, familiarly, with the particular fondness of someone who has said it ten thousand times and never once tired of it.

"Casper," the voice would say. "Casper. Can you hear me? It's been so long. Please try to remember."

And Casper, in the dream, would press their palms flat against the stone—which was warm, impossibly warm, warm as a living body—and strain toward the voice, and feel the memory of a face hovering just out of reach like a word on the tip of the tongue, and wake with tears already drying on their cheeks and no idea whose grief they belonged to.

There was one thing the dream gave them, one solid fact among all the fog. Not a face. Not a place. Just a name, surfacing each time like a drowned thing bobbing up:

*Lior.*

Casper had written it in the hidden book the first week, after the third or fourth recurrence. Beneath it, in the same cramped hand: *I don't know who this is. I think I'm supposed to.*

* * *

Evening brought the goats down again, and the ritual pause at the tree line, and Casper walking ahead through the birches as the light went long and gold and the shadows pooled black between the trunks. They always walked slowly here. Partly for the goats' sake. Partly because the tree line deserved a certain respect, the way a border between countries does, or a grave.

Today, passing beneath the largest birch—the one the village children called the Grandmother, its bark scarred with generations of initials—Casper stopped.

Something was wrong with the light.

Not the light itself. The air. At the very edge of perception, where the eye meets the brain's refusal, the air between two trunks shimmered faintly, the way heat shimmers over a summer road. Casper had seen the wards described once, in the water-stained book: *a veil woven of forgetting, paid for in blood and sealed in sorrow.* They had assumed it was metaphor. Books full of truths tended to dress them up.

But now the veil shivered, and in the shiver Casper saw—or thought they saw, standing very still while thirty-one goats pressed anxiously against their legs—a flicker of movement on the far side. Beyond the valley. Out in the world where magic lived and hunted.

A figure. Small, distant, stumbling along the forest's outer edge. Moving toward the wards.

Moving *through* them—that was the impossible part. The wards did not admit the gifted; that was their entire purpose, the covenant's first clause, older than any living memory. Nothing touched by power could cross. Yet the figure came on, lurching, one hand pressed to their side as if holding something in, and where they passed the shimmering bent and warped like heat-haze disturbed by a hand, and did not stop them.

Casper's heart began to hammer—not with the sweet fear of the morning's forms, but with an older, colder recognition. The hum beneath their breastbone woke unbidden, rising without a single word spoken, answering something in the stranger the way one bell answers another across a valley.

The figure staggered. Fell to one knee at the exact boundary where the birch shadows met the open grass. Even at this distance Casper could see the dark stain spreading down their side, could see the way the light around them *flickered*, guttering like a candle in wind—ward-sickness, some buried fragment of knowledge supplied, the poison that ate the gifted when they lingered too near a veil.

The stranger lifted their head. Across fifty paces of evening air, their eyes found Casper's.

And the voice from the dream—patient, fond, unbearably sad—rose without warning through Casper's mind, saying *please try to remember*, and the name *Lior* flared behind their eyes like a struck match, and the hum in their chest swelled until their teeth ached with it.

The stranger's mouth moved. No sound carried across the distance. But Casper read the shape of it easily enough, the way you read the shape of your own name shouted across a crowded room:

*Help me.*

Behind Casper, down the slope, the bell of the church that never rang began, for the first time in living memory, to toll.

* * *

Casper did not remember deciding to move. Their feet simply carried them—up the path, past the milling goats, through the grass gone silver with dusk—to the place where the stranger knelt bleeding at the edge of everything safe.

Up close the stranger was young, younger than Casper, with close-cropped dark hair plastered to her forehead and a face sharp as a folded knife. The wound beneath her hand was long and shallow but wrong somehow, weeping a darkness that was not entirely blood. Ward-sickness, the buried knowledge insisted. It eats from the inside. She does not have long.

"They'll see me," the stranger rasped. Her voice was rough as gravel, but her eyes were clear and desperate and fixed on Casper with terrible hope. "The bell—they'll come. Please. I can't go back out there. They're hunting us. They're hunting *all of us* now."

Every law Casper had ever sworn to—every unwritten clause of the oath they could not remember, every rule of the silence kept for nine generations—rose up in a wall between them and the stranger. *Turn away. Walk back down the hill. Let the wards do what they were made to do.*

Instead, Casper knelt, and pulled the stranger's arm across their shoulders, and lifted.

The pain hit immediately—not physical, nothing so simple. It was the oath itself, waking, objecting, a white-hot wire drawn tight through the center of the chest, and with it came a flash of something erased: a kitchen, sunlight, a laugh, a hand reaching for theirs—gone before it could become a picture, leaving only the ache of its absence, the shape of a hole.

Casper gasped. Kept walking. One step, then another, hauling the stranger's weight through the birches, past the Grandmother tree, down into the valley where magic came to die.

"It hurts," the stranger whispered, watching Casper's face. "Doesn't it. What you're doing."

"Yes," Casper said.

"Then why—"

"I don't know," Casper said honestly, and found that it was the truest thing they had said in years. "I think I owed someone. I just can't remember who."

Below them, the bell tolled on, and lights were beginning to move in the village, and the first voices were rising on the wind—voices calling Casper's name, asking where they were, asking what they had done.

The stranger's blood soaked warm through Casper's shirt. The hum beneath their breastbone sang like a wire in a storm. And somewhere far beneath the mountains of the world, patient as stone, a voice that Casper could not remember went on calling a name that Casper could not forget.
