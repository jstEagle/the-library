## 2. A Stranger at the Wards

The goats knew before I did.

I had learned to read them the way other people read weather—the way they stopped cropping the grass at the tree line and turned their heads all at once, ears forward, toward a place where nothing should have been. Old Marta's flock had done it the autumn she died, staring at the pines as if something in their black depth had spoken her name. The village said it was wolves. The village said a great many things that kept the silence comfortable.

But there were no wolves. There was never anything. That was the whole point of the valley.

Magic could not cross the wards. Whatever moved beyond the tree line—whatever hunted or hungered or whispered—stopped dead at the boundary like a wave against a sea wall. I had lived my twenty-six years inside that stillness, and I trusted it the way you trust stone under your feet: not with thought, but with the body, with every step taken without looking down.

So when the goats went silent and turned as one, I felt the wrongness before I understood it, a cold thread drawn up through the soles of my boots.

"Easy," I told them, though my voice came out thinner than I meant it to. "Nothing's there."

Bruma, the oldest nanny, looked back at me with her flat amber eyes, and I swear she did not believe me either.

* * *

I found the stranger an hour later, where the meadow grass began its long climb toward the pines, in the exact seam of ground where the valley ended and the world began.

She was lying on her side in the wet grass like something the mountain had spat out. A woman, young, dark-haired, dressed in road-leathers gone gray with dust and stained darker at the shoulder with blood. Her breathing made a sound I recognized from lambs caught too long in the birth-sack—a thin, tearing whistle, each breath fought for and half-won.

And around her, the air *moved*.

That was the impossible part. Not the blood, not the stranger herself—strangers came, sometimes, peddlers and pilgrims who walked the high road past our gate without stopping—but the way the light bent near her skin. It shimmered off her in slow ripples, like heat off summer stone, except it was autumn and cold and the shimmer had a color to it, faintly silver, faintly green, the color of pond ice in the last week of thaw.

Ward-sickness. I knew the word from stories, from the old songs the elders pretended not to remember. When a gifted soul crossed a ward uninvited, the ward fought back. It flayed the magic out of them layer by layer, and the flaying bled. Not always from the skin. Sometimes from somewhere deeper, somewhere the eye couldn't follow but the body could feel, the way you can feel a storm building behind hills.

She should not have been able to cross at all. That was the law of the world as surely as the law of falling. Magic died at the tree line; the gifted turned back or burned out against the boundary like moths against glass. Every story agreed. Every elder repeated it. The valley's peace was bought on that certainty, sealed in treaty, paid for in—

Paid for in something. The thought slid sideways when I reached for it, the way a dream detail slides away at waking. Paid for in some coin I couldn't name. I stood over the bleeding woman and felt that absence like a missing tooth, my tongue going again and again to the gap.

Her eyes opened. Gray, fever-bright, fixed on me with a ferocity that had no fear in it anywhere, which frightened me more than blood would have.

"You're real," she rasped. "Good. I wasn't sure—" A cough folded her nearly double. When she straightened, there was fresh red on her lips. "Listen. They're coming behind me. Four riders, maybe five. If they reach the wards—"

"No one can reach the wards," I said. "No one crosses."

Something like a laugh broke out of her, jagged and disbelieving. "Tell that to my ribs." She tried to push herself upright, failed, and lay back in the grass, chest heaving. Up close I could see the silver-green shimmer guttering along her arms like a lamp running out of oil. Dying light. "You're one of them. Valley-born. You have that look—like nothing bad has ever happened within walking distance of you."

"Nothing has."

"Then enjoy it while it lasts." Her eyes were closing. She forced them open again, and this time I saw the fear arrive, late and enormous. "Please. I didn't crawl across a ward-line on my hands and knees to die in a goat pasture. Help me or don't, but decide fast, because if I pass out I'm not waking up."

I should have walked away. I want that written plainly, because everything after follows from the fact that I did not. The law of silence was the first law of the valley, older than any elder, taught to children before they could write their names: *what lies beyond is not our concern; what comes from beyond does not exist.* Turn away. Report nothing. Forget quickly. The valley endured because the valley did not look.

I looked. And what I felt, looking down at her, was not duty or pity or even curiosity, but recognition—sharp and inexplicable, like hearing a voice through a wall and knowing whose it is without knowing how you know. Something about the set of her jaw. Something about the way she refused to beg even while begging.

"Lior," I said. The name came out of me unbidden, from some locked room in my own head, and meant nothing and everything. I didn't know why I'd said it. My heart was suddenly hammering as though I'd run the length of the meadow.

The woman frowned. "What?"

"Nothing." I crouched and took her arm across my shoulders. She weighed less than she should have, as if the ward had already begun unmaking parts of her. "Can you walk?"

"I can pretend convincingly."

* * *

Getting her to the croft without being seen was the longest hour of my life, and I include in that count every hour since.

There are no true roads in the valley, only sheep tracks and the one cart-way to the gate, so we went the way the goats go—along the stream cut, through the alder scrub, keeping to hollows. Twice I heard hooves on the far side of the ridge, muffled by distance, and both times I pressed her down into the bracken and lay beside her with my hand over her mouth, feeling her breath hot and ragged against my palm, counting the hoofbeats until they faded.

Once, lying there, she whispered against my fingers: "Why are you doing this? You don't know me."

I had no answer then. I have a better one now, but it belongs to a later chapter of this story, and I will not spend it early.

The pain started when we crossed the stream.

Not her pain—mine. It came up through my chest like a hook being set, sudden and specific, a white-hot point just beneath the sternum that pulled tighter with every step I took away from the tree line, away from the place where I'd found her, deeper into the valley's quiet heart. By the time we reached the croft door I was sweating despite the cold, and my teeth were clenched so hard my jaw ached, and I could not have explained to anyone what hurt or why, only that it was the pain of a thing torn, of a thread pulled taut past its strength.

The oath, some part of me whispered. You swore. You swore, and this is the cost of the first crack.

I did not remember swearing. That was the horror of it—I knew I had sworn, the way you know you have a spine, structural and unquestioned, but the moment itself was gone, scooped out, leaving only the shape of its absence and the words' residue humming in my bones. *Keep the silence. Keep the peace. Keep—* Keep whom? Keep what? The sentence ended in fog.

I got us inside, barred the door, and built up the fire with shaking hands.

* * *

She slept through the afternoon and most of the night in my bed, and I sat in the chair by the hearth with my sword across my knees and watched her breathe.

The sword deserves mention, because it is the only inheritance I claim. It had come to me from no one I could name—found among my effects as a child, the elders said, brought from *before*, which was their word for everything they would not discuss. Plain blade, worn grip, and along the fuller a line of script too worn to read. When I practiced the forms in the dawn mist—and I practiced them compulsively, without ever being taught, my body moving through patterns no one in the valley knew—the blade hummed. Not audibly. In the teeth. In the breastbone. A held note, patient as a struck bell waiting to be allowed to ring.

In the valley, it never rang. Nothing rang. That was the bargain.

The stranger stirred near midnight, and I was on my feet before I knew I'd moved, sword half-raised, absurd as a man defending a one-room croft against a dying woman.

"Put it down," she said hoarsely. "If I wanted to hurt you, I'd have done it while you were carrying me. You carry like a mule with opinions, by the way."

"You talk a lot for someone who was dying."

"Dying's boring. I recommend it briefly, then stop." She pushed herself up against the headboard, wincing, and studied me across the firelight. The shimmer on her skin had dimmed further, barely visible now, a candle seen through fog. "Vess," she said. "My name. Since you're apparently committing crimes for me, you should have it."

"Casper."

"Casper." She tried it out, tilted her head. "Valley name. Old ones, the pre-treaty names—they still use those here?" Something flickered across her face when she said *treaty*, quick as a fish turning, and was gone. "How much do you know about what's happening out there?"

"The crown hunts gifted blood. Everyone knows that much. It hasn't touched us. It can't touch us."

"It reached the wards today. I reached the wards today." She held up her hand, and for a moment let the silver-green light gather in her palm—a small, exhausted flame, beautiful and wrong in my hearth-lit room, the first magic I had ever seen with my waking eyes. "Your valley isn't hidden, Casper. It's *kept*. There's a difference, and the difference has a price, and I don't think you know what you've been paying." She closed her fist and the light died. "Neither did I, until three days ago."

The hook behind my sternum pulled tight. I put my hand flat against my chest and waited for it to ease, and it did not, and Vess watched me do it with an expression I would learn well in the months ahead—noticed, filed, understood more than she said.

"That pain," she said quietly. "Started when you decided to help me?"

"Yes."

"Then whatever you swore to—and you've sworn to something, I can see it in how you hold yourself, people who've sworn oaths stand like fence posts—it knows what you're doing. And it objects." She settled back against the wall, gray-faced, spent. "Sleep. I'll watch. And Casper—when the riders come to your gate, and they will, because wards that bleed a fugitive leave a mark—think hard about which law you serve. The one you remember. Or the one you don't."

I did not sleep. I sat with the sword across my knees until the fire burned to coals, and the hum in the blade rose and fell like breathing, and behind my sternum the hook held its tension, patient as a debt.

Near dawn, hooves sounded on the cart-way. Many of them, unhurried, certain.

Through the shutter-crack I watched them come down the valley road: riders in the crown's gray-and-gold, and at their head a banner I did not recognize—a mountain wrought in silver thread, with a crown of open iron above its peak. At the gate they halted. One rider dismounted, unrolled a proclamation stiff with wax and ribbon, and nailed it to the ancient wood with four strokes of a hammer that echoed across the whole sleeping valley like a knell.

Vess came to stand beside me at the shutter. Together, in the gray first light, we read the words from across the distance:

*By order of the Crown: all persons of gifted blood within these bounds are hereby placed under the crown's protection. Surrender them at the gate within three days, and be rewarded. Harbor them, and be counted among them.*

"'Protection,'" Vess breathed. Her voice shook, and it was not fear in it—or not only fear. It was hatred with nowhere to go. "That seal. That's not the king's seal. That's the court beneath the mountain." Her knuckles went white against the shutter-frame. "They took my sister wearing that seal. They called it protection too."

Below, the riders wheeled their horses and rode on to the next farmstead, unhurried, certain, patient as the debt behind my sternum.

Three days. I looked at Vess, and Vess looked at me, and neither of us said aloud what both of us already knew: that I had already chosen, that the choice had been made in the bracken by the stream when I pressed my hand over her mouth instead of calling out—that the oath had known it too, and had begun, even then, to collect.
