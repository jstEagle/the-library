## 3. The Proclamation

Vess woke before dawn to the sound of hooves she should not have been able to hear.

That was the first wrongness. The valley swallowed sound the way it swallowed everything else—magic, memory, the sharp edges of a fugitive's fear. She had been sleeping in Casper's hayloft for three days now, wrapped in wool that smelled of lanolin and old summers, and every morning the silence had pressed against her ears like wet cloth. But this morning there were horses. Many horses. Coming up the valley road at a pace that meant ceremony rather than chase.

She lay still in the straw and listened the way her mother had taught her, back when listening was still a skill and not a survival tactic. Six riders, maybe eight. Shod hooves—the army shod its horses with iron stamped with the crown's sigil, and iron on packed earth had a particular ring, a bell-note buried under the clatter. Chain mail shifting against leather. The creak of a standard-pole turning in its socket.

And underneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wall: the hum of wards being fed.

Vess sat up too fast and her vision swam. The ward-sickness had not left her; it had only learned to be quieter, coiling in her blood like a snake digesting. Three days inside the valley's dead air had slowed it, but it woke with her every morning, hungry, reminding her that she was a lamp running low on oil. Her fingertips still prickled when she flexed them. Her shadow still moved a half-beat late.

She climbed down the loft ladder without waking Casper, who slept below by the hearth with their sword across their knees even in sleep—a habit Vess recognized from people who had something to guard besides themselves. She eased the door open onto cold blue dark and the smell of frost on grass.

The village gate stood at the end of the lane where the road narrowed between two leaning pines. It was less a gate than an idea of one: a timber arch, a hanging lantern, a notice-board gone grey with weather and older proclamations about tithes and road taxes. Beyond it, the tree line. Beyond the tree line, the world.

The riders stopped just outside it.

Of course they did. Vess felt the truth of it in her teeth before her mind caught up. They could not cross. Whatever bargain had bought this valley its peace—and she was beginning to understand, in the slow way one understands a debt, that there *had* been a bargain—it held even against the crown's own horsemen. Eight of them sat their mounts in the frost-fog like figures carved on a tomb, and none of them set so much as a boot past the shadow of the arch.

They wore black and silver. Not the king's colors. The king's men wore scarlet, and Vess had seen enough of scarlet in her life to know it on sight, to flinch from it in dreams. This was different. This was the livery of the court beneath the mountain: black dyed so deep it drank the light, silver thread worked into patterns that hurt to look at directly because they were not quite patterns—they were script, ward-script, the written form of binding-speech. Vess had seen that script once before, branded into the shoulder of a woman who came to her village selling charms, and the woman had wept while she talked about how much it had cost her to have it put there, and how much more it would cost to have it taken off.

At the head of the column, a rider dismounted. A herald, by the scroll-case at his hip, by the way he walked—too straight, too deliberate, a man performing his own importance. He carried a hammer and a sheaf of papers and a long nail held between his lips like a rose stem.

He crossed under the arch without hesitation.

Vess went very still behind the woodpile at the corner of the barn. So the wards kept out soldiers but not servants. Or perhaps the wards made distinctions she couldn't see. Or perhaps—her stomach turned slowly over—perhaps the mountain's people had never needed the wards' permission at all. Perhaps they simply chose, day after day, year after year, to leave the valley alone. Until they didn't.

The herald nailed the proclamation to the notice-board with four blows of the hammer, each one cracking across the frozen morning like a small execution. Then he unrolled a second copy and read it aloud in a voice trained to carry, a voice with no warmth in it at all, a voice like a ledger being recited.

*"By order of the Crown Sovereign, Keeper of the Deep Throne, Warden of Voices: let it be known to all dwellers within the chartered vale—"*

The chartered vale. Even the name was a cage dressed in courtesy.

*"—that the Crown, in its mercy, extends protection to those of gifted blood. Let all such persons present themselves at the valley gate within seven days, that they may be conveyed to the capital for the Crown's protection. Shelter, sustenance, and honor shall be provided. Refusal shall be construed as treason against the throne, and the harboring of gifted persons as complicity therein."*

Protection. Vess closed her eyes. She knew that word. She had heard it before, seven years ago, spoken in her mother's kitchen by a man in black and silver who had smiled with all his teeth and called it *protection* when he wrote her sister's name in his book. Sera had been fourteen. Sera had lit a candle with a breath once, laughing, showing off, and a neighbor had seen, and neighbors always saw, and the man in black and silver had come with his book and his smile and his word like *protection*, and their mother had signed because what else did you sign when the alternative was spelled out in the same gentle voice? And Sera had gone up the mountain road in a wagon with other children, waving, because she thought it was an honor, because everyone had told her it was an honor—

—and the letters that came afterward were printed, unsigned, and said only that she was *well* and *studying* and *serving the Crown*, and then the letters stopped, and the wagon never came back down.

Seven days. The herald rolled up his copy. He looked at the notice-board the way a man looks at a grave he has dug for someone else, and Vess thought, with a clarity that felt like a blade laid flat against her skin: *he knows what he is nailing up.* He wasn't proud of it. He was just obedient. That was somehow worse.

Then he mounted, the column wheeled, and the hoofbeats faded down the valley road toward the lowlands, taking the sound of the world with them, leaving the silence to close back over everything like water over a stone.

* * *

Vess stood in the frost until her feet went numb, staring at the paper.

It was the seal that held her. Black wax, thick as a coin, pressed with a device she had hoped never to see again: a mountain split by a vertical line, and within the split, a small radiant shape—not a sun, though it was meant to suggest one. An eye. Or a flame. Or a soul, rendered in the abstract geometry of the deep court, the thing they harvested and hoarded and fed to their throne, stylized until it looked like nothing at all. Beneath it, in the court's high script, a motto Vess had seen on the wax of her sister's last letter:

*Ex luce, lex.* From light, law.

She had spent seven years telling herself the mountain was far away. That was the trick of mountains; they let you believe distance was safety. But here it was, its seal nailed to a board in a valley that was supposed to be beyond reach, and the words beneath it were not a summons but a harvest-list. *Gifted blood.* She knew what happened to gifted blood in the capital. She had paid a broker in Greyhollow half of everything she had to learn it, and the knowledge sat in her chest like swallowed glass: the vessels, the shelves, the quiet rooms where souls were kept in lattice-glass and murmured to in the dark by keepers whose job was not cruelty but *maintenance*.

Sera would be twenty-one now. If she was anything at all anymore. If a soul kept seven years in glass remained a person, or became only a voice, a function, a candle burning in someone else's chandelier.

Dread was a familiar country to Vess. She had lived in it so long she knew its roads. But this morning dread did something new: it cooled, and hardened, and took an edge. She felt it happen physically—a tightening behind the sternum, a settling of the shoulders, the body's old animal arithmetic converting fear into purpose. Seven days. The proclamation gave them seven days, which meant the mountain believed seven days was enough, which meant the mountain already knew things about this valley that the valley did not know about itself.

Which meant Casper's secret was already a countdown.

* * *

Casper found her at the gate. Vess heard the door of the house, the crunch of boots on frost, and didn't turn until the presence settled beside her—tall, quiet, wrapped in a shepherd's coat with a sword worn like an apology.

"They came at dawn," Casper said. Not a question. Their eyes were on the paper, and Vess watched them read it twice, watched their jaw tighten on some word. "I heard the horses. I told myself it was drovers."

"Drovers don't nail things."

"No." Casper reached out and touched the edge of the proclamation, careful, the way one touches a wound to learn its depth. "This seal. I've seen it on the treaty-stone at the shrine. The founding accord. I always thought it was just... the old kingdom's mark. Everyone's grandfather's mark." A pause. "You know it."

It wasn't a question either. Vess realized she had gone rigid, and made herself breathe. This was the problem with honesty; it wanted everything, all at once, and she had survived this long by rationing herself. But she had slept under this person's roof. She had eaten their bread. And the paper on the board had her sister's whole history folded inside it.

"The court beneath the mountain," Vess said. "That's what my mother called it, the night they took Sera. Not the crown. Not the capital. The *court beneath*. Like it was a thing that lived under the country and wore the country as a coat." She touched the black wax, and the script in the silver thread seemed to shift, the way script does when it is alive and resentful of being read. "My sister is up there. Has been for seven years. She breathed a candle alight when she was fourteen and someone told, and they came with a book and a smile and they called it protection."

Casper said nothing for a long moment. In the silence, Vess heard the valley doing its valley-things: a goat complaining somewhere, wind combing the pines, the creek under its skin of ice. A place built out of ordinary sounds, holding its breath.

"Seven days," Casper said finally.

"You can't give me up. I know how this looks. I'm exactly what that paper describes, sleeping in your barn, eating your bread. Turning me in might buy the village—" 

"I'm not going to turn you in." Casper's voice was flat and certain, and something in it made Vess look up. There was a strange expression on their face—not kindness, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone reading their own future in another person's warrant. "But if they know to come here, if they're nailing that to our gate, then they already know more than they should. About the valley. Maybe about..." They stopped. Their hand drifted, unconsciously, toward their own chest—the gesture of a person touching a scar through clothing. "There are things I need to ask you. Things I didn't think I'd ever say aloud."

"Then say them inside," Vess said. "Before someone else reads that paper and starts counting their neighbors."

Casper nodded slowly, but didn't move. Their eyes stayed on the seal—the split mountain, the small captive light—and Vess saw something move behind their expression, some door straining against its hinges, some name rising in them like a fish rising toward ice.

"Lior," Casper said. Just the name. Barely voiced. As if testing whether it still fit in their mouth.

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere behind them, the village was waking, and soon the lane would fill with people coming to read the board, and the counting would begin.

"We have six days now," Vess said, and pulled her coat tight, and turned toward the house.
