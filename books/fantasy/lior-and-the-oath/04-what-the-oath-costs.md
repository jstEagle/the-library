## 4. What the Oath Costs

The elder's house smelled of tallow and dried yarrow, of smoke that had soaked into the beams over forty winters, and beneath all of it—faint as a memory of a memory—the cold mineral breath of the mountain. Casper sat on a stool too small for them, hands flat on their knees, while Maren Ostergaard arranged herself across the hearth like a magistrate arranging a court.

She was not a large woman. She had never needed to be. The valley bent around her the way water bends around stone.

"You were seen," she said.

"Feeding goats."

"You were seen at the tree line, Casper Eriksen. At dusk. With someone who does not belong to this valley." She turned a cup in her hands without drinking from it. "The wards held. Whatever crossed them did not come *in*. But you stood at the boundary, and you did not report it, and you have been strange since—stranger than your ordinary strangeness, which we have all learned to forgive."

Casper kept their eyes on the fire. In the loft above Vess's head, they knew, Vess lay silent under wool and rags, her wound packed with moss and comfrey, her breathing trained shallow by years of practice at being hunted. Every hour that passed was an hour the fever could take root or recede. Casper could do nothing about it from this stool. So they did what oath-bound people do: they waited, and they lied with their silence.

"There was no one," Casper said.

Maren sighed—not with anger but with something worse, something like disappointment worn smooth by use. "Do you know why I called you here? Not for the stranger. Strangers wash against these wards like rain against glass. I called you because Halvard's boy says he heard you *speaking* to your sword last week, out past the mill. Form-words. Old ones."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"I say form-counts," Casper said. "Like everyone. One through eight. Breath and cut."

"Halvard's boy is nine years old and half-witted with fear, but he is not deaf. He said the words had *edges*." Maren set down her cup. "Say the count for me, Casper. All of it. From the beginning."

And there it was—the trap laid so gently Casper only now felt its teeth. Because the forms were not secret, exactly. Every child in the valley learned the counts the way they learned prayers: sounds without meaning, a rhythm to move the body by. *One through eight, breath and cut.* That much was safe.

But the beginning—the true beginning, the words under the words—was not safe, and had never been safe, and Casper had sworn, long ago, in a night they could not fully remember, never to speak it aloud inside the valley.

"I don't remember the beginning," Casper said.

Maren studied them for a long moment. Then she nodded slowly, as if Casper had confirmed something she'd suspected for decades. "No," she said softly. "I suppose you wouldn't. None of you ever do."

*None of you.*

The phrase went through Casper like a splinter entering flesh—clean at first, painless, and then deepening, finding nerve.

"What do you mean," Casper said carefully, "*none of us*?"

Maren rose and went to the cedar chest beneath her window—the one no one was permitted to open, the one every child in the valley had been told held winter blankets and was therefore never once curious about, because obedience in this place ran deeper than curiosity. She lifted from it a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it, and set on the table between them a book.

It was not a grand book. It was a shepherd's ledger, its cover cracked, its corners gnawed round by some long-dead mouse. But the pages inside were filled with names. Columns of them. Dates beside each name. And next to some of the dates, in a different ink, a single word:

*Sworn.*

"Do you know what this is?" Maren asked.

"It looks like a census."

"It is a census of a kind." She traced a column with one blunt finger. "Every generation, this valley produces one or two who are touched—who can put power into speech, whose words can cut or bind or burn. And every generation, those one or two are taken aside before their gift matures, and they are given a choice dressed up as a duty, and they swear an oath." Her finger stopped on a line near the top of the page. "My grandmother's hand wrote this entry. *Astrid Eriksen. Sworn.* Your great-grandmother, I think. Or your aunt. The blood keeps coming back to the same few families, like water finding the same channel."

Casper's mouth had gone dry. "I don't understand what this has to do with—"

"The oath erases, Casper." Maren said it plainly, without cruelty, which made it unbearable. "That is its price and its purpose. To bind the gift, the speaker gives up the reasons that would make them use it. The love, the grief, the rage—all the fuel—is fed into the binding. You cannot keep both the fire and the reason for the fire. So the oath takes the reasons. It eats backward through your life, taking whatever it needs, until what's left is a person who can hold a sword and feel nothing in particular about anything." She closed the ledger. "You've been reciting fragments of it your whole life. Every time you spoke even part of the old words—even the counting, if you spoke it with intent—you paid a little more. Do you truly remember your mother's face? Your father's voice? Do you remember why you live alone at the valley's edge with goats instead of in the village among people?"

Casper opened their mouth to answer—and found nothing there.

Not a blank, exactly. Worse than a blank. A *shape* where something should be, the way a bed holds the impression of a sleeper after they rise. There had been parents. There must have been parents; everyone had parents. But when Casper reached for a face, a voice, a single afternoon, they found only the shape, smooth and warm and utterly empty, like a stone worn hollow by a river that had moved on.

"I—" Their voice came out wrong. Thin. "There was a treaty. The valley's peace. I swore to guard—"

"You swore to guard the peace," Maren agreed. "That much survives, because the oath needs its servant to know the duty. Everything else goes. And here is the thing your oath never told you, because telling would cost it another piece of you: the peace was never threatened by anything outside these wards." She leaned forward. "The crown beneath the mountain *fed* on this arrangement. A valley full of quiet, obedient, memory-hollowed speakers, producing nothing, threatening nothing, paying in souls—"

"*Paying in souls*—"

Maren stopped. Her eyes flicked to the door, to the shuttered window, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "You don't know. Of course you don't know. They never tell the sworn ones. But my family has kept this ledger for four generations, and ledgers notice things. Every twenty years or so, a name goes missing from the village rolls. No death recorded. No grave. Just gone, and within a season, the mountain sends down its tribute-collectors, and the tribute is always the same weight, and the elders before me always signed, and the valley stayed peaceful." She met Casper's eyes. "Peaceful. That is the word we use. Like a house is peaceful after the screaming stops."

The room seemed to tilt. Casper gripped the edge of the stool.

"Lior," they said.

The name came out of them unbidden—a stone pulled from deep water, wet and shining and entirely unexpected. They hadn't meant to say it. They hadn't known until this moment that they still carried it. But there it was, sitting in the air between them and Maren, and attached to it, faint as heat off a summer road, a *cadence*. A way a voice might rise at the end of a sentence. A laugh, maybe. The ghost of a laugh.

"That name again," Maren said quietly. "You said it in your sleep, when you were young and we watched you during the fever-years. Always the same name. Never anyone else's."

"Who are they?" Casper's hands were shaking. "Who is Lior? What happened to—"

"I don't know. The ledger doesn't say. But look at the date." Maren turned the book back around and set her finger beneath a line near the bottom. *Lior — gone. Treaty year.* "The treaty year, Casper. The year the mountain's collectors first came and the first payment was made, and the year you—according to the fever-record—woke up screaming with a new word in your mouth and an empty place behind your eyes." She folded her hands. "Whatever Lior was to you, the oath took it as payment. Or the mountain did. I honestly no longer know which is crueller."

* * *

Casper walked home through the blue hour without remembering any of the path.

Their body knew the way—the ruts, the stiles, the place where the willow root buckled the lane—and their body walked it while their mind sat somewhere else entirely, turning over the shape of an absence. Parents. A home. A childhood with color in it. And threaded through all of it, one name, one cadence, one almost-laugh, preserved like a fly in amber because the oath had somehow failed to digest it—or because Casper had swallowed it so deep, in that forgotten night, that even the binding couldn't reach it.

They let themselves into the dark of their own house. Vess was awake. He could tell by the quality of the silence—hers had edges, like a held breath.

"You're bleeding through the bandage," Casper said. "I can smell it."

"Your nose is wasted on goats." A pause. "What did the old woman want?"

"To show me a ledger." Casper sat down heavily on the floor beside the cot, back against the wall, sword across their knees out of habit rather than intent. "To tell me that everything I am is a scar tissue grown over a hole. That I've been paying for my gift in memories since before I can remember—which is rather the point, apparently." A laugh came out of them, dry and awful. "There's a name in my head, Vess. Lior. It's the only thing left of—I don't know what. A person. Someone the oath ate around, like a moth eating a page and leaving one word legible."

Vess shifted in the dark. When she spoke, her voice had lost its fever-edge and gained something steadier. "In the city where I'm from, they have a saying about the mountain court. *The crown keeps voices.* I used to think it was poetry." She was quiet a moment. "It isn't poetry, is it. It's inventory."

"They pay in souls," Casper said. "Every twenty years. My own valley. My whole life built on top of it."

"And now the proclamation's nailed to your gate, and the hunt's coming over the ridge, and your wards won't hold forever, because wards are just promises, and promises need someone strong enough to keep them." Vess sat up slowly, wincing. "So. What's it going to be, swordspeaker? Stay here and be hollow and safe until the soldiers find me anyway? Or—"

"Or leave," Casper said.

The word landed in the room like a dropped coin. Both of them looked at it.

"Beyond the tree line," Casper went on, and their heart was hammering in a way it never had inside the valley, where nothing had ever been worth hammering for. "Where magic works. Where my words might actually *be* something. Where I can find out what a soul-payment looks like, and whether payments can be—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "Whether things sold can be bought back."

Vess was silent for a long moment. "You understand what you're saying. You break the law of silence, you break your oath just by crossing, and the oath doesn't forgive—it *collects*. Every step out there costs you more of what's left."

"I have a name," Casper said. "One name. It's the only thing in my head that feels like mine and not like something installed in me. If the oath wants the rest, it can have the rest. But I'm keeping the name."

Outside, far off toward the gate, a bell began to ring—not the evening bell, but the alarm, quick and arrhythmic, the sound the valley made perhaps once in a generation. Hooves on the frost-hardened road. Torches blooming along the palisade like a rash of orange flowers.

Vess went very still. "Riders. More than the proclamation party."

"The hunt," Casper said, standing, and found their hand already on the sword hilt, already speaking the first count under their breath—*one, breath*—and feeling, for the first time in their life, the words lean toward the door like dogs scenting rain. "They're not waiting for morning."

They looked at each other in the dark, two liars and two traitors, and understood the same thing at the same moment.

"Not through the gate," Casper said. "The tree line. Tonight."
