## 5. Crossing the Tree Line

The tree line did not look like a border. It looked like a wound.

Casper had walked its length a hundred times in their life—first as a child chasing a runaway goat, later as an adult who told themselves they were only checking the fence posts. The pines stood dark and patient where the meadow grass gave out, and the air between the last birch and the first spruce had always seemed to Casper like held breath: the valley's silence pressing up against whatever waited beyond it, neither willing to move first.

Now Vess stood at that boundary with her hood down and her hands shaking, and the wards were singing.

They had been singing since dawn—a thin, glassy note at the edge of hearing, like a wet finger circling the rim of a cup. Casper had felt it all morning in their back teeth, in the scar over their heart that ached whenever the oath stirred. The valley's magic could not follow them past the trees. That was the whole point of the valley. That was the whole point of everything.

"You don't have to come," Vess said for the fourth time. She was looking at the forest the way a drowning woman looks at shore. "I can go alone."

"You'll die alone." Casper adjusted the strap of their pack. "You nearly died crossing *into* the valley, and you were running downhill. Out there—" they nodded at the world beyond the wards, the world they knew only from their father's warnings and their own careful, cowardly ignorance, "—out there is the hunt."

"Then stay." Vess turned to face them fully. Her eyes were still wrong—the ward-sickness had left a silvered ring around each iris that caught the light strangely—but her voice was steady now, steadier than it had been any day since she'd collapsed bleeding at the valley's edge. "You have a life here. Goats. A garden. A village that thinks you're just a quiet person who talks to animals. I'm asking you to trade all of it for a stranger and a maybe."

"It isn't a stranger and a maybe." Casper heard their own voice come out flat and strange. "It's Lior."

The name did what it always did: opened a door in them onto a room full of nothing. They knew the name the way they knew their own heartbeat—absolutely, intimately, without knowing why. Every other memory of the person behind it had been eaten, syllable by syllable, by years of reciting the oath. But the name remained, and it pulled.

Vess studied them for a long moment. Then she looked back at the trees.

"They say it hurts," she said quietly. "Crossing. For the gifted. Like being born."

"I'm not gifted," Casper said, because they had said it every day of their life, because the oath required it, because the alternative was unthinkable.

Vess smiled without any humor in it at all. "No," she agreed. "Of course not."

* * *

They crossed at noon, when the shadows were shortest and the ward-song was loudest, because Vess said the wards thinned with the sun's height and she wanted every advantage she could get. Casper had argued for dusk—less chance of being seen—and Vess had looked at them with something close to pity and said, "You're thinking like someone who's never been hunted. They watch the crossings at dusk. At noon they're eating."

So: noon. The last fence post. The last birch, white as bone in the flat light. Casper stopped with one hand on its bark and made themselves look back at the valley one final time—the green folds of the hillsides, the smoke rising straight from the village chimneys, the goats scattered across the high pasture like dropped stones. It looked exactly as it had looked every day of their life. It looked, for the first time, like a place they were leaving.

The ache in their chest was not grief. Grief would come later, they suspected, when there was time for it. This was something older and simpler: the vertigo of a bird on a ledge, understanding wings.

"Casper." Vess was already three steps into the shadow of the pines, waiting. "It's better if you don't stop halfway. Halfway is the worst part."

"How would you know?"

"Because I've watched people try." She held out her hand. "Come on, swordspeaker. Be born."

Casper took her hand and stepped across the line where the grass ended.

The world broke open.

There was no other way to say it, no gentler way to hold it. One moment Casper was walking through ordinary noon light into ordinary pine shadow, and the next, every sense they owned tore wide like a shutter ripped from its hinges. Color came first—the greens of the forest suddenly so deep and various they seemed to be falling *into* the green, each needle distinct, each shaft of light carrying its own temperature. Sound arrived like a flood: sap moving in the trees, water speaking somewhere underground, the enormous slow respiration of the mountain itself, miles away, audible. And beneath all of it, threaded through everything like veins through marble—

*Words.*

Not spoken words. Older than speech. The words under the world, the ones Casper had spent a lifetime mouthing in sword-forms without ever once hearing answer, and here, past the tree line, they were not silent anymore. They rose up through the soles of Casper's boots and poured into the hollows of their bones, and every sword-form they had ever practiced lit up inside their body like lamps coming on floor by floor in a dark house.

"*Oh,*" Casper said—or tried to say—and the word left their mouth and became a thing.

That was the part no one had warned them about. The word did not simply make sound. It made *edge*. Casper felt it leave their lips and take shape in the air, a bright curved thing, and the pine bough nearest to them parted along a clean diagonal and fell, sliced through, though nothing visible had touched it.

Vess had let go of their hand and stepped back. She was staring at them with an expression Casper couldn't read—wonder and fear in equal measure, and beneath both, something that might have been vindication.

"You *are*," she breathed. "You're really one of them. An actual speaker. I thought—I thought your kind was gone."

"I'm not—" Casper's voice shook. They could feel the words crowding behind their teeth, dozens of them, hundreds, each one sharp, each one wanting out. "Make it stop. How do I make it stop?"

"You don't." Vess's silver-ringed eyes were wide. "You learn to live with it. Everyone says the first hour is the worst. Breathe. Pick one thing and look at it."

Casper picked the fallen bough. They stared at the clean cut through the pine limb until their breathing slowed, and slowly, slowly, the flood resolved into something bearable—not silence, never silence again, but a kind of music they could stand underneath instead of drown in. The words settled into their blood like sediment finding a riverbed.

And gods help them, it felt *good*.

That was the horror of it, though Casper wouldn't understand the shape of that horror until later. It felt like the first drink of cold water after a fever. It felt like the moment a cramp releases. Twenty-nine years of muffled living, of practicing forms that hummed and never sang, of being a bell wrapped in wool—and now the wool was off, and every nerve in Casper's body was singing *yes, yes, this, finally this*, and some deep animal part of them wanted to open their mouth and let all the words out at once and see what the world looked like afterward.

"Vess," they managed. "Is it always like this?"

"No." Her voice was gentle now. "Only at first. Only when you've been starved." She glanced up through the canopy, and her expression changed. "Speaking of which. We need to move. Now."

Casper heard it a heartbeat later—no, before that, actually; they realized with a lurch that they'd been hearing it for several minutes without recognizing it, because their new ears sorted it under *threat* automatically: hoofbeats. Many. Coming fast along the old logging road that ran below the ridge.

"The hunt?" Their mouth had gone dry.

"Scouts, probably. Tracking ward-sickness." Vess was already moving, pulling Casper off the road and upslope into denser timber. "They can smell what I am. They may not know yet what *you* are. If we can reach the ravine before—"

The horn cut her off. One long note, then two short ones, and the hoofbeats broke from a trot into a gallop.

* * *

They almost made the ravine.

Later, Casper would replay those minutes obsessively—the crashing descent through the ferns, Vess ahead of them, silver-eyed and sure-footed, the ravine's gray lip appearing through the trees like salvation—and they would count the ways it could have gone differently. If they had crossed at dusk. If they had gone north instead of south. If Casper had not stopped, just for one breath, when the soldiers' voices reached them through the trees.

"—ward-sick girl, silver eyes, traveling with a local. The elder sold us the description. *Local*, do you hear me? There's a speaker in this valley after all—"

A local. Casper stopped because the word went through them like a blade of its own. Someone in the village—someone whose bread they had eaten, whose fences they had mended—had described them to the crown's riders. Had named them. The numbness they had carried all their life, the comfortable quiet, cracked all the way through, and what flooded out of the crack was not grief but fury, white-hot and utterly new.

"Casper!" Vess hissed from below. "*Move!*"

But the soldiers had heard them too. Brush exploded on the slope above, and then the riders were there—four of them, dismounting, fanning out through the trees with the unhurried confidence of men who had done this many times and never once lost. Their armor was black-lacquered, unmarked by any heraldry Casper recognized, and each of them wore a collar of dull iron set with a single pale stone that seemed to drink the light.

"Surrender the girl," the nearest one said. He was young, broad-faced, bored. His eyes found Casper and dismissed them. "You can walk away, farmer. The proclamation only wants the gifted."

"She hasn't done anything," Casper heard themself say. Their voice sounded far away. The words were rising again behind their teeth, and this time they were not content to hum. "She's sick and she's scared and she hasn't done anything."

"That's not how it works anymore." The soldier sighed and drew his sword with the ease of long habit. "Last chance, farmer."

What happened next, Casper would spend the rest of their life trying to describe, and never quite succeed.

The fury crested. The words came up out of the place where the oath lived, out of twenty-nine years of swallowed syllables, and Casper opened their mouth and gave them shape.

"*Sunder.*"

One word. The soldier's sword broke.

Not bent, not knocked aside—broke, along its whole length, the steel parting like the pine bough had parted, and the sound it made was a sound Casper felt in their own teeth, a high ringing note that the forest repeated back from every trunk. The soldier stared at the hilt in his hand, at the two halves of his blade spinning away into the ferns, and his boredom fell off his face entirely.

Then he screamed something—orders, a name, Casper never learned—and the trees filled with iron.

Time went strange. Casper remembered it afterward in flashes, in sensations rather than events. The second soldier lunging, and the word "*Turn*" leaving Casper's mouth and the man's own momentum betraying him, hurling him sideways into a birch hard enough to crack the trunk. The third one smarter, hanging back, throwing a weighted net, and Casper's voice tearing it to ribbons midair with a word that meant *unmake* in a language older than any kingdom. The fourth one running—actually running, dropping his sword, scrambling downslope—and Casper feeling the next word rise, a killing word, a word with weight and hunger, and catching it between their teeth like a dog catching a thrown stone.

And the second soldier, the one who had hit the birch, getting up. Getting up with his sword still in his hand and his face gone from boredom to terror to something worse: resolve. He came at Casper low and fast and committed, the way trained men kill, and there was no clever word ready, no elegant solution, only the oldest form Casper knew—the first one their father ever taught them, the simple descending cut that every child in the valley learned with a stick before they learned their letters—and Casper spoke the word that lived at the heart of that form, the word they had mouthed ten thousand times in the meadow grass, silent, dormant, waiting.

The world went very quiet and very bright.

Afterward, Casper knelt in the ferns for a long time, shaking. The soldier lay nearby, and Casper could not look at him, and could not stop looking at him. There was so much red. No one had ever told them there would be so much red. In the practice-yard of their imagination, across all the years of solitary forms in the morning mist, enemies had always fallen cleanly, had always gotten up afterward, had always been somehow unreal. This man was real. His blood was soaking into the same earth that fed the ferns, and his face was young—younger than Casper—and his eyes were open, and they were not looking at anything at all.

"I killed him," Casper said. Their voice sounded like someone else's. "Vess. I killed him."

Vess crouched beside them. She had a gash on her forearm she didn't seem to have noticed. She didn't touch them—some instinct told her not to—but she stayed close, and her voice was low and even.

"Yes," she said. "You did. He would have killed both of us, and worse. You know that."

"I know it." Casper's hands would not stop trembling. "Knowing doesn't—it doesn't feel like anything. Knowing is just words. He's *dead*, and I did it with a *word*, and gods forgive me, Vess, ten minutes ago it felt *good*. All of it. The power. The cutting. I wanted more. What does that make me?"

Vess was quiet for a moment. Down in the ravine, water talked to itself over stones. Somewhere behind them, the fourth soldier's horse was still tied where he'd left it, cropping ferns, oblivious.

"It makes you someone the crown is right to fear," she said at last. "And wrong to hunt. Both things are true, Casper. You'll carry both. That's what the gift costs—that's what it's *always* cost, I think. Not the power itself. The fact that you'll love it."

Casper looked down at their hands. They were still trembling, but beneath the tremor, faint as a pulse, the words were singing. Content. Fed. *More*, they whispered, from somewhere below thought. *Again.*

Casper closed their eyes and stood up, and made their legs work, and followed Vess down into the ravine.

Behind them, on the slope, the surviving soldier's horse lifted its head and screamed once at the treeline—and far to the north, beyond sight, another horn answered. Then a second. Then a third, closer than the others.

The hunt had heard them now. The hunt knew what was walking toward the mountain.

And in the deepest hollow of Casper's memory, in the room with no door, something that had slept for twenty-nine years rolled over in its sleep—and whispered a name.

Not Lior's.

Their own.
