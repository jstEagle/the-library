## 6. The Road of Small Betrayals

The valley let us go the way a mother lets go of a child's hand at a market stall—reluctantly, with fingers that lingered, as if it expected us back before dark.

I felt it release us. I don't know how else to say it. Ward-sickness had lived in my bones for three years by then, a cold that no fire touched, and when we crossed out of the dead air beneath the tree line and onto the open road, that cold loosened its grip for the first time since the soldiers took my sister. The world outside the wards was loud with magic—thin threads of it in the hedgerows, old sleepy power in the standing stones along the way, the bright animal hum of Casper's gift walking beside me like a lantern they didn't know they carried.

They didn't know. That was the first thing I understood about Casper Eriksen, and it would take the whole road to teach them.

"Stop humming," I said on the second morning.

"I'm not humming."

"You are. Under your breath. It's not a song—it's your gift leaking. Every gifted person does it when they're nervous. Out here, it's a beacon." I pulled my hood lower against the drizzle. "From now on, you're a mule-driver's cousin from the coast. You've never seen magic. You don't believe in it. If someone asks what you are, you laugh like they've told a joke."

Casper walked three paces in silence. Rain dripped off the brim of their hat. When they finally spoke, their voice had the flat, careful quality of someone handling something sharp. "You want me to lie."

"I want you to live."

"It's the same thing to you, isn't it."

I stopped in the middle of the road and looked at them—this oath-bound swordspeaker who had spent a lifetime inside a valley where truth was easy because nothing ever came to test it. Mud to their ankles. A sword wrapped in sacking across their back because I'd made them wrap it. Eyes the color of slate after rain, and behind them, I could see, the genuine bewilderment of a person who had never once needed to decide whether honesty was worth dying for.

"Yes," I said. "It's the same thing. Get used to it."

* * *

I taught them the way my mother taught me, which is to say quickly, harshly, and with the understanding that mistakes would be paid for in blood—mine or theirs or some stranger's.

Hiding first. How to walk so you take up no space: shoulders rounded, eyes down, hands busy with some humble task. How to be forgettable. Gifted people glow—not visibly, not to ordinary eyes, but to hounds, to ward-sensitives, to the hunt-captains with their bone needles and their brass instruments that clicked like beetles. So we learned to smother ourselves. I showed Casper how to press their awareness down into their boots, into the mud, until the lantern of them guttered to an ember.

"Bribery," I said, counting out the last of my coins on a tavern table in a town whose name I've since forgotten. "Rule one: never bribe the hungry. Rule two: never bribe the proud. Rule three: pay the innkeeper's wife, not the innkeeper. She runs everything anyway."

"And lying?" Casper asked. They'd taken the coins from me to pay, and their hand hovered over them like the money might bite.

"Lying is just hiding with words. Same skill. You make yourself small in a sentence instead of in a body." I watched them count the coppers twice, wrong both times, and something in my chest twisted that I refused to name. "Say less than you know. Answer questions with questions. And if someone catches you in a lie—" I tapped the table, "—don't defend it. Defended lies grow. Abandoned ones die."

"They taught us the opposite in the valley." Casper pushed the coins across to the barmaid, and I saw their jaw tighten around whatever the words cost. "An oath is a sentence you can't take back. Words mean what they mean. You stand behind them or you're nothing."

"How's that working out for the valley?" I asked, gentler than I meant to, because the proclamation was nailed to every gate between there and the capital, and the valley's peace had been bought with souls, and Casper knew it now, or half-knew it, the way you half-know a wound under a bandage.

They didn't answer. But that night, at the edge of the firelight, I heard them practicing. Whispering. *I'm a mule-driver's cousin from the coast.* Over and over, like a sword-form, like a prayer said backward. Each repetition seemed to hurt a little less. I told myself that was progress.

* * *

We fell into a rhythm that should have frightened me more than it did. Days on the road, speaking little; nights in ditches or barns or the occasional room bought with my dwindling coin. Casper learned fast—their body already knew concealment from a lifetime of guarding secrets, and only their conscience lagged behind, dragging like a stone in a cart.

In Marrowford, a toll-warden with watery eyes asked our business, and Casper said, "Mule-driving, mostly," without a flicker, and I could have kissed them.

At a shrine-crossing, a gray-cloaked sister of the road pressed a blessing to each traveler's brow, reading for the taint of gift, and Casper stood in line with their head bowed and received her cold thumb like any honest farmer, and afterward walked a mile before saying, "That felt like drowning."

"You get used to it."

"I don't want to get used to it." But they kept walking. Kept lying. Kept alive.

And I—I started telling them things. Small things at first. That my mother had been a hedge-witch who could coax blight out of barley. That my sister Sera could light candles with a breath and did it constantly, uselessly, beautifully, right up until the riders came through our village with the mountain's seal on their banners. I hadn't spoken Sera's name aloud in three years. It came out of me on the road somewhere past Marrowford, in the dark, unasked-for, and Casper didn't say anything at all, just shifted their bedroll closer to mine against the cold, and that was somehow worse and better than comfort.

Trust, I thought. This is what trust feels like. Like handing someone a knife handle-first and hoping they remember which end is which.

* * *

The Hollis farm sat in a fold of hills two days south of Greyhollow, and it looked like every farm I'd ever loved: low stone walls, a crooked barn, woodsmoke standing straight in the still evening air. A woman was pulling onions in the last light. Two children chased a dog through the stubble. A man repaired a gate, hammer ringing slow and even as a heartbeat.

"We ask for the barn," I murmured. "One night. We offer labor at dawn and coin besides. We do not mention the road, the hunt, or where we're bound."

"Mara and Tam," Casper said, trying the false names out. "Coast folk. Mules."

"Mara and Tam," I agreed.

Old Hollis—gray-bearded, wind-burned, with a laugh like gravel in a bucket—took our measure in about four seconds and decided we were harmless. His wife, Brenna, fed us onion soup and barley bread at their own table, which I hadn't expected and liked them too much for. The children stared at Casper's wrapped sword until their father told them to mind their bowls.

"Where you headed?" Hollis asked, casual, ladle in hand.

"North," I said. "Work."

"Lot of folk heading north lately." He smiled. "Lot of strangers on the roads this season. King's business, they say." The word *king* sat strangely in his mouth, and I noticed—filed it away—noticed the way Brenna's hands went still over the bread.

We slept in the barn, in the sweet rot-smell of hay, and I woke once in the black hours to hear Casper breathing beside me, slow and even, and thought: *one night, then gone before dawn, and no harm done.*

I was wrong about the dawn.

* * *

They came at first light, and I knew before I opened my eyes. Ward-sickness has its uses; it makes you a tuning fork for other people's fear. The whole farm was vibrating with it—a high, sour note, like a wire about to snap.

Four riders in the yard. Not crown livery—hunt colors, deep red with the mountain's seal stitched in thread that seemed to drink the morning sun. Their captain dismounted slowly, a lean man with a bone needle strung on a cord around his neck, and the needle was swinging toward our barn like a compass finding north.

Brenna stood on the doorstep with her arms crossed tight. Hollis wasn't anywhere I could see.

"The reward," she was saying, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, and I hated her and pitied her in the same breath. "You said forty crowns for a pair. There's two of them. Forty crowns. You'll write it down? You'll witness it?"

"Ma'am," the captain said, bored, "if they're gifted, the crown pays. If they're not, they're just vagrants, and you've wasted the king's time."

I was already moving. Barns have two doors if the gods love you; this one had a gap in the back wall where a board had rotted away, and I had marked it the evening before out of habit, out of the long education of being hunted. I shook Casper awake with a hand over their mouth.

"Hollis sold us," I breathed. "Back wall. Now. Quiet."

Casper's eyes went wide, then flat, then—this was the part I remember, the part that stayed with me—they went *sad*. Not afraid. Sad, like a person watching a house burn that they'd grown up in.

We went through the gap into the grey morning, into the stubble field, bent double, running low. Behind us the barn door banged open and the captain's voice rang out, and then the needle's hum rose to a shriek I could feel in my teeth, and hooves thundered.

They cut us off at the stone wall. Of course they did. Open ground, two runners, four horses—it was never going to be a footrace.

"Down!" I shouted, and dragged Casper behind the wall as the first crossbow bolt buried itself in the stone above our heads with a crack like a snapped bone.

And then Casper did something I will think about for the rest of my life, however long the crown allows it to be.

They stood up.

Not all the way—just enough to put their mouth near the gap between stones, and they spoke, and the word they spoke was not a word I knew, but the air *tore*. I felt it pass over my head like a hot wire. The lead rider's horse screamed and veered, throwing him into the stubble. A second bolt splintered on the wall an inch from Casper's cheek, and they flinched, and spoke again, softer, and the captain's crossbow simply came apart in his hands, stock and string and all, clattering into the mud.

Silence. Four riders staring at a farm wall, at a figure in a rain-dark coat with a wrapped sword and eyes full of something old and terrible.

"Forty crowns," Casper called out, and their voice carried wrong, carried *too far*, the way gifted voices do when the gift gets into them. "For two heads. Is that what a person costs this season?"

Nobody answered. The captain gathered his reins, backed his horse three steps, and turned it. Whatever he'd come expecting, it wasn't this. They withdrew to the crest of the hill, conferred, and held there—watching, waiting, sending no one. Smart man. He'd send for help. He'd send for worse than himself.

Casper sagged against the wall, shaking. I got my shoulder under their arm. Their skin was burning, and beneath the burn, faintly, I could feel the gift guttering like a lamp running dry.

"That was stupid," I said.

"That was *them*," Casper said. "Those were people. He has children. I saw them chase the dog." Their voice broke on the last word. "He sold us for forty crowns and I nearly killed him for it, and I don't know which of those things is worse."

"Both," I said. "Both are worse. Welcome to the road." I hauled them upright. "Now walk, Mara of the coast, because in an hour that man comes back with friends, and I promise you they won't stop to talk."

* * *

We walked the whole day, off-road, through wet gorse and sheep tracks, putting miles between ourselves and the Hollis farm. Casper didn't speak for hours. When they finally did, the sun was going down behind us and Greyhollow's distant smudge of smoke sat on the horizon ahead, and their voice was rough as bark.

"When I was small, my grandmother told me about the oath. She said the words would keep me safe, and true, and whole. She said a sworn thing can't be shaken." They laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a kind of wonder. "She never said what happens when everyone else breaks faith first. She never said the world would be a market where everything has a price, including us."

"People aren't good," I said. "They're scared. Scared people sell each other. My own neighbors pointed out our house to the riders, Casper. I watched them do it. Mrs. Anselm, who taught me letters. She couldn't look at me while she did it." I kept my eyes on the road. "You learn to expect the betrayal. What you don't learn—what nobody teaches—is what to do when someone *doesn't* betray you. When someone hides you anyway. That's the part that keeps you human."

Casper was quiet a moment. Then: "Is that why you helped me? At the wards?"

"You were bleeding on my escape route," I said. "Practicality."

"Liar," they said softly, and for once I didn't correct them.

We made camp in a hollow with no fire, sharing a blanket against the cold because warmth mattered more than propriety and we'd both stopped pretending otherwise. Somewhere far behind us, I imagined the hunt-captain writing his report: *two fugitives, one armed, one displaying unregistered speech-force, heading north.* Somewhere far ahead, the mountain waited with its sealed court and its appetite, holding—though neither of us yet knew how much—of what we'd each lost.

"Casper," I said, into the dark. "Tomorrow we reach Greyhollow. Everything I've taught you—hiding, bribing, lying—you'll need all of it doubled. Tripled. In the city, the walls have ears that aren't figurative. If I tell you to run, you run. If I tell you to leave me, you leave me. Swear it."

"Don't ask me to swear," they said. "I'm done with oaths."

"Then promise."

A long silence. Rain started somewhere above us, ticking through the gorse.

"I promise," Casper said at last. "Truth between us, always—even when the truth is a lie we have to tell. That's the only vow I have left in me."

It wasn't much of a promise. It was the best thing anyone had said to me in three years.

I slept, and dreamed of Sera lighting candles with her breath, and woke before dawn to Casper shaking my shoulder, pointing north, where the lights of Greyhollow had come up out of the dark like a scatter of embers—and among them, moving down the north road toward the city gates, torches. Dozens of them. A column of the king's hunt, riding in formation, and at its head a banner I recognized from the worst night of my life: the seal of the court beneath the mountain.

"They're going the same place we are," I whispered.

"No," Casper said, and I heard something new in their voice, something hardening, something that sounded almost like the gift itself taking shape as resolve. "They're going where we're going. There's a difference. Come on—we need to reach the gates before they do, and find out who sells souls in this city, and buy our names into a ledger."

I looked at them—at the oath-bound speaker learning to lie, learning to survive, learning, God help me, to fight—and I followed them down toward the lights.

The broker we needed, though I didn't know it yet, was already expecting us.
