## 7. The Soul Market

Greyhollow announced itself by smell before it ever showed its walls.

Casper had thought they knew what cities were. The valley had a market day, twice a season, when traders came up the pass with salt and iron and dyed wool, and the village square filled with shouting and the sweet rot of overripe plums, and Casper had believed that was noise, that was crowd, that was the world at its loudest.

Greyhollow was to that square what an ocean is to a rain barrel.

The road crested a ridge of black shale in the late afternoon, and there it lay below them: a city built in the bowl of a dead caldera, ringed by hills the color of old ash, its rooftops stacked like scales on some enormous sleeping fish. Smoke rose from ten thousand chimneys and flattened against the sky as if even the air here had learned not to rise too high. And beneath the smoke, beneath the coal-stink and the tanneries and the river of grey water threading through the streets, there was something else—something faint and wrong, like the smell of a room where someone has wept for a very long time.

"Do you smell it?" Vess asked. She stood beside Casper at the ridge's edge, hood up, hands buried in her sleeves. Three days on the road had carved hollows under her eyes, but her voice was steady. "That sweetness under everything."

"I smell smoke."

"You smell souls." She said it flatly, the way one says *it will rain*. "They leak. Stored light sweats through whatever you keep it in. Every soul-broker's cellar in this city breathes it out, and the whole city breathes it in, and nobody notices anymore because—" she gestured at the sprawl below, the carts, the bells, the gulls fighting over refuse in the grey air, "—because everyone here has been breathing grief since before they were born."

Casper wanted to say she was being dramatic. They had learned, over three hundred miles of road, that Vess was never dramatic about the things that mattered. She saved her drama for small betrayals—a stolen apple, a lie told to a toll-keeper—and spent truth like a miser.

"Remember," she said now, turning those pale eyes on them. "You are my hired sword. You don't speak unless spoken to. You don't look at anyone longer than courtesy allows. And if you hear your own name—"

"I don't have a name here," Casper said. "I'm Ash, from the coast, and I've never seen magic in my life except once, from a distance, and it frightened me."

"Good." Something almost like a smile touched her mouth. "You're learning to be a liar. I'd say I'm proud, but lying well is nothing to be proud of. It's just the price of walking through the world with blood like ours."

*Blood like ours.* Casper still flinched at the phrase. In the valley, they had been no one—the quiet one who kept goats and practiced sword-forms alone at dawn, whose words sometimes hummed in their teeth like bees looking for a way out. Here, past the tree line, those same words could open a man's chest. Here, that blood was a death sentence with a royal seal on it.

They went down into the city together, two more shadows among thousands.

* * *

Greyhollow's streets were canyons of wet stone, lit by lamps that burned a color Casper had no name for—not quite flame, not quite glowworm-light, but something between, a greenish pallor that made every face look drowned. Vess moved through them like water finding its course, and Casper followed, hand near the hilt of the sword wrapped in sacking on their back.

The market found them before they found it.

It began as sound: a bell, high and thin, struck three times. Then the crowd around them changed. It did not part, exactly—it *poured*, flowing down a side street toward some common gravity, and Vess caught Casper's sleeve and pulled them along with it without explanation.

The Soul Market occupied a sunken plaza where four streets met, a bowl of flagstones worn concave by centuries of feet. Stalls ringed the depression in three tiers, and above each stall hung a lantern of colored glass, and inside each lantern—

Casper stopped walking.

Inside each lantern was light. Not firelight. Not lamplight. It moved. It pressed against the glass the way a moth presses, the way a child presses a palm to a window watching a parent walk away. Some of the lights were white and calm. Some flickered fast and frantic, battering themselves against their prisons. One, in a lantern of deep blue glass, pulsed slow and steady, patient as a heartbeat, and Casper found they could not look away from it.

"Don't stare at any single one," Vess murmured. "They feel attention. It's cruel."

"They feel—" Casper's throat closed. "Vess. Those are people."

"Those are pieces of people, mostly. Whole souls are rare. Expensive." Her voice had gone very quiet, very controlled, the voice she used when she was holding herself together with both hands. "A whole soul buys a house. A fragment buys a winter. A whisper—" she nodded toward a stall where tiny vials glimmered like dew, "—buys a night of dreams. That's what they call the smallest cut. A whisper. Enough to dream vividly, or to remember someone you've lost, or to make a memory sweeter than it was. The poor sell whispers when the hunger gets bad. You can sell a whisper a dozen times before there's nothing left of you that remembers why you started selling."

Around the plaza, the trade went on with the easy rhythm of fishmongers and fruit-sellers. A woman in a broker's apron held a lantern up to a fat merchant's squinting eye. "Harvested clean off a soldier, master, died at the border with no kin—bright as new snow, hardly used—"

"Died how?"

"Fighting for the crown, gods keep him."

"Then it's crown-tainted. Half price."

"The *crown* bought his soul first, master, this is the surplus—"

Casper's hand had closed around the hilt of the hidden sword without their noticing. Vess's fingers pried it loose, gentle and firm.

"Not here," she breathed. "Not for a lantern. If you draw steel in this plaza, they'll cut you down, and worse—they'll *keep* you. Do you understand? Death here isn't an ending. It's inventory."

Casper understood. That was the horror of it. They understood all the way down to the marrow, because somewhere in this reeking bowl of stone, if Vess was right, there was a record—a ledger, a list, a line of ink—and on that line was a name Casper had carried across half a kingdom like a coal cupped in bare hands.

*Lior.*

The name that was all they had left. Everything else—the face, the voice, the reason, the terrible night of the oath-swearing—had been eaten bite by bite by the words they recited. But the name remained, and the love remained, love without an object, like a lamp burning in an empty room. They had followed it out of the valley. They had killed with it singing in their blood.

Somewhere in Greyhollow, that name had been written down by someone who thought of it as merchandise.

"Where," Casper said, and their voice came out lower than they intended, rough as a blade drawn dry, "is the broker who keeps the crown's accounts?"

* * *

The answer took them until full dark, and it took them through the city's descending circles—from the market plaza where souls were sold retail, down through streets where the shops sold the *tools*: silver needles for the cutting, lead-glass lanterns for the keeping, wax seals stamped with the mountain's sigil, and little books bound in grey leather titled, in gold leaf, *On the Proper Storage of Kept Light*. Down further, to a street called Ledger Row, where the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators and the doors were iron and unmarked, and men with the flat eyes of career clerks came and went carrying strongboxes that clinked with a sound no coin makes.

Vess worked the street the way she worked everything: patiently, obliquely, buying a meat pie from a vendor and eating it slowly while listening; asking a linkboy, with a copper and a shy smile, which house had the most lights in its windows; noting which door the clerks knocked on twice instead of once.

"The tall one," she said finally. "Third from the corner. Green lamp. That's Halloway Fen. He keeps the institutional accounts—temple tithes, military purchases, crown contracts. If the throne bought anything in this city in the last thirty years, it passed through his books."

"And he'll just show us?"

"He'll show us what we can pay for." Vess turned her hood back, and in the green lamplight her face looked carved from something older than youth. "Which brings us to the hard part. Fen doesn't take coin. Coin can be stolen; coin leaves no mark. He trades in the only currency that can't be counterfeited."

Casper looked at her and felt cold spread through their chest. "No."

"It's a whisper, Casper. A drop. Everyone who walks into that shop pays a drop."

"*No.*" The word cracked out of them louder than they meant, and a clerk across the street glanced over. Casper lowered their voice to a furious whisper. "You've seen what this does to people. The woman in the market—the one who sold her sister's location for passage east—she couldn't remember her sister's *face*, Vess. She remembered that she'd loved her the way you remember a room you grew up in. I have already lost—I have lost almost everything, do you understand? There is so little left. I am not paying him in the only thing I have."

Vess was silent a moment. When she spoke, her voice was careful, and underneath the care ran something raw.

"Then we find another way. We break in. We steal the page." She exhaled. "And if we're caught, we die in a cellar, and our light goes into a lantern on Ledger Row, and Lior stays under the mountain feeding a throne until the sun burns out. Or—" she held up a hand before Casper could speak, "—or you let me go in first and pay my own drop, and you stand there and watch, and you hate yourself for letting me, and we get the name. Those are the choices. I've been weighing them for an hour. I don't like any of them either."

Casper stared at her. Three days ago they had been strangers. Now this woman—who had lost a sister to the mountain, who had taught Casper how to lie, how to hide, how to survive—was offering to carve a piece out of her own soul to buy them a line of ink.

"Why?" Casper asked. "Why would you pay for my name? You don't even know who he was."

Vess looked up at the green lamp burning in Fen's window, and her expression did something complicated.

"Because I know what it is to have one name left," she said. "And because when we find yours, maybe you'll help me find mine." She straightened her shoulders. "Wait here. If I'm not out in a quarter hour, come in swinging. Fen's guards are human. Your words work on humans."

She crossed the street before Casper could argue, knocked twice on the iron door, and was admitted by a clerk with a candle, and the door closed behind her with a sound like a book shutting.

* * *

The quarter hour was the longest of Casper's life.

They stood in the shadow of a doorway across from the green lamp and watched the window, and tried not to imagine what was happening inside—tried, and failed. They imagined Vess seated across a desk from a soft-voiced man with inkstained fingers. They imagined the silver needle. They imagined the small bright thing lifted out of her, the way a splinter comes out of flesh, and the place where it had been going dark and smooth and empty.

*I have lost almost everything,* they had said. But standing there in the cold, Casper counted what remained and found the count shorter than they'd claimed. A name. A feeling. Fragments: someone laughing with their head thrown back; someone saying *you always sharpen it too much, you'll wear the blade away before you ever need it*; someone's hand, warm, in theirs, though whether in blessing or farewell Casper could no longer tell. The oath had eaten in rings, like rot in timber, leaving the heartwood standing but hollowed all around it.

And still the heartwood loved. That was the unbearable thing. Love with no memories attached, love that persisted like a scar persists, aching in cold weather, meaning everything and explaining nothing.

The door opened.

Vess came out slowly, one hand braced on the frame, and even across the street Casper saw the change. It wasn't visible, exactly. It was a diminishment, a subtraction—the sense that slightly less of her stood there than had gone in. She caught Casper's eye and nodded once: *come.*

Fen's shop smelled of tallow and dust and, underneath, that same sweet grief-smell from the market, concentrated here into something almost solid. The interior was lined floor to ceiling with ledgers, thousands of them, their spines dated in faded gilt. A single desk sat in the middle of the room, and behind it sat Halloway Fen: a narrow man of perhaps sixty, with spectacles, neat hands, and eyes like two drops of well-water—clear, cold, and very deep.

He did not ask their names. Names, Casper would learn, were the one thing brokers never recorded of the living.

"The lady paid her fee," Fen said. His voice was mild, unhurried, the voice of a man reading aloud in church. "A whisper, cleanly taken. She asked for the crown's purchase ledger, restricted entries, the founding treaty year forward." He opened a drawer and drew out a single loose page, copied fair in a clerk's hand. "I will show you one entry. One. Additional entries cost additional fees, and I suspect"—his well-water eyes rested on Casper with what might, in another man, have been curiosity—"that you have little left to pay them with. So choose your question carefully before I turn the page over."

Casper's mouth was dry. Somewhere behind their ribs, their heart was trying to climb out.

"One question," Fen repeated. "Ask."

The valley. The treaty. The year the wards went up, the year the elders signed the peace that bought the valley its silence, the year Casper had been a child too young to understand what peace cost or who paid it. They knew—had known once, before the oath ate it—that the treaty had involved a payment. Everyone in the valley knew that much and no more. *The mountain takes its due,* the elders said, and would not say what the due was.

One question. Casper reached for a dozen and discarded them all. Finally they said, in a voice they barely recognized:

"What did the crown buy from the valley, in the treaty year—and where is it kept?"

Fen turned the page over. His finger came down on a single line, and he read it aloud in that mild churchgoing voice, and the words fell into Casper like stones into a well:

"Crown acquisition, treaty year, source: the valley settlement, south passes. One soul, whole and willing, tendered as surety of peace. Designation: *keystone.* Current disposition: not warehoused. Assigned to the Throne Engine, primary lattice, permanent attachment." Fen paused. "Name of record—"

He said the name.

*Lior.*

The shop tilted. The ledgers on the walls blurred into grey rivers. Casper heard a sound and realized distantly it was coming from their own throat—a small, broken noise, the noise of a lamp in an empty room suddenly, violently, filled again. *Lior.* Not a name floating free. A name with a date, a transaction, a designation. *Willing.* The word swung on its hook like a butcher's carcass. Willing—or made to seem so. Tendered—as if a person were coin. And *keystone*—the word rang with terrible architectural sense, the stone that holds the arch, remove it and everything falls.

Everything falls.

"Where," Casper whispered, and their hands were shaking, and fury was rising in them like floodwater, hot and clarifying, washing away fear, washing away caution, washing away everything but the line of ink and the man who had read it so gently, "where is the Throne Engine?"

Fen removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, unhurried, and when he looked up again there was something in his well-water eyes that Casper would spend a long time trying to name—pity, or warning, or the weary respect of one keeper of secrets for another.

"That," he said, "is a second question."
