## 8. The Broker's Price

The broker's shop did not look like a place where souls changed hands. It looked like an apothecary that had given up on healing—shelves of dusty jars, bundles of dried herbs gone gray with age, a counter scarred by decades of knives and coins and, I would learn, other currencies entirely. The sign above the door was blank. In Greyhollow, the places worth finding never named themselves.

Casper waited outside, which had been my condition and their reluctant concession. *You make people honest by standing there looking like you've never lied in your life,* I'd told them. *I need this man dishonest.* They hadn't argued. They were getting better at not arguing, which worried me more than if they'd fought me on it.

Inside, the air was thick and sweet, like overripe fruit left too long in a closed room. Soul-smell. I'd smelled it once before, years ago, in the antechamber of a magistrate's office where my sister's fate had been decided with a stamp and a fee schedule. I had thought I'd forgotten it. The body keeps better ledgers than the mind.

The broker sat behind the counter as though he had grown there, an old man folded into himself like a letter no one wanted to read. His eyes were the pale, washed-out gray of eyes that had seen too much light of the wrong kind—the light that comes off kept souls when they're handled. He looked at me the way a fishmonger looks at a trout.

"You have the look," he said. "The hunted look. It's fashionable this season."

"I'm told you keep records." I set the broker's token on the counter—a sliver of bone stamped with a sigil I'd won off a drunk carter two nights ago, along with his dinner and most of his dignity. "Purchases made for the crown. Vessels taken north."

He picked up the token, turned it over, set it down again without any expression at all. "Records are my trade. Information is my product. Records are free to read—for those who can pay the reading price."

"Name it."

His smile was small and dry as a moth's wing. "Oh, I don't name prices to people who flinch. Let me see your hands first."

I almost laughed. "My hands?"

"Gifted hands have a grain to them. Like wood that's been rained on." He beckoned with one finger, and something in his voice shifted—dropped its performance, dropped its irony, became simply old and tired and terribly certain. "Show me, girl. I've been reading hands since before your mother learned to walk. I'll know what you are, and what you're carrying, and what it will cost you to carry it further."

I gave him my hands. There seemed little point in pretense; he already knew why I'd come, and in Greyhollow, pretending not to be gifted was like pretending not to breathe. He turned my palms up, ran a thumbnail along the lines of them, and stopped at the heel of my right hand, where the skin had been smooth all my life and now, I realized with a cold turn of my stomach, was not. A thin seam, silver-pale, like a scar from a cut I'd never received.

"When did you sell?" he asked quietly.

"Sold what?"

"Don't." He said it gently, which was worse than saying it cruelly. "Not with me. I deal in truth the way other men deal in flour. You sold a piece of yourself. Recently. Within the year. You paid someone for passage or protection or silence, and they took their price out of your soul instead of your purse, because your purse was empty and your soul was not." He released my hands. "It happens more than you'd think. The roads are full of the half-bought."

*The half-bought.* I stood very still and let the words settle into me like stones into a pond. I had known, of course. I had known what I traded in that magistrate's antechamber six years ago, when I gave up my sister's hiding place to save my own skin—I had known it again three months ago, when I paid a smuggler in the only coin I had left. But knowing a thing and hearing it named by a stranger in a dusty shop are different weathers entirely.

"How much is left?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"Enough." He studied me a moment longer. "Enough to matter. Enough to be worth taking, if you're careless. And enough—" he leaned forward, and the sweetness of the air seemed to thicken around him, "—to buy what you came for."

* * *

He brought out the ledger himself. Not the public ledger, the one chained to the counter for anyone with coin to flip through—the crown's purchases were listed there in tidy columns, dates and sums and vessel-numbers, souls reduced to entries the way cattle are reduced to heads. I had already seen that one, three days ago, when Casper found Lior's name in it and went white as valley snow. That ledger had told us *when*. It could not tell us *where*, or *why*, or *what happens next*.

This second book was smaller, bound in leather so dark it drank the lamplight. He opened it only halfway, angling the pages away from me even as he offered them—a merchant's habit, showing the goods while keeping hold of them.

"The crown's private acquisitions," he said. "Kept separate because they aren't purchases at all, properly speaking. Purchases imply the thing bought sits still afterward." His finger traced down a column of dates. "Here. Your friend's name—or the name attached to the soul your friend came asking after. See the notation?"

I saw it. Beside the entry, in ink darker than the rest, a single word: *KEystone.*

"That's a mistake," I said. "Or a code."

"It's neither." He closed the book. "And now we arrive at the reading price."

"Coin? I have some."

"Coin buys the chained ledger. You've read the chained ledger." He folded his hands atop the counter, and for a moment he looked less like a fishmonger and more like a priest at an altar, which I suppose, in his way, he was. "What you want to know is what the word means. What the throne wants with a keystone. Why the hunt sweeps the provinces for gifted blood when the prisons of Greyhollow are already full of it. Am I close?"

Every hair on my arms had risen. "You know why."

"I know many whys. It's my trade." The dry smile again. "And the price for this particular why is not coin. Coin is common. Coin can be stolen, counterfeited, buried and dug up again. No." He tapped the counter once, softly, directly over the place where I knew—without being shown—that a drawer lay full of glass vials and silver seams and the small, terrible instruments of his craft. "For this, I take a piece of the goods themselves."

* * *

I want to say I refused him first. I want to say I stood up, pushed back my chair, told him what he could do with his ledger and his vials, and walked out into the street where Casper waited with their honest face and their terrible oath, and that we found another way.

That isn't what happened.

What happened was that I sat down. Because I thought of my sister, whose face I could no longer see clearly—six years of distance will do that, six years of guilt will do it faster—and how every road I'd walked since had bent, however crookedly, toward the mountain. Because I thought of Casper in the doorway of our last campsite, saying *Lior* the way you say the name of a country you were exiled from. Because I thought of the proclamation nailed to the valley gate, the seal of the under-court gleaming in the sun, and the words beneath it: *for the crown's protection.* Protection. As if the crown were the sheep and we were the wolves, and not the other way around, and not worse than either.

And because—this is the truest reason, and I have promised myself I would deal in truth the way the broker deals in flour—because a piece of me had already been sold twice, and it is astonishing how little ceremony the third sale requires. The soul, I learned that afternoon, does not cry out when you cut it. It has no voice of its own. It only echoes whatever you tell yourself while you're selling.

"What piece?" I asked.

He assessed me the way a carpenter assesses timber. "Small. A memory, perhaps. Or a capacity. We'll see what parts cleanly." He rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and fetched from the back room a shallow bowl of black glass and a knife with a blade of the same material. "Sit. Hands flat on the counter. This doesn't hurt—not exactly. It hurts the way grief hurts. Afterward rather than during."

"And what do I lose? Tell me that much before I agree."

"I don't choose. The blade finds what's loosest." He said it as gently as a man can say such a thing. "Sometimes it takes a face. Sometimes a season of childhood. Once it took a woman's ability to taste sweetness—she lived forty more years and drank her tea bitter and never knew what she'd lost, only that everyone else spoke of honey like a religion." He paused. "Sometimes it takes nothing you'd notice for years. Those are the worst ones, in my opinion. But I am not consulted. I am only the hand."

Outside the window, Greyhollow went about its evening. A cart rattled past. Someone laughed. Somewhere a bell rang the hour, indifferent as weather. I put my hands flat on the counter.

"Do it," I said.

* * *

He was right about the pain. During: nothing. The black-glass knife touched the base of my palm, along the seam that was already there, and drew a line of light that wasn't blood—it was paler than blood, brighter, and it ran down into the bowl like water finding its level, humming faintly, the way a struck string hums after the note ends. I watched a piece of myself pour out of my own hand and felt absolutely nothing at all, and that nothing frightened me more than any pain could have.

Afterward was different. Afterward was walking through a familiar house and feeling a draft from a door you can't find. Something was gone. I turned in circles inside my own head looking for it, and every room I checked was furnished, inhabited, whole—which meant the missing room was one I couldn't remember checking. The broker corked the bowl, labeled it in a spidery hand, and shelved it among dozens of others, each glowing faintly, each humming its struck-string hum. A library of the half-bought. I wondered how many of them, like me, had walked in certain and walked out hollow, hunting the shape of their own absence.

Then he opened the dark ledger all the way, and began to talk, and I forgot everything else.

* * *

"Understand the architecture first," he said, "or the details will drown you. The throne beneath the mountain is not a chair. It is not even, properly speaking, a thing. It is a hunger wearing the shape of a thing. The court calls it the Seat of Kept Voices. The founders built it—how long ago, no record says—to answer questions. That was all. A device for seeing forward, for hearing truths at a distance. Every oracle needs fuel. The founders fed it willingly, a drop apiece, a tithe of self, and it prophesied, and the valley prospered, and all was well for a hundred years."

He turned pages. Dates marched past like soldiers.

"But hungers grow. The tithe was never enough. And the throne learned—this is the part the histories leave out, the part I charge for—it learned that souls do not merely feed prophecy. Souls *are* prophecy. Each one kept is a future that can be read, unwound, unfolded like a map. One soul gives you glimpses. Ten give you weather. A thousand—" he looked up at me, and his washed-out eyes held no triumph at all, only a terrible professional calm, "—a thousand give you certainty. The crown does not guess at harvests or rebellions or wars. It knows. It has known for generations. Every defeat it has ever suffered was suffered on purpose, to prune a rival, to steer a king, to keep the future flowing in the channel it prefers."

"Lior," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "Why is Lior the keystone?"

"Because of when." His finger stopped on the entry. "The year of the founding treaty. The year the valley sealed itself and magic died at the tree line. You know that story—the wards were a gift, a mercy, a border against the crown's reach." He shook his head slowly. "Wards are not woven from nothing, girl. A ward that strong, that permanent, that hungry for power across centuries—it has an engine. And the treaty-makers, may their names rot, did not build the engine. They *bought* one. They purchased peace with a person. One soul, vast enough, bright enough, rooted deep enough in the valley's soil, to anchor the whole working. The throne agreed because the arrangement suited it perfectly: the valley's keystone would sit at the mountain's heart, feeding both ends of the bargain. The valley got its forgetting. The throne got its strongest voice."

The room tilted. I gripped the counter. "Lior's soul powers the valley's wards."

"Powers them, anchors them, *is* them. Pull that thread and the valley's protection unravels—but far more importantly, from the throne's perspective, the throne loses its brightest instrument. So it guards the keystone jealously. And it prepares." He flipped the ledger toward me fully, finally, and I saw page after page of entries, decades of them, each marked with the same notation. *Keystone. Reserve. Keystone. Reserve.* "Because engines fail. Even soul-engines gutter, especially when the soul fights—which yours, whoever they were, evidently has, for six years, magnificently. The hunt, girl. The great sweeping hunt for gifted blood that has emptied villages and filled the roads with the half-bought and the wholly taken. It is not punishment. It is not policy. It is a *search*. The throne is auditioning replacements. Every gifted soul taken north is weighed against the keystone's strength, and found wanting, and fed to the lesser workings—the wards of the capital, the scrying pools, the throne's minor appetites—while the search goes on. But someday it will not be found wanting. Someday the hunt will bring home a soul bright enough, and then the keystone will be spent—burned out in a single night of prophecy, the way a candle is spent—and the valley's wards will die with it, and the throne will have a new voice, and no one above ground will ever know why the borders moved."

I thought of Casper. I thought of the tree line, and the way the gift woke in them the moment they crossed it, raw and enormous, a talent starved for twenty years suddenly drowning in the world's fullness. Bright enough. Rooted deep enough. An oath-bound speaker of the old order, the last of a lineage the throne had spent lifetimes cultivating and culling and cultivating again.

They weren't hunting Vess. They weren't hunting me.

They were hunting Casper, and didn't yet know it.

* * *

I walked out into the street with the evening coming down blue and cold, and Casper straightened from the wall where they'd been waiting, and their face did what it always did—opened like a door, honest as bread. And I stood there in the lamplight with a hole in me shaped like a room I couldn't find, holding the weight of what I'd learned, and understood that the next words I said would decide whether we walked into the mountain as thieves or as offerings.

"Casper," I said. "We need to talk about why the hunt hasn't caught up with us yet."

Because that was the other thing the broker had told me, the last thing, the thing he'd thrown in free as I reached the door, almost as an apology: *The hunt-captain who's been tracking you is oath-bound, same as your friend. Old order. Throne-sworn. And the old orders never chase their quarry quickly—they herd it. They drive the bright ones north.*

"They're not trying to catch us," I said, as the lamps of Greyhollow flickered alight one by one down the street, like souls being counted. "They're trying to deliver us."
