## 9. Words Like Knives

The rain came in off the lowlands two days after Greyhollow, and with it came the feeling of being watched.

Casper had learned to read weather the way other people read faces—goats had taught them that, years of squinting at cloud-shapes over the valley rim—but this was different. This was not a storm gathering on the horizon. This was a stillness inside the storm, a held breath in the air between raindrops, and it followed them along the river road like a second shadow.

"You feel that too," Vess said. Not a question. She walked ahead of Casper on the muddy track, hood up, her gifted blood humming so faintly under her skin that Casper could almost see it—a shimmer at her wrists, a heat-haze around her shoulders when she was afraid. She was afraid now.

"It's been following us since the ford," Casper said. "It stops when we stop. It moves when we move."

"Then stop."

They stopped. The rain fell. The birches along the road dripped and shivered, and somewhere behind them, upstream where the water went silver over stones, something that was not a bird called once and went quiet.

Vess turned slowly, scanning the treeline. "Hunt-captains don't travel alone," she murmured. "And they don't travel slow. If it's been pacing us since the ford, it's because it wants to. It's choosing its ground."

"Then let's not give it one." Casper's hand drifted to the sword at their hip—not to draw it, just to touch the worn leather of the grip, the way a child touches a talisman. The blade had hummed ever since the tree line. Ever since the first killing. Words lived in it now, coiled like spring water under ice, and every mile south they seemed to press harder against their containment.

"No," Vess said quietly. "I think we already have." She nodded down the road.

Ahead, where the track bent around a mossy outcrop toward an old stone bridge, a figure stood waiting in the rain. Alone. Unhurried. Cloaked in the grey of the hunt, but without the livery of the crown—the crowned-mountain sigil had been torn away, or never sewn there at all. The figure carried no visible weapon. Their hands were folded before them like a scholar's, or a mourner's.

Casper knew the stance anyway. Knew the set of the shoulders, the deliberate emptiness of the hands. Knew it the way you know your own reflection in dark water.

*An oath-bound speaker.*

"That's impossible," Casper breathed. "The order is dead. I'm the last. I've *always* been the last—"

"Apparently," Vess said, "someone lied to you."

* * *

The figure did not move as they approached. Up close, Casper saw a woman perhaps fifty years old, grey-streaked hair plastered to her cheeks by the rain, a face lined not with cruelty but with something worse: patience. The patience of someone who has done a hard thing many times and no longer finds it hard.

"Casper Eriksen," the woman said. Her voice carried strangely through the rain—too clearly, each syllable arriving whole and undiluted, as if the words refused to be weathered. "Of the valley. Of the old valley, I should say. You have your teacher's cadence. Maren's. I'd know it anywhere."

"Maren is dead," Casper said.

"Yes." The woman's eyes did not soften. "I know. I was there."

The words landed like a stone dropped into still water, and the ripples went outward through everything Casper thought they knew. "You were—" They stopped. Started again. "Who are you?"

"Halvard Senn. Once. Now I am called Captain, and that is enough of a name for the work." She unfolded her hands at last, and Casper saw that her palms were scarred in the old way—in the ritual way, thin white lines crossing in patterns Casper had only ever seen on their own skin, cut there by Maren's knife on the night of an oath. "You're wondering how I can stand here, speaking the high speech, wearing the scars, and serve the crown that hunts our kind. I can see it in your face. Your teacher left you innocent. That was cruel of her."

"She left me *free*," Casper said.

Halvard smiled, and it was the saddest thing Casper had seen in weeks of sad things. "Did she? Then why are you standing in the rain with a fugitive, hunted by every rider between here and the mountain, carrying words in your chest that you're afraid to speak aloud?" She tilted her head. "Freedom looks different than I remembered."

Vess shifted at Casper's shoulder. "We should go," she said low. "This is a speech. Speeches are what they do before the killing."

"This is a parley," Halvard corrected, without looking at her. "Because I am going to offer you something, Eriksen, and I would rather you hear it standing than bleeding. But if you run, I will follow, and I will not parley twice." She rolled her shoulders once, loose, easy, and the rain around her seemed to bend—just slightly, just for a heartbeat—as though the water itself had leaned in to listen. "So. Let us do this properly. As the order used to do it."

"And how was that?"

"With words." Halvard drew breath, and the world changed.

* * *

Later, Casper would try to describe it to Vess and fail, because there were no words for the words themselves. What Halvard spoke was not a shout, not a command, not any sound a throat makes. It was the high speech unbound—the language Casper had practiced in empty goat-pastures all their life, the forms Maren had drilled into muscle and bone, spoken at last with full force into open air.

*"STAND."*

The word hit like a wall. Casper felt it take hold of their body from the outside, felt their knees try to lock, their lungs try to still—and then the oath rose in them, the old sworn words waking in their marrow, and they answered without deciding to.

*"NO."*

The single syllable tore Halvard's command apart like wet paper. Rain scattered outward from between them in a ring. A birch at the roadside shed every leaf it had, a sudden green snowfall in the grey morning.

Halvard's eyes widened—one heartbeat of genuine surprise—and then she laughed, short and fierce, and moved.

What followed was not a duel as Casper understood dueling. There was no footwork, or none that mattered; there was no steel, or none that decided anything. It was a conversation conducted at the speed of violence, in a grammar made of force. Halvard spoke *FALL*, and Casper spoke *ROOT*, and their feet became things planted, immovable, while the ground beneath Halvard cracked in a spreading web. She spoke *BLIND*, and darkness poured over Casper's eyes like ink dropped in water—and Casper reached for the oldest form, the first one Maren ever taught, the one that was barely a word at all, more a shape the mouth remembers—

*"SEE."*

The darkness split. The world returned doubled, sharpened, every raindrop distinct, every thread of Halvard's intent laid bare in the half-second before she voiced it. Casper saw the next word forming in her throat, saw its shape, and spoke its opposite before she could finish breathing it.

Her shield-word shattered. The recoil lifted her off her feet and threw her back across the bridge stones, and she landed hard, rolled, came up coughing blood into the rain.

Casper stood shaking in the center of the road, both hands pressed flat against their own sternum, feeling the oath roar through them like a river through a broken dam. It had never been like this in the valley. In the valley the gift had been a rumor, a hum, a warmth in the wrist. Here it was a living thing, vast and hungry, and it wanted *more*. It wanted Casper to keep speaking. It wanted the next word, and the next, and it did not care what those words unmade.

"Enough," Halvard rasped. She knelt on the wet stones, one arm cradled wrong, blood threading pink down her chin. "Enough. You win. Do you hear me? You *win*."

Casper didn't move. Couldn't. Every instinct screamed to finish it—to speak the ending word, the one Maren had named but never demonstrated, the one that closed a speaker's throat forever. The gift surged toward it like a dog toward an open gate.

"Don't," Vess said softly, at their elbow. Her hand found their wrist. "Casper. Look at her. *Look.*"

Casper looked. And the hunger receded, grudgingly, like a tide remembering the moon.

* * *

They sat Halvard upright against the bridge's parapet and bound her arm with strips of Vess's cloak-lining, because Casper's hands were still trembling too badly to manage the knots. The captain endured it without complaint, watching Casper with an expression that was almost—almost—tender.

"I trained twenty years to fight a speaker like you," she said. "And you've been free of the mountain, what—five days? Six? You fought like someone remembering a language they dreamed in as a child." She shook her head slowly. "Maren always said the valley would keep one of us pure. I thought she meant weak. She meant exactly this."

"Tell me about the order," Casper said. "Tell me how you're alive. Tell me how you serve *them*."

Halvard was quiet for a long moment. The rain thinned around them, the storm spending itself eastward, and through the thinning grey the mountain was visible far to the north—a smudge of darker shadow on the horizon, patient as a debt.

"The order never died, Eriksen. It was bought." She said it plainly, without shame, and somehow that was the worst part. "When the crown beneath the mountain began to keep souls—before your valley was even founded, before the treaty—the old speakers were the only power in the world that could threaten it. So the crown didn't fight us. It hired us. It gave us purpose, rank, permanence. It told us we were the last line of order in a world coming apart, and it paid us in the only coin we'd ever truly wanted." Her mouth twisted. "Meaning. We swore our oaths to protect, and the crown simply... redefined what needed protecting. We've been guarding the throne for three hundred years. Every hunt-captain who rides down a gifted child is an oath-bound speaker doing what they believe is their duty. The words don't ask whether the cause is just. They only ask whether the master is kept."

Casper's stomach turned over slowly, like something dying. "My oath. My *oath*—"

"—is the same oath I took. Word for word, scar for scar. Yes." Halvard met their eyes, and there was no gloating in it, only a terrible kindness. "Which means the same voice that just threw me across this bridge has spent centuries throwing children into wagons. The gift isn't clean, Eriksen. It was never clean. It's a blade, and blades belong to whoever holds the hilt. Maren hid you from the hilt. The crown built one big enough to hide the whole order."

Vess had gone very still. "The hunt," she said. "The proclamation. 'Protection for gifted blood.' You're not hunting replacements for the throne because the old ones are failing. You're hunting because—"

"Because the throne eats what feeds it," Halvard said. "Souls burn. Even kept ones. Especially kept ones. Lior's light has fed that lattice for a generation, and it gutters a little more each year, and the crown grows hungry, and we ride out to fill its table." She closed her eyes. "I have written that sentence in my own head a thousand times and it has never once gotten easier. That's the price of keeping your conscience, if you're wondering. You keep it, and it keeps you awake."

"Why tell me this?" Casper asked. "Why lose? Why parley at all? You could have brought thirty riders."

"Thirty riders would have killed your friend and dragged you north in chains, and the throne would have offered you Halvard's chair, and eventually you'd have taken it, because meaning is meaning." Halvard opened her eyes again. "I lost on purpose, boy. Girl. Whatever Maren's valley raised you to be. I lost because I wanted to see, with my own eyes, whether the old order could still produce something worth being loyal to." She looked down at her ruined arm, then back up, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "It can. Which is why I'm giving you the only gift I have left."

She spoke then—quietly, carefully, each word placed like a surgeon's stitch—and the high speech wrapped the sentence in silence so that only Casper and Vess could hear it:

"The throne's lattice has a keystone. One soul older than all the others, set at the center, holding the whole architecture together. Cut the keystone free and the lattice unravels, the court goes dark, and every soul it holds spills out like birds from a burning rookery." Her gaze held Casper's, merciless and gentle at once. "You already know whose soul it is. You've known since the dreams started. Don't pretend the voice under the stone hasn't been calling you your whole life."

The rain had stopped. Somewhere downstream, water moved over stones, indifferent and bright.

"How do I reach it?" Casper heard themself ask. Their voice sounded far away, borrowed, belonging to someone who had already decided.

"Greyhollow has a broker who sells maps to fools," Halvard said. "But understand what you're asking. The keystone isn't kept behind walls. It's kept behind *words*—the deepest binding the order ever wove, laid down by the first captains, sealed in the same tongue you just beat me with. To break it, you'll need more than strength. You'll need to be willing to unmake the very thing that gives you power over it." She pushed herself slowly up the parapet, testing her weight, wincing. "The oath that arms you, Eriksen, is woven from the same thread as the binding that holds her. Old law. Symmetry. The founders built it that way on purpose, so no speaker could ever turn against the throne without turning against themselves."

Casper stood very still. The sword at their hip had gone silent—not dormant, but listening, the way a room listens after a door closes.

"So that's the choice," Vess said quietly. "The gift, or her."

"That's always the choice," Halvard said. "It's the only one the order ever really offered anyone. We just dressed ours up in banners." She stepped off the bridge, favoring her arm, and paused at the roadside with her back to them. Over her shoulder, she said: "Kill me now, if you're wise. I'll heal, and I'll report, and the hunt will come for you with everything the crown has left. But I find I'm not wise anymore. I find I'd like to see what happens if, just once, somebody chooses the girl over the words."

She walked north, into the thinning rain, toward the mountain she served and, perhaps, no longer believed in.

Casper watched until the grey took her. Then they turned to Vess, and the question in their chest came out smaller than it had felt:

"What do we do?"

Vess looked down the road—south, toward Greyhollow, toward brokers and ledgers and a name dated the year of a treaty—and her jaw set in the way Casper was learning meant the argument was already over.

"We go buy a map," she said. "And then we go learn how to break an oath."

Above the trees, far to the north, thunder rolled off the mountain—though the storm had passed, and there was nothing left in the sky to make it.
