## 10. The Memory Beneath the Scar

The wound in Casper's side had a voice.

It spoke to them through the long hours of the night, in the grammar of pain—short sentences, sharp as chisels. *Here. Here. Here.* The hunt-captain's blade had opened them from hip to rib, and though Vess had stitched the flesh with thread boiled in wine and packed the gape with moss that smelled of crushed green things, the body kept its own ledger, and it was not inclined to forgive.

They had sheltered in the ruin of a shepherd's bothy high on the mountain's shoulder, three days' walk below Greyhollow's burning gates. The walls were dry-stone and shoulder-high, roofed with slates that had slipped like scales off an old serpent, so that the stars came through in long silver seams. Wind moved through the gaps and made a sound like breathing. Somewhere below, a fox screamed once and was answered by nothing.

Vess sat against the far wall with her knees drawn up, keeping watch. She had not slept since the duel. Casper could feel her eyes on them whenever the fever let them surface—a cool, weighing attention, like a hand laid flat on a hot forehead.

"You're burning," she said, when Casper surfaced this time.

"I'm fine."

"You're lying, and you're bad at it." She crossed the small room and knelt, pressing her palm to Casper's brow. Her skin was cold as stream-water. "Fever-talk already. You were calling someone."

Casper went very still.

"Who is Lior?" Vess asked quietly.

The name sat in Casper's mouth like a stone they had carried so long they had forgotten it was not part of their tongue. "I don't know," they said, and heard how the words rang hollow even to their own ears. "That's the truth. I know the name. I know—" Their breath caught. "I know the way my chest turns over when I say it. Like a door swinging on rusted hinges. But there's no one behind the door. There's just... dark."

Vess studied them for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried at the loose slates.

"Then sleep," she said finally. "And if the door opens while you're under, walk through it. Fever strips things. Sometimes what's underneath is worth the stripping."

She settled back against the wall, and Casper lay down again on the pile of bracken and sheepskins, and the pain spoke its short sharp sentences, and the fever rose like water in a locked room, and Casper sank.

* * *

The dream did not begin as dreams begin. It began as memory begins: mid-motion, mid-breath, already believing itself true.

Casper stood in the valley, but the valley was wrong—or rather, it was right in ways the waking valley had forgotten. The wards were young. Casper could see them, which no one in the waking years could do: great slow rings of pale gold light rising from the tree line like heat-shimmer given architecture, humming a note too low for ears, felt instead in the teeth and the breastbone. The air tasted of ozone and cut grass. Magic pressed against the wards from outside like faces against glass—the wild gift of the world leaning in, hungry, and being refused.

And Casper was twenty-three, and whole, and unscarred, and walking up the hill toward the standing stone with Lior beside them.

They knew Lior the way you know your own hands. Not the details—the details would come later in the dream, arriving one by one like lanterns lit down a corridor—but the *fact* of them. The way Lior walked half a step ahead always, impatient with hills. The way their braid had come loose on one side and they hadn't noticed. The smell of woodsmoke and juniper that clung to their coat. When Lior laughed—and they laughed now, at something Casper had said, some joke worn smooth by a hundred tellings—the sound went into Casper's chest and made a warm hollow there, the way a hearth makes a warm hollow in a winter wall.

"Say it again," Lior said. "The thing about the goats."

"The goats are philosophers. They've rejected all authority except the authority of food."

"There it is." Lior grinned, and the grin was crooked on the left, and Casper's heart did the thing it always did, the small treacherous lurch. "You'll be the death of me, Eriksen. Slowly. Over decades. I accept these terms."

They reached the standing stone as the sun went down behind the western ridge, and the light came long and amber across the grass, and everything was so beautiful that Casper understood, even inside the dream, why the memory had been buried. Beauty like this, remembered precisely, is a kind of wound that never closes.

Lior stopped at the stone and turned, and their face changed. The laughter drained out of it slowly, like water finding a crack.

"They're coming tonight," Lior said. "Aren't they."

"Yes."

"And you're going to swear it."

Casper could not speak. In the dream, as in the buried truth beneath the dream, the answer was yes, and the yes was a physical thing, a stone behind the sternum.

Lior nodded, slowly, looking out at the valley—the rooftops going gold and then grey, the smoke rising straight in the still air, the distant specks of goats on the high pasture. All of it lit like something about to be photographed by dying light.

"How many of us are there?" Lior asked. "Gifted ones. In the valley."

"Eleven. Twelve if you count old Maren, and her gift is knowing when bread is done."

Lior laughed despite themselves—a wet, broken laugh—and wiped their eyes with the heel of their hand. "Twelve. And the court wants all twelve, or they burn the valley and take forty times that number along with everything else. That's the arithmetic."

"That's the arithmetic."

"And I'm the largest piece." Lior said it without self-pity, which was somehow worse. They turned back to Casper, and took both of Casper's hands in theirs, and their grip was strong and sure and shaking all at once. "Listen to me. Listen. This isn't your doing. You understand? Whatever happens tonight, whatever they make you forget—you didn't sell me. You carried me to the door because the alternative was carrying eleven others, and children, Casper. There are children on that list."

"I know."

"Do you?" Lior's voice cracked. "Because you look at me like I'm already a grave. Don't. Don't you dare stand at that stone in an hour and make yourself a murderer. You're making yourself a wall. There's a difference, and I need you to know the difference, or the knowing won't survive the forgetting, and then it will just be you and the guilt alone in the dark for the rest of your life, and I will *not* have that. Do you hear me?"

"I hear you."

"Say the difference."

"You're—" Casper's throat closed. They forced it open. "You're not a grave. I'm a wall."

"A wall," Lior agreed, fierce and gentle at once, pressing Casper's hands between their own like something being sealed. "Walls keep people alive. Remember that if you can. Remember anything you can. They'll take most of it—that's how the oath works, that's the price, it eats the reasons and leaves the rules—but fever remembers, Casper. Blood remembers. Grief remembers. If you ever find me again—if you ever learn what happened—don't let the guilt be the only thing left. Let the love be louder."

The sun touched the ridge. The amber light went red.

Down in the valley, a bell began to ring—not the alarm bell, but the treaty bell, the small cracked bronze in the gate tower that had rung only twice before in living memory. Once at the founding. Once at the last negotiation.

This was the third time.

Lior looked toward the sound, and then back at Casper, and smiled—the crooked smile, the left-side smile, the smile Casper had spent fifteen years unable to place in any memory and yet had woken aching after every dream of falling.

"There's my wall," Lior said softly. "Go on, then. Go be load-bearing."

* * *

The standing stone at full dark was a different country.

Torches ringed it—six of them, their flames bent inward by some pressure Casper could feel but the flames themselves seemed resigned to. The elders stood in a crescent, robed, their faces shadowed by hoods that the firelight filled unevenly. And before the stone, waiting, stood a figure Casper's dreaming mind recognized with a horror that had no bottom: the hunt-captain. Not as they had fought him on the road—younger by decades, unbloodied, his oath-marks barely faded on his throat—but unmistakable. The same stillness. The same eyes like coins left on a dead man's eyelids.

He had come to administer the oath. He had come, Casper understood now, as the crown's notary.

"Casper Eriksen," he said, and his voice was the same voice that had spoken cutting words across a forest road, aged but not changed. "Do you come freely?"

"I come freely."

"Do you understand the terms?"

Casper's waking self strained against the dream like a moth against glass. *Don't say it. Don't—*

But the dream-Casper said it, in a clear voice that carried across the torchlit grass, and every word fell like a coin into a well whose depth Casper was only now measuring:

"I give my word against my memory. I bind my speech to my silence. What I swear, I serve; what I serve, I lose; and what I lose, I lose willingly, for the peace of the valley and the lives of its people. Until the crown falls or the speaker breaks, the bargain holds."

The words went into the stone. Casper felt them go—felt the standing stone drink them the way dry earth drinks rain, greedily, with a low subsonic shudder that traveled up through Casper's boots and spine and teeth. And then the taking began.

It was not violent. That was the obscenity of it. It was gentle as snowfall. Memories did not tear free; they *dimmed*, one by one, like lamps in a house where someone walks from room to room behind you, considerate, thorough, leaving darkness in their wake. Casper felt Lior's face go first—the features softening into a blur, then a warmth without a shape, then nothing but the name, the single syllable-pair, *Lior*, orphaned and glowing like a coal raked from a fire. Then the voice. Then the crooked smile. Then the decade of mornings, the shared work, the arguments about philosophy and goats, the winter Lior had been sick and Casper had sat up every night—

"No," the dream-Casper whispered, reaching for it, and the elders bowed their heads, and the hunt-captain watched with his coin-flat eyes, and the memory went out like a lamp.

Then the reason. Then the arithmetic. Then the twelve names, and the children among them. Then the treaty bell, and why it had rung.

Last of all, the oath took the *why of the why*: the simple human fact that Casper had loved Lior more than they had feared the crown, and had chosen anyway, and had needed to forget the choosing in order to survive it.

What remained was a scar with no history. A grief with no object. A name with no face. A swordspeaker who knew the rules of their binding and not one single reason for them—who would spend fifteen years reciting fragments in the dark, feeling each recitation eat a little more, mistaking the hunger of the oath for the ordinary erosion of time.

The torches guttered. The elders murmured the closing words. The hunt-captain stepped forward and pressed his thumb briefly to Casper's brow—an old gesture, sealing, notarial—and said, in a voice pitched for Casper alone:

"Forget him well, little wall. The throne thanks you for your stone."

*Him.* The word detonated silently in the dream, and the dream began to collapse around it—*

* * *

Casper woke with Lior's name in their mouth and blood on their lips.

They had bitten their tongue in the night; the fever had wrung them hard enough to convulse. The bothy was grey with pre-dawn, the star-seams in the roof gone pale. Vess was kneeling over them, her hands on their shoulders, her face tight with alarm.

"—calling again," she was saying. "You were calling. The same name, over and over, and then you started saying other things. Words I couldn't understand. Your scars were *glowing*, Casper. The oath-marks. Like coals under the skin."

Casper pushed themselves up on one elbow. The wound tore hot, and they gasped, and kept pushing anyway, until they were sitting, shaking, the dream still clinging to them like riverwater.

"It wasn't a dream," they said. Their voice came out sanded raw. "Vess. It wasn't a dream. I remember."

"Remember what?"

"Everything." The word broke open as it left them, and what spilled out was worse than grief—it was arithmetic. "There were twelve of us. Gifted, in the valley. The court gave the elders a choice: surrender the strongest soul among us, freely given, sworn and bound—or they'd burn the wards and take everyone. And the strongest soul—" Casper's hand found their own sternum, pressed there as though to hold the ribs together. "The strongest soul was Lior's. And I'm the one who carried him to the standing stone. I'm the one who swore the oath. Me. Freely. I built the wall myself, and I put him in it, and then I paid the oath to let me forget I'd done it."

Vess was silent for a long moment. The wind breathed through the broken roof. Down the mountainside, faintly, a bird began the day's first argument with the light.

When she spoke, her voice was careful, stripped of pity, which Casper understood suddenly was the greatest kindness available. "And now you remember the choosing. Every hour of it."

"Every hour." Casper laughed—a terrible sound, cracked down the middle. "Fifteen years I thought the ache in my chest was the cost of the gift. It was the bill. It was always the bill." They looked down at their hands—the sword-calloused, steady hands that had held Lior's at sunset and signed away his morning. "He told me not to make myself a murderer. He told me to be a wall. And I forgot even that, Vess. I forgot the mercy he handed me with both hands. I've been alone in the dark with the guilt and none of the love, exactly like he said I would be, exactly what he begged me not to—"

"Stop." Vess's hand closed on their wrist—not gently, but firmly, an anchor thrown to someone drifting. "Look at me. You remembered. That's the part he wanted. Fever remembers, blood remembers—he bet on you remembering, even if it took fifteen years and a blade in your side to do it. So don't waste it on the floor of a ruined bothy. Get up. We're two days from Greyhollow, and somewhere under that mountain is a man who has been waiting in the dark for you to walk through the door."

Casper looked at her. Something shifted in their chest—the guilt, which had been a dead weight, turning over and becoming something with a pulse. An engine. A direction.

"Not under the mountain," Casper said slowly. "In it. At the center of it. Vess—the broker's ledger, the throne, the keystone. If Lior's soul is what holds the crown alive, then every year the throne endures, he endures. Alone. Aware." They got their feet under them, swaying, white-faced, upright. "I spent fifteen years forgetting him. I'm not spending one more hour than I have to."

Vess rose with them, shouldering the pack, and paused at the doorway with the dawn breaking grey-gold behind her.

"There's something else," she said. "Something you should know before we take another step toward that place. About the night I lost my sister. About what I did." Her jaw tightened. "If we're going to walk into the dark together, you deserve to know what's in my dark."

Casper, still tasting blood and memory, nodded once.

"Tell me on the road," they said. "We've got two days, and I have fifteen years to outrun."
