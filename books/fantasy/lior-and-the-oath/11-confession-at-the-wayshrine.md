## 11. Confession at the Wayshrine

The shrine had been a waystation once, back when the road between Greyhollow and the mountain passes carried pilgrims instead of soldiers. Now it was a ribcage of pale stone open to the sky, its roof long fallen, its altar furred with moss the color of old copper. Rain had come and gone through the afternoon, leaving everything slick and shining, and the air smelled of wet granite and crushed thyme where Vess's boots had broken the stems growing up through the cracked floor.

She had built the fire small, tucked into the hollow of the altar where the old votive niche still held its shape—a shallow bowl carved with worn figures whose faces time had rubbed away. Whoever they had been, gods or saints or simply names someone wanted remembered, they could not help her now. She fed the flame a twig at a time, hoarding the rest of their wood, and watched Casper sit apart with their sword across their knees like a penitent with a lash.

They had walked hard since Greyhollow. Two days of hedgerows and drainage ditches, of sleeping in hay barns with one ear open for hounds, of Casper flinching every time a bird burst from cover. The wound from the duel had closed but not cleanly; Vess had seen the fever building behind their eyes all morning, the way they pressed a hand to their side when they thought she wasn't looking, the sweat that came without heat. By the time the shrine rose out of the mist, Casper was swaying in the saddle-less walk of the truly exhausted.

Now they sat on a fallen lintel, firelight moving over their face, and Vess understood—before a word was said—that something had been waiting inside them for days, coiled and heavy, and that tonight it was going to come out whether they willed it or not.

"The captain," Casper said. Their voice was rough as unpolished wood. "Before the end. Did you hear what he said to me?"

Vess kept her eyes on the fire. "You were both shouting words that made my teeth ache. I heard pieces."

"He said the order never died." Casper turned the sword slowly, so the light ran along the blade like water finding a channel. "He said we didn't go extinct. We *enlisted*." A sound escaped them—not quite a laugh. "I grew up believing I was the last of something noble. The last keeper. The last wall between the valley and everything outside it. And he stood there telling me the wall was always a door, and the ones who built it held it open."

"You didn't know."

"I didn't *ask*." The words came out sharp, and Casper seemed surprised by their own edge. They looked down at their hands. "That's the thing, Vess. All my life, when something hurt to think about, I recited. That's what the oath does—it gives you the words before it takes anything else. And every time I said them, something went missing, and I let it. I *chose* the numbness. It was easier than the question underneath."

Vess said nothing. She had learned, in the years since her sister was taken, that silence was sometimes the only gift you could give a person that they would actually accept.

The rain started again, soft against the ruined roof beams, dripping through gaps onto stone with a patient, ticking rhythm. Somewhere down the valley a dog barked twice and stopped.

Casper set the sword aside. When they spoke again, their voice had changed—lower, stripped of performance, the voice of someone standing at the edge of deep water.

"There was someone. In the valley. Before you."

Vess went very still.

"I don't remember them." Casper's hands opened and closed in their lap. "That's the truth of it. I have a name—Lior—and I have—" They stopped, jaw working. "I have a feeling, like an ache in a tooth that isn't there anymore. I know that I loved them the way you know a room is warm after you've left it. But the oath ate the rest. Every recitation took another bite, and I fed it willingly because the alternative was remembering what I'd done."

"What did you do?"

And then it came out—all of it, in a flood that seemed to frighten Casper more than any enemy had. The night of the treaty, which they had only recovered in fragments of fever-dream: the delegation from beneath the mountain, the elder's gray face, the choice laid out like knives on a table. Give us one soul of gifted blood, freely surrendered, and the valley sleeps safe behind wards no hunt can cross. Refuse, and the harvest comes for everyone.

"I gave them Lior." Casper's voice cracked on the name. "Not under threat. Not tricked. I stood in that room and I said yes, and I remember—I *remember* now—believing it was mercy. Believing one grief was kinder than a hundred. I believed it so completely that I swore the oath on top of it to make sure I could never take it back. I sold the person I loved most in the world to buy my own peace, and then I paid good words to forget the price."

The fire popped. A drop of rain fell into the votive bowl and hissed.

"They're alive," Casper said. "In the throne. I heard them. Under all those voices, I heard Lior, and Lior is *aware*, Vess. Aware and waiting and—" Their breath shuddered. "Every mile we've walked toward the mountain, I've told myself I'm doing this to free them. To make it right. But I keep wondering if that's just another recitation. Another comfortable numbness. Because maybe I'm not going to save Lior. Maybe I'm just trying to find a version of this story where I'm not the monster who put them there."

Vess watched them across the small fire: this person who had taken her in off the ward-line when the law of their valley said leave her to bleed, who killed for her at the tree line, who burned their name in Greyhollow to pull strangers out of a collapsing street. A monster. The word sat wrong in her chest, and she examined why, and examining why led her somewhere she had not gone in years, down a stair she had boarded over and pretended was load-bearing.

Because she knew what Casper was describing. She knew it from the inside.

"Say something," Casper whispered. "Even if it's condemnation. Especially if it's that. I can carry being hated. What I can't carry is carrying it alone anymore."

Vess fed the last of the twigs to the fire, buying herself three breaths. Then four. The rain ticked. The worn faces in the altar stone watched with their erased eyes.

"When I was sixteen," she said, "the hunt came through Marrowfen. My town. You've never heard of it—they made sure of that afterward."

Casper lifted their head.

"My sister Sable was gifted. Small things—she could coax light out of dark rooms, make candles burn colors, that kind of gentle nonsense. Everyone loved her for it until the proclamation, and then everyone loved the reward money more." Vess heard her own voice go flat and careful, the tone of someone reading an inventory. "There was a cellar under the tanner's house where the gifted families hid their children when riders came. Six of us knew the location. Sixteen, actually, but six who mattered—the ones the riders would believe.

"They caught me alone at the well. Not even roughly. A captain bought me honey wine and asked me questions like he was asking about weather, and I told him where the cellar was, and I told myself the whole time that I wasn't really telling him, that I'd misremembered, that it wouldn't matter. He gave me a coin. One coin. I still have it." Her hand went to the cord at her throat, to the small tarnished disc hanging there that she had never once explained. "I wore it ever after so I couldn't pretend it didn't happen. Some people wear scars to remember. I wore payment."

Casper had gone white. "What happened to the others?"

"Sable got two of them out through the coal chute. She always knew the exits of things." Something moved through Vess's face then, brief and terrible, like fish under ice. "The third was a boy named Ollin. Eleven years old. They took him, and I don't know what happened after, because I ran. I ran north with my coin and my sister's curses following me the whole way, and I have spent nine years telling myself I did it to survive, which is true, and that survival excuses it, which is a lie I tell so smoothly I almost believe it in the mornings."

The rain thickened. Water threaded silver down the fallen lintels, pooling in the moss, finding the old channels cut by masons who had expected pilgrims' tears and got the sky instead.

"So when you ask me to condemn you," Vess said, "you're asking me to stand in a glass house and throw stones at my own reflection. I won't do it. Not because you deserve absolution—you might not, and neither might I—but because I know exactly how heavy that coin gets, and no one should carry it alone. That's not mercy. That's just arithmetic."

Casper stared at her. In the firelight their eyes were wet and wide, and something in their expression was coming apart—not collapsing, Vess thought, but *unclenching*, like a fist held too long finally opening, blood rushing back into the fingers.

"You sold your sister's location," they said slowly, "and she survived."

"She survived and she hates me, and she has every right to, and I'm going to find her anyway." Vess touched the coin. "That's the other thing about betrayals, Casper. They don't end. You don't finish them. You just keep walking beside them, and either they teach you something or they eat you. Sable taught me that the only thing worse than what I did is becoming someone who'd do it again without noticing. So I notice. Every day. I notice so hard it hurts."

"And that's enough? Noticing?"

"No." Vess laughed, short and joyless. "It's not enough. Nothing is enough. That's the joke. But it's *something*, and something is what we have."

Casper looked down at the sword lying in the moss between them. When they spoke again, their voice had steadied into something new—not the numb recitation-voice, not the fevered confession-voice, but a third thing, quiet and deliberate, like a hand setting a stone into a wall.

"I want to make a pact with you. Here. At this shrine, in front of whatever these worn-out gods used to be." They gestured at the altar. "Truth between us. Always. Even when it costs. Even when it's ugly. Especially then. Because I've seen what comfortable lies build—wards around valleys, coins around necks, oaths around hearts—and none of it holds. Lies are the mountain's whole economy. Souls bought with promises. I don't want to trade in that currency anymore, not with you. If we're going to walk into that court and steal a soul out of a throne, I need one place in the world where I don't have to perform."

Vess considered them for a long moment. The rain, the fire, the ruined holiness of the place—all of it seemed to lean in and wait.

"Truth between us," she said. "Always. Even when it costs." She drew the coin from her throat and held it out over the fire, and for a heartbeat she was tempted—genuinely tempted—to drop it into the flames, to let the metal take her sin and melt it away. Then she closed her hand around it again and tucked it back beneath her collar. "No. It stays. That's part of the pact too. We don't get to throw our betrayals in the fire. We carry them where we can see them. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Casper extended their hand across the altar, and Vess took it, and her grip was iron and their grip was fever-warm, and the pact needed no oath to seal it—which was, Vess thought, precisely the point. Oaths were the mountain's language. This was just two liars agreeing to stop lying to each other, and it bound tighter than any spoken force ever could.

For a while neither of them spoke. The fire settled. The rain eased to a whisper. Casper's eyes grew heavy, the fever pulling them down at last, and Vess eased them back against the lintel and draped her cloak over them and sat watch with her knife across her knees.

It was near midnight, the fire burned to orange coals, when she heard it.

Hooves. Many of them, far off down the valley road, moving fast—not the scattered patrol-rhythm she'd learned to catalog over two days of flight, but a concentrated mass, riding together, riding with purpose. And beneath the hoofbeats, faint but unmistakable, a sound she had hoped never to hear again: the low chanted cadence of spoken force rolling ahead of the riders like thunder looking for somewhere to land.

Vess was on her feet with her hand on Casper's shoulder, shaking them awake, whispering the only truth that mattered.

"They found us. Get up—we have about four minutes, and I need you to tell me exactly how much of that duel-magic you have left."
