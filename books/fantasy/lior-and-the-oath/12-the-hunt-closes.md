## 12. The Hunt Closes

The fire began, as fires do, in a small and stupid place.

Casper saw it first as a smudge of orange against the eastern sky, past the tanneries where the smell of rendered fat hung thick enough to chew. They had been counting coins at the market well—Vess's coins, stretched thin across three days of bread and cheese and a room above a dyer's shop that smelled of mordant and old urine—and for a moment they simply watched that smudge and thought it was dawn coming wrong.

Then a bell began to ring. Not the measured tolling of Greyhollow's temple bell, which Casper had learned marked the hours with a kind of patient indifference. This was faster. Frantic. The sound of someone hauling on a rope with both hands and no thought for rhythm.

"Fire," said the well-keeper, an old woman with forearms like ship rope. She said it the way people say *rain* or *taxes*—a thing that happens to cities, not to you. Then she looked east again, and her face changed, because the smudge had grown teeth. "That's not the tanneries. That's the *tanner's row*. That's—" She stopped. Her eyes went wide and white. "That's the whole quarter."

Casper left the coins on the well's lip.

They found Vess already in the street, pressed against the wall of the dyer's shop, her hood up, her face turned toward the smoke. She had gone very still—the stillness she wore like armor, the stillness of a woman who had learned that being overlooked was the difference between living and dying. But her hand was shaking against the stone.

"That's not an accident," she said when Casper reached her. Her voice was flat, which Casper had learned meant fear. "Tannery rows don't catch all at once. Look at it. Three fires. Four." She pointed with her chin, not her hand—a hunted woman's pointing. "They're spaced. That's a pattern."

"The hunt-captain," Casper said.

"He said he'd find us." Vess's jaw tightened. "I didn't think he'd burn a city quarter to do it."

Down the street, people were spilling from their doors with buckets and blankets, forming the ragged chain that every city makes when fire comes—the human bucket-line from well to flame, neighbors who hated each other yesterday passing water hand to hand tonight. Casper watched them and felt something twist beneath the scar tissue of the oath. These people did not know what the fire was. They thought it was fire.

"They'll sweep the refugees," Vess said. "It's clever, I'll give them that. Burn the quarter where the gifted poor hide, and every soul that runs out of it gets counted at the gates. Names checked. Blood pricked." She laughed, short and bitter. "You can't hide a gift from a prick-test. It *answers*, Casper. It wants to be found."

"Then we go out the west side. Now. Before they close it."

"We could." Vess looked at them, and in the growing light of the burning quarter her eyes were very bright and very tired. "We could walk away right now and be two leagues gone by morning. That's what survivors do. That's what I taught you."

Casper did not answer, because they were watching the bucket-line.

A boy had joined it—young, maybe ten, hauling a bucket nearly his own size, staggering between a cooper and a fishwife. His hair was singed at the ends. He passed his bucket up the line with the grim determination of someone doing a man's work, and when he reached the well he leaned on its lip and coughed, and Casper saw the cough shake something loose in him—saw the air around him shimmer, just slightly, like heat off summer stone.

Gifted. A child, gifted, and terrified, and his gift leaking out of him like blood from a wound because fear strips us all down to what we are.

The boy looked up. Across the crowded street, through smoke and panic and forty strangers, his eyes found Casper's—and Casper understood, with a lurch, that the boy hadn't found them by accident. The gift answered the gift. The shimmer had called to whatever slept under Casper's skin, and the boy had followed the call the way a drowning man follows light.

He mouthed one word. Casper read it easily, though no sound crossed the street.

*Help.*

* * *

Later, Casper would try to remember making the decision, and would fail. There was no moment they could point to, no scale tipping. There was only the boy's mouth shaping that word, and then there was Casper moving toward him through the crowd, and Vess hissing "*Casper*" behind them in a voice that cracked like a whip.

"You can't," Vess caught their arm at the corner of Tanner's Row, where the heat came off the burning buildings in waves you could lean against. "Listen to me. Every instinct you have is wrong right now. That boy is dead the moment anyone looks at him twice, and so are you, and so am I if you make me come after you. We *leave*."

"The soldiers will be at the gates counting refugees."

"Yes!"

"And he's ten years old."

Vess stared at them. Smoke drifted between them. Somewhere close, a roof beam collapsed in a gout of sparks, and neither of them flinched—they were past flinching now, both of them, worn down to the bare wire of purpose.

"You don't even know him," she said quietly.

"I know what it is to be hunted for something you didn't choose." Casper pulled free, gently. "You taught me how the gifted survive, Vess. Hide. Bribe. Lie. You taught me all of it. But you never taught me to watch a child burn, because you knew I'd say no."

For a heartbeat she looked like she might strike them. Then her face crumpled into something older and wearier, and she said, "Where?"

"The dyer's cellar has a coal chute to the alley. I saw it yesterday. If we get him off the street before the sweep reaches this block—"

"If. If. Everything with you is *if*." But she was already moving, already scanning the street with those sharp fugitive's eyes, and Casper loved her a little for that—for the way her no became yes without ever quite saying so. "The cooper's house connects to the dyer's yard. Cooper owes me a favor; I mended his wife's fever-charm last week. Go get your boy. I'll open the back way. *Quickly*, Casper. The sweep is two streets over. I can hear the horses."

* * *

Getting the boy was the easy part. Children, Casper discovered, will follow almost anyone who crouches to their height and speaks calmly, and this boy—Tam, he said, Tam of the tanneries, though his voice shook on the words—was past questioning. He had seen his street catch fire in four places at once. He had seen men in crown-black riding through the smoke, not fighting the flames but herding the fleeing, funneling them toward the gates with leveled spears. He clung to Casper's arm and asked only one question: "Are you one of the hidden ones? Mam said there were hidden ones. Mam said—"

"I'm going to get you somewhere safe," Casper said, which was not an answer, and both of them knew it.

The cooper's house was chaos and kindness. The cooper himself—a huge gray-bearded man with burned thumbs—took one look at Tam's face and stood aside from his own family's packing to let them through to the yard. "Back gate sticks," he rumbled. "Lift, then shove. And lad—" he caught Casper's shoulder, and his grip was iron, and his eyes were wet, "—they're taking people at the gates. Taking them *away*. My brother's girl had the knowing-gift, small thing, reading weather in her joints. They took her in spring. No one's seen her since." He let go. "Run fast."

The chute, the alley, the dark. Vess met them at the dyer's cellar door with a lamp and no expression at all, which was its own kind of eloquence. Below the dyer's shop was a low brick vault where dye vats sat empty, and beyond it, half-collapsed, an old drainage tunnel that ran toward the river—a smuggler's passage from some earlier, less lawful century.

"It floods at high water," Vess said. "We have maybe an hour before the tide turns. After that, the far end drowns." She set the lamp down and finally, fully looked at Tam, who was staring around the vault with the wide eyes of a child cataloguing wonders. Something in her face went soft and broke a little. "Gods. He's so *small*. They're hunting children now. They're hunting children and burning quarters to do it."

"Mam hid me in the flour bin," Tam said conversationally, to no one. "But the fire found the flour bin anyway. Fire finds everything."

"Fire doesn't find everything," Casper said. "Come here. Can you run?"

"I can run fast." Tam lifted his chin. "Faster than the soldiers. I made the soldiers look silly once. I made their boots stick to the cobbles and they fell down and everyone laughed." His face flickered—pride, then terror, in quick succession. "That's why they want me. Mam says never use it. Mam says using it is like lighting a lamp in a dark room. Everyone sees."

Your mother is wise, Casper did not say. Instead: "Then we keep your lamp unlit. All the way to the river."

* * *

They might have made it. Casper would spend the rest of their life wondering about that—the narrowness of it, the terrible arithmetic of minutes. The tunnel was ankle-deep and rising, the air thick with river-smell and rot, Tam riding Casper's shoulders with his thin legs locked around their throat and his fists knotted in their collar. Vess ranged ahead with the lamp, calling the turns: left at the broken grate, right where the ceiling dipped, mind the gap where the bricks had fallen into black water.

Then the light came from behind them.

Not lamplight. Torchlight, many torches, and with it voices—boots in water, the ring of steel, and cutting through it all a voice Casper knew, a voice like a drawn blade wrapped in velvet:

"*There.* Do you feel it, speakers? Like a struck bell. Our little valley-fledgling, hiding in the dark with a stolen whelp."

The hunt-captain. Ser Adric Vale, whom Casper had last seen bleeding in a rye field outside Greyhollow, courtesy of a duel fought in spoken force. He had healed. He had returned. And he had brought with him, judging by the echo, a dozen men and something worse—Casper could feel it now, a pressure in the ears, a wrongness in the water's flow, the sensation of wards being laid across the tunnel behind them like a net closing.

"Casper Eriksen," the captain called, conversational, echoing. "Last of the oath-bound. Did you think I wouldn't know the feel of swordspeech? I told you in the rye field—I *served* with your order, before your order chose exile. I know what you are. And I know what you're carrying." A pause, and then, silkily: "The boy's gift is worth three crowns' bounty alive. Yours is worth a title. Walk out, surrender the whelp, and I'll write your name in the restoration rolls myself. The throne *wants* speakers, Eriksen. The throne *misses* us."

Casper kept walking. Water rose past their knees. Tam's breath was hot and rapid against their neck.

"He won't come quietly," another voice said—newer, younger, nervous. "The wards are set, Captain. He's boxed."

"Boxed, yes. But oath-bound folk are stubborn as mules and twice as pious. Give him the choice properly. It's more fun when they choose wrong."

Vess had stopped at a junction where the tunnel split, one branch running darker and lower toward the river's sound. She held the lamp high, and its light showed her face: white, set, furious—not at Casper, Casper realized, but at the darkness behind them, at the whole machinery of the hunt.

"The left branch floods first," she breathed. "If we take it, we swim or drown."

"And if we take the right?"

"There is no right. The right branch is where they want us—it surfaces inside the tannery yard, and the tannery yard is full of crown-black." She closed her eyes for one heartbeat. Opened them. "Left, then. Get ready to swim."

But the water was already at their thighs, and Tam was shivering violently now, and Casper could hear the soldiers' boots splashing closer, closing the distance with the patience of men who knew the net was good. And underneath the splash and shout, Casper heard something else—the captain's voice again, but changed now, *charged*, each word arriving with weight behind it, the way Casper's own words had weight when swordspeech woke:

"*By the authority of the crown beneath the mountain, I bind this passage.*"

The tunnel behind them lit blue-white. Wards, strung across the flooded dark like harp strings, humming. In thirty seconds they would reach the junction. In sixty, the whole tunnel would be a cage.

And Tam, on Casper's shoulders, began to cry—not loudly, not hysterically, but with the terrible silent crying of a child who has learned that noise brings soldiers. His tears hit Casper's neck, hot as coals. His grip tightened. And his gift, leaking out of him in his terror, made the water around them shiver and steam.

*Help,* he had mouthed. *Help.*

Casper stood in the flooding dark, with a child on their shoulders and a hunted woman beside them and death walking up the tunnel with a drawn sword and a crown's warrant, and felt the old familiar pressure rise in their chest—the words, the *true* words, the ones that lived below language like bedrock below soil. Swordspeech. The gift they had spent a lifetime burying in a valley where magic could not follow. The gift that had killed men in a rye field. The gift that, used here, in front of a dozen witnesses and a hunt-captain who would carry the tale to the throne itself, would strip away the last thin disguise of anonymity and paint a target on everything Casper loved.

Vess saw it happen. She had spent weeks teaching Casper to hide, and she knew every sign of un-hiding. "*No,*" she whispered. "Casper. *No.* Not here. Not for—one boy—"

"For one boy," Casper said. "Yes."

They lifted Tam from their shoulders and set him on their hip, one arm locked around him, and turned to face the light.

"Seren," Casper called down the tunnel, and their voice carried impossibly, filling the brick throat of the passage like the passage itself had become a horn. "You wanted to know if the order still lives. Watch."

The first ward-string reached the junction and sang.

Casper spoke.

Not shouted—*spoke*, and the word was **SEVER**, and it left their mouth as force, as a blade of pure command that struck the nearest ward-string and cut it like a scythe through wheat. The string snapped with a sound like a dying star, and the blue-white light guttered and died for twenty feet of tunnel. Behind the severed line, the soldiers shouted. The captain's voice rose, cold and fast, weaving new bindings—

"*Bind the water. Bind the air. Bind his tongue—*"

**NO.**

One word. Casper put everything into it—all the grief of the stolen memories, all the fury of the ledger with Lior's name in it, all the terrible tenderness of a child crying silently on their hip—and the word went down the tunnel like a landslide. Torches guttered out. Men staggered, clutching their ears. The captain alone stayed upright, and even he took a step backward, boots splashing, and in the sudden dark Casper heard him breathe one word that was almost reverence:

"*There* it is."

"Take Tam," Casper said to Vess, and their voice was ordinary again, rough and human. "Left branch. Swim. I'm right behind you."

"You're lying."

"I'm right behind you," Casper said again, and this time it was true, because they intended it to be, and intention was the whole of swordspeech. "Go."

She went—she grabbed the boy and plunged into the left branch, and Tam looked back over her shoulder with huge eyes, and Casper turned to hold the dark.

What followed was not a duel. Duels have rules. This was a butcher's work of necessity: Casper standing hip-deep in floodwater in a collapsing tunnel, speaking ruin. **BREAK** brought down a section of ceiling behind them, sealing the passage with rubble. **BLIND** stripped the torches to embers. When soldiers waded forward anyway, Casper spoke **TURN** and the water itself rose up and shoved them back, gasping, and Casper felt each word cost them something vital—felt the gift drinking from them like a leech, their vision graying at the edges, their heart stuttering—but they kept speaking, because behind that rubble wall a child was swimming for his life, and the words were the only wall between him and the crown.

The last thing Casper did, before turning to follow, was speak one final word down the ruined tunnel, aimed at the captain alone, soft and clear:

**REMEMBER.**

Let him remember. Let him carry it to the throne. Let the whole rotten court beneath the mountain know that the last oath-bound speaker had chosen a stranger's child over the restoration rolls—and that he would do it again, and again, until the throne itself came down.

* * *

The river took them like a mother takes a child—roughly, and all the way in.

The left branch surfaced through a culvert in the embankment wall, and they came out of it half-drowned, Vess dragging Tam by the collar, Casper crawling on hands and knees with their vision full of gray moths. Greyhollow burned behind them, painting the clouds the color of an open wound. On the water, fishing boats were putting out, and the fishermen were looking at the fire, not at three dripping figures on the mudflats.

Tam threw up river water, then started to laugh—a shocked, hiccupping laugh, the laugh of the delivered. Vess gathered him against her side and held him there, and over his head she looked at Casper with an expression Casper had never seen on her before: awe and grief in equal measure, love and mourning braided together.

"Do you understand what you did?" she said quietly. "In that tunnel. There were twelve witnesses, Casper. Twelve soldiers and the captain of the royal hunt. By tomorrow, every garrison from here to the mountain will have your description and your name. You're not a rumor anymore. You're the *proclamation's face*." She swallowed. "You became exactly what they hunt."

"I know."

"And you did it anyway."

"I did it anyway."

Tam tugged Vess's sleeve. "Mister," he said to Casper—then corrected himself, having clearly spent days puzzling over it, "Miss. Sir. *Person*." Casper had given up explaining; the valley-raised spoke themselves in ways the lowlands never learned to parse. "Was that magic? Real magic? Like in the stories?"

Casper opened their mouth to say *never use it, never show it, hide it, bury it*—every lesson of a lifetime, every law of silence.

What came out instead was: "It was a promise. Someone needed keeping."

Tam considered this with the gravity of the young. "My mam says promises are heavier than rocks."

"Your mam is right."

Behind them, Greyhollow burned. Ahead, upriver, the mountain waited—a jagged black tooth against the smoke-stained sky, crowned with a light that did not flicker, that never flickered, that fed and fed and was never full. Casper looked at it and felt the gray moths still dancing at the edge of their vision, felt the gift's toll in their bones, and understood with perfect clarity that there was no road left but the one that led up.

Vess followed their gaze. "They'll expect us to run now," she said. "Every road south will be watched. Every port. Every gate." A pause. "Which means the only place they *won't* expect—"

"—is up the mountain," Casper finished.

The boy shivered between them. The fire roared behind them. And the light on the peak burned on, patient and insatiable, waiting for them to come home.
