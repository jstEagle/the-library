## 13. Beneath the Mountain

The broker's map was drawn on skin.

Not paper made to look like skin, not vellum scraped thin enough to be mistaken for it—actual skin, cured and inked, soft as a moth's wing when Vess unrolled it across her knees in the mouth of the mine-shaft. She tried not to think about whose. The broker had smiled when he handed it over, that grey-toothed Greyhollow smile, and said, *It keeps better than parchment. And it remembers the way home.* She had paid a piece of herself for it. She could still feel the absence, a cold draft blowing through a room in her chest she hadn't known was there until it emptied.

"Tell me again why we're trusting a man who sells people," Casper said.

Vess traced the inked line of the shaft with one finger. "Because a man who sells everything has no reason to lie about where things are. Lies are bad for business." She looked up. The last grey light of evening stood behind Casper like a door left open, and beyond it the mountain rose against the sky, blacker than the sky, its summit lost in cloud. Somewhere inside all that stone was a throne fed on voices. Somewhere inside all that stone was her sister's light, scattered or stored or burned—she didn't let herself finish the thought. "Besides," she said, "we're not trusting him. We're trusting what he sold us. There's a difference."

"There isn't."

"No," Vess admitted. "There isn't. Come on."

The shaft mouth yawned at the base of a slag heap two miles from the city's eastern wall, hidden behind a curtain of dead bramble that had grown through the timbers of an old support frame. The wood was rotten as bread. Vess went first because Casper would have insisted otherwise and she was tired of arguing about who got to die first. The dark took her the way water takes a dropped stone—all at once, completely, without ceremony.

She lit nothing. That was the first rule the map had taught her, scrawled in the margin in the broker's neat merchant's hand: *Light travels down. What lives below is older than light.*

So they descended blind, hands on the rail, boots finding the rotted ties of an ore-cart track that hadn't carried ore in two hundred years. The air changed within the first hundred paces—thickened, sweetened, took on a taste like copper coins held too long in the mouth. Vess had felt ward-sickness before; this was its cousin, its wealthy uncle. Magic down here wasn't absent. It was *hoarded*.

"Casper," she whispered, though whispering was pointless—the shaft swallowed sound whole. "Can you feel it?"

A pause behind her. Then, low: "It's like standing next to a furnace with the door shut."

That was exactly it. Vess had spent her whole gifted life feeling magic the way other people felt weather—in patches, in seasons, leaking through the world's poor seams. But here the seams had been cut wide and everything bled inward, down, toward the heart of the mountain. Every step they took, the pressure built. By the time the first gallery opened around them, wide enough that their footsteps stopped echoing, Vess's teeth ached and the fine hairs on her arms had risen like the fur of a frightened animal.

"We should rest," Casper said.

"We should keep moving."

"You haven't eaten since Greyhollow."

"Neither have you." But she stopped, because her knees had stopped being negotiable, and sat on the cold rail with her back against stone that breathed warmth. Not geothermal warmth. She knew geothermal warmth from the springs near her childhood village, and it smelled of sulfur and indifference. This warmth smelled of—she searched for it and hated what she found—of candles. Of a sickroom. Of the little clay lamp her mother had kept burning beside her sister's bed in the last week of the fever, when everyone pretended the burning was for the saint and everyone knew it was a bargain: *stay, stay, stay.*

"Vess." Casper's voice came out of the dark, careful. "What did the broker show you? All of it. You told me the throne runs on souls. You didn't tell me how you looked when you heard it."

She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "How did I look?"

"Like someone reading their own name in a ledger."

Ah. So Casper had seen. Of course Casper had seen; Casper missed nothing, watched everything with those oath-scarred eyes, catalogued the world the way other people breathed. It was one of the things Vess had decided not to fall in love with, somewhere on the road, and had failed at deciding thoroughly.

"The ledger," Vess said, "had columns. Purchases. Deliveries. Yield per vessel." Her voice stayed flat, which was the only way she could carry it. "My sister's village is listed under a date four years ago. Forty-one vessels. Average yield, high. The broker said the mountain prefers them young and—" she stopped. Started again. "There was a column for *residuals*. Souls that keep some awareness after processing. They're marked with a symbol. A little crown."

The silence between them stretched long enough to grow things.

"And yours?" Casper asked quietly. "Is your sister—"

"I don't know. That's why we're going down instead of running east until the hunt forgets our faces." Vess stood. Her legs shook and she let them, briefly, in the dark where no one could see. "Come on. The map says the old ventilation galleries connect to the under-halls past the third collapse. We cross while the shift-change bells ring above. The court's servants go deaf during changeover—they're listening to something else."

"What are they listening to?"

"The throne," Vess said. "Prophecies. It murmurs to them. Didn't you wonder why a kingdom that hunts magic lets its capital sit on top of the largest hoard of it in the world? They're not guarding it, Casper. They're *attending* it. Like clergy."

* * *

They crossed the third collapse at what Vess guessed was midnight, though time below the mountain had gone strange, pooling and stagnating like water with nowhere to drain. The collapse itself was a wall of fallen rock choked with the bones of timber framing, and they went over it hand to hand, slow as climbers on a cliff face, the map rolled and tucked into Vess's shirt where it lay against her skin like a second heartbeat. Twice the rubble shifted under her and twice Casper's hand found her wrist in the absolute dark—unerring, immediate, as if Casper could see heat, or grief, or her.

On the far side, the character of the mine changed.

The ore-cart rails continued, but they were bright now, polished by use, and the walls had been faced with dressed stone cut in blocks so large and so seamless that Vess's fingers, trailing along them, found no joint at all. Whoever had built these halls had built them to last longer than kingdoms. Carvings emerged under her fingertips as they walked: spirals, chains of small linked figures, mouths. Rows upon rows of carved mouths, all open, all singing or screaming—it was impossible to tell which, and the not-knowing sat in Vess's stomach like a swallowed stone.

Then the light began.

It came first as a glow around a bend, pale gold shot through with green, flickering in a rhythm too organic to be torchlight. Vess pressed herself flat against the carved wall and eased forward until she could see around the corner, and then she forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

The gallery ahead was veined with light.

That was the only word for it. Veins—great branching rivers of luminescence—ran through the dressed stone the way veins run through marble, through leaves, through the backs of hands. They pulsed. Slowly, steadily, in a rhythm Vess recognized from somewhere deep and animal: the rhythm of a sleeping pulse, of breath drawn and released, drawn and released. And within the light—if she narrowed her eyes, if she let herself see rather than merely look—there were shapes. Threads. Countless fine bright threads, each one distinct, each one moving with its own small urgency, tangled together into the vast glowing web that filled the walls from floor to vaulted ceiling.

"Oh," Casper breathed behind her. "Oh, what is—"

"Souls." The word came out of Vess like a tooth pulled. "Harvested ones. Woven into the lattice." She had known. The broker had told her, the ledger had shown her, she had *paid* for the knowledge—and knowing was nothing, nothing, against seeing. Against watching a thread near the gallery's edge strain toward them, fluttering like a moth against glass, before the lattice's pull drew it gently, irresistibly, back into the weave.

"They can feel us," she whispered. "Casper. They know we're here."

Some of the threads had begun to gather along the vein nearest the corridor, pressing toward the surface of the stone, brightening. Not all of them. Many hung slack, dim, worn smooth as river stones—souls that had been woven so long they'd forgotten they were anything else. But others were new. Others still remembered having hands.

One thread glowed a color Vess knew. Not gold, not green—a fierce violet-white, the exact shade of the little lamp in the sickroom, the shade her mother had sworn meant *the saint is listening*. Vess drifted toward it without deciding to move. Her hand rose. Her fingertips touched the stone.

Cold. The wall was cold, and the light within it gave no heat at all, and the thread flared beneath her touch and pressed against the inside of the stone like a face pressed to a window, and Vess felt—not heard, *felt*, through her palm, through the piece of soul she'd sold away leaving her thin enough to feel such things—a cadence. A pattern of brightening and dimming. Two quick, three slow. Two quick, three slow.

Her sister had invented that pattern when they were children, tapping messages through the wall between their beds when their parents thought they slept. *I'm here. Are you there? I'm here.*

Vess did not cry. Something in her had gone past crying into a country with no weather at all. She kept her hand on the stone and tapped back, clumsy, through flesh and rock and woven light: *I'm here. I'm coming. Hold on.*

The thread flared so brightly that for an instant the whole gallery lit like dawn, and every slack, worn, forgotten soul in the walls stirred at once—and turned, Vess realized with a horror that arrived slowly and then all at once, *toward her*. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The veins pulsed faster. The carved mouths on the walls seemed suddenly less like decoration and more like vents. And from somewhere far below—far deeper than any gallery, down where the map's inked lines converged into a single black point the broker had labeled only with the crown symbol—came a sound.

Not a roar. Not a scream. A murmur. A great, layered, many-voiced murmur, rising through the stone like water rising through a well shaft, and threaded through it, unmistakable now that she had heard the throne's voice described, was the cadence of prophecy. The servants above were right to go deaf at changeover. No living mind was meant to hold this.

Beside her, Casper had gone rigid. In the light of the veins, Vess saw the oath-scar on Casper's forearm—for the first time since she'd known them—glowing. Faintly, reluctantly, like an ember refusing to die.

"It knows we're here," Casper said. Their voice was steady in the way that cost everything. "The lattice. It's waking the words in me. Vess—we need to move *now*."

Down the gallery, around the bend where the light ran thickest, a doorway stood open—grand, arched, framed in carved mouths—and through it Vess could hear voices. Real voices. Servants' voices, chanting the changeover, counting vessels, calling numbers into the dark.

And beneath the numbers, patient as stone, the throne murmured on—and in its murmur, threading through ten thousand stolen voices, Vess heard one cadence rise to meet Casper's glowing scar like a key finding its lock.

Two quick. Three slow.

*I'm here. Are you there?*

Lior had been listening for them all along.
