## 14. The Court of Kept Voices

The tribute-bearers went down into the mountain in single file, and the mountain swallowed them the way a throat swallows water.

Casper walked with their head bowed beneath the weight of a wicker frame loaded with winter apples—tribute from a valley village that had traded its harvest for its silence. The disguise had been Vess's idea: no one looks at tribute-bearers. No one looks at people who carry things for other people. They were furniture with legs, and so they passed through three gates, four checkpoints, and one hall where a hunt-captain stood close enough that Casper could smell the iron on his sword belt without ever lifting their eyes.

But it was not the guards that made Casper's hands shake inside their gloves.

It was the walls.

* * *

They had seen the veins first, hours ago, in the abandoned mine-shafts above: threads of pale light running through black stone like frost through a windowpane. Vess had touched one and flinched as if burned. "Souls," she'd whispered. "Loose ones. Stray light the court hasn't gathered yet." But here, deep in the under-halls, the light was not stray. It was *kept*.

The corridor they now descended was veined floor to ceiling, wall to wall, every crack and seam filled with imprisoned radiance. It did not flicker like firelight or glow like a lamp. It *breathed*. Slow pulses of silver-white moved along the stone in rhythms too irregular to be mechanical and too patient to be alive—or perhaps exactly patient enough to be alive, alive in the way that moss is alive, or a heart in a jar.

And the walls were not silent.

At first Casper thought it was wind, some draft moving through the shafts far above. Then they realized the sound was coming from everywhere at once—from the stone itself—a murmur, a hush, hundreds of voices speaking just below the threshold of words. Like standing outside a house where a great feast is happening, all the guests talking at once, none of them audible, all of them *there*.

"They're dreaming," Vess breathed beside him. She had dyed her hair dark for the disguise and pulled a laborer's cap low over her brow, but nothing could make her voice small enough to hide what it carried. "Or praying. I can't tell which."

"Neither," said a voice ahead of them.

It was the broker's guide—the man the broker had sold them to for a price Casper still didn't want to think about, a wiry under-hall servant named Tam with a face like wet paper and eyes that never quite focused on anything in the present. He walked at the head of their file of six, carrying the seal-staff that let them pass.

"The lattice doesn't dream," Tam said, not turning around. "Dreaming is for things that have somewhere else to be. The kept voices speak. That's their whole existence now. They speak, and the crown listens, and what they say comes true more often than chance would allow." He paused at a junction where two corridors met, letting a procession of robed acolytes pass, their faces hidden behind gauze veils. "You'll hear them soon enough. Everyone does. First time down, most tribute-bearers weep. The second time, they don't hear anything at all." He glanced back, and his unfocused eyes found Casper with unsettling precision. "Try to be someone who weeps."

* * *

The Hall of Audience opened before them like the inside of a bell.

Casper had imagined thrones before—every child in the valley had, in the way children imagine the dark places their parents warn them from—but nothing had prepared them for this. The chamber was vast and round, carved not built, its ceiling lost in shadow far overhead. And rising from the center of the floor, climbing toward that darkness, was the throne itself.

It was not a chair. It was a *lattice*—a structure of interwoven light, thousands upon thousands of strands of soul-fire braided together into something like a tree, something like a web, something like a cage that had grown so large it had become architecture. The strands pulsed and shifted, and within each strand Casper could see—could not help seeing—shapes. Faces, sometimes. Hands. A curve of shoulder. A child running. Fragments of lives suspended in the weave, each one dimmer than a candle flame but burning, burning, refusing to go out.

And the murmur that had haunted the corridors was here a chorus.

The voices rose and fell in waves, overlapping, interrupting, weaving. Casper caught fragments as they crossed the threshold with the other bearers, heads bowed, setting their tribute frames along the wall among dozens of others:

*—the river will rise in the third month, tell them, tell them to move the granaries—*

*—she lies, the eastern governor lies, mark his name, mark it—*

*—cold, it is cold in the small hours, does anyone remember sun, does anyone—*

*—a swordspeaker comes, a swordspeaker comes, the oath walks again—*

Casper's blood turned to ice water. They kept their eyes on the floor. Kept their breathing even. *A swordspeaker comes.* Did the lattice see them? Could it? Or was it prophecy, scattered and blind, throwing words in every direction the way a broken clock strikes random hours?

Beside them, Vess had gone rigid. Casper could feel her trembling through the space between their shoulders. Her sister might be in there. In any one of those ten thousand strands. Might have been in there for years, murmuring harvest predictions to a crown that ate her words and gave nothing back.

"Do not look up," Tam murmured, passing behind them. "Especially you two. The lattice notices attention the way a spider notices vibration. Look at your feet. Grieve quietly."

So Casper looked at their feet, and grieved quietly, and the chorus washed over them like a tide that would never recede.

* * *

They were arranging the tribute when Casper heard it.

Not a word. Not yet. A cadence.

The lattice spoke in a thousand voices, and most of them blurred together the way rain blurs against a roof—but one thread of sound cut through the wash the way a single struck string cuts through an orchestra. It was nothing Casper could have explained. It was the rhythm of a particular person's speech, the little hesitation before a joke lands, the upward lilt at the end of a question that isn't really a question, the way certain vowels softened at the end of long words because the speaker had learned language from a mother who came from the coast—

Casper knew that cadence the way they knew their own heartbeat. Knew it somewhere deeper than memory, in the place the oath had stripped bare and left only the name: *Lior.*

Their head came up before they could stop it.

The strand hung high in the lattice, third tier from the top, brighter than those around it—not blazing, but steady, the way a lamp burns in a window when someone is home and waiting. And though Casper could not make out individual words at this distance, the voice within it was speaking, always speaking, part of the endless murmur—and the cadence wrapped around Casper like arms.

*Lior.*

The name rose in them with such force that it was physical, a fist closing around their lungs. Every erased memory pressed against the inside of their skull at once—not returning, never returning, but *insisting*, ghosts of ghosts, the shape of a absence so complete it had its own gravity. They had loved this person. They had loved this person so much that when the memories were taken, what remained was a wound in the exact shape of a laugh. And here was the laugh's owner, woven into a throne, feeding prophecies to the thing that owned them, and Casper was standing twenty paces away holding a basket of apples.

"Casper." Vess's whisper was barely breath. "Your hands."

Casper looked down. Their fists had clenched so hard the apple in their grip had burst, juice running between their fingers, dripping onto the polished floor in bright red drops. One drop. Two. A small red constellation forming at their feet in the most watched room beneath the mountain.

An acolyte turned. Beneath the gauze veil, a face like everyone's face, and eyes that had the flat patience of furniture.

"You've crushed your offering," the acolyte said. Its voice was pleasant. Its voice was terrible. "That's a tithe wasted. The crown dislikes waste."

"I'm sorry," Casper heard themselves say. Their voice sounded very far away. "I'll kneel and gather it."

"Don't—" Vess started.

But Casper was already kneeling, head bowed, gathering bruised apple halves into their cupped palms—and from this angle, kneeling, head turned as if studying the mess of their own failure, Casper could see the base of the lattice. Could see that the lowest strands ran down into channels cut in the floor, rivers of light flowing inward toward a single point behind the throne: a door. Tall, narrow, banded in pale metal, glowing faintly at its seams.

And beside the door, flanking it, stood two figures in armor Casper recognized from a duel on a forest road. Hunt-captain's grey. The order of speakers, serving willingly.

One of them turned its head—just slightly—as if catching a scent on the air.

*The lattice speaks,* Casper thought, pressing the ruined fruit against their chest like a penitent. *A swordspeaker comes.* It hadn't been prophecy. It had been inventory.

"Enough," the acolyte said. "Stand. You'll wait with the others. The crown receives tribute-bearers at the changing of the watch, and the watch changes soon."

*The crown receives tribute-bearers.* Casper stood, juice still sticky on their palms, heart hammering a rhythm that felt loud enough to echo off the vaulted dark. Beside them, Vess had gone pale as bone, and her hand found Casper's sleeve and gripped it—not for comfort. To hold them still. To hold them *here*.

Above them, the lattice murmured on, ten thousand kept voices speaking their endless, harvested truth. And one strand, high in the third tier, bright as a window at dusk, spoke in a cadence Casper would have known in death, had known through death, had carried nameless through years of comfortable numbness like a coal carried in cupped hands across a frozen country.

The strand flickered—just once. Just slightly. As if, somewhere inside the weave, something had noticed being noticed.

The tall door behind the throne began, silently, to open.
