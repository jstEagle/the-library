## 15. Audience with the Crown

They came for us at the hour the under-halls called morning, though no sun had ever reached this deep.

I knew it first as a change in the light of our cell—the veined glow in the walls dimming and brightening in a slow rhythm, like breathing, like something vast turning over in its sleep. Then boots. Many boots. The sound came down the corridor with a precision I had only ever heard from soldiers on parade ground, each footfall landing exactly where the last one had, as if the stone itself were keeping time.

Vess was awake before I was. She had been awake for hours, I think, sitting cross-legged against the wall with her hands flat on the floor, reading the mountain through her palms the way she read everything—by touch, by tremor, by the small honest language of things that could not lie.

"They're coming," she said, before I could speak.

"I know."

"Not for you." Her eyes were dark in the soul-light, and there was something in them I hadn't seen since Greyhollow burned: fear, but fear that had made its peace with itself. "For you. They want you walking in on your own feet, Casper. That matters to them. Remember that."

The door unsealed with a sound like a held breath released. Four guards in lacquered black, their faces hidden behind masks of pale ceramic shaped like sleeping faces—eyes closed, mouths soft, expressions of perfect artificial peace. Behind them stood a woman in grey with a chain of office and no weapon at all, which frightened me more than the guards did.

"Casper Eriksen," she said. My name in her mouth sounded like an item being read from an inventory. "The Crown will see you now."

"And my companion?"

"The Crown will see *you*."

Vess caught my sleeve as I rose. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested; it always had been. "Whatever they show you," she said quietly, "whatever they offer—it will be true. That's the cruelest thing about them. They never need to lie."

I put my hand over hers. "Then neither will I."

She let go. I walked out between the sleeping faces, and I did not look back, because I knew if I did, I would ask her to come anyway, and she would come, and we would both die in a corridor for nothing.

* * *

The way up into the court was long, and it was lit.

That was the thing I could not stop seeing, could not stop feeling, the whole climb: the light. It ran through the walls in branching veins like frost across a window, gold and white and occasionally—when I let my gaze linger too long—a color I had no name for, a color that felt like a voice heard through water. The broker's map had warned us. *The walls are veined with imprisoned light.* Knowing had not prepared me. Every step upward, the veins thickened, brightened, and the murmuring grew louder—not words, not quite, but the shape of words, hundreds of voices speaking just beneath the threshold of sense, a tide of almost-speech that pressed against my skull like a hand.

And beneath it, threaded through all of it, one cadence I knew.

I had been hearing it since we entered the under-halls, faint as a memory of a memory. Here, climbing toward the throne, it strengthened until my chest ached with it. Lior used to hum when thinking—half a tune, never the same tune twice, a habit so ordinary I had forgotten I remembered it until I heard its ghost running through the walls of a mountain built on stolen souls.

The guide-woman glanced at me once. "You hear someone," she said. Not a question.

"I hear everyone."

"Yes." Something moved behind her professional blankness—pity, or its fossil. "Most tribute-bearers don't. Most speakers do. That is why the Crown wished to see you specifically, Casper Eriksen. Last of the oath-bound. The order that fed us once, and might feed us again."

We came up through a final archway into the Court of Kept Voices, and I stopped understanding where I ended and the room began.

It was a cavern the size of a cathedral turned inside out. The ceiling soared into darkness stitched with veins of light so dense they formed constellations—souls arranged in patterns, in *sentences*, I realized, whole paragraphs of the dead glowing overhead. And at the center, raised on steps of black stone veined silver, stood the throne.

I had imagined a chair. A seat of bone or iron, something a body could occupy.

It was a lattice. A cage of interlocking light, thousands of strands woven into the rough suggestion of a seated figure, and within it the souls turned slowly like motes in a sunbeam, and every strand pulsed in time with a heartbeat that was not any single heart's. At its crown—literally, at the top of the lattice, where a head would be—burned a knot of brilliance so dense it hurt to look at directly.

The sovereign sat before it. Not in it. Before it, on a plain chair of dark wood, dressed plainly, a small person with tired eyes and ink-stained fingers, like a clerk who had wandered into a temple and never left.

"Sit," the sovereign said, gesturing to a second chair opposite. Ordinary. Wooden. "Please. Ceremony is for people who need it."

I sat. My legs were less steady than I wanted them to be.

"You're smaller than the stories," I said.

A dry smile. "So is the truth. Shall we trade in it? You've come a long way to steal from me, swordspeaker. I'd rather we be honest about that at least."

"I've come a long way to take back what was stolen first."

"Ah." The sovereign folded their ink-stained hands. "Lior."

The name went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor. I kept my face still. Barely.

"You know the name," I said.

"I know everything the throne knows, and the throne knows what it holds." The sovereign leaned back. Above us, the constellation-souls murmured on, and beneath their murmur that familiar half-tune wove and wove. "Do you know what you're hearing, right now? What's in these walls? Go on. Say it aloud. I find people lie less when they have to give their lies a body."

"Souls," I said. "Harvested. From the gifted your hunt drags off the roads. From tributes. From—" My throat tightened. "From bargains."

"Bargains," the sovereign agreed softly. "Yes. The valley knows about bargains, doesn't it? Peace for a soul. One soul, freely given—or given by someone with the right to give it—and the wards stay strong, the magic stays out, the valley sleeps safe inside its forgetting." Their tired eyes fixed on mine. "You swore an oath once, Casper Eriksen. Do you remember what it bought?"

"No," I said, and the word cracked down the middle. "That's rather the point of oaths, isn't it? They take the payment out of your pocket before you notice it's gone."

Something flickered across the sovereign's face—too quick to name, gone before I could. Regret, perhaps. Or recognition. "The old order understood the arithmetic," they said. "Power has a price. Protection has a price. Peace has a price. The only question worth asking is *who pays*. The old speakers answered: we pay. We choose it. We bind ourselves, we spend our memories, our futures, our loves—and the valleys sleep, and the roads are safe, and no one else has to burn." They spread their hands toward the blazing lattice above. "I am the same arithmetic, done at scale. The throne must be fed or the wards of a hundred settlements fail. The hunt gathers what the feeding requires. And the keystone—" their voice gentled, which was somehow worse than cruelty— "the keystone has held for twenty years without faltering. Do you know why?"

"Because souls endure," I said. "You told your brokers to say that. Vess heard it too."

"Because yours was a true bargain," the sovereign said. "Souls given in coercion fray. Souls given in love hold forever." They let that sit in the air between us, patient as stone. "Someone loved Lior very much, swordspeaker. Someone gave them up believing it would save everything they cared about. The throne runs on that belief. On that love. It is the strongest fuel there is."

The chamber tilted. Or I did. Twenty years of comfortable numbness, of a name with no face attached, of grief I couldn't source—all of it tore open at once, and I saw the erased night standing behind my own eyes like a figure at a doorway: myself, younger, kneeling, speaking words I had paid to forget.

*I did this,* I thought. *I built this throne's heart with my own mouth.*

"That's the look," the sovereign said quietly. "I've seen it before, in the last of the old order who came here still remembering. Grief is honest, at least. Now." They rose, and the lattice above them brightened, the murmuring swelled, and the sovereign's voice took on a weight it hadn't carried before—the weight of a crown, even a crown that was only a knot of stolen light. "Let me make you an offer, Casper Eriksen. The order of speakers died scattered and hunted, but the need for it never died. Wards still need feeding. Borders still need holding. I am restoring the order—rebuilt, honored, sworn anew. Speakers at the right hand of the crown. Purpose. Power. Belonging. Everything the oath took from you, returned with interest."

"And the price."

"You already know the price." The sovereign's eyes did not move from mine. "The fugitive. Vess. Gifted blood, unregistered, unsworn—a loose thread in the design. Give her to the harvest, take the oath, and take your place. That is the whole of it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you leave the way tribute-bearers who refuse always leave, and she leaves with you, and the hunt resumes at dawn, and eventually the arithmetic collects you both. I would prefer otherwise. I am tired of collecting." For a moment the clerk showed through the crown again—exhausted, human, appalled by its own ledger. "Consider it honestly, swordspeaker. One life, freely offered, against a restored order that could protect thousands. You've made this trade before. You know how it's done."

*You've made this trade before.*

The words went in like a hook and caught on something deep.

And then the throne spoke to me.

Not the sovereign. The throne. The lattice flared, the murmuring stilled as if a great hand had hushed a crowd, and out of the knot of light at its crown a voice emerged—one voice, singular, unmistakable, humming half a tune that was never the same tune twice.

"*Casper.*"

The sound of my name in Lior's voice. After twenty years. After I had sold the memory of the mouth that shaped it.

I was on my feet without deciding to stand. The guards' spears came level; the sovereign lifted one finger and they froze. "Gently," the sovereign said. "Let them see."

The light descended. It came down out of the lattice in a slow spiral, gathering, condensing, taking a shape at the base of the dais—a shape of radiance roughly human, roughly the height, the stance, the *tilt of the head*, and though it had no face, no features, nothing a portrait could hold, I knew it the way you know your own heartbeat. The way you know home in the dark.

"Lior," I said. My voice came out ruined.

"*You came.*" The soul-voice was Lior's and more than Lior's—layered, resonant, a bell struck in a vast room—but underneath the resonance, underneath twenty years of burning as fuel for a crown, it was them. It was the voice that had argued with me about goat names. That had sung badly on purpose. "*Oh, you stubborn, faithful fool. You actually came.*"

"I didn't remember you." The confession tore free before I could dress it up. "The oath took it—I couldn't remember your face, I couldn't remember why I—Lior, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, I didn't know what I'd—"

"*Hush.*" Warmth moved through the light like laughter moving through water. "*I know. I've known for twenty years, watching you practice sword-forms at dawn, wondering if you'd ever hear me calling. I never blamed you. Not for one hour. Do you understand me? Never.*"

"You should."

"*Probably.*" The humor in it was so precisely theirs that my knees nearly went. "*But I don't. Listen to me, Casper. Listen carefully, because they'll let me say this much and no more.*" The light steadied, and beneath its warmth I felt—for the first time—strain, like a voice pushed to the edge of its endurance. "*Whatever they offer, refuse it. Whatever they show you, look past it. And whatever you decide to do—do it quickly. The keystone frays a little more every year. When it goes, it won't go gently.*"

"Lior—"

"*Don't swear anything new,*" the soul said, and now the warmth was fierce, urgent, blazing. "*Not to them. Not to anyone. You've paid enough. Do you hear me? You've paid enough.*"

The light began to rise, drawn back up toward the lattice like breath drawn back into lungs, and I lunged forward and the guards caught my arms and I didn't care, I pulled against them, shouting Lior's name into the rising glow, and the last thing the light did—before the lattice swallowed it, before the murmuring resumed and the throne's heartbeat closed over the silence—was turn. Tilt. The particular tilt of a head, fond and exasperated and unbearably familiar, saying without words what it had apparently spent twenty years waiting to say:

*I forgive you. Now get me out of here.*

* * *

They let me sit again after that. That was the cruelty of it—they simply waited, courteous as hosts, while I shook, while the guards released my arms and stepped back, while the sovereign poured two cups of something steaming from a clay pot and set one near my hand as if we were two farmers settling a boundary dispute.

"Well?" the sovereign said at last.

"You keep them aware." My voice was gravel. "You keep them *awake*. All of them. Burning in your walls, knowing, for—how long? How long has Lior been conscious in there?"

"Twenty years."

"Twenty years." I looked at my hands. They were steady. That seemed obscene. "And you offer me a place beside you."

"I offer you what the old order had," the sovereign said. "A terrible job, done for good reasons, by people willing to pay. Yes. I keep them aware. Awareness is what makes the fuel burn clean—that's not cruelty, swordspeaker, it's thermodynamics. I did not design the arithmetic. I inherited it. Every ruler of this throne inherits it, and every one of us has searched for another way, and every one of us has failed." They leaned forward, and the tired eyes were suddenly very sharp. "But you—you are the answer I've been looking for. A speaker of the old blood, oath-bound, proven. Restore the order with me. Feed the wards properly instead of harvesting strangers off the roads. End the hunt. Isn't that worth one fugitive? Isn't that worth more than one fugitive? Think of the hunts that will never happen. Think of the valleys that will never sell their children. You can't save everyone, Casper Eriksen. But you can save *most*."

The trap of it was beautiful. That was what made my blood run cold—not the threat, but the *reasonableness*. One life against thousands. I had done this math before; the sovereign was right about that. I had done it kneeling in the dark with Lior's hand in mine, and I had paid for the doing with the memory of doing it, and the interest on that payment had been accruing for twenty years in the walls of this mountain, humming half a tune.

Vess's voice, from a cell far below: *they never need to lie.*

Lior's voice, from the lattice above: *you've paid enough.*

Truth against truth. Loyalty against loyalty. The whole war of my life compressed into a single wooden chair in a room full of the dead.

I picked up the cup. I drank. The drink was hot and bitter and grounding, and I set it down, and I stood, and the guards tensed and the sovereign watched me with the patience of someone who believed the arithmetic always won in the end.

"You're right about one thing," I said. "I've made this trade before. I know exactly how it's done. I know what it costs, and who pays, and how long the bill takes to come due." I looked up at the lattice—at the knot of light at its crown, pulsing with a borrowed heartbeat, humming, always humming. "And I know what it sounds like, twenty years later, when the one you sold finds out it was you."

The sovereign's expression did not change. But above us, for half a heartbeat, the entire lattice flickered—every vein in every wall, thousands of souls flaring at once, as if the mountain itself had drawn a sharp breath.

"So here is my counter-offer," I said, and reached for the words rising in my throat like blades leaving a sheath. "Give me back Lior. Freely. Tonight."

The sovereign smiled, sad and certain, and rose from their plain chair, and behind them the throne blazed like a captive sun.

"No," they said. "I don't think I will."

Above the dais, the lattice began to sing.
