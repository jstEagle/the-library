## 16. The Refusal

I had learned to read rooms by the way their shadows behaved. In Greyhollow's back alleys the shadows pooled like spilled ink, quick to hide a body. In my sister's old bedroom they had stretched long and gentle across the floorboards, patient as a mother waiting up. But here, in the court beneath the mountain, the shadows did not behave at all. They leaned. They gathered behind the pillars like courtiers hungry for favor, and every one of them bent toward the throne.

Casper stood before it with their back straight and their hands loose at their sides—the posture of someone who had decided to die standing, or worse, to live saying something unforgivable.

The sovereign sat above us on a seat that was not entirely a chair. It was grown, not built—black stone braided through with veins of pale light, the same imprisoned glow I'd seen threading the walls of the under-halls for miles. Souls. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one a small sun caged in mineral, and all of them feeding downward, funneling into the lattice beneath the throne where one light burned brighter than the rest.

Lior.

I could feel them from where I stood, the way you feel a hearth through a wall in winter. Not heat exactly. *Attention.* A soul that had been aware of its own imprisonment for years, and aware now, sharply, of the two small figures standing in its light.

The sovereign's voice came down from the dais like cold water poured over stone.

"You are the last of the order. Do you understand what is being offered? The hunt ends. Your name is struck from every ledger. You will be given quarters in the upper courts, students, a title older than any kingdom on the surface. All we ask—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat, "—is the girl."

*The girl.* As if I were luggage. As if I hadn't already paid half of myself to be standing in this room. What remained of my soul was a stub of candle, guttering, and I felt it gutter harder now, because I knew what Casper would say. I had known since the wayshrine, since the confession, since the pact we made with shaking hands and no absolution: *truth between us, always.*

Truth between us meant Casper would refuse. Truth between us meant everything after that refusal would have to happen very fast.

"The offer is generous," Casper said.

My heart stopped. Actually stopped—I felt it stumble in my chest like a runner catching a root.

Then Casper turned, slowly, deliberately, so that they faced me instead of the throne, and I saw what they were doing. They weren't addressing the sovereign. They were making sure I heard every word.

"But you've made an error about what I am." Their voice carried in that strange way swordspeech carries—not loud, but *present*, as if the air itself leaned in to listen. "You think I'm the last oath-bound speaker. I'm not. I'm the first person in this valley's history to break one. And I broke it for love, not for a crown. So no. I won't surrender Vess. I won't surrender anyone to you ever again. I did that once already." A breath. "Her name was Lior. You're sitting on her."

The silence that followed was the loudest thing I had ever heard.

Every vein of light in the chamber flickered at once—a shudder running through the walls like the mountain itself had flinched. And beneath the throne, in the lattice, the brightest flame *flared*. I saw it. I felt it. Something in that caged light recognized its own name spoken aloud for the first time in years, and it pressed against its prison hard enough that hairline cracks of radiance spidered out across the black stone.

"Take them," the sovereign said, without inflection, the way you'd order a spill cleaned.

*

What happened next happened fast, which was the only mercy in it.

Guards came from alcoves I hadn't known were alcoves—six of them, then ten, armored in that dull grey metal that drank light rather than reflecting it. And among them moved a figure I knew by gait alone: the hunt-captain, the other speaker, the one Casper had beaten on the road and left alive. His lips were already moving. Words with edges. I felt the pressure of his speech build against my eardrums like a change in altitude.

"Casper!" I shouted. "Left pillar—*now!*"

They moved. I'll give them that; all those years of sword-forms in a valley where power slept had carved reflexes deeper than thought. They dove behind the pillar as the captain's word detonated against it, and the stone didn't crack—it *forgot itself*, crumbling into sand that hissed across the floor.

But I wasn't watching the fight. I was watching the lattice.

Here is what the broker's price had bought me, the piece of my soul I'd cut away and handed over like a coin: knowledge. The ward-lattice beneath the throne wasn't just a cage. It was a *load-bearing wall*. Every ward in the mountain—the ones hiding the court, the ones blinding the surface kingdoms, the ones that had once reached all the way to our valley and sealed it shut—drew their strength from the kept souls below. Break the lattice and you didn't free the souls. You *unmade the architecture of everything.*

Which was, of course, the plan. It had always been the plan. Vess the fugitive, Vess the liar, Vess who had sold her own sister's location to save her skin once and hated herself with a purity most people reserve for gods—all of her had walked into this mountain carrying one idea, and the idea was simple:

If the crown ran on stolen light, then steal the light back.

There was just one problem, and the problem was arithmetic. To shatter a lattice woven from thousands of souls, you needed force of a similar order. Casper's swordspeech was strong, but it was a blade, and blades don't break walls of this kind—they only notch them.

A soul-fragment, though. A soul-fragment offered freely, spent all at once, *given* rather than taken—that was different. That was the one currency this place understood, because it was the one currency this place had never been paid honestly.

I looked at what remained of myself. The stub of candle. My sister's laugh lived in it somewhere, and the smell of rain on the road, and the memory of choosing to come back for Casper instead of running. Everything I had left that was worth keeping.

Reckless hope, my mother used to call it, back when she was alive to disapprove of things. She'd said it like a diagnosis.

I said it like a prayer.

*

The fight was going badly. Of course it was. Casper fought beautifully—words like thrown knives, each syllable leaving a wake of shredded air—but there were too many guards and the captain circled them like a wolf that had learned patience, waiting for the moment their speech ran dry. Speech cost breath. Breath cost time. I could see the arithmetic failing in the set of Casper's shoulders.

So I did the thing I do best. I lied my way into position.

I raised my hands. "Wait—wait! I'll take the offer!" My voice cracked perfectly, cracked the way real desperation cracks, because none of it was performance except the direction. "Don't hurt them. I'll go willingly. Just—just let me say goodbye, let me—"

The guards hesitated. Hierarchy hesitated. Even thrones hesitate when prey walks toward them voluntarily; it offends the story they tell themselves.

I walked toward the throne. Toward the lattice. Toward the brightest flame in the cage, which was pressing outward still, thin cracks of light bleeding from it like wounds, and as I got close I swear I felt Lior notice me—felt that attention swing around and fix on me with something that was almost a voice, almost words, almost *please*.

"I'm sorry," I whispered. "This is going to hurt you a little more before it hurts you never again."

And I put both hands against the black stone, over the brightest crack, and I opened what was left of me like a door.

*

People think spending a soul-fragment feels like dying. It doesn't. Dying takes something from you whether you consent or not. This was the opposite. This was *consent*, total and immediate, every remaining piece of me pouring out through my palms into the stone—

—and the stone, which had only ever known theft, did not know what to do with a gift.

The lattice shrieked. There is no other word. A sound like a thousand violins snapping their strings at once, like glass remembering it was once sand, like every caged voice trying to speak through a single mouth. Light poured into the fault lines I'd fed it—not my light anymore, mine was gone, I could feel the absence already, a cold clean hollow where my center used to be—but the light of everyone the lattice had ever swallowed, suddenly given permission, suddenly *reminded* that they had once been free.

The cracks spread.

The captain stopped mid-word. Even the sovereign rose from the grown throne, and I saw its face for the first time without the shadow-curtain, and what I saw there was fear—old fear, practiced fear, the fear of a thing that has spent centuries telling itself it is necessary.

"No," the sovereign said.

"Yes," I said, with the last breath of a voice that was rapidly becoming someone else's.

The lattice broke.

*

I want to describe it properly, because I was closer than anyone, and because I suspect I'm the only one who'll remember it whole.

It did not explode. Explosions are violent and blind. This was neither. The black stone came apart along seams of light like a loom coming undone thread by thread, and the souls came out the way birds come out of a barn when the doors are finally opened—first one, tentative, circling, then three, then a storm of them, a *weather* of released light filling the chamber from floor to unseen ceiling. They made sounds. That's what undid me. Not music, not screaming—something between, the sound of voices that had been silent for decades remembering how to carry a tune. Some of them shot upward through the rock as if they'd always known the way home. Some of them circled the room like swallows before a storm. One of them, small and bright and quick, brushed past my cheek as it passed, and though I had no soul left to recognize it with, some animal part of me knew anyway.

*Sister,* I thought. Or maybe I just wanted to. Maybe hope, reckless hope, will claim anything.

The guards fell—not dead, but *empty*, their harvested purpose dying with the throne that fed it. Armor clattered in the sudden quiet like hail on a roof. The hunt-captain stood frozen, his mouth still shaped around a word that no longer had anywhere to land, and I watched something leave his eyes that might have been faith.

And the sovereign—the sovereign was screaming. Not in rage. In *withdrawal*. I understood then, in the cold hollow where my self used to live, what the crown actually was: not a ruler but an addict, a thing so long fed on kept souls that without them it was nothing but hunger wearing regalia. It clawed at the broken lattice. It grabbed at threads of escaping light and they slipped through its fingers like water, like minnows, like everything it had ever stolen deciding, all at once, to leave.

Above us, deep in the bones of the mountain, something enormous shifted its weight.

Dust sifted down through the bird-storm of light. A crack ran up the far wall, slow as lightning seen through amber.

"CASPER!" My voice came out ragged. "The keystone! Go—*go now!*"

They were already moving. Of course they were. Through the falling dust and the wheeling lights, sword drawn, face lit from below by a thousand freed souls, Casper Eriksen ran toward the brightest flame in the ruin of the throne—the one light that hadn't fled yet, the one that was waiting, cracks webbed across its prison but heart still beating, still *aware*, still theirs.

The mountain groaned again, louder, a sound like the world clearing its throat to end.

I stood in the wreckage with empty hands and a hollow chest and I have never in my life felt so much like I was flying.

* * *

The ceiling of the great chamber split while Casper was still twenty paces from the lattice-heart. Stone the size of houses came down in slow, terrible silence, and the freed souls scattered before it like sparrows before a hawk—and through the widening wound in the mountain's skull I could see, impossibly far above, a coin of grey daylight.

An exit. An actual exit, if anything in this collapsing dark could hold long enough to matter.

"Hold it open!" I screamed at the light around me—at the souls, at the birds, at anyone listening. "Whoever you are—whatever you were—we need the sky to stay!"

And the light, God help me, the light *listened*.
