## 17. Lior

The lattice opened before Casper like a wound in the world.

They had come through corridors of veined light, past halls where harvested souls pressed against the stone like moths against glass, and now the passages narrowed to a single throat of black rock that breathed—slowly, terribly—with a rhythm not its own. The air here was warm. That was the first wrongness Casper noticed, after the cold of the deep shafts and the chill of Vess's hand gripping theirs. Warmth, in the roots of a mountain. Warmth like a body. Like a hearth someone had kept burning for years without ever being thanked.

*Someone has been keeping this place alive,* Casper thought. *One someone.*

The thought was a knife they could not stop turning.

Vess stopped at the mouth of the final chamber, her face gray in the spilled light. "I can't go further," she said, and her voice cracked on the words. Not from fear—from the pull. Casper could feel it too now, the way the chamber ahead tugged at something behind their ribs, gentle as a hand on a sleeve. But for Vess, who had already sold a piece of herself to the broker in Greyhollow, who had spent another fragment shattering the ward-lattice so the prisoned souls could spill like released birds through the under-halls—the pull must have been a hunger with teeth.

"Wait for me," Casper said.

"If you're not back before the mountain starts coming down—"

"Then you run."

"I won't." Vess's jaw set in the way it did when she had decided to be stubborn about a kindness. "But I'll be close. Go. He's waited long enough."

Casper stepped through.

* * *

The throne room—if it could be called that—was small. Smaller than any hall in Greyhollow, smaller than the village meetinghouse in the valley. A dome of living rock, its ceiling lost in darkness above, its floor a smooth basin of stone worn glassy by centuries of murmured prophecy. And at its center, rising from the basin like a tree grown from grief, stood the lattice.

It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Threads of light woven into a shape like a human spine, vertebra upon vertebra of pale gold, each strand pulsing with a rhythm Casper knew in their bones because they had once fallen asleep to it, head against a chest, listening to a heartbeat under a summer sky. The lattice breathed. The mountain breathed with it. Every soul in the walls of the under-halls, every servant in the court above whose eyes burned with borrowed purpose, every prophecy whispered into the sovereign's ear—all of it drew from this one woven light, this one kept voice.

"Lior," Casper said.

The name came out broken, half-swallowed, the way it always came out now—a word with no history attached, a key to a door whose house had been demolished. They knew the name. They knew nothing else. The oath had eaten everything: the sound of his laugh, the color of his eyes, whether he was tall or slight, whether he'd had a scar or a habit of humming when he worked. Eleven years of recitation, and all that remained was four letters and an ache shaped exactly like him.

The lattice flared.

Light poured along the golden threads, gathering, condensing, and where it gathered it took form—not flesh, never quite flesh, but a suggestion of it, a figure woven from radiance standing within the weave as though the weave were water and he had simply risen up through it. Tall or slight, Casper still couldn't tell. It didn't matter. It mattered more than anything in the world.

"Casper." The voice was Lior's. Of course it was. Some things even an oath couldn't digest. "You came."

"You know me." Casper's legs nearly failed. They caught themselves on the rim of the basin, stone cold beneath their palms while the light-warmth washed over them. "You—you remember me?"

"Souls don't forget." The figure tilted its head, and though it had no true face—only brightness shaped like one—Casper felt the smile reach them anyway, landing somewhere old and safe. "That's what they never understood. They think a soul is memory. It isn't. Memory is what the body keeps. The soul is what keeps *you*. I've been down here eleven years, and I have forgotten my own mother's face, the taste of bread, which hand I favored. But I never forgot you. You're not a memory, Casper. You're the shape I'm cut around."

Casper laughed, and it came out wet and awful. "You always talked too much."

"And you never talked enough. So we balanced." The light-figure drifted closer, threads trailing from it like roots, like chains dressed as ribbons. "Tell me why you came. Tell me truly. I can feel truth down here—it's the only food they let me keep."

And because Casper had promised Vess truth, always, and because lying to Lior would have been like lying to gravity, they told it. All of it. The oath they had sworn on the night they could no longer remember. The valley bought with his soul. The years of numbness, the sword-forms that hummed, the stranger bleeding at the wards, the road, the broker, the duel, the fever-dream that had shown them the erased night at last—*you gave him up yourself, believing it would save everyone, and then you paid with your memories so you wouldn't have to live knowing it.* They told him about the hunt-captain, about the burning streets of Greyhollow, about Vess in the corridor behind them with half a soul and all of her courage.

"I didn't come to save you," Casper finished, and their voice shook. "I came because I owed you. Because I—" The word *love* rose in them and stuck, enormous, insufficient. "Because there's a hole in me shaped like eleven years, and every path out of it leads to your name."

The lattice dimmed and brightened, dimmed and brightened, and Casper understood with a lurch that this was how he wept.

"They made you forget," Lior said softly, "so you could live. Do you know what I asked them, that night? When they took me? I asked them to take the memory of me from you too. I begged. I thought—I thought if you remembered, you'd follow me down, and the valley would lose its last speaker, and everything we traded for would be worth nothing." A pause; the light trembled. "You swore the oath to protect the valley. I begged them to make sure you could never unswear it. We betrayed each other out of love, Casper. Isn't that the cruelest magic there is?"

"Yes," Casper whispered. "I've been learning that on the road."

* * *

They sat together—or Casper sat, on the glassy rim of the basin, and Lior settled beside them in his woven light, near enough that warmth soaked through Casper's travel-ruined clothes like sun through a window. For a long time neither spoke. Above them, faint through miles of stone, the court went on murmuring. Somewhere far off, a rumble passed through the rock—Vess's shattered lattice still bleeding light through the under-halls, the mountain's foundations beginning to notice they were being unmade.

"Tell me what it's been like," Casper said finally. "Here. I want to know. Don't soften it."

Lior was quiet a moment. Then: "Do you know what they use me for? Prophecy. The crown hears the future through me—but that's a lie they tell themselves. I don't see the future. No soul does. What I do is *remember forward*. I hold every thread of every soul they've harvested, thousands of them, and when the sovereign asks what will happen, I read what people intend, what they fear, what they'll almost certainly do—and it passes for prophecy because people are so dreadfully predictable." His light flickered, bitter. "Eleven years of being a lie's engine. And worse—they talk to me. The servants, the stewards, sometimes the sovereign herself. They think I'm furniture that answers. They ask me questions like *will the harvest be sufficient* and *when will the gifted blood arrive*, and I answer, because refusing costs me pieces. Do you understand? Every time I resist, they shave a little more of me into the walls." He lifted a hand of woven gold toward the veined dark beyond the chamber. "Some of those lights in the stone are mine. I've met myself in the walls, Casper. Fragments. Little orphaned pieces of me that whisper and don't know whose voice they carry."

Casper's hands curled into fists on their knees. Fury rose in them like floodwater, and with it the gift stirred—the swordspeech pressing at the backs of their teeth, words wanting to become edges. They breathed it down. Not here. Not near him.

"What did you endure," Casper said, "that you could forgive me? Because I felt it, in the dream. In the fever. Forgiveness. Before I'd even confessed anything, I felt you forgiving me, and I couldn't bear it. I hadn't earned it."

"Oh, Casper." The light-figure turned fully toward them. "You think forgiveness is earned? That's an oath-speaker's arithmetic. Debts and payments and balances. I'm not an oath. I'm just a person, and I loved you, and love doesn't keep ledgers." He reached out, and where his woven fingers touched Casper's wrist, warmth sank in like water into drought-cracked earth. "You gave me up to save everyone you could. I asked to be given up to save you. We were both cowards and both brave and both wrong, and I forgave you the first year, when I realized that if our positions had been reversed I'd have done exactly the same thing, and hated myself for it, and sworn the same oath, and forgotten me on purpose so I could get up in the morning." A pause. "Forgiveness isn't a reward, my heart. It's just deciding the debt is dead so you can both live. I decided. Eleven years ago. I've just been waiting for you to climb down here so I could tell you."

Casper bowed their head, and the tears came without permission, hot and silent, and Lior's warmth stayed on their wrist like a hand that refused to let go.

* * *

It was Lior who broke the silence, and when he did, his voice had changed—lighter, but edged underneath, the way a blade is lighter than a hammer and cuts deeper.

"You've worked out how to free me. I can see it in you. You've been carrying the plan since you walked in, the way you used to carry bad news—right between the shoulders."

Casper didn't deny it. "The broker said it. The captain confirmed it before she died. You're the keystone. The whole throne draws through you—every harvested soul, every ward, every servant's borrowed purpose, the wards over the valley itself. Cut the keystone free and the crown gutters. The court dies. The valley opens." They looked up. "And the mountain comes down."

"Yes," Lior said simply.

"The shafts we came through are already unstable. Vess's work saw to that. If the lattice fails—if *you* fail—the whole mass settles. Thousands of tons of rock. Everyone left inside. Vess might outrun it. I might. But the servants—" Casper's jaw tightened. "Most of them are hollowed. Kept voices. They can't choose to flee. They'd die standing at their posts, still waiting for orders from a dead throne."

"I know."

"There are also the newly taken. Souls in vessels in the vaults above—people from the hunt, still fresh, still recoverable if the lattice dies gently instead of violently. If I sever you clean, the release might spare them. If the mountain crushes the vaults first—"

"Then some survive and some don't, and either way, you cannot know until you do it." Lior's voice gentled. "This is the choice, isn't it? This is why you came down here looking like a person walking to their own trial."

"The mountain could fall on the valley too." Casper forced the words out. "The wards are fed by you. When you go free, the wards die—and the valley sits at the foot of this range. If the collapse runs wrong—"

"It won't. I've held this rock for eleven years, Casper. I know its grain better than the masons ever did. Give me a warning before you cut, and I'll spend my last moments as keystone easing the western fault-lines closed. The mountain will groan and shed and settle, and it will not bury your valley. I promise you that. It's the one prophecy I'll ever give that's certain, because I will *make* it true." The light steadied, and for a moment Casper could see through the radiance to something underneath—an expression, tired and fierce and achingly familiar. "But you should know the other cost. The real one."

"Say it."

"When the keystone frees, the binding unravels backward along every thread tied to it. Your oath is tied to me, Casper. It was *sealed* with me—that was the price, that night. My freedom for your forgetting. Break my chains and the oath breaks with them, and everything the oath holds goes with it." He paused. "Your power. And whatever memories you have left."

Casper stared at him. "I don't have anything left. The oath ate it all. There's just the name."

"That's what frightens you." Lior's woven hand rose, hovered near Casper's cheek without quite touching. "You think the name is all you have of me. But listen to me, and believe me, because I am the one creature in this mountain who cannot lie: the oath doesn't hold your memories. It holds the *door* shut on them. They're still in you. Every one. Sealed, not eaten—sealing was my one condition, that night, and they honored it because a sealed soul remembers its seal. When the oath shatters, the door opens. Everything floods back. Eleven years. Our whole life. And it will break you open like a dam breaking, in the middle of a collapsing mountain, with no power left to steady you—because the gift goes with the oath, Casper. You'll walk out of here ordinary. Mortal. Memory-drunk and swordless, in the dark, while the rock comes down."

Silence filled the chamber, thick as water. Far above, the mountain rumbled again, longer this time, and a fine rain of dust sifted down through the dark.

"So that's the trade," Casper said at last, and heard their own voice go very quiet and very clear. "My power for your freedom. My certainty for my past. I break the oath, and I become someone who once loved you, instead of someone built to forget you."

"Yes."

"And if I refuse? If I leave you here and take Vess home and let the throne stand?" The question tasted like ash. "Tell me you'd understand that too. Tell me forgiveness covers that."

Lior was silent for a long moment. Then he laughed—soft, startled, real, the first true laugh Casper had heard in eleven years even though they couldn't remember any others—and the lattice blazed with it, and every soul in the walls of the great dark must have felt it pass through them like spring wind.

"No," he said. "That one I wouldn't forgive. Freeing me is the one thing I *won't* absolve you for skipping." His light leaned close. "You didn't climb all this way to ask my permission, Casper Eriksen. You climbed down here to say goodbye to the version of yourself who could still hesitate. So say it, and then take my hand, and let's end this."

Casper looked at him—at the woven light wearing the shape of the person they had sold and mourned and forgotten and crossed a kingdom to find—and felt the last hesitation go out of them like a breath released.

Above, the mountain groaned. Dust fell. Somewhere in the black corridors, Vess was waiting, counting heartbeats.

Casper reached out and took Lior's hand, and the light closed warm around their fingers.

"Now," Casper said. "Before I lose my nerve. Tell me how to begin."

Lior's voice, when it came, was steady as a hearthfire:

"We begin with the truth. Say it aloud—all of it, everything you remembered in the fever, everything you confessed at the shrine. The oath is made of silence, my heart. It can only be broken by someone speaking."
