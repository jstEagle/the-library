## 18. Breaking the Oath

The mountain was dying around them, and it sang while it died.

Casper heard it through the soles of their boots—a deep, groaning chord as the under-halls shed stone into darkness, as the lattice of kept voices unraveled thread by thread and the architecture that had been woven from those threads began to forget how to stand. Dust fell like grey snow. Somewhere far above, whole galleries of the court were collapsing into the mine-shafts Vess had led them through, and the sound of it was almost gentle, almost like a house settling after a long day.

Almost.

They ran. Lior's soul-light streamed ahead of them through the corridor—not a body, not a face, but a presence, a warm gold radiance that moved the way Lior had always moved: quick, certain, laughing even in flight. It lit the walls as it passed, and Casper saw what the light touched and understood why the servants of the court had fled screaming rather than stay to fight. The walls were veined with souls. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Threads of pale fire threaded through black stone like frost through a windowpane, and now that the throne's grip had loosened, they were waking—flickering, stirring, pressing against the stone that held them like moths against glass.

*Free us,* they did not say, because souls beyond the lattice could not speak. But Casper felt it anyway, felt it in the swordspeech humming in their chest like a second heartbeat: *free us, free us, free us.*

"Not yet," Casper gasped, running. "One thing at a time."

The corridor opened without warning into the heart of the mountain—the chamber beneath the throne room, the keystone vault, where the crown's oldest prisoner hung suspended in a cage of woven light. The lattice here was denser than anywhere else, a sphere of interlocked filaments, and inside it burned the brightest soul Casper had ever seen. Gold shot through with green, the exact color of the valley in high summer, of the hillside above the goat pasture in the hour before dusk.

Lior.

Not the streaming radiance that had led them here—that had been only an outrider, a fragment sent ahead like a hand reaching through a door. This was the rest of Lior, the weight of them, the years of them, held in the keystone cage that fed the throne its prophecies and the valley its peace.

Casper stopped running. Their legs simply refused.

* * *

They had imagined this moment for eleven days—since Greyhollow, since the ledger, since the broker's ledger-inked line: *Lior of the Vale. Year of the Treaty. Keystone.* They had imagined rage. They had imagined weeping. They had imagined speaking Lior's name and hearing it answered.

What they felt instead was smaller and vaster than all of that. They felt *recognized.*

The soul in the cage brightened when they entered. Not the general brightening it gave to everything—the slow pulse it must have given the throne for twenty years, feeding prophecy like milk from a wound—but a specific, sudden, joyful flare, the way a lamp flares when someone you love walks into the room.

"You know me," Casper whispered. Their voice cracked on the second word. "Even in there. You know me."

The light pulsed. Once. Twice. And then—impossibly, beautifully—it shaped itself, just for a moment, into something like a hand pressed flat against the inside of the lattice. The gesture was so familiar that Casper's knees nearly went. Lior had done exactly that, a hundred times, a thousand times, palm against the window of the cottage when Casper left for the pasture in the morning. *Go on,* the gesture said. *I'll be here.*

"I'm here now." Casper crossed the vault floor in four strides and put their own hand against the lattice, opposite Lior's. The filaments bit—not painfully, but deeply, the way cold water bites, the way truth bites. Through the contact they felt everything the cage had been holding back: twenty years of awareness compressed into gold. Lior had watched the seasons turn through the mountain's eyes. Had listened to the crown murmur its hunger. Had felt every soul added to the lattice, each one a small cold weight laid against their own. Had known, with terrible clarity, that they were the keystone—and had chosen, again and again, every single day, to keep holding.

*So the valley sleeps safe,* the light said, not in words but in meaning, in the old private grammar they had built between them across one shared childhood and too few shared years. *That was the bargain. I remember making it. Do you?*

And there it was. The question beneath everything, the wound beneath the wound.

"No," Casper said aloud, and the word echoed in the vault like a dropped stone. "I don't. That's the oath's cruelty, Lior—I swore to protect the valley, and the price of the swearing was the reason for it. I've walked past your grave-marker in the village yard for twenty years and felt nothing but a vague, polite sorrow, like mourning a stranger. I forgot your laugh. I forgot—" Their voice failed. They pressed their forehead against the lattice, against the biting light, and let themselves feel all of it at once, every stolen year, because they would need the grief. Grief was fuel. Grief was the only honest thing they had left. "I forgot you. And I am so sorry. I'm sorry it took me twenty years and a stranger bleeding at my door to come find you."

The light flared soft. Forgiveness, before Casper had even finished asking for it. That was Lior. That had always been Lior.

Then the light changed. Sharpened. And the meaning that came through the contact was not comfort.

*You cannot free me by force. You know that. The hunt-captain told you, or the broker did, or you read it in the lattice itself—you are not strong enough to cut a keystone loose. Twenty years of feeding has made me part of the mountain's bones. If you break the cage with power, the mountain breaks with it, and everyone still inside breaks too.*

"I know," Casper said.

*There is one other way.*

"I know that too." Casper lifted their head. Their hand stayed where it was, palm to palm with the light, and they made themselves hold Lior's attention the way they'd once held it across a kitchen table, over candles, saying things that mattered. "The oath. The binding runs both directions—the oath holds me to silence and forgetting, and my power holds the oath intact. Every word I ever spoke in service of it, every recitation, every form practiced at dawn—all of it is mortar in this wall. Unmake the oath, and the wall loses its footing. A keystone bound by an oath-bound speaker can be released by no hand but that speaker's renunciation. I read it in the lattice. I felt it. Didn't I."

*Yes.* The light dimmed, and the dimming was fear—Lior's fear, for Casper, after twenty years of captivity, still spending what remained of themselves on fear for someone else. *You know what it costs.*

"Everything," Casper said. "Whatever memories are left. The gift itself. The words will go out of me like heat out of an opened forge. I'll be—" They laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, wet and thin. "I'll be ordinary. A goatherd with a sword they can't speak to."

*Casper.* The light pressed closer against the lattice, straining toward them. *You don't remember enough to weigh it fairly. Whatever fragments survived—the road, Vess, the duel, the burning city, whatever pieces of me you've recovered since Greyhollow—renunciation takes them all. You will stand up from this floor and not know why your hands are shaking. You will look at me and see a stranger's light. You will have traded everything you are for someone you cannot remember loving.*

"Yes," Casper said.

*Listen to me—*

"I spent twenty years as half a person and called it peace." The words came out steady, which surprised them; somewhere in the last eleven days they had used up all their trembling. "I tended goats beside a grave I couldn't grieve. I practiced forms whose purpose I'd forgotten. I was a locked room walking around in the shape of a person, Lior, and I didn't even know enough to be lonely. You want to talk about trading everything?" They shook their head. "I already did. The oath took its price quietly, in installments, and called it duty. At least this way I get to choose what's taken. At least this time the surrender buys something true."

The light was silent for a long moment. Dust sifted down through the gold glow. Far above, the mountain groaned its long dying chord, and somewhere in the dark behind them, water found a new path through broken stone.

Then, gently, the light began to change. It gathered itself, drew inward, condensed—and became, for the first time, a shape. A figure of gold and green standing within the cage, roughly human, unmistakably *Lior*: the set of the shoulders, the tilt of the head, the particular way of standing that Casper's body remembered even if their mind did not. Their hands knew it. Their hands had known it all along.

*Then let me do it with you,* the light said. *You shouldn't have to speak it alone.*

* * *

They knelt together at the center of the vault, Casper on the cold stone outside the lattice, Lior's luminous shape folded down inside it, close enough that the light warmed Casper's face like late sun through a window. The mountain shook. Filaments of freed soul-light drifted past the vault entrance like seeds on wind, escaping upward through cracks toward a sky none of them could see.

"The words," Casper said. "I need the words. The full recitation—I never learned the ending. The elders only ever taught us fragments."

*You don't need to be taught,* Lior said. *You need to remember. Close your eyes. Give me your hands.*

Casper pressed both palms flat to the lattice. The bite of it was deeper now, almost sweet, like cold air after fever. And Lior's light flowed up through the contact—not taking, never taking, but *showing*, laying images into Casper's mind the way one lays out letters on a table.

The night of the oath-swearing bloomed behind their closed eyes.

Rain. The wayshrine at the valley's edge, its roof leaking, candlelight drowning in wet stone. Themselves, younger, hollow-eyed, kneeling before the elder while the treaty-delegation from the mountain waited outside in the dark. And Lior—Lior alive, Lior in flesh, Lior with rain in their hair, standing beside them with their hand on Casper's shoulder, saying, *Say it, love. Say it well. I'll carry the remembering for both of us until you can take it back.*

And then the words. All of them. The whole architecture of the oath rising in Casper's memory like a building seen by lightning-flash: *I bind my tongue to silence. I bind my gift to the valley's keeping. I bind my knowing to the oath alone, and let all else fall away, that the vale may sleep unhaunted and the mountain be paid in full.*

Paid in full. In full. They had spoken those words themselves, twenty years ago, while Lior stood beside them and smiled through tears, and Casper had understood—even then, even through the fog of the binding closing over their mind—that they were paying the mountain with the only coin they had. Lior's freedom for the valley's peace. Their own wholeness for the sealing of the bargain.

*I carried it for you,* Lior said softly, the memory still glowing between them. *Every year. Waiting for you to come take it back.*

"I'm here to take it back," Casper said.

They opened their eyes. The vault shuddered; a crack ran up the far wall, weeping dust. There was no more time for gentleness, and perhaps there never had been—perhaps love had always been this, two people agreeing to break together rather than survive apart.

"Speak with me," Casper said. "Steady my voice. When the words start pulling, when the binding starts to fight—hold me to the end of it. Don't let me stop halfway. Halfway would kill us both."

*Always,* said Lior's light. *From the beginning. To the end.*

Casper bowed their head. Drew one breath, deep as the valley, slow as the pasture at dusk. And spoke.

"I unbind my tongue from silence."

The first words tore coming out—not from the throat but from somewhere deeper, some root-place where the oath had grown into them like a tree into a fence. Power surged up reflexively to defend the binding, the old familiar hum flooding their chest, their arms, their sword-hand, twenty years of disciplined strength rising like a garrison to repel betrayal. Casper gasped. Their voice wavered.

Lior's light rose to meet it. Warmth poured through their joined palms, wrapping the wavering words, steadying them the way a hand steadies a cup, a shoulder, a life. *Again. Again, love. I'm here.*

"I unbind my gift from the valley's keeping." Louder now, and the vault answered—every filament of soul-light in the walls flickering, turning, listening. The mountain's groan deepened into something like alarm. "What was sworn in darkness I release in truth. What was bought with a beloved I return unbought. Let the vale wake. Let the mountain be *unpaid*."

The last words struck the lattice like a hammer. Cracks spidered outward from beneath Casper's palms, fine lines of white-gold light, and through them Casper felt the binding begin to unravel—not violently, but thoroughly, patiently, like a knot being undone by careful fingers. And with every loop that slipped free, something left them.

They felt the forms go first—the dawn-practice, the discipline, the twenty years of morning ritual dissolving like frost in sun. Then the duel with the hunt-captain, the taste of spoken force, the terrible joy of it—gone. Then Vess's face at the wards, bleeding and impossible—thinning, fading, becoming a shape without a name. Casper clung to what they could and knew it was useless and clung anyway, weeping openly now, speaking the final words through the tears because stopping was death and Lior was holding them to the end, exactly as promised, exactly as always.

"I unbind my knowing from the oath alone." Their voice was breaking apart even as it spoke, syllables going strange and foreign in their own mouth. "Let all else return. Let the vale wake. Let the sleeper wake. Let *Lior*—"

The name was the last word, and the name was the key. As they spoke it, the lattice shattered.

Not broke—*bloomed*. The cage of woven light burst open like a seed-pod, and twenty years of imprisoned gold poured out and inward and *home*, flooding into the luminous shape at the center, filling it, thickening it, until Lior stood in the ruined vault wearing light the way they had once worn skin. For one impossible moment their eyes met Casper's—green as summer hills, alive, *knowing*, full of everything the oath had stolen and the renunciation had returned—

And then the mountain screamed, and the floor tilted, and the world came down.

Casper felt the last of the gift gutter out like a candle pinched between wet fingers. Felt the silence where the hum had been. Felt, beneath the roaring collapse, a vast and terrifying quiet open up inside them—an emptied room, a blank page, a self with the load-bearing walls removed.

Hands caught them. Strong, warm, solid—impossibly solid, impossibly *real*, flesh where light had been. A voice, roughened by twenty years of disuse but unmistakable, saying their name like a promise finally kept:

"Casper. Look at me. I've got you. I remember enough for both of us—now let's get out while the mountain forgets how to stand."

Above them, daylight split the collapsing dark.
