## 19. The Mountain Remembers

The silence came first.

Not the silence of an empty room—Vess had known that kind all her life, the hush of places where nothing had ever spoken. This was different. This was a silence *withdrawing*, like a tide pulling back from a shore it had covered for centuries. The lattice of souls that veined the under-halls of the mountain had been murmuring since the moment she and Casper first descended into the dark: a thousand small voices braided together into prophecy, into counsel, into the low continuous hum that kept the crown alive. Vess had learned to stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

Then it stopped.

And the stopping was louder than any scream.

She felt it in her teeth first—a high, thin ache, as though the mountain itself were a struck bell finally falling still. Then in the walls. The veins of imprisoned light that ran through the stone like frozen lightning began to gutter, one by one, each dimming pulse taking with it a voice she hadn't known she was listening to. Somewhere far above them, she knew, the throne sat emptying. Somewhere above that, a sovereign who had fed on kept voices for longer than Vess's grandmother's grandmother had been born was learning what hunger was.

"Casper." She said the name and got nothing back.

Casper knelt on the shattered flagstones where the ward-lattice had burst, where Vess had spent the last bright fragment of her own soul to crack it open like an egg. Released light still drifted through the hall—slow, wandering motes of gold and blue and one strange green that made her chest hurt to look at, because green had been Sera's color, once, before the mountain took her. The freed souls moved without purpose now, directionless as moths in a lampless room. They did not know where to go. They had been kept so long they had forgotten there was anywhere else.

But Casper—Casper was looking at none of it. Casper was looking at nothing at all.

Their eyes were open. That was the worst part. They were open and fixed on some middle distance that contained no middle distance at all, and behind them was a vacancy Vess recognized because she had seen it before, in Greyhollow, in the vessels who had been drained too far—the hollow-eyed stare of someone whose house had burned down while they were standing inside it. Casper's sword lay on the stones beside their knee, and their hand rested near it but did not grip it, and Vess understood with a cold drop through her stomach that Casper no longer knew what the sword was for. Perhaps no longer knew what *they* were for.

The renunciation had taken everything. Every word of the oath, every memory the oath had been built to guard—and then, when the foundation went, the whole house of them. Vess had watched it happen. Had held Casper's shoulders while the light left their face like water leaving a cracked bowl, and Casper had whispered, in a voice gone suddenly young, suddenly lost: *Who is Lior? Why do I know that name?*

There had been no time to answer. There was no time now either.

Because the mountain had begun to remember what it was.

* * *

It started as a sound beneath the sound—a groan in the deep rock, long and mineral, the complaint of a thing shifting its weight after centuries of stillness. Dust sifted from the ceiling in pale threads. One of the drifting soul-lights flickered out entirely, its last ember spiraling down like a leaf.

"The shafts," Vess said aloud, because saying things aloud kept panic from setting like mortar. "The daylight shafts. The broker said—" She stopped. The broker was dead or fled or worse; the map was folded against her ribs, ink smudged by sweat, and half its markings were guesses anyway. It didn't matter. It was the only map there was.

She crouched in front of Casper and took their face in both hands. Their skin was cool. Their eyes tracked to hers with a lag, like a lamp needing oil.

"Listen to me," she said. "I don't know if any of this is landing. I don't know what's left in there. But we are going to stand up, and we are going to walk, and I am going to talk the entire time, and you are going to hate how much I talk, and that's fine. Hating how much I talk means you're still in there."

Nothing. Then—something. A blink that wasn't quite blank. The smallest furrow between the brows, the ghost of the person who had once told her, exasperated, on the road to Greyhollow, that Vess could fill a funeral with chatter.

"Good enough," Vess whispered, and hauled them up by the arm.

Casper was heavier than they should have been. Not in body—in *willingness*. A body that doesn't know why it moves resists moving, and she had to bear most of their weight across her shoulders, her boots skidding on dust-slick stone, as the first real tremor rolled through the floor. Somewhere behind them, deep in the halls they'd come through, stone met stone with a boom like a door closing forever.

Around them, the court was dying in its own ways.

They passed the first of the servants in a corridor lined with dead lantern-veins—a tall figure in grey livery, standing perfectly still, its hands open at its sides. As Vess dragged Casper past, the figure's head turned. Its eyes were wide and wet and utterly empty of anything except the question it had apparently just asked itself for the first time in decades: *why am I here?* It didn't reach for them. It didn't speak. It simply stood, a puppet with cut strings that hadn't yet fallen over, and Vess found she couldn't look away until Casper's weight pulled her onward.

Others were not so still. She heard them in the branching dark—footsteps running without direction, voices calling names that no longer answered back. A woman in the robes of the prophecy-keepers knelt in an alcove, striking her palms against the stone, over and over, whispering *the voices, the voices, give them back, give them back.* A man in a captain's uniform walked with terrible calm toward a wall and did not stop walking when he reached it. The court beneath the mountain had been built on harvested purpose, and now that purpose was gone, and everything it had propped up—every duty, every devotion, every manufactured certainty—was collapsing along with the tunnels.

Vess should have felt triumph. Part of her did, a hot coal lodged under her sternum: *we did this, we actually did this, Sera, wherever your light is scattered, we broke it open.* But mostly what she felt was the awful intimacy of watching a house fall on the people inside it, even the ones who deserved it. Even the ones who'd built it.

Another tremor. Closer. A crack raced along the ceiling overhead like lightning drawn in chalk, and a slab the size of a cart-horse came down thirty feet behind them, and the sound of it rang in her skull for a hundred steps.

"Almost there," she lied, to Casper, to herself, to whatever god kept maps of collapsing mountains. "Almost there, almost there."

* * *

The broker's map led them wrong twice.

The first time, they followed a marked passage into a chamber that had clearly not been entered in living memory—air thick and sweet with rot, the floor furred with pale fungus that glowed faintly, miserably, like the last coals of a fire. Vess got Casper turned around in the dark, their breath coming shallow and quick against her neck, and for one black moment she thought the vacancy had swallowed them entirely, that they would simply stand in the rot-light until the mountain closed over them.

"Casper." She gripped their collar. "Lior."

The name worked like a hook set in flesh. It always had, since the throne room, since the lattice, since Casper first heard that cadence among the kept voices and come apart with longing. Even hollowed out, even stripped to the studs, something in them leaned toward the name the way a plant leans toward a window.

"That name is yours," Vess said fiercely, forehead pressed to theirs, dust sifting down around them like grey snow. "You lost everything else, but that one's yours, do you hear me? You gave up *everything*, you absolute stubborn idiot, you gave it up to bring Lior home, and Lior is *free* now—free, do you understand, the keystone is loose and the throne is dying and every single thing you broke yourself on was worth it. So you are going to walk out of this mountain and you are going to be there when Lior finds their way back, because I did not spend my last piece of my own soul on a ward-lattice just to bury you in the dark. Do you hear me?"

A tremor answered for them. The fungus-light shuddered. And Casper's hand—slow, uncertain, like a child learning the shape of a thing—closed around Vess's wrist.

Not speech. Not recognition. But grip. Weight. Presence.

"Good," Vess breathed, and laughed, and the laugh came out cracked and half a sob. "Good enough. Again."

The second wrong turn nearly killed them. The passage the map promised ended in a collapse—old, but freshly aggravated, a wall of rubble breathing dust. Vess stood before it for three full seconds, letting despair rise and then deliberately, methodically, putting it down again, the way she'd once put down a knife she'd been holding too long. There was always another way. There had to be. Her sister's soul-light was scattered somewhere in the world above, and Casper's person was half-gone beside her, and she refused—*refused*—to let the mountain keep either of them.

She backtracked. Listened. And heard it: air. A thread of it, cool and moving, tasting faintly of pine and rain and distance. Wind meant surface. Wind meant sky.

"This way," she said, and this time the mountain agreed.

* * *

The daylight shaft was exactly what its name promised: a throat of stone rising toward a coin of grey-gold light so far above it looked painted on. Old timbering braced the walls in rotten ribs, and a rusted ladder-chain dangled down the shaft's side, and the wind came down it in slow breaths, carrying the smell of a world that still existed.

Vess stood at the bottom of it and wanted to weep.

Getting Casper up was the hardest thing she had ever done, and she had once talked her way out of a hunt-captain's chains with a lie about a forged seal. There were iron rungs set into the wall, ancient and flaking, and she went first, testing each one, calling instructions downward in a voice she kept bright and steady because Casper needed bright and steady even if they couldn't say so. Casper climbed the way sleepwalkers climb—hands finding rungs by instinct, eyes unfocused, pausing at long intervals as though forgetting mid-motion what hands were for. Twice Vess had to lean down through the swaying dark and catch a fistful of shirt and hold on, her arms burning, her shoulder screaming, dust and rust raining past her face, while somewhere below the mountain groaned and shifted and tried its doors.

Halfway up, Casper stopped and looked at her.

Really looked. For the first time since the lattice broke, something moved behind those hollowed eyes—not memory, not quite, but the shadow cast by where memory used to live. Their mouth worked. What came out was barely a voice at all.

"...talking too much."

Vess laughed so hard she nearly fell off the ladder.

"There you are," she gasped, clinging to the rungs, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on her face. "Oh, there you *are*. Keep climbing, you insufferable swordspeaker. Complain the whole way. It's the best thing I've heard in days."

They climbed. The mountain complained below them—a long, descending roar, stone settling into stone, the under-halls folding shut one by one like a book closing over its secrets. Vess did not look down. She kept her eyes on the coin of light overhead and counted rungs and talked, because talking was the only spell she had left, and she spent it freely: about the valley they were going back to, about wards that would be dead and gates that would stand open, about how Casper was going to learn to be a person without an oath and it was going to be terrible and wonderful, about Sera, about the road, about anything, about everything, words poured out into the dark like water into cracked ground, hoping some of it would soak in deep enough to stay.

Near the top, the tremors reached the shaft itself. Timber cracked somewhere below. The chain ladder swayed wild. Casper's grip slipped from a rung and Vess caught their wrist with both hands and held, teeth bared, muscles shaking, the whole weight of a hollowed human being swinging over a darkness that was swallowing itself—and held, and held, and hauled.

Then Casper's other hand found the rung. Found the next. Found the light.

They came out of the mountain's throat onto a mountainside drowning in evening—real evening, open sky going amber and violet over forests that ran unbroken to the horizon, wind in the pines, the enormous indifferent beauty of a world that had never known there was a court beneath its feet. Vess collapsed onto scree and grass and pulled Casper down beside her, and they lay there side by side, filthy and bleeding and breathing, while behind and below them the mountain shuddered through its long death. Dust rose from its heights in a slow grey plume, catching the last light, almost beautiful. Almost like the released souls, drifting upward, going home.

Casper stared at the sky with an expression Vess couldn't read—grief and emptiness and, underneath, the very first faint stirring of something else. Something like a man waking in an unfamiliar room and deciding, tentatively, not to be afraid of it.

"I don't know who I am," Casper said at last. Flat. Honest. The first complete sentence since the throne.

Vess turned her head. Below them, the mountain was still falling. Ahead of them, the valley waited—wards dead, gates open, and somewhere in the world between, a freed soul searching for its way home to a body that had forgotten how to wait.

"No," Vess said, and reached over, and laced her dusty fingers through theirs. "But I know who you were. And when you're ready, I'll tell you everything. Starting with the name you're already carrying."

Casper's fingers tightened. Faintly. Like a promise remembered by the body before the mind.

Far below, the mountain gave one final groan—and then, from somewhere in its collapsing heart, a single point of green light rose into the evening air, and began to drift north. Toward the valley. Toward home.

Vess sat bolt upright. "Casper," she whispered. "Look."
