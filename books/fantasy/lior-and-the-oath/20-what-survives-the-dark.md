## 20. What Survives the Dark

There was a time when the world had edges.

Casper knew this the way one knows a story told by someone else—secondhand, worn smooth at the corners, belonging to a person who might as well have been a stranger in a book. Now there were no edges. There was only the dark, and the dark was warm, and somewhere far above it someone was calling their name in a voice like water over stones.

*Casper.*

Not *speaker*. Not *oath-bound*. Not the word the mountain had used, the word the hunt-captain had spat, the word the crown had offered like a coin between teeth. Just the name. Their name, the small one, the one that fit.

They tried to answer and found they had no mouth. They tried to open their eyes and found they had no eyes. This should have frightened them. It did not. Fear required a self to be afraid *for*, and whatever Casper was now seemed to have been poured out onto the floor of the mountain along with all that released light, all those freed birds, all the murmuring voices of the throne going silent one by one like a choir exhaling its last note.

*Vess,* they thought—or felt, or were—and the thought had weight, had hands, had urgency. *Vess was dragging me. Vess was—*

Light. A seam of it, thin as a knife cut, splitting the dark overhead.

* * *

The first memory came back as a smell.

Woodsmoke and juniper, and beneath it the green smell of goats and hay, and beneath *that* something sweeter—bread, yes, but not plain bread; bread with honey melted into the crust so that it wept golden down the sides when you tore it. Casper stood in a kitchen that did not exist anymore, in a body that still had hands, and watched a pair of flour-white palms press dough against a scarred wooden table.

The hands were laughing before the face appeared. That was the thing about Lior—the laugh always arrived first, rolling ahead through a room like weather, so that everyone within earshot braced themselves for joy without knowing why. The laugh was low and cracked in the middle, an imperfect sound, a sound with a stone in its shoe, and Casper had spent years of their life doing stupid things deliberately just to hear it.

"You're staring," the hands said.

"I'm memorizing," said a voice Casper realized, with a lurch, was their own—younger, unguarded, soft in a way they had not been soft since.

"Memorize this instead." The hands let go of the dough. Flour drifted up into the low rafters where the smoke gathered, catching the morning light like a slow gold snowfall. And then Lior turned around, and—

The face.

Oh, the face.

Casper had carried the name for eleven years like a coin sewn into a coat lining, felt but never seen, and now here it was: the face the name belonged to. Brown skin dusted white at the cheekbones. Eyes the deep amber of resin held up to fire. A crooked front tooth that showed when the smile went wide enough, which it always did, because Lior had never once in their life done anything by halves.

"There," Lior said. "Now you'll keep it."

And some part of Casper, the part floating in the warm dark of the present, wanted to scream: *I didn't keep it. I gave it away. I fed it to an oath like grain to a hungry god and I can't remember why, I can't remember why—*

But the memory wasn't listening. Memories never are.

* * *

The second fragment came as a promise, spoken on a night full of rain.

They were under the eaves of the goat shed, shoulder to shoulder, watching water sheet off the thatch in silver ropes. The valley was young then—or they were; it amounts to the same thing. Beyond the tree line the world was a rumor, and inside the tree line the wards hummed their flat gray note, and everything felt safe the way a locked room feels safe, which is to say: like a coffin that hasn't noticed it's occupied.

"When the treaty's signed," Lior said, "we'll go over the pass. Just once. Just to see."

"The wards won't let us."

"The wards won't *stop* us." Lior bumped their shoulder. Rain drummed. Somewhere a goat complained about the weather in the tone of a creature who believed the sky was a personal insult. "Casper. Listen to me. Whatever they ask of us—whatever anyone asks—I'm choosing you first. Do you understand? Before the valley. Before the elders and their treaties and their gray little rules. You first. Say it back."

"You first," Casper said back.

"Swear it."

"I swear it."

Lior laughed—that laugh, the cracked one, the one with the stone in its shoe—and kissed them with rain on their lips, and the memory ended there, mercifully, because Casper-in-the-dark already knew how the next part went. Knew it the way a burn knows fire.

*You swore,* said the dark, or said nothing, and the silence said it. *You swore. And then you swore again, differently, to people who weren't Lior, and the second oath ate the first.*

*I don't remember why,* Casper answered the silence. *That's the worst of it. I don't remember what could have made me trade them away. I don't remember if I cried. I don't remember if they begged.*

The dark held them. The dark was very patient.

Somewhere far above, boots on stone. Someone's breath, ragged and determined. Hands under Casper's arms, hauling. *Stay with me, Eriksen, stay with me, you great stubborn sword-swearing fool—*

Vess. Vess was carrying them out.

Casper sank again.

* * *

The third fragment was the last night. The true last night, the erased one, the one the fever-dreams had shown them in pieces at the wayshrine—but now it came whole, and wholeness was its own cruelty.

Torchlight in the elder's hall. The smell of tallow and cold stone. Casper on their knees—not from reverence; their legs had simply stopped agreeing to hold them. Around them, the elders in their gray wool, faces like closed doors. And on the table between them all, a contract written on vellum so fine it glowed, sealed with the sigil Casper would not see again until Greyhollow: the crown beneath the mountain.

"The treaty requires a keystone," the eldest was saying, in the tone of a woman describing drainage ditches. "A soul of sufficient strength, freely given, to anchor the wards. In exchange, the court beneath withdraws its claim on the valley forever. No harvests. No hunts. Peace."

"And if we refuse?" Casper heard themselves ask. Their voice sounded scraped hollow.

The eldest looked at them for a long moment, and something almost like pity moved behind her eyes before she shuttered it. "Then the court takes what it wants, as it did before the founding. You were born after the last harvest, child. You did not see the carts."

The carts. Even now, even bodiless in the dark, Casper flinched from a memory of a memory—a story told to frighten children, of wagons rolling up the valley road with their beds hung in black cloth, and the cloth moving sometimes, faintly, as though something inside were breathing.

"They offered a choice of keystones," the eldest continued. "Any soul in the valley. The court will take the strongest offered. If none is offered freely, they choose for themselves."

And Casper—who loved Lior more than the valley, who had sworn *you first* under the eaves in the rain—Casper had understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only to the drowning, exactly what the elders were not saying. They were not saying *give us someone*. They were waiting for a volunteer. Because a soul given freely anchored better than a soul taken; everyone knew that; the brokers' ledgers said so; the treaty's fine print said so.

And Casper had thought: *the strongest soul in the valley is mine to give, and if I give Lior, the court will be satisfied for a hundred years, and no carts will ever roll again, and Lior will forgive me because Lior forgives everything, because Lior chose me first and would choose the valley too, would choose it to spare the miller's daughter, to spare the shepherd twins, to spare anyone—*

*Lior would have volunteered,* whispered the dark. *Wouldn't they. That's what you told yourself. That's how you made it bearable. You didn't sell them. You merely spoke first.*

"I give," young Casper heard themselves say, and each word came out like a tooth. "I give my beloved. Lior. Take Lior."

And the elders bowed their heads. And the vellum drank the words. And something vast and cold beneath the mountain turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to smile.

Here was the thing the fever had never shown, the thing the dream had kept behind its final door: after the words were spoken, Lior had come to Casper's door. Of course Lior had come. Lior always came first. And they had stood in the doorway with their amber eyes dry and their crooked tooth hidden, and they had said:

"You could have told me. I'd have gone anyway. But you could have *told* me."

And then they had walked down the valley road toward the tree line, alone, in the gray hour before dawn, and Casper had watched from the window and done nothing, because the oath was already drinking the memory even as it happened, even as Lior's figure shrank and blurred and smoothed away like a footprint filling with rain, until by full light Casper stood at the window feeling only a vague ache and no reason for it, holding a name with no face attached, wondering why the bread tasted of ash.

* * *

Casper surfaced.

It took a long time, surfacing—like rising through deep water where the light comes down in columns and your own arms look strange to you, pale and borrowed. Sound arrived first: wind, real wind, open-sky wind, not the dead air of the under-halls. Then cold, clean and enormous, scouring. Then pain, everywhere, democratic in its distribution. Then weight—their own weight, lying on something sloped and gritty. Scree. A hillside. Sky overhead, gray-gold, the color of dawn deciding whether to bother.

"Casper." Vess's face swam into view above them, smudged with soot, one eye swollen nearly shut, hair half burned away on the left side so that she looked like a coin worn smooth on one face. She was crying and laughing at once, which Casper would later learn was simply her face's way of handling relief. "You're awake. Oh thank every loose star, you're *awake*."

Casper opened their mouth to speak and produced a sound like a door in a house no one lived in.

"Don't talk. Here—" Water, from a skin, tasting of iron and snowmelt. Casper drank like the animal they'd once been, all throat and gratitude.

Then they tried to do the other thing. The reflexive thing. Eleven years of habit: reach inward, find the words coiled at the base of the skull, feel them hum.

Nothing.

Casper reached again, harder, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth. Where the swordspeech had lived—those sleeping syllables, that dormant thunder—there was only smooth, empty quiet. A cleared field. A room swept bare. They had broken the oath, renounced it aloud with Lior's voice steadying theirs, and the gift had gone with it, poured out of them like light from a shattered lamp, and what remained was—

What remained was everything else.

"Lior," Casper rasped.

Vess's expression flickered—grief and gentleness wrestling briefly for control of her mouth. "The lattice broke when the throne died. All of them went free, Casper. Every soul in that wall of light. Yours included." She hesitated. "I saw them rise. Through the shaft. Like—like a migration. Like birds that had forgotten they were birds, remembering all at once."

"Where—" Casper's voice cracked apart. They tried again. "Which way. Which way did they go."

Vess looked at them for a long moment. Wind moved the scree below them, ticking and settling. Far below, the mountain was still smoking, a gray plume standing straight up in the still air like a candle nobody had blown out.

"North," she said quietly. "They went north. Toward—" She stopped. She didn't have to finish. They both knew what lay north, past the pass, past the burned road, past everything: a valley where magic could not follow, where the wards had hummed their flat gray note for eleven years.

Except the wards were dead now. Dead with the throne that fed them. The valley's gates, for the first time in living memory, stood open.

"They're going home," Casper said. Not asking. Knowing. Feeling it in the hollow place where the gift used to live—a new thing growing there, tender and green as a shoot through ash. Not power. Not duty. Just direction. Just the oldest compass there is.

Vess helped them sit up. The world tilted and righted itself. Casper looked down at their own hands—flour-white in a memory, torchlit in another, now just hands, ordinary hands, nicked and dirt-grained and trembling slightly in the cold. Sword-calluses that would never again mean what they'd meant. They flexed the fingers. They felt like a stranger's hands, and like home, both at once.

"I lost it," Casper said. "All of it. The words. The—" They stopped, testing the shape of the loss, expecting grief and finding something stranger. Lightness. As if a coat they'd worn so long they'd mistaken it for skin had finally been cut away, and underneath: a person. Smaller. Colder. Realer. "I can't remember the oath. Any of it. I try to reach for the words I used to recite and there's just—nothing. Fog."

"Good," Vess said fiercely, and then caught herself, and softened. "I mean—it cost you. I know it cost you. But Casper, listen to me." She gripped their wrist, hard, her burned side shuddering with the effort. "When you were delirious, on the climb, you talked. For hours. And you know what you talked about?"

Casper shook their head.

"Not the oath. Not the order. Not the duel, not the crown, not one single word about duty." Her voice went rough. "You talked about bread with honey in the crust. About rain on a roof and someone bumping your shoulder. About a laugh that arrives before the person does. Over and over. Like you were counting them. Like you were making sure they'd survived the flood."

Casper closed their eyes.

There. Beneath the fog where the oath had been, intact, unburned, *kept*: the kitchen. The gold snowfall of flour in the morning light. The promise under the eaves. The crooked tooth. The doorway, and the gray hour, and the words he wished with a whole new kind of grief he could unmake—*take Lior*—and the words Lior had answered with, the ones that mattered, the ones that had survived everything:

*You could have told me. I'd have gone anyway.*

"I remember them," Casper said. "Vess. I remember *all of them* now. The oath took the memories and breaking the oath gave them back, and I don't deserve a single one, and I'm going to spend whatever's left of my life earning them anyway."

Vess smiled—lopsided, soot-streaked, real. "Then stand up," she said, "because north is a long walk, and I want to see this valley of yours. I want to see the place that bought its peace with my sister's neighbor's souls." She paused, and her voice dropped, and for a moment all the iron went out of it. "And I want to see a gate standing open."

Casper stood. It took three tries and Vess's shoulder and a curse in a language they hadn't known they remembered. The wind hit them full in the face, smelling of pine and snowmelt and distance, and far to the north, past the smoking ruin of the mountain, past the pass, the morning was breaking gold over the low green hills—and somewhere ahead of them, riding the wind like pollen, like sparks off a fire, like birds that had forgotten they were birds—

Something laughed.

Low. Cracked in the middle. A sound with a stone in its shoe, arriving before the person does, the way it always had.

Casper turned north and began, haltingly, to walk.
