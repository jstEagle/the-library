## 21. The Valley That Can Be Left

Vess felt the wards die before she saw them.

It came as a silence, a lifting—like a hand that had pressed against her sternum for weeks finally withdrawing. All the way down the mountain's lower slopes, through the scree fields and the blackened pine stands where the fire of Greyhollow still smoldered on the horizon behind them like a wound that refused to close, she had carried a low hum in her bones. The valley's ward-song. She'd heard it the first night Casper dragged her half-dead to the tree line, and she had hated it: that soft, smug thrumming, the sound of a place that had bought its peace with someone else's soul.

Now it was gone. And in its absence, the world was so loud.

Birds, for one thing. She had forgotten birds could be this loud. Wind moving through grass without being combed flat by enchantment. The creak and groan of living wood. Somewhere downslope, a goat bell clanked its idiot music, and Vess laughed—a cracked, disbelieving sound that made Casper stumble beside her and look up with those hollowed eyes.

"What?" Casper asked. Their voice was a ruin. Half their memories were a ruin too; Vess could see it in the way they kept touching their own chest, as if checking whether they were still there.

"Goats," Vess said. "You have an entire secret valley full of magic-eating silence, and what I can hear from here is goats."

Casper smiled. It was a small, uncertain thing, a smile built by someone who had lost the blueprint but remembered that smiles existed. "They're not mine," they said. Then, after a moment: "I think they were mine. There's a—" They touched their temple. "There's a path I know how to walk. My feet know it. I don't."

"That's all right," Vess said, and meant it more than she had ever meant anything she'd said on the road. "Feet remember better than heads anyway. Heads lie. Feet just walk."

They walked.

The tree line came up to meet them, and this time it did not stop them. Vess braced for it out of pure habit—for the sick lurch of ward-sickness, the feeling of her own gift being wrung out of her like water from cloth—but nothing happened. She stepped over the old boundary, over the moss-grown stones where the elders had once set their silent, hungry wards, and her soul-light stayed exactly where it belonged: inside her, small and stubborn and hers.

She stopped and looked back at the boundary stone. It was just a stone now. Lichen had already begun its slow work on the carved sigils, though the carvings themselves were only days dead. In a year no one would know there had ever been a wall here at all.

"Goodbye," she told it quietly.

Casper looked at her. "You knew it?"

"I hated it," Vess said. "Hating something means you know it very well."

* * *

The village had not changed, and that was the strangest thing.

Vess had expected ruin, or at least upheaval—the kind of convulsion that follows when the floor of a life gives way. But the valley had always been a place that practiced forgetting as a discipline, and it seemed the death of its great secret had not disturbed the smaller ones. Smoke rose from chimneys. Someone was beating a rug. Two children chased each other around a rain barrel, shrieking, and scattered when the strangers came up the lane—one of them a stranger with a sword-scarred face and ash in their hair, the other a stranger whose eyes, if you looked too long, held a faint ember-light that no ungifted person should carry.

An elder met them at the well. Not the same elder who had interrogated Casper—Vess gathered that one had died in the winter, peacefully, never knowing what his questions had cost—but one cut from the same bolt of gray wool. She looked at Casper for a long time, and Vess watched recognition move across her face like weather.

"Casper Eriksen," the elder said. "We thought you dead."

"You thought me gone," Casper said. "It isn't the same."

"No." The elder's gaze slid to Vess, catalogued the ember-eyes, the road-worn clothes, the way she stood slightly in front of Casper without deciding to. "And you've brought gifted blood inside the wards."

"There are no wards," Vess said. "That's rather the point."

The elder's mouth thinned. Around them, the lane had begun to fill—not with hostility, Vess noted, but with that particular valley attention, the careful listening of people who had spent generations perfecting the art of hearing everything and acknowledging nothing. A woman holding a basket of late apples. A man with a smith's forearms and a boy's frightened face peeking around him.

"The mountain," the elder said slowly. "There were lights. Three nights past. The whole peak lit from within, like a lantern, and then—" she made a small gesture, helpless, "—darkness. And since then, the quiet at the tree line is just *quiet*. Birds cross it. Mist crosses it. Old Marta swears her rheumatism has eased for the first time in twenty years, which she attributes to prayer, though she has never prayed before in her life."

"The throne is dead," Casper said. Flatly, the way you'd report weather. Vess heard what underlay it: *I killed it. I don't remember choosing to. I'm told I chose it.* "The crown that fed on souls has fallen. The hunt is ended—or will be, once word travels. No one will come for gifted blood again."

A murmur went through the lane like wind through wheat.

"And you," the elder said to Vess. "What are you?"

"Free," Vess said. "Same as everyone else. I just noticed sooner."

* * *

They were given a bed—two beds, in the house of the smith, who asked no questions beyond whether they ate salted fish, and who left extra bread outside their door both mornings as if feeding fugitives were simply what one did with surplus bread. Vess slept like the dead and woke like the living, which after the road felt like a miracle she hadn't earned.

On the third day, she found Casper at the tree line.

Not crossing it. Standing at it, one hand resting on the old boundary stone, looking down the long green fall of the valley and then up at the open sky beyond, where the land rolled away toward roads Vess knew and rivers Vess had sailed and cities where a person with ember-eyes could now, theoretically, walk in daylight.

"They're leaving," Casper said, without turning. "Some of them. The Halvard family packed a cart yesterday. The twins from the mill—they're gifted, did you know? Faintly. Little weather-sense, nothing the old law would even have bothered with. But they grew up learning to hide it, and hiding became the whole shape of them, and now they don't know how to stop, so they're going somewhere they can learn." A pause. "I think I used to know them. I think I taught one of them sword-forms. I don't—I can't—"

Their hand tightened on the stone.

"It's all right," Vess said.

"You keep saying that."

"Because it keeps being true." She came to stand beside them, shoulder almost touching shoulder. Below, the valley lay gold and green in the morning light, absurdly beautiful, the kind of beauty that had been purchased at a price no one down there had ever been allowed to see itemized. "Look at them. Some stay, some go. Do you understand what that means? For two hundred years nobody in this valley did anything by choice. Staying wasn't a choice—it was a sentence served so softly they mistook it for safety. Now the Halvards leave because they want to, and old Marta stays because she wants to, and both of those are worth exactly the same now. That's what you bought, Casper. Not victory. Choice."

Casper was quiet a long moment. "I don't remember buying it."

"No," Vess said gently. "But your feet walked you there. I told you feet remember better than heads."

* * *

She gave herself four more days.

She told herself it was to see Casper settled—to make sure the hollow-eyed swordspeaker would eat, would sleep, would not wander back up the mountain looking for whatever had been carved out of them. And that was true, partly. But Vess had learned on the road to be honest with herself about the things that mattered; dishonesty was for strangers and reward-seekers.

The truth was that she needed to say goodbye properly, and she had never in her life known how to do that. She had left her sister without saying goodbye. She had sold her sister's location to save her own skin, and then spent years telling herself the story where that was survival and survival was neutral, until a feverish oath-breaker confessed a worse sin at a ruined shrine and Vess had discovered that the only absolution available in this world was the kind two betrayers could grant each other.

So on the fourth evening she sat with Casper on the smith's doorstep, watching the light go amber over the valley, and she said, "I'm going."

Casper nodded slowly. "For her. Your sister."

"For what's left of her." Vess turned her hands over, looked at the faint glow beneath the skin of her palms, dimmer now since she'd spent her last great fragment shattering the ward-lattice. What remained of her gift was small. But small things could find other small things. A sister's soul, torn loose when the throne died, had not been destroyed—nothing that bright simply stopped—and scattered light leaves traces. Embers drift. "The broker's ledger listed dispersals. Unanchored soul-light gets drawn to certain places—old shrines, deep water, high passes. There's a map. It'll take years."

"Years," Casper repeated.

"Probably." She smiled crookedly. "I'm good at probably. Probably kept me alive this long."

They sat with that. Down the lane, someone was singing—an actual song, unguarded, the twins' mother by the sound of it, her voice carrying the particular wobble of a woman singing freely for the first time in her adult life. The goats added their commentary. The valley absorbed it all without flinching, without filtering, without the great silent hunger at its edge drinking down every loose drop of magic. Just a place now. Just home, for whoever chose it.

"Come with me," Vess said, and immediately wished she could unsay it—not because it wasn't true, but because she saw the answer in Casper's face before they spoke.

"I can't." Casper's voice was gentle. "Someone's coming home here. I felt—" they pressed a hand to their chest again, that gesture that had become their compass, "—the first night back, I dreamed of a laugh. I don't know whose. I don't remember any face. But the dream said *wait*, and I think waiting is the last promise I get to keep. Whoever I was, they spent everything to bring someone home. The least I can do is be here when they arrive."

Vess blinked hard and looked at the mountains. "You're insufferable," she managed. "You know that? Hollowed out and memory-stripped and still nobler than anyone I've ever met. It's disgusting."

"You could stay," Casper said. "There's room. The valley takes people now. That's the whole miracle."

"And someday I might. When my hands stop glowing, or when I find her, or when I'm too old to walk passes." Vess stood, brushed off her trousers, and offered her hand. "But not yet. Yet, I have embers to chase."

Casper took it. Held it a moment longer than a handshake required. "Truth between us," they said—the old pact, the shrine-pact, the only vow either of them trusted anymore. "Always."

"Always," Vess said. "Even when it costs. *Especially* when it costs."

* * *

She left at dawn, alone, the way she'd arrived.

At the tree line she paused—out of habit again, though the habit meant nothing now—and looked back once. Casper stood in the lane by the smith's house, small in the morning mist, one hand raised. Behind them the valley opened like a cupped palm, holding its people loosely now, ready to let go or hold fast as each of them chose. Somewhere in that soft golden distance, a freed soul was finding its way down out of the broken mountain toward a body that waited, patient as planted ground.

Vess lifted her own hand in answer. Then she turned north, toward the high passes, following the faint warm pull beneath her skin like a compass needle made of grief and hope—

—and stopped.

Because there, on the far side of the old boundary stone, sitting on a mile-marker in the pale light, was a traveler. Dusty boots. A worn cloak. A face Vess had not seen in eleven years and would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea.

Her sister looked up at her, and smiled, and said, "You're harder to find than you used to be."
