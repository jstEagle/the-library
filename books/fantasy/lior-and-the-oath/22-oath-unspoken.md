## 22. Oath Unspoken

The valley had changed its smell.

Casper noticed it before anything else—the way the air came down off the high meadows carrying not just pine and cold stone and goat-milk mornings, but something thinner underneath, something like a door left open in a house that had been shut for years. The wards were dead. Everyone said it differently. Old Maren called it *the hush lifting*. The children simply ran to the tree line and stood there with their arms out, palms flat against air that no longer pushed back, laughing at nothing, at everything, at the strange new fact of a world without edges.

Casper walked up through the terraces alone, as they had walked most mornings since coming home, and the goats followed for a while out of habit before losing interest in a person who no longer smelled like purpose.

The sword was on their back. It had been on their back for eleven days, through the pass and down the long gray shoulder of the mountain and across the river shallows where Vess had finally stopped walking and turned around and looked at them with an expression Casper was only now beginning to understand—not farewell exactly, but the particular tenderness of someone leaving a person they trust to be all right.

*Truth between us, always.* That had been the pact at the wayshrine, and Vess had kept it to the last: she told them she was going east, toward the coast towns, following the rumor of a light scattered in fragments through three different vessels, hunting her sister's soul the way one hunts a song heard once and never forgotten. She did not ask Casper to come. That was its own kind of truth. Some griefs want company; some want a road with no one on it who remembers you weeping.

So Casper had stood in the shallows and watched her go until the reeds took her, and then they had turned west, toward home, toward the valley that could now be left—and had not left it.

Not yet. Perhaps not ever. But the difference between *could not* and *did not* was the whole width of the world now, and Casper meant to stand in that width this morning and feel every inch of it.

The tree line rose ahead, dark firs shouldering up against a sky rinsed clean by autumn. Beyond it, past the last rank of trees, the old ward-stones leaned in their ring like tired shepherds, moss-eaten, silent. Casper had touched them yesterday and felt nothing—no hum, no resistance, no faint pressure against the skin like a held breath. Just stone. Just weather. Just the ordinary miracle of a boundary that no longer existed.

They stopped where the grass grew tall and seed-heavy, where the shadow of the first fir reached out long fingers over the meadow, and they drew the sword.

It came out of the scabbard with the sound it had always made, a low sliding note, and for a moment—only a moment—Casper's throat tightened around words that were no longer there. The gift was gone. They had known it since the mountain, since waking hollow-eyed in the daylight shafts with Vess's hand gripping theirs and half a life missing from behind their eyes. There was no speech in the steel now. No cutting force waiting under the tongue. When Casper spoke, the words were only words, air shaped into meaning, and they fell to the ground like everything else that was spoken honestly.

That was the trade. They had made it with open eyes, or as open as eyes can be when half of what they see has been burned away. The oath unbound, the memories unbound, the power gone with both. What remained was this: a body scarred along the forearm, hands that still knew the forms, and a name—*Lior*—that had survived every erasure like a coin too deep in the earth for any plow to reach.

Casper knelt and drove the sword into the earth.

It went in easier than expected, as if the ground had been waiting for it, as if the valley itself had opinions about endings. The blade sank to the crossguard and stood there, quivering faintly, catching the early light along its edge—a thin bright line drawn between the past and whatever came next.

"You were never mine," Casper said aloud, because saying things aloud was a habit they were learning to keep instead of fear. "You were lent to me. And I'm giving you back."

The sword did not answer. Swords never had, even in the old days; it had been Casper's voice that cut, always, the steel only the place where the words came to rest. But standing there with their hand still on the pommel, feeling the last warmth of metal against their palm, Casper thought they understood something about oaths that no elder had ever taught them: an oath is not a chain. A chain breaks and clatters and is done. An oath is more like a road you walk so long that you forget there was ever anywhere else to put your feet. Breaking it does not free you by itself. You still have to choose, each morning, to walk somewhere new.

They let go of the pommel.

Their hand hung in the air a moment, empty, strange, the fingers curled around a weight that wasn't there. Then Casper stood, brushed the dirt from their knee, and turned away from the sword.

They did not look back. That was the hardest part, harder than the mountain, harder than the renunciation spoken with Lior's voice steadying theirs word by word like a hand on a rope over a drop. Every step away from the blade pulled at them—not magic now, nothing so grand, only the plain gravity of habit and grief. The sword had been the shape of their whole life. Oath-bound speaker, last of the order, keeper of the valley's silence. Take that away and what was left was a person with a limp that ached in cold weather and a memory full of holes and a name they loved that belonged to someone who was—

Who was here.

Casper felt it before they saw it, the way you feel sun come around a wall. Warmth on the left side of their face, though the morning light fell from the right. They stopped in the tall grass and closed their eyes and breathed, and the breath came back changed, sweeter, the way bread smells different when someone you love has baked it.

"Lior," they said, to the open air, to the tree line, to the brightness gathering at the edge of sight.

The soul came home slowly. That was the truth no one warned you about—freedom was not a door flung wide but a long walk back, and Lior's soul had been kept beneath stone for years, murmured into prophecies, stretched thin across the lattice of a throne. It moved now like light moving through water, arriving in pieces: a warmth at the shoulder, a shimmer above the grass, a pressure against Casper's cheek like a forehead resting there. And threaded through all of it, unmistakable, unbearable, *familiar*—a cadence. A way of being present. The particular silence Lior had always carried, the one that made rooms feel held rather than empty.

"I know," Casper whispered. "I know. Take your time."

The villagers would say later that they saw it from the terraces—a light walking the meadow beside the Eriksen child, unhurried, like two people strolling after supper. Maren would press her lips together and say nothing, because Maren had spent eighty years learning which silences were kindnesses. The children would run closer than the adults dared and report, breathless, that the light laughed. That it sounded like bells underwater. That when it passed over the grass, the grass bent toward it the way wheat bends toward evening.

Casper walked with it. There was nowhere else to be.

* * *

They talked all that day, in the manner of their talking—which was to say, mostly Casper spoke and Lior's presence answered in pressures and warmths and occasional flickers of almost-language, and somehow it was enough, somehow it had always been enough, even before, even in the years Casper could no longer fully remember. Memory returned in fragments, the healers had said, if it returned at all. Not the oath—that was gone for good, burned out at the root—but other things. Things chosen rather than sworn.

A laugh came back to Casper on the second morning, sudden as a sneeze, while they were mending a fence: Lior's laugh, high and disbelieving, the laugh that had always followed Casper's worst jokes, and Casper sat down in the dirt of the dooryard and cried for an hour and then got up and finished the fence, and the light drifted around the posts as if inspecting the work, and something in Casper's chest that had been clenched for years finally, grudgingly, began to unknot.

A promise came back in the third week, midwinter approaching, snow dusting the terraces: *whatever they take, they can't take this*, and Casper stood in the falling white with their bare hands burning cold and understood, for the first time, that the promise had been true. The crown had taken Lior's soul and Casper's memories and the valley's freedom and the gift itself, and still this remained—two presences in a snowy yard, choosing each other again from the wreckage, which was the only kind of love that had ever counted for anything.

The face came last. It came in a dream, of course, because the important things had always come to Casper in dreams, even back in the numb years when the recurring dream was only a voice calling from beneath stone. This dream was different. In this dream there was no stone. There was a kitchen, or maybe a workshop, lamplit, and someone with their back turned humming off-key, and when they turned around Casper woke weeping—not from grief but from the sheer blunt fact of it: brown eyes, a crooked smile, a small scar above the eyebrow that Lior had gotten falling out of the ward-oak at nine years old and had lied about ever since.

Casper knew the face the way you know your own hands. They had known it all along. That was the cruelest joke of the erasure—that the memories could be taken and the knowing remain, nameless, homeless, aching in the bones like weather.

Now it had a home again.

* * *

Spring came late that year, the way springs do after hard winters, and with it came the question everyone in the valley was asking and no one was answering: stay, or go?

The road west was open now—truly open, no wards to stop a body at the tree line, no law of silence to seal a mouth. Riders came sometimes, honest ones, traders and mapmakers and once a nervous young priest of no particular god, and they brought news of a world rearranging itself. The mountain capital was rubble and legend. The court beneath it was a story mothers told to explain why you shouldn't covet what isn't yours. In Greyhollow the soul market had burned, and in its ruins someone had raised a shrine of small lights—candles, lanterns, bits of mirror—for the souls that had spilled free and scattered, and pilgrims went there looking for their dead the way Vess had gone east looking for hers.

Casper read the letters Maren brought, and listened to the news, and watched the young ones pack carts and argue about the coast, and felt none of the old pull. The valley had been a prison; now it was a place. The difference was everything. You could love a place you were free to leave. You could only endure a prison.

Besides, there was work. The terraces wanted tending. The goats, absurdly, had multiplied. And every evening the light came down off the meadows to sit with Casper by the fire, folding itself into the space across the hearth that had been Lior's and apparently still was, and they would talk—or Casper would talk, and the firelight would bend and brighten in answer—and the nights passed the way good nights pass, without anyone counting them.

Some evenings Casper practiced the forms. Old habit, muscle-deep, the body remembering what the mind had lost. Without the gift the forms were only movement, only breath and balance, a dance with a stick of iron—and yet they had never felt so honest. Before, every form had been a recitation, the oath riding under the motion, eating a little more of Casper with each repetition. Now it was just a body in a meadow at dusk, swinging a sword because the sword was there and the body liked the rhythm. Nothing was being paid for. Nothing was being spent. It was, Casper thought, the first time in their life the forms had been free.

The sword itself stayed planted at the tree line. Rust took it slowly, season by season, a reddish bloom climbing the blade like ivy. Children dared each other to touch it. Once, a traveling smith offered good money for the steel, and Casper considered it seriously for a whole afternoon before shaking their head—not out of reverence, exactly. More like the way you keep a letter you'll never reread. Proof that a chapter happened. Proof that you are not the same person who wrote it.

* * *

On the first warm evening of the new spring, a year almost to the day since the soldiers breached the wards, Casper climbed to the tree line with a jug of cider and two cups—one clay, one a cup of clear glass filled with water, because Lior had always preferred water and some preferences outlast everything, even death, even thrones.

The sword stood where it had stood, rusted now nearly to the guard, leaning slightly, patient as a fencepost. The ward-stones beyond it lay in their ring like sleeping animals. West of them, the land rolled away green and open and utterly unguarded, all the way to the horizon, all the way to everywhere.

Casper poured the cider, set the water where the light gathered, and sat down in the grass with their back against a warm stone.

"So," they said. "Maren wants me to take the elder's seat. Can you imagine. Me. Telling people what to do." A swallow of cider. The light pulsed gently, amused. "I told her I'd think about it. Which you know means yes. It always meant yes. You used to tease me about that—how I'd fight every decision like a cat in a sack and then do exactly what was asked." Their voice caught, just slightly, on *used to*, and they let it catch. Truth between them, always. Even now. Especially now.

Below in the valley, lamps were coming on one by one in the windows, little gold answers to the darkening sky. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing. Somewhere a cart creaked on the west road, going out or coming in—it hardly mattered which anymore, and that was the whole miracle, wasn't it, that it didn't matter, that the valley was no longer a mouth closed around its people but a doorway standing open in both directions.

Casper looked at the sword a long time. Rust and patience and a thin line of old light along the edge where the rust hadn't reached. It looked smaller than it used to. Everything from that life did.

"No oath binds me," they said quietly. "I checked. Every morning, I check." A pause. The wind moved the tall grass in long silver waves, all the way down the meadow. "And every morning, anyway."

The light rose from the water cup and settled against Casper's shoulder, warm as a hand, familiar as breath, and together they watched the stars come out over a valley that could be left, and was not left, and never had to be either thing again—until, far off down the western road, past the dead ward-stones and the rusting sword and all the country the oath had forbidden, a small light appeared. Traveling. Coming closer. Moving with the particular, stumbling, stubborn gait of someone who has walked a very long way on very tired feet, and who is, at last, almost home.

Casper stood up.
