## 4. The Woman Who Sold Yesterday

The rain came down on Saltcross like a debt being collected — steady, impersonal, owed by everyone.

Gideon stood at the redemption window of the counting house and watched it sheet off the copper eaves while the clerk behind him sorted the morning's petitions into three trays: *buy*, *sell*, and *disputed*. It was good work, watching weather do what ledgers did. Everything accounted for. Nothing left over.

"Redemption inquiry," the clerk said, and slid a slip of paper across the counter.

Gideon read it twice. Redemption inquiries were common enough — a widow buying back her husband's last words, a sailor repurchasing the memory of a coast he'd sworn never to return to. People sold their yesterdays in hard seasons and spent years earning them back, coin by coin, and the Guild was patient because patience was profitable. But this slip had been flagged with the black wax of the senior assessors, and the name on it stopped his breath for half a second before his training caught it and filed it away.

*Isolde Marrec.*

He hadn't heard that name spoken aloud in nine years. He'd seen it once, three years ago, in a ledger — a sale entry, dated the autumn after she'd left Saltcross, itemized in the Guild's flat hand: *One (1) day, contiguous, late summer. Seller: I. Marrec. Contents: personal.* No further description. Personal meant the assessor had viewed it and sealed the record, which meant it had been worth sealing.

He had told himself then what he told himself now: that every name in the ledger was equal, that a day was a day, that the Guild's cold arithmetic was the only honest thing in a dishonest world. He had been very good at telling himself things.

"Show her in when she comes," he said, and his voice came out level, and he hated how much work level cost.

* * *

She came at the turn of the afternoon tide, when the counting house was emptiest, as if she'd timed it — and of course she had. Isolde Marrec had always understood systems. She walked through the door with rain still beaded on her shoulders and looked around the counting floor the way a surgeon looks at a body: knowing where everything was, and what it would look like opened.

Nine years had made her a stranger wearing a familiar face. Her hair was pinned up in the severe style of someone who worked among important people. Her coat was good wool, court-cut, salt-gray. But her eyes were the same — quick, dark, always slightly amused at the world's expense — until they found him, and then they weren't amused at all.

"Gideon Joubert," she said.

"Madam Marrec." He inclined his head the precise degree the Guild required. "You're here about a redemption."

"I'm here about a redemption." She set a purse on the counter between them, heavy enough that the wood acknowledged it with a small knock. "Entry number four thousand one hundred twelve. Sold eight years ago this month. I want it back."

He pulled the ledger without letting his hands shake. That was the discipline of the counting house: your hands belonged to the work, whatever the rest of you was doing. He found the entry. *One (1) day, contiguous, late summer. Seller: I. Marrec. Contents: personal.* The seal on the record was Vess's own.

"The contents are sealed," he said. "You'll understand I can't discuss—"

"I know what I sold, Gideon. I was there." A flicker of something crossed her face — not quite a smile, not quite its opposite. "That's rather the point."

He turned the ledger toward her. "The buyback price is calculated on age of the asset, condition, and market scarcity. Personal days appreciate poorly, I'm afraid. Most sellers find the sum... sobering."

"I didn't come here to be sobered. I came to pay." She opened the purse and began counting coins onto the counter in neat stacks, and there was something almost ceremonial in it, something that said she had rehearsed this moment the way he rehearsed audits. "Count it yourself. You'll want to."

He counted it. It was exact, to the copper, which meant she knew the figure already, which meant she had asked, or bribed, or calculated it herself from the tariff schedules — and all three possibilities told him she had been planning this for a long time.

"You've kept the entry current," he said, stalling, hating himself for stalling. "Most abandoned assets lapse after five years. Someone paid the holding fees."

"I paid them. Through intermediaries." She met his eyes. "I wasn't sure I'd ever have the full price. I didn't want the day to lapse while I was still poor."

*A day.* He looked at the entry again. Late summer, eight years ago. The autumn she'd left. His mind went to that season and found — nothing. Not a gap, exactly; gaps were noticeable, edges you could feel. This was worse. It was like a tooth that had never existed, a smooth place in the gum where the tongue kept returning anyway. He remembered that summer in general: the fish fleet in, the Guild's harvest of sailors' vows, the long light on the harbor. He remembered everything except any hour he might have spent with her, and he had only realized the shape of the absence years later, when he'd tried to remember her leaving and found no scene at all, just the fact of it, like a sentence with the middle cut out.

He had never asked anyone about it. Asking would have meant admitting there was something to ask about.

"The transaction requires the seller's consent to release," he said. "Standard procedure. The original seller signs a release, or the asset reverts to—"

"I am the original seller."

"Yes, but—" He stopped. Read the entry again. *Seller: I. Marrec.* And beneath it, in the field he'd skipped a dozen times because it was usually blank, the co-signature line:

*Purchaser of record: the Salt Guild, holding for —*

And there the ink had been scraped away. Deliberately. With a knife, by the look of it, long ago.

Gideon felt the counting house tilt very slightly, the way a ship tilts when something shifts in the hold.

"Holding for whom?" he asked.

"That," said Isolde, "is an excellent question. I've wondered for eight years." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to the register people used in this building when they wanted to be overheard by no one, not even the gods — especially not the gods, though the gods hadn't answered anything in a century. "I sold a day of my life to this Guild. I signed the papers. And when I asked the clerk at the time who was buying, he told me the Guild held it *for a party who wished to remain unnamed*, and that the fee structure suggested the party intended to keep it indefinitely. Gideon. Nobody keeps a stranger's happy day indefinitely unless it matters to them. And nobody scrapes a ledger with a knife unless they're afraid of it."

He should have closed the ledger. He should have cited procedure, called Vess, done any of the ten correct things. Instead he heard himself say, quietly, "What was the day?"

Her expression changed — softened and sharpened at once, which he hadn't known an expression could do.

"You don't know," she said. Not a question. She'd watched his face and gotten her answer, and something in her look was so raw that he had to look down at the ledger to survive it. "They took yours too. Or you sold it. Which?"

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Gideon." She said his name the way she'd used to say it, back when saying it was easy — no, that was wrong; he had no *back when*. He had a smooth place in the gum and a woman across a counter who was looking at him like a person looks at a house they grew up in. "I have spent eight years reconstructing my own life from the outside. I know exactly what a missing day feels like from both sides of the counter. So I'll ask once more, and then I'll pay my money and we can pretend this is business. Do you remember a day, late summer, eight years ago? The harbor festival. Lanterns on the water. Rain that started at dusk."

He reached for it. He reached the way a man reaches for a stair in the dark that isn't there — full commitment, total faith, and then the lurch of nothing under his foot.

"No," he said.

Something went out of her shoulders. Not defeat. More like confirmation — grief finally given a shape to stand in.

"All right," she said. "Then we'll do it as business." She pushed the stacks of coin across the counter, one by one, until they sat squarely in front of him. "Full price. Release the asset. I'll sign whatever you need me to sign."

* * *

The vault below the counting house was cold and smelled of brine and paper. The memories were kept in vials in ranked cabinets, thousands of them, each labeled in the Guild's hand: dates, names, contents where contents could be named. Gideon walked the aisles with Isolde behind him and felt, absurdly, like a man giving a tour of his own grave.

"Four thousand one hundred twelve," he said to the vault-keeper, an ancient deaf man who read lips and trusted no one, and the old man shuffled off and came back with a tray, and on the tray was a single vial.

It was smaller than most. The liquid inside caught the lamplight and held it — and Gideon had handled ten thousand vials in his career and knew their behavior the way a shepherd knows sheep. Memories of ordinary days lay quiet in their glass, dim as pond water. Traumatic ones churned. Sweet ones, rare ones, glowed faintly warm.

This one hummed.

Not loudly. A vibration more felt than heard, a fine tremor in the glass, in the tray, in the bones of his fingers where they touched it. Like the vial in the shipment last week — the one that had hummed when no prayer should be answerable, the one he'd flagged and sealed and couldn't stop thinking about. But this was different too. That vial had hummed like a struck bell. This one hummed like a throat deciding whether to sing.

Isolde saw his face. "What is it?"

"It's nothing. Condition variance. Old assets sometimes—" He stopped lying, mostly because she was watching him lie and he found he couldn't bear the weight of it. "It's humming."

"I know," she said. "It hummed when I sold it, too. They told me it was normal."

"It isn't normal."

"Then we have that in common, the vial and I." She held out her hand for it.

He gave it to her. Protocol said the release happened at the counter upstairs, witnessed, signed, the asset decanted or consumed per the buyer's instruction. Protocol said a great many things. What actually happened was that Isolde Marrec stood in the cold aisle between the cabinets with a day of her life humming in her fist, and looked at it, and did not drink it.

"Aren't you going to—" he began.

"Not here." She tucked it into her coat, against her heart, which was such an Isolde gesture that his chest hurt. "I've waited eight years. I can wait one more night. I want to be somewhere that means something when I take it back." She looked up at him. "There's a bench on the harbor wall. Under the lantern pole. Do you know it?"

He searched. The harbor wall he knew; the lantern poles he knew; the benches were furniture, unremarkable, interchangeable. And yet the phrase *under the lantern pole* landed somewhere in him and rang, faintly, like a coin dropped in an empty room.

"No," he said.

"No," she agreed softly. "You wouldn't." She straightened her coat. Professional again, court-composed, and only her eyes still open. "Thank you for the efficient service, Master Joubert. The Guild's reputation is deserved."

"Isolde." It came out of him before he could file it. "Why now? Eight years — why come back now?"

For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she stepped closer, close enough that he could smell rain and some courtly perfume failing to cover the antiseptic tang of a physician's life, and said, very low:

"Because I work in the palace now, and I've seen what sits on the throne, and I think the day I sold may be the only true thing I have left. And because—" her voice caught, and she mastered it the way he mastered his hands, visibly, expensively, "—because whoever bought my yesterday has been keeping it in *your* vault, Gideon, under *your* seals, and I no longer believe anything in this building is an accident. Including me walking through that door today."

She left. He stood in the aisle a long time, listening to the vials not hum.

* * *

That night he did something he had never done in fifteen years of service. He opened the sealed records.

Vess's key hung in the senior assessor's office, and Vess was at the harbor dinner, and Gideon told himself it was an audit — that the scraped entry, the humming vial, the fisherwoman's son who had come back from the drowned dark remembering other lives, all of it constituted a discrepancy requiring review. He was good at telling himself things. It was practically a professional skill.

The record of entry 4112 was thin. Sale date. Price — oddly low, a fraction of market, as if the buyer had gotten a bargain or the seller had been in a hurry. Seller: Isolde Marrec. Purchaser of record: *the Salt Guild, holding for* — and here the archive copy matched the ledger, ink scraped to parchment.

But archives kept duplicates. And duplicates were stored by date, and the duplicate shelf for that autumn was in the third cabinet, and the third cabinet's lock was older than the others and had been replaced, recently, with a lock whose key he did not have.

Someone had gotten here first. Recently. Within the year, by the brightness of the brass.

Gideon stood in the dark of the archive with the lamp turned low and understood two things with perfect clarity. First: that he was not the only person asking questions, and that whoever else was asking had better access than he did. Second — slower, colder, arriving like water rising in a hull — that the missing day was not missing from his memory alone. It was missing from the record. From both records. As if someone had gone through the world removing every trace of a single late-summer day, except for one vial that hummed like a living thing and one woman who had crossed the country to take it back.

He thought of the lantern pole. Of a bench he apparently knew and could not know. Of Isolde's voice: *I no longer believe anything in this building is an accident.*

Above him, through the floorboards, he heard the counting house door open — hours past closing, boots on the boards, more than one pair — and then Vess's voice, dry as old paper, calling his name into the dark.

"Gideon. Bring the lamp up, boy. We need to talk about what you sold her."
