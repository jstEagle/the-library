## 5. What Wears the Crown

The palace physician's office smelled of camphor and old stone, and Isolde Marrec had spent six years making sure it stayed that way — clean, orderly, unremarkable. Unremarkable was armor. Unremarkable was the whole point.

She ground valerian root with a mortar that had belonged to her predecessor, a man who had died of nothing in particular and been replaced without ceremony, the way palace physicians always were. Below her window, the morning court assembled in the Rose Hall, and she could hear it through glass: the murmur of petitioners, the scrape of chairs, the voice of King Aldric the Third rising and falling in its familiar cadence.

Except this morning, for three seconds, it wasn't his cadence at all.

She set down the pestle. She had learned to catch those slips the way a sailor catches weather changes — not by listening to what was said but by feeling the pressure drop. The king's voice would go flat, or too deep, or it would pause in the wrong places, like a man reading music he'd never been taught. Three seconds. Then it folded back into Aldric's warm, tired baritone, and the court went on applauding whatever he'd said, because courts applauded everything, and no one else seemed to hear it.

No one else was listening for it.

Isolde had come to the palace with one suitcase, one forged letter of recommendation from a physician in Corvane who had unfortunately drowned before he could be consulted, and the proceeds of a single sale. She had told herself, on the coach south, that she would think about it later. That was the trick of selling a memory — you didn't lose it all at once, you lost it the way you lost a tooth: there was a gap, and your tongue kept going back to it, and everyone told you the gap would close over, and mostly it did.

Mostly.

She knew she had sold something important. The Salt Guild's receipt said *one (1) memory, personal, non-transferable, quality: high*, and high-quality memories cost enough to fund six years of medical training and a forged identity besides. She knew the shape of the hole. It was from her time in Greyharbor, her last year there, and it was — this she knew with the strange certainty of negative space — the most important thing that had ever happened to her. She knew this because of what remained around the edges. A man's laugh, heard from another room. The smell of rain on harbor stones. Waking once with her hand curled closed, holding nothing, weeping without knowing why, and deciding, with the cold clarity available only at four in the morning, that she needed to leave Greyharbor entirely and become someone who could walk into any room in the kingdom and not be turned away.

The Guild clerk had asked her, standard procedure, if she wished to know what she was selling before she signed. She had said no. She had her reasons, though the reasons were also partly gone now, folded into the sale like a letter sealed inside a letter.

*You'll thank me,* someone had said to her once, in a voice she could almost place. Or maybe she had said it to someone. The grammar of the memory wouldn't resolve.

"Physician." A knock. A page, fourteen, freckled, terrified in the way pages were permanently terrified. "His Majesty won't take his tincture this morning. He's — " the boy lowered his voice as if the walls had ears, which, in this palace, they might have — "he's talking to the chairs again."

"The chairs," Isolde said.

"The empty ones, ma'am. He's answering them."

*Answering.* Not speaking to. Answering. She filed the distinction away where she filed everything, in the drawer that had no lock because locks invited questions.

* * *

Aldric's private chamber was warm, over-warm, the fire built high though it was nearly summer. The king sat by the window in his nightclothes, an old man made older by the posture he slumped into when no one official was watching. The chairs — six of them, arranged in a half-circle facing him — were empty. Courtiers' chairs, brought up from somewhere, still bearing the crests of houses that had fallen out of favor decades ago.

"Aldric," Isolde said gently. "Your tincture."

He didn't turn. His lips were moving. She caught fragments — *yes, yes, I understand, but the terms* — and then his head tilted at an angle no human neck found comfortable, listening to something, and he said, in that other voice, the one that came from somewhere beneath his own:

*"The fire is low. We are patient. Patience is the oldest debt."*

Then Aldric again, blinking, confused, looking at the empty chairs as if he'd never seen them before. "Isolde." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man surfacing from deep water. "How long was I — "

"Not long." She pressed the cup into his hands. "Drink all of it."

He drank. He was obedient about his medicines; that was one thing she'd give him. In public he was still kingly, still sharp when it mattered, and the council had noticed nothing — or had noticed and decided a king who spoke to chairs was preferable to a king who couldn't speak at all. But the episodes were getting longer. Three seconds had become ten. Ten had become, twice last month, entire minutes during which the man sitting in front of her was not entirely the man she'd sworn, in the loose way physicians swore things, to keep alive.

"Isolde." He set down the empty cup. His hands were steady now, but his eyes weren't. "Do you ever feel that you're being *worn*? Like a coat? Like something put you on one morning and hasn't taken you off since?"

She should have written it in the log. *His Majesty experiences dissociative episodes, recommends rest.* Instead she sat down across from him, in the first of the six empty chairs, and said: "Tell me about them. The others. The ones you answer."

Something moved behind his face — relief, or fear, or both wearing each other's clothes. "You hear it too."

"I hear something."

"They ask me things." He rubbed his temples. "Old things. They want to know if the terms still hold. If the payment is still being made. And I tell them I don't know what terms, I don't know what payment, I've never — " His voice cracked. "Isolde, I have reigned thirty-one years and I cannot remember my coronation. I remember the feast after. I remember the oath, the words in my mouth. But the moment itself — the crown touching my head — there's nothing. Like a page torn out. And every year there's more torn out. My father's face. My wedding. The names of my children when they were small." He looked up at her, and the tears in his eyes were an old man's, ordinary and human and unbearable. "I am being *eaten*, physician. Slowly. Politely. With excellent bedside manner. And everyone tells me kings forget things, it's natural, it's age — "

"It's not age." She said it before she could weigh it. "Age doesn't eat in order. Age doesn't leave the coronation and take the childhoods. This is — " *selective*, she almost said. *This is a harvest.* "This is something else."

He stared at her. Then, quietly, with the dignity of a man stepping off a ledge: "You believe me."

"I believe my patients." She stood, crossed to the window, looked down at the Rose Hall where the court was dispersing, and made the decision she had probably been making since she stepped off the coach six years ago with a suitcase full of someone else's money. "Aldric. I need to ask you something strange, and I need you to answer honestly, because I think our problems may be the same problem."

"Ask."

"When you were crowned — the oath you swore. Do you remember swearing it to a god?"

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere below, a herald's bell rang the hour.

"No," Aldric said slowly. "There are no gods. There haven't been gods for a hundred years. Everyone knows — " He stopped. His hand went to his mouth. "Everyone knows," he repeated, but differently now, the way a man repeats a sentence he's just realized was taught to him rather than true. "Why do I know that so *firmly*? Why does it feel less like knowledge and more like — a wall?"

Because, Isolde thought, someone had built the wall. Someone had gone through the mind of every person in the kingdom — or the minds of kings, at least, and let the kings administer the rest — and bricked up the century. She thought of the Salt Guild's ledgers, the ones she'd glimpsed during her sale: columns of debts, entries in a hand so old it predated the current alphabet. She thought of the receipt in her strongbox, and the vial it corresponded to, and the day she could not remember, and the man whose laugh lived at the edge of the hole.

And she thought — with a cold that started in her spine and spread outward — of the timing. Her sale had been eight years ago. The king's episodes, according to the logs she'd inherited, went back nine.

*What did I pay for my future with?* she thought. *And who held the vial while I rode south?*

"Isolde?" Aldric said.

"I'm going to help you," she said. "But I need you to do exactly what I say, and I need you to trust me more than you trust your council, your priests, and possibly your own eyes. Can you do that?"

The king of the realm looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed — a real laugh, rusty, startled out of him, the first she'd heard in months. "Physician, I talk to empty chairs. Trusting my own eyes is already off the table."

* * *

That night she opened her strongbox.

The receipt lay where it had lain for eight years, soft at the folds, the Guild's wax seal intact. *One (1) memory, personal, non-transferable.* Beneath it, in the clerk's precise hand, the lot number, and beside the lot number the notation she had never let herself read closely until now, because reading closely meant asking questions, and asking questions at the Salt Guild was how people became entries instead of customers.

*Held: central archive. Status: reserved.*

Reserved. Not shelved with the general stock, not available for resale even to herself — she had inquired, years ago, politely, through intermediaries, and been told the item was unavailable. Reserved items had a purpose. Reserved items were *kept*.

She sat with the paper under lamplight and made herself follow the thread to its end. She had sold a memory to fund her escape from Greyharbor. Fine. Ordinary. The Guild bought memories the way granaries bought wheat. But the Guild did not *reserve*. The Guild traded; it did not hoard; hoarding was sentiment and sentiment was bad bookkeeping. Unless the memory wasn't stock. Unless it was collateral. Unless the day she'd sold — the day she couldn't remember, the day that mattered most — was worth something to someone beyond its market price.

Unless whoever held it knew what was in it.

She thought of the king, answering his empty chairs. *The fire is low. We are patient.* She thought of the wall in his mind where a god should have been. She thought of the word that surfaced now, unbidden, from wherever reserved things surface: *Binding.* Capital B. A word from the old ledgers, a word the Guild clerks said the way sailors said a storm's name — quietly, and only once.

Eight years ago, a clerk had taken her most precious day, sealed it in glass, and set it aside — not sold, not destroyed, *reserved* — and she had walked out of that counting house lighter and richer and certain she'd gotten the better end of the trade.

Someone had wanted that day out of her head. Someone had wanted it *held*.

Isolde put the receipt back, closed the strongbox, and blew out the lamp. In the dark of the physician's office, with the palace breathing around her and the king two floors up dreaming of torn-out pages, she allowed herself, finally, to say the name she'd been circling for six years — the name attached to the laugh at the edge of the hole, the name that had come to her once in a dream and felt less like remembering than like bleeding:

"Gideon Joubert."

The dark didn't answer. But somewhere across the city, she would learn soon enough, in a counting house full of humming glass, a man with orderly ledgers and a hole where his heart's best day used to be had just picked up a vial that hummed back.

And beneath the palace, beneath the vaults and the wine cellars and the bones of the old keep, something ancient turned in its iron cradle, felt the fire gutter one degree lower, and began — patiently, politely, with excellent bedside manner — to count the years remaining.
