## 6. Swordspeaking

The knock came at the third bell of the night, and it was wrong.

Gideon knew the sound of the counting house at night the way a man knows his own heartbeat — the settling of salt in the bins upstairs, the tick of the great brass scales cooling, the creak of Vess's chair two floors above where she sometimes slept when the ledgers shamed her into wakefulness. None of that was knocking.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. A knuckle, not a fist. The kind of knock you use when you want the person inside to think you're polite right up until you aren't.

He set down his pen mid-entry. The line read: *One vial, provenance unknown, humming.* He had not been able to finish that entry for six days. Every time he tried to write what he'd found under the harbor — the cracked seal-stone, the prayer answered wrong, the boy who remembered other lives — his hand stopped, as if the ink itself had terms and conditions.

The knock came again.

"Guild business," said a voice through the door. "Master Joubert. Open, please."

There were three of them. He could tell from the way the floorboards outside took weight — one by the door, one angled toward the window alcove, one standing back with the loose stance of a man whose job was to watch the other two's hands. Enforcers traveled in threes. It was in the Guild charter, article eleven, though the charter called them *auditors* because the men who wrote it had a sense of humor like a knife in a velvet bag.

Gideon looked at the unfinished line. *Provenance unknown.*

Then he looked at the door, and understood.

They hadn't come about the vial. They'd come about the questions. He had spent six days asking them — to the harbor master, to the fisherwoman Maren whose son had come back wearing someone else's drowned years, to the old tide-priest who wept when Gideon showed him the crack in the seal-stone and begged him, actually begged him, to stop looking. Questions were entries too. And somewhere above him, someone kept a ledger of those.

"Master Joubert." Third knock. Less polite now. "You know why we're here."

He did. That was the worst part. Some cold, orderly corner of him had known since the seal-stone, since Vess's face went gray when he mentioned it, since she said *stop asking* in a voice that wasn't warning but grief. The Guild did not send three auditors across town at the third bell for a conversation.

He could run. There was a window over the salt bins; he'd oiled its frame himself last autumn because everything in the counting house should move without complaint. He could be on the roof, then the tiles, then gone into a city that had forgotten how to notice him.

But the ledgers were here. Sixty years of them, and beneath them the older ones, the sealed ones, the ones Vess wouldn't let him touch — the Ledger of Names with his own name struck through in ink that predated his birth. If they burned the archive tonight, no one would ever know what the Guild had been keeping. What it had been *feeding*.

Gideon Joubert had given thirty years of his life to the principle that everything must be accounted for.

He opened the drawer beside his desk and took out the letter opener, which was not a weapon, and stood up straight, which was.

* * *

The door came open without being forced, which told him the man outside had a key, which told him this had been authorized above Vess's head or by her, and he made himself not decide which yet.

The auditor in front was broad, gray at the temples, with the patient face of a man who had done this many times and found it neither pleasant nor unpleasant. Work. Behind him, the window man had already moved inside — fast, quiet, boots making no sound on Gideon's own floors, which felt like a violation more intimate than a blade. The third stayed in the doorway, watching hands.

"Master Joubert." The broad one inclined his head, courteous as a headsman. "You've been asking questions outside Guild channels."

"I'm an auditor," Gideon said. "Questions are my channel."

"You've been asking about the Binding." The word landed flat and heavy, like a coin dropped on stone. Not *the seal*. Not *the harbor incident*. The Binding. Named. Which meant they weren't here to warn him off. They were here because he already knew too much to warn.

"I've been asking about a boy who drowned and didn't stay drowned," Gideon said. "If that leads to your Binding, that's the Binding's problem, not mine."

The broad one sighed, genuinely, and Gideon believed the sigh. That was the horror of the Guild — everyone in it was reasonable. Everyone followed procedure. The burning of Alexandria would have been filed under *inventory adjustment*.

"It doesn't have to hurt," the man said. "There's a vial in the case behind you. Standard extraction, standard storage. You'll lose maybe a season. The harbor, the stone, the boy. You'll keep your name, your post, your pension. Most people say afterward it's like setting down a heavy bag."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we take more than a season." No threat in it. Arithmetic. "And Master Joubert — you're a swordspeaker. Retired. We both know what's in the old oaths. So I'll ask you once, formally, before witnesses: do you consent to adjustment?"

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above, the scales ticked as they cooled. Gideon thought about the vial that hummed. About Maren's son, who had looked at Gideon with eyes that held forty years of drowning and said, in a voice like tide over rock: *They bound them with words, little clerk. Words are debts. Someone will have to pay.*

He thought about Isolde Marrec, who had walked into his office three days ago to buy back a memory and looked at him like a woman reading a book she'd loved and lost her place in. He still didn't know why that look had gone through him like a splinter working toward the heart.

"No," Gideon said. "I don't consent."

The broad one closed his eyes briefly, the way a good accountant does before writing a loss. "Then," he said, "we proceed."

* * *

They were good. That was the thing people never understood about Guild enforcers — they weren't thugs, they were craftsmen, and craftsmanship is terrifying in any trade.

The window man took his legs out from under him before Gideon had fully turned, a sweep so clean Gideon's back hit the desk and the ledger slid off it and lay open on the floor between them, pages fanning, his half-finished entry staring up at the ceiling. The broad one pinned his sword arm — not twisted, just held, controlled, professional — and the third man finally left the doorway and came forward with the extraction kit, unrolling it on the desk with the tenderness of a surgeon.

"Hold still," the third said. "It's easier if you hold still."

Gideon held still. Not because he obeyed — because some part of him, the part that had spent thirty years believing in order, wanted to see the procedure done correctly even on himself. He watched the needle-vial catch lamplight. He watched the third man find the vein at his temple, where memory lived close to the skin.

And underneath all of it, deeper than fear, something old turned over in his chest like a ship coming about.

He had sworn, twenty years ago, never again.

They make you swear it when they retire you from the swordspeakers. Not a formality — an oath, spoken over salt and iron, bound into the body. *I will keep my words cheap. I will spend nothing that cannot be repaid. I will speak as mortal men speak, and let the world turn unassisted.* Because a swordspeaker's words are not words. They are withdrawals. Every true word spoken burns something out of the speaker — a year, a face, a love, a name — and the Guild had learned, in the bad centuries, that men with that power eventually spend themselves down to nothing and become things that must be put down. So they retired their swordspeakers young. They gave them ledgers instead of blades. They taught them that accounting was a gentler magic.

Gideon had believed it. He had built a whole life on believing it.

The needle touched his temple.

Later, he would try to reconstruct the moment and fail, because the moment was the exact place where the memory he lost used to live. He knew only this much, gathered from evidence like a man reconstructing a fire from ash: there was pain, brief and total; there was the taste of iron; there was the sound of his own voice saying a word he did not recognize, in a language that predates the Guild, that predates the city, that the sea itself might have learned second.

And there was light. Not lamplight. The kind of light that has an opinion.

* * *

When he came back to himself, he was kneeling in the wreckage of his own counting house, and the three auditors were not dead, which surprised him, and were not whole, which did not.

The broad one sat against the far wall holding his sword arm at an angle arms are not meant to hold, breathing in careful whistles. The window man was simply sitting, blinking, his extraction kit spilled around him like the contents of a dropped purse, looking at Gideon with the expression of a man who has watched a colleague walk into the sea. The third — the surgeon — was pressed into the corner farthest from the desk, and he was whispering. Praying, Gideon realized. Actually praying, to gods who hadn't answered in a hundred years, which either made it pointless or, lately, made it the most rational act in the room.

The desk was split. Not broken — split, along the grain, the way wood splits when lightning finds it, and the split was blackened and smelled of ozone and old incense. His ledger lay untouched in the middle of it, open to the unfinished entry, and the ink of the last line — *provenance unknown* — had changed. It was darker. Wet-looking. As if the page had decided to agree with him.

Gideon got up. His knees complained. His hands shook. Everything else in him was terribly, perfectly calm, the calm of a scale finding its balance.

"What did I say?" he asked.

No one answered. The broad one shook his head, small movements, a man declining a dance.

"What. Did I. Say."

"You said—" The window man stopped. Started again. "We couldn't hear it. That's the thing, sir. That's the thing that's wrong. A swordspeaker's word should break windows. Should ring like a bell. Everyone knows that from the stories. But yours was—" He swallowed. "It was quiet. Like it happened underneath hearing. And then Hal's arm was wrong, and the desk was split, and none of us heard anything at all."

Underneath hearing. Gideon looked at his own mouth's work and felt the shape of the truth settle over him: the old words weren't loud because they weren't for ears. They were for the terms underneath the world. Contracts written into stone and tide and bone. When a swordspeaker spoke truly, reality didn't shatter — it *complied*.

And compliance had a price. It always had a price. He was an auditor; he knew better than anyone that nothing in this world was free, least of all power.

He took inventory of himself the way he took inventory of a shipment. Body: intact, aching. Voice: present. And then he reached for the shelf where he kept his years, the way every man keeps them, casually, assuming they'll always be there —

— and found a gap.

Not a wound. A gap. Smooth-edged, like a space where a book had been removed by someone who respected books. He probed it gently, the way you probe a missing tooth, and found the edges of it: something about a winter. Something about — a street? A festival? Lanterns? There had been lanterns. He was almost sure there had been lanterns, and someone laughing, and the specific gold color of low sun on water, and—

Nothing. Where the middle of it should have been, nothing. Burned clean. Paid out. Spent on one word he couldn't remember saying to save a life he couldn't quite remember wanting to save.

He sat down slowly on the floor among the ruins of his desk, and laughed, once, without much humor in it.

"That's the trick of it," he said to the room, to the auditors, to the Guild that had trained him and was now trying to hollow him. "They retire us because they're afraid of what we'd spend. And then they push until we spend anyway. And the joke is—" he gestured at the gap in himself, at the lanterns that weren't there anymore, "—you can't even audit the loss. The entry burns with the asset."

The broad one laughed too, wetly, cradling his arm. "Gods. You really are one of them. The old ones. Vess said you were the last, but I thought she meant the title."

Vess. The name went through Gideon like a hook through cloth.

"She sent you?"

The broad one's face did something complicated. Pity and loyalty wrestling, and pity winning, because his arm was broken and the fight was over and men tell the truth when the ledger closes. "She argued against it. Three hours, they said. In the end she signed the authorization, same as required." He paused. "But she signed it late. After the deadline. She bought you a day, Master Joubert. We came the night after the day she bought you."

A day. Vess had given him a day, and he had spent it asking questions instead of running, because he was Gideon Joubert, and order was the only god he'd ever trusted.

Well. He was done trusting gods. All of them — the silent ones under the harbor, the crowned one in the palace he was only beginning to suspect, and the paper god of the Guild with its columns and its seals and its beautiful, merciless arithmetic.

He stood. He took the ledger off the floor, closed it, and tucked it inside his coat against his ribs, where a man carries the things he cannot afford to lose.

"You'll report this," he said. It wasn't a question.

"We'll report this," the broad one agreed. "Word of power, unsanctioned. They'll strike you from the rolls. They'll send more than three, next time."

"Then I'll be gone before the ink dries." Gideon looked at each of them in turn, and found, to his mild surprise, that he meant them no harm. They were clerks of violence, following entries someone else had written. That was a sickness, but it was the Guild's sickness, and he was done treating symptoms. "Tell Vess—" He stopped. What? *Thank you*? *Why is my name struck from the Ledger of Names?* *What was I promised to the fire?*

Because that was the other thing, the thing that had surfaced in the white silence after the word, rising like a body in a harbor: he had spoken that word before. Long ago. As a child. He had the muscle-memory of it in his throat, the way you remember swimming. Someone had taught a child the word that costs a memory, and the child had paid, and the payment had been taken from him so smoothly he'd never noticed the gap until tonight.

Someone had spent him before. And the Guild had struck his name from a ledger older than his life.

"Tell her nothing," he finished. "She'll know."

He blew out the lamp — habit, thrift, the small virtues of a small life — and walked out through the door they'd unlocked, past the salt bins, past the window he'd oiled and never needed, down the stairs into a city that smelled of low tide and smoke.

Behind him, faintly, from the sealed cabinet where he kept the shipments awaiting audit, came a sound.

Humming.

The vial. Still humming. Still unanswered-for, unaccounted-for, the one entry he had never been able to close. And tonight, for the first time, Gideon could have sworn the hum had changed pitch — risen, almost, like a voice remembering a song — as if whatever slept inside the glass had felt him speak, and recognized the language.

He stood in the dark stairwell with his stolen ledger against his ribs and his missing winter aching in him like a pulled tooth, and understood that the choice had been made for him somewhere between the needle and the word.

He could not go back to the counting house. He could not go to Vess. He could not stop asking.

Which left only forward — toward the palace on the hill, where a king spoke to empty chairs, and a woman who had sold yesterday was buying it back piece by piece, and something behind a crown was very carefully, very patiently, keeping the lights of a prison burning.

Gideon pulled his coat tight and started walking.

The vial hummed behind him, all the way up the stairs, like a debt that had finally found its debtor.
