## 7. The Last Dragon-Fire

The palace had a hundred locked doors, and Isolde Marrec had keys to most of them. That was the quiet joke of her position: the court physician was beneath suspicion, beneath notice, beneath everything except the king's health — and the king's health had been failing in ways that did not fit any of her books.

She carried the lantern low, so its light pooled at her feet instead of spilling down the stair. The stair itself was not on any plan of the palace she had ever seen. She had found it three nights ago, behind the linen stores in the east wing, when she'd gone looking for cooler air and found instead a draft that moved *wrong* — not flowing down from the world above but rising up from somewhere below, warm and dry and smelling faintly of struck iron.

A draft should not be warm. A palace should not breathe.

She had told herself she would report it. She had not reported it. Instead she had spent two days memorizing the guards' rotations, learning which of them still answered to Aldric and which answered only to the crown — because those were no longer, she suspected, the same thing — and now here she was, past midnight, descending a staircase older than the dynasty that built the palace around it.

The steps were cut from black stone that predated every mason's mark she knew. Her hand trailed the wall and came away gritty with salt. Of course salt. Everything circled back to salt lately; the Guild's ledgers, the harbor seal, the vial hidden in the false bottom of her medicine chest that hummed against her palm like a trapped wasp whenever she let herself think about what she'd bought with six years of her own yesterday.

*Not yet,* she told the vial silently, as she did every night. *When this is over. When I know what I'm walking into.*

The lie of *when this is over* comforted her the way lies are meant to: cheaply.

* * *

The stair went down farther than the palace had depth. She counted two hundred steps and stopped counting after four hundred, and the whole time the air grew warmer and drier, until her lips cracked and her eyes stung and the lantern flame stood perfectly upright, as if afraid to lean.

Then the stairs ended, and Isolde understood why the fire below needed no chimney.

The vault opened before her like the inside of a bell. It was round, vast, its ceiling lost in shadow above the reach of her light, and every surface — walls, floor, the ribs of stone arcing overhead — was iron. Not cladding. Not reinforcement. Iron all the way through, poured and forged and fused into a single seamless shell, and across all of it ran lines of script so old the letters had worn soft, like words repeated ten thousand times by someone who no longer remembered their meaning.

And in the center of the floor, in a cradle of black iron wide as a fishing skiff, burned the last dragon-fire.

Isolde had seen fires her whole life. Hearth-fires, forge-fires, the great bonfires of midwinter, the green witchlight the Guild sold in bottles. None of them prepared her for this. The dragon-fire did not flicker. It *breathed*. It rose and fell in long slow swells, gold shot through with veins of white, and where its light touched the iron script the letters glowed in answer, waking one by one like sentries called to posts. The heat of it reached her across thirty feet of open floor and pressed against her skin like a hand — not cruel, not kind. *Attentive.* That was the word her mind supplied, absurdly. The fire was paying attention to her.

It was also dying. She saw it in the way the swells came shorter now, the pauses between breaths longer. In the patches of iron nearby where the script had gone dark and cold, frost creeping over letters that no longer woke. The fire guttered the way a candle gutters, and the vault — the whole vast iron shell of it — seemed to hold its breath each time the light sank, waiting to learn whether the next breath would come.

Isolde stood at the top of the last step and felt awe move through her like a tide. This was the flame from the oldest stories, the one the priests said had gone out a century ago, the one the Guild's founding charters referred to only as *the Seal*. Every prayer answered since the silence broke, every wrong thing returned from the harbor, every god stirring in its long prison — all of it traced back to this room, this cradle, this dying gold.

She thought: *I should run.*

She thought: *I will never forgive myself if I do.*

She started down onto the vault floor, one careful step at a time, boots silent on iron, and that was when she saw that the cradle was tended.

* * *

There was a figure kneeling beside the fire with its back to her, and for one merciful moment Isolde thought it was a priest — some order she'd never heard of, keeping a vigil older than the crown. Then the figure turned its head, and the light caught the thing it wore upon its brow, and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

It was the crown. Aldric's crown — she knew it, she had stood close enough to touch it at the winter court, heavy gold worked into the shapes of nine kings — and it sat on the head of a shape that was almost a man. Almost. The body beneath the robes was Aldric's build, Aldric's stoop in the shoulders, but the way it knelt was wrong, too still, too balanced, like a puppet hung correctly on strings. Its hands moved over the cradle's rim in slow deliberate strokes, smoothing the iron the way a nurse smooths a fevered brow, and wherever its fingers passed, the script brightened and the fire breathed a little easier.

It was feeding the fire. Or the fire was feeding it. Isolde could not tell which, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"Aldric?" she said, and hated how thin her voice came out.

The figure rose. It turned fully, unhurried, and the lantern light climbed it, and she saw the face.

It was the king's face. It was the king's face worn slightly badly, the way a coat fits a different man — the features right, the expression assembled rather than felt, the eyes holding a patience no living man's eyes could hold. When it spoke, the voice was Aldric's voice an octave lower, the way his voice sounded through a closed door.

"You are the physician." Not a question. "You have been counting my heartbeats. I counted yours coming down the stair. Four hundred and eleven. You slowed at the two hundredth mark, when you first heard it."

Heard *what*? She hadn't heard anything. And then, listening past her own pulse, she did: beneath the fire's breathing, beneath the groan of cooling iron, there was a sound like a voice reciting something very far away, syllables without end, patient as erosion.

"What is that sound?"

"The law," the thing said, "being read to the prisoners. It has been reading for nine hundred years. It is nearly finished." The crowned head tilted. "You came down four hundred and eleven steps in the dark to ask questions you already carry answers to. I watched you find the stair. I watch everyone. That is what a warden is."

"Warden," Isolde repeated. The word tasted of ash. "Of the fire?"

Something moved across the borrowed face — not a smile, but the place where a smile would go, the way a scar marks where a wound was. "Child. Look again."

She looked. And this time she made herself see what her healer's eye had flinched from: the way the fire's breathing hitched, rhythmic and labored, like lungs working around damage. The way the veins of white pulsed in time with a heartbeat far too slow. The way the iron cradle was not shaped like a brazier at all, she realized now, but like a ribcage — great curved bars arching over the light, and the light pressing against them from within.

The fire was not a fire. The fire was *caged*, and the cage was the vault, and the vault was the kingdom, and every prayer answered in the city above was a crack spreading through bars that had held for nine centuries.

"It burns," Isolde said slowly, "because it is alive."

"Dragons were alive once," the warden said. "This is what remains of the last one. My brother-in-flame, they called it, in a language your tongue cannot survive. We caught it at the end of the war and folded its fire into a seal, and its burning became the binding, and its dying will become the unbinding. Nine hundred years I have kept it burning. Fed it. Tended it." The hands spread, showing her the palms, and she saw they were marked all over with old burn scars in the shapes of letters. "Do you understand what I am telling you, physician? I am not the jailer standing outside the cell. There is no outside. I am the lock, and the lock must love the door."

"And the king?" Her voice came out flat and clinical, the voice she used to deliver deaths. "Where is Aldric while you wear him?"

"Gone deep. Asleep in the rooms of himself I have not needed. I take a little each day — his memories, his certainties, the small warm weight of being a particular man. Memory is the finest fuel there is. A king's memory best of all; it is dense with consequence." The warden resumed its slow circuit of the cradle, stroking the iron, and the fire leaned toward its hands like a plant toward sun. "He fights me less than you would expect. There is a mercy in forgetting. Ask yourself, physician — you who sold a day of your own life to buy your future — whether he is not the lucky one."

The words went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. It knew. It had read her the way she'd read the harbor seal — casually, completely, the way one reads a ledger. Her hand rose without her willing it toward her chest, toward the flat shape of nothing, because the vial was safe at home in its hiding place, and still she felt it humming against her sternum like a second heartbeat.

"You know about the Guild," she said.

"I know about everything the Guild has ever written down. The Guild keeps my ledgers. They believe they employ themselves." The warden paused at the far side of the cradle, firelight crawling up the crown's nine points. "They were founded to administer the debts of the bound gods. Interest accrues. Prayers are collateral. For a century the accounts lay dormant, and then the fire began to fail, and the creditors woke, and now the payments flow again — every answered prayer a withdrawal against a prison wall." It looked at her over the dying gold. "You want to save your king. I can see it. It is the largest thing in you. Larger than your fear of me. Larger even than the other large thing, the one you keep in glass."

"Then hear me," Isolde said, and stepped closer, close enough that the heat pressed her clothes against her body and the sweat sprang hot on her spine. "If the fire dies, what happens? Tell me plainly. You owe me that much for the fright."

"Plainly, then." The warden's borrowed face went very still. "When the last flame gutters out, the seal breaks, and the gods walk free — all of them, the gentle ones and the hungry ones alike, nine hundred years of accumulated grievance wearing bodies of storm and stone. Your city has forgotten how to fear them. Your laws were built in their absence. It will be a season of settling accounts such as the world has never survived." A pause. "Unless the fire is fed. Not wood. Not oil. Memory. Willing memory, freely given, burns longest of all. A kingdom's worth would hold the seal another age."

The horror arrived quietly, the worst ones always did. She thought of the emptied servants in the upper halls, the ones who smiled too readily now. She thought of Aldric speaking to empty chairs. She thought of a fisherwoman's son walking out of the harbor remembering other lives — other lives spent, spent *somewhere*, spent as fuel.

"You've been feeding it people."

"I have been feeding it volunteers," the warden said, "who did not know they were volunteering. It is a distinction the Guild would appreciate. Everything in their books is consensual, technically. That is what makes it lawful." The crowned head lowered, and for a moment — one strange, vertiginous moment — the thing behind the king's eyes seemed almost tired. "I have kept this fire alive since before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. I have committed every sin a warden can commit, and I would commit them all again, because the alternative is the end of your world's long afternoon. Do not mistake me for a monster, physician. Monsters have appetites. I have only arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Isolde whispered. Somewhere above her, impossibly far away, a city slept, full of people whose names were entries in books kept by a lock that loved its door. "You're going to starve the whole kingdom to feed one dying animal."

"One dying animal," the warden agreed, "whose death kills everything else. Yes. That is the sum. Show me your better figures."

And she couldn't. That was the terrible part, standing in the heat with her principles melting like tallow — she couldn't, not cleanly, not honestly. If the choice were only numbers, the warden might even be right. But the choice was never only numbers. The choice was a boy walking out of the sea with dead men's memories behind his eyes. The choice was a king alone in a room with chairs. The choice was a woman who had sold a single day and been paying for it ever since, in a currency no ledger recorded.

"There is a third figure you haven't entered," she said. "What happens if the fire is put out gently. On purpose. By the ones it holds — not escaping, but *released*, with terms. With witnesses. With—" she groped for the word and found it, the word Gideon Joubert used when he talked about his cursed profession, the word that had always sounded like a prayer wearing a clerk's coat— "*amnesty.*"

The warden went utterly still.

For a long moment the only sounds were the fire's laboring breath and the far-off endless recitation of the law, and Isolde stood her ground on the cooling iron and made herself meet the thing's borrowed eyes.

Then the warden laughed. It was Aldric's laugh, ruined, played at the wrong speed, and it echoed off the iron vault like something falling down a well.

"Amnesty," it said. "Nine hundred years, and the mortals send me a physician with a word." The crowned head shook slowly. "You cannot amnesty a fire, child. But you may be able to answer a question I have carried since the Binding: whether anything born of your brief warm species can hold a debt that large without breaking." It turned back to the cradle, hands resuming their tender, terrible ministrations. "Go home, Isolde Marrec. Sleep. Count your king's heartbeats tomorrow as usual. And consider —" the voice dropped, almost gentle, almost Aldric's own — "that the Guild's last oath-bound swordspeaker has begun asking dangerous questions. When he comes to you, and he will, decide quickly whose side of the iron you stand on."

Her blood went cold despite the furnace-heat. "Gideon."

"He carries a name that was struck from every ledger before he could walk," the warden said, stroking the dying fire. "Names like that burn beautifully. Go. Before I remember that wardens are permitted to tidy."

Isolde climbed the four hundred and eleven steps without counting them, and the whole way up, against her palm, against her sternum, against the inside of her skull, she could feel the vial humming — and for the first time she could not tell whether it was calling to her, or warning her, or simply reading aloud, in its small glass voice, the newest entry in a book she had never agreed to sign.
