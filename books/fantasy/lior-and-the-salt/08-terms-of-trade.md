## 8. Terms of Trade

The sealed ledgers lived on the ninth floor of the counting house, behind a door that had no handle.

Gideon had walked past it every working day for eleven years. He knew its dimensions the way he knew the dimensions of everything in the Guild — four feet wide, seven tall, salt-black wood that drank the lamplight instead of returning it. He had never once seen it open. When he was an apprentice, Vess had told him the room beyond was empty, kept for tradition's sake, and Gideon had believed her because believing Vess was the closest thing to prayer his life contained.

Now he stood in front of it at the hour before dawn, when even the auditors who slept at their desks had gone home, and he said, "I know you're watching."

The corridor did not answer. The Guild rarely answered. It recorded.

"I found my name," he said. "Struck through. In a ledger older than my apprenticeship, in ink I didn't write and a hand I don't recognize. And I've spent six days pretending that's a bookkeeping error, because the alternative is that someone entered me into something before I ever agreed to be entered." He pressed his palm flat against the door. The wood was cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather. "You taught me that every debt has two sides, Vess. So here's mine: open this door, or I walk out of the Guild with what I know and start telling it to people who still pray."

Silence. Then, from the dark end of the corridor, the sound of a key turning in a lock that should not have existed — because there was no lock, only the door — and Vess came walking into the lamplight with her coat over her nightclothes and her silver hair unbound, which in forty years he had never seen.

"You'd really do it," she said. Not a question. She was studying him the way she studied a ledger that wouldn't balance: not with anger, but with the particular grief of a woman who could see exactly where the column went wrong.

"You raised me on the rule," Gideon said. "Never bluff a counterparty who knows your books."

"I did raise you on that rule." She stopped three paces away. Up close, without the armor of her office, she looked old in a way that frightened him more than anything under the harbor had. "So let's not waste the night pretending either of us doesn't know why you're here. You want the ledgers of the Binding."

"I want to read them."

"No one reads them. That is their entire function. They are not records, Gideon. They are *restraints*. Every page is a clause in a contract older than the kingdom, and the kingdom is one of the smaller parties involved." Her voice stayed level, professional, and that was how he knew how bad it was — Vess got calm in direct proportion to catastrophe. "You crack those covers, and you're not a man reading history anymore. You're a signatory."

"Then I'm already one." He took his hand off the door. "My name is in them, Vess. Struck through. Someone made me party to this before I could hold a pen. I'm owed an accounting. The Guild itself says so — Article One, the Founding Principle: *every entry may be audited by the party named therein.*"

Something moved behind her eyes. Not surprise. Recognition — the look of a teacher whose student has finally used the lesson against her.

"You've been rereading the charter," she said.

"I've been rereading everything. That's the problem, isn't it? That's why my name got struck in the first place." He stepped closer. "Open the door."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I go to the fisherwoman's son and ask him what he remembers from before his own life. Because he remembers, Vess. He remembers the Binding from the inside, and he's eight years old, and right now the only person protecting him from the Guild's attention is me."

That landed. He watched it land — watched her glance down the empty corridor as though the walls themselves might be taking minutes.

"The Guild knows about the boy?" she asked quietly.

"The Guild knows about everything. That's what the Guild is for." He held her gaze. "So here are my terms."

Vess exhaled slowly. "Terms. From you." A ghost of the old warmth crossed her face and was gone. "You used to bring me your first audits like offerings. Now you're negotiating with me in a hallway at dawn."

"People change when they find out they're collateral."

"They do," she said. "Worse things than that happen to them. Say your terms."

He had rehearsed them all night, standing at his desk with the humming vial locked in his drawer and the harbor-stone's crack drawn in charcoal on the back of his hand where he couldn't lose it. But saying them aloud, in front of the door, made them real in a way rehearsal never did.

"One," he said. "Full access. Unsupervised. I read what I need, copy what I need, and neither you nor anyone else in the Guild alters, annotates, or *balances* my findings while I work."

"Granted," Vess said immediately, and the speed of it told him she'd expected worse.

"Two. The boy — Tam, his name is Tam — goes onto the protected rolls. Full sanctuary status. If the crown-thing or the Guild or anything else comes for him, the Guild stands between."

This time she paused. "Sanctuary for an unregistered memory-bearer. That's not a term, Gideon, that's a declaration of war against whatever wears Aldric's crown. You understand that once we enter it in the rolls, we've taken a side."

"We took a side the day the prayers started being answered again. We just haven't admitted it yet."

"Granted," she said, after a moment. "Provisionally. Pending ratification by the full bench, which will take a day and cost me three favors I was saving for a funeral."

"What funeral?"

"Mine, if this continues. Your third term?"

And here was the one he hadn't wanted to say. Here was the one that had sat in his chest all night like a stone swallowed whole.

"My oath," Gideon said. "As collateral."

Vess went very still.

"Swordspeakers swear twice," he said. "Once to the Guild, once to the word itself. Everyone thinks the second oath is just ceremony — the old magic died with the last generation, everyone said so, I said so. But it isn't dead. I spoke a true word six nights ago, in the salt-yard, when the enforcers came. I felt it burn something out of me to do it." His voice stayed steady. He was proud of that, later. "So. My swordspeaker's oath, formally lodged as security against my conduct. If I misuse what I find in those ledgers — if I break the Binding rather than mend it, if I act outside the terms you set — the Guild holds my oath. And you know what that means."

"It means the Guild can call it due," Vess said. "It means we can compel you. It means—" She stopped. For the first time since the corridor began, her composure cracked, and what showed underneath was not the auditor but the woman who had found a nameless child on the Guild steps twenty-nine years ago and entered him into the world with her own pen. "Gideon. Do you understand what you're offering? Your oath isn't a signature. It's the load-bearing wall of who you are. Everything you trust about yourself — your discipline, your word, the shape of your honor — it all stands on that oath. Surrender it as collateral and you're not pledging an asset. You're pledging the foundation."

"I know what it is."

"Do you? Because I watched the last man who lodged his oath as security. Sorrel Vance. You never met him; we struck him from the teaching rolls before your time. He pledged his oath in a negotiation very much like this one, over a ledger very much like these, and the Guild called it due inside a year, and what was left afterward—" She shook her head. "What was left could stand in a room and agree with you, and there was nothing behind the agreement. No weight. A man-shaped hole where a conviction used to be."

"Then the Guild had better hope I keep my terms," Gideon said. "Because I intend to keep them, and I intend to make sure the Guild keeps its side too. That's what collateral is, Vess. It's not surrender. It's a hostage both sides can see."

For a long moment she simply looked at him. The lamps guttered. Somewhere below them, in the vaults, he imagined he could hear the faint hum of bottled memories — or maybe just the blood in his own ears.

"You sound like the founding auditors," she said at last. "Did you know that? The ones who built this place. They talked about collateral the way priests talk about communion." She reached into her coat and drew out a ring of keys — actual iron, actual teeth, the first keys he had ever seen leave her body. "There's a form for this. Of course there's a form. There's always a form. Come to my office."

* * *

The form was called a Lodgment of Personal Covenant, and it was written on paper made from saltmarsh reeds that had been dead for two hundred years. Vess filled in the header herself, in the old script, her pen moving with the unhurried precision of a woman performing a rite she had hoped never to perform.

Gideon read every line. She noticed, and something in her expression softened.

"Good," she said. "Read it all. Most signatories don't."

Clause by clause, it gave the Guild what he had offered: custody of his swordspeaker's oath, held in escrow against his conduct, callable upon breach. Clause nine specified the conditions of default. Clause twelve specified what happened upon call — and Gideon read clause twelve three times, because Vess had not exaggerated. Upon call, the Guild was entitled to *compel performance of any act consistent with the original oath*, and the signatory would experience such compulsion as *his own most certain conviction.* They wouldn't force him. They would simply reach into him and rearrange what he was sure of.

"That's obscene," he said quietly.

"That's contract law," Vess said. "Everything sacred becomes obscene eventually. It's what happens when institutions outlive the fear that built them." She set down the pen. "Last chance, Gideon. Walk away. Keep your name, keep your oath, keep the comfortable lie that the Guild is a warehouse and not a warden. I'll even strike your name from tonight's attendance record myself."

"And the ledgers stay shut. And Tam stays exposed. And the thing wearing the king keeps eating him alive while we audit shipments." He picked up the pen. "No."

He signed. The reed-paper drank the ink the way the door drank light. Where his name dried, it looked less written than *branded* — and beneath it, in a space he hadn't noticed until the ink touched, a second line appeared in handwriting that was not his and not Vess's: a long, looping notation in the old script that he could not read, though some part of him understood it anyway, the way you understand a fall.

Vess saw it too. She went white.

"That's not supposed to happen," she whispered. "That line hasn't appeared on a lodgment in—"

"In how long?"

"Since Sorrel Vance." She closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, she was Vess again — the auditor, the warden of small catastrophes, the only mother he had ever been able to itemize. "It means the ledgers have accepted your pledge. It means they're awake enough to countersign." She gathered the form with hands that were almost steady. "Ninth floor. Don't touch anything you haven't read. And Gideon—" She hesitated at the office door, her back to him. "When you find what you're going to find in there. Remember that I struck your name to protect you, not to erase you. Whatever else is true, remember that."

She left. Gideon stood alone in the office with the taste of ink in his mouth and a strange hollowness in his chest, as though something essential had been carried out of the room along with the paper — as though he had handed over not a document but an organ, and his body had not yet learned to compensate.

He flexed his sword-hand. It worked. It felt like his. But somewhere, in a vault or a shelf or a black-wood room behind a handleless door, the thing that made his word *true* now sat in escrow, waiting to see what kind of man he proved to be.

* * *

The door opened for him.

No key, no effort — he pressed his palm where a handle should have been and the salt-black wood swung inward without a sound, and the air that came out was old in a way that made the harbor-stone's breath seem young. Cold, mineral, patient. The air of a room that had been holding its breath for a century.

Lamps lit themselves as he descended the spiral stair — not flame but a pale, steady glow, like moonlight trapped in glass. The shelves went down farther than the building had floors. And the ledgers — the ledgers were not books, not really. They were slabs of bound vellum thick as tombstones, chained to their shelves with iron that had been braided, not forged, each chain ending in a clasp shaped like an open hand.

He found the first one by instinct. THE BINDING OF THE NINE, the spine said, in letters that shifted slightly when he wasn't looking directly at them. The hand-clasp released at his touch, warm as skin, and the cover fell open on its own to a page near the middle, as if the book had been waiting years to show him this exact place.

The heading read: *Collateral Accepted.*

Below it, a list of names. Some struck through. Some not. And there, third from the top, in ink that had faded to the color of old blood:

*Sorrel Vance — oath lodged, oath called, remainder archived.*

And beneath Vance, in fresher ink, in a hand he recognized because he had just made it himself:

*Gideon Joubert — oath lodged.*

And beneath that, in the same impossible hand that had appeared on the lodgment form, a single line of the old script, translating itself in his mind the moment he tried to look away:

*Pending: the name entire.*

He stood in the cold light of the buried library with his own signature drying somewhere above him, and understood, finally and completely, what he had done. Not sold his oath. Pledged it — the way a farmer pledges a field, the way a king pledges a crown. Collateral that could be called. A self that could be collected.

Somewhere far above, dawn was coming up over the harbor, and the cracked seal-stone was breathing its slow wrong breath, and a boy named Tam was waking in a safehouse bed with other people's lives behind his eyes.

Gideon turned the page.

The next heading read: *Terms of the Original Contract — Nine Parties, One Fire.*

He began to read.
