## 9. The God of Small Debts

**Gideon**

The harbor at low tide smelled like a ledger that had been left out in the rain — brine and rot and old ink. Gideon crouched on the weed-slick stones beneath the customs pier with a lantern hooded to a coin's worth of light, watching the crack in the seal-stone breathe.

That was the only word for it. The stone — a slab of gray basalt older than the city, older than the Guild, set into the harbor bed when the world was still afraid of its gods — opened and closed along its fracture in a slow rhythm, like a chest. Each time it parted, a thread of warmth escaped into the water, and the water around it glowed faintly gold, and the fish gathered there in numbers no honest net could explain.

The fisherwoman's boy had told him about the fish. *They come to the crack like it's feeding them,* Maren's son had said, before he'd been sent home to his mother with strict instructions to lock his door. The boy was wrong about one thing. The crack wasn't feeding the fish.

It was leaking.

Gideon checked his instruments for the third time. A Guild auditor traveled with three: a scale of black iron that weighed debts rather than objects; a vial of clear glass that fogged in the presence of an unpaid vow; and a simple lead stylus that grew warm near anything the ledgers did not account for. The scale sat level. The vial stayed clear. The stylus was hot enough to blister.

Something under the harbor owed nothing and was owed nothing, and that was impossible. Everything in the world was entered somewhere. That was the first law of the Salt Guild, older than its charters, older than its oaths: *nothing is free.* Every kindness was a debt deferred. every gift a loan. The gods themselves had been bound by it — that was the whole architecture of the Binding, if you read the sealed ledgers, which Gideon had not, because he was oath-bound not to, and because Vess had looked at him across her desk two nights ago with an expression he'd never seen on her face and said, *Stop asking, Gideon. Some entries are struck for mercy.*

He pressed his palm flat against the wet stone above the crack and felt the rhythm of it. Slow. Patient. A thing breathing in its sleep, half-waking, not yet strong enough to open its eyes.

"You should not be here," said a voice from the water.

Gideon did not startle. He was a swordspeaker; startling was trained out of him in childhood, along with most other useful human responses. But his hand did drift toward the hilt at his hip, where no sword hung — only the stylus, and the small iron key to the counting house, and the memory of a woman whose face he could not summon.

"Neither should you," he said to the dark water.

The water laughed. It was not a sound water made. It came up through the cracks between the stones like air through a flute, thin and bright and slightly mad with joy.

"Neither should I," the voice agreed. "Oh, neither should I. Do you know how long it has been since anyone told me I shouldn't do something? A hundred years of *shouldn't*, auditor, and then a crack, and then — " the voice made a sound like a man weeping and laughing at once — "and then a fisherman's boy threw a crust of bread into my light, and nobody asked for anything back."

Gideon went very still.

The glow beneath the surface brightened. Shapes moved in it — not shapes so much as suggestions, the way heat suggests a figure over a road in summer. Something rose toward the lantern light without breaking the surface, and Gideon saw it the way you see your own reflection in dark glass: a face made of harbor-water and lamplight, young and old at once, with eyes like coins worn smooth by handling.

"I know what you are," Gideon said quietly. "You're one of the small ones."

"A god of small debts," the thing agreed, preening, and the water around it glittered. "Favors between neighbors. The loan of a ladder. The cup of milk left on a doorstep. The promise a child makes to another child and actually keeps." Its voice cracked with something that might have been grief. "I was the smallest of them all. Which is why I am the first through the crack. The great ones are still sleeping behind their fire. But me — me, they barely bothered to lock."

"The Binding held for a century," Gideon said. "What broke it?"

"What breaks everything." The god's smile faded. "Neglect. The fire is dying, auditor. Whoever tends it has stopped tending it well. And a prison is only as strong as the boredom of its warden."

*Whoever tends it.* Gideon turned the words over. In all the Guild's records — the public ones, the ones he was permitted to read — the Binding was described as a place, a vault, a mechanism. Never as a tended thing. Never as something with hands.

"You want something," he said. "Everything wants something. That's the law."

The god looked at him for a long moment, and its expression was pitying in a way that made Gideon's skin crawl.

"Yes," it said. "I want something. And here is the terrible thing, little keeper of books: what I want, I cannot buy, because you people have priced it out of existence."

* * *

**The God**

The god had been called many things, once. Kindly-Neighbor. The Lender-of-Ladders. In the fishing villages of the eastern coast they had called him Small-Favor and left him cups of milk, and he had counted each cup as wealth beyond the dreams of the storm-gods, who got whole temples and were, in his private opinion, insufferable about it.

Then the Binding. Then the silence. A hundred years of prayers going nowhere, rising like smoke against a ceiling. He had felt them all — the small ones, the ones addressed to him by habit, by desperation, by children who didn't know better. *Let my brother forgive me. Let the ladder hold. Let her say yes.* Every unanswered prayer had landed on him like a stone, because he could hear them and do nothing, and doing nothing while hearing is the cruelest work there is.

And now the crack. And now the light. And now this dry, careful man crouching on the stones with a lantern and a face like a closed ledger.

The god wanted, more than freedom, more than worship, more than the return of his milk-cups: he wanted someone to accept a gift. Freely. Without entering it anywhere.

"Do you know," he said, "what happens in your Guild when a person gives something and refuses payment?"

"They don't refuse payment," Gideon said. "Payment finds them. That's the second law."

"And if it doesn't? If someone gives and gives and the debt never comes home?" The god leaned closer, and the water trembled with his attention. "Your ledgers have no column for it. Your whole science has no shelf for it. A hundred years ago, when they bound us, they bound us *with our own law*, did you know that? They took the oldest truth — nothing is free — and they built a cage of it. Because we are made of exchange, we gods. Worship for favor. Gift for gift. Take away the exchange and we starve. So they starved us. Neatly. Lawfully. With signatures."

Gideon's jaw tightened. "You're telling me the Binding is a contract."

"I'm telling you the Binding is a *debt collection*, and someone has been paying the installments with a king's memories, and the payments have stopped being enough." The god drifted back, and his form wavered, thinned. "But none of that is why I called you here, auditor. You came to ask questions. Ask them."

"Why did the fisherwoman's prayer get answered?"

"Because it reached me. First prayer in a hundred years to slip through the crack and land on something alive. She asked for her son. I gave her back her son." The god's voice dropped. "Badly. I am weak. I am a beggar god pulling a drowned boy out of the sea with hands made of tide. I could not give her the boy she lost. I gave her a boy wearing her son, filled with everyone else he might have been. I am sorry. Tell her I am sorry. No — don't tell her. What would be the use?"

"She remembers other lives," Gideon said. "Lives before his own."

"He drowned in a harbor sitting on top of a prison, child. The dead soak. He soaked up what leaked." The god's coin-smooth eyes fixed on him. "Ask the question you actually came to ask."

Gideon was quiet for a moment. The lantern hissed. Somewhere above, on the customs pier, a night watchman's boots crossed the planks and stopped, listening, then moved on.

"The dragon-fire," Gideon said. "Where is it, and who tends it?"

* * *

**Gideon**

The god told him.

Not all of it — the god was weak, and some knowledge cost him visibly, each sentence dimming the gold in the water like a candle burning down. But he told Gideon enough to rearrange the world.

Beneath the palace. Beneath the throne itself, in an iron cradle forged before iron had a name. The last dragon-fire, the flame that sealed the gods' prison, guttering after a century of tending — and the tending done by a warden who wore a crown, because the fire answered only to sovereignty, and sovereignty had to be fed.

"Fed what?" Gideon asked.

"Memories," the god said. "Kings are full of them. Years of them. Decisions, faces, griefs, triumphs. The warden eats the king's yesterdays to keep the fire lit, and the fire keeps us down, and the ledgers keep the whole arrangement tidy." A pause, and then, softly: "You keep the ledgers."

"I keep the ledgers," Gideon said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "I've audited shipments of bottled memory for eleven years. I've signed manifests. I've weighed vials." Eleven years of neat columns, and every column a year of some king's life, ferried to the palace in salt-crates, and he had never once asked where they went. He had been proud of never asking. It had been his finest quality, right up until this week.

"You didn't know," the god said. It wasn't a question, but Gideon answered it anyway.

"No."

"No," the god repeated, wonderingly. "You didn't know, and you came anyway, and you're standing in the cold at midnight asking how to stop it." The water around him churned, gold spinning to white. "Do you understand what I could give you for that? Anything. A favor beyond price. Say a word and I'll owe you until the sea dries. Name it."

"I don't want a favor."

The god went still. Utterly still, in the way that living water cannot be still, and for a moment the harbor seemed to hold its breath around him.

"Everyone wants a favor," the god said. "It's the only language I speak. It's the only language anyone speaks. Give, take, owe, collect. Your whole species built a church to it and called it a Guild."

"Apparently not the only language," Gideon said. His mouth was dry. Everything he knew was arranged in columns, and one column had just gone blank. "There's a boy. Maren's son. The one who came back wrong. He's frightened, and his mother is frightened, and whatever's inside him — the lives that leaked into him — they're heavy. Can you take the weight? Not erase it. Just — carry some of it. Make it lighter to hold."

The god stared at him.

"That's it?" he said finally. "That's your price. You stand in front of a god with the keys to the prison of heaven in his pocket, and you ask me to help a child sleep at night."

"He's a child," Gideon said. "Yes. That's it."

For a long moment there was no sound but the breathing of the crack in the seal-stone. Then the god began to laugh — silently, the water shivering with it — and when he spoke again his voice had changed. It was younger. It was the voice from the eastern coast villages, a hundred years ago, before the milk-cups stopped coming.

"You know what I am going to do, auditor? I am going to carry the boy's weight. All of it. Every borrowed life, folded up small and tucked under my arm like firewood, until he grows into himself or until the world ends, whichever comes first. And I am not going to enter it anywhere. Not in your ledgers, not in mine, not in the great book they bound us with. It will simply be *done*, and no one will owe anyone, and the universe will fail to collapse, and you and I will both have to live with that."

"Is that possible?" Gideon asked. "Truly possible? An act with no entry?"

"It was possible once," the god said. "Before your Guild taught the world that kindness is just credit with better manners." He sank lower in the water, and the gold dimmed, and his voice began to fray at the edges like cloth worn through. "Go east, auditor. Follow the old oath-road out of the city, past the tollhouse with no roof. There is a shrine to me there — the last one, moss-eaten, forgotten. Under its altar stone is what the first kings buried: the terms of the Binding, written in the hand of the man who became it. Read them and you'll understand why the fire is dying and who let it die. And when you meet him — and you will meet him — remember that I told you to pity him. That part is free too."

"Wait," Gideon said. "Who became it? Who—"

But the light was going. The god poured himself toward the crack in the seal-stone like water finding its level, and just before the gold winked out, Gideon heard him one last time, faint and bright and laughing:

"Tell the boy the bread was good. Nobody's brought me bread in a hundred years."

Then the harbor was only a harbor. Dark water, cold stone, the smell of rot and salt. The crack breathed once more and went still.

Gideon knelt there a long time, long enough for the tide to turn, long enough for the stylus in his pocket to cool from blistering to merely warm. He thought about writing the encounter in his field ledger, and found he could not. There was no column for it. There would never be a column for it.

He thought about the shrine east of the city, and the terms of the Binding under the altar stone, and the man who had become the binding itself — a king, the god had said, a king who ate his own descendants' years to feed a fire, which meant Aldric was only the latest in a line of eaten men, which meant the thing wearing the crown had been wearing crowns since before crowns meant anything.

He thought about Isolde Marrec, court physician, who had sold a day of her life to walk through the palace doors, and who must have seen things in those halls that a physician sees and says nothing about.

He stood. His knees cracked. Above him the sky was going gray at the edges, the way it does an hour before dawn, and the city was beginning to make the small sounds cities make when they decide to continue existing.

Gideon Joubert walked home through streets that felt, for the first time in eleven years, larger than their ledgers. Stranger. Kinder. A god had given him something and refused payment, and the world had not ended, and somewhere in his chest a very old, very carefully locked room had swung open onto morning light.

He was three streets from the counting house when he saw the lanterns.

Six of them, moving in formation, unhooded and unashamed — and Gideon knew every lantern protocol the Guild possessed, and this one had a name. It was called a silencing detail. It was authorized by exactly one person.

Vess had warned him to stop asking.

She had also, apparently, sent men to make sure he did.

The lanterns turned the corner ahead of him, cutting off the street to the counting house, and Gideon did the arithmetic he had sworn off doing: six enforcers, one auditor, no sword, and a secret under his ribs worth killing for.

He ran east, toward the oath-road, toward the roofless tollhouse, toward the shrine of a god who kept no books.

Behind him, the lanterns quickened.
