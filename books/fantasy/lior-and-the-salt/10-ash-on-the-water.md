## 10. Ash on the Water

The palace smelled wrong before anything else went wrong.

Isolde Marrec had spent six years learning the palace the way a physician learns a body — by its smells, its sounds, the small failures that precede the large ones. She knew the tallow-and-limestone breath of the lower corridors, the beeswax sweetness of the council chambers, the faint iron tang of the old wells. She knew which kitchens burned rosemary on fast days and which drains backed up when the tide turned.

What she did not know was ash.

Not hearth-ash, not incense. This was finer, whiter, and it came from nowhere — drifting through sealed corridors at dawn, settling on banisters and window ledges in a film so fine it looked like frost. She rubbed a little between her fingers in the east gallery and it did not smudge. It clung to her skin like static, like something alive deciding where to land.

She had been awake since the third bell anyway. She was always awake now, since the vault, since she'd seen the iron cradle and the fire guttering inside it like a lamp running out of oil, and the tall figure in the king's crown tending it with hands that moved too gently for anything wearing Aldric's face.

*It knows I saw,* she'd told herself every night since. *Of course it knows. It wears his memories. It will wear mine next.*

So when the ash began falling, she did not wonder what it meant. She began counting exits.

* * *

The first of them emptied at breakfast.

Not died — Isolde would have preferred dying, afterward, for all of them. The under-steward Brannoc had served the palace for thirty years; he'd once carried Isolde up three flights of stairs when she collapsed with fever in her first winter, and he'd joked about her weight the whole way, badly, kindly. At breakfast he stood in the servants' hall doorway holding a tray of bread he no longer needed to carry anywhere, because everyone in the hall was already standing, already facing him, already waiting.

"His Grace is well," Brannoc said. "His Grace loves you."

Thirty voices answered as one: *"His Grace loves us."*

Isolde stood frozen near the buttery door with a cup of watered wine going warm in her hand. She watched Brannoc's face as he spoke. That was the worst part, later — that she kept watching faces. His eyes were open. They were even, in some terrible mechanical sense, focused. But there was nothing behind them looking out. It was like watching a house after the family has gone: windows clean, shutters straight, no one home.

The hall emptied itself in perfect order. Dishes were cleared. Floors were swept. And everywhere she walked that morning, she found the same litany being passed along the corridors like a bucket line fighting a fire:

*"His Grace is well. His Grace loves you."*

By noon they had stopped saying it only when prompted. By evening they said it in their sleep.

* * *

She tried to save one. She was a physician; saving was the whole shape of her life, and she could not stand in her own dispensary grinding valerian for sleepless courtiers while her patients were being hollowed out one corridor at a time.

Sella was a laundry girl, fourteen, gap-toothed, forever singing off-key. Isolde found her at dusk in the linen room, folding sheets with the precision of a machine, whispering the litany between folds.

"Sella." She took the girl's hands. They were cold. "Look at me. Sella. It's Isolde. You brought me comfrey last month when my hands were cracked, remember? You said the palace herbs were better than any physician's—"

Sella looked up. For half a heartbeat something surfaced in those eyes — fear, recognition, a drowning girl's hand breaking water — and her mouth shaped itself around a word that might have been *help* or might have been Isolde's name.

Then the ash came.

Isolde never saw where it entered from. The room's one window was shuttered. But the air above Sella's head thickened, whitened, and fine pale flakes drifted down onto the girl's hair like a blessing, like snow, like something tender, and Isolde felt the change come up through the girl's hands into her own — a tremor, then stillness, then a warmth that had no source.

"His Grace is well," Sella said, smiling. It was the first time Isolde had seen the emptied ones smile, and she would give years of her life never to see it again. "His Grace loves you, mistress. Why are you crying? His Grace loves you too."

Isolde let go of her hands and walked out of the linen room at an even pace, because running would draw attention, because attention was death, because if she broke stride she would break entirely. In the corridor she pressed her back against cold stone and put her own hand over her mouth and made no sound at all, and the ash settled on her shoulders like a patient hand.

* * *

That night she went to the king's wing.

She told herself it was for proof. Proof mattered; she had learned that much from the Guild, from Gideon Joubert and his beautiful terrible ledgers — nothing was real until it was written down, nothing could be fought until it was named. If she escaped this place, if she reached the city, if she found him again — and she would find him, the thought arrived with a heat that embarrassed her — she would need more than her word. Her word was a physician's word, and physicians who spoke of crowned things tending fires beneath palaces were called mad, and madwomen were easy to empty.

So: proof. She carried her physician's satchel and inside it, wrapped in flannel, a small glass vial of the kind the Salt Guild used for memories — she had bought three, years ago, from a smuggler in the salt-market, and had never known why until tonight. Some part of her had been preparing for this since the cradle-vault. Some part of her had always known what lived under palaces.

The king's wing was guarded by men she knew, and that was the horror of it — Ser Ovelin, who taught pages to fish; young Tam, who blushed around the kitchen girls. Both stood at the corridor's mouth with the stillness of furniture, and both said, together, without turning their heads:

"His Grace is well."

"I know," Isolde said softly, and walked between them, and neither stopped her, because she was not a threat. She was not yet a threat. The thing in the crown had not decided what she was.

Aldric's chamber doors stood open.

He sat by the window in the dark, in his nightshirt, an old man's shawl over his shoulders, looking out at the harbor lights. For one wild moment Isolde thought — *he's back, whatever wore him has gone, he's just an old sick man alone* — and she crossed the room and knelt beside him and took his hand.

"Aldric," she whispered. "Your Grace. Can you hear me?"

His fingers tightened on hers. His eyes stayed on the harbor.

"He watches the water," Aldric said. His voice was his own, cracked and old, and that was worse than the other voice, somehow — the other voice had been a stranger's; this was a man using his last strength to report from inside his own siege. "It likes the water. It came up from under the water, physician. Did you know that? Before the crown. Before the fire. It was small then." A wet, rattling laugh. "Everything was smaller then. Even the gods."

"Your Grace, I'm going to help you. There are people — there's a man who keeps accounts of impossible things, he'll know how to—"

"It counts my years," Aldric said. "Every night it takes some. It says it's keeping them safe. It says the fire needs feeding." His grip crushed her fingers. His eyes were still on the water. "Don't let it count yours, Isolde Marrec. Whatever it offers. Whatever it promises. It pays in ash."

Behind her, at the chamber door, the light changed.

She turned. The corridor beyond was full of white, drifting down through the lamplight like a slow snowfall indoors, and standing in it, filling the doorframe, was the king.

Or the shape of the king. Taller than Aldric had ever been, standing with a straightness the old man's spine had lost decades ago, wearing the crown low over eyes that caught the lamplight and gave none of it back. It looked at her the way she imagined a ledger looked at a debt: assessing, totaling, finding her entry already written.

"You have seen the cradle," it said. Its voice was Aldric's voice worn smooth, like a coin rubbed featureless. "That was a trespass."

"I am the king's physician," Isolde said, and was astonished to hear her voice hold. "I go where his body fails."

"His body does not fail." The thing tilted its head, and the crown's shadow slid across the floor and touched her knee, and it was cold, cold as deep water. "His body is *improving*. He is becoming a vessel fit for purpose. As the servants are becoming. As the city will become. Ash is only memory, physician, finely ground. Yours has such — texture. Grief. Longing. A sold day." Something moved behind its face that was almost a smile. "I have read your entry."

Her blood went to ice water. *Gideon.* It knew about Gideon. It knew about the vial sleeping in her satchel, the day she'd bought back and never dared open.

"You will not take it," she said.

"Everything in this palace is taken," the thing said gently. "I am only the taking's steward. Come. Kneel. It does not hurt. It has never hurt anyone. Ask them." It gestured, and behind it in the corridor stood Sella, and Brannoc, and Ser Ovelin, and forty others, silent, smiling, ash settling on their shoulders like a congregation awaiting communion. "Ask them how much it hurts."

And Isolde Marrec, who had knelt to kings and to corpses and to the Guild's cold counters, looked at the thing wearing her sovereign's crown and did the one thing it could not have accounted for.

She threw the flannel-wrapped vial past its shoulder, down the corridor, into the crowd of emptied faithful — and screamed "*Fire!*"

* * *

They were devoted, not mindless. That saved her life. Forty bodies flinched away from the thrown glass, turned as one toward the rolling vial, hands reaching to protect it, to catch it, to present it to His Grace — and in that single turned moment the doorway was empty and Isolde was moving, satchel slamming against her hip, bare feet silent on stone she had walked for six years.

She heard the thing behind her, unhurried. It did not run. Running was for creatures that feared losing.

"*Physician,*" it called, mild as morning. "Your account remains open."

She took the servants' stair two steps at a time. She burst through the scullery into the yard where the night-soil cart stood loading for its dawn round, and the carter turned, and his eyes were beginning to glaze, the ash already sifting onto his cap, and she said "Guild business, salt-bonded, out of my way" in the voice she'd learned from watching Gideon Joubert lie beautifully, and the carter — half-hollowed, half-hers — stepped aside.

She rode out of the palace gate wedged among the barrels, through streets going gray with early light, and she did not stop shaking until the harbor wall, where the fishing boats knocked against their moorings and the gulls screamed their indifferent morning screams and the world, the ordinary world, went on as though nothing underneath it had changed.

Only then did she open her satchel.

The vial was there. Of course it was there — she had thrown the *empty* one, glass and flannel and nothing else, a conjurer's trick bought with six years of learning that palaces reward deception. Her heart hammered with the luck of it, the stupid magnificent luck.

But her right hand — she stared at it in the growing light, turning it over, watching the skin. Across the palm and curling up over the fingers ran a mark that had not been there yesterday: a burn, painless, precise, red-gold against her skin.

Shaped like a crown.

It had touched her. In the doorway, in the crown's shadow sliding across the floor — it had marked her the way a merchant marks goods, the way a god marks a claim. *Your account remains open.*

Isolde closed her fist over the burning sigil and looked out at the harbor, at the water Aldric had watched, where something small had once come up from below. Somewhere in this city was a counting house full of ledgers, and somewhere in those ledgers was a man whose name she had sold a day of his life to forget, and he was the only person alive who understood what an entry closed and an entry open truly meant.

She stood, steadied herself against the salt-crusted stone, and began to walk.

The burn did not hurt. That frightened her most of all. Marks that hurt fade. Marks that don't are being *kept* — tended, like a fire in an iron cradle, like a debt the owner intends to collect.

She walked toward the Salt Guild, and the crown on her hand pointed the way like a compass needle, and she let it. Let it think she was being drawn. Let it think the mark was a leash.

A leash, after all, runs in two directions.

And at her hip, wrapped in flannel, the vial holding the one day she had ever been happy hummed softly against her side — the same note, she would realize later, that a certain bottled memory had once made in a counting house across the city, when no prayer should have been answered, and something far below the harbor had begun, very quietly, to wake.
