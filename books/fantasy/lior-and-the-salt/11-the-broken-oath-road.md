## 11. The Broken Oath Road

The oath-road began where the harbor road ended, and it began badly — a seam of cracked flagstones running north through salt marsh, half-swallowed by reeds, marked every hundred paces by a leaning stone carved with words nobody could read anymore.

Gideon could read them. That was the problem with being a swordspeaker: the old script lived in your bones whether you wanted it there or not. *I swear, therefore I am bound. I am bound, therefore I am.* The road had been built by people who believed an oath was a physical thing, a weight you laid down on stone so the world could hold you to it. He'd spent his whole life in the Guild's counting house learning that an oath was instead an entry in a ledger, and entries could be bought, sold, struck through.

He was beginning to suspect both were true, and that neither comforted him.

"Stop walking like that," the boy said.

Gideon glanced back. Tam — the fisherwoman Maren's son, drowned at nine and returned at nine, wrong, remembering other lives — stood on a milestone with his arms crossed, watching the marsh behind them with the patience of someone much older wearing a small body.

"Like what?"

"Like you're being followed. You keep turning around. If they're following us, turning around tells them we know. If they're not, you're wasting time." He hopped off the milestone. "My mother says you walk like a man reading a ledger. She means it as an insult."

"She's not wrong." Gideon adjusted the strap of his pack — three days of bread, a waterskin, and, wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, the humming vial from the shipment he should never have opened. "We should move. Light won't hold past the causeway."

Tam didn't move. He was looking at the milestone again, at the worn carving, and his face had gone somewhere else entirely. It did that, sometimes. A nine-year-old's face opening like a door onto a room where a very old person sat waiting.

"This one still works," the boy said quietly. "The others are dead stones. This one remembers."

"Remembers what?"

"What it heard." Tam pressed his palm flat against the carving. "Oaths. Thousands of them. People used to stop here before walking the road and say their names out loud, so the stone would know who to hold accountable if they broke their word on the way." He looked up, and for a moment his eyes were the gray-green of deep water, of a harbor bottom where a boy had lain for two years before something cracked open beneath him and gave him back. "You should say yours."

"I don't have one to say."

"Everyone has one. Yours is just heavy, is all." He took his hand off the stone and walked on, small boots squelching in the marsh grass. "Come on. You said light won't hold."

* * *

They made the causeway as the sun went down behind the Saltreach hills, and Gideon allowed himself, for the first time since he'd fled the counting house with enforcers' boots echoing behind him, to breathe.

The broken oath-road ran straight as a ruled line across the flats toward the interior — toward the palace, yes, but also away from the Guild, which was the direction that mattered tonight. Three days ago he had been a man whose entire world fit inside ledgers. Then a vial had hummed when no prayer should be answered. Then a drowned boy had come home remembering lives he never lived. Then Gideon had found the seal-stone under the harbor cracked like an egg, and found his own name struck through in a ledger entry written forty years ago in ink he recognized as Vess's hand, and then the Guild had sent men with knives to explain to him, permanently, why asking questions was bad for bookkeeping.

So now: the road, the boy, the dark coming down.

"We'll sleep in the shrine," Tam said. "There's one ahead. Roofless, but the walls are high."

"You've been this way before?"

"Not me." The boy said it without drama, the way he said everything about the other life. "Him. The one whose memories I have. He walked this road when it wasn't broken. When it had pilgrims on it, and lanterns on the milestones, and the stones were warm because people touched them." He kicked a loose flagstone. "It makes my mother cry sometimes, when I talk about it. Not because she's sad. Because I use words wrong. I call things by names that died before she was born."

Gideon thought about that. "What do you call the road?"

"The Oath-Road of the Ninefold Binding." Tam shrugged. "Because that's what it was for. Not travel. Binding. Every mile of it is a clause." He glanced sideways at Gideon, and there was nothing childish in the look at all. "You knew that. Your feet know it. You keep stepping over the joints in the stones like they're sleeping dogs."

Gideon looked down. He hadn't noticed himself doing it. But the boy was right — he'd been picking his way along the seams between flagstones all day, some instinct older than his training keeping him off the lines where the road's power ran.

"Who was he?" Gideon asked. "The one whose memories you carry?"

Tam was quiet for a long stretch of flagstones. The marsh gave way to gorse and low wind-bent trees. Somewhere behind them, far off, a bird cried once and stopped.

"A warden," the boy said finally. "One of the ones who built the prison under the palace. He helped make the fire. He helped make the crown." His voice went flatter. "And then near the end, when he understood what they'd done — what *he'd* done — he tried to unmake it, and they caught him, and they put his memory in a bottle and sank it in the harbor. And a hundred years later a little boy drowned next to the crack in the seal-stone, and the memory leaked out, and I drank it like seawater." He smiled, and it was a terrible, gentle smile. "That's what I am. A leak."

Gideon stopped walking.

Ahead, maybe half a mile on, the roofless walls of the shrine stood black against the last light. Behind them, the road stretched empty back toward the coast. Nothing moved. But Gideon's skin had gone cold, because Tam had just told him, casually, in the voice of a child listing the contents of his pockets, that the memories inside him included the making of the Binding itself — the thing Vess had refused to speak of, the thing the sealed ledgers held, the thing the Guild would kill to keep administered rather than understood.

"You could have led with that," Gideon said.

"You didn't ask." Tam kept walking. "Also, you wouldn't have believed me yesterday. You might believe me today. People believe faster when they're scared." He paused. "Are you scared?"

"Yes."

"Good. Scared people listen." The boy turned back toward the shrine. "Come on. There's a hearthstone in there that still has heat in it, if you know how to ask. Him — the warden — he knew how to ask."

* * *

The shrine had been built, like everything else from that age, as a sentence. Its walls were inscribed floor to fallen-roof with the same looping script, and even in the dying light Gideon could feel the words working — a low hum in his teeth, the sense of standing inside a mouth mid-speech. In the center, a round hearthstone sat in a ring of nine smaller stones, and when Tam knelt and put both hands on it and said something in the dead language — a word or two, soft, almost shy — warmth bloomed up through the stone like a remembered kindness.

Gideon built a small fire on it anyway, because heat asked-for was one thing and heat trusted was another, and he was not yet ready to trust anything the old world offered for free. Free things, the Guild taught, were the most expensive kind.

They ate bread. Tam talked.

Not the way children talk — not a stream, but a well-ordered account, delivered between bites, of the Binding as the warden had known it. Nine gods, not seven as the common stories said. Nine laws, each god the living enforcement of one. And a first king — Aldric's ancestor, though Tam didn't yet know to name him, and Gideon felt the shape of chapter seventeen of his own life waiting in that fact like a knife in a drawer — who had grown tired of justice that answered to no court. Who had wanted law that stayed law.

"So he made the gods into prisoners," Gideon said slowly, "and made himself the jailer, and called the jail peace."

"And made the fire that keeps them, and made the crown that feeds the fire, and made the Guild—" Tam stopped. Chewed. Looked at Gideon with an expression of genuine apology. "I was going to say the Guild. Was the Guild part of it? From before?"

Gideon turned the bread in his hands. "Vess says the Guild predates the Binding. Says we kept trade honest back when the gods' law was the only law."

"Him — the warden — he said the ledgers came after. That the first clerks were theBinding's own auditors. That somebody had to keep track of what the fire cost, and who owed what to whom, forever." Tam tilted his head. "But maybe he was wrong. Maybe he hated them too much to be fair."

"No," Gideon said quietly. "I don't think he was wrong."

Because he had seen the entry. His own name, struck through, in Vess's hand, dated the year of his birth. *Promised to the fire.* He had spent three days telling himself it meant something else — a debt, a tithe, a scribal error. But sitting in a dead saint's shrine listening to a drowned boy describe the architecture of the world's oldest crime, the other explanation stood up and introduced itself: the Guild had always known what the fire needed. And the fire, guttering for a century, had been promised a feeding.

His name. Struck through not to erase him but to reserve him. A mark on a lamb.

"Gideon?" Tam was watching him. "Your face went somewhere."

"Just doing arithmetic." He fed a stick to the fire. "Why are you helping me? Truly. Your mother nearly drowned herself pulling you out of the harbor twice — once the day you died, once the day you came back wrong. She needs you home. Instead you're walking a haunted road with a fugitive clerk."

Tam considered this with the seriousness of a merchant weighing coin.

"Because you're the only person who's asked me questions since I came back," he said. "Everyone else asks *him*. They look at me and talk to the warden. 'What was it like, the old days?' 'Did the gods really walk?' My mother does it too. She loves me, but she looks through me at him." He picked a crumb off his knee with great precision. "You asked *me* why I'm helping. So. That's why."

Gideon found, to his surprise, that his throat was tight. He was thirty-four years old and he could recite the Guild's founding charter from memory and he could not remember the last time anyone had told him plainly why they'd chosen him.

"Well," he managed. "For what it's worth — I'm glad it's you on this road. Not him. You."

The boy grinned, and for a moment he was exactly, only, entirely nine years old, gap-toothed and firelit, and Gideon thought: *this is what the world is supposed to be full of. Not ledgers. This.*

Then Tam's grin faded, and he cocked his head toward the dark doorway of the shrine, and Gideon heard it too.

Hooves. More than one horse. On the causeway behind them, moving fast, unhurried-fast, the pace of riders who knew their quarry was on foot and had nowhere else to go.

* * *

They doused the fire and went out the shrine's north side, through a gap in the wall where a century of weather had undone a clause, and Gideon took Tam's hand and ran.

The road bent east here, climbing away from the marsh into gorse hills, and the milestones came faster, and Tam — breathing hard, small legs pumping — gasped out directions between strides. "Left — at the split — the left-hand road is a lie, it goes down to a flooded quarry — the right one looks worse but holds—"

"How do you know?"

"Because *he* laid the lie-road himself!" A ragged laugh. "Wardens built traps into the binding-roads! Trust me!"

Gideon trusted him. That was the whole strange truth of the last three days: he had trusted a ledger for twenty years and it had sold his name to a fire, and now he trusted a nine-year-old leak in the dark, and the nine-year-old had not yet lied to him once.

They took the right-hand fork. Behind them the hoofbeats reached the shrine and slowed — riders dismounting, checking a cold hearth — and Gideon pulled Tam off the road into the gorse, down into a dry stream-cut, and they crouched among the roots while torchlight swung along the ridge above.

Three riders. Guild cloaks. And leading them, on foot, moving with the terrible economy of a woman who had trained half the enforcers in the counting house —

Vess.

Gideon's heart did something complicated. Relief and dread in the same beat, like a coin spinning.

She stood at the fork a long moment, torch raised, reading the ground the way she read everything — for entries, for intent, for what had been written and what had been erased. Then she did something no pursuer would do. She set her torch down on the milestone at the fork, upright, still burning, a beacon saying *here, here, here.*

And she spoke, in the carrying voice of a woman who had once sworn oaths on this very road, loud enough to reach a stream-cut fifty yards below:

"The left-hand road floods at spring tide. The quarry water is neck-deep and cold. If you take it, you'll live, and I'll find you tomorrow, and we will talk — you and I, Gideon, boy, alone, no knives. If you take the right-hand road, you'll reach the palace in four days, and so will the thing in the crown, and it knows you're coming, because I told it." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I had to tell it. Read your ledger, Gideon. All of it. Before you decide anything. Ask the boy what happened to wardens who acted without reading everything first."

Silence. Torchlight retreating. Hooves, going back the way they'd come.

In the dark of the stream-cut, Tam's hand found Gideon's sleeve. The boy's eyes were wide and wet and ancient.

"She's right," he whispered. "About the wardens. Him — the warden — he acted without knowing everything. Just once. One thing he didn't read." The boy swallowed. "That's how they caught him. That's how he ended up in the bottle. That's how I ended up—" He stopped. Started again, smaller. "Read everything first. Please. I don't want to be a leak twice."

Above them, on the ridge, Vess's torch burned alone at the crossroads, patient as a lit candle in a window, and Gideon understood that it was not a trap and not a mercy but a summons — and that whatever waited in the pages of his own ledger, in the struck-through line where his name used to be, was the one thing he had never dared to audit:

Himself.

He took the boy's hand, and they climbed back up toward the light.
