## 13. What Isolde Bought

The vial had been in her pocket for three days before she could bring herself to open it.

She had carried it through the palace corridors with her hand pressed flat against her coat, the way a woman carries a wound she has decided not to look at yet. She had slept with it beneath her pillow, where it hummed faintly — not the way Gideon's vials hummed, that wrong answering note from the harbor, but something smaller and more private, like a voice murmuring behind a door. Her own voice, probably. The voice of a woman she used to be.

On the third night, after the burn on her wrist had stopped weeping and started itching, after the palace had gone quiet in that particular way it went quiet now — too quiet, the quiet of held breath — Isolde Marrec sat down at the little desk in her rooms, set a single candle beside her, and unstoppered the memory of the day she loved Gideon Joubert.

Salt first. That was how it came back: salt on the wind, salt drying white on the boards of a fishing boat, salt in her hair where he'd pushed it back from her face with fingers rough from rope work. Then light, hard and silver off the harbor water, and his laugh — she had forgotten he laughed, or she had told herself she had, and here it was anyway, low and startled, as if he surprised himself every time.

*You're going to drop it,* he said, in the memory, in the past, in the only place he still spoke to her.

*I never drop anything.*

*You're going to drop this.* He held out the bottle — a real bottle then, green glass, half full of cheap white wine they'd bought with her last coins — and she took it and did not drop it, and he looked at her with an expression she had spent six years trying to reconstruct from nothing and failing, because you cannot rebuild a face from absence. Absence gives you the shape of the hole and nothing else.

Now the hole filled in.

They were on the boat because he had stolen an afternoon. That was the phrase he used, *stolen*, said with the small guilty pleasure of a man who audited other people's thefts for a living. The Guild gave him one day in nine and he had traded two of them to a colleague for this one, and when she asked him why, why today, he'd gone quiet in the way that made her want to shake him, and finally said:

*Because Vess asked me yesterday what I'd do if I ever found something in the ledgers I couldn't balance.*

*And what did you say?*

*I said I'd balance it anyway. And then I thought about it all night, and I realized I lied to her. There is one thing.* He'd turned the wine bottle in his hands, not looking at her. *One thing I couldn't put in a column.*

Isolde, in the present, in the candlelight, felt her breath go thin. She had known — she had always known, in the bone-deep way you know a thing you refuse to examine — that the day she sold was not an ordinary day. You do not sell ordinary days. Ordinary days go for coppers; the Guild wouldn't take them. This day had bought her six years of medical schooling, letters of introduction, a place in the palace infirmary, a life built deliberately within sight of the throne. Whatever was in this vial had been worth a future.

She had assumed it was worth a future because it was the best day of her life.

It was not the best day of her life. It was the last day.

*Say it plainly,* she told him, in the memory, on the boat, while the gulls argued overhead. *You're a ledger man. Say it in columns.*

He almost smiled. *Debit: one afternoon. Credit:* — and here he did look at her, straight on, no deflection, and the memory let her feel again the full weight of being looked at like that, like being the only entry on the page — *credit: everything after.*

*That's not plain. That's poetry. Terrible poetry.*

*Isolde.* And his voice changed, and the gulls seemed to hush for it, and even inside the memory she felt the cold come in under the warmth, the way cold comes in under a door. *I have to tell you something, and then I have to ask you something, and you're going to hate me for both.*

*Try me.*

*The Guild doesn't just keep accounts,* he said. *It keeps people. Every debt is a hook. Every favor is a leash. I've seen them pull men across the city by debts so small the men didn't even remember owing them. And I've seen what happens to the ones who matter. The ones whose names are worth something.* He stopped. Started again. *There's a reason swordspeakers don't marry. There's a reason we don't love where the Guild can see it. Love is leverage. Love is the one debt you can't discharge, because you can't pay it off — you can only keep paying, forever, and the Guild knows it, and the Guild collects.*

*Gideon—*

*Let me finish. Please.* His hands were steady. Hers were not. *Vess asked me that question for a reason. Someone above Vess has started a file on you. Do you understand? A file. On a fisherman's daughter who mends nets and wants to study medicine. There is no honest reason for that file to exist. Which means there is a dishonest one, which means someone has already decided what you're for.*

The harbor light moved on the water. In the memory, she watched herself understand it — watched her own face do the arithmetic, quick and cold, the same arithmetic she would use for the rest of her life. *Then I sell the day,* she said. *I sell it to the Guild myself, before they can price it. If they own my best memory, they own nothing else about me. It's clean.*

*No.*

*It's the only clean thing—*

*Not that day.* He said it fast, before she could shape the argument, and she saw — she saw now, six years late, with the whole truth poured back into her — that he had rehearsed this. That he had sat up nights rehearsing it, the way she rehearsed diagnoses. *Sell a different day. Any day. Sell the market morning, sell the storm, sell your mother's funeral if you have to, I'll help you pick, I'll carry the vial myself. But not this one.*

*Why not this one?*

*Because this is the one they'd use.* His voice cracked, just once, and he mastered it the way he mastered everything, and somehow that was worse than weeping. *If they hold this day, they hold you by me. They walk you into any room they want and say his name, and you follow. But if you sell some other day — some day without me in it — then the leash runs through something you can bear to lose. And I—*

He stopped. The gulls came back. The water slapped the hull, patient, indifferent, keeping its own ledger.

*And I can't be your leash,* he finished quietly. *I won't.*

*So instead,* Isolde said, and her voice in the memory was terrible, flat, the voice she used on dying men when tears would only slow them down, *you want me to sell the day I love you. To cut it out and hand it over, so that I can walk into the palace and spend the rest of my life not knowing why the sight of you makes me want to weep.*

*Yes.*

*And you'll keep yours?*

*No.* He said it without hesitation, and that was the part that broke her, in the memory and out of it, both at once. *Mine goes too. Same day, same hour. Vess will witness the extraction herself. We'll be two strangers who met once over a fisherwoman's prayer and nothing more.*

*That's insane.*

*That's safe.*

*Those aren't the same word, Gideon.*

*No,* he agreed. *But I only get to choose one.*

* * *

In the present, the candle had burned down two fingers' width. Isolde realized her cheeks were wet and had no memory of beginning to cry, which was, she thought, almost funny — a woman who dealt in memories, missing the memory of her own tears.

She made herself keep watching.

The rest came in fragments now, the memory fraying at its edges the way old vials frayed, but the important parts were sharp as scalpels. The argument on the boat — she had fought him, of course she had fought him, she had called him a coward and meant it and not meant it, and he had taken it the way he took everything, silently, storing it somewhere she couldn't audit. The walk back along the quay. The Guild's counting house, all iron and lamplight, smelling of salt and ink. Vess — younger then, her grey braid still mostly dark — standing witness with her hand on the extraction cradle, and her face, gods, Vess's face, which Isolde had never truly seen until now, because she had been looking at Gideon. Vess had looked like a woman performing an amputation she believed necessary. Vess had looked at Gideon with something close to grief.

And Gideon. Gideon had stood beside the cradle with his sleeves rolled up, helping position the apparatus, precise and professional, and only at the very end — only when the technician asked him the standard question, *do you consent to the removal* — had he hesitated. One heartbeat. Two.

*Do you consent?*

*Yes,* Gideon said. And then, so quietly the technician clearly wasn't meant to hear it, and the vial preserved it anyway, faithfully, cruelly: *Keep it safe. Whatever it costs. Keep it safe.*

The memory ended. Not faded — ended, cleanly, the way a page ends, and Isolde sat in the dark of her rooms with the empty vial in her hand and understood, at last, the full architecture of what he had done.

He had not sold their day to protect himself. He had sold it to protect *her* — and he had done it in the only way she would have accepted, which was to make himself pay equally, to stand beside her and lose the same thing at the same moment, so that she would never have to be the one who remembered alone. He had chosen a wound they could share over a wound she'd carry solo. And then he had lived six years walking past her in corridors, polite and correct, a stranger by design, while she built her career on the proceeds of their amputation and told herself the ache under her sternum was ambition unsatisfied.

Heartbreak, she had expected. What she had not expected was this inverted thing, this heartbreak turned inside out, grief braided through fury braided through something that refused to stop being love no matter how she handled it. He had lied to her with an absence. He had proven everything by removing the proof. Six years of silence, and every year of it had been a sentence in a letter he was writing her in the only ink the Guild couldn't read.

*Keep it safe. Whatever it costs.*

She looked down at the empty vial. Green glass, cheap, the kind the Guild used for low-value extractions. He had insisted on the cheap glass, she realized. He had paid extra for the cheap glass, because expensive preservation would have shown up in the ledgers, and the whole point was that this entry should look like nothing. A fisherwoman's daughter selling a market day. Coppers. Forgettable.

Except someone hadn't forgotten. Someone had opened a file.

Isolde set the vial down and flexed her burned wrist. The crown-shaped scar pulled tight, hot, and beneath the heat she felt the other thing she'd been carrying since the vault — the knowledge of what tended the dragon-fire, what wore the king, what had begun answering prayers it had no right to answer. Gideon knew pieces. She knew pieces. Between them, maybe, a whole.

And he was out there somewhere — fled from the Guild, hunted by it, carrying an oath he'd sworn away and a name the ledgers were already learning to forget. He would come to the vault eventually. Everything in her said he would; he was a man who balanced columns, and the column was unbalanced, and he could not live with that.

When he came, he would find her there. Or she would find him first.

Isolde snuffed the candle, wrapped the empty vial in cloth, and tucked it into the pocket over her heart — not because it held anything now, but because it had held everything, and because she was done letting the Guild decide what she got to keep.

Outside her window, far below, the harbor water lay black and still, and somewhere beneath it the cracked seal-stone waited, and somewhere above it the false king lit no lamps and spoke to empty chairs in a voice like grinding stone.

Three days, she thought. She gave it three days before the whole city tore itself apart.

She was wrong. It took one.
