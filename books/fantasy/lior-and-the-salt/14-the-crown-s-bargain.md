## 14. The Crown's Bargain

The summons came the way debts come: quietly, and then all at once.

Gideon had been three days on the broken oath-road, sleeping in shrine-niches with the fisherwoman's son keeping watch, when a courier in palace livery found them at a waystation that had not seen a traveler in a generation. The boy — Tam, though he answered to other names from other lives — read the letter over Gideon's shoulder and went very still.

"It knows your handwriting," Tam said.

The letter was unsigned. The hand was Gideon's own, or close enough that his skin crawled: *Come to the palace. Come alone. Come before the fire dies. — A.*

"Aldric doesn't know how to write to you," Tam said. "I've been in his head. There's nothing of him left that remembers your face."

"Then who wrote it?"

Tam looked at him with eyes that had been old for longer than the Guild had kept ledgers. "You know who."

* * *

He left the boy at the shrine with instructions that were really just fears dressed up as orders. *If I'm not back by dawn, go north. Find Isolde Marrec. Tell her—* He had stopped there, because he didn't know what to tell her. Tell her the man she bought back a piece of was going to sell the rest?

The palace rose out of the morning fog like a ledger entry made stone — columns of imported granite, each one paid for by someone who could not afford it. Gideon had audited the crown's accounts once, years ago, and remembered thinking: *no kingdom balances. They only defer.* Now he walked up the great stair past guards who did not stop him, past servants whose eyes followed him with a devotion so smooth it had no seams, and understood that the deferral had ended. Something down here had called in every debt at once.

The throne room doors opened without being touched.

King Aldric sat on the throne. That was the first wrongness — that he sat like a king, upright, hands on the arms of the chair, when Gideon knew from Isolde's letters that Aldric had spent months slumped and muttering, a man being slowly eaten. This figure was composed. Attentive. Its breathing was even, and its eyes—

Its eyes were patient the way stone is patient.

"You came," it said, and the voice was Aldric's voice worn like a coat two sizes too large. "I wondered if you would make me send again. Couriers are expensive. Everything is expensive now. The fire eats coin faster than men can mint it."

"What are you?" Gideon asked. Not *who*. He wanted to see if it would correct him.

It smiled, and the smile fit the face better than the voice did. "You audit," it said. "Good. Then let us keep proper books." It gestured, and the throne room changed — not transformed, but *revealed*, the way a ledger reveals what a merchant wishes hidden. The gold leaf dimmed. The banners grayed. And beneath the floor, visible now as if the marble were water, Gideon saw the vault: the iron cradle, the guttering white flame, and around it, radiating outward through the stone of the city like roots through soil, threads of light. Thousands of them. Millions. Each thread ending in a person.

"The Binding," Gideon said.

"The Binding," the thing agreed. "My life's work. Though *life* is a generous word for what I have left." It rose from the throne, and up close Gideon could see what the composition hid: the king's body was failing, held together by will the way a bad wall is held together by paint. "Sit. No — stand. You are a swordspeaker; you distrust comfort offered by strangers. I respect that. I was the same, nine hundred years ago."

Nine hundred years. Gideon kept his face still because he had learned stillness in counting houses, where a flinch could cost more than a lie. But inside him, everything Vess had confessed, everything the boy remembered from a life before his own, rearranged itself around those four words.

"The first king," Gideon said.

"I bound the gods." The thing said it plainly, without pride, which was somehow worse. "Not for power. You may choose to believe that or not; belief is not a currency I can spend anymore. I bound them because their justice was drowning us. Do you know what divine law is, swordspeaker? It is arithmetic without appeal. A village offends, a village burns. A man blasphemes, his line is ended to the seventh generation. The gods were *fair*, Gideon Joubert. Fairness is a horror when you are small. So I built them a prison, and I built a fire to keep it shut, and then I discovered that a prison needs a warden, and the only warden who cannot be bribed is one who *is* the prison."

"And Aldric?"

"A vessel. A maintenance cost. Every year I take a little more of him to hold the walls together — his memories, mostly. Memory is the densest fuel mortal flesh produces. A man who forgets his childhood has years of it to burn." The thing tilted its head. "Your Guild understood this early. Where do you think they learned to bottle it? I taught your founders the craft. They were my clerks. They kept the ledgers of what I took, so that someday, if the debt could ever be paid, there would be an accounting."

Gideon thought of the struck-through name in the old ledger. His own name. *Promised to the fire.* "And me."

"You especially." For the first time, something moved behind the patient eyes — not warmth, nothing so human, but attention sharpening to a point. "A child promised to the flame grows up saturated in oath-magic. Swordspeakers were not warriors originally, whatever your Guild pretends. They were *anchors*. Living words driven into the Binding to hold it fast. I have burned through eleven of you across the centuries. You are the last. And you are the strongest, because they saved you for last, because they were afraid of you, because—" the thing paused, and Gideon realized with a lurch that it was choosing its words carefully, which meant it needed something, which meant it could be bargained with, "—because your mentor loved you enough to hide what you were, and love makes poor bookkeeping."

Outside, somewhere far above, a bell began to ring. Not the hour. Not the alarm. The thin, cracked peal of a bell rung by someone with no authority and no plan, ringing anyway. Gideon filed it away. Bells like that gathered crowds, and crowds like this city's crowds — half-fed on returned memories, dreaming other people's griefs — gathered into something with edges.

"Say what you want," he said.

* * *

The thing wearing the crown walked him to the window. Below, the capital lay in morning haze, and Gideon could see the harbor, and beyond the harbor the faint wrong shimmer where the seal-stone had cracked, and between here and there, streets already filling with people who moved like sleepwalkers remembering too many lives.

"Here is the state of the account," the crown-thing said. "The dragon-fire gutters. Three weeks, perhaps four. When it dies, the Binding dies with it, and the gods come out — not gently, not gradually, but all at once, the way a dam does not leak but *fails*. And here is what no one has told you, because no one alive knows it except me: the Binding does not merely cage the gods. It holds their memories apart from the world. Nine hundred years of everything they are, pressed against the inside of the seal. When it breaks, that pressure releases. Every stolen memory the fire has ever consumed — mine, Aldric's, yours, the gods' own natures — comes home. All at once. Into every mind within reach of the seal."

"How far does it reach?"

"The city. Perhaps the coast. Perhaps further, if the wind is wrong." The thing said it the way a physician says *the surgery may kill him*. "Mortal minds are shallow vessels, swordspeaker. Pour a god's lifetime into a fisherman and you do not get an enlightened fisherman. You get a drowned man standing up. You have seen the riots already — the Unremembered, your papers call them, and they have tasted only drops. This will be the ocean."

"So the fire must be fed," Gideon said. "More memories. More kings."

"No." The thing turned from the window, and now it looked at him with the full weight of nine centuries, and Gideon felt his knees want to bend and refused them. "Feeding it delays. I am done delaying. I am offering you a trade, and I will offer it only once, because I am — this is the joke of my existence — *bound*. Bound by the terms of my own working. I cannot unmake what I made. But a free agent can. An oath-breaker. A man with no entries left to lose."

"Say it."

"Aldric goes free. Whole. Every year I took from him, returned — his mind restored, his body released, the crown emptied of me. In exchange, you open the Binding yourself. Controlled release instead of catastrophic failure. You speak the unbinding word — and yes, I know you have found it, the god of small debts sells everything eventually — and you do it *now*, while the fire still has strength to meter the flood. Slowly. Over years. The memories return in measure mortal minds can survive. The gods come back the way dawn comes, not the way a wave comes." The thing spread Aldric's ruined hands. "Or you refuse, and in three weeks the seal fails on its own, and everything returns at once, and we discover together whether humanity drowns."

Gideon stood very still. In the counting house, he had learned to hear the shape of a bargain before its terms were spoken — the pause before the number, the generosity offered too freely. Generosity was always a cost transferred.

"What do you get?" he asked. "If the Binding opens, you end. You said it yourself — you are the binding. Why would you negotiate your own death?"

For a long moment the thing did not answer. When it did, the king's voice had frayed, and underneath it Gideon heard something older, and tired, and — he hated himself for hearing it — grieving.

"Because I have been alone in here for nine hundred years," it said. "Because I have watched everything I protected forget why it was protected. Because the justice I feared has become the justice I cannot perform, and the law I replaced it with has become a machine for bottling children's summers and selling them by the dram." It looked at its hands. "Because a warden who can no longer believe in the prison is just a man in a cell, and I would rather be a dead man in an open world. Audit that, swordspeaker. Find the fraud in it. I have looked for nine centuries and failed."

And that was the trap. Not the terms — the terms were almost clean, and that was what made them filthy. Gideon had spent his whole life among liars and had developed a nose for truth, and this monster smelled true. It wanted to die. It wanted the world saved. It was offering the best available outcome, priced honestly, with the costs disclosed in full.

The costs. He made himself look at them straight, the way he'd audit anything else.

*Aldric freed.* One man, whole. Isolde's king, her ticket into the palace, the reason she'd sold their day to buy her way in — freed, and she would weep, and it would be right.

*The memories returned in measure.* Measured by whom? By the fire's dying strength, metered by a mechanism nine hundred years old that no living soul understood. Some would drown anyway. The arithmetic of mercy always leaves bodies in the margin; he had written enough of it to know.

*The gods returning slowly.* Into a world that had built its laws, its Guild, its entire moral architecture on their absence. Returned to find their debts still on the books, collected in their name by clerks. Justice outside the law — that was what this was, both versions of it, the crown's and his. There was no clean option. There was only the option that killed fewer people, and the option that killed them slower, and the question of whether slow killing was mercy or just easier to watch.

And underneath all of it, coiled and quiet: *you*. The anchor. The twelfth swordspeaker. Open the Binding and the opening needs an anchor, and the anchors burn. The thing hadn't said it. It hadn't needed to. It had simply arranged the terms so that Gideon would arrive at the conclusion himself, voluntarily, the way the best cons work — and this might not be a con, that was the horror, this might be entirely sincere, and sincerity changed nothing about the price.

"If I refuse?" Gideon asked.

"Then I wait for the fire to die, and I do what wardens do." The patience settled back over the thing's face like a lid. "Though I confess I hope you won't. You are the first interesting visitor in a century. The others came with armies or prayers. You came with a ledger in your head and a woman's ghost in your chest. I could almost have liked you, in another age."

Gideon turned toward the doors. Behind him, the bell was still ringing, and now he could hear, under it, a sound he knew from audits gone wrong — the sound of a crowd that had stopped being a crowd.

"One question," he said, without turning. "The fisherwoman's son. The boy who came back wrong. Was that you? A leak, a test, a message?"

Silence. Then, softly, almost kindly:

"The seal cracks where the pressure is greatest. I don't choose where. I only count what escapes." A pause. "Ask yourself, swordspeaker: of all the minds in this city, why did the first escaped memory land in a child who would grow up remembering the Binding — and find his way to *you*? Even I don't control everything. Some things are just debts coming due."

Gideon walked out. The devoted servants bowed as he passed, smooth as water, and none of them saw the vial in his pocket — Isolde's vial, the one she'd carried out of the burning archive, the one holding the day he'd asked her to forget. He had told himself he was carrying it for her.

In the courtyard, the crowd was already arriving — hundreds of them, drawn by the bell, moving with that terrible borrowed grief, and at their center a woman on a cart was shouting words that were not hers in a language dead nine hundred years. The guards were lowering their spears. Somewhere in the press of bodies, a child was crying in a voice that had wept at the funeral of a god.

Three weeks, the crown had said. Standing in the courtyard with the crowd closing like a fist, watching the first stones fly, Gideon understood that the crown had lied about one thing after all.

There weren't three weeks. There weren't three days.

The flood had already started, and it was coming for the palace, and he was standing directly in its path.
