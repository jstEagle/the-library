## 15. Riot of the Unremembered

**Gideon**

The bells started at dawn, and they were wrong.

Gideon knew every bell in Salt Harbor by its voice. The Guild's counting bell rang sharp and even, nine strokes for market open. The harbor bell tolled low for drownings. The palace bell had not rung in his lifetime except for coronations and royal deaths.

This morning all three rang at once, out of rhythm, as if someone had seized the ropes in panic or joy, and neither was a thing bells were built for.

He stood on the steps of the Guild with Vess's sealed ledgers under his arm — the ones he'd bargained for with his oath, the ones that told him what he already feared: that the Binding was fraying from a hundred points at once, that every crack in the seal-stone let memory bleed through like bilgewater into a hold. He had thought they had days. Maybe a week.

The city gave them hours.

It began, as most things did, at the fish market. A woman selling mackerel stopped mid-haggle and burst into tears — not her own grief, Gideon would learn later, but a soldier's, some dead man's weeping for a wife two hundred years gone. She couldn't stop. She didn't know whose tears they were. By the time the sun cleared the breakwater, half the market was crying and the other half was screaming, because grief that arrives without a name is worse than any named grief. It has nowhere to go. It just fills you up like seawater fills a boot.

By midmorning it wasn't only grief. A cooper on Tanner's Row tore his own workshop apart looking for a daughter he'd never had. Two dockhands who'd never met fought each other bloody over an insult one of them remembered receiving in a war that ended before the Guild kept ledgers. A priest of the drowned god — a faith so old its temples were tourist curiosities — knelt in the street outside his own shuttered church and said, over and over, *She's answering, she's answering, I heard her,* until the crowd around him grew and grew.

Gideon watched it from the Guild steps and felt the ledgers under his arm grow heavy as anchor-stone.

"Half-returned," he said to Tam, the young clerk beside him, because saying it aloud made it arithmetic instead of horror. "That's what this is. The memories are coming back in pieces. A man gets a dead child's sorrow without the child's name. A woman gets a betrayal without the betrayer. The mind can't file what it can't account for."

Tam was gray-faced. "Can we recall the shipments? The bottled stock? If the seal is leaking anyway—"

"No." Gideon had already turned this over in the dark hours before dawn. "Bottled memory is stable. It's the *bound* memory that's bleeding — everything the gods took when they made their law, everything the first king locked behind the fire. We could burn every vial in the Guild and it wouldn't slow this down a drop."

"Then what do we do?"

Gideon looked out at the city. Smoke rose from three districts now, thin white threads against the morning. Somewhere a mob was chanting something he couldn't make out, and the terrible thing was that it didn't sound angry. It sounded like prayer.

"We do what the Guild was founded to do," he said. "We keep accounts."

**Isolde**

Isolde ran through corridors she had walked for six years and did not recognize, because the palace had begun remembering too.

The tapestries in the east gallery showed hunting scenes, she had always believed. Now the threadwork shifted when she wasn't looking — hounds becoming wolves, quarry becoming kneeling figures, and once, horribly, a crowned man standing over a fire while smaller figures burned around its edges. She had reported it to the steward. The steward had thanked her with eyes that were not entirely his own and said, "His Majesty is much improved, thank you."

*Improved.* Aldric had been found at dawn sitting upright on his throne, lucid for the first time in months, weeping and asking for paper. He had written, in a shaking hand, four words before the crown-thing clamped down on him again: *IT IS NOT ME.*

She carried those four words folded against her heart now, along with the burn scar shaped like a crown that rode her forearm beneath her sleeve, hot as a brand whenever the warden noticed her. Which, today, was often. The palace was waking the way a body wakes to fever — doors slamming, servants marching in pairs, devotion pouring off the crown-devoted like heat off cobbles in summer.

And below it all, beneath her feet, she could feel the dragon-fire guttering. She didn't know how she knew. She simply did, the way you know a hearth has gone cold in the next room. The last flame that held the gods' prison was dying, and everything it had contained was pressing outward through the cracks, and the city above was drowning in the flood.

She reached the physicians' stair and stopped. Below her, in the lower court, the crowd had arrived.

They had come up from the harbor district through the Salt Gate, thousands of them, and they were not armed — that was the worst of it. No torches, no pikes. They came carrying things: a child's shoe, a wedding ring, a lock of hair wrapped in oilcloth, a chipped cup. Objects that belonged to memories they had received like debts collected in error. They pressed against the palace gates holding their borrowed relics up to the light and crying out names that were not theirs, demanding entry, demanding *audience*, demanding that someone tell them whose lives these were and how to give them back.

"The king will restore what was taken!" a woman shouted, and the crowd took it up, and Isolde felt her stomach turn, because the king would do nothing of the kind. The thing wearing his crown would open the gates wide and drink them all.

Unless someone stood between.

She was still turning that thought over — the shape of it, the madness of it — when she saw him.

* * *

**Gideon**

He went alone. That was the first decision, and the easiest. Whatever the Guild decided to do about the riot — and Vess had sent no orders, which was itself an answer — Gideon would not lead enforcers into a crowd of grieving people. Swordspeakers settled accounts. This was not an account. This was a wound.

The second decision was harder, and he made it walking down Coronation Avenue with the crowd flowing around him like water around a post.

They recognized him. Of course they did — the swordspeaker's coat, the Guild colors, the ledger-satchel. In a century the Guild had never been loved, but it had been *trusted*, the way you trust a scale. And now the scales were broken and here was the weighing-man walking calmly toward the palace gates as if the world hadn't ended.

"You!" A man grabbed his sleeve — a ferryman, hands like rope, face wet. "You're the debt-keeper. Tell me whose grief this is. Tell me and I'll pay it. I'll pay anything. I just need to know whose it is, I need it *filed*, I need—"

"I don't know," Gideon said, and the honesty of it stopped the man like a wall. "I don't know whose it is. But I'll tell you what I do know."

The crowd around them had gone quiet. Thousands of faces, tear-tracked, furious, lost. Gideon climbed onto the base of the old victory column so they could see him, and set down his satchel, and spoke the way he audited: plainly, without ornament.

"The gods took your memories a hundred years ago. Not to steal them — to *hold* them, because the first king asked them to, because mortal minds were breaking under the weight of what the Binding cost. The gods have been keeping your grief in trust ever since. Now the vault is failing, and the trust is being returned in pieces, and there is no law yet for how it should be given back whole."

A woman near the front called out: "Then open the vault! Let it all come back!"

"And drown every mind in this city," Gideon said. "Some of you would survive it. Most wouldn't. The crown knows this. The crown is counting on it."

Murmurs. Someone spat. A stone arced out of the crowd and cracked against the column a hand's width from his head, and he didn't flinch, because flinching was a withdrawal and he was not withdrawing.

"You want to march on the palace," he continued. "I understand it. I've read the ledgers. Every stolen year, every swallowed name — I've read more of them than anyone alive. If rage were currency, you'd all be kings. But listen to me, because I am the last man in this city whose word is bound by oath: the thing on that throne is not your creditor. It is a thief wearing a creditor's face. It wants you at these gates. Your grief is its food. Every name you cry out loud, it drinks."

Silence, then — the dangerous kind, the kind where ten thousand people decide at once.

And then the gates opened.

Not all the way. A gap, wide enough for one line of the crown-devoted to march out, robed in white, faces empty and serene, carrying no weapons Gideon could see. Behind them, from the palace heights, came a sound like a choir heard underwater — the warden's voice, pouring down over the crowd, sweet and vast:

*"Bring me your sorrows. Lay them down. You have carried them so long, little ones. I will carry them for you."*

And the crowd *swayed*. Gideon watched it happen — watched grief-stricken people lean toward that voice like plants toward sun, watched the ferryman beside the column close his eyes and smile, watched the woman with the child's shoe take one step, then another, toward the white robes and their open arms.

Every instinct he had screamed to draw steel. He was a swordspeaker; power lived coiled in his chest like a second heartbeat, and one true word would scatter the devoted like leaves. One word, and maybe the crowd woke. Or maybe the crowd saw a Guild man cut down unarmed believers, and the last chance this city had of coming through this without a massacre died on the cobbles.

He left the sword sheathed.

Instead he stepped down off the column, into the space between the crowd and the advancing white line, and stood there — one man, arms open, empty-handed.

"Look at me," he said, and put the swordspeaker's resonance into it, not the killing word, just the carrying voice, the one that made oaths audible across a courtyard. "You don't have to give it to them. Your grief is *yours*. It's the proof you loved something. Nobody — no god, no king, no thing in a crown — has the right to buy it off you for the price of forgetting."

The white line slowed. The voice from the heights faltered, just slightly, the way a singer falters when the congregation stops singing along.

"They told you the old law was justice," Gideon went on, louder, to all of them now. "It wasn't. It was bookkeeping. And I am the last bookkeeper of that law, and I am telling you the books were cooked from the first page. What comes after doesn't need gods. It needs *you*, awake, holding your own names."

The ferryman opened his eyes. Looked at the white robes. Looked at Gideon. And sat down — right there on the cobblestones, cross-legged, refusing with his whole body.

One by one, then dozens, then hundreds, the crowd sat. Ten thousand people settling onto the stones of Coronation Avenue like a tide going out, holding their relics in their laps, silent, unmoving, *theirs*.

The voice from the palace rose to a shriek that cracked windows in the gallery above.

And through the palace gates, past the frozen line of the devoted, Isolde Marrec walked out into the open with her sleeve pushed back and her crown-shaped scar blazing, and shouted his name across the square.

"Gideon! The vault — I know the way down — *now*, before it stops asking!"

Behind him, the crowd began, very quietly, to rise.
