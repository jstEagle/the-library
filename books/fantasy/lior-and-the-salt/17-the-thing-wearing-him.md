## 17. The Thing Wearing Him

The stair went down farther than the palace had any right to be deep.

Gideon counted the steps because counting was what he had. Two hundred and eleven steps from the broken grate where the boy had pressed a salt-crusted hand into his and said *down here, and don't say anything you can't pay for*. Two hundred and twelve. The boy — Tam, though he'd been four other names in four other lives — had stopped at the threshold above and refused to go further, and Gideon hadn't asked why. A boy who remembered the Binding from inside another body had earned the right to his fears.

So Gideon counted, and the dark counted with him.

The air changed at three hundred steps. It was not warmer, as he'd expected fire to make it. It was *thinner*, as if the stone itself were being slowly breathed out of existence, every atom of it spent like coin. His lantern flame stood perfectly still in the dead air, a small yellow tear that would not fall.

At three hundred and forty, the stairs ended.

* * *

The vault was not what the ledgers described. The ledgers — the sealed ones, the ones Vess had traded him access to for his oath, the ones he'd read by candlelight with his hands shaking — described an iron cradle the size of a cathedral, a furnace of chains, a prison built on the scale of gods.

What he found was a room no larger than the Guild's smallest counting house. Low ceiling. Dry stone. And in the center, an iron cradle barely big enough to hold a sleeping child, and in the cradle, a fire the size of a fist.

It guttered as he watched. Flared. Guttered again. Each flare lit the room in a white so total it erased everything, and each dimming left him blinder than before, afterimages of the cradle burned into his eyes. The last dragon-fire. The flame that held the gods' prison shut. Dying in a room the size of a pantry, tended like a candle someone couldn't bear to blow out.

"You're late."

The voice came from the far wall, and Gideon turned with his hand on his sword — the oath-bound blade, the one that had spoken its first word of power in years when the enforcers came for him, the one that had taken a memory as its price. He still didn't know which memory. There was just a smooth place in him now, a patch of river ice over deep water.

The king sat against the wall like a beggar.

That was the first wrongness. He wore the crown — Aldric's crown, or the crown Aldric wore, iron and salt-white, older than any portrait — but he sat on the floor, legs folded under him, hands resting open on his knees. His robes were royal once. Now they were gray with ash and age, patched at the elbows with scraps of other cloth, and his face —

His face was Aldric's face, worn down to the grain. The same long jaw, the same heavy brow. But where the king's face had gone soft and vacant under the feeding, this face was carved hollow, cheekbones like shipwreck timber, eyes that had watched something die for a very long time.

"The boy wouldn't come down," the thing said. "They never do. Not the ones who remember."

"You know Tam."

"I knew everyone." It said it without pride. Without anything. "Before the names started repeating. Before I stopped being able to tell one century from the next." It tilted its head, and the crown caught the fire's flare and threw it back white. "You're the swordspeaker. The Guild sent you to negotiate. They always send someone to negotiate. It's how they pray."

"I didn't come for the Guild." Gideon kept his voice level. Counting-house level. "I came to see what's wearing my king."

"Your king." Something moved behind the hollow eyes — not anger. Something older and sadder. "He isn't yours. He isn't anyone's. That's rather the point of kings."

* * *

Gideon did not sit. But he did not draw either, and after a while — time moved strangely in the thin air; he'd look at the guttering fire and lose whole minutes between flares — he found himself leaning against the opposite wall, mirroring the creature without meaning to, two figures bookending the little iron cradle like the pillars of some ruined temple.

"Aldric speaks to empty chairs," Gideon said. "Sometimes he answers in a voice not his own. The physicians call it grief. The court calls it madness. The ledgers—" he stopped.

"Say it. You've come too far to flinch at your own profession."

"The ledgers show withdrawals. Memories drawn against the crown's account, going back a hundred years. Small at first. Then larger. Then everything." He'd spent three nights with those entries. He knew them the way he knew his own handwriting, because in places they *were* his own handwriting — copied forward by clerks who never asked what account they were balancing. "Someone has been eating him to keep the seal fed."

"Not eating." The warden's mouth bent, and it might have been a smile in a living face. "Feeding. There's a difference, swordspeaker, and the difference is the whole history of your Guild. Eating is hunger. Feeding is duty." It lifted one hand toward the cradle. "The fire doesn't burn wood. It burns remembering. Every prayer answered since the silence broke, every god stirring in their prison — they press against the seal, and the seal needs weight to hold them, and the only weight that works is a mind. A human mind, full up with years, poured into the flame a cupful at a time."

"So Aldric—"

"Has been pouring himself out for nine years. Since the fire began to fail in earnest. Before him it was his father. Before him, his grandmother. A hundred years of kings, each one thinner than the last, each one crowned and then *hollowed*, and the court physician dosing them with poppy and calling it melancholy." The hollow eyes fixed on him. "Isolde Marrec knows. She figured it out before I did what I had to do to stop her figuring it out louder."

Gideon's hand tightened on nothing. "You hurt her."

"I gave her a scar she'll thank me for someday. Scars are honest. Memory is the liar." The warden unfolded itself, rising with the stiff care of something that had sat too long, and crossed to the cradle, and knelt beside it the way a man kneels at a deathbed. Up close, in the flare-light, Gideon could see that the crown wasn't sitting on its head. It had *grown* there. Iron filaments ran down into the scalp like roots, like the veins of a leaf, and where they entered, the skin had gone the same salt-white as the metal.

"Do you know who bound the gods?" the warden asked.

"The first king," Gideon said. "Aldric's ancestor. The histories agree. He made the Binding with the dragons' consent, sealed the old law away, and gave the world to men."

"The histories were written by his clerks." The warden laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as ledger paper. "Yes. The first king. And do you know *why*, swordspeaker? Why a man with a whole world in his hands would build a prison for heaven?"

Gideon thought of the sealed ledgers. Of Vess's face when she'd told him: *the Guild was founded to administer the debts*. Of the entry in the oldest book, struck through in ink that predated the Guild's own founding: *Gideon Joubert — promised to the fire.*

"Because their justice was unbearable," he said slowly. "Because a god's justice is perfect, and perfect justice doesn't forgive. It balances. Everything. Everyone. All at once."

The warden looked at him then — really looked, the first time — and there was such naked relief in the hollow face that Gideon felt something in his chest turn over.

"Yes," the warden whispered. "Yes. You understand. No one has understood in a thousand years. They all come down here wanting to know *how* the Binding works, *what* it costs, *can it be repaired*. Never *why*." It sank back against the wall, the crown scraping stone. "I was a king in a world where prayers were answered. Do you know what that meant? A farmer's child died of fever, and the farmer prayed, and the god of small mercies *answered* — brought the child back, balanced the scales, took the fever from the child and gave it to the farmer's neighbor's child, because that was justice, that was *equity*, debt for debt, life for life. Perfect. Precise. And the neighbor prayed too, and the god answered him, and by spring half the valley was dead of a fever that had never existed until mercy made it real."

The fire flared. In the white light the warden's shadow filled the whole room, vast and crowned.

"I begged them to stop. I went to the temples. I fasted. I offered my own children as payment to stay their hands — and they *accepted*, swordspeaker, they accepted with love, because a debt freely offered is the sweetest kind, and they loved me, and their love was arithmetic." Its voice cracked like drying mud. "Perfect justice is a machine that grinds everything it touches into balance. And everything it touches is *everything*. So I found the dragons. I found the last flame. I asked them to help me build a door with no handle on this side, and I put the gods behind it, and I sealed their law away from the world, and I have been the lock ever since."

Gideon's mouth was dry. "You became the binding yourself."

"The Binding needed a keeper with skin. Fire eats memory; memory must be replenished; a mortal mind is the only vessel that refills." The warden spread its hands, and Gideon saw that the fingers had gone translucent at the tips, worn thin as old vellum. "So I passed the crown down. Son to daughter to son. Each heir takes up the keeping, pours their years into the flame, and when they're empty, the crown passes on. A dynasty of wardens. A kingdom built as a feeding trough." The laugh again, worse than before. "And the clerks wrote it all down as glory. *The king who freed us from heaven.*"

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. The fire guttered and flared, guttered and flared, and Gideon found himself thinking about ledgers. About the whole architecture of his life — every debt recorded, every favor priced, every grief assigned a value and a column. He had believed, all his life, that the ledger was the honest thing. That the world outside it was chaos, sentiment, the soft corruption of the unmeasured.

But the ledger had been founded by this. By a man who couldn't bear a world that balanced itself without asking permission.

"You're dying," Gideon said. "The fire. It's almost out."

"The dragons are extinct. The flame doesn't renew. For a century I've been feeding it kings and it buys less time each year." The warden touched the cradle's rim with one translucent finger, tenderly. "Then the prayers started being answered again. Cracks in the seal. Gods pressing through — not escaping, not yet. Leaking. And every leak costs more fire, and more fire costs more mind, and I have emptied a hundred years of kings into this little box and it is *not enough anymore*." It looked up. "Do you understand what happens when it goes out? Not freedom. Freedom would be clean. When the seal breaks, a thousand years of compressed justice releases at once. Every unpaid debt. Every unanswered prayer. Every balance owed since the day I closed the door. It will come through like a flood, and it will be *fair*, and fairness will kill this city to its foundations."

"Then keep it burning."

"With what?" The warden rose, and the crown flared white, and for one moment Gideon saw it as it must have been at the beginning: not a monster, not a warden. A king, young and desperate, holding a door shut with his own body while the world screamed on the other side. "There's one mind left in the line with enough years in it to buy the seal another century. One. And he's standing in front of me wearing a sword he swore never to use, and the Guild promised him to this cradle before he could walk."

The words landed like cold water.

"*Promised to the fire,*" Gideon said. "My name. Struck from the ledger. Vess said—"

"Vess saved your life by hiding you. The Guild raised you as a clerk instead of a king, and I let them, because a swordspeaker who keeps accounts is worth ten princes who pour themselves out on schedule." The warden stepped closer, and the thin air grew thinner, and Gideon felt his own memories pull gently toward the flame like water finding a drain. "You are the last payment, Gideon Joubert. Your name, your years, your whole self, poured into the iron, and the seal holds another hundred years, and the world goes on unjust and alive. That is the trade. It has always been the trade. It is the only trade there has ever been."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the fire dies within the year, and the flood comes, and everything balances." The warden smiled, and it was Aldric's smile, worn to the bone. "I am not threatening you. I am *accounting* for you. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole history of my damnation."

* * *

Gideon stood very still. The fire flared, and in the white light he saw it all at once — not the monster, not the warden, but the shape underneath both: a man who had done the arithmetic of heaven and found it obscene, and had answered obscenity with a prison, and had spent a millennium paying for the prison with everyone he loved. A god of justice locked away by a man who wanted mercy, and mercy locked away with it, because the two could not be separated. Because that was the horror. That was always the horror. Justice and mercy were one thing, and it was unbearable, and everything cruel in the world had been built by people trying to cut it in half.

He felt pity rise in him like floodwater, and he let it, because pity was not agreement. Pity was just seeing clearly.

"You're not the binding," Gideon said quietly. "You're the first prisoner. You've been in here longer than the gods."

The warden went very still.

"And I'm not going to feed you my name," Gideon went on, "and I'm not going to let the fire die. There's a third column in this ledger. You just never learned to write in it, because you only ever had two hands — one for the scales and one for the door." He drew his sword. The oath-blade hummed, low, the way the vial had hummed in the counting house a lifetime ago, a sound like something remembered rather than heard. "The gods aren't escaping, warden. They're being *released*. There's a difference, and the difference is the rest of my life."

The warden stared at him. And then, beneath the crown, beneath the salt-white roots and the hollow face, something ancient and exhausted began — very slowly — to weep.

Above them, muffled through three hundred and forty steps of stone, the palace erupted into screaming.
