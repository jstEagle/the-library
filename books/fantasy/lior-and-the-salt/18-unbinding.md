## 18. Unbinding

**GIDEON**

The iron cradle was smaller than he had imagined. That was the cruelty of it — a century of dread, and the thing that held the world's oldest prisoners was no bigger than a hearth, guttering like a lamp left in an empty room.

He knelt at the edge of the vault floor with the boy's hand in his. Tam's palm was cold and salt-rough, a fisherwoman's son down to the bone, and his eyes were old in a way no child's eyes should be.

"When it flares," Tam said, "don't look away. Everyone looks away. That's how it kept them."

"I won't look away."

"You'll want to. It will feel like being unmade." The boy squeezed his fingers once, hard, then let go and stepped back to the stair, where Isolde waited with the king.

Aldric could barely stand. Whatever the warden had left of him was thin as winter light — a man-shaped absence wearing a crown it had forgotten how to hold up. But when Gideon had spoken to him on the road down, Aldric had said, *I remember my mother's kitchen,* and then wept, because he could not remember whether it was true.

That was what they were here to give back. All of it. Every stolen year.

Gideon rose and faced the fire.

The warden stood on the far side of the cradle, patient as stone. It wore the first king's shape still — the broad shoulders, the beard braided with iron rings — but the crown had sunk into its skull now, roots of gold threading through gray flesh, and its eyes were two coals banked low. It did not speak. It had made its bargain and been refused; there was nothing left between them but arithmetic.

*Every entry,* Vess had said, pressing the master ledger into his hands at the top of the stairs, her face wet. *You understand what you're spending.*

*I understand.*

His name appeared in four hundred ledgers. His apprenticeship, his oaths, his debts, his wages, the fine he'd paid at nineteen for speaking a word of power in a tavern argument, the memory he'd sold at twenty-two so he could afford his first set of white robes. Every transaction that proved Gideon Joubert had existed, entered in ink by hands that trusted other hands. He would pay with all of them. And when the last page burned clean, there would be no record anywhere in the world that he had ever been written down.

The Guild's oldest law: *what is not in the ledger does not exist.*

He opened the master ledger across his forearm. The vellum was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything in this room was burning, one way or another.

"Isolde," he said.

She came forward, and Aldric with her, and she looked at him across the cradle with an expression he had spent three chapters of his life trying not to name. She had remembered everything for two days now. She remembered the harbor, the stolen afternoon, the way he'd begged her to sell it. She remembered, and he never would again, and they had agreed — had they? Had they managed to agree? — that this was not a tragedy but a price, and prices were not tragedies, prices were just what things cost.

"Gideon." Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "Say it whenever you're ready. I'll hold him."

"The word needs the oath behind it. The whole oath. Everything I swore at seventeen."

"I know."

"It will take the ledgers as I speak. Not after. As I speak. So if I stop halfway—"

"You won't stop halfway." Almost a smile. "You're the most stubborn man who ever counted anything."

Behind her, Tam sat down on the stair like a boy waiting out a storm, knees drawn up, watching with those old eyes. *Don't look away.*

Gideon breathed in. The air tasted of iron and hot salt and, faintly, impossibly, of the sea — the god of small debts had left that gift in the room, or left it in him, a kindness with no entry anywhere.

He began to speak.

---

**ISOLDE**

She had rehearsed this. In the physician's quarters, over three sleepless nights, she had rehearsed holding a mind together while it was refilled — the way you hold a cracked vessel steady while someone pours wine into it, thumb over the worst fissure, patience against pressure. She had done it before with fevered sailors, with mothers in the grip of childbed madness. She had thought herself prepared.

She was not prepared.

The moment Gideon spoke the first syllable of the oath, the vault changed. Not the light — the *weight*. The air thickened until breathing felt like wading. The dragon-fire, which had been guttering, stood up straight in its cradle like a soldier called to attention, and every shadow in the room leaned toward Gideon as if the darkness itself wanted to hear.

And Aldric screamed.

Not loudly. That was the horror of it — a thin, continuous sound, the sound of a man being *opened*. Because the years were coming home. She felt them arrive through her palms where she pressed them to the king's temples: decades pouring back into a vessel that had learned to live empty, memories stacked like cargo shifting in a flooding hold.

*Hold him.* She anchored herself. *Hold the cracks.*

"Hold on to me," she said into Aldric's ear. "Your name is Aldric. You are the king. Your mother's kitchen had blue tiles, chipped at the corner by the door — *hold on to that*—"

Because she knew it. Gods forgive her, she knew it from the vial, from the day she'd bought back — the day she'd carried Gideon's confession of it. Aldric's childhood had been sold to the crown-thing too, and some of it had passed through Guild hands, and some of those hands had been hers, and so she stood in the flood with a rope made of other people's memories, feeding the king himself one thread at a time.

"Blue tiles," Aldric gasped. "There was — bread — she burned the bread—"

"She burned it every saints' day and blamed the oven. Hold that."

Gideon's voice went on behind her, and it was changing. She heard it happen. The words of the oath were old — older than the Guild, older than the binding — and each one cost him something she could measure by the way the ledgers whispered. Yes, *whispered*: the master ledger under his arm had begun to turn its own pages, entries lifting off the vellum like ash off a bonfire, and each lifted line took a piece of him with it.

She risked one glance back.

He was standing straighter than she'd ever seen him. White fire ran along the seams of his coat where the oath lived in the cloth. And his face — his face was doing what faces do when a man watches his own house burn and decides to stand in the doorway anyway.

"Gideon," she said, and his eyes found hers, and for one heartbeat neither of them was doing anything heroic at all. They were just two people looking at each other across a dying fire, knowing that one of them was about to forget the only afternoon that had ever belonged to them both.

"Don't say it," he said gently. "Whatever you're going to say. Save your breath for him."

"I wasn't going to say anything clever."

"You never do."

She laughed — actually laughed, in the middle of the end of the world — and turned back to the king, and poured.

---

**GIDEON**

The ledgers were eating him in order.

He felt each entry go. The fine at nineteen went first, and with it the tavern, the taste of bad wine, the reason he'd sworn off power — gone, a smooth blank where a scar had been. Then his apprenticeship, and Vess's face blurred in his memory like a portrait left in rain; he loved her still, he knew that much, but he was beginning to lose the *why*, and the losing felt like drowning in shallow water, slow enough to watch.

*Keep speaking. The word doesn't work unless the whole oath goes. A partial payment is worse than none — the Guild taught him that in his first year. The Guild. Had there been a Guild? There had been — there was — the counting house, the smell of ink—*

He gripped the memory deliberately, the way you grip a rail in a storm, and spent it too. Deliberate spending hurt more than theft. That was the point. That had always been the point of oaths: they only carried weight if you felt what they weighed.

Halfway through, the warden moved.

It came around the cradle with the terrible unhurried gait of something that has watched ten thousand years and never once been surprised, and it reached for him with a hand like a bundle of black rods, and Gideon — still speaking, would not stop speaking, the word was a bridge and bridges collapse if you pause mid-span — raised the master ledger between them.

The warden stopped.

Because the ledger was nearly blank now, and what remained of Gideon Joubert in the world's records fit in the space of a closing paragraph, and the warden looked at the shrinking book and then at the man, and something moved in its banked-coal eyes that in any other creature might have been called respect.

"You could stop," it said. Its voice was Aldric's voice, worn smooth. "Half a name remains. Half a man can walk away."

"He's not half a man," Tam shouted from the stairs, furious, tears running down the boy's face. "He's *more* than what's written, that's the whole — that's what nobody ever—" And the god of small debts, wherever it hid in the walls of the world, seemed to agree, because the salt-smell strengthened, and the fire stood taller.

Gideon smiled at the warden. It cost him the smile's own memory even as he wore it.

"No," he said, and spoke the next word, and the next.

---

**ISOLDE**

The king came back all at once, the way dawn comes — gradually, and then completely.

One moment she was feeding him threads. The next, his hands closed over hers with the strength of a man in his prime, and his eyes cleared, and he said, in a voice that was entirely, unmistakably his own:

"Isolde Marrec. You have ink on your wrist."

She looked down. She did. She always did. "Welcome back, Your Majesty."

"Not yet." He was crying without any shame at all, a king weeping like a boy, decades landing on him — his coronation, his wife's face, the treaty he'd signed, the daughter he'd— oh, gods, the daughter, and Isolde held him through that one especially, because grief that arrives forty years late arrives all at once. "Not yet a king. First a man. Give me a moment to be a man."

"You have exactly as long as it takes Gideon to finish."

Aldric turned toward the fire, toward the warden, toward the ancestor who had eaten his life to keep the gods caged, and Isolde braced for rage. It didn't come. What came instead was pity — vast, exhausted, kingly pity.

"Poor old man," Aldric said softly. "All those years alone with the fire. Nobody to tell him it was finished. Nobody to tell him he could put it down."

And the warden — the first king, the binder of gods, the thing that had worn crowns and faces and centuries — made a sound. Small. Cracked. The sound of a door opening somewhere inside a fortress that had been sealed since the world was young.

Gideon spoke the final word.

---

**GIDEON**

There was no word. He understood that in the last instant, in the space between the second-to-last syllable and the last. The unbinding was not a word at all. It was a *surrender* — the complete, itemized, witnessed surrender of a self, read aloud into the fire, every entry given back, every debt forgiven in both directions, the whole account closed.

The ledgers finished turning their pages.

The last line lifted — he never learned which one it was, his name or his oath or the small fine from a tavern he no longer remembered — and Gideon Joubert became, in the sight of every record in the world, no one at all.

The fire went white.

Not the orange of hearths or the red of ruin — white, total, silent, a color that was less a color than a decision. For one endless moment the vault held nothing else. He felt it pass through him like a tide through an open window, taking nothing, because there was nothing left to take, and giving back everything, because everything had been paid for.

The crown fell from the warden's head and rang on the stone like a bell.

The first king folded down beside it — small now, shrunk, an old man at last after ten thousand years of being an institution — and the fire gathered itself out of the iron cradle and rose, and rose, and went out through the ceiling of the world in a column of white, and somewhere above them, muffled by a hundred feet of palace rock, Gideon heard the city scream and then, strangely, go quiet, the way a room goes quiet when a long argument finally ends.

The gods were free.

He knew it the way you know weather. Something vast had shifted its weight off the world's chest, and the whole earth drew one enormous breath.

Then his knees gave out, and the floor came up kind and cool, and the last thing he saw was Isolde's face above his, saying his name — except it wasn't his name anymore, couldn't be, there was no such name, and she knew it, and she said it anyway, once, like setting a flower on a grave.

* * *

**ISOLDE**

He woke at dusk on the vault steps with Tam's jacket rolled under his head and no idea who any of them were.

She had known this would happen. She had told herself she was prepared for it. She had lied.

"Easy," she said, when he tried to sit up. "Slowly. You've spent rather a lot of yourself."

He looked at her with perfect courtesy and zero recognition, and it was the courtesy that broke her heart — the politeness of a stranger, offered to a woman kneeling in the ash of his sacrificed name.

"Where am I?" he asked. His voice was the same voice. That was unbearable. The same voice, asking *who are you*.

"A vault," Tam said, before she could manage anything. "Under a palace. You did a big magic and saved everyone. You don't remember because you paid with your name. It's fine. I'll explain on the way up."

"Will there be a way up?"

"There's always a way up," the boy said with the absolute authority of someone who had died once and come back wrong. "That's the other thing nobody remembers."

Above them, through the broken seal-stone, the evening sky was doing something no evening sky had done in a hundred years. Lights were moving in it. Not stars — stars stayed where they were put. These wandered, and wheeled, and descended slowly toward the harbor, toward the hills, toward the city, radiant and curious and utterly unbound, and Isolde watched the first freed god set foot on mortal ground in a century and thought: *no ledgers for them either. No law. Nothing but what we build.*

Aldric stood at the mouth of the vault, bareheaded, diminished, more himself than he had been in thirty years, watching the lights come down with tears on his face.

"We have work to do," the king said quietly. "All of us. Even you, Marrec. Especially you."

"In a moment, Your Majesty."

She turned back to the man on the steps. He was watching the sky too, and his face held wonder, pure and unowned, the wonder of a man with no past to complicate it.

"What's your name?" he asked her. "Since we're introducing ourselves."

Isolde Marrec looked at the man who had bought her love back with every record of his own existence, and found — this was the miracle, this was the thing she would carry into every chapter after — that she was not afraid.

"Isolde," she said. "And yours?"

He considered the question honestly, the way he had once considered ledgers.

"I don't know yet," he said. "But I think someone up there might help me find out."
