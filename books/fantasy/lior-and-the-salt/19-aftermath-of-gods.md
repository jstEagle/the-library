## 19. Aftermath of Gods

The fire went white at dawn, and the whole city saw it.

Isolde stood in the palace courtyard with her burn scar aching under her sleeve and watched the light climb out of the mountain's throat like something exhaled after a century of holding its breath. It was not dragon-fire anymore. Whatever it had become when Gideon spoke the unbinding word, it rose in a column that did not burn the clouds but parted them, and through the parting came — she would spend the rest of her life failing to describe this — *attention*. The sensation of being looked at by something vast and patient and no longer angry.

Then the column folded itself into shapes, and the shapes walked down out of the sky.

She counted eleven before she stopped counting. They were not what the old reliefs showed. The carvings in the temple ruins had made them monstrous — wings, too many eyes, mouths full of law. What stepped onto the terraced fields above Harrowgate were smaller than that, wearier, dressed in the rags of their own iconography. One wore a farmer's coat gone to seed. One was mostly hands. One looked like a woman Isolde had buried two years ago, which she understood later was not cruelty but courtesy: the god of thresholds took the shape of whatever door you were standing in.

They did not speak. That was the first thing everyone noticed, and the thing that frightened people most. A hundred years of sermons about the gods' terrible voices, their judgments pronounced in thunder — and they came down silent, looking at the city the way a man looks at a house he left as a child, checking what still stands.

The god of small debts paused at the harbor gate. It found the fisherwoman Maren there — Isolde learned this from the boy, who seemed to be everywhere now, trailing the freed gods like a gull following a plow — and it set something in her palm. No one saw what. Maren closed her hand around it and wept without sound, and the god moved on.

No law for them now. No ledger. No binding. The thought arrived in Isolde like a stone dropped down a well, and she waited for the splash and never heard one.

* * *

Aldric abdicated on the third day.

He did it in the great hall, before the assembled court, and Isolde stood at the back in her physician's grays because someone had to be there who knew what he had been. The crown-thing had fed on him for years — she still didn't know how many; the ledgers said nine, Aldric's own counting said more like thirty, and both could be true, because memory is not a storehouse but a garden, and things had been planted in his.

He stood at the throne and did not sit on it. He had not sat on it since the night the fire flared white. He touched the armrest once, the way you touch a coffin lid.

"I am Aldric," he said, "son of Maro, and I ruled you for—" He stopped. His jaw worked. Isolde saw him reach for the number and find the shelf empty where it should have been. "For some years," he finished, and there was a rustle through the hall, half laughter, half horror, and he let it pass through him like weather. "I am diminished. I will say it plainly because plainly is all I have left. There are years missing from me the way teeth are missing from a mouth. I do not know what I signed. I do not know whom I condemned. I know that a warden wore my face and spoke with my voice, and that everything done in this room for a generation must be presumed poisoned until proven clean."

He lifted the crown off the cushion beside him — not off his head; he hadn't worn it since — and held it out at arm's length, and even now, even emptied, even grieving, he held it the way you hold a snake you have agreed to carry to the river.

"This bound a god once," he said. "It should be melted. Not stored. Not studied. Melted, and the metal given to the harbor so it can be lost honestly." He turned, scanning the hall, and his eyes found Isolde's and stopped there for a moment. She did not look away. She owed him that much, and more than that, and he knew it, and neither of them said anything, because some debts are only bearable if no one names them.

"There will be a council," Aldric said. "Harbor guilds, temple remnant, the low courts, the salt-works. Nine seats. Drawn by lot where wisdom fails." A thin smile. "Wisdom will fail often."

The hall erupted, of course. Lords who had spent their lives currying a king now had no king to curry, and the noise was enormous and ugly and alive. Isolde slipped out through a side door while they shouted, because she had somewhere else to be, and the somewhere else was ash.

* * *

The vault beneath the palace was open to the sky now. When the fire left its iron cradle it had taken the ceiling with it, politely, the way a guest takes a coat, and what remained was a shaft of morning light falling into a round chamber floored in slagged iron. The cradle itself was a black ring, cracked along one seam, cooling. Ash lay over everything fine as flour.

The Guild had been here first. Of course the Guild had been here first. Their enforcers had cordoned the stair, and clerks moved through the chamber with brass scoops and labeled jars, gathering ash the way they gathered everything — methodically, reverently, as if the world were inventory.

Vess stood at the cradle's edge.

Isolde had prepared herself for many versions of this meeting. Vess the interrogator. Vess the penitent. Vess the woman who had signed away a child's name to a furnace and called it arithmetic. What she found instead was an old woman standing very straight with her hands clasped behind her back, looking down into the broken ring, and when Isolde approached, Vess did not turn.

"He paid with every entry," Vess said. "Do you understand what that means? Every ledger in every counting house. Every debt recorded against the name Gideon Joubert — discharged. Every favor owed to him — forgotten by the owing. Every record of his apprenticeship, his oath, his sentence, his service." Her voice was level, which meant nothing; Isolde had known her long enough to know level was where Vess put the worst things. "The Guild does not forget, physician. It cannot. Forgetting is the one sin we have no rite for. And so the ledgers did the only thing they could."

"Which was?"

"They kept the shape of the hole." Vess turned then. Her eyes were dry and very tired. "There are entries in the master rolls that read *paid in full, payee unrecorded*, hundreds of them, going back thirty years. The clerks think it a scribal error propagated by a careless copyist. We built a religion on exactness, and exactness has left us a ghost with our own handwriting."

Isolde's throat was tight. "Where is he?"

"Gone. Before the ash settled. The boy was with him, and then neither of them was anywhere." Vess looked back at the cradle. "I searched. Don't look at me like that — yes, I searched, I broke three protocols doing it, I queried the harbor rolls and the road-tolls and the parish registers of every settlement within a week's walk. Nothing. He isn't hiding, Isolde. Hiding leaves traces. He has simply stopped being an entry in the world." A pause. "You of all people should understand what that costs. You bought your way into this palace by selling a day. He bought a kingdom's freedom by selling himself entire."

"You knew," Isolde said. "When he was a child. You knew he was promised to the fire, and you struck his name to hide him, and then you raised him inside the machine that wanted him."

"Yes."

"And you never told him."

"No." Vess's hands tightened behind her back. "I told myself it was protection. Perhaps it was. Perhaps it was also that I could not bear to watch him flinch from me. Both things can be true. The gods taught us that much before they left." She finally looked at Isolde fully, and there was something raw under the clerk's composure, something that had been bleeding a long time. "If you find him—"

"If?"

"*When.* You're a searcher by nature. It's why you sold the day instead of burning it. Burning is surrender. Selling is faith that you'll buy it back." Vess stepped aside, clearing the path to the stair. "If you find him, tell him the ledgers keep his shape. Tell him the Guild owes a debt it cannot enter, and that I intend to die owing it, because discharging it would require remembering him, and remembering is beyond us now. Tell him—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Tell him the first lie I ever entered into a ledger was his age. I wrote him a year younger so the fire's claim would come due late. It bought him nineteen years. I have been spending those nineteen years ever since, and I am nearly out."

Isolde nodded once and went up the stair into the light, and did not let herself shake until she reached the top.

* * *

She searched for six days.

Not frantically — she was a physician; she knew how to work a crisis, how to triage hope from evidence. She worked the harbor first, because the boy had come from the water and water returns what it takes eventually. Maren had seen nothing, but Maren's son had — the returned boy, the wrong boy, the boy who remembered other lives. He sat on the sea wall eating bread and watching the freed gods wander the tideline like pilgrims with nowhere prescribed to go.

"They went north," he said, when she asked. "Most of them. The god of small debts stayed to settle accounts — it's paying back every kindness anyone ever did unpaid, did you know that? Old Mother Quill got a sack of coins on her doorstep for a bowl of soup she gave a stranger forty years ago. The city doesn't know what to do with it. People are afraid to accept. It feels like being invoiced in reverse."

"And Gideon? The swordspeaker. Did he go north with them?"

The boy chewed, considering. He had a way of looking at you slightly past your shoulder, as if your earlier selves were standing behind you. "He can't go where they go. They walk on the old roads, the ones underneath. He walks on top now." He swallowed. "But he asked me for a name before he left. I gave him one. I'm not supposed to say what it is. Names are gifts, and gifts stop working if you tell."

"Did he say where he'd go?"

"He said—" The boy frowned, reaching. "He said he'd always wanted to see a place where nothing was owed. And then he laughed, because there's no such place, and he knew it, and he went anyway."

North, then. Through the salt marshes, up the old oath-road, toward the hill country where the Binding's architects had kept their summer houses and the temples stood roofless and honest. Isolde provisioned quietly, told the new council's health office she was inspecting sanitation in the northern settlements — a lie so administrative no one questioned it — and rode out on the seventh morning with a saddlebag of bread, a physician's kit, and a vial.

The vial rode against her heart. She had opened it weeks ago, in chapter and candlelight, and remembered everything: the market day, the rain starting, the way Gideon had laughed with his whole chest because he so rarely permitted himself to. And she remembered the end of it, the part she'd sold away without knowing she was selling it — Gideon holding the vial himself, already knowing what it was, saying *sell it, sell all of it, if the Guild sees what I am to you they'll use you to reach me, please, Isolde, forget me and live.* Love proven by absence. She carried it now like a bone knitted crooked but strong.

The northern road was crowded, strangely. Refugees going the wrong direction — not fleeing the gods but following them, drawn by the same gravity she felt. At a shrine ruin outside Marsh End she watched a woman leave bread on the altar stone and walk away without asking for anything, and the god of thresholds — or one shaped like a doorway — stood in the ruin's empty frame and received it, and wept, or did whatever gods do that looks like weeping.

No law for them. No law from them. The world had spent a century praying into silence and building its own clumsy courts and contracts, and now the silence had ended and the prayers' recipients were walking among them with nothing to administer and nowhere assigned, and the strangest thing, the thing Isolde kept turning over at night, was that they seemed *relieved*. Freed of their own law the way Aldric was freed of his crown. She caught herself wondering what justice would grow in the space they'd left, and whether it would be kinder, and whether kinder was the same as better, and whether she'd know it when she saw it.

On the ninth day, in a hill town whose name she never learned, she found a school.

It was nothing — a converted granary, benches of rough plank, maybe twenty children sitting in the dust-light while a tall man with a voice like a closed ledger taught them to speak oaths they could keep. *Say only what you'll do,* he was saying. *Do everything you say. That's the whole art. Everything else is decoration.* She stood in the doorway with her travel dust and her burn scar and her heart going like a bird against a window, and he had not yet seen her, and she had time to notice that he moved differently now — lighter, unbearably light, a man carrying nothing because he was nothing, no entries, no debts, no name except whatever a child had invented for him —

And then the smallest student tugged his sleeve and pointed at the door, and Gideon Joubert turned around, and looked at a woman he had never met, and went pale the way men go pale when the body knows before the mind does.

Isolde stepped inside, out of the light.

"I'm told," she said, and her voice almost held, "that you teach children to keep their word. I've brought bread. And I've brought a story about a market day, and rain, and a man who laughed with his whole chest — and since you can't hold it, I'll hold it for both of us, and tell it as many times as you need."

Gideon stared at her. Behind him, the children had gone utterly silent, twenty pairs of eyes moving between them like spectators at a duel.

"That's the trouble with strangers," he said at last, hoarsely. "They arrive already owing you something. And I have no way to enter it."

"Then don't," Isolde said, and set the bread down between them like a treaty. "Call it the first unpaid gift."

Outside, far off across the hills, something vast and patient turned its attention northward — and hummed.
