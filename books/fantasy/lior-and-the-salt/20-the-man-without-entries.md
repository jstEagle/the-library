## 20. The Man Without Entries

The first thing I learned about being no one was that doors open differently.

Guild doors had always opened for me — the wardens knew my face, the ledgers knew my hand, and even when neither did, the oath-mark on my wrist had been a key that turned in every lock from the harbor to the counting house. Now the mark was gone. Not scarred over. *Gone*, the skin smooth as a child's, as if I had never spoken a word of power in my life, as if the Guild had never written me down at all.

So when I walked out of the ash-smoke and the ringing silence of the vault, past enforcers who looked straight through me, past crown-devoted kneeling in the gutters with their emptied eyes refilled with something bright and terrible, past the whole burning machinery of a city deciding what it would become — not one person stopped me.

A warden at the palace gate held up his hand for the crowd behind me and waved me through.

"Name?" he asked, because the forms required it, and the forms were the last thing still working in Aldric's city.

I opened my mouth to say *Gideon Joubert* and found nothing there. Not forgotten — erased. The way a ledger line looks after the ink is scraped away: you can see the ghost of letters, feel where the pen pressed, but the words themselves are gone into the wool. I knew I had a name. I could not make it come.

"I don't have one," I said, and heard how strange it sounded, and stranger still, how true.

The warden grunted. "Next."

And that was all. That was the entire cost of everything I had paid. No arrest, no recognition, no debt collector stepping out of an alley with my account balanced against my bones. The Guild's whole architecture of obligation rested on names, and mine had been spent to the last coin.

I walked down the Processional with the freed gods' aftermath crackling in the air like the quiet after lightning, and I felt light. Unbearably light. Like a man who has carried stones so long he has forgotten they were stones, walking suddenly without them, staggering under the weight of nothing.

* * *

The city did not know what to do with itself, which meant, for once, it behaved like a city instead of a ledger.

Shops stood open with no one minding them. A baker set loaves on his windowsill and a chalk sign that read TAKE WHAT YOU NEED, PAY WHAT YOU CAN, and by midmorning the sign had been amended three times in different hands until it finally settled on TAKE, and somehow there were still loaves at dusk. People wept in doorways over memories that weren't theirs — a woman sobbing over a childhood in a village she'd never seen, a dockhand staring at his hands like they'd belonged to someone braver. The half-returned years were still finding their owners, or finding people who had no claim on them at all, and grief moved through the streets like weather.

Nobody robbed anybody. I kept waiting for it — the old Guild arithmetic said that a city unbound from its debts becomes a city of thieves — but the arithmetic didn't hold. What happened instead was stranger. People gave things away. They couldn't explain why. Somewhere under a century of careful accounting, it turned out, the city had been keeping a different set of books, and those books were opening now, and every entry in them was a kindness someone had never been repaid for.

I passed the Salt Guild itself around noon and made myself look.

The great bronze doors stood shut. Someone had chalked across them, in letters a meter high: **CLOSED UNTIL WE KNOW WHAT WE OWE**. Below it, smaller, in a shakier hand: *if anything*. Enforcers — former enforcers — stood outside without their badges, uncertain as men whose uniforms had been repossessed overnight. One of them I recognized. Brannoc, who had put a knife to my throat on the oath-road and been stopped by a word I swore I'd never speak again. He saw me. I watched him see me. And his face did the arithmetic — *the swordspeaker, the traitor, the man who unbound the world* — and then lost count, because whatever ledger held my face had burned with the rest.

He touched his forehead anyway. An old gesture, pre-Guild, the kind sailors used for luck. Then he looked embarrassed and went back to standing guard over a door that guarded nothing anymore.

I kept walking. My feet knew the city better than I wanted them to. They took me down Coincutter's Lane, past the pawnshop where I'd once appraised a bottled grandmother's laugh, past the assay office, past the narrow stair where Vess had caught my elbow twenty years ago and said, *You have a good hand for grief, boy. Don't waste it on your own.* I wondered where Vess was now. I wondered if she remembered me, or if my erasure had climbed the ledgers all the way up to her. Part of me hoped it had. It would be a mercy, and Vess had never once in her life permitted herself one.

By late afternoon I had walked the whole of the lower city and confirmed what I already suspected: I was free, and freedom was a country where I did not speak the language.

Here is what no one tells you about having no name. You also have no reflection in other people's eyes. A barkeep doesn't know whether to trust you. A beggar doesn't know whether you're worth begging from. Children don't point and whisper *that's the swordspeaker*. You pass through crowds like water through a net, and it is exactly as lonely as it sounds. I sat on the harbor steps as the light went copper and tried to remember my mother's voice, and got only the shape of remembering — the pressed-ghost of letters again. Whatever else the fire had taken with my entries, it had taken the people who wrote me into being.

I was still sitting there when the fisherwoman found me.

* * *

Maren Odile had a walk like the deck of a boat in rough water and a face that forgave nothing, and she came down the harbor steps carrying a basket of smoked fish and the particular expression of a woman who has been looking for someone all day and is annoyed to have found him.

"There you are," she said.

"You know me?" I said, before I could stop myself. Hope is undignified. I've never managed to kill it.

"No," she said flatly, and sat down beside me, and offered the basket. "But I know a man sitting where my son told me to find one. Eat. You look like a candle that's been burned at both ends and then apologized to."

I took a fish because refusing Maren Odile was apparently not something my body was willing to attempt. We ate in silence while the fishing boats came in, their lanterns lighting one by one, and the harbor water did its slow dark breathing.

"He's wrong," she said eventually. "Tam. He remembers too much. The priests want to study him, the Guild — what's left of it — wants to interview him, and the neighbors cross the street because a boy who talks about the Binding like he was *there* makes them feel like their own heads are rented rooms." She tore a piece of bread, handed me half. "But he sleeps at night. That's more than most of this city can say. So I've decided he's my son and the rest is just weather."

"You decided," I said.

"That's what mothers do. Nobody writes it down. It holds anyway." She looked at me sideways, with eyes the color of the water off the breakwater. "He says you paid something enormous. He won't say what. He says you wouldn't want it said."

"It's gone either way," I said. "Saying it wouldn't bring it back."

"Mm." She chewed. "That's what a man with no name would say."

The tide turned. Somewhere behind us a bell rang the hour in a tower whose god had walked out of it two days ago, and the bell rang anyway, because bells don't need gods, only rope.

Then I heard footsteps on the steps above us, quick and light, taking them two at a time the way children do when they've stopped caring whether adults approve, and Tam came down into the lantern light with his hair full of wind and his arms full of driftwood, and stopped dead when he saw me.

His face did something complicated. Relief and fear and a fierce, proprietary gladness, all at war.

"You came back," he said. "You said you'd be gone and you came back anyway. That's the third time you've done that."

"I haven't gone anywhere yet."

"That's what you said last time." He dropped the driftwood, sat down on my other side with the absolute entitlement of the young, and studied me with those eyes that had seen a century of Binding through somebody else's memory. "It's worse, isn't it. Than you thought."

"Yes."

"Good." He nodded, satisfied, as if honesty between us were a debt he'd been worried about. "Lying people smell like wet rope. You used to smell like wet rope. Now you don't."

"Tam," his mother warned.

"I'm being *observant*." But he subsided, hugging his knees, and for a while the three of us watched the boats. Then, quietly, in the voice children use when they are asking the real question underneath the small one: "Do you remember your name?"

"No."

"Do you remember *me*?"

"Yes," I said, and it was true, and it was the strangest thing of the whole strange day. The ledgers had taken Gideon Joubert — the Guild's swordspeaker, the oath-bound auditor, the man with the good hand for grief. But they had not taken the harbor steps, or the smoked fish, or the boy who remembered dying and coming back wrong. Those had never been written down anywhere. They existed only in the keeping of the people who'd lived them.

Tam considered this. Then he said, matter-of-fact, the way he announced tides and weather:

"You need a new one, then. Names are like boats. If yours sinks, you borrow one till you build."

"I don't think it works like—"

"*Saltrow.*"

I stopped. "What?"

"Saltrow." He said it again, tasting it, testing the hull. "Because you walked the salt road and you rowed yourself back, and because you smell like the harbor now, and because—" he shrugged, eleven years old and certain, "—because it's yours. I made it. Nobody else has it. I checked."

Maren made a sound that might have been a laugh swallowed whole.

"Saltrow," I said. The word sat strangely in my mouth, the way new boots sit strangely on new feet. Not a name anyone had given me. Not a name any ledger held. A name invented by a boy who had died and returned, offered freely on the harbor steps with bread and fish, the first thing anyone had given me since the fire that wasn't payment for something else.

An unpaid gift. The second one I'd ever received. I was beginning to suspect they were habit-forming.

"Say it back," Tam commanded.

"Saltrow."

"Again. Properly. Like it's yours."

"Saltrow." And this time something in my chest turned over, the way a lock turns, the way a page turns, and the unbearable lightness shifted — not gone, but ballasted now, weighted with one small stone that was mine because a child had thrown it to me across the water.

Maren reached over and squeezed my shoulder once, hard, the way she probably handled rigging. "Well, Saltrow. You'll take supper with us. Not charity — Tam will sulk, and I'll hear about it for a week. Call it crew wages. You can haul the basket."

I hauled the basket. It weighed almost nothing. I carried it like treasure anyway.

* * *

We ate in her kitchen above the chandlery, a low room smelling of tar and rosemary, and Tam talked with his mouth full about the freed gods — the little ones first, he said, the god of thresholds seen sitting on a doorstep in the Weaver's Quarter simply watching people come and go, delighted, for hours; the river-god walking upstream against the current like a soldier returning late from a war; somewhere in the hills, they said, the old storm-mother had taken up residence in a lighthouse and was learning, slowly, not to smite. The big ones hadn't shown themselves. The big ones, Tam said, were doing what big things do after long imprisonment.

"Sleeping?" Maren asked.

"Deciding," Tam said, and would say no more, and the fire popped, and nobody pushed.

Afterward I washed dishes in a sink for the first time in a life I could no longer verify, and Maren stood beside me drying, and said, without looking at me: "There was a woman asking after you today. At the fish market. Dark hair, court accent trying to hide itself, burn scar on her wrist shaped like—" she paused, choosing, "—like a crown."

The dish in my hands was very suddenly the only object in the world.

"She wouldn't give a name," Maren went on, merciless, mild. "Just asked if anyone had seen a man with no shadow in the ledgers. Said if anyone found him, tell him—" Maren frowned, retrieving it exactly, because she was a woman who retrieved things exactly, "—tell him *she still has the receipt.*"

I set the dish down. My hands were steady. Everything behind my hands was not.

"Did you tell her where to find me?"

"I told her nothing. I don't sell information." She hung the cloth on its hook. "But Tam drew her a map, because he's a menace, and she left before I could confiscate it. Down toward the bread ovens on Tallow Street. The ones that stay lit all night." She looked at me then, level as a horizon. "You don't have to go. That's the whole point of what you bought. Nobody can call you anywhere ever again."

"I know," I said.

I was already reaching for my coat.

Outside, the night smelled of woodsmoke and salt, and the city glimmered with a thousand small unsanctioned kindnesses, and somewhere ahead, down Tallow Street, an oven burned with a light like dragon-fire but kinder, and a woman who had once sold our yesterday waited with a receipt for a debt that no longer had a ledger to live in.

*Tam's map was wrong twice,* I would learn later. *It didn't matter. Some addresses you can find blind.*

I walked toward the ovens, and for the first time since the iron cradle, I was not light, and I was not heavy.

I was Saltrow, and I was expected.
