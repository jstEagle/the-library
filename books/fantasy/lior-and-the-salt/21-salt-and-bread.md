## 21. Salt and Bread

**Gideon**

The city had learned to feed itself again.

That was the thing no one had predicted. When the gods walked free and the ledgers burned their ink to ash, everyone assumed hunger would follow — that a world without accounts was a world without bread. But the bakeries had opened anyway. The fishing boats went out anyway. It turned out most of what the Guild had called commerce was just people choosing, over and over, to give each other things.

Gideon stood in the fish market at dawn because it was the only place he knew how to stand.

He had been nameless for eleven days. He'd counted them the way he used to count entries — out of habit, not need. Eleven days of walking streets where no one turned when he passed, where innkeepers asked him his name and he said *I don't have one* and they shrugged and wrote *the tall man* in their books, or nothing at all, because half the city had stopped writing things down. The Guild's counting house stood empty on the hill, its windows dark, its vaults open to anyone strong enough to carry what was inside. Most people looked at it and shuddered and walked faster.

He could not stop looking at it.

He still dreamed in columns. Debits and credits. What was owed, what was paid. He woke some mornings with his hand moving against the mattress as if writing, and there was nothing under it, and no name for the hand either. That was the part he hadn't expected — not grief exactly, but lightness so total it felt like falling. A man who has carried stones all his life does not know what to do with his arms.

The fisherwoman found him first.

She came through the market with her baskets the way she always did, broad-shouldered and salt-crusted, and she saw him standing at the edge of the stalls like a man waiting for a ship that had already sunk, and she did not ask his name. She just set a basket down beside him and said, "You look like a man who needs a job."

"I don't know how to do anything," Gideon said. It was almost true. He knew ledgers and he knew swordspeaking and both of those were over. "I can count."

"Can you gut mackerel?"

"No."

"Then you'll learn." She handed him a knife. "My boy says you're the one who fixed him. Whatever that means. He says you don't remember it, which he thinks is funny. Boys are cruel that way."

"He remembers more than I do," Gideon said. "He remembers lives I never lived."

"Aye, well." She watched her son across the market — the returned boy, the wrong boy, the boy who remembered the Binding from a life before his own, who had walked Gideon down the broken oath-road singing songs in dead languages. "We decided we'd keep him anyway. Wrong and all. You decide a thing like that once, and after that it's easy."

She left him the knife and the basket and went to sell her fish, and Gideon stood holding a blade he had no oath to and understood, dimly, that this was what freedom tasted like. Salt. Work. Someone deciding to keep you.

He was still holding it an hour later when Isolde Marrec came down to the water.

* * *

**Isolde**

She had rehearsed this. That was the humiliating truth of it — a woman who had talked her way into a king's court, who had held a scalpel steady while the crown-thing wore Aldric's face two feet away, who had walked out of a burning palace with a scar shaped like a crown seared into her shoulder — she had rehearsed a conversation with a fishmonger's helper in front of her bedroom mirror like a girl before her first dance.

*You'll offer him bread,* she told herself. *That's all. Bread is simple. Bread doesn't require anything.*

She had baked it herself, which was also absurd, because Isolde Marrec had not baked anything since she was nineteen years old and poor enough to count every coin twice. But the memory she had opened — the day, their day, the one she had sold and bought back and finally, finally remembered — began with bread. A stall by the harbor. He had bought her a loaf warm enough to burn their fingers, and they had eaten it sitting on the sea wall with their feet dangling, and he had said something about salt making everything honest, and she had laughed until her ribs hurt.

She remembered all of it now. Every hour. And he remembered none of it, because he had asked her to sell it, and then he had given away his own name to unbind a king, and now the only person alive who held their day whole was her.

So she carried the loaf down through the morning market, wrapped in cloth, feeling like a courier delivering something explosive.

He was easier to find than she'd feared. The fisherwoman had told her — Isolde had gone asking, days ago, too much a coward to come herself — *the tall man helps Tam gut fish Tuesdays, if you can call it helping.* And there he was at the stall, sleeves rolled, hands red to the wrist, doing violence to a mackerel with the focused intensity of a man defusing a trap. He had cut himself twice. She could see the bandages.

"Gideon," she said.

He looked up.

She had prepared for several versions of his face. Recognition. Confusion. The polite blankness of a stranger being addressed by a stranger. What she got was worse than any of them: he looked at her the way you look at a door you've walked past a hundred times and only now notice is there. Nothing. Then, quickly, apologetically, something kind.

"You know me?" he said.

"Yes," Isolde said. Her voice held. She'd made sure of that in the mirror too. "I'm going to buy your entire basket so Tam doesn't yell at you for stopping work. And then I'm going to sit on that wall over there and eat bread, and you're welcome to join me, and you're equally welcome not to."

Tam, who remembered everything about everyone, grinned up at her with a face that belonged to no living child. "Take the break," he said. "He's terrible at this anyway."

* * *

**Gideon**

They sat on the sea wall with their feet hanging, and she broke the loaf and gave him the larger half without appearing to think about it, and the bread was warm and good and slightly burnt on one edge, and none of it explained why his chest hurt.

"You knew me," he said. "Before."

"Yes."

"How?"

She chewed. Swallowed. Looked out at the water the way people do when they're buying time. "I'm a physician," she said. "Was. At the palace. I helped hold King Aldric together while his stolen years poured back into him. You may have heard of that. It was fairly loud."

"The fire flared white," Gideon said. "I've heard. I was... apparently there."

"You were there." She turned to look at him fully then, and her eyes were gray-green and very steady, and he had the sudden disorienting sense of having been seen by them before, many times, in a life that wasn't in any ledger because there was no ledger anymore. "You were the center of it. And afterward you walked out of the ashes and nobody knew your name because you didn't have one, and I went looking for you for eleven days, and here you are gutting mackerel badly."

"Is that what I am to you? Something you went looking for?"

The question came out of him before he could weigh it, the way true questions do. She didn't flinch.

"You're the man I loved," Isolde said. "For one day, entirely. And parts of other days, less entirely, before and after. And then I sold the best of it to pay my way into the palace, and you let me, and neither of us knew at the time what we were trading away." She brushed crumbs from her hands with great care. "You don't remember any of it. I checked. I've spent eleven days checking, hoping some piece survived the way pieces survive in everyone else — but you gave all of yourself, Gideon. Every entry. There's nothing left over. You're the cleanest man in this city."

"That sounds like a eulogy."

"It's the opposite." For the first time her voice cracked, just barely, a hairline fracture in stone. "Do you know what I've been doing since the fire? I've been carrying our day around alone. Like a coin from a country that doesn't exist anymore. I can spend it nowhere. I tried to give it back to you three times and lost my nerve at your stall, at your lodging house, at the harbor gate. Because you'd look at me exactly the way you're looking at me now. Politely. Kindly. Like a stranger."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize. That's the worst part. You'd apologize beautifully." She laughed, wet and short. "You were always apologizing for things that weren't your fault. It was infuriating. It was one of the things I —"

She stopped.

Gideon waited. The tide moved below them. Somewhere behind, Tam was yelling at a customer about the freshness of herring with the authority of a boy who had died once and found death negotiable.

"Tell me," Gideon said quietly. "The day. If I can't hold it — tell it to me. Out loud. I'll hold it for as long as you speak. Maybe that's enough. Maybe that's what it's for now."

* * *

**Isolde**

So she told him.

Not all at once — she was a physician, she knew dosage. She started with the bread, because the bread was safe. The stall by the harbor, the loaf too hot to hold, the way he'd juggled it between his palms and refused to put it down because he'd already paid and it was hers, damn it, and a man honors his transactions.

"You said salt makes everything honest," she told him. "You said a lie can't survive contact with the sea. I told you that was nonsense and you'd clearly never met a fisherman. And you said —" she closed her eyes to get it exact, because exactness mattered, because this was the last copy in existence — "you said, *then I'll be careful which of us the sea talks to.*"

Gideon huffed something that might have been a laugh. "That does sound like me. Whoever that was."

"He was very sure of himself," Isolde said. "And completely wrong about everything except the important things. We walked the length of the seawall. You showed me the seal-stone under the harbor — this was years before it cracked — and you put your hand on it and told me what it was sealing, and I thought you were mad, and then I realized you weren't mad, you were lonely. You were the only person in the Guild who still believed the old gods deserved better than a ledger line. You'd been carrying that alone for years. The way I've been carrying our day alone for eleven days." She looked at him. "It's a familiar weight. Carrying something alone that should be shared."

"What else?" His voice was rough. "What else did we do?"

"We argued about whether debts could be gifts. You said no — a gift unpaid corrupts the giver. I said yes — a debt unpaid corrupts the receiver. We never resolved it. I suspect we were both right." She tore another piece of bread and did not eat it, just held it. "In the evening it rained and we sheltered under the customs arch and you took off your coat and put it around my shoulders even though you were cold, and I told you that was economically irrational, and you said —"

"*Some things aren't entries,*" Gideon said.

Isolde went absolutely still.

The bread fell from her fingers onto the stones. Below them the tide kept its time; above them the gulls kept theirs; and between those two vast indifferent clocks, a man with no name sat on a sea wall with bread crumbs on his shirt, frowning faintly at his own mouth, as if someone else had borrowed it for a moment.

"I didn't —" he started. "I don't know why I said that. It was just — it was there. Like a step in the dark that turns out to be a stair."

Isolde found she was crying, which was professionally embarrassing and personally inevitable. "It's what you said," she managed. "Word for word. Under the arch. In the rain."

"Then tell me the rest," Gideon said. "All of it. Everything I can't hold — say it, and I'll hold it while you speak, and maybe if you say it enough times, some of it will learn to live in me the way that sentence did. Not as an entry. As..." He searched for the word and, finding none, invented one, the way the boy had invented a name for him. "As a tenant."

"A tenant," Isolde repeated, laughing and crying at once.

"Something that lives in you without owning the deed."

She wiped her face with her sleeve, took a breath that shook only a little, and began again from the beginning: the bread, the wall, the rain. And he listened the way he must have done everything — completely, with his whole attention, as if listening were a transaction he intended to honor.

* * *

**Gideon**

She spoke until the sun stood high and the market emptied and refilled around them, and Gideon learned himself the way you learn a foreign country: secondhand, imperfectly, with wonder. He learned that he had been stubborn and gentle and afraid of deep water. He learned that he had once bought a dying woman's memory of her wedding just so she wouldn't have to sell it to a broker, and had never recorded it anywhere, and Isolde had caught him at it and said *that's theft from yourself* and he had said *some things aren't entries.*

He learned that she had sold their day to become a court physician, and that she had done it partly to watch over a king going strange, and partly — she admitted this with difficulty, staring hard at the water — because she had hoped the Guild's records would someday bring her back to him, and the palace was the best place to wait.

"You waited six years," Gideon said.

"I'm patient. It's my worst quality."

"It's the best one you have."

She turned and looked at him then, really looked, and the distance between stranger and not-stranger narrowed to a single held breath. "You can't love me," she said. "You have no reason to. I'm a story you heard today."

"I know," Gideon said. "It's very inconvenient."

"And yet?"

"And yet." He picked up the fallen piece of bread, considered it, ate it. Salt and wheat and a little char. Honest food. "I don't remember loving you. But I appear to be the kind of man who loves you. I have testimony from the only reliable witness. And I've spent my whole life trusting records over feelings, so." He shrugged. "The record says I loved you. I'm inclined to trust it."

Isolde made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and leaned her shoulder against his, and they sat like that while the tide turned, two strangers who were not strangers, sharing a day that lived in only one of them — for now.

It was Tam who broke the spell, coming down the quay at a run with his basket abandoned and his face lit like a lamp. "There's a crowd at the Guild house," he panted. "Hundreds. They're saying—" he caught his breath, and the words came out in the old cadences, the ones from before his life, the ones that made the hair on Gideon's arms rise— "*they're saying the gods have come to the counting house. All of them. And they're asking for the man without entries.*"

Gideon rose. Beside him, Isolde rose too, her hand finding his without hesitation, as if that, too, were in the record.

"Well," he said. "I suppose we'd better go see what I owe them."
