## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The trick to surviving a poisoned court was to be the kind of man no one bothered to poison twice.

Oscar Kavanagh had learned that lesson before he'd learned to read, back when his mother still sold eels off a cart in the Saltrow and the crown's men came through collecting children with clean blood and quick tongues. He'd been eight. He remembered the recruiting sergeant looking him over — small, wiry, forgettable — and saying, *This one will do,* in the same tone he might have used for a barrel of salt pork.

Twenty-two years later, *this one will do* had become the whole architecture of his life.

He stood now at the prince's left shoulder in the Amber Hall, watching steam rise off a plate of quail in plum glaze, and waited for the ritual. It was always the same. The under-steward would lift the silver dome with a flourish meant for someone who mattered; Oscar would step forward without being asked; the hall's conversation would dip, just slightly, the way water dips around a stone. He would take the small fork, cut a portion from the thigh where the juices pooled, and eat it while forty pairs of eyes pretended not to watch him chew.

Then he would count.

Not aloud. Never aloud. He counted in his head the way other men prayed: thirty heartbeats for fast venoms, sixty for the slow ones, ninety if the cook had used anything bitter enough to mask. His tongue was the finest instrument in the kingdom — trained on a hundred poisons in the cellars beneath the Hall of Records, dosed and dosed again until his body knew arsenic the way a sailor knows weather. He could taste henbane in honeyed wine. He could taste the difference between rat-bait and widow's-tears in a stew of strong onions. Once, at the autumn feast, he had caught a whisper of gallowroot in the prince's sweetmeats because the assassin had been clever enough to hide it under candied figs, but not clever enough to know that gallowroot leaves a ghost of smoke at the back of the throat, like a candle snuffed in a far room.

The prince had never thanked him. The king had never learned his name. That was the arrangement, unspoken and eternal: Oscar Kavanagh would stand between the royal line and death, and in exchange, the royal line would pretend he did not exist.

He swallowed. Counted. Swallowed again.

"Clean," he said, and stepped back into the shadow of the pillar, and the hall's conversation resumed as though a door had opened and closed somewhere far away.

* * *

The storeroom they gave him was below the kitchens, past the ice cellar, down a stair so narrow two men couldn't pass abreast. It was technically a pantry. In practice it was the place where the palace kept everything it didn't want to look at: cracked serving ware, condemned wine, and one food-taster whose continued existence was an embarrassment to everyone who dined above.

Oscar loved it the way a hermit loves a cave.

The walls were stone, cold even in summer, and he had covered them — against all regulation — with paper. Not letters. He received no letters. Maps. Dozens of them, drawn in his own cramped hand, copied from manifests and toll ledgers and the drunken boasts of caravaneers who never guessed that the quiet man refilling their cups was memorizing every word.

Smuggler routes. That was his secret vice, the one indulgence permitted to a man permitted nothing else. The Thornfell passes especially — the high roads through the mountains where the kingdom shattered three hundred years ago and never quite healed. He knew them the way a blind man knows a room: by touch, by ledger, by inference. The Coldgate trail, closed by royal decree since before his grandfather's birth, yet still showing up in confiscated cargo manifests with dates too regular to be accident. The Serpent's Stair, which appeared in no official document at all but showed its existence in the price of mountain iron in the river towns. The long switchback road called the Widow's Teeth, where toll ledgers recorded deaths that never matched the season's weather.

He traced one now with a fingertip, standing at his wall in the lamplight, a cup of cheap wine forgotten in his other hand. Somewhere up there, past the last checkpoint, men walked roads he had catalogued down to the number of switchbacks and never once stood upon.

It was a strange grief, wanting a thing you were built never to have. He had made his peace with it years ago — or told himself he had. A taster's body belonged to the crown. A taster's tongue belonged to the crown. But the ledgers had taught him something the crown hadn't ordered him to learn: that the world was larger than the palace, and that somewhere beyond the Amber Hall's windows there were roads with no names in any manifest, and men who walked them answered to no steward's bell.

He drank the wine. It tasted of nothing. Most things did, when you spent your life tasting for death instead of living.

* * *

They arrested the courier during the change of kitchen shifts, which was either incompetence or design, and Oscar never decided which.

He was coming up the service stair with a tray of tasting spoons when the shouting started — boots, the scrape of a body shoved against flour sacks, the particular flat authority of the Warden's men. He stopped in the doorway and did what he did best: made himself furniture.

Two guards had a man face-down across the prep table. A third was going through his cloak with rough efficiency, pulling out wax cylinders, a lead-sealed letter case, a strip of dried meat. The courier himself wasn't fighting. That was the first thing Oscar noticed, and the thing that stayed with him after everything else: the man lay there with the terrible calm of someone who had already run the arithmetic of his situation and found no remainder worth fighting for.

He was young. Mountain-born, by the look of him — lean in the way of men who'd grown up walking grades that would kill lowland legs, dark hair cropped short, hands scarred rope-and-rock. And across the shoulders of his grey travel cloak, half-hidden by the guard's fistful of fabric, was a brand.

Oscar's breath went shallow.

He knew brands. Cataloguing them was half his work — smuggler marks, toll-house stamps, the sigils of the free companies that moved goods through the Thornfell gaps. He had drawn forty of them on his wall-maps in careful little notations. This one he knew from a single source: a confiscated manifest, seven years old, that the Warden's clerks had burned the same week it crossed his desk. He had copied the mark first. Some instincts you don't question.

A circle, broken at the top, with a single vertical stroke descending through the gap like a blade dropped into a well.

The mark of the Sigil-keepers. The order that officially had not existed since the Shattering. The order that, according to every history taught in every schoolroom, had died out with the war it failed to prevent.

The courier turned his head on the table — just slightly, just enough — and his eyes found Oscar's in the doorway.

Oscar had spent twenty-two years being unseen. He knew every variation of the way people looked through him: the steward's bored flick, the noblewoman's dismissive slide, the guard's categorizing glance that filed him under *tool*. This was none of those. The courier looked at him the way a drowning man looks at a piece of driftwood — sudden, desperate, precise — and then, deliberately, slowly, let his eyes fall shut again, as if closing an account.

"Nothing on him but trade papers," the third guard said. "Forged, probably."

"Veyra will want him breathing," said the first. "Spymaster's orders. Cell nine."

Cell nine. Below the records. Where men went in talkative or came out talkative, and Veyra's methods decided which.

The guards hauled the courier up. As they dragged him past the doorway, his shoulder brushed the frame — and something dropped from the folds of his cloak, small and soft, landing without sound in the shadow of the flour sacks. The guards didn't notice. The courier didn't look down. But as they pulled him into the corridor, his head turned once more toward Oscar, and this time his mouth moved, shaping two words with no voice behind them:

*Storeroom. Please.*

Then he was gone, boot-heels fading up toward the cells, and the kitchen resumed around Oscar like water closing over a stone.

He stood very still for a long moment. Then, when the last scullery boy had passed, he crouched, eased the flour sack aside, and drew out what the courier had dropped.

A map. Folded small, worn soft at the creases, stained along one edge with something dark that had dried brown — blood, old and new together. He should have taken it to the Warden. It was the simplest act of his life, the act of a tool doing what tools do: carry it upstairs, hand it over, return to his spoons and his counting and his beautiful invisible wall of maps about roads he'd never walk.

Instead he carried it below, to his cold little room, and unfolded it by lamplight with hands that were not quite steady.

It was the Thornfell spine. All of it — not the merchant copies, not the toll-house fragments, but the whole range drawn with a precision that made his own wall look like a child's scrawl. And threaded through the ink, following the high ridges like a vein follows the arm, ran markings he had never seen in any document: a script older than the kingdom, dense and deliberate, crowding the passes around a single peak marked with that same broken circle and descending stroke.

The Sigil-keepers' mark. At the center of everything.

Oscar sat back. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above him, muffled through floors of stone, the palace went on dining, and the prince ate food that Oscar's tongue had certified safe, and the spymaster Veyra began whatever she began in cell nine.

He looked at the blood-stained map, and at his own reflection faintly ghosted in the lamplit ink, and understood with perfect clarity that the invisible life he had built — the careful, erasing, survivable life — had just ended, though he could not yet say how.

Upstairs, faintly, a bell rang the hour. And in the silence after it, from the direction of the cells, came a sound he almost didn't hear: footsteps. Many. Moving fast.
