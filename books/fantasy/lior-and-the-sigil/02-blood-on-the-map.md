## 2. Blood on the Map

The storeroom smelled of tallow, dried thyme, and the particular staleness of a room no one entered unless they needed something from it. Oscar Kavanagh had spent eleven years making himself into that kind of thing — useful, unremarkable, forgettable. He counted sacks of flour by candlelight because counting was what he did when his hands wanted to shake, and tonight his hands wanted to shake.

They had taken a courier in the kitchens at dusk. Oscar had watched it happen over the rim of a soup tureen he was pretending to inspect: four of the king's men moving through the steam and shouting scullions with the unhurried certainty of men who had never once been told *no*. The courier hadn't run. That was the strange part. He'd stood very still in the middle of the chaos, gray cloak dark with road dust, and looked directly at Oscar across twenty feet of crowded kitchen — looked at him the way no one ever looked at a food-taster, as if he were not furniture but a door.

Then they'd dragged him down toward the cells beneath the east tower, and the kitchen had gone back to being a kitchen.

Oscar had gone back to being a tureen-inspector. That was the trick of survival: you never saw anything, you never wanted anything, you were a body with a useful tongue and nothing behind the eyes worth interrogating. He had catalogued the courier's cloak in the ledger of his memory anyway — the way he catalogued everything, smuggler marks and toll-road tariffs and which lords watered their wine — because under the dust there had been a brand on the wool. A circle broken by three lines. Thornfell. The mountain roads. The routes he copied into ledgers and would never, in his life, be permitted to walk.

He was counting flour for the third time when the sound came: a wet, dragging scrape against the door of the storeroom, and then a weight falling against it hard enough to rattle the hinges.

Oscar did not move. Eleven years of instinct said *you heard rats, you saw nothing.* But the handle turned, slowly, and the door swung inward, and a man fell through it onto the cold flagstones like a sack of grain dropped from a height.

It was the courier. Of course it was the courier. His wrists were slicked red where the irons had chewed them, and there was a wound low on his side that had soaked through his shirt until the fabric clung black. He had gotten out of a cell under the east tower and up two flights of stairs and across half the castle's service ways on that wound alone, and now he lay in Oscar's storeroom among the flour sacks, breathing in short, tearing pulls, and looking up at Oscar with eyes that were already going glassy.

"You're the taster," the man said. His voice was wrecked but steady, the voice of someone who had rehearsed this on the stairs. "Kavanagh. They don't watch you. Nobody watches you."

"Guards—" Oscar began, and the man's hand shot out and closed around his ankle with shocking strength.

"No guards. No one. Listen to me." He fumbled at his cloak, at the inner seam, and Oscar watched him work a knife free one-handed and slit the lining open. What came out was wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was stained so thoroughly with blood that it seemed less wrapped than bandaged. "I'm Lior," the man said, absurdly, formally, as if they were being introduced at court. "Lior of the Vale Road. Last keeper. Do you understand? The last."

"I don't—"

"You will." Lior pushed the bundle into Oscar's hands. It was heavier than it looked, dense as a paving stone, and warm — warm from the man's body, or from something else, something Oscar felt through the oilcloth like a second pulse. "Take it north. Not to any lord, not to any mage, not to the crown — do you hear me? Especially not the crown. The mages started this. The king's own mages killed my brothers on the Vale Road and they'll kill everyone else until they hold it." He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it ended there was blood on his lips and a terrible calm in his face. "There's a map inside. Don't open it here. Don't open it anywhere with stone walls older than the kingdom. And Kavanagh — the Sigil is not a treasure. Whatever it tells you, whatever it shows you, whatever it promises — it's a prison. Keep it prisoned."

"The Sigil," Oscar repeated. The word meant nothing. The word felt enormous.

"They'll ask you if you saw me." Lior's grip on his ankle tightened. "You didn't. You've never seen me. You're nobody, aren't you? You've made yourself nobody. Good. Be nobody a little longer. Be nobody all the way to Thornfell." He smiled then, and it was the worst thing Oscar had ever seen, because it was relieved. "I carried it nine hundred miles. I get to stop carrying it. That's the whole of the oath, in the end. Someone carries it after you."

"Lior—" Oscar said, using the name without deciding to, and the man's eyes fixed on his with sudden fierce attention, as if hearing his name in a stranger's mouth was a kind of sacrament.

"Good," Lior whispered. "Good. Then it knows you now. I'm sorry about that. Truly."

And he died. Not dramatically — no arching, no last grand breath. He simply stopped pulling air, somewhere between one heartbeat and the next, and his hand slid from Oscar's ankle, and the storeroom was very quiet except for the candle flame and, faintly, impossibly, a warmth against Oscar's palms where the bundle rested, keeping its own time.

Oscar knelt there for a long moment. Then, because he was a practical man who had survived poison and smugglers' roads alike, he moved. He dragged Lior's body behind the flour racks, out of the sightline of the door. He stripped the dead man's boots — no, no, that was looting, that was what a desperate man did — and then he did it anyway, because the boots were road-worn and his own shoes were court-soft, and he was going to need road-worn. He took the knife from Lior's belt. He took the gray cloak off the corpse and folded it small, brand inward, and pushed it into the bottom of his ration satchel along with the oilcloth bundle, which he could not make himself set down once he had picked it up.

That was when he heard the boots in the corridor.

* * *

Veyra did not knock. The spymaster never knocked; doors were things that happened to other people. She filled the storeroom doorway the way winter fills a valley, gray-eyed and gray-cloaked, two of her quiet men fanned out behind her, and her gaze went around the room — flour, tallow, thyme, taster — with the flat thoroughness of a woman reading a ledger.

"Kavanagh." She pronounced it correctly, which somehow was worse than getting it wrong. "You're awake late."

"Inventory, my lady. The harvest accounts come due at month's end."

"Mm." She stepped inside. Her boot passed within a foot of the flour rack behind which a dead man was cooling. Oscar kept his breathing even by an act of will he would remember for the rest of his life. "A courier was taken this evening. Thornfell mark on his cloak. He's escaped his cell — killed a turnkey doing it, which means he's dangerous, which means he's close. You see anyone pass through the service ways?"

Here it was. The moment. Oscar had spent eleven years never lying to anyone who mattered, because lies were debts and debts got collected. He felt the bundle's warmth against his ribs where it sat under his apron, patient as a coal.

"No, my lady," Oscar said.

The lie came out smooth. That was the horrifying part — how easy it was, how natural, as though some door in him had been waiting eleven years to be opened and swung wide at the first touch. Veyra studied him. He let his shoulders be tired, his eyes be dull, his hands be floury. He was a nobody. He had made himself nobody. Be nobody a little longer.

"You taste the prince's food," Veyra said abruptly.

"When required."

"And you've never wanted more than that?"

The question hung there, and Oscar understood suddenly that it was not idle, that Veyra asked it the way an angler tests water. He thought of the ledgers full of roads he'd never walk. He thought of a dying man's relieved smile.

"What would be the point, my lady?" he said. "I have a useful tongue and nothing else. Men like me stay alive by staying small."

Something flickered in Veyra's gray eyes — not belief, exactly. Acknowledgment. She knew a survivor's grammar when she heard it; perhaps she respected it. "If you remember anything," she said, turning for the door, "a smell, a sound, a shadow out of place — you bring it to me before anyone else. There are people in this castle who would pay well for what that courier carried, and I'd rather their gold stayed in their purses and their necks stayed out of my work. Understood?"

"Understood, my lady."

She paused at the threshold. "Your hands are shaking, Kavanagh."

"It's the cold, my lady. The storerooms don't hold heat."

She held his gaze a moment longer — long enough for the silence to acquire weight — and then she left, and her men left, and the corridor boots faded, and Oscar stood alone in the tallow-smelling dark and discovered that he was laughing. Quietly, helplessly, with his floury hand pressed over his mouth. Terror and exhilaration had married somewhere in his chest and produced this: a silent, shaking laughter that tasted, absurdly, like the first sip of wine after a long fast.

He had lied to Veyra. To *Veyra*. And lived.

* * *

He worked until the bell rang terce. He packed a taster's satchel the way he'd pack for any errand — dried meat, hard cheese, a skin of watered wine — nothing that would be missed, nothing that would be questioned. He wrote out his inventory neatly and left it weighted on the table, because a man who vanished messily was a man who was chased, and a man who left tidy accounts was a man presumed to be about his business. He put on Lior's boots. They fit as if they'd been waiting for him.

Only when the castle slept did he allow himself to unwrap the oilcloth, just a corner, just enough to confirm what he was fleeing with.

It was a map. He had known it would be a map, and still the sight of it stopped his breath. Drawn on leather gone soft and dark with age — and blood, so much blood, soaked into the mountains themselves so that the Thornfell range ran in rust-brown ridges — it showed the passes he had spent years copying from secondhand ledgers, except drawn truly, drawn by someone who had walked every mile of them. And at the heart of the range, where no ledger he had ever read marked anything at all, the mapmaker had inked a single symbol: a circle, broken by three lines.

The same mark as the cloak. The same mark, he realized with a slow cold roll of his stomach, as the brand on the wax seal of certain royal dispatches he had been required to taste-test the documents beside — dispatches from the king's own mages.

*The mages started this.*

Under the symbol, in a fine old script, ran a line of text. Oscar could read four languages and the words still refused to settle into sense, shifting like fish when he looked at them directly. All he could hold of them, all that stayed fixed, was the last word:

*Sigil.*

The bundle pulped warm against his palm. Not warm from his body. Warm from itself. Keeping its own time.

Somewhere below the floor, deep in the bones of the castle, a door slammed, and voices rose — alarm bells would come soon, the escape discovered, the search widening, Veyra's gray eyes remembering a shaking pair of hands. Oscar wrapped the map, stowed it against his chest, pulled the gray hood up over his hair, and slipped out through the buttery into the blue hour before dawn, past the sleeping guard who had never once been taught to look at a food-taster.

The north gate was a rumor and the mountain passes were a ledger's promise, and for the first time in eleven years, Oscar Kavanagh was walking toward both instead of merely writing about them.

Behind him, faint in the stone, he could almost swear the castle was waking — and ahead, in the dark, the map beat against his chest like a second heart, counting out a rhythm he was beginning to fear he already knew.
