## 3. The Road He Was Never Allowed

Oscar had spent eleven years learning the Thornfell passes from paper.

He knew them the way a blind man knows a face: by touch, by ledger, by the dry arithmetic of trade. The Salt Road ran from Greyharrow to the mining camps at Kest's Hollow, four days' cart travel in summer, impassable after first snow. The Widow's Stair was a mule track cut into the cliffside above the Vane River, too narrow for wagons, taxed at the second switchback where the king's tollmen kept a fire burning year-round. The Smuggler's Cut — never named as such in any crown document — appeared in the ledgers only as *unrecorded losses along the northern route*, and Oscar had catalogued three years of those losses, cross-referenced against grain shipments and guard rotations, until he could have drawn the Cut from memory.

On paper. From a storeroom. Without ever once standing on any of it.

Now he stood on it, or near enough, and the paper had lied to him his whole life.

The pass above Greyharrow was not the neat ascending line the survey maps promised. It was a wound in the mountains, all scree and deadfall, the trail vanishing and reappearing like something that resented being followed. Snowmelt roared somewhere below him, unseen. The air tasted of stone and pine and distance. Oscar crouched behind a granite shoulder with Lior's map pressed flat against his chest and his lungs working like bellows, and he understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grief, that everything he knew was a drawing of a thing, and he had never known the thing itself.

Except that wasn't true either. Not anymore.

Because when he looked up at the ridgeline — the one the ledgers called the Grey Spine, elevation given in fathoms, timberline marked in red ink — he didn't see a line. He saw the place where a boy named Lior had broken his collarbone falling through a cornice, and had been carried down this same slope on his father's back, and had laughed the whole way because the pain meant he was alive and the mountain meant he was home.

Oscar had never fallen through a cornice. Oscar had never had a father who carried him anywhere.

The memory arrived the way blood arrives through cloth: slowly, then all at once, warm and wrong and impossible to stop.

* * *

He walked through the night because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant the memories came faster.

They came anyway. They came with every step.

It was the strangest thing about them, Oscar decided somewhere past midnight, hopping a frozen stream by starlight: they weren't visions. They weren't dreams. They were *recollections*, arriving with the flat certainty of things he himself had done. When he reached for a handhold on the trail above the stream, he already knew which stones were loose — not because he'd tested them, but because Lior had tested them, twenty years ago, running this path with a satchel of sealed letters and the particular joy of a boy who trusted the mountain completely.

When he smelled woodsmoke an hour later, he knew before cresting the rise that it would be a charcoal burner's camp, abandoned since the war, its kiln collapsed into a mossy green hump — and it was.

When he grew thirsty and cut east toward the sound of water, he knew the spring would run cold and iron-bright out of a split boulder, and that if he knelt at exactly the right spot there was a hollow worn smooth by generations of drinking cups, and his own knees found that hollow without his asking them to.

*This is what it is to know a road,* Oscar thought, kneeling at the spring, water dripping from his chin. *Not the weight of it. Not the tolls. The shape of it under a body.*

Eleven years of ledgers, and he had never once known anything.

He should have been grateful. He was grateful. That was the horror of it — how quickly gratitude curdled into appetite. He caught himself, walking, reaching for more: *What else? What did you eat on this stretch? Who taught you the stars? Did you love someone? Was there someone waiting at the end of these roads?*

And the blood answered. It always answered. A girl's laugh on a rooftop in some town he couldn't name. A teacher with burned hands reciting oaths in a language that made Oscar's teeth ache. A door, black iron, deep under stone, and a voice behind it saying *not yet, not yet, little keeper.*

He pulled his hand away from the water and saw the blood still crusted under his fingernails, brown now, Lior's last hours ground into his skin like pigment. He scrubbed at it with gravel until his fingers bled their own fresh red over the old. It changed nothing. The memories didn't live on his hands. He understood that now. They lived wherever the map lived, and the map lived against his heart, and he was beginning to suspect that the blood on it had never been an accident at all.

Lior had chosen him. Dying in a smuggler's cellar with hunters at the door, the courier had looked at a food-taster — a nobody, a useful body, a man built to be overlooked — and had *chosen* him.

*I'm sorry,* Oscar said to the dark, to the dead man whose childhood he was wearing like a stolen coat. *I don't know why me. I'll try to be worth it.*

Somewhere far behind him, a hawk cried once and went silent, and Oscar froze, because Lior's memory told him that hawks did not hunt at night in the Thornfells, but the king's falconers trained birds that did.

* * *

They found his trail at dawn. Or rather, they found what he'd left of it, and Oscar learned the difference between the two in the worst possible classroom.

He'd been careless in the first hours — no, worse than careless; he'd been *ignorant*, blundering through brush like the courtier he was, snapping branches, leaving boot prints in soft ground, doing everything the ledgers never mentioned because ledgers recorded goods, not men. He'd made it perhaps eight miles before the memories began correcting him, and by then the corrections were lessons delivered too late.

So when he heard the dogs — two of them, deep-voiced, working the slope below — he did the thing Lior's memory offered without being asked: he stopped panicking, and he listened.

*Water kills scent,* said the memory, calm as a schoolmaster. *But only moving water, and only if you walk IN it, not beside it. Cold stone holds heat till midmorning; lie on stone and the birds will mark you before the dogs do. And never, ever take the high trail when they're behind you — the high trail has no exits.*

The high trail was the one the survey maps showed. The one the ledgers described. The sensible route south toward Kest's Hollow and the river and, eventually, the coast.

Oscar took the low one.

It wasn't a trail at all. It was a seam in the mountain, a crack filled with alder and loose shale, dropping steeply toward the Vane River through country that no crown document acknowledged existed. He descended it on his heels and one palm, the map wrapped in oilcloth and strapped tight against his spine, breathing through his nose, listening past his own heartbeat. Twice the dogs seemed to swing toward him, and twice he slid into the river — freezing, waist-deep, gasping — and let the current take his scent apart while he waded downstream over slick stones, guided by a dead man's certainty.

Near midday he climbed out on the far bank, wrung out, half-dead with cold, and lay on a sun-warmed slab of granite while feeling returned to his hands in needles. Above him, across the valley, he heard the dogs cast in circles on the south bank, frustrated, and then a man's voice, sharp with anger, calling them off.

Veyra's hunters. The spymaster did not send fools.

*You've done this before,* Oscar thought at the memory, at the blood, at the ghost. *You've run from hunters before. Who hunted you, Lior? Who?*

And the answer rose up in him like cold water, and he wished with everything he had that it hadn't.

*A court mage knows a keeper's trail the way a wolf knows a deer's,* the memory said, in Lior's own voice, aged now, hardened, the boy grown into a man with gray in his beard. *They cannot read the Sigil, but they can read us. Master Ellery taught me the old ways so I could unlearn them fast enough. "Run where the land forgets," he said. "Never where the crown remembers."*

*The crown remembers,* Oscar thought. *The crown sent dogs after YOU too.*

Yes. Yes, it had. He could feel it now, feel the shape of it: couriers going out and not coming back. Keepers called to the capital and never heard from again. An order that had guarded the Sigil for a thousand years, thinned season by season, until only Lior remained, carrying a prison-map through the mountains with the king's own mages at his heels.

The court hadn't stumbled onto the Sigil. The court had been *hunting* it. For years. Patiently. With dogs and blades and the whole machinery of a kingdom.

Oscar lay on the warm stone and shook, and it was not the cold.

* * *

He made the charcoal burner's ruin by dusk and allowed himself a fire the size of a cupped hand, buried in the old kiln's shadow where even a hawk's eye would find nothing. He ate hardtack and dried sausage from the pack he'd fled with, and he laid the map open on his knees by firelight for the first time since the cellar.

In the dark it was just vellum and ink: mountain ranges drawn in a hand centuries dead, rivers, passes, the Thornfell spine rendered with loving precision. But where the heart of the range should have been, the mapmaker had done something strange — the lines converged, tightened, folded inward on themselves, again and again, until the center of the drawing was a knot of ink so dense it looked less like geography than like a wound. Like a door drawn shut a thousand times over.

His fingers hovered over it. The blood-memory stirred, cautious, like an animal sensing weather.

*Don't,* said Lior's voice, gentle and exhausted. *You're not ready. I wasn't ready, and I was born to it.*

"I have to be," Oscar whispered. "There's no one else."

*That's what I said.* A pause, and then, softer, with something that might have been pity: *You taste poison for a living. You know how this works. The deadliest things are the ones that seem like gifts.*

Oscar stared into the fire. Below him, miles down the valley, a light moved — a lantern, steady, methodical, sweeping the riverbank in long deliberate arcs. Then another. Then a third, higher up, on the ridge he'd refused at dawn.

They weren't chasing his trail anymore. They were closing a net around the whole valley, patient as tide, certain as tax collectors. Veyra hadn't guessed where he was going. She had simply worked out that the mountains were finite, and he was inside them.

He rolled the map, strapped it down, and killed the fire.

There was one route out of this valley that the net wouldn't cover, because it wasn't on any map — crown, survey, or smuggler's. It existed only in a dead man's memory: a fissure in the eastern wall, a day's crawl through the mountain's gut, emerging on the far side of the Grey Spine where the old order's roads began. Lior had gone through it once, at nineteen, terrified, following his master's voice in the dark.

Oscar would go through it alone, following a dead man's voice in his own head.

As he packed, the memory surfaced one last time, unprompted, and it was not a lesson about trails or dogs or stone. It was Lior, older, standing before that black iron door beneath the world, pressing his palm against it, saying words Oscar's mouth suddenly ached to repeat — and the door answering, in a voice like grinding continents:

*Who carries me?*

Oscar stood very still in the ruined kiln's darkness, his heart slamming, the lantern-light of hunters sweeping the valley below.

*Answer it honestly,* Lior's memory whispered. *It always knows.*

Far to the east, faint as a thought, something beneath the mountains turned over in its sleep — and Oscar felt it notice him.
