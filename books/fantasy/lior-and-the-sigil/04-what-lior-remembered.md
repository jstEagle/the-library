## 4. What Lior Remembered

The blood on Oscar's hands had dried to a brown crust by the second night, but the memories came anyway.

He woke in the hollow beneath a fallen pine with Lior's name already in his mouth — not his own name, but the other one, the dead man's — and for three heartbeats he did not know which of them he was. The cold helped. He pressed his palms into the frost-brittle needles and breathed until the world narrowed back down to aching feet and a map that burned against his ribs like a coal sewn into his coat.

*You are Oscar Kavanagh,* he told himself. *Food-taster. Nobody. A body with a useful tongue.*

But even the litany had begun to feel like someone else's.

He built no fire. Fires drew hunters, and Veyra's hunters were behind him — he could feel them the way a dog feels weather, a pressure at the base of his skull that might have been imagination and might have been something the blood had taught him. Instead he chewed a strip of smoked goat from Ashka-less days, from before the smugglers' road, when it had just been him and the map and the mountains swallowing him whole, and he let the memories come.

They came like water finding cracks.

* * *

A courtyard of white stone, older than any kingdom Oscar knew. Lior was seventeen, kneeling on flagstones worn smooth by a thousand years of knees, and an old woman was pressing her thumb to the center of his forehead.

"Say it," she said.

"I am the door that does not open," Lior said. His voice cracked on the vow, boyish, terrified, certain. "I am the hand that does not take. I am the last of the Keepers of the Sigil, and while I live, it sleeps."

"While you live," the old woman echoed. "And after?"

"After, another takes up the watch."

"And if there is no other?"

Lior looked up. In the memory, in the borrowed light of that impossible courtyard, Oscar saw the answer arrive in the boy's face before he spoke it — saw the exact moment a seventeen-year-old understood what the order actually asked of him.

"Then I die holding the door shut," Lior said.

The old woman smiled, and it was the saddest smile Oscar had ever seen, and he felt it on his own face, felt his own mouth shape it, alone under a dead pine in the Thornfell passes, weeping for a stranger's ordination.

* * *

He walked through the morning with the memory lodged in him like a splinter. The pass narrowed; the ledgers in his head — the ones he'd memorized from the crown's own route-books, tariffs and tolls and the quiet margins where smugglers moved — gave way to Lior's knowledge, which was better. Lior knew these roads the way a priest knows his own church. Here the trail split around a shelf of black rock, and here, Lior remembered, the rock was hollow, and here a rope had been anchored once, long rotted now, but the iron ring remained, green with age, and Oscar climbed it without thinking because Lior's hands remembered the grip.

That was the part that frightened him. Not the memories themselves. The ease of them.

By midday he could read the mountain's moods. By late afternoon he caught himself humming a song he'd never heard, a low walking-song with words in a dialect the crown had outlawed two hundred years ago, and he stopped so abruptly he nearly went off the trail.

"Stop it," he said aloud, to the blood, to the dead man, to whatever was seeping out of the map and into the marrow of him. "You're dead. I'm me. That's the whole arrangement."

The mountain said nothing. The map burned against his ribs.

* * *

That night the memory came stronger, and this time it was not a ceremony. This time it was fire.

* * *

The sanctuary at Vael Corren — Lior's sanctuary, home of the order for as long as there had been an order — was burning, and Lior was running through it barefoot, and the smoke tasted of something wrong. Not woodsmoke. Ozone and copper. Mage-fire.

He found Master Ellery in the scriptorium, pinned to her own desk by a spear of white light, and standing over her were three figures in the grey-and-gold livery of the royal court of Callowmere. Court mages. He knew their faces. They had dined at the sanctuary only that spring, all courtesy and curiosity, asking gentle questions about the vaults, about the seals, about how many Keepers remained.

"How many remain?" one of them asked Ellery now, in the same gentle voice, while she died. "We only want the map, mistress. The king wants only the map. Give us the location and the order can go on as it always has. We'll even let you keep your little vows."

"The vows are not little," Ellery said, and laughed, blood on her teeth, "and you know it, and that is why you burn us instead of buying us."

The mage sighed, the sigh of a bureaucrat inconvenienced, and turned to Lior in the doorway.

"You," he said. "Boy. How many of you are left?"

Lior ran. He ran through burning halls and out a postern gate he alone among the living knew, and behind him the sanctuary collapsed into itself with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and Lior knelt in the snow outside and swore a new vow, wordless, that he would carry the map beyond the reach of kings or die trying.

He was twenty-three years old. He had four more years, and he would spend every one of them running.

* * *

Oscar came back to himself sitting upright against the pine trunk, gasping, his hands shaking so badly he had to press them flat against the ground.

The court. *The court.*

All the way through the flight — through the kitchens, the storeroom, the lie he'd told Veyra with sweat running down his spine, the smugglers' roads, the passes — some part of him had assumed the shape of the thing: couriers hunted, a spymaster chasing, a fugitive running toward something the crown wanted stopped. He had cast himself, without ever saying it aloud, as the thief. The man who'd stolen something.

But he hadn't stolen anything. Lior had given it to him. And the men hunting it —

"They started it," he said to the dark. His voice came out ragged. "Your own king started it."

Because he knew the arithmetic of courts. He'd spent eleven years tasting food, watching faces over goblets, learning who feared whom and who paid whom. He knew what it meant when the crown's mages dined somewhere in spring and the place burned in autumn. He knew what it meant when a courier was arrested in the palace kitchens within hours of crossing the city walls — not caught, *arrested*, quietly, before dawn, before anyone official was supposed to know he existed. Someone inside had known Lior was coming. Someone inside had sold the route.

Veyra hadn't been chasing a thief. She'd been cleaning up her master's mess. And Oscar — invisible, useful, nobody Oscar — had been standing in the middle of it the whole time, holding the one thing in the world the king's mages would burn a sanctuary full of people to get.

Rage rose in him slow and total, like floodwater. It was not a feeling he had practice with. Eleven years of being nobody had taught him to swallow everything — insults, fear, the ache of watching other men live larger lives — and he swallowed this too, but it sat in him like a stone, cold and enormous, and he understood that it was not going anywhere.

They had killed an order that had kept the peace for a thousand years. They had killed a twenty-seven-year-old man in a cellar, and they would have done it in his storeroom too if he'd answered honestly when Veyra asked. And they called it law. They called it crown business. They would call it necessary, afterward, in the histories they wrote.

"No," Oscar said. The word came out steady. That surprised him. "No. You don't get it."

The map pulsed against his ribs, warm as a heartbeat, and for one vertiginous moment he could not tell whether the rage was his or Lior's, whether it mattered, whether *he* was still entirely anyone's.

* * *

The last memory of the night was small, and it undid him worse than the fire had.

* * *

A roadside shrine, weeks ago, weeks before the kitchens. Lior on his knees before a carved niche in the rock, the map wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. He was exhausted — Oscar could feel the exhaustion in his own legs now, in his own back, four years of running settled into the body like sediment — and he was talking to the shrine's forgotten god the way tired men talk to anyone who will listen.

"There's a taster," Lior said. "In the palace. I've seen him. He stands behind the prince's chair and he never looks at anything, and he's the only person in that whole glittering cage who looks like he knows exactly what everything costs." A pause. A tired laugh. "I don't know why I'm telling you. If it goes wrong — if they catch me — someone has to take it somewhere they can't follow. And he knows the roads, that one. You can see it in him. He's memorized roads he's never been allowed to walk." Another pause, longer. "I hope it doesn't come to that. I hope I'm fast enough. But if it comes to that —"

He didn't finish. In the memory he touched the map through the oilcloth, the way a man touches a wound, and stood, and walked on south toward the city and the kitchens and the cellar and the end.

* * *

Oscar sat until the sky went grey, and then pink, and then the colour of ordinary mornings, and he did not sleep again.

*He chose me.* Not chance. Not desperation. A dying man, with the whole of the Thornfell wilderness around him and a hundred villages between him and the border, had looked at a palace food-taster he'd spoken to perhaps twice and thought: *him. He knows the cost of things. He'll carry it.*

Grief and fury braided together in his chest until he couldn't separate them, and maybe they weren't meant to be separated, maybe that was what it meant to inherit something. He was grieving a stranger and he was becoming the stranger and the crown that had made him invisible had made him invisible precisely so that no one would ever guess he could be dangerous, and now the most powerful mages in Callowmere wanted him dead, and he found — testing the thought carefully, the way he tested food, a grain at a time — that he was not afraid.

Or he was afraid, but it was underneath something else now.

He broke camp at first light, rolling the map's weight against his ribs, and set his face toward the high passes, toward the sealed vault at the spine of Thornfell that Lior's memories knew and the crown's ledgers only guessed at. Somewhere behind him, patient as winter, Veyra's hunters were climbing.

Ahead, in the low country where the passes dropped into the river valleys, there were smugglers — routes in his head, names in the margins of ledgers, and one name written bolder than the rest: *Ashka Vey*, who moved cargo the crown didn't ask about, and who, if half the ledger gossip was true, owed favors in places no honest person survived.

Oscar Kavanagh, food-taster, nobody, heir to a murdered order, went looking for a criminal.
