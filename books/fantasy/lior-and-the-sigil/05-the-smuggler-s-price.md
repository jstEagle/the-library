## 5. The Smuggler's Price

The Saltwife rode at anchor in the drowned harbor of Greyhaven, and she looked exactly like what she was: a lie with sails.

Oscar had walked three days to reach her, following the route he'd memorized from a ledger seven years ago — the one entry the crown's clerks always skipped, the salt run that came down the coast and vanished into the fens. He'd known it existed the way he'd known everything at court: precisely, uselessly, from a distance. Now his boots were actually on the shingle, and the shingle was black with old tar, and somewhere behind him Veyra's hunters were combing the passes for a dead man's map.

He'd stopped sleeping. The memories came anyway.

They arrived now without warning, riding up through his palms where Lior's blood had soaked into the skin and never washed out — he'd tried, in a mountain stream so cold it burned, scrubbing until his hands were raw, and the brown stain had only spread, delicate as frostwork, up past his wrists. *The ferryman at Duskwater takes silver but not stamped silver.* *There is a hollow under the third cairn past the split oak; the order kept letters there for two hundred years.* *Lior's mother sang off-key and didn't care who knew it.*

That last one had come while he was eating a stolen apple, and Oscar had sat down hard on a rock and wept for a woman he'd never met, tasting apples that weren't his memory of apples at all.

*You're becoming him,* said a voice in his head that might have been his own and might have been older. *Or he's becoming you. At this point, does it matter?*

It mattered. He clung to that. It mattered because if it didn't matter, then Oscar Kavanagh was already gone, and all that remained was a vessel walking toward Thornfell with someone else's grief in its chest.

He hired a fisher boy to row him out, because shouting across open water at a smuggler ship was how men ended up in the fen with their throats opened. The Saltwife's hull rose over him, barnacled and patched, and a face appeared at the rail — a woman, broad-shouldered, gray shot through her black braid, with a scar that split her left eyebrow and gave her a permanent expression of having heard better lies than yours.

"You're either brave or stupid," she called down. "Rowing alone to my ship."

"I'm neither," Oscar said. "I'm desperate, which is worse, and therefore honest."

Something flickered in her face. Interest, maybe. "Come up, then. Honest men are rare enough to be entertaining."

* * *

Her name was Ashka Vey, and her cabin smelled of pitch, pepper, and old smoke. She poured two cups of something that tasted like a forge fire felt and watched him drink without touching her own.

"Oscar Kavanagh." She turned the name over like a coin she suspected of being clipped. "Food-taster. I know what you are. Half the coast knows by now — the king's spymaster has your face on every dock from here to the Thornfell gates. Dead or alive, and alive pays double." She smiled without warmth. "Which tells me whatever you're carrying is worth more than your life. So. Show me."

Oscar set down the cup. His heart was going like a drum in a procession, but his hands were steady — they were always steady; it was the one thing twenty years of poison-adjacent living had taught them. "Passage first. Then the map."

"Passage to where?"

"Thornfell. The high passes. The spine of the mountains."

Ashka laughed, short and sharp. "Nobody goes to the spine. Nobody's gone to the spine since the Shattering, and the ones who tried before that came back wrong or didn't come back." She leaned forward. "So either you don't know what you're asking, or you know exactly, and both answers interest me. Map. Now. Or my second throws you back in the water and we see if the fish are hungry."

He could have lied. He considered it — weighed the odds, ran the routes, did all the careful arithmetic that had kept him alive this long. And then he thought about Lior dying in his arms with the truth still wet on his lips, and understood that the arithmetic had changed. Careful men died alone in storerooms. Whatever got him through the next month would not be caution.

He unrolled the map on her table.

The blood-marks caught the lamplight and moved in it — he'd seen that before, refused to believe it, saw it again now. Ashka went very still. Her hand drifted toward the map and stopped an inch above it, as if above a stove.

"That mark," she said quietly. "The circle with the broken line through it. My grandmother drew it on my palm when I was six. She told me if I ever saw it on anything — paper, stone, skin — I was to walk away and keep walking." Her eyes came up. "She was Sigil-keeper's kin, third generation removed. She used to say the order wasn't dead, just sleeping, and that when it woke, the world would shake loose from its hinges." She straightened. "You're carrying the prison, aren't you. Not a map of it. The thing itself."

"The map is the seal," Oscar said. "And it's failing. That's why the blood moves. That's why the court wants it. Their mages mean to open it."

"And you mean to do what? Re-seal it? You're a food-taster."

"I mean to reach the vault," Oscar said. "What happens after that, I'll decide there. With what's left of me."

Ashka studied him for a long moment. Outside, gulls screamed against the hull, and the tide knocked the ship gently against its own anchor chain.

"Here's my price," she said at last. "Not coin. Coin I have. Here's my price: if we're taken — if Veyra's hunters board us or corner us in the passes — you give up the location. The vault. Exactly where it is, how it's entered, all of it. To save my crew. That's the price. Take it or swim."

Oscar stared at her. "You want me to promise to betray the thing I'm risking my life to protect."

"I want you to promise to value lives over secrets," Ashka said. "My crew follows me into the teeth of the king's navy for a stranger and a cursed map. If it goes wrong, they die for it. Unless you make sure they don't. That's the price, taster. Trust isn't free, and immunity doesn't exist. There's only what you're willing to lose, and for whom."

He thought of Veyra's face in the storeroom doorway, patient as a spider. He thought of the hunters in the passes. He thought of Lior, who had given everything to a stranger rather than let the order's work die with him.

"All right," Oscar said. "If we're taken, you get the location. But hear me, Captain — if we're taken, the map fails anyway, and everyone dies slower. So maybe we should both be very good at not being taken."

Ashka grinned, and for a moment the scar relaxed and she looked younger. "Now that," she said, "is the first honest thing anyone's said on this ship all year. We sail on the morning tide."

* * *

They sailed on the morning tide, and for four days the sea was almost kind.

Oscar learned the crew: Doss, the second-in-command, a lean, cheerful man with a laugh like a door hinge and a knife he spun when he was thinking; the twins, Marrin and Sefa, who worked the rigging like extensions of it; old Brannoc at the wheel, who had sailed these waters before the war scars on the coast were scars. They treated Oscar with the rough courtesy sailors extend to bad luck — politely, from a distance, superstitiously.

Doss broke the distance on the second night, sitting beside him at the rail with a jug of small beer.

"Heard you taste poison for a living," Doss said. "That true? You can tell death by the tongue?"

"Sometimes," Oscar said. "Bitter almonds. Certain metals. Rot under honey."

"Huh." Doss took a pull of beer. "Must be a strange way to live. Knowing everything's trying to kill you and nobody knowing your name."

"It's a safe way to live," Oscar said. "That's what I told myself, anyway."

"And now?"

Oscar looked out at the dark water, where the blood-stain on his wrists seemed faintly luminous, though he knew it wasn't. "Now I'm on a smuggler ship carrying a god's prison into the mountains," he said. "So apparently safety was a story I told myself too."

Doss laughed his hinge-door laugh. "Captain likes you. She doesn't like anyone. Must be the honesty. It's rarer than you'd think, even among thieves." He stood, stretched. "Get some sleep, taster. Tomorrow we make the fen channels, and after that the passes, and after that—" he waggled his hand, "—whatever the mountains decide."

Oscar slept. For the first time since the storeroom, he dreamed of nothing at all, and woke feeling almost like himself — whoever that was turning out to be.

* * *

It was Sefa who spotted the sloop, on the fifth day, hanging back at the edge of visibility with the patience of a wolf that knows the herd must eventually tire.

"Customs cutter," Brannoc said, squinting. "Or wearing the paint of one."

"Veyra," Oscar said, and the name tasted of copper.

Ashka ordered the channels — the labyrinthine fen passages where deep-keeled ships couldn't follow — and for a day they threaded them, silent, poles and sweeps instead of sails, the crew moving like ghosts. It almost worked.

It was Doss who went ashore at the fen village of Mireholt to buy fresh water and word of the road ahead. He went grinning, spinning his knife, whistling. He never came back.

They waited until the tide turned. They waited until Ashka's knuckles went white on the rail. Then a fen boat came poling out through the reeds, unmanned except for a bundle lashed amidships, and the bundle was Doss, alive, bound, and smiling apologetically around his gag — and pinned to his coat was a letter sealed with the crown's black wax.

Ashka read it once. Her face did not change, which was how Oscar knew it was very bad.

"She has him," Ashka said flatly. "Has had him since yesterday. This is her offer." She read aloud, voice like gravel sliding: "'To the captain of the Saltwife. Deliver the fugitive Kavanagh and the item he carries, and your man returns whole, your ship unmolested, and your names struck from the crown's lists. Refuse, and I will return him to you piece by piece, and I will begin with the parts he uses to smile.'" She lowered the letter. "Veyra signs her threats like poetry. It's unsettling."

"What do we do?" Sefa whispered. Marrin had gone gray. Old Brannoc said nothing, but his hands shook on the wheel, and Oscar saw that they all loved Doss, and that love was the wound Veyra had chosen to cut.

Ashka looked at Oscar. And Oscar understood, with a clarity like cold water, that this was the price coming due — not the one she'd named, but the real one, the one underneath. He could give himself up. He could give up the map. He could stand here and watch a good man be dismantled for the crime of buying water.

"No," Oscar said, before anyone else spoke. "Don't answer her. Don't trade me, don't trade the map, and don't trade Doss."

"Then what, taster?" Ashka's voice cracked like ice. "You have a better idea? Because I promised my crew—"

"You promised me the location if we were taken. We haven't been taken yet." He pulled the map from his coat, and the blood-marks writhed in the daylight, and every member of the crew flinched from it. "But listen to me. If Veyra gets this seal broken open, there won't be a crown to strike names from. There won't be coasts. The mages want to start the old war again, the one that shattered Thornfell, and they need this to do it. Doss is bait because she thinks fear works on us. So we prove it doesn't."

"How?" Sefa asked, tears cutting tracks through the salt on her face.

"We go to the exchange," Oscar said. "On our terms, at a place of our choosing, in the passes where my knowledge beats hers. And we take him back."

Ashka stared at him. "You're proposing we ambush the king's spymaster."

"I'm proposing," Oscar said, and felt Lior's memories rise in him like a tide, the passes, the paths, the hidden ways between the cairns, *his* knowledge now, whether he wanted it or not, "that we stop running. She expects a taster and a smuggler to flee to the last. Let's show her what a keeper's heir does instead."

The words were out of his mouth before he knew them. *Keeper's heir.* He hadn't decided that. Or he had, somewhere deeper than deciding.

Ashka held his gaze for three long breaths. Then she crumpled the letter in her fist and turned to her crew.

"Brannoc — the old channel, the one my grandmother used. Sefa, Marrin — break out the pass-cargo, we go light and fast. We're going into the mountains." She looked back at Oscar, and there was something new in her scarred face, something that might have been faith. "And gods help us all, the taster leads."

Far behind them, at the mouth of the fens, the sloop turned and began, unhurried, to follow.
