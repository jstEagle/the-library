## 6. The Court of Broken Oaths

Veyra had knelt before kings and liars and learned to tell them apart by the smell. Kings smelled of incense laid over old stone. Liars smelled of ink.

The chamber beneath the Sunspire smelled of both, and something else besides — a sweetness like rotting honey that clung to the back of the throat. She stood at the foot of the long table in the Conclave Room, where the king's mages kept their records, their instruments, and their secrets, and she watched Archmagister Corvane unroll the courier's recovered satchel across black oak with the tenderness of a man handling a lover's hair.

"The cell was empty," she said. "The prisoner gone. Your wardens saw nothing."

"Prisons are for men," Corvane said mildly. He was a narrow man, dry as a winter reed, with eyes the grey of ash after rain. "The map is what matters. And the map is not gone — it is *found*. It has simply changed hands." His fingers hovered above the bloodstained leather without touching it. "You felt it when you entered the room, didn't you? Don't lie to me, spymaster. Lying is my trade."

She had felt it. A pressure behind the sternum, like a hand pressing gently on a door she had nailed shut thirty years ago. She said nothing, which was its own confession.

Corvane smiled. "Of course you did. Blood remembers." He turned the satchel over. The Thornfell brand was seared into the leather — a mountain split by a line of light — and beneath it, half-scoured but legible, a second mark: a circle of nine small stars. Veyra's stomach turned over slowly, the way it does on a stair missed in the dark.

"You know that mark," Corvane observed.

"I know many marks."

"You know *that* one." He said it without heat, almost kindly, and that was worse. "Your mother wore it stitched inside her collar. I had the garment burned, if you're wondering. There was very little left of her otherwise."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere above them, the Sunspire's bells rang the third hour, and the sound came down through the stone like water through a cracked jar.

"I was eight," Veyra said.

"Yes." Corvane folded his hands. "And the crown took you in, fed you, trained you, and made you the most dangerous woman in the kingdom. Gratitude is a debt most people pay gladly. You have paid yours for twenty years without once asking what it purchased." He gestured at the satchel. "It purchased this. So let me show you what your loyalty has been buying all along, Veyra. You've earned the truth. Truth is a reward we give to those who can no longer be harmed by it."

He led her deeper into the Conclave, past shelves of sealed jars and charts of the night sky annotated in dead languages, down a stair that had not existed when she'd memorized the palace plans fifteen years ago. That fact alone should have sent her running. Instead she followed, because that was what she did, and because some traitorous part of her had been waiting thirty years for someone to open this particular door.

* * *

The room at the bottom held a map of the world.

Not a map of the kingdom — a map of everything, drawn on a table of fused glass forty feet long, mountains raised in silver, seas poured in blue enamel. And across the northern reaches, where every schoolchild learned to say *the Shattered Marches* and lower their voices, the glass was scarred. Blackened. Fused into shapes like frozen lightning.

"The Sundering," Corvane said. "Three hundred years ago. Nine kingdoms became cinders and quarrels. The histories blame a war, a plague, an angry god — whatever keeps children quiet." He walked along the table's edge, his reflection sliding across the glass. "It was none of those things. It was the Sigil. A working so vast it could bind the wills of nations — or break them. Its keepers shattered it rather than let a tyrant wear it, and the breaking tore the north apart. The power didn't die, Veyra. It went to ground. It slept in the bones of Thornfell, waiting for someone with the wit to find the map and the spine to use it."

"And the king wants to use it."

"The king wants his grandfather's borders back." Corvane stopped at the scarred heart of the table. "We want him to have them. One crown. One law. One will, from the southern sea to the ice. No more smuggler roads, no more free cities, no more little kingdoms squabbling while the world rots at the edges. Order, Veyra. Real order. The kind that lasts a thousand years because no one alive can remember anything else."

She looked at the blackened glass and thought of the stories her mother had told her in the last year before the soldiers came — told her in whispers, with the shutters closed, stories about nine stars and a circle of oath-keepers who died rather than let any one man hold the world's throat. Her mother had sung those stories as lullabies. *Sleep, little knife, the circle holds.* She had thought they were nonsense. Children believe their mothers are made of nonsense; it takes thirty years and a room full of fused glass to learn otherwise.

"The keepers," she said carefully. "The order. You destroyed them."

"We outlived them. There's a difference, though I grant it's subtle." Corvane traced one silver ridge — the Thornfell spine. "The last keeper died three days ago in your cells. Or rather, he escaped your cells and died anyway, which amounts to the same thing. But he passed the map to someone. A taster, our sources say. A nobody." He smiled again, and this time there was nothing kind in it at all. "A nobody carrying the most dangerous object in the world through country you know better than anyone. Which is why you will find him, Veyra. And why, when you do, you will bring me the map intact — and the taster breathing. The Sigil requires a bearer. We've waited three centuries for a vessel with no attachments, no history, no one to mourn him. Your food-taster is perfect."

Something cold slid down Veyra's spine and pooled there.

"And if he refuses?"

"Then the mages will take what they need from him the hard way. Bearers can be broken. It's been done before." Corvane said it the way a man discusses grain tariffs. "But I'd prefer him willing. A broken vessel cracks under the weight. An empty one pours clean."

*Empty*, she thought. *He means erased.* She had seen what the Conclave's older workings did to the minds they touched — the penitents in the deep cells who smiled at walls and answered questions in other people's voices. Oscar Kavanagh would become one of those: a body walking around with the Sigil seated in it like a rider on a gentle horse, and the man himself nowhere at all.

She had hunted thieves and traitors for two decades and slept well afterward. This was the first time the work had ever tasted like blood in her mouth.

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She sat in her tower office with the lamp turned low and a decanter she did not drink from, and she read the file she had built on Oscar Kavanagh over four days of frantic inquiry.

It was thin. That was the remarkable thing — thin as parchment, thin as a blade. A taster for eleven years. No debts, no lovers, no vices worth the name. He had once reported a bribed under-cook and refused the reward. He knew the smuggler routes better than her own road-agents, from ledgers he was never permitted to act on, and had never once tried to sell that knowledge, though she could name a dozen men who'd have paid a farm for it. He had spent his whole life making himself invisible, and he had done it so thoroughly that even her network had nearly overlooked him.

*A nobody,* Corvane had called him, with contempt.

Veyra had been a nobody once too. A girl in a burning village in the Thornfell foothills, watching soldiers in royal blue carry her mother out by the arms, learning in a single night that the crown's justice was a door that only opened inward. The crown had picked her up, polished her, pointed her at its enemies, and called the pointing *purpose*. She had believed it because believing was easier than the alternative, and the alternative was admitting she had spent her life serving the men who made her an orphan.

Now the alternative sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

She opened the drawer where she kept the things she was supposed to have destroyed. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was a scrap of embroidered collar-cloth, rescued from a fire twenty-nine years ago by an eight-year-old's quick hands and a lifetime of silence. Nine small stars in a circle. Her mother's stitching. She had told herself she kept it as evidence. She had never once shown it to anyone, which meant she had always known it was something else.

An inheritance.

Her mother had been a keeper. Not a soldier of the order — Veyra understood that now, reading between the lines of Corvane's elegant menace — one of the last, hiding in plain sight in a border village, passing the circle's truths to her daughter in the shape of lullabies. And the order's truths were simple enough for a child: *no one man may hold the world's throat. Break the hand that tries.*

Corvane wanted her to deliver a boy into a working that would hollow him out and seat an empire in the empty place. The king wanted his grandfather's borders. The mages wanted their godhood. And somewhere on the smuggler roads of Thornfell, a food-taster who had never hurt anyone was running for his life carrying the only thing standing between the realm and a second Sundering.

Veyra put the collar-cloth back in the drawer and closed it gently, the way you close a coffin.

Then she pulled a fresh sheet of paper, dipped her pen, and wrote the orders that would go out at dawn: double the watch on the mountain passes. Seal the river crossings. Every informant, every cutpurse, every friendly ear from here to the ice — find the taster.

She wrote it all in her precise, merciless hand. And then, at the bottom, in letters so small they might have been an accident of the pen, she added a line no clerk would ever transcribe:

*Kavanagh — if you can read this, run west, not north. They expect north.*

She sealed the dispatch, set it aside, and sat looking at the lamp flame until it guttered, listening to the bells count the hours toward morning, wondering which of them she was now: the loyal hound, or the daughter of the nine stars.

Outside her window, far to the north, thunder rolled off the Thornfell peaks — though the sky was clear, and had been for days.

The Sigil, she thought, was waking up.

And so, God help her, was she.
