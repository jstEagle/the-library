## 7. Where the Map Ends

The mountain's spine rose against the dawn like a row of broken teeth, and Oscar had been climbing toward it for three days on roads that existed in no ledger he had ever catalogued.

He knew this because Lior knew it. The thought arrived without bitterness now, the way a scar stops aching and simply becomes part of the body. Somewhere between the second pass and the frozen waterfall they'd crossed at dusk, the memories bleeding through his hands had stopped feeling like intrusions. They felt like furniture. Like rooms he'd always lived in.

*The trail forks at the split stone,* Lior's memory supplied, calm as a clerk reciting inventory. *Take the left, even though it looks wrong. The right-hand path was cut by miners who never came back.*

Oscar took the left. Behind him, Ashka Vey moved with the economical silence of a woman who had spent thirty years making other people's mistakes profitable, her hand never far from the curved knife at her hip.

"You keep stopping," she said. "You keep stopping, and then you know things."

"I told you how it works."

"You told me blood remembers. I've bled enough to know blood mostly just wants out." She came up beside him, breath steaming. "What I want to know is what happens when it finishes remembering. When there's nothing left of Lior to give you and all that's left is you wearing him."

Oscar didn't answer. He didn't have one.

They crested the ridge as the sun cleared the eastern peaks, and there it was: a cleft in the mountain's spine, narrow as a wound, its walls bearing carvings worn nearly smooth by ten centuries of wind. Oscar's feet — Lior's feet — knew the way down before his eyes did. Stone steps hidden under scree. A passage that smelled of cold iron and old snow.

And at the bottom, a door.

Not wood. Not stone. A seam in the mountain itself, sealed with a sigil burned into the rock in lines that still held a faint heat after a thousand years — a knot of interlocking strokes that hurt to look at directly, the way the sun hurts. The map in Oscar's coat had grown warm against his chest since dawn. Now it was almost feverish.

"This is it," Ashka said quietly. "Where my maps end and yours begins." She looked at the door a long moment. "My grandmother told stories about this place. Keeper-stories. I thought they were songs to put children to sleep." Her jaw tightened. "She died poor and loyal and forgotten. So mind yourself, taster. Whatever's behind that door has been waiting a long time for someone foolish enough to open it."

Oscar raised his blood-marked hand toward the seal.

The Sigil spoke.

Not in sound. It bypassed his ears entirely and bloomed inside his skull like ink dropped in water, a voice made of every voice he'd ever half-heard — courtiers, kings, smugglers, and beneath them all something vast and patient and terribly gentle.

***You brought it back to me, little taster.***

Oscar staggered. Ashka caught his arm. "What? What did it say?"

"It doesn't talk to you," he managed. "It talks to me."

***You have carried my keeper's blood across kingdoms. You have kept my map from the crown's soft-handed thieves. I have watched you your whole life, Oscar Kavanagh. I have seen you swallow poison so a prince might eat safely, and be thanked with a nod. I have seen you memorize roads you were forbidden to walk, names you were forbidden to speak, a life you were forbidden to live.***

The door's seal pulsed. Warmth spread up Oscar's arm, into his chest, and with it came — offered, not forced, held out like a merchant displaying wares —

*Lior. All of him.*

It was a single heartbeat of complete memory: a boy on a mountain path learning the keeper-signs; a young man swearing an oath in a circle of hooded elders; Lior laughing, Lior grieving, Lior running through the palace undercity with the map burning against his ribs, Lior dying in a storeroom and choosing, with his last breath, to give the map to a stranger because a stranger might be free enough to finish what he could not.

Every hour of him. Every year. Everything the blood had been leaking into Oscar drop by drop, offered now as the whole ocean.

***And more than that.*** The voice curled around him, honeyed, reasonable. ***I am the power that shattered kingdoms, yes. I am also the power that could remake them. You have spent your life being no one, Oscar Kavanagh. Invisible. Useful. Discarded. Take me, and you will never be invisible again. Kings will taste their own food. Spymasters will knock before entering your thoughts. The world will know your name —***

"Stop," Oscar whispered.

***— or keep it secret, if you prefer. That is the difference between me and the crown, little taster. The king would take. I only ask.***

His hand hovered over the seal. It would be so easy. One touch and he would never again be the man who stood in doorways while better men talked. He would know what Lior knew, love what Lior loved, grieve what Lior grieved — he would *be*, in every way that mattered, someone who had lived a real life instead of a useful one.

His fingers trembled an inch from the stone.

That was when Ashka shouted.

He turned in time to see Dren — Ashka's second, the quiet man who'd carried their rope and cooked their meals and laughed at Oscar's terrible jokes around the fire — standing at the mouth of the passage with six soldiers fanned out behind him and Veyra's grey cloak snapping in the wind at his shoulder.

"I'm sorry, Captain," Dren said, and he sounded like he meant it. "They have my brother."

"They *had* your brother," Ashka snarled, knife already out, "until you handed them the only thing keeping him alive — me standing between the crown and that door!"

Steel rang. Ashka moved like something out of the old songs, all economy and fury, putting herself between the soldiers and Oscar with no hesitation at all, and Oscar understood in one lurching instant that this was the price she'd named in the smuggler's den come due — not gold, not the Sigil's location, but this: her body in the gap, buying seconds.

A blade found her side. She grunted, twisted, opened the man's throat, and went down to one knee.

"*Go*, taster!" she roared. "Open the bloody door!"

Veyra pushed past her own soldiers, and for a moment the spymaster's eyes met Oscar's across the chaos — and there was something in them that wasn't hatred. Something almost like grief.

"The mages are coming," she said. "Not me. *Them.* Whatever you're about to do, boy, do it fast."

Oscar pressed his palm to the seal.

Heat, light, a sound like a great bell struck underwater — and the mountain opened.

He stumbled through into darkness lit by veins of pale fire running through the walls, heard Ashka scream something wordless and defiant, heard the door begin grinding shut behind him — and in the last sliver of light he saw Veyra turn, blades drawn, not toward him but toward her own king's men.

Then stone met stone, and Oscar Kavanagh stood alone in the dark with a voice that was still speaking, patient as mountains, offering him everything.

***Well done, little taster. Now — shall we finish what your Lior started?***

And God help him, some traitor part of him — the part that had swallowed poison for princes, that had memorized roads he'd never walk, that had wanted, all his invisible life, to matter — whispered back:

*Yes.*
