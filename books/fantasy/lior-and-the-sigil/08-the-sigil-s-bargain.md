## 8. The Sigil's Bargain

The vault did not have walls. It had memories.

Oscar understood this the way he understood poison on his tongue — instantly, completely, without any hope of pretending otherwise. The chamber beneath Thornfell's spine was a sphere of black stone veined with light that moved like blood through a body, and every vein was a thought, and every thought was older than the kingdoms that had grown and shattered above it. He stood at the center of it, and the center of it stood in him.

*You came,* said the Sigil, in a voice that was not a voice. It spoke in Lior's cadence, in Veyra's, in Oscar's own — a chorus of borrowed mouths. *You carried me across the passes. You kept me warm against your ribs like a second heart.*

"You killed Lior," Oscar said. His voice cracked against the stone and came back to him changed.

*I gave him everything,* the Sigil answered, and there was something almost wounded in it, something vast and childish at once. *I showed him the war entire. I offered him the world made whole again. He chose instead to die in a smuggler's cellar with my map pressed into a stranger's hands. Do you know what that is, taster? That is ingratitude.*

The veins of light flared, and the vault opened its throat, and Oscar fell into the past.

* * *

He saw mountains before they were broken. He saw the Thornfell range whole, green-shouldered, crowned with snow that no army's smoke had ever blackened. And beneath them he saw the Sigil as it had first been made: not a map, not a prison, but a covenant — nine mages standing in a ring on the spine of the world, binding their own lives into the stone so that the power they'd unearthed would never walk free again.

He saw what they had buried. It had no name he could hold. It was hunger wearing the shape of glory; it was the promise that every wound could be unmade, every dead man raised, every border redrawn by whoever held it. It had come up out of the deep places when the first kings mined too greedily, and it had whispered, and the kings had listened, and the listening had been the beginning of the war.

The ancient war unfolded around him like a wound being reopened. He watched three kingdoms burn each other for the right to carry it. He watched cities whose names were struck from every ledger afterward — he knew ledgers; he had catalogued half the crown's — erased because remembering them meant remembering what men had done to win. He watched the nine mages die one by one over two hundred years, each death renewing the seal, each seal thinner than the last, until only the map remained: ink and blood and oath, a prison small enough to hide in a courier's cloak.

And then he saw Lior.

Not the dying man in the storeroom. Lior young, Lior sworn, Lior kneeling in a hidden chapel under the palace itself while grey-robed keepers laid their hands on his head. *Last of the order,* the memory whispered. *Sworn to carry the seal far from court, to let it sleep, to die nameless if namelessness would keep it safe.* Oscar felt the oath settle into Lior's bones like cold iron, and felt, beneath it, the thing Oscar himself had always known from the other side: the ache of a person built to be invisible, wanting, just once, to matter.

The court's mages had found him anyway. Of course they had. They had spent generations searching for the order they'd betrayed, and when they finally cornered Lior in the archive stacks, they hadn't killed him. They had offered him everything the Sigil offered — memory, power, a name that would outlive nations.

Lior ran. Lior bled. Lior carried the map through roads only smugglers knew, and when the poison they'd fed him finally closed its fist around his heart, he did not spend his last hour cursing them.

He spent it choosing.

Oscar watched the choice happen from inside it — inside Lior's failing body, in the dark of the king's own kitchens, hearing footsteps that might have been guards or might have been a food-taster coming off shift. And he heard Lior think, with the last clarity of a dying man: *Not power. Not even to save myself. A stranger will do. A stranger who knows how to be nobody — that is the safest place in the world to hide a dangerous thing.*

"He chose you," the Sigil said softly, all around him. "Do you see? He looked at every soul in that palace and he chose the one who tasted poison for a living. He chose you *because* you were nothing. Is that the legacy you want, little taster? To be someone's hiding place?"

"Yes," Oscar said.

The word surprised him with how easily it came.

* * *

The light dimmed. The vault breathed. And in the breathing silence, Oscar knelt on black stone and wept — not for himself, but for Lior, whom he now knew the way he would never know anyone again: entirely, briefly, from the inside. He knew the scar on Lior's knee from falling out of an orchard tree at eight years old. He knew the prayer Lior muttered before every road. He knew that Lior had been afraid, all the way down the mountain, and had run anyway.

"Show me the rest," Oscar said hoarsely. "Show me what I'd be if I took you."

*Gladly.*

And the Sigil showed him: Oscar Kavanagh, crown's food-taster, walking into the throne room with death in his pocket and no tongue quick enough to detect it. Oscar unmaking Veyra with a thought. Oscar reforging the three kingdoms — it would be easy, the Sigil promised, it would be *kind*, no more wars, no more hungry winters, only peace, forever, enforced by a power no blade could reach. Oscar remembered forever. Oscar mattering forever.

All he had to do was stop being Oscar.

That was the part the visions didn't say aloud, but he heard it anyway, the way he tasted bitter almond under honey. Every memory the Sigil gave him was real because it had been taken from someone. Every power it offered was built out of the same theft. To wield it was to become the hunger wearing the shape of glory. There was no version of this where he remained the man Lior had chosen. There was only the version where Lior's last act — the dying stranger pressing a map into a nobody's hands, trusting the nobody to be exactly what he was — became the seed of everything the nine mages had died to prevent.

"No," Oscar said.

He said it quietly, and the vault went very still, the way a court goes still when a servant says something true.

"I'm not going to wield you," Oscar said. "And I'm not going to give you to people who will. Lior sealed you once. I'll finish it."

*Then you are nothing,* the Sigil said, and for the first time it sounded afraid. *A taster. A body. A man who exists so that others may live past their poisons—*

"Yes," Oscar said. "Exactly that. It's enough. It was always enough."

* * *

Boots on stone. Many boots, echoing down the stair the smugglers' tunnel had opened into — no. Not the smugglers' tunnel. Another way, older, a way that should have been sealed for a century. Oscar turned, empty-handed, as torchlight spilled into the vault.

Veyra came first.

She moved like a woman descending into her own grave: steady, deliberate, her blades drawn and low, her face grey in the veined light. Behind her came four of the king's mages in their silver-grey robes, and behind them, soldiers — but the mages were the point, and everyone in the vault knew it. Their eyes had already gone to the light in the walls, and their eyes were hungry.

"The spymaster," Oscar said. "Come to collect."

"Come to choose," Veyra said.

She stopped ten paces from him. Her blades did not waver, but they did not rise either, and Oscar understood that she was fighting a battle older than this night — the battle her father's father's fathers had lost in the archive stacks, the slow rot of serving a crown that wanted the old war back because war was how absolute rule was forged.

"My mother was a keeper," Veyra said, so quietly the mages could not hear. "She told me stories about the order when I was small. She told me what the seal cost. Then the crown took me, and trained me, and I told myself the stories were superstition." Her jaw tightened. "I hunted you across three passes, Kavanagh. I turned your smuggler's man. I did everything the king asked." Her eyes lifted to the living light in the walls, and Oscar watched something break in them. "And I am the daughter of the order, and I am standing in the vault, and I cannot—" Her voice failed and reformed. "I cannot kill you."

"I know," Oscar said gently. "Lior knew too. That's why he picked a stranger. Strangers don't inherit our sins."

"You're not a stranger anymore."

"No," Oscar said. "I'm the heir."

The lead mage stepped forward, hands rising, silver light gathering between his fingers like frost forming. "Spymaster. Secure the vessel. The crown claims the Sigil in the king's name—"

"The king," Veyra said, turning, her blades finally rising, "can claim it himself."

The light in her hands was not silver. It was the grey-white of keeper's oaths, old and cold, and Oscar realized she had been carrying it her whole life the way he had carried his tongue — a weapon disguised as a service, waiting for the moment it was needed.

Above them, far above, the mountain shuddered. Somewhere in the dark, stone began to crack.

"They've breached the upper vaults," the mage snarled. "Take them both—"

"Run," Veyra said to Oscar, not looking at him, her blades meeting the first burst of silver fire in a shriek of grey light. "Whatever you're going to do — do it *now*."
