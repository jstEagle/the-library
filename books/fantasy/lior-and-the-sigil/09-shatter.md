## 9. Shatter

The vault door did not break. It *melted*.

Oscar felt it before he heard it — a wrongness in the stone behind him, the ancient wards that had held for three hundred years surrendering all at once, like a held breath finally let go. The sigil-etched door slumped inward in rivulets of silver slag, and through the gap came torchlight, and voices, and the smell of scorched myrrh that he had learned, through Lior's blood, to associate with the king's mages.

He was standing at the heart of the vault, and the Sigil was everywhere.

The map had been only its skin. Here, unrolled across the vault floor and up the walls in veins of light, it was vast — a living cartography of the Thornfell mountains rendered in gold and blood-dark lines, every pass and peak and buried road drawn in light that pulsed like a heartbeat. And beneath it, beneath everything, something old turned over in its sleep and looked at him.

*You could have it all,* it murmured, in a voice like snow sliding off a roof. *His whole life. Every memory. You would not be a taster anymore. You would never again be a body someone else uses.*

"Shut up," Oscar whispered.

Behind him: boots on stone. Many boots.

* * *

Veyra came through the melted door first, because her king's men expected her to, and because she wanted to see his face when they took him.

She found instead that she could not look away from the walls. The light-map of Thornfell spread above them, and she knew those mountains — knew them from her mother's hands tracing shapes in spilled tea, from lullabies sung in a dead order's cadence. *Keeper's daughter.* She had spent thirty years burying that girl under spymaster's craft, and now the vault had dug her up in a single breath.

"Veyra." Magister Corvane's voice, silk over a blade. He stepped past her into the chamber, six mages fanning out behind him, their staves already drinking light. "Stand aside. The crown claims what is the crown's."

"The crown," Veyra said, and heard how flat her own voice sounded, "sent me to retrieve a map."

"The map has become something more useful." Corvane smiled. He was an old man made young by ambition, white-bearded, bright-eyed, and his gaze on the burning walls was the gaze of a man looking at a throne. "A weapon, Veyra. Do you understand? With this we finish what the first kings failed to do. One kingdom. One law. No more smuggler roads, no more free holds, no more—" he gestured at the mountains overhead as if brushing away flies, "*mountains* in our way."

Her mother's voice, then, from thirty years deep: *They will come for it someday, little wren. And when they do, you must decide which side of your blood you stand on.*

Veyra drew both blades.

It was, she reflected later, the simplest decision of her life. Thirty years of double-dealing, of letters burned and informants buried, and in the end it came down to this: Corvane's smile, and the shape her mother's hands had made in the tea leaves.

"Kill the spymaster," Corvane said mildly. "Then the boy."

* * *

Oscar saw none of the fight clearly. He saw fire bloom against the golden walls; he heard steel ring on ward-stone; he heard a mage scream in a voice that cracked mid-cry, and he heard Veyra laugh — actually laugh, wild and young, a sound no one at court would believe came from her.

But mostly he looked down at the Sigil, and the Sigil looked back.

*He died here,* it said. *Your Lior. Not in your storeroom — that was only where his body stopped. He died here, choosing. Would you like to know what he chose? I can show you. I can show you everything. His childhood in the high passes. The first time he swore the oath. The woman he—*

"Don't." Oscar's throat was raw. "Don't you dare use him."

*I am not using him,* the Sigil said, almost gently. *I am offering him back. That is what I do, taster. I hold what the world cannot bear to lose. Kings want me for power. Keepers want me sealed out of fear. But you — you are neither. You are a man who wants only to remember. Say yes, and Lior lives in you forever. Refuse, and I take him with me when I go.*

And there it was. The bargain laid bare, no smuggler's haggling, no courtly varnish. Everything, or nothing.

Through Lior's borrowed blood Oscar had learned the passes, the oaths, the taste of snowmelt at dawn on the Spine. Through it he had grieved a stranger until the stranger wasn't strange. He thought of the memories as a coat he'd been wearing — but it wasn't a coat. It had grown into his skin. Taking it off would take skin with it.

Above him, Veyra shouted his name. "BOY! Whatever you're going to do — DO IT!"

*Choose,* said the Sigil.

"I'm a food-taster," Oscar said. His voice shook, and then steadied, and he understood that this was the first true sentence he had ever spoken aloud. "My whole life I've survived by knowing what I am. A nobody with a useful body. But Lior didn't give me the map so I could keep him. He gave it to me so I could end it." He knelt, pressed both palms flat against the burning gold. "So I choose what he chose. Seal it. End it. Take whatever you have to take."

*Everything,* the Sigil warned. *Every memory the blood gave you. Him. All of him. You will not even know what you lost.*

"I know," Oscar said, and wept without knowing why, already grieving a grief he was about to forget. "That's the price. I pay it."

He broke the Sigil the way one breaks a seal — not with force, but with permission. He opened himself completely, gave the thing every door, and spoke the word that rose unbidden to his lips in a language he had never studied and would never recall:

"*Lioraeth.*"

Release.

* * *

From outside — from the world — it looked like the mountain exhaled.

Veyra, bleeding from a burn along her jaw, backing toward the vault's heart with two dead mages at her feet and Corvane's wards cracking around her like river ice, saw the golden map flare white. Saw the boy kneeling in the center of it, arms thrown wide, mouth open in a soundless cry. Saw the light leave the walls not like an explosion but like a flock of birds rising — thousands upon thousands of luminous threads tearing free of the stone and pouring upward, through rock, through snow, out into the night sky over Thornfell where they scattered like seeds on wind.

The old war's prison, opening. The old war's prisoner, freed — and dispersing, harmless now, dissolved into the mountains it had once shattered. Every pass it touched, she would swear afterward, grew warmer by a degree. As if something long clenched had finally unclenched.

Corvane screamed. It was not a battle cry. It was the scream of a man watching a lifetime of ambition evaporate, and Veyra put her blade through his ward-heart while he was still screaming, and did not look away as the light went out of him.

Then the vault began to come down.

* * *

Oscar woke to darkness and dust and Veyra's hand hauling him upright by the collar.

"Can you run?" she demanded.

He blinked at her. At her burned jaw, her bloody blades, her eyes — fierce, exhausted, and searching his face for something she did not find.

"Do I know you?" he asked.

Something moved behind her expression, quick and painful, like a fish turning under ice. "No," she said finally. "Not anymore. Run anyway."

They ran. The vault was folding in on itself, its purpose ended, three hundred years of ward-craft collapsing into rubble behind them. Oscar's legs knew the way out though his mind did not — some trace of borrowed knowledge, or perhaps just animal terror — and they burst into starlight as the mountain mouth sealed itself behind them with a groan like a dying giant.

Snow. Silence. Stars thick as spilled salt.

Oscar stood in the drifts, chest heaving, and tried to understand why his face was wet. He wasn't hurt. He checked — hands, ribs, throat. Nothing. Just an emptiness somewhere behind his sternum, a socket where something had been, shaped like nothing he could name.

"You're crying," Veyra observed. Her voice was gentler than he'd ever heard it, and he couldn't remember ever hearing it at all.

"Am I?" He wiped his face, puzzled by his own glove coming away dark. "I don't know why. There was — there was someone. Wasn't there?"

Veyra was quiet a long moment. Snow settled on her shoulders. Somewhere below them, faintly, horns were sounding — Ashka's people, or the remnants of the king's men, or simply the mountain talking to itself.

"There was," she said at last. "The best man I never met. Go home, taster. Live well. That's the only monument he gets."

* * *

They descended the Spine together in silence, because there was nothing else to say and no one left to say it to. At the tree line Veyra turned south, toward the capital and a weakened crown and a reckoning she intended to deliver personally, and Oscar turned north, toward a road he did not recognize and somehow did not fear.

Behind them, the Sigil's last light faded into the peaks — not destroyed, only dispersed, threaded through root and glacier and stone, a power too old to die and too broken to war. Free, at last, of everyone who had ever wanted to own it.

And in Oscar's pocket, folded small, a scrap of bloodstained vellum he could not bring himself to discard and could not begin to explain. Blank now. Every line, every mark, every name gone — except one, inked in a hand that was not his, near the bottom edge, where a courier might once have signed his work.

*Lior.*

Oscar read it by starlight, and wept again, and did not know why, and kept it anyway.
