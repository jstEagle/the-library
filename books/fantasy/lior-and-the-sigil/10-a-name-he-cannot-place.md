## 10. A Name He Cannot Place

The court had learned to be quiet.

That was the first thing Oscar noticed when he came back — not the broken towers, not the scaffolding on the west wing where the mages' quarters had burned, but the silence. A year ago the halls of Kestrelmark had hummed with the confident noise of a kingdom that believed itself eternal: clerks calling figures, guards laughing at the wine cisterns, the king's voice carrying down the marble stair like a man who had never once been contradicted.

Now they whispered. Now they flinched when doors opened too fast.

Oscar stood in the great kitchen with a silver spoon in his hand and tasted the prince's morning porridge the way he had tasted it for eleven years — small sip, roll of tongue, wait, swallow. Honey and oats and nothing else. He gave the cook his nod, and the cook gave him back the tired half-smile of a woman who had buried two of her scullery boys in the vault collapse and still came to work because bread did not bake itself while kingdoms grieved.

The crown was weaker. Everyone said so in the careful way people say things that might get them hanged. The king appeared less often; when he did appear, he moved like a man walking on river ice. Three of the seven royal mages were dead beneath Thornfell's spine, and the four who lived had stopped speaking of the old war entirely, as though the words themselves might summon something out of the mountains that would finish what the collapse had started.

Good, some part of Oscar thought, and he did not examine that part closely. There were rooms inside himself now that he had learned not to open. Whole corridors of his own mind stood dark, and when he pressed a hand against them he felt only a smooth blankness, like plaster over a doorway.

He knew why. Everyone told him why, in fragments, with pity or awe or fear.

*You broke the Sigil,* they said. *You saved us all.* And then, quieter: *It took its price.*

He had lost a season of his life. That was the official accounting — one season, autumn into winter, gone like a page torn from a ledger. The physicians called it a fever. The few who knew better called it mercy. Oscar called it nothing at all, because there was nothing there to name. He remembered the smugglers' roads up to a certain night, a certain pass, a certain fire built against a cliff face — and then he remembered waking in Ashka Vey's wagon, three weeks later, with frost in his beard and an ache behind his eyes that never quite left.

Everything between was white space. Everything after was this: the spoon, the porridge, the nod.

A nobody with a useful body. He had come back to it almost gratefully. It was easier to taste food than to be a legend, and the court preferred him invisible again — the taster who vanished and returned and asked no questions, which made him safe, which made him furniture, which was the only kind of safety a man like him had ever been offered.

He swallowed the porridge. Nothing. He nodded.

* * *

In the afternoons, when the kitchens went quiet between services, Oscar took inventory of the storerooms. It was the work he had always done, the work that had kept him alive through poison and purge and smugglers' war: count what is there, record what is missing, ask nothing about either.

He moved down the row of doors with his slate in hand. Salt. Dried fish. Candle wax. His boots echoed on stone worn smooth by a hundred years of servants' feet, and the lamps guttered in drafts that came from somewhere deep under the castle, from cellars older than the crown itself.

He reached the last door — his own storeroom, the little vault of ledgers and lamp oil where he had spent more hours of his life than in any bed — and stopped.

There was a name carved into the wood.

Not scratched. Carved, with a knife worked patiently into the grain, the letters deep enough to catch lamplight and hold it. Four letters, a short name, cut in a hand he knew the way a man knows his own reflection — because it *was* his own hand. He recognized the way the L leaned. He recognized the slight drag at the end of the R, where the blade had caught and stuttered, the same stutter his knife made on every R he had ever cut into a crate or a cask.

**LIOR**

He stood very still.

The name meant nothing. He turned it over in his mind the way he'd turn an unfamiliar coin — weighing it, finding no mint-mark, no country, no face he knew. Lior. A word, not a memory. And yet his chest had gone tight in a way that had nothing to do with breathing, and his eyes were burning, and he realized with distant astonishment that he was weeping — silently, helplessly, the tears running hot down his face while his body stood at attention before a door like a soldier before a grave.

He wiped his cheek with the back of his hand. The hand trembled.

"Sir?" One of the scullery girls, round-eyed, a basket of turnips on her hip. "Are you all right?"

"It's nothing." His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Dust in my eye."

She looked at the door, then at him, then away — the quick, practiced look of a servant deciding not to have seen something. "They say you were in the mountains," she offered, too casually, the way people fish. "When it happened. They say you carried something out."

"I carried nothing out." This was true. He had checked his pockets a hundred times. He had gone through his belongings like a man searching a burned house for one unburnt thing, and found nothing, and understood that whatever he had carried, he had paid it away.

"The name," she said, hesitating. "Was it there when you left?"

"No," Oscar said. "I don't think it was."

But he wasn't sure. That was the terrible arithmetic of it — he could not remember whether the door had been blank when he'd walked out of his own life, because he could not remember walking out of it at all. Someone had cut those letters. His hand knew the knife-stroke. His heart knew the grief. Only his memory had been excised, cleanly, like a tumor taken out whole.

He ran his fingers over the carving. The wood was smooth at the edges — aged, oiled by passing hands, or perhaps just by time. However long it had been there, it had been there long enough to become part of the door.

Lior.

He said it aloud once, quietly, testing the shape of it in his mouth. It tasted of nothing, and it tasted of everything — of smoke and high cold air and a loyalty so fierce it had outlived the man who felt it, surviving in the one place no power could reach: the muscle-memory of a stranger's hand.

He unlocked the door and went in and counted his stores, and his handwriting on the inventory slate shook only a little.

* * *

Ashka Vey came to him at winter's end, riding through the gates of Kestrelmark with snow in her black hair and a scar across her jaw that hadn't been there before. She found him in the courtyard, because everyone always found him eventually; being findable was the one price of having once mattered.

"You're looking well," she said, "for a dead man."

"So are you."

"I'm not dead. I'm worse — I'm respectable." Her mouth twisted. "The new council on the borderlands wants roads guarded. Trade regulated. Smugglers turned into merchants." She spat, elegantly. "So I'm going home instead. Thornfell. What's left of it."

Oscar looked at her for a long moment. "Why tell me?"

"Because you'll forget this conversation anyway, if the stories are true, and I can say anything I like." But her eyes were gentle when she said it, which ruined the joke. She dug in her coat and pressed something into his palm: a small iron pin, shaped like a mountain peak crossed by a single line. "The order's sign. There are others like you — well. Others touched by it. Keepers' blood scattered through the lowlands, hiding in plain sight, same as you always were. I mean to find them. Rebuild what should never have been allowed to rot."

"And if the crown objects?"

"The crown can barely object to its own shadow these days." She glanced up at the scaffolding on the west wing, at the king's banner hanging limp and faded above it. "Your majesty is a husk, taster. Whatever happened under that mountain didn't just break a prison. It broke the idea that the throne was untouchable. People are remembering things the mages made them forget. Old names. Old wrongs." She smiled, thin and sharp. "Old orders."

"Ashka." He closed his fist around the pin. "Did I—"

"Don't." It came out fast, almost harsh, and then she softened. "Don't ask me what you were. You chose to pay that price so that none of us would have to carry it. Let it stay paid." She swung up onto her horse, gathered the reins, and looked down at him with something that in a smuggler captain passed for tenderness. "For what it's worth — whoever he was, the man whose memories you carried — I think he'd have liked how it ended. He spent his whole life keeping a secret no one thanked him for. You set it free."

"I don't know who you're talking about," Oscar said.

"I know," Ashka said. "That's the point."

She rode out through the gates and south toward the mountains, and Oscar stood in the courtyard until the snow closed behind her, holding the iron pin so tightly it printed its shape into his palm.

* * *

That night, unable to sleep, he sat in his storeroom with a single lamp and looked at the door from the inside.

Lior.

He had asked around, carefully, in the weeks since his return. No courier by that name in the palace rolls. No servant, no guard, no merchant in the city records. The name existed nowhere except carved into this door in his own handwriting, and in the hollow place behind his ribs where it sat like a stone in a shoe — small, persistent, impossible to ignore.

The physicians said the fever had taken a season. The gossips said the mountain had taken more. Oscar had begun to suspect the truth was simpler and crueler: that he himself had taken it, deliberately, the way a man amputates a limb to save the rest of the body. That somewhere in the white space of that lost season he had held something so precious that the only way to protect it was to destroy his knowledge of it.

And yet.

He dipped a knife-tip into the lamp's tallow, warmed it, and traced the letters without touching them — L, I, O, R — the way other men trace the faces of the dead on memorial walls.

"Why do I know your name?" he asked the empty room. "Why does it hurt?"

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the deep cellars, from the old dark under the castle where the foundations of Kestrelmark met foundations far older, and for one heartbeat Oscar had the strangest sensation — of standing at the mouth of a road he had traveled once, a road he knew every turning of, a road he had chosen never to walk again.

Then the flame steadied, and the feeling passed, and he was only a food-taster in a storeroom, alone with a name.

He blew out the lamp. In the darkness, his fingers found the carving one last time, resting on the letters the way a man rests his hand on a sleeping child.

"Goodnight, Lior," he said, not knowing why, and slept without dreams for the first time in a year.

In the morning, the pin from Ashka lay on his table beside the salt, and he picked it up, and turned it over, and pinned it inside his coat where no one would see — over his heart, where a name he could not place had already made its home.
