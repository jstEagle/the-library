## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There are two hundred and eleven people employed in the palace of Vellmark whose jobs exist to make the king look larger than he is, and exactly one whose job exists to keep him from dying of his breakfast. I am the one. Nobody remembers this, which is the entire point.

My name is Theo Dravenmoor. If you have heard it, you are either my mother — who does not write often — or you have made an error, and I would thank you to correct it before it spreads.

I rose before dawn, as I always do, in the narrow room above the scullery that comes with my post. The window looks out on the ash-heap where the kitchen boys burn the day's refuse, and beyond that, the grey wall of the Valley proper, where the mountains go up like a held breath and never come down. I dressed in the uniform of my office: undyed wool, no clasp, no color. A taster should be the sort of person a room forgets the moment she enters it. I have spent thirty years becoming that sort of person, and I will tell you now, since no one else is listening: it is the finest craft I know. Finer than poison. Poison is honest work by comparison.

The kitchens were already roaring when I came down. Cook Brannic stood over the king's breakfast with the expression of a man defending a city, which is how he stands over everything, including soup.

"Porridge with cream and honey," he said. "Smoked trout. And the new soup — the almond one."

"The what?"

He blinked at me. "The almond soup. From the queen regent's own kitchens. Came up with a seal on it and everything." He said *seal* the way other men say *plague*.

"There is no almond soup on the king's menu," I said. "There has never been an almond soup on the king's menu. Almond appears twice a year, at Midwinter and the coronation feast, and both times I taste it first and both times I complain about it."

Brannic shrugged the shrug of a man who cooks and does not think, which is a lie he tells himself daily. "It's got a seal on it."

A seal is a promise made in wax, and wax is cheap. I took the tureen to my station in the corner — a small table, a small stool, a small spoon, all of it positioned so that anyone glancing into the kitchen would slide past me like water off grease — and I began.

You will want to know how tasting works, so I will tell you, briefly, because it is the only interesting thing about me and I keep it hidden the way other people keep money.

I was born with a tongue like a ledger. Most people taste *soup*. I taste a list. Cream from the eastern pastures, slightly off — the cow is ill or the milk is old, note it. Honey cut with syrup, note it. Trout smoked over alder, not oak, someone in the smokehouse is cutting corners, note it. And beneath the list, beneath the flavors themselves, there is a second reading, the way a page has writing on the back if you hold it to the light. That is where poison lives. Bitterness that arrives a half-breath late. Sweetness that sits wrong, like a smile held too long. I cannot name the poison — I am not a physician — but I can tell you the dish is lying, and in my trade, a lying dish is as good as a confession.

I tasted the porridge. Clean. The trout. Clean, though under-salted; Brannic would hear about it. Then the soup.

Almond, certainly. Almond everywhere, thick and sweet and oily, the way almonds get when they are pounded fine and coaxed with sugar until they forget themselves. But underneath —

I set down the spoon.

Underneath the almond there was something else, something shy, the way a killer hides behind a crowd. Not bitter. That was the cleverness of it. Every apprentice poisoner reaches for bitterness, and every taster alive is trained to flinch from bitter. This was sweet. It was sweet the way the bottom of a well is dark — you only notice when you've already fallen in. It coated my tongue and then it *sank*, and for a moment I felt nothing at all, and the nothing had a shape, and the shape was wrong.

I did not spit. Spitting is for amateurs and people who want attention. I swallowed, sat very still, counted to sixty, and then went to the shelf where I keep my remedies and drank the emetic that tastes like rotten plums and regret. Then I drank the second one, the one that tastes worse, and I sat with my arms around my middle while my body did what bodies do when they have been insulted, and I thought, with the calm part of my mind that never stops working even when the rest of me is on the floor: *almond. Someone sent almond to a palace where the food-taster is famous — quietly famous, invisibly famous — for her feelings about almond.*

Either they were stupid, or they wanted me to find it.

I did not like either answer.

* * *

The chamberlain's name is Wren. He is a thin man with ink-stained fingers and the posture of a question mark that has given up asking, and he had come down personally to collect the king's breakfast, which he almost never does. That should have been my second warning. Chamberlains do not fetch trays; trays are fetched *to* chamberlains. But Wren had been doing odd things all week — standing in doorways, watching the king with an expression I recognized because I wear it myself in mirrors. The look of a man counting something that shouldn't need counting.

"Is it fit?" he asked.

"No," I said.

His eyebrows performed a small, controlled emergency. "Explain."

"Almond soup. Not on the menu. Poisoned underneath the almond, and the poison is good — better than good, I've not met it before, and I've met most of them. Whoever made it knew about me and didn't care, or wanted me to care." I wiped my mouth with the back of my hand, elegantly. "Also I've poisoned myself rather thoroughly just now, so if you could have someone else carry the tray down the stairs, I'd be grateful."

"You ate it?" His voice went up half an octave. "You're meant to *taste* —"

"I did taste. Taste tells you *that*; swallowing tells you *how much* and *how fast*, and someday that difference will matter." I had learned this from my mother's letters, or what I remembered of them, which was less every year. "Besides, if I don't swallow, I can't dose myself against it, and if I can't dose myself against it, then the next time it comes up on a tray I'll die of surprise instead of experience. I do not intend to die of anything so pedestrian."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. People rarely look at me; it was almost flattering. "Who else handled the soup?"

"It came sealed from the queen regent's kitchens. Which means either her kitchens have a traitor, or her kitchens have orders, and I'm not paid enough to say which out loud."

"Neither am I," Wren said, "and yet here we both are, saying things." He turned and shouted for a footman, and the tray went back down to the kitchens, and the almond soup went — by his order, delivered in his dryest voice — to the pigs.

The pigs died by evening. All six of them, quickly, without drama, like courtiers at a disgraced minister's dinner party. Brannic wept over them, in his way, which is to say he swore for ten minutes and then made the kitchen boys dig a very deep hole.

And that was the whole of it. No inquiry. No arrest. Wren wrote something in a small book and put the book away, and the queen regent sent down a note expressing her *dismay* at the incident, and the king — the king ate his porridge and his trout and never knew that death had come up the service stairs wearing sugar.

That is the part people never understand about my work. They imagine drama. They imagine the taster flinging herself between the monarch and the assassin's cup, crying *hold!* They imagine gratitude, titles, a place at table. What actually happens is this: a soup goes to the pigs, a note gets written, and by the next morning everyone has agreed, without ever saying so, to remember none of it. A court is an animal, and its first instinct is always digestion. Anything unpleasant gets swallowed and dissolved and passed, and the court goes on grazing.

I saved a man's life today. Several men's lives, if you count the footman who would have carried the tray and the under-taster who would have covered my absence and the king himself, who is, whatever else he is, still a man with a throat. And the sum total of acknowledgment I received was Wren pausing at the door on his way out and saying, without turning around:

"Theo Dravenmoor. Isn't it?"

"It is," I said, surprised he knew.

"Hm," he said, and left.

From him, I suspect, that was a speech.

* * *

Here is what I know about being invisible, since I have made a lifetime's study of it.

Invisibility is not a gift. It is a discipline, like fasting, like memorizing, like holding your breath. When I was a girl I was loud — my mother's letters say so, the few pages I kept, the ones I allow myself to reread once a year and no more. Loud, and quick, and forever asking questions. And then I came to the palace at fourteen, apprenticed to the old taster, a dried-up wisp of a woman named Maren who taught me the trade in whispers and taught me survival in silence. *Be furniture,* she told me. *Furniture sees everything and is seen by no one. Be furniture, and you will die old.*

Maren died old. I intend to follow her example with the same stubbornness she applied to everything.

So I have practiced unremarkable the way other women practice the harp. I walk at the edge of corridors. I speak only when spoken to, and then briefly, and in a voice pitched to be forgotten. I dress in wool the color of nothing. When the court laughs, I laugh a half-beat late and too softly, so that no one later remembers whether I laughed at all. I have watched ministers rise and fall, favorites bloom and rot, three mistresses and one war and two purges pass through these halls like weather, and through all of it I have remained precisely as memorable as a coat-rack. It is the greatest performance of my life and no one has ever applauded it, which is how I know it is working.

People think the danger of my office is the poison. It isn't. Poisons are honest; they announce themselves to a tongue like mine, and I have built up my blood against the common ones the way a mason builds a wall, course by course, year by year, small doses taken on purpose until my body learned the alphabet of harm. No — the danger of my office is *notice*. The moment someone important thinks about you, you begin to die. You become a piece on the board. You become a witness, a tool, a loose thread, a name written in a small book. Everything I have, everything I am, depends on remaining a thing the powerful look through.

Which is why, when Wren paused in that doorway and said my name, I felt the particular cold that furniture feels when someone stops and looks at it and says: *hm.*

* * *

That evening I sat in my room above the scullery with the window open to the ash-smell and ate bread and hard cheese off my knees, and I reviewed the day the way I review every day, looking for the flavor underneath.

Someone had sent poisoned soup into the palace under the queen regent's seal, using almond — *almond*, of all things, the one ingredient I am known to watch for, if "known" can apply to a woman nobody knows. Either an idiot, or an invitation. Idiots do not survive long in the queen regent's kitchens. So: an invitation. An invitation to whom? To me? To Wren, who had come down for the tray himself, oddly, that morning? Or to no one — a test, a probe, someone measuring the palace's reflexes the way a physician taps a knee?

And underneath that question, quieter, the one I did not want to look at directly, the way you do not look directly at the sun or at your own funeral:

Why now?

Because things had been strange lately, even by the standards of a court where strangeness is the local currency. The king had stopped attending chapel — no, that wasn't quite it; he attended, but he stood through the prayers with his eyes open, which the priests pretended not to notice. The king had stopped eating in public. The king had — and this I had heard from the laundry girls, who hear everything, being invisible themselves, my colleagues in the great silent guild — the king had begun standing at his window at night, hours at a time, not moving. Just standing. Looking east, toward the mountains, toward the dead shrine on the hill where no god had answered a prayer in a hundred years.

The valley has no magic. Everyone knows this. The gods walked out of the Valley of Ash before my grandmother's grandmother was born, and they took their miracles with them, and what remains is ash and iron and hard work and the particular silence of a place that has been left. We are proud of it, in our grim way. Nothing answers here. Nothing whispers. A prayer in the Valley of Ash falls like a stone in a well, and you learn to stop listening for the splash.

So why did the laundry girls whisper that the king stood at his window *listening*?

I finished my cheese. I banked my candle. Below me the scullery fires were going out one by one, and the palace settled into its nighttime creaking, and somewhere far off in the dark a dog began to bark at the hill where the dead shrine stood.

I lay down and told myself, firmly, in the voice I use for unruly thoughts: *a poisoned soup is a poisoned soup. It is work. It is Tuesday. Do not make it into an omen; omens are for people who matter.*

But sleep came slowly, and when it finally took me, I dreamed — and I never dream, or I never remember dreaming, which amounts to the same thing — of a stone door in a hillside, sealed with wax, and behind it something patient saying a word over and over, soft as ash falling.

It took me until nearly waking to realize the word was my name.
