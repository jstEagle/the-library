## 2. Prayers in the Ash Valley

The shrine on the hill had been dead longer than the city had been boring, which was saying something, because the city of Vael Corren had been boring since before my grandmother's grandmother learned to curtsy.

I want to be clear about what I mean by *dead*. Not ruined—ruined implies something happened to it. The shrine hadn't happened anything. It had simply stopped, the way a clock stops, the way a heart stops, and everyone had agreed without ever discussing it that this was fine, actually, preferable even. The Valley of Ash did not do miracles. Magic didn't cross our borders; it never had, not in living memory or in any of the histories I'd been made to memorize as a girl. Whatever gods had once bothered with us had packed up their portfolios and moved somewhere with better returns. We were left with taxes, turnips, and a king whose greatest virtue was that he was rarely interesting.

So when the shrine began to smoke on a Tuesday, it caused exactly the sort of stir you would expect from people who had forgotten what stirring felt like.

I heard about it first from Marta, the under-cook, who heard about it from her cousin, who drove a cart of onions past the hill every morning and was therefore, in his own estimation, an authority on all things visible from a cart.

"It's weeping," Marta said, kneading bread with the intensity of a woman who took theological developments personally.

"Shrines don't weep."

"Smoke comes out of the stone like tears come out of eyes. Same principle." She punched the dough. "My cousin says it started at dawn. Just—*smoke*, out of solid rock, no fire underneath, no heat. And the smoke doesn't rise. It just sits there. Like it's waiting."

"Your cousin sells onions."

"He sees a lot of the world," she said, with dignity, and I let it go, because arguing with Marta about the metaphysical reliability of onion carters was a losing proposition, and because some small traitorous part of me had already decided to go and look.

Here is the thing about being a food-taster: you develop a professional relationship with the improbable. Most poisons announce themselves if you know how to listen—bitterness where there should be none, sweetness laid on too thick, a texture that clings wrong against the tongue. My whole trade is noticing the half-degree off, the thing that shouldn't be there. And for eleven years, the thing that shouldn't have been there had been nothing at all. The valley was flat. The valley was safe. The valley was ash and grain and the particular gray contentment of a place that has given up expecting visitors.

A smoking shrine was a flavor in a soup that had no business containing it.

I told myself I was going out of diligence. Tasters are supposed to notice changes in the environment—bad water, tainted grain, a well someone's enemy might have reached. That was my official reasoning as I walked out the Ash Gate that evening with my hood up and my face arranged into the expression of a woman going nowhere in particular, which is the only expression that has ever gotten me anywhere.

* * *

The shrine sat on a bald hill a mile beyond the walls, a circle of gray stones around a central altar, the kind of architecture that says *we built this quickly and meant it sincerely*. It had been dedicated to Lior, once—the old god of thresholds and small mercies, the one they still named children after out of habit even though nobody prayed anymore. Praying here was like posting letters to a house that had burned down. You could do it. Nothing would read them.

Except now there was smoke.

Marta's cousin had undersold it. The smoke didn't pour from the stones so much as *seep*, thin white threads rising from the cracks between them and then hanging in the air above the shrine like breath on a cold morning, refusing to disperse. There was no smell of burning. There was no smell of anything, which struck me as its own kind of wrong—I could smell the sheep pasture downwind, the woodsmoke from the city, the iron tang of the gate behind me. The smoke itself smelled of nothing at all, a hole in the air shaped like scent.

And there were pilgrims.

Six of them when I arrived, kneeling in the wet grass at various respectful distances, and more coming up the road—a farmer with his hat in his hands, two sisters supporting each other, a boy who couldn't have been older than twelve walking with the careful deliberateness of someone who has recently been healed of something expensive.

I hung back by the wall of the old caretaker's cottage and listened, because listening is the other half of my trade.

"My knee," the farmer was telling anyone who'd stand still. "Thirty years. The bone-grinders said nothing to be done. I came up here last week because my wife made me—she said, pray to Lior, what's the harm—and I'll be honest, I prayed the way you spit over your shoulder, no faith in it, just habit. And I woke the next morning and I could feel *grass*. Through my boot. Feel the grass."

"Feel the grass," one of the sisters repeated, in the tone of a woman confirming scripture.

"The grass, woman. Under my foot. Do you know how long it's been since I felt grass?"

The boy said less. He just stood near the altar with his sleeves pushed up, looking at arms that had presumably once been withered or burned or worse, flexing his fingers over and over like a man counting money he didn't trust.

I watched the smoke. It rose and hung and did not move, and the longer I looked at it the more certain I became that it wasn't smoke at all. Smoke is an aftermath. This was an arrival. Something was coming through, slowly, the way water comes through a ceiling—one drop, then another, patient, inevitable—and the stones were just the shape of the hole it had found.

The Valley of Ash held no magic. Everyone knew that. It was the one fact about our home that every child learned before they learned their letters: *the gods left, the magic left, and good riddance, because look what magic does to places that keep it.* Wars, plagues, kings who lived three hundred years and went mad in year forty. We were the clean country. We were the empty room.

Empty rooms don't smoke.

I should have gone back through the gate right then. I want that on the record, wherever records go. Every instinct I had honed in eleven years of royal kitchens said: *strange thing, unexplained thing, walk away, let it be someone else's problem.* Invisibility isn't a personality trait for me; it's a load-bearing wall. Curiosity is how tasters die.

But I've already confessed the traitorous part. I crossed the grass.

The pilgrims had left a gap directly before the altar, either out of reverence or because the smoke hung thickest there and nobody wanted it in their hair. I stepped into the gap. Up close, the central stone was ordinary granite, lichen-spotted, chipped at the corner where centuries of bored teenagers had presumably tested their hammers. No inscription remained legible. Just weathering, and the hairline seams from which the white threads rose.

I touched it with two fingers, the way I touch everything—lightly, ready to withdraw, ready to spit.

The stone was warm.

Not hot. Warm the way a hand is warm. Warm the way a cheek is warm when someone leans close to whisper.

And a voice said my name.

I'm going to try to describe this accurately, because accuracy matters and because writing it down later will be the only proof I didn't dream it. The voice did not come from the stone, or from the smoke, or from behind me. It came from somewhere inside the sound itself, the way a flavor lives inside a mouthful rather than on top of it. It was low and worn and familiar in the specific, horrible way that a voice is familiar when you've spent years refusing to remember whose it is.

It said: *"Theo."*

Just that. My name. Not Theodora, which appears on exactly one document in the kingdom and which I paid a clerk handsomely to ensure stayed there. Not the nickname the kitchen girls use. *Theo*, said softly, with the particular falling cadence of someone who used to say it across a dinner table, or over a cradle, or through a door at bedtime.

I snatched my hand back. The warmth stayed on my fingertips like grease.

Around me, the pilgrims continued their murmurings. Nobody else had heard it. Nobody else was standing close enough, or maybe nobody else was the name it wanted. The smoke hung. The boy flexed his fingers. Somewhere down the hill, the city bells rang the hour, indifferent and tinny, and the sound of them broke whatever spell had glued my boots to the grass.

I walked back through the Ash Gate at a pace I maintained, with enormous effort, just short of running.

* * *

By morning, the king had heard of it, which meant by morning it was a problem.

His Majesty Aldric the Third—may he be well, may he eat heartily, may he continue to require my services—did not tolerate the inexplicable. This was, historically, one of his better qualities. A king who shrugs at omens invites every charlatan in the province to manufacture them. Aldric's response to strangeness was always the same: contain it, tax it, or pave it. When a comet appeared over the harbor two years ago, he'd ordered the observatory closed and the astronomers reassigned to bridge inspection. The comet, deprived of an audience, had eventually gone away.

The shrine did not get the chance.

The proclamation went up on the gates by noon: the hill was crown property pending survey, access forbidden, the site to be sealed under mason's supervision by week's end. Guards were posted on the road. Pilgrims were turned away with the promise that His Majesty shared their devotion to the old ways and merely wished to protect the sanctity of the site, a sentence constructed with such care that it managed to be a lie in four directions at once.

In the kitchens, opinion divided along predictable lines. Marta thought it was an outrage. The head cook thought it was sensible. The scullery boys thought it was an opportunity, since forbidden hills were, in their cosmology, primarily useful for dares.

I kept my own counsel, which was easy, because my own counsel was a voice in a stone saying my name, and I did not intend to share that with anyone. Names are currency in a palace. A taster's value lies in being nobody; a taster with a mystery attached becomes somebody, and somebody gets invited to things, and invited people end up in front of dishes I'd never taste-test voluntarily.

For three days I told myself the voice had been the wind, or fatigue, or the fermented plum wine I'd been quietly siphoning from the cellars in quantities I described to myself as *quality control.*

On the fourth day, Chamberlain Wren appeared in the kitchens.

Wren was the kind of man the palace grew instead of hired—gray, precise, seemingly assembled from ledgers and discretion. He had served three reigns' worth of paperwork and was rumored to know where every body in Vael Corren's political history was buried, in several cases because he'd chosen the plots. He did not visit kitchens. Kitchens were beneath his jurisdiction and above his suspicion, which suited me perfectly.

He stood in the doorway while the head cook fawned, and his eyes—pale, unhurried, cataloguing—moved across the room the way a man reads a ledger, line by line, until they reached mine and stopped.

"Taster," he said. "A word."

There is no correct answer to that sentence, so I wiped my hands and followed him into the corridor, where the steam couldn't reach and neither, I hoped, could anything else.

"You visited the hill," he said. Not a question. Wren didn't ask questions; he issued findings and waited for you to confirm them.

"I walked outside the walls. As is permitted."

"Four evenings ago. You approached the altar. You touched the stone." He watched me with the patience of a man who had watched better liars than me decompose mid-sentence. "The guards noted it in their report. They note everything now. His Majesty wants the hill watched until the masons arrive."

"Then you know what I did. Why ask?"

"Because the guards saw a taster touch a rock." He lowered his voice, and something in the lowering was almost human. "I'm asking what the taster saw."

I considered him. Considered the cost of silence versus the cost of speech. Wren was dangerous, but he was dangerous in the direction of the truth—he collected facts the way misers collect coins, and he had never, in palace memory, spent one frivolously. If anyone in this building could be told a strange thing without it becoming a weapon, it was possibly him.

Possibly.

"A voice," I said finally. "It said my name. Once. No one else heard it."

Something flickered across his face—not surprise. Confirmation. As though I'd handed him a coin he'd been counting on.

"And you're telling me this," he said slowly, "because?"

Because the stone had been warm like a cheek. Because the voice had known the private shape of my name. Because for four nights I had dreamed of a kitchen I didn't recognize and woken with the taste of almonds in my mouth, and I am a woman who knows precisely what almonds mean, and I did not like what I knew.

"Because you asked," I said, which was true, and insufficient, and we both knew it.

Wren studied me a moment longer, then inclined his head, a transaction concluded but not closed. "The masons seal the shrine tomorrow," he said. "If the stone speaks again before then, taster, I would hear of it. Discreetly."

He left. I stood in the corridor smelling bread and ash and the faint, impossible ghost of almond, and I understood—with the same sick clarity I feel when a dish goes from *merely bad* to *lethal*—that the valley had changed flavor.

Something had knocked. And it knew my name.

That night, I packed a small bag I told myself was for no reason, and I did not sleep, and far away on its bald hill, the shrine smoked patiently into the dark, waiting for the masons, or for me, whichever arrived first.
