## 3. The King Who Stopped Blinking

I have served the crown of Lior for thirty-one years, and in that time I have learned that the health of a kingdom can be read the way a physician reads a face: by the small things. The color of the wax in the treasury seals. The punctuality of the night-soil carts. Whether the Master of Coin laughs at jokes he does not understand. Kingdoms do not collapse all at once; they fray at the edges first, and only a fool waits for the center to go.

So when I say I noticed the king's eyes before anyone else did, I am not boasting. I am doing my job.

It began, as these things do, with something too small to report. The king blinked less.

I want you to appreciate how absurd that sentence sounded when I first thought it. I was standing at my post behind the council table—chamberlains are furniture with opinions—and watching His Majesty preside over the autumn tariffs, and I thought: *he hasn't blinked in rather a long time.* And then I thought: *Wren, you are fifty-eight years old and you are counting a grown man's blinks.* And then I counted them anyway, because that is also my job. Everything in this palace is someone's job. Counting blinks simply became mine.

A man blinks, on average, fifteen times a minute. I looked it up afterward in the physician's own ledger, because I wanted to be sure I wasn't mad. Fifteen times a minute, or roughly once every four seconds. The king, across a two-hour session of council, blinked eleven times. I know because I made marks in the margin of my memorandum book, disguised as notes about grain levies. Eleven blinks in one hundred and twenty minutes. By the end of the session my page looked like a tally of a very slow death.

And it wasn't only the frequency. It was the *quality*. A blink is a small surrender, a momentary admission that the world will keep turning without your supervision. When the king finally did blink—that eleventh time, during Lord Cassel's interminable report on the eastern weirs—it was wrong. Too slow. Like a door closing in honey. Like his eyelids had to be persuaded.

* * *

The eating came next, and here I must confess to a failure of nerve, because I noticed it and said nothing for six days.

The king has always eaten well. Aldric IV was a soldier before he was a king—a real one, mud and lice and the whole unpleasant catalog—and he ate like one: fast, unselfconscious, with an appetite that reassured people. There is politics in a king's appetite. A king who cleans his plate tells his subjects the harvest was good. A king who pushes food around his plate starts rumors in the kitchens before the plates have even left the room.

Six days ago, the king stopped clearing his plate.

Not dramatically. He still lifted the fork. He still cut the meat, buttered the bread, raised the cup to his lips at the appropriate ceremonial intervals. But I began collecting what came back on the trays—I told the under-steward it was for inventory purposes, which is the sort of lie that costs nothing and buys everything—and the arithmetic was damning. Over six days, the king consumed perhaps a third of what was set before him. The rest went back down untouched, arranged so neatly it might have been a still life. On the fourth day he had not so much as broken the crust of the honey loaf. On the fifth, the wine in his cup had not gone down by a finger's width, though he had toasted the Ambassador from Vess three times.

And yet he did not thin. That is the part I keep circling back to at night, the part that sits in my chest like a swallowed stone. A man who stops eating thins. His collar loosens, his rings slip, his face goes hollow above the jaw. The king looked exactly as he always had—broad, ruddy, hale. Better than hale. There was a *sheen* to him lately, a kind of polished vigor, like fruit waxed for market. The court commented on it approvingly. *His Majesty is in excellent spirits.* *The king seems ten years younger.*

Ten years younger. Yes. That was precisely the trouble. He didn't look healthy. He looked *preserved*.

* * *

The shadow I would have dismissed entirely, if I hadn't already been counting blinks and weighing plate-scrapings. One strange thing is a coincidence. Two is a pattern. Three is a haunting, and I am too old and too well-read to pretend I don't know what hauntings mean in palaces. They mean somebody is about to die, and it is usually the person who noticed.

But I get ahead of myself. The shadow.

Council meets in the morning in the Long Chamber, where the east windows throw bars of light across the floor like the ribs of some enormous fish. Every man at that table casts a shadow, and those shadows behave themselves. They move when you move. They lag not at all. This is the ancient contract between bodies and light, and no one thinks about it, which is exactly why it makes such an effective alarm: you cannot fake a shadow's honesty.

On the ninth morning, the king rose to close the session, and his shadow rose a half-second later.

I want to be precise, because precision is all I have left now that sleep has abandoned me. Not a full second. Half of one, perhaps less—the space between a lightning strike and its thunder, compressed. The king stood; the shadow stood. The king turned toward the door; the shadow turned, but late, the way an echo arrives after its shout. And then, catching up, it moved *quickly*, with a little scurry at the edges, like a servant hurrying to resume a position before being caught out of it.

No one else saw. Of course no one else saw. Councillors watch the king's face, because faces are where favors live. I watch everything, because everything is where trouble lives.

I stood very still until the chamber emptied, then walked to the spot where the king had stood and cast my own shadow against the floorboards. It obeyed me instantly, slavishly, the way shadows should. I felt obscurely comforted, and then foolish for feeling comforted, and then frightened, properly frightened, for the first time since the plague year of my boyhood.

Because here is the thing I have not yet said plainly, even to myself, even in the privacy of my own skull: the shadow did not move like a reflection that had fallen behind. It moved like a thing that had been *waiting*. Like it had somewhere else to be, and the king's body was merely the errand it was running.

* * *

That evening I did three things, in order, and the order matters because it shows you exactly how far gone I already was.

First, I wrote a letter to the court physician, Master Halloway, requesting a private consultation regarding His Majesty's health. Formal, courteous, deniable. I sealed it with my personal signet rather than the office seal, which any competent reader would understand to mean: *off the books.*

Second, I burned the letter. I sat with it in the candle flame and watched Halloway's name curl up black, because I realized I could not trust him—or rather, I realized I could not trust whoever might read over his shoulder. Thirty-one years in this palace teaches you that there is always someone reading over the shoulder. The palace itself reads over shoulders. The walls have better memories than the archivists.

Third—and this is the act that will, I suspect, turn out to matter most—I went down to the tasters' hall.

* * *

You must understand what the tasters' hall is, because no one thinks about it, which is precisely its genius. It sits below the kitchens, a low stone room smelling of onions and lamp oil, where a dozen men and women eat small portions of everything destined for the royal table. It is the least glamorous office in the kingdom and the only one whose entire purpose is to fail loudly—to convulse, to foam, to die—in place of someone more important. We dress it up with titles. The Chief Taster has a stipend and a livery. But strip away the ceremony and the truth is simple: the crown employs a row of human canaries, and the mine is every meal the king eats.

I have walked through that hall a thousand times without looking closely at its inhabitants. They are meant to be invisible. That is their craft. A taster who draws the eye is a taster who gets poisoned by the living instead of the dead, if you follow me.

But there was one I had half-noticed, over the years, the way you half-notice a well-made chair. A woman. Theo Dravenmoor. Small, gray, unremarkable in the deliberate way of people who have practiced unremarkableness until it became a discipline. She had been in the hall longer than most—longer, I realized when I checked the rolls, than seemed quite possible. Her service record went back nineteen years, and she could not have been much past five-and-thirty. I mentioned this to the steward, who shrugged and said she'd come up from the Valley of Ash as a girl, apprenticed young. Everyone comes from somewhere. In her case, apparently, from the one place in the kingdom where nothing grows and the gods gave up.

Here is why she mattered to me. Six months ago—before the blinks, before the plates, before any of it—there had been the incident with the almond.

You may have heard the official version, which is that a kitchen boy with a grudge put bitter-almond oil in the king's morning soup and was flogged and dismissed. That version is tidy and mostly false. What actually happened, according to the steward's own report, which I pulled from the archive that night: the soup contained no almond. None. The kitchen swore it, the stores ledger confirmed it, and the boy swore he'd touched nothing—which, given that he was being flogged, he had every reason to lie about, except that he kept swearing it *while being flogged*, and there is a limit to how long a kitchen boy sustains a fiction under the lash.

What Theo Dravenmoor reported—and I quote the report verbatim, because the phrasing has lived in my head ever since—was: *"Almond. Faint, underneath. Not in the soup. In the steam."*

In the steam. I have read that line forty times. A poison in the steam of a dish, detectable by no means I know of, in a soup that contained no almond at all. Either the woman has a tongue like no other tongue in the kingdom, or she tastes things that are not entirely of this world, and I no longer find those possibilities mutually exclusive. Not after the shadow. Not after the honey loaf returned unbroken and the king grew younger while eating nothing.

There was one more detail in the old file, easy to miss. After the almond incident, the king himself had requested—by name—that Dravenmoor be assigned to his personal tasting roster. The request was unusual. Kings do not know their tasters' names. Kings do not *want* to know their tasters' names; familiarity breeds sentiment, and sentiment breeds hesitation, and hesitation at the wrong moment puts poison on the royal tongue. But the king had asked for her specifically, and the request had been entered into the rolls and forgotten, because the rolls are where requests go to be forgotten.

At the time, it had seemed like a sensible precaution following a scare. Now it looked different. Now it looked like a predator learning which dog barks at it.

* * *

I found her at the end of the evening shift, alone, cleaning her tasting spoons with a thoroughness that struck me as either devotion or avoidance. Up close she was even less remarkable than at a distance, which I suspect took work. Gray eyes, gray-brown hair pinned without vanity, hands scrubbed pink. She looked at me the way servants look at chamberlains: with polite blankness, waiting to learn what I wanted so she could give it and be rid of me.

"Dravenmoor," I said. "Theo Dravenmoor."

"That's me, my lord."

"How long have you tasted for the king?"

"Nineteen years, my lord." No hesitation. No pride. Nineteen years of sitting at death's table three times a day, delivered flat as a laundry list.

"And in nineteen years, how many poisons have you caught?"

She considered the question honestly, which I appreciated. Most people would have inflated the number. "Four that mattered," she said. "Dozens that were mistakes. Nutmeg reads like certain liver flours. Foxglove smells sweet if the cook is careless with the honey." A pause. "The almond one that didn't matter. Officially."

"*Officially,*" I repeated.

Her eyes came up and met mine for the first time, and I saw that behind the gray blankness there was a mind doing rapid, unsentimental arithmetic—the same arithmetic I had been doing all week. *Why is the chamberlain asking? What does he know? What will it cost me to tell him? What will it cost me not to?*

"My lord," she said carefully, "if there's a question, I'd sooner hear it straight. I'm tired, and I've got porridge to taste at dawn."

Straightness. Very well. Straightness it was. I glanced at the doorway, at the empty corridor beyond, and lowered my voice anyway, because some sentences change the air around them and I wanted witnesses to be scarce.

"I am going to describe the king to you," I said, "and you are going to tell me whether I am mad. He does not blink. He does not eat—he lifts the fork, but the food returns. And yesterday morning, his shadow came to attention half a beat after he did."

I expected her to laugh. I expected the polite, horrified laugh of a servant managing a superior's eccentricity. Instead she went very still, in the particular way of someone confirming a fear they had been refusing to confirm, and when she spoke, her voice had dropped to match mine.

"The soup," she said. "The almond that wasn't there. I taste for the king's breakfast every morning, my lord. For the last month—" she stopped. Started again. "For the last month, everything tastes faintly of ash. Every dish. Even the water. And underneath the ash—" 

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. I finished it for her, and the word sat between us in the lamplight like a third person in the room.

"Almond."

"Almond," she agreed.

We looked at each other for a long moment, the chamberlain and the taster, the man who watches and the woman who tastes, each holding half of a truth that neither of us wanted whole. Then I did the thing I had come down to do, the thing I had dressed up in six days of excuses before admitting was a decision: I took out my memorandum book and wrote her name onto the king's personal tasting roster. Every meal. Breakfast, dinner, supper, the wines, the medicines. Full access to the royal table.

"You'll be closer to him than anyone but his barber," I said. "It will be dangerous in ways I can't enumerate, because I don't know what the dangers are. If you refuse, no mark against you. I'll find another way."

She looked at the roster. At her own name, written in a chamberlain's careful hand, promoted from the row of canaries to the front line. I watched her weigh it—I watched her weigh *freedom from knowing* against the cost of knowing—and I recognized the scales, because I had been staring at the same ones all week.

"If I say yes," she said slowly, "you'll answer a question of mine. Whenever I ask it. Honestly."

"That seems fair."

"Not yet." She folded her tasting cloth with great precision. "When I ask it. Which won't be today. But it'll be soon, and it'll be about the Valley of Ash, and you're going to tell me the truth even if it's ugly. Especially if it's ugly."

I agreed, because what else does a man agree to when he has just discovered his king is being worn like a coat? I agreed, and shook her hand—her grip was stronger than her size promised—and climbed the stairs back into the lit and ordinary palace, where pages hurried with candles and the night cook sang off-key and everything was exactly as it had always been.

Except that as I passed the Long Chamber, I looked in. Moonlight lay across the council table in pale bars, and on the floor, thrown by nothing I could see, there was a shadow standing where no one stood.

It was facing the door.

Facing, I could not help noticing, the direction of the tasters' hall.

I did not sleep that night. I spent it drafting, in my head, the letter I would eventually write and eventually burn: *Your Majesty, I write to inform you that you are absent from your own body...* And beneath the dread, doing what duty does best—dressing terror in procedure, giving panic a schedule—I found myself thinking, with something almost like hope:

*We have a taster. Let us see what her tongue finds that our eyes cannot.*
