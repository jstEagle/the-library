## 4. Almond, Again

The second dish came at midday on a Tuesday, which I found insulting. Poison is a morning profession. It belongs to the hushed hours before dawn, when the kitchen boys are still half-asleep and the guards are yawning through their rounds and a taster's tongue is at its sharpest because the world hasn't yet coated it in anything else. Nobody with any craft poisons a soup at noon, under forty pairs of eyes, in a broth so thin you could read a psalm through it.

And yet there it was: almond, again, folded into a parsnip soup that had no business knowing almonds existed.

I stood at the tasting table with the spoon halfway to my mouth and let the flavor sit on my tongue like an uninvited guest. Parsnip, cream, a whisper of nutmeg — all correct, all accounted for, all matching the recipe I'd memorized from Master Corvel's ledger years ago. And underneath, delicate as a spider's leg across the back of the throat, that same bitter sweetness I'd caught in the king's breakfast two days prior. Amygdalin. Bitter almond. The ghost of cyanide's older, slower cousin.

I did not swallow. I set the spoon down with the particular care one uses for things that might go off, and I looked at the young footman who'd carried the tureen up from the kitchens. He was maybe sixteen, freckled, sweating through his livery as though the soup itself were radiating heat.

"You," I said. "Where was this between the kitchen and here?"

"N-nowhere, mistress. Straight up. I swear it."

"Did anyone hand it to you? Touch it? Breathe on it with unusual enthusiasm?"

"No, mistress." He swallowed. "Cook made it herself. She's been in a state all morning, says someone's been in her stores."

Someone had been in her stores. Of course they had. The question that was beginning to itch at me — the question I would spend the next two days failing to stop scratching — was why they kept being *so bad* at it.

Because here is the thing about poisoners: the good ones are invisible, and the bad ones are dead. There is very little middle ground in this profession, and I have spent my life occupying the middle ground myself, so I know how narrow it is. A poisoner who strikes once and misses is a mystery. A poisoner who strikes three times in two days with the same signature, the same clumsy delivery, the same *almond* — that is not a poisoner. That is a message written in a language only one person in the palace can read.

I looked at the tureen. I looked at the footman, who was now watching me with the wide-eyed terror of a boy who has heard what happens to servants near royal deaths. And I thought, with a clarity that felt almost like grief: *they're not even trying to hide it.*

* * *

Chamberlain Wren found me an hour later in the tasting room, which is not a room anyone visits voluntarily. It's a narrow stone chamber off the king's antechamber, windowless, smelling permanently of silver polish and the faint sourness of old fear. I keep a chair there, and a book of recipes I never consult, and a small window of my own making: a loose grate near the floor through which I can hear everything said in the antechamber beyond. A taster who hears nothing is a taster who dies surprised.

Wren did not knock. Wren never knocks; knocking implies the possibility of refusal, and Chamberlain Wren does not traffic in possibilities he hasn't already priced.

"Mistress Dravenmoor." He closed the door behind him. "You flagged the noon service."

"I flagged the soup."

"Three times in two days." He sat down across from me without being asked, folding his long frame into the chair like a man settling in for a tax audit. "Breakfast two days past. Last night's wine. Today's soup. All almond."

"All bitter almond. All detectable. All—" I paused, choosing the word with care, because words are the one poison I can't spit out. "—all *convenient*."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Not surprise. Confirmation. "Go on."

"Chamberlain, I have tasted for this court for eleven years. In that time I have caught nine attempts on the king's life, four on the queen's before she passed, and one truly inspired effort involving the Lord Marshal's breakfast honey that I still think about fondly. Every one of those attempts was made by someone with something to lose. Which means every one of them was careful, until panic made them careless, and even then — even panicked — no one signs their work. No one repeats a method. You change your poison when you fail, or you don't fail." I turned the cold spoon over in my fingers. "This isn't failure. This is repetition. Deliberate, unmistakable repetition. Someone wants the almond found."

"By whom?"

"By the only person whose job it is to find it." I heard my own voice go flat and quiet, the way it does when I'm frightened and trying to sound merely bored. "Me."

Wren was silent for a moment. Outside the door, footsteps passed — the king's retinue moving toward the council chamber, the soft shuffle of men who had lately begun walking more carefully, as though the floors might be listening. Perhaps they were.

"And yet," Wren said at last, "the king ate the wine. All of it. He drank the whole cup while Lord Cassel watched, and smiled, and asked for more."

"I know. I tasted that wine too. It should have dropped him before the second swallow."

"It should have killed him," Wren agreed, "and instead he toasted the ambassador with it. So either our poisoner is incompetent—"

"—or the poison isn't working because it isn't being aimed at a man." I said it before I could stop myself, and then we both sat with it, the way you sit with a corpse: respectfully, briefly, unwilling to touch.

Wren rose. At the door he paused, and for just a moment the chamberlain's polished mask slipped enough to show me the face underneath — tired, frightened, and honest, which in this palace is the most dangerous combination there is.

"Taste everything," he said. "Even what you're told to skip. Especially what you're told to skip."

Then he was gone, and I was alone with my spoon and my arithmetic, which refused to come out to anything comforting.

* * *

That night the shrine burned.

I saw it from the tasting room's high slit of a window — the one real window, arrow-slit narrow, facing east over the walls. The sealed shrine on its hill outside the city, the one the king had ordered shut up with lead and wax and soldiers after the pilgrims started coming, had begun to smoke at dusk. By full dark it glowed like a coal wrapped in cloth, light pulsing behind the sealed doors in slow rhythm, and I do not mean the rhythm of fire. Fire doesn't breathe. Whatever burned in that shrine breathed.

The court pretended not to see it. The court has gotten very good at pretending; pretending is the local industry, along with embroidery and treason. But the servants crossed themselves with the wrong hand, the old way, the way you do when you've stopped believing the new gods will notice. Cook wept into her stockpots. The stable master fed his horses extra and wouldn't say why. And down in the barracks, I'm told, the soldiers drew lots to see who'd be sent to reinforce the shrine seal, and the loser cried.

I should have gone to bed. Instead I climbed to the window and watched the hill burn its slow, patient, unhungry burn, and I pressed my palm flat against the cold stone beside the slit, and I thought about the voice I'd heard in the shrine stone a week ago. My own name, spoken in a tone I almost remembered — the way you almost remember a house you lived in before you could walk.

Three dishes. Two days. One signature.

I am not a woman given to grand conclusions. Grand conclusions get grand people killed, and I have survived by being small, by being forgettable, by being the last person anyone looks at in a room. But standing there with the burning shrine breathing behind the glass and the taste of almond still ghosting my tongue like a debt unpaid, I arrived at one anyway, and it fit my mouth like a key fits a lock it was cut for:

No one was trying to kill the king.

Something inside him was testing the court. Laying out poison the way a fisherman lays bait — not to catch us, but to see *who bites*. Who flinches. Who runs. Who tastes. It wanted to know what we were made of, the way I want to know what a dish is made of: thoroughly, and without mercy, and one ingredient at a time.

It had found me on the first cast. Of course it had. I was the one creature in the palace built to notice exactly this — and something in that thing, whatever wore the king like a coat, had turned its attention on me the way a cook turns on an unfamiliar spice.

I didn't sleep that night. Around the third bell I went down to the kitchens, where Cook sat alone over the embers, and I asked her who had been in her stores.

"No one, miss." Her hands were shaking around her mug. "That's what frightens me. Stores were locked. Nothing taken, nothing missing. But the almond was *there*, in the soup, in the wine, in the morning porridge yesterday — I never put it there, I'd swear it on my mother's grave, but it was *in the food*, like it grew there. Like it wanted to be there."

Like it wanted to be there.

I thanked her, and I walked back through the sleeping palace past the king's wing, where a single candle burned in the royal bedchamber though the king, I knew, had not slept in weeks. Had not eaten — truly eaten — in weeks. Had not blinked, if the chambermaids were to be believed, since the first smoke rose off the shrine hill.

At my own door I stopped. From beneath the king's door, faint as memory, came a smell I knew better than my own heartbeat.

Ash and almond.

I stood in the dark corridor of the palace I had served invisibly for eleven years, and I understood at last whose table I had been set to, whose palate I had been sharpened against, whose meal I had been seasoning myself into all this time without ever once being asked.

I was not the king's taster.

I was the god's.

And somewhere across the city, on its breathing hill, the sealed shrine pulsed twice — slow, deliberate, like something turning over in its sleep to look at me.
