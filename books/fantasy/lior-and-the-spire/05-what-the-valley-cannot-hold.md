## 5. What the Valley Cannot Hold

There are two kinds of people in the world: those who sleep well after tasting for a god, and liars.

I did not sleep well.

I lay in my narrow bed above the taster's quarters with the shutters closed against the ash-light and listened to the city breathe, and every time I drifted toward sleep I felt it again — that sensation from the breakfast table, the soup sliding down the king's throat like a letter being delivered to someone who had been waiting for it. Almond and something under the almond. Ash. The same taste as the smoke that had come off the dead shrine when I touched the stone and heard my own name in a voice I almost remembered.

You do not survive twenty years as a royal food-taster by being imaginative. Imagination is how tasters die — they imagine a flavor where there is none, they imagine safety where there is poison, they imagine themselves important. So I told myself: you are tired, the almond was real, the rest was nerves.

But I have never had nerves. That is rather the point of me.

So instead of sleeping, I did what I always do when a problem will not resolve itself. I went looking for the recipe.

* * *

The taster's office keeps records. Not because anyone reads them — no one has read them since the old queen's time — but because the office is old, and old offices accumulate paper the way ships accumulate barnacles. There was a ledger room behind the wine cellar, down three flights of stairs that smelled of vinegar and mouse, and in that room were shelves of tasting logs going back further than any living memory.

Further than any living memory. I would think about that phrase later, and hate myself for not thinking about it sooner.

I took a lamp and started at the beginning, which is to say at the shelf where the ledgers stopped having dates anyone recognized. And there, wedged between a log of the reign of some king whose name had been scraped off the spine — scraped, deliberately, with a knife — I found a bundle of letters tied with ribbon gone the color of old tea.

They were addressed to *The Keeper of the Taster's Office*, which is a title nobody uses, and they were signed *M.*

My mother signed her letters M. She died when I was six. Everyone said so. Everyone said it the way people say things in the Valley of Ash: quickly, flatly, without detail, the way you'd say *it rained*. Died when she was six. Moved on. The valley is good at moving on. It is, I now understand, professionally good at it.

I untied the ribbon with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be, and I read.

* * *

The first letters were ordinary. Complaints, mostly. My mother had a complaint in her the way other women have songs. She complained about the kitchens, about the chamberlain's predecessor, about the price of saffron, about a junior taster who chewed with his mouth open. She wrote the way she cooked — precisely, with an edge, and always leaving out the one ingredient that mattered.

Then, in the fourth letter, this:

*They have asked me to write down what I know of the valley, for the archive. I have refused twice. They say the archive must be complete. I say some recipes should not be written down. But they pay me, and I am tired, so here it is, once, and then never again:*

*The Valley of Ash is not a place. It is a lid.*

*I know how that sounds. Write it anyway, they say. Fine. The valley was made — not founded, MADE — after the god-wars, by people who had learned what prayer does when it is answered too well. Magic is memory. That is the whole secret, the one the spire libraries burn books to keep. A spell is only a memory performed with intent. A blessing is a memory offered. A curse is a memory imposed. And so the founders built a valley where memory cannot root. Nothing remembers here. Seeds forget how to grow wild. Dogs forget their whelps. People forget — oh, we are champion forgetters. Ask anyone in Ash City what happened fifty years ago and they will smile at you with lovely blank eyes and offer you bread.*

*It is not that magic cannot enter. It is that nothing can remember itself long enough to work. The valley eats remembering the way a fire eats wood.*

*And the crown sits just outside the lid, in the capital, where everything is remembered forever, whether it wants to be or not.*

I sat down on the floor of the ledger room. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above me, through three floors of stone, the city went about its evening, forgetting things.

I read on.

*You will ask why build such a place. Here is why. When the god was bound — and it was bound, whatever the priests say about its death — the binders needed somewhere to put the cost. Binding leaves a scar, and scars remember. So they cut a wound in the land and called it a valley, and they made forgetting into geography, and they told everyone it was cursed ground, abandoned by the gods, so that no one would ever want to look closely at it.*

*A clever prison needs no walls. Only a reason not to visit.*

*And here is the part I swore I would never write, and here I am writing it, because the archive insists and because someday — I don't know when, I don't know who — someone will need to find this and be angry at me properly:*

*I left my daughter there on purpose.*

* * *

I want to tell you I read that line once. I read it eleven times. I read it until the ink stopped meaning anything and became just marks, just ash on paper, and then I read it again and it meant more than before.

*I left my daughter there on purpose.*

Not abandoned. Not orphaned. Left. The way you leave a thing in a safe place. The way you leave a letter in a box marked DO NOT OPEN UNTIL.

The letters that followed were harder to read, because her hand shook. She wrote about the binding — she had done it, or helped do it, she never said plainly which, and I have learned since that plainness is a luxury of people who are not hiding something enormous. She wrote about the cost. She wrote about memories she could feel going soft at the edges, names of teachers, the taste of her first meal, her own mother's voice. She wrote: *the valley takes payment in small coin, and you do not notice the purse getting lighter until you reach for it one day and it is not there.*

She wrote about me exactly four times.

Once: *She has my chin. I check every year. It is still my chin.* As if I were a property she inspected remotely.

Twice: *The tasters' guild will take her when she is of age. It is the safest trade in the palace. No one looks at a taster. Invisibility is armor, and I would rather she wear armor than beauty.*

Three times: *If she ever reads this, she will hate me. Good. Hate is a memory, and memories are the only currency worth anything outside the lid.*

Four times, the last letter, undated, the ribbon knotted differently from the others, as if it had been added later by different hands:

*If the god stirs — and it will stir; bindings are not endings, they are postponements — it will reach for the one thing in the valley that can hear it. Do not let her touch the shrines. Do not let her taste for the king. Keep her small, keep her blind, keep her—*

The letter ended mid-word. Someone had torn the bottom off. Deliberately, neatly, the way you tear the corner off a page to mark your place.

Someone had wanted her instructions unfinished.

* * *

I climbed back up the three flights of stairs carrying the letters against my chest like contraband, which is what they were. The kitchen was quiet. The city was quiet. On the far side of the wall, past the districts where the ash fell thickest, the sealed shrine stood dark against the sky — the king had ordered it bricked up, and masons had bricked it, and everyone agreed this had solved the problem, because agreeing is what the valley does instead of remembering.

I stood at my window and looked at the dark rectangle of it and thought: *you knew. Some part of you, some buried, valley-eaten part of you, knew what you were touching when you touched that stone.*

And then, because I am a professional and professionals do not stand at windows all night, I went to bed.

That is when the dream came.

* * *

I dreamed of the kitchen where I grew up. Not the palace kitchen — the one before, the one in the village near the shrine, the one I had not thought about in thirty years because thinking about it was like pressing on a bruise the size of a childhood. The copper pots. The smell of bread. Rain on a roof, actual rain, which does not fall in the valley anymore except once a year, grudgingly, like an apology.

My mother stood at the stove with her back to me. She was humming. I knew the tune. I had forgotten I knew the tune, and the dream handed it back to me like a coat returned from storage, still warm.

"You're not her," I said, because I am a taster, and I know counterfeit when it's in my mouth even asleep.

The figure turned. It had my mother's face the way a coin has a king's face — accurate, official, and utterly without the person. Its eyes were the color of the smoke off the shrine.

"No," it agreed. Its voice was every voice at once, which sounds impressive and is actually just very loud loneliness. "But I remembered her for you. Someone had to. He doesn't keep much."

"The king," I said.

"The king." Something moved behind its face, patient and vast and tired beyond telling. "Or the coat he wears. Words. You may choose whichever frightens you less."

"What do you want?"

"Such a direct question. Your mother took three years to ask it." It came closer, and the kitchen got colder, and I noticed that the rain on the roof had stopped, and the silence where the rain had been was worse than any sound. "I want to make you an offer. That is all. An offer, freely given, which is more than anyone has ever done for me."

"I don't make deals with things that wear kings."

"It isn't a deal. A deal implies I get something." It smiled, and the smile was wrong in a way I couldn't catalogue, which bothered me more than the eyes. "I am offering to give. The valley took things from you, taster. Small things, one coin at a time, all your life. Your teacher's face. The name of your first friend. The taste of your first meal — you've noticed, haven't you, that you remember eating it but not what it tasted like? The tune your mother hummed. I kept those. All these years, while the valley forgot, I kept them. I am the only archive you have."

The kitchen flickered. For half a heartbeat I saw it whole — every pot, every scratch on the table, a woman's laugh from another room, a dog, a door painted blue — and then it dimmed again, and the loss of it hit me like a fist, grief for things I hadn't known I'd lost, a childhood returned for one second and taken back, and I understood suddenly, completely, the cruelty of what it was doing.

"That's not an offer," I said. My voice cracked. I hated it for cracking. "That's a hook."

"Everything is a hook," it said gently. "The question is only what you're willing to be caught by. Come to the shrine, taster. The bricks are thin. And bring your mother's letters — oh yes, I know you found them; I watched you read them; watching is nearly all I do anymore. Bring them, and I will return what the valley cannot hold."

"And if I don't come?"

It looked at me with my mother's borrowed face, and for just a moment — one flicker, like a candle finding its wick — the face was not a coin anymore. It was tired. It was so tired.

"Then I will go on wearing him," it said, "until there is nothing left inside the coat. And you will go on tasting, and forgetting, and calling it survival. We are both very good at that, you and I. It runs in the family."

The kitchen went out like a snuffed lamp.

* * *

I woke with dawn coming gray through the shutters and the taste of ash and almond in my mouth, though I had eaten nothing.

I lay still and took inventory, the way I do every morning: tongue, present. Hands, steady. Name — and here I paused, because for one full second I could not find it, the way you reach for a word and grasp air, and then it came back, *Theo*, plain as salt, and I breathed again.

The valley takes payment in small coin. You do not notice the purse getting lighter.

I got up. I dressed. I went down to the kitchens, where the morning's dishes waited in their careful rows, and I tasted each one, and found them clean, and reported them safe, and was invisible, and was armored.

But I caught myself, twice, standing at the window. Looking east. At the thin line of smoke rising — impossibly, patiently — from behind the new brick of the sealed shrine.

The bricks are thin.

I had spent my whole life believing the valley protected me. It occurred to me, standing there with almond still ghosting my tongue, that I had never once asked what it was protecting me *from* — or, worse, what it was protecting *everyone else* from, and whether I had been left inside the lid not as a daughter in a safe place, but as a lock.

Someone had torn the bottom off her last letter. Someone had wanted her instructions unfinished.

Tonight, I decided, I would find out who.
