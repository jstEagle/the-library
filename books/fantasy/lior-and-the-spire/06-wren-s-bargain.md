## 6. Wren's Bargain

I had served the crown for twenty-two years, and in that time I had learned exactly three things worth knowing. First: never accept a compliment from a treasurer. Second: the best lies are delivered while yawning. Third: if you want to know what is truly happening in a palace, do not ask the people at the top of it. Ask the woman nobody looks at.

The woman nobody looked at was named Theo Dravenmoor, and she was currently scrubbing a tasting spoon with the concentration of a surgeon and the expression of someone who would rather be anywhere else on this earth, including the bottom of the cistern.

I had been watching her for six days.

Not in the way the Master of Whispers watches people — he watched for weakness, for leverage, for the little handles by which a person might be lifted out of their life. I watched her because she was the only person in the palace whose behavior made sense anymore. Everyone else had begun to orbit the king like moths around a lamp that burned the wrong color. They flattered harder. They laughed sooner. They agreed to things they'd have spat at a year ago. But Theo Dravenmoor tasted his food, recorded her findings in a small cramped hand, and went home to her rooms above the scullery as though the world were still a place where soup was only soup.

Except that she had started recording things she did not report.

I knew this because I read other people's correspondence. It is an unglamorous skill, reading other people's correspondence, rather like gutting fish: necessary, faintly shameful, and best done before breakfast. Theo kept a private ledger — not the official one she surrendered to the steward each evening, but a second one, smaller, hidden behind a loose brick in her hearth. The official ledger said, *All dishes pass.* The private one said things like:

*Day 41. Almond again. Fourth time. He drank it anyway.*

*Day 43. The poisoner is not trying to kill him. The poisoner is asking him something.*

*Day 44. I think he answered.*

I sat in my office with her stolen sentences spread across my desk and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from confirmation. For weeks I had been assembling evidence that my king was no longer entirely my king, and I had told myself I might be mad. Chamberlains who suspect their sovereigns of being inhabited tend to have short careers and shorter lives. But here was a food-taster — a woman whose entire profession consisted of noticing what others missed — arriving independently at the same terrible arithmetic.

There are moments in a man's life when he must choose between the comfort of caution and the necessity of action. I chose action, which is to say I chose to go and stand awkwardly in a scullery corridor holding a folder, like a man arriving at a dinner party he has not been invited to.

* * *

She saw me before I saw her, which should have warned me about everything that followed.

"Chamberlain Wren." She did not curtsy. Tasters rarely did; there is a certain democracy among people who put strange things in their mouths for a living. "If you've come about the spoon inventory, the answer is forty-one, and the missing one is in the east tower, and I don't recommend eating anything up there."

"I've come about your second ledger."

The change in her face was very small and very complete — like watching a door close inside a house whose windows are all still open. Most people would have denied it. She looked at me for a long moment instead, and then said, "You went through my hearth."

"I go through everyone's hearth."

"That doesn't make it better."

"No," I admitted. "It mostly makes it worse. May we speak somewhere with fewer knives?"

We ended up in the linen room, which is the traditional venue for treason in any well-run palace. It is warm, it is windowless, and the sound of the great presses drowning conversation means you can say almost anything at a normal volume. I had conducted three marriages' worth of secret negotiations in that room and once hid a cardinal in the sheet press for two days. The cardinal had come out smelling of lavender and grievance, but alive.

Theo stood with her arms crossed, back against the shelves, and waited. It occurred to me that she had probably been waiting her whole life for someone to finally notice her, and now that it was happening, she resented it profoundly.

"You wrote," I said, "that the poisonings aren't attempts on the king's life."

"They aren't."

"You wrote that they're questions."

"The almond is a question." She said it flatly, the way you'd recite a shopping list. "Bitter almond is cyanide, or near enough. It's the classic. Every assassin learns it first; every taster learns to catch it in their sleep. Whoever — whatever — keeps putting it in his dishes knows that. It's not an attempt. It's a test. You don't test a man you're trying to kill. You test a thing you're trying to measure." Her jaw tightened. "And he drinks every drop. A man can't drink four lethal doses in two days unless the man isn't the one doing the drinking."

"And your conclusion?"

"My conclusion is that I'm not employed by a king anymore." She said it quietly, and I heard under the quietness a kind of grief, which surprised me. I had expected fear, or calculation, or the hungry excitement of conspiracy. Not grief. "My conclusion is that I'm tasting for something wearing him. And my further conclusion, Chamberlain, is that you didn't dig through my hearth just to confirm your suspicions. You already had them. So let's skip to the part where you tell me what you know, and I decide how much of it is wrong."

I liked her enormously, right then. It was an inconvenient feeling and I have never fully forgiven either of us for it.

So I opened the folder.

* * *

I showed her the physician's charts first, because numbers are less frightening than faces. The king's weight had fallen by two stone in eleven months. His pulse ran slow and even as a metronome — too even, the physician had written in the margin, in a hand that shook. No man's heart keeps perfect time. Hearts stumble. This one didn't.

Then the sketches. I had commissioned them myself, through an artist who owed me money and asked no questions, which is the best kind of artist. Portraits of the king taken at council over the past year, arranged in sequence. In the earliest, he blinked like anyone else, frowned like anyone else, aged like anyone else. In the latest, drawn six days ago, his eyes were open and dry and fixed on nothing, and beneath the portrait the artist had written, unprompted: *He does not blink. I checked. I counted. God forgive me, I counted.*

"He hasn't eaten in nine months," I said. "Publicly he 'fasts for piety.' Privately, the kitchens send up trays and the trays come down full, except he's gained weight. His shadow lags. Half a second, perhaps less — you have to watch for it, but once you see it you cannot unsee it. And last month he stood at the window of the council chamber for four hours without moving, and when the Master of Coin asked him a question, he answered in a voice that—" I stopped. There are limits to what a man can repeat in a linen room without the words changing him. "It wasn't his voice, Dravenmoor. It was older. It was *hungrier*. And every person in that room nodded along as though nothing were wrong, and I have been wondering ever since whether I'm the madman or whether I'm simply the only one still awake."

"The shrine," she said.

I blinked. "What?"

"The sealed shrine, outside the walls. The one that started smoking after a hundred years of being a pile of romantic rocks. The king ordered it sealed the same week the prayers started getting answered. That wasn't piety either, was it. That was containment." She was looking past me now, at the shelves of folded sheets, seeing something else entirely. "I touched the stone. Before it was sealed. I know I shouldn't have — it's not in a taster's remit — but it was warm, and it said my name."

"Said your—"

"In a voice I almost remembered." Her eyes came back to mine, and they were hard, and underneath the hardness was the same grief I'd heard before, and underneath the grief was something old and patient and terribly tired. "So. You have a folder full of doom and you've brought it to a food-taster. Either you're more desperate than you look, or you think I can do something about it. Which is it?"

"Both," I said. "I have a plan. It is a bad plan. All plans are bad plans; the art of statecraft is choosing the bad plan whose failure kills the fewest people. Mine requires taking the king to the valley's border."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. In most women that would have been a full theatrical gasp.

"The Ash Valley suppresses magic," I continued. "That is not folklore; that is engineering. My predecessors built it that way, or found it that way, depending on which historian you prefer to believe. Whatever sits in our king came in on the wind of answered prayer, and prayer is magic's smallest coin. At the valley's edge, where the dead ground begins, its grip should weaken. Perhaps loosen. Perhaps break. I don't know. What I know is that here, in the capital, it grows stronger every day, and it has begun—" I hesitated.

"Begun?" she prompted, in the tone of a woman who already knew.

"It has begun holding court. Not the king. *It.* Nobles come to confess to it, Dravenmoor. Sins, guilts, shames — they kneel and pour themselves out, and the thing in our king listens with its wet black eyes and grows fatter. I watched the Duchess of Marle confess to a murder no one knew she'd committed, and afterward she wept with relief, and the king smiled with a mouth that was not his mouth, and I understood that we are not being ruled anymore. We are being *eaten*, slowly, and the kingdom is lining up to be dessert."

The silence between us filled with the thump of the laundry presses, steady as a heartbeat.

"So take him to the border," she said at last. "Kings travel. Hunts, pilgrimages, inspections. It's done all the time. Why do you need me?"

"Because the plan fails the moment anyone with power notices we're doing it. The thing in him will notice — it noticed the shrine, it sealed the shrine — so the journey must look like nothing. A hunting party. A handful of servants. And a taster, because a king always travels with a taster, and no one guards against a taster. You are the invisible woman, Dravenmoor. You are the one person in this palace whose presence raises no alarm in anything, human or otherwise. I need you to be invisible in the direction of the border."

"And when we get there? If the grip weakens — then what? Do you imagine the god politely steps out and waits in the coach?"

"I imagine," I said carefully, "that whatever happens next will require someone who can taste poison in a soup that should have none. Someone who notices what the rest of us miss. I don't have a full answer. I have a direction and a bad plan and a folder. It's more than anyone else has."

She was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "You're offering me freedom papers."

It was not a question, and I realized with a small internal wince that I had, in fact, prepared them — drawn up, signed, sealed, sitting in my coat pocket like a bribe I hadn't yet had the courage to present. "A taster bound to the palace can't leave the valley," I said. "Yours expires when you die or when the office dissolves. These make you free. Any gate, any road, any life you like. For service rendered."

I held them out.

She looked at the papers the way one looks at a gift from someone who has wronged you. Then she pushed them back across the table with one finger.

"No."

"No?"

"I spent four days deciding whether to trust you, Chamberlain. I read your correspondence habits into your posture. I watched how you treat the under-cooks. I concluded you were probably honest, in the way that ambitious men are occasionally honest, which is to say honestly self-interested, which is nearly as good." She folded her arms. "But freedom papers are payment, and payment ends a transaction. I don't want a transaction. I want the truth."

"Which truth? There are so many available these days."

"*Mine.*" The word came out sharp enough to shave with. "Who left me in the Ash Valley. Who decided a child would grow up in the one place in the world where memory goes soft and magic doesn't work. Because I've been doing the sums since I could hold a spoon, Wren. Nobody abandons a baby in the Valley of Ash by accident. It's deliberate. It's *designed*. Somebody wanted me forgotten, or wanted me dulled, or wanted me — kept. Like a knife in a drawer. And I have spent my entire life being unremarkable on purpose because some part of me knew that being remarkable would get me found." Her voice dropped. "I want to know who put me in that drawer. You're the Chamberlain. You read everyone's papers. Find out. That's my price. Not freedom. *Origin.*"

I confess I had anticipated gold, or silence, or a promise of discretion. I had not anticipated being asked to excavate the one mystery I had hoped, selfishly, to avoid. Because I had gone looking already — of course I had; I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a drawer unopened — and what I had found was a personnel file with her name scraped off it, and beneath the scrape, the ghost of an older ink, and beneath that, a signature I recognized.

I recognized it because it appeared, in the same year, on the resignation papers of the last royal taster. A woman who had served the crown for nineteen years and then walked out of the palace and out of history on the same day a certain dangerous artifact was last reported bound.

I looked at Theo Dravenmoor — at the ash-grey eyes, the careful hands, the patience of someone raised in a valley built to bury things — and felt the floor of my certainty tilt gently sideways.

"Give me a fortnight," I said.

"You already know something." It was not a question. Nothing she said was ever a question; questions required admitting ignorance, and ignorance was a luxury neither of us could afford. "Your face just did a thing. I taste intentions for a living, Wren. Yours tastes like a man swallowing a confession."

"Fortnight," I repeated. "And in exchange—"

"In exchange, I'll get your king to the border." She took the freedom papers after all — but she did not sign them. She tucked them into her apron, unsigned, which I understood, with a chill, was her way of keeping the transaction open. Keeping *me* open. "One condition of my own. When we ride out, we stop at the sealed shrine."

"The king ordered it—"

"I know what the king ordered. I want to know what the *stone* says. It knew my name, Wren. Things that know your name usually know why." She pulled her hood up, and just before she turned to go, she added, almost as an afterthought, in a voice stripped of everything but fact:

"Also, you should eat nothing from the west kitchen until I've cleared it. Something in tonight's menu tastes like ash and almond, and it isn't the almonds."

She left. I sat alone in the linen room among the lavender sheets, listening to the presses beat like a heart, and understood that I had just made a bargain with the only person in the kingdom more frightened and more necessary than myself.

Two cautious people, deciding to trust. Historians, if we survived to have any, would call it the moment the kingdom turned.

At the time, it mostly felt like indigestion.

* * *

That night I opened the scraped file again, by candlelight, with the door barred. The ghost of the old signature swam up under my lens, letter by patient letter, and I copied it out onto clean paper in my own hand, because some names are safer written twice.

Then I compared it to the resignation record of the royal taster who had vanished thirty years ago, and set the two pages side by side, and read the name aloud to my empty office, very quietly, the way you speak of the dead.

They matched.

Tomorrow I would have to tell Theo Dravenmoor who had left her in the Valley of Ash.

Tonight, I poured myself the good brandy and did not taste it at all.
