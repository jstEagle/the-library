## 7. The Road Out

I had spent my whole life learning to be small, and it turned out that leaving the Valley of Ash required exactly the opposite.

Wren met me at the eastern gate before dawn with two horses, a wagon of supplies disguised as a merchant's consignment, and the expression of a man who had rehearsed his goodbyes and found none of them adequate. The king rode in the middle of our little procession, wrapped in a merchant's cloak, hood up, hands folded in his lap like a sleeping man's. He hadn't spoken since we crossed the palace threshold. His eyes were open. They were always open now.

"You'll want to stay ahead of the relief wagons," Wren said, checking a strap he'd already checked twice. "And behind the pilgrim traffic. Nobody looks at merchants going downhill."

"Nobody looks at me," I said. "That's rather my specialty."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Theo." He said my name the way you set down something fragile on an uneven table. "If anything happens to me out there—if I don't come back—"

"Then I'll be the only person who knows what happened," I said, "and I'm famously forgettable. You've read my file."

"I wrote your file."

"Then you know what I mean."

The gate guards waved us through without a glance. Twenty-two years of practicing invisibility, and it worked even when I didn't want it to. There was a strange grief in that, passing under the arch where I'd been born, past the shrine district where the dead stone still smoked faintly behind its seal of imperial wax, and no one—no one—looked twice at the wagon or the woman driving it or the silent king folded into its shadow.

The valley narrowed as we went east. Ash gave way to grey scrub, scrub to a stubborn yellow grass that grew in the cracks of the old road like it was apologizing for being alive. And then, near midday, we came to the border.

You'd think there would be a wall. A gate. Something. But the Valley of Ash ends the way a fever breaks—not with a line but with a change in the air. One moment the world tasted of cinders and boiled mutton and the particular staleness of air that has been breathed too many times by too many people who never left. The next moment—

* * *

I have tasted poison eleven times in my life. Properly tasted it, in doses measured to the grain, building my immunity the way a mason builds a wall—one stone, one day, one deliberate act of harm against my own body. Each time, there is a moment when the bitterness arrives and my body understands *this is wrong* before my mind catches up. A full-body flinch. A bell rung inside the blood.

Crossing the valley's edge was like that. Except the poison was everything.

Magic came back into me like blood into a limb that has slept for years—the hot, agonizing rush of feeling returning to something that had gone numb so long ago I'd forgotten it was ever part of me. I reined in the horse because my hands stopped being mine. The road tilted. The sky was suddenly *loud*.

Here is what no one tells you about a land where magic lives: it has a flavor. Everything does. The grass tasted green and slightly smug. The wind off the eastern hills carried iron and distant rain and something older underneath, something like the smell of a church you half-remember from childhood. And people—I could taste people now. Wren, riding beside me, radiated duty and fear and a stubborn thread of guilt so consistent I could have identified him blindfolded in a crowd of thousands. The king said nothing, but his silence had a flavor too: ash and almond, sweet underneath, wrong all the way down.

"Oh," I said. My voice sounded very far away. "Oh, that's what I am."

"Theo?" Wren's hand hovered near my elbow, not quite touching. "What's happening?"

"I can taste intentions." I laughed, and it came out cracked. "Twenty years of training my tongue, and it turns out half my talent was just this, leaking through the valley's dead air like water through a bad roof. Lies taste like—" I hunted for it. "Copper and honey. Everyone lies a little. It's like walking through a market where every stall is selling copper and honey."

"And truth?"

"Bread," I said. "Plain bread. It's unbearable."

We rode on. I gripped the reins and let the world pour into me and tried not to scream, because some of it was beautiful. A hawk crossed overhead and I tasted its hunger, clean and cold as well-water. Wildflowers on the roadside hit me like a struck bell. I had spent my life in a room with one candle, and someone had just opened every shutter in the house at noon.

It lasted until the village.

* * *

You could smell Harrow's Ford before you saw it. Not sewage, not smoke—though there was smoke—but the specific, sour reek of a place where something has recently gone very wrong and everyone has agreed not to discuss it.

The village sat where the road crossed a brown river, maybe forty houses, a mill, a market square. And in the market square, a gallows. Fresh-built. The wood was still pale.

A priest hung from it.

They'd left him there. That was the thing that made my stomach turn over—they'd left him, boots swinging a hand's width above the cobbles, a placard wired around his neck. The letters were careful, official, painted by someone with a steady hand: FOR PRAYING TO THE WRONG GOD.

"He's been dead days," Wren said quietly. He'd positioned himself between the body and the king's wagon without appearing to. "Look at the birds. They've already—"

"I see it."

Villagers moved through the square below us, going about their marketing. Buying onions. Arguing about the price of eels. Walking under their hanged priest the way you walk under an archway. A woman scolded a child for dawdling; the child was staring up at the boots. She pulled him along by the wrist, hard, and did not look up herself.

I tasted the square from horseback and nearly retched. Fear, everywhere, soaked into everything like rain into cloth—but not the clean fear of a battle. This was old fear, domesticated fear, fear that had moved in and started paying rent. And threaded through it, from house to house, a flavor I recognized from the court kitchens: copper and honey. Lie after lie after lie. *I saw nothing. I agree. It was right. It was necessary.* The whole village lying in unison, the way a choir sings.

An innkeeper flagged us down as we passed, all smiles, offering stabling and stew. His welcome tasted of copper and honey laid over a substrate of pure terror. "Travelers! Merchants, are you? Good road east if you keep moving. Keep moving, that's my advice. Don't stop anywhere long." He glanced up at the gallows and away again, quick as a tongue flicking a sore tooth. "Times are strange. God's awake again, they say. Prayers get answered, they say." His smile didn't move but everything behind it curdled. "Answered prayers are dangerous things, friend. Best to pray to nothing. Best to pray quiet."

"What happened to him?" I asked, nodding at the priest. "If it's not rude to ask."

The innkeeper's terror spiked so sharply I tasted it like biting foil. "Rude? No. No, it's—it's known. Everyone knows." He lowered his voice, though the square was loud enough to swallow it. "Brother Aldous. Old priest, been here forty years. When the prayers started getting answered, everyone prayed to the new god, the one in the capital, the one the criers talk about. But Aldous was old school. Kept praying to the *old* ones. Little gods. River god, hearth gods, the small saints his grandmother taught him." He wiped his hands on his apron though they weren't wet. "Said a god that eats guilt isn't a god, it's a tax collector. Said it in his sermon. In front of everyone."

"And?"

"And three nights later the king's men came. Or men wearing the king's colors." The innkeeper's eyes flicked to our wagon—to the hooded figure within—and I watched the thought move through him like a fish under ice. "Hanged him themselves. Left him up as a lesson. Lesson is: pick correctly." He straightened, smile snapping back into place, voice lifting to market-volume. "Stew's fresh! Rooms are clean! Moving on, are you? Wise. Very wise."

We bought nothing. We took water from the well at the square's edge, because the horses needed it, and I stood there ten feet from a dead man whose only crime was loving the wrong gods, and I made myself look at his face. He looked peaceful, which was worse. Someone had closed his eyes. Someone in this village of liars had climbed up in the night and gently shut their priest's eyelids, and then come down and bought onions and said nothing.

I tasted the well water. Under the mineral cold, faint as a fingerprint: ash and almond.

It was everywhere now. It was in the water table. It was in the wind.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow off the road, no fire, the horses hobbled and dozing. Wren took first watch. I sat against a stone with the king's supper—plain bread, cheese, dried apples, nothing that required a taster, because out here who would poison him and why, when the thing wearing him could simply refuse to die—and I couldn't eat.

The king spoke. First words since the gate.

"You tasted the village," he said. His voice was rusty, like a door opening in a house long shut. Not his voice entirely. Something using his throat carefully, the way you drive a borrowed cart. "I felt it. Through the crown. You flinched at the well."

"There was almond in the water," I said. "There shouldn't have been."

"There is almond in everything now." He turned his head. In the dark his open eyes caught what little light there was and held it. "Do you know what I taste, taster? Every hour of every day? Guilt. An entire kingdom's guilt, piped into me like water into a cistern. Every secret cruelty, every betrayal, every small sin anyone commits within reach of my crown—they feed it to me whether I want it or not. Do you know what they fed me last week?" A pause. "A woman drowned her sister's child in a well in the northern provinces. I tasted it while eating breakfast. I tasted it *instead of* tasting breakfast."

"That's not food," I said. "That's not—no one should have to eat that."

"No," he agreed softly. "No one should." And for one moment the rust fell away and the voice underneath was just a tired man's voice, human and frightened, and I understood that the king was in there, pressed flat against the inside of his own skull like a flower in a book, watching everything, tasting everything, unable to blink. "The girl in the village," he said. "The child staring at the boots. Tell me, taster—when your kingdom hangs its priests and calls it piety, whose sin is it? Mine? Theirs? The crown's?" The almond-sweetness crept back in. "It all comes to me eventually. Everything does. I am the kingdom's stomach."

I should have been afraid. I was afraid. But underneath the fear, something else was turning over—the memory of Brother Aldous's closed eyes, the child dragged away by the wrist, the choir of lies rising from forty houses. And the thought arrived, unwelcome and enormous:

*This is what it eats. This is what we feed it. We built the gallows and called it a mouth.*

"The shrine," I said. "Back home. The one they sealed. It smoked when the prayers started again. Why there? Why my valley, where magic doesn't work?"

The king was quiet a long time. When he answered, the god's flavor was thick in every word, ash and almond, patient as sediment.

"Because that is where it was bound," he said. "And binding leaves a scar, little taster. Scars ache when the weather changes." His head tilted, birdlike. "Ask yourself the question you have been avoiding since you touched that stone. Ask yourself why the voice in the smoke knew your name."

I didn't answer. I couldn't. The road ahead of us ran east through country I'd never seen, toward a border I'd only read about, and every mile of it tasted of copper and honey and ash and almond, and somewhere behind us a village slept beneath its hanged priest, and somewhere ahead—

Ahead, Wren stood up from his watch, sudden and rigid, staring down the dark road.

"Theo," he said. "Lights. A lot of them. Coming fast."
