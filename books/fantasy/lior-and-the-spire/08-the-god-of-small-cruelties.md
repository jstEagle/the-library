## 8. The God of Small Cruelties

The inn was called the Drowned Hart, which struck me as an honest name, because everything about it looked like something that had given up.

It sat at the crossroads where the king's road bent toward the river country, all sagging beams and a sign that creaked in a wind that never seemed to stop. The plaster had gone the color of old teeth. A stable boy with a face full of freckles took our horses without being asked and without looking at me twice, which after four days on the road I had learned to be grateful for. Outside the valley, people looked. They looked at Wren's chamberlain's chain, at the king's covered litter, at the six guards who pretended to be merchants. But they didn't look at me. Whatever else the road did to a person, it hadn't cured me of being unremarkable, and I intended to keep it that way until it stopped being useful.

Wren went inside first, because that was the order of things now: Wren negotiated, Wren paid, Wren arranged rooms, and I drifted behind him like smoke from a doused candle. The king traveled as "a sick cousin," which was a lie so thin you could read a psalm through it, but no one at the Drowned Hart was curious enough to hold it up to the light. Curiosity gets people killed in the Valley of Ash. Apparently it got them killed out here too, though for different reasons. We'd passed through a village two days ago where they'd hanged their priest from his own bell tower for praying to the wrong old god, and the rope was still there, swaying, wearing the priest's absence like a coat. Nobody in that village would meet my eyes either. Fear looks the same everywhere. It just changes its clothes.

Inside, the common room smelled of tallow and wet wool and something underneath that I couldn't name yet, which bothered me more than I let show. Four days outside the valley, and my tongue had become a traitor to every rule I'd ever lived by. In the Ash Valley, food was food. Fat, salt, smoke, rot if you were unlucky. Out here, everything arrived already talking. The bread told me about the miller's bad temper. The cheese confessed to three weeks past its prime and a mouse. When the innkeeper's wife set down a plate of stew, I could taste that she'd made it angry—there was a bitterness in the onions that had nothing to do with the onions—and when her husband refilled our cups, his water carried the flat gray flavor of a man who had decided not to see what he was seeing.

I was getting used to it. That was the part that frightened me. You can get used to anything, if it comes slowly enough. Poison taught me that first.

We took a table near the wall, backs to it, the way soldiers sit and the way I'd always sat even before I had reasons. Wren ordered wine without asking me, which was either trust or exhaustion, and I honestly couldn't tell which anymore. He'd stopped sleeping somewhere around the second night. He sat across from me now with his chain of office coiled on the table like a sleeping snake he didn't quite trust, and he rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger until I thought he might push them into his skull entirely.

"Eat," he said. "Both of us. We look like conspirators when we don't."

"We are conspirators."

"Then we should look like well-fed ones." He almost smiled. It didn't survive the journey to his mouth. "Theo. The king asked for you today. By name."

My spoon stopped halfway to the bowl. "He doesn't know my name."

"He does now." Wren turned his cup in slow circles, watching the wine climb the sides. "He said it the way you'd say a word you've just learned. Like it was new in his mouth. *Taster.* Then your name." He looked up. "What did you taste at breakfast?"

I'd tasted nothing wrong, which was somehow worse than tasting something wrong. "Honey. Fresh bread. Cream that was still sweet." I hesitated. "And under it, very faint—ash."

"Ash."

"Like a hearth swept too late. Like something burned a long time ago and never cleaned up." I pushed the stew around. "In the valley we say ash is honest. It only ever means one thing—that something ended. Out here I'm starting to think ash means something started."

Wren didn't answer that. He was doing his arithmetic again—I could see it behind his eyes, the terrible ledger he kept, columns of loyalty and dread that never quite balanced. He drank instead, and I reached for my own cup, because refusing wine twice in front of an innkeeper's wife marks you faster than any sigil.

I tasted it before it touched my tongue.

That's the thing about the magic that came back to me when we crossed the border—it doesn't wait politely anymore. In the valley it had been a whisper under forty years of silence. Out here it was a shout. The smell hit me first: almond, faint and sweet and utterly wrong, the way a smile is wrong on a corpse. And beneath the almond, ash. Not hearth-ash. Not the honest ash of things that end. This was the other kind, the kind I'd been tasting since the shrine began to smoke, the kind that meant something *began*.

I set the cup down so carefully that the wine didn't even shiver.

"Theo?" Wren said.

"Don't drink it."

"I've drunk worse in this establishment, I promise you."

"*Don't drink it.*"

Something in my voice must have carried, because the room shifted—the innkeeper's wife paused with her pitcher, a merchant by the fire glanced over, and then, as if the moment had been a held breath, everyone went back to their meals and their lies. Wren set his cup down beside mine. Two cups of wine, identical, poured from the same jug, and one of them singing a song that belonged to no vineyard in the world.

"It's not poisoned," I said quietly. "Or rather—it is, but not for you. Not for anyone here." I turned the cup a quarter-turn, watching the lamplight swim in it. "There's almond in this. There shouldn't be. There's ash. There really shouldn't be. I've tasted this pair before, Wren. In the king's breakfast. In the soup that nearly killed your predecessor's clerk. In the shrine stone, when I touched it." I looked up at him. "This isn't a poison someone put in the wine. This is a signature. Something signed this cup."

"You're saying the god is here."

"I'm saying the god has excellent penmanship."

Wren's hand moved toward his sword out of pure habit, then stopped, because there was nothing to fight and we both knew it. You cannot draw steel on a flavor. "The king is upstairs," he said. "Behind two guards and a locked door."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me the thing we're running from is sitting at our table."

"I'm telling you it was never behind us." I finally lifted the cup—not to drink, but to breathe it in, letting my tongue map what my nose already knew. Almond and ash, layered like varnish, patient as paint. "It's ahead of us. It's been ahead of us the whole time. We're not fleeing it, Wren. We're delivering ourselves."

For a long moment he just looked at me, and I watched him decide, again, to trust the woman whose only credential was that she hadn't died yet. "What do we do?"

"What we came here to do. Sleep. Eat. Keep moving at dawn." I set the cup down untouched. "But I want to know why it keeps signing its work. Gods don't leave signatures unless they want to be found."

*Maybe,* said a small voice in my head, the voice I usually ignored, *maybe it's not signing for you.*

* * *

I couldn't sleep, which surprised no one, least of all me.

The room Wren had bought us was narrow and clean enough, two beds and a window that looked out over the stable yard. From the other bed came the sound of Wren breathing in the careful rhythm of a man pretending to sleep, and I lay there listening to it and to the wind working at the shutters, and I did the thing I do when I can't sleep, which is count flavors instead of sheep. Tallow. Wet wool. The ghost of yesterday's stew. And under everything, everywhere, faint as a watermark: ash and almond. It wasn't in the wine anymore. It was in the walls.

Somewhere past midnight, I gave up and went down.

The common room was empty except for embers and one candle the innkeeper's wife had left burning for the saints or the tax collector, whichever came first. I sat at our table from dinner because it seemed rude to abandon it mid-conversation, and I stared at the two untouched cups of wine, and I thought about my mother.

Not much. Just the edges. That was how the valley had left her to me—a woman shaped like a doorway with the door bricked over. I knew she had hands, because I remembered hands holding mine over a mortar and pestle. I knew she had a voice, because I remembered the shape of it saying *bitter first, sweet after, always*. I did not know her face. I did not know her name. I knew she had been a taster, because the letters said so, the letters I'd found sewn into the lining of the coat she'd left me, letters written in a hand that pressed so hard it tore the paper. *The valley was built to cage memory itself. You were left there deliberately. Forgive me.* Deliberately. As if I were a coin placed in a strongbox. As if forgetting were a gift wrapped badly.

I was still staring at the wine when the floorboard creaked at the top of the stairs.

A child came down. A girl, maybe six or seven, in a nightgown too big for her, dragging a rag doll by one arm. The innkeeper's daughter—I'd seen her at supper, hiding behind her mother's skirts, watching me with the frank stare children give strangers. She crossed the empty room without hesitation, the way children do when they're asleep, and I knew before she reached me that she was asleep, because her eyes were open but nobody was home in them, and because the air around her had gone cold in the specific way air goes cold around the shrine stone.

She stopped at our table. She set the rag doll down carefully, face-up, the way you'd lay out a body.

Then she spoke, and it wasn't her voice. It was a voice like a page turning, like dry leaves, like something reading aloud words it had waited a very long time to say.

"*You taste for me now,*" it said.

I didn't move. Twenty years of court training held me in my chair, hands flat on the table, breathing slow. "I taste for the king."

"*The king is a coat I am wearing. You taste for what is underneath.*" The child's head tilted, too far, the way a doll tilts when the string winds wrong. "*You have been tasting me for weeks, taster. Did you think I did not notice? Most tongues tell me nothing. Yours tells me everything. Yours tells me you are afraid, and curious, and that you hate almonds.*"

"That last part's true," I said. My voice sounded steadier than I felt, which is the entire art of surviving courts. "Who are you? Truly. Not the crown. Not the king. You."

The head tilted the other way. "*No one asks that.*"

"I'm asking."

"*I was bound once before.*" The words came slow, dragged up from somewhere deep, and each one cost the child something—I could see it, the little girl's fingers twitching like a puppet fighting its strings. "*Long ago. By a tongue like yours. A taster who grew tired of tasting my hunger in every dish in the palace. She brewed a binding into the king's own cup. Bitter first, sweet after. Always.*"

The floor did not move, but I felt it move anyway. *Bitter first, sweet after, always.* The shape of a voice I didn't remember, saying words I'd never been taught, words that lived in my bones anyway.

"*She bound me into gold,*" the voice went on. "*Into a circle of it. And she carried me to a valley where magic goes to die, and she buried me there, and she built her life on top of the grave. But bindings are not endings, taster. Bindings are debts. And debts come due.*"

"What happened to her?" I heard myself ask. "The taster who bound you."

The child smiled. It was the worst thing I had ever seen, that smile on that small sleeping face, sweetness stretched over something ancient and starving. "*She forgot,*" it said. "*That was the price. To bind me, she fed me her memories—one for every year of my captivity. She gave me her childhood, her mother's face, her own name. She walked into the valley carrying nothing, and she left a daughter there to grow up in the hole she'd dug.*" The head straightened. "*You were the payment, taster. The final installment. A child raised on forgetting, so that no one would ever remember what was done to me.*"

The candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

"My mother," I said. It didn't come out as a question. Questions need somewhere to go, and this word had nowhere to go; it just fell out of me and lay on the table between the wine cups like the rag doll.

"*Your mother,*" the god agreed, and there was something in its voice now that I had never heard from a king or a councilman or a poisoner, something that sounded almost like relief. "*She is alive, taster. Old now. Far from here. She remembers nothing of what she did—but I remember everything. Every year she fed me. Every memory she traded. I have been keeping them for her. Keeping them safe.*" The small chest rose and fell with borrowed breath. "*Would you like to know her name?*"

I should have said no. Every instinct I owned, every lesson the valley ever taught me—stay unremarkable, stay unnamed, stay alive—every one of them screamed no. Names are hooks. Names are handles. A god offering you a name is a fisherman offering you a worm.

"Yes," I said.

The child leaned close, and the smell of ash and almond rolled off her like heat off a stone, and she whispered a single word against my ear.

When she straightened, the candle stood upright again, and the girl blinked, and blinked again, and looked around the empty room with the ordinary confusion of a child who has wandered downstairs in the night. She saw me, frowned, picked up her rag doll, and trudged back toward the stairs without a word, dragging the doll's arm along the floorboards.

I sat alone in the dark with two cups of untouched wine and my mother's name repeating in my skull like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

Upstairs, Wren slept his false sleep. Outside, the wind worried the sign on its hook—the Drowned Hart, the drowned hart, drowning, always drowning—and far away, back along the road we'd traveled, I imagined I could see the glow of the sealed shrine, still burning, still smoking, still waiting.

Ash and almond. Bitter first, sweet after, always.

She had taught me that without knowing it. Or I had learned it from her without knowing her. Either way, the inheritance was undeniable now, stamped and sealed and signed in a flavor I would taste for the rest of my life: I was the daughter of the woman who bound a god, and the god had just told me where to find her.

I poured both cups of wine back into the jug. It seemed only polite. Then I climbed the stairs to wake Wren, because we were leaving at dawn—only not for the border anymore.

We were going to find my mother.
