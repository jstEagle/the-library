## 9. Crown and Cage

The court physician kept his ledgers the way other men kept mistresses: hidden, expensive, and full of things that would ruin him if they ever came to light.

I knew this because I had spent eleven years as Chamberlain of the Royal Household learning where everyone hid their sins. The treasurer's were in a strongbox beneath his wife's floorboards. The Master of Horse's were in a stable boy he paid too well. And Physician Aldous Vane's were in three leather-bound volumes locked in a cabinet behind his examination chair, which was why, on the morning after the third poisoning attempt failed to kill a king who no longer ate, I let myself into his chambers with the key I had copied from his belt seven months ago and had never yet needed.

Eleven years, and I had never once broken into a colleague's rooms. I want that recorded somewhere. Whatever else history says of Wren, son of a cooper, risen too high for his own comfort — I did not do it lightly.

The cabinet lock was better than the door lock. It took me the length of two bells and one near-discovery by a scullery maid who would remember nothing but an old man coughing. Then the ledgers were in my hands, and I sat down in Vane's own chair because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me.

I read until the candles burned down to stubs.

* * *

Here is what a kingdom looks like when you dissect it: smaller than you think, and wetter.

Vane's hand was precise, a physician's hand, each entry dated and dosed and signed. He had been treating His Majesty for eighteen months. Not for any illness you could name — no fever, no flux, no growth or wasting. For *dying*. That was the word Vane used, over and over, in the careful Latin of his profession, and then, increasingly, in plain Lioran when the Latin stopped being enough:

*The king is dying. The king continues dying. The king has not died.*

The first entry was dated fourteen months past. *His Majesty collapsed at evening prayers. No pulse for the space of forty counts. Pulse returned unbidden. No treatment administered; none required. Subject revived without cause.*

Without cause. Except that Vane had been in the room, and Vane had seen what I had since seen at council: the crown sitting on the royal head like something that had grown there, and the king's eyes open and unblinking, and — though Vane wrote it only once, in a hand gone suddenly small, as if ashamed of its own testimony — *the shadow lags. God forgive me, the shadow lags.*

I turned pages with fingers that wanted very much not to.

The entries grew stranger. The king stopped eating in the spring; Vane noted it without alarm, because the king also stopped losing weight. Stopped sleeping in high summer; no decline followed. A wound from a hunting knife in autumn closed overnight, and here Vane abandoned Latin entirely: *It healed wrong. It healed as if the flesh remembered being whole and simply agreed to be so again. This is not medicine. I am recording miracles and calling them symptoms so that I may keep my post.*

And then, six weeks ago, the entry I would carry out of that room like a stone in my chest:

*His Majesty asked me today whether he is alive. I told him yes. He said, "Then why can I not feel it?" I have no answer. The body functions. The heart beats — I have checked it against my own wrist, hour upon hour, and it beats steady as a clock, and clocks are not alive either. Something wears him. Something keeps him running the way a mill keeps running, whether or not there is grain. I believe we have not had a king for some time. I believe we have had a vessel, and I believe the vessel knows, and I believe he is asking me for help I cannot give, because the help he needs would require me to stop the thing that is keeping him breathing, and I swore an oath to do the opposite.*

There was more. There was always more. Doses of valerian and poppy that had no effect. Bleedings that produced blood "of unusual sweetness" — Vane's word, *sweet*, underlined twice. And at the back of the final ledger, loose among receipts, a single page in different ink, newer:

*Attempted removal of the crown while subject slept (he does not sleep; he consented to lie still). The band would not lift. It lifted for the space of a breath and the subject's heart stopped, truly stopped, and I set it back and he breathed again and opened his eyes and looked at me with an expression I will not describe except to say that it was patient, and old, and not his. Conclusion: the crown is not ornament. The crown is life support. Remove it and we bury our king within the minute. I have destroyed my notes of the mechanism. Let someone braver than Aldous Vane solve it.*

I sat in his chair for a long time after that, listening to the castle settle around me the way a body settles around a tumor.

* * *

I went to find Vane himself, because there is a difference between reading a man's sins and hearing him confess them, and I needed — God help me, I needed to hear a voice say it aloud so that it would stop being only mine.

He was in the infirmary gallery, supervising the evening dose-rounds, a stooped man whose hands had never once shaken in my memory and shook now, faintly, as he folded a compress. When he saw me standing in the doorway with his ledgers under my arm, he did not bluster or deny. He simply dismissed his assistants with a nod and said:

"How long have you known?"

"That the king is dead?" I said. "Since roughly the time it took me to read your handwriting."

"He is not dead." Vane said it quickly, reflexively, and then heard himself, and closed his eyes. "That is — I don't know what he is. Sit down, Chamberlain. My legs have been carrying this alone for a year and they are tired."

We sat among the empty cots, and he talked, and I listened, and between us we assembled the terrible arithmetic.

"The first collapse was fourteen months ago," he said. "Or — no. I'll tell you the truth, since you've already stolen it honestly. The first collapse was before that. There were earlier episodes I recorded as fainting spells. Heat, I wrote. Humors. I wrote whatever let me sleep." He rubbed his face. "But the pattern is consistent. Every time the king comes near death — truly near, heart failing, breath gone — the crown pulls him back. Like a hand catching a falling cup. Only the cup is a man, and the hand does not ask whether the man wants catching."

"You've tried removing it."

"I've tried everything short of treason." A dry, bitter sound that might once have been a laugh. "Though I begin to suspect those are the same thing now. Yes. Once. In the night, with his consent — that is the worst part, Chamberlain, he *consented*, he lay there and said 'if it kills me, let it,' and I got the band half a finger off his brow and his heart simply... resigned. No struggle. No arrhythmia. It just stopped, politely, like a servant dismissed. I put the crown back and it started again, and he opened his eyes, and I swear to you on my mother's grave that what looked out of them was sorry for me."

"Sorry."

"As one is sorry for a bird in a cage one did not build." Vane's hands were flat on his knees now, steadied by the telling. "I have thought about that look for weeks. It wasn't menace. Menace I could have borne. It was pity. Whatever sits on our throne has been sitting longer than we know, and it is tired, and it cannot put itself down."

I thought of the shadow that lagged. I thought of the king at council, watching debates about grain tariffs with the fixed attention of a man memorizing a language he would never speak again.

"And the taster," I said. "Dravenmoor. The poisonings that fail."

Vane shrugged, helpless. "Nothing touches him. I've tested what she catches — the almond compounds, the arsenic salts. Lethal doses, all of them. They should drop a horse. He takes them with his soup and nothing happens, and I mean *nothing*, Chamberlain. No purge, no pallor. It is as if the body declines to be poisoned, the way a stone declines to drown." He paused. "Why do you think she can reveal anything? She tastes for a man. She is tasting for a god."

"That is rather my point," I said. "She tastes for what's *in* him. And something in him keeps answering the door every time poison knocks. Three attempts in two days. Either the court has gone mad, or—"

"Or it isn't assassination," Vane said quietly. "Or it's feeding."

The word sat between us. Neither of us picked it up.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic, then. I laid it out that night in my own chambers, in my own hand, in a cipher of my own devising that I burned the morning after, because I am cautious, not brave. There is a difference, and my whole life has been the difference.

If we leave the crown on the king's head, the thing wearing him grows stronger. It eats poison meant for murder and calls it supper. It holds court. It does not blink. And the court — this is the part that chilled me worse than the rest — the court *adapts*. Already the ministers defer differently. Already petitions are granted with eerie swiftness. Already people have begun to say, in the lowered voices reserved for piety, that the king seems *blessed* lately. A kingdom will worship whatever sits still long enough. We are not a wicked people. We are a convenient one.

If we take the crown off, the king dies. Not might. Vane tested it with a physician's rigor and a coward's honesty, and the result was unambiguous: the vessel fails the moment the hand lets go. Fourteen months of borrowed breath. Every heartbeat since that first collapse has been a loan, and the lender sits on his brow.

So: a man dies, or a kingdom is eaten. Those were the terms. I have served this crown — this *household*, I should say, the household is the true country, the king is only its weather — for eleven years, and I have always believed that my loyalty ran to the institution and not the man, because institutions outlast men and deserve the longer faith. But institutions are made of men, and one of them is currently being worn as a coat by something that accepts arsenic as a condiment, and I found, to my considerable discomfort, that I could not make the sums come out clean.

Because here is what the arithmetic leaves out. Here is what Vane's ledgers left out, in all their meticulous horror: the king *asked*. *If it kills me, let it.* He lay still on his own bed and gave a frightened old man permission to end him, and what stopped Vane's hands was not failure of nerve but the look of pity — the cage looking out through the bars and feeling sorry for the jailer.

A man who asks to die and is refused, for the good of the realm, is not being kept alive. He is being kept. There is a difference, and it is the width of a kingdom.

I am not a good man. I want that understood as well. I am a functional one, and functionally, the answer is obvious: the realm outweighs the man; it must; every chamberlain who ever served learned that lesson with his first budget shortfall. But mercy is not functional. Mercy is the line item that ruins the ledger, and I sat up until the fourth bell doing sums I already knew the answers to, and every sum ended the same way: *someone must find a third option, Wren, because you cannot sign your name to either column.*

Which is why, at dawn, I sent for Theo Dravenmoor.

Not the tasting roster. Not another quiet addition to her duties. I sent for *her* — the woman herself, the invisible woman, the one person in this madhouse whose talent consists precisely of detecting what should not be in a thing. Because Vane had proven the problem was not medical. And I had begun to suspect, watching that shrine smoke on the hill like a lit wick, watching the king accept death at every meal and decline it like a course he'd already tasted — I had begun to suspect the problem was not even divine.

It was historical. Someone had done this before. Things that wear kings are old things, and old things have been handled before, by someone, somewhere, and the valley where magic goes to die seemed as likely a place as any to start asking questions.

She arrived at my office at the seventh bell, smelling of ash and kitchen steam, and looked at my desk — at the space where Vane's ledgers had been, and the single sheet I had left out for her, and the freedom papers I had drafted and redrafted three times — with the expression of a woman deciding how much trouble she was willing to become.

"Chamberlain," she said. "You've added me to the king's personal roster. I assumed you'd eventually explain why. I didn't expect an audience."

"No audience," I said. "A bargain. Sit down, Taster. What I'm about to tell you is going to cost us both more than we've paid for anything in our lives."

She sat. And I began — with the ledgers, with the crown, with the arithmetic — and watched her face do something I had never seen it do in nine years of passing her unnoticed in corridors.

It went pale. And then it went *hungry*, like a woman who has just been handed the first page of a story she realizes, with a lurch, is her own.
