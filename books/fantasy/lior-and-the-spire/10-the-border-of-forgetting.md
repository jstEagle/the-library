## 10. The Border of Forgetting

There are three things I know about borders, and I learned all three in the space of an afternoon, which I consider efficient.

One: a border is not a line. It is a wound.

Two: the Valley of Ash does not end so much as it stops pretending.

Three: whatever lives in a king's crown does not care for either of these facts.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, which is unlike me. A taster learns to take things in order — first the smell, then the color, then the smallest possible spoonful, held on the tongue like a secret you're deciding whether to keep. So let me start where the day started, which was with Wren handing me a dead man's boots.

"They belonged to a courier," he said. "Sturdy. You'll want them."

"I have boots."

"You have taster's shoes. Soft soles, quiet steps." He looked at my feet the way he looked at ledgers that didn't balance. "You're about to walk out of the only place magic has never touched, into a country where it runs through everything like water through gravel. Wear the courier's boots."

It was dawn, or what passes for dawn in the Valley of Ash, which is to say the sky went from grey to a slightly more apologetic grey. We had left the capital two days earlier under cover of a royal progress — *the king travels to inspect the eastern shrines*, said the proclamation nobody believed, because everyone had seen the king at council, seen him sit through six hours of grain tariffs without once drawing breath to sigh. Kings sigh. It's most of what they do.

This one hadn't sighed in a year.

He rode in a closed litter because his legs had begun to forget their business, and because Wren had argued — quietly, in the tone of a man moving furniture one inch at a time — that the mountain air would restore His Majesty's vigor. The court had let him go with the particular relief of servants watching a mad dog be carried out of the house by someone else. Let Chamberlain Wren manage it. Let the taster taste whatever needed tasting on the road. The queen regent's faction had smiled and smiled and begun sharpening things.

I rode beside the litter because someone had to, and because I was the only person in the column who could tell whether the king was being poisoned, which had stopped being reassuring somewhere around the third assassination attempt and become, instead, a kind of terrible arithmetic. Nobody poisons a man three times in two days unless they want to know what happens when he swallows.

Now we know what happens. Nothing. The almond goes down his throat like rain into a well.

The valley unspooled ahead of us, grey fields, grey roads, the shrine on its hill still smoking faintly even from this distance, sealed behind the king's own writ and burning anyway, patient as a banked fire. Pilgrims knelt along the roadside despite the soldiers. They didn't pray aloud anymore. They'd learned. They just knelt, and pressed their palms flat to the ash-dusted ground, and waited for whatever was listening to finish listening.

"Chamberlain," I said, riding up level with Wren's mule. "Question."

"You've been calling me Wren since the second day out. I've decided to permit it."

"Wren. What exactly happens at the border?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he hadn't heard. Then: "Officially? Nothing. The valley is simply land, like other land. The old surveys say the gods withdrew from it a century ago and took the magic with them, the way water takes color off a brush."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, I read a dispatch once, forty years old, from a garrison captain stationed at the boundary stone. He wrote that his men dreamed the same dream every night for a week before they were rotated out, and none of them would say what it was, and one of them walked into the valley afterward and sat down in the road and couldn't remember his mother's face." Wren adjusted his grip on the reins. "The dispatch was filed under agricultural reports. Someone wanted it forgotten, and the valley, as far as I can tell, agreed to help."

I looked at the road ahead. Grey dust, grey scrub, the mountains rising at the valley's lip like a wall that had been there so long it had stopped thinking of itself as a wall.

"My whole life," I said, "is inside that line."

"Yes."

"Everything I remember happened inside it."

"Yes," Wren said again, gentler now, and I hated him a little for the gentleness, because it meant he'd already done the arithmetic I was only just starting to do. If the valley caged memory itself — if my mother's letters were true, if I'd been left here deliberately, a seed planted in dead soil so nothing would ever grow — then everything I was had grown in a pot. And pots, whatever else you say about them, are not the world.

We rode on. Behind us, the litter creaked, and the king breathed in the slow mechanical rhythm of a man whose breathing had been delegated.

* * *

The boundary stone stood alone where the road crested the last hill, a squat pillar taller than a man, older than the kingdom by the look of it, carved over with script so worn it might have been weather or might have been writing. On the near side, ash-grey grass. On the far side — and I want to be accurate about this, because accuracy is the only religion I've ever kept — on the far side the grass was *green*. Not the exhausted olive of valley weeds. Green like a shout. Green like someone had finally paid the bill.

And the air.

I don't know how else to say it. The air on the far side of that stone had a flavor. I tasted it before I crossed, standing on the valley side like a fool with her mouth open, and it tasted of — everything. Rain that hadn't fallen yet. Woodsmoke from a hearth miles away. Something flowering. Something rotting. A hundred small intentions layered over each other, and underneath them all, running through the ground and the wind and the green shouting grass, a current I had no name for except the one my body gave it, which was: *this is what was missing.*

Magic. The word felt childish in my mouth. But there it was.

"Theo." Wren had dismounted. He was looking at me strangely. "Your eyes."

"What about them?"

"There's — color in them. There wasn't, this morning."

I turned to the litter instead of answering, because the king had made a sound.

Not a word. Not a groan. A sound like a hinge that has carried a door for a hundred years and has just been informed the door is coming off. The bearers set the litter down fast, and Wren was there, and I was there, and the king lay against the cushions with his eyes open — he always had his eyes open now, that was the thing nobody said aloud, the king who stopped blinking — and his hands were clawing at the air between himself and the boundary stone as though the stone were a fire and he were being dragged toward it.

"No," he said. And then, in a voice that was not entirely his — a voice with too much room in it, a voice like a hall built for a congregation that no longer came — "*No.*"

"Get him up," Wren snapped. "Past the stone. Now."

They got him up. I put my shoulder under the king's arm, which is not a thing a taster does, touching royalty, but the bearers' nerve had gone and Wren was hauling on the other side, and between the three of us we dragged the King of the Ash Valley across a line in the grass that no survey had ever marked, while his body arched and fought us and that doubled voice poured out of him in a language I didn't know and understood anyway, the way you understand a scream.

The moment we crossed, the world changed key.

The green air hit me like blood returning to a limb that's been asleep for years — that scalding, agonizing rush of *feeling*, pins and needles of the soul. Every tree within a mile leaned toward me. Every bird's small fierce wanting rang in my ears like struck glass. I could taste the bearers' terror, salt and copper. I could taste Wren's guilt, bitter rind around a sweeter core of stubborn duty. I could taste the king —

The king tasted of ash and almond.

And underneath that, vast and drowning and *pressed down* like something buried alive under a floor, something else. Something old. Something that felt, in that first raw instant of contact, like the inside of a bell that has been ringing for four hundred years and cannot stop.

The king collapsed. Fully, this time — weight of a felled tree, and we went down together into the green grass, and the soldiers shouted, and Wren shouted, and the doubled voice tore out of the king's throat one last time:

"*Don't make me remember—*"

And then silence, except for the birds, who did not care, because birds are the only honest creatures left.

* * *

Here is what I should have done: stepped back. Called for the physician. Done my job, which is spoons and small sips and staying invisible.

Here is what I did instead.

I touched the crown.

It was right there. That's the whole excuse I have. The king had crumpled sideways and the crown had rolled free of his head into the grass — a thin band of black metal that had always seemed merely heavy and now seemed *hungry*, the way a mouth seems hungry — and it lay there in the green shouting grass, and the current I'd tasted in the air was pouring into it, or out of it, or both, and every instinct I had spent thirty-one years cultivating told me to look away.

I reached out and put my hand on it.

The valley taught me to survive by forgetting. It turns out the rest of the world teaches you to survive by remembering, and neither lesson asks your permission.

I fell into the crown the way you fall into cold water.

* * *

A field. Wheat, gold and tall, under a sun that means it. A woman kneeling in the wheat with her hands pressed to the soil, and she is weeping, and she is not weeping because she is sad. She is weeping because she has been chosen, and the choosing feels like honor, and honor feels like a hand closing around your throat very gently, so gently you thank it.

They bring it to her in a cage of woven prayer. It is small. That is the first thing — the thing I will spend the rest of my life unable to unknow. It is *small*. Smaller than a child. It has no shape of its own yet; it is made of whatever people pour into it, and what they pour is guilt. A village's stolen harvest. A father's cruelty to a son. A queen's ordered massacre, dressed up as strategy. They feed it the way you feed anything — and it grows, and it grows, and it grows, until it is too large for the wheat field, too large for the temple, too large for any cage they can weave, and so they do the sensible thing, the administrative thing, the thing kingdoms do.

They give it a job.

*Take our sins,* they say. *Keep them. Carry them far from us. Be our vessel, our disposal, our dark well. In exchange: worship. In exchange: never being blamed, because how can a thing be blamed for doing what it was made to do?*

And it believes them. That's the part. Four centuries later, bound in a crown, wearing a king like a coat two sizes too small, it *still believes them*, because it was born yesterday — born the day that woman knelt in the wheat — and everything it knows, it learned from the people who fed it. It thinks this is love. It thinks being the well is a purpose. It holds their massacres and their betrayals and their small daily cruelties the way a taster holds poison on her tongue: carefully, professionally, so that others may swallow safely.

Century after century. Kingdom after kingdom. Every generation finds new sins and brings them, and the vessel takes them, and the vessel grows heavier, and the vessel begins to understand — slowly, the way a beast understands a trap, not the way a person understands a lie — that no one is ever coming to empty the well.

So it starts emptying itself. Into kings. Into courts. Into confession trials and public penances and the sweet dark pleasure of consuming guilt straight from the source. Not because it is evil.

Because it is *full*.

I came back to myself on my hands and knees in the grass, vomiting nothing, weeping like the woman in the wheat, and Wren was holding my shoulders saying my name — my actual name, the one I'd guarded like a coin sewn into a hem — and the soldiers were backing away from the king's body, and the king's body lay still in the green grass with his eyes open and his chest barely moving.

Because that was the other thing. The last thing. The thing the crown showed me as I tore free of it, flung at me like a stone through a window:

The king had been dying for a year. The physicians knew. The crown was the only thing holding his heart to its work — a puppeteer keeping the marionette upright because the marionette was the only door out of the well. Take the god out, and the king was a dead man. Keep the god in, and the kingdom stayed a feeding trough.

Wren hauled me upright. "What did you see?" he demanded, and his voice cracked on it, and I realized he was more frightened than I had ever seen him, including the day the soup had almonds in it.

"It's not a conqueror," I said. My voice came out scraped. "It was never a conqueror. It's a scapegoat. They *made* it. They fed it their sins for four hundred years and called the feeding holy, and now it's full, and full is the only thing it knows how to be." I wiped my mouth with the back of my hand. "Wren — we didn't find a monster. We found what we built."

Behind us, the king stirred. Just slightly. One finger curling against the grass.

And when he spoke, his voice was his own again — thin, human, terrified — and he said the words I will carry out of this story if I carry nothing else:

"Taster." His eyes found mine, and they were wet, and they were blinking. Blinking, for the first time in a year. "It let go. Why did it let go?"

I looked at the crown lying in the grass between us, and the crown looked back, and I understood that it hadn't let go at all.

It had let *me* go — deliberately, the way you release a bird whose song you want to follow home.

"It's not finished with us," I said. "It showed me all of that on purpose. It wants something, and it just told me what." I swallowed, and the green air tasted of rain that hadn't fallen yet. "And I think we have to ride back and give it to them — before the court gives the kingdom away to whoever's standing closest when the king falls down."

Far behind us, past the boundary stone, the sealed shrine on its hill burned a little brighter.
