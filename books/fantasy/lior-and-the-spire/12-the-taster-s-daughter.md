## 12. The Taster's Daughter

The letter that found me in Saltmere was written on the back of a fishmonger's receipt, which told me two things before I read a word: whoever sent it was poor, and whoever sent it was careful. Poor people don't waste paper. Careful people don't leave trails.

*If you are who I think you are,* it said, *come to the house with the blue door on Gull Street. Knock twice, then once more, then not at all even if someone calls out. Especially if someone calls out.*

No signature. But the handwriting had a habit I recognized from my own hand — the way the letters *t* and *h* leaned into each other like conspirators. I'd spent twenty years assuming I'd invented that lean myself.

Wren would have called it a trap. Wren, at this point, was three days behind me with a forged writ and a face like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like, so for once I got to make a decision without him sighing about it. I went to Gull Street alone.

Saltmere is what happens when the Valley of Ash ends and nobody quite agrees on what should come next. It's a fishing town built on the wrong side of forgetting, where the magic comes back thin and sour, like milk left out. The people there have the look of those who moved somewhere on purpose to be overlooked. I fit right in. That's the thing about being invisible your whole life: you can stand in any doorway in the world and belong to none of them equally.

The blue door was blue the way rust is red — technically, and only if you were generous. I knocked twice. Once more. A voice from inside said, "Go away," in a tone so perfectly bored that I knew it was rehearsed, and I did not go away, because I am also capable of rehearsal.

I stood there while gulls argued over something dead in the street. Then the door opened, and a woman looked at me, and my whole life rearranged itself around her face.

She had my chin. She had my hands — taster's hands, scarred faintly at the fingertips where decades of hot spoons and suspicious surfaces leave their record. Her hair was grey where mine was brown, and her eyes were the same unremarkable brown as mine, the color of a valley that grows nothing, chosen by whatever gods arrange such things for women whose survival depends on being forgettable.

"You're late," she said.

"I didn't know I was expected."

"Thirty years," she said. "You're thirty years late. Come in before someone sees you."

* * *

The house smelled of herbs and old smoke and, underneath it, faintly, of almond. My stomach dropped through the floorboards. She saw me flinch and almost smiled.

"That's just the oil," she said. "Bitter almond oil. I rub it on the sills. Keeps away thieves, tax collectors, and anything wearing a crown." She paused. "It doesn't keep away daughters, apparently."

"You know why I'm here."

"I know you crossed the border, which no one does. I know the shrine on the hill started smoking again after a hundred years of good behavior. And I know that if you're standing in my kitchen asking questions, then the god has found a king it likes." She set a kettle on the fire without asking if I wanted tea, which is a mother's way of saying yes to things she can't say aloud. "Sit down, Theo."

My name in her mouth. I hadn't heard it spoken by anyone who meant it since — well. Since never, as far as I could remember, because remembering was exactly the problem.

"How do you know my name?" I asked. "How do you know me? The valley takes memories. That's its whole job. You left me there and it took everything—"

"It took what you let it take," she said. "And it took what I paid it to take. Sit. This is a long story and I've only got enough patience for telling it once, and I spent most of my patience on the knocking code."

I sat. The tea, when it came, tasted of chamomile cut with something sharper — valerian, maybe, or just the flavor of a woman who'd been dosing herself for decades against things that came in dreams. Tasters learn to read a cup like a letter. Hers said: *I don't sleep much either.*

"My name was Maren Dravenmoor," she said. "Before it was nothing at all. I was the royal taster for eleven years. Same as you. Same as my mother before me, though she'd have denied it under torture, and possibly was tortured, now I think of it, but the records are blank, so who's to say."

"The records are always blank," I said. "That's the valley."

"That's the *arrangement*," she corrected. "The valley wasn't built to cage magic, whatever the priests say. Magic never needed caging; it blows through everywhere like weather. The valley was built to cage memory. Someone, a very long time ago, decided that some things were too heavy for a kingdom to carry, so they built a place where those things could be set down and forgotten. Fed the mountain our worst centuries and let the ash bury them." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "And then, being what kingdoms are, they found other uses for it. Why bury only the kingdom's sins when you can bury an inconvenient person? An inconvenient truth? An inconvenient *god*?"

"The god in the king."

"The god in the king," she agreed. "Though 'in' is generous. It wears him. It poured itself into the crown four hundred years ago when the last binding failed, and it has been leaking ever since, and every leak gets patched with the same material." She looked at me. "Tasters."

The kettle ticked. Somewhere outside, the gulls had found a new argument.

"There's a reason the office exists," she went on. "Not the official reason — the official reason is poison, and poison is real, but it's the small reason. The true reason is that a taster builds immunity. Dose by dose, dish by dish, we teach our bodies to survive what should kill us. And the god's presence is a kind of poison. It seeps into a court the way arsenic seeps into wallpaper. Slow. Tasteless to everyone else. Fatal over years." She turned her cup. "A taster who has been dosed all her life can stand close to it. Can touch it. Can, if she's foolish or desperate or both, do what I did."

"Which was?"

"Bind it." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *swept the floor*. "Thirty-one years ago, the king of that day began to change. Stopped blinking. Stopped eating. His shadow lagged. Sound familiar?"

"Chamberlain Wren keeps notes," I said. "He's thorough."

"He sounds like a man I'd have liked." Something moved across her face — grief, maybe, for a life she couldn't hold onto — and then it was gone, filed away behind the same professional blankness I wore to work every morning. "I bound it the only way a taster can. I took it into myself. All of it. Four hundred years of everything a kingdom fed it to be rid of. Every confessed sin, every guilt poured down the crown's throat like medicine. I held it, and I carried it to the valley, and I gave it to the ash, and the ash forgot it, and I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "And it forgot me. That was the price. To bind a memory-eater, you pay in the same coin. It hollowed me. Everything before that night is gone. My mother's face. My own wedding, if there was one. Whether I had sisters. Whether I laughed, ever, at anything." She shrugged, a small precise motion. "I woke up on the far side of the border with a name I didn't recognize and a child I'd apparently left instructions about."

"You left me there," I said. "On purpose."

"On purpose," she said. "Because the binding wouldn't hold forever. Bindings never do; the god is patient and kingdoms are stupid, and eventually someone would feed it again — a guilty noble, a frightened priest, a king with secrets — and it would grow strong enough to wear another crown. And when that happened, someone would need to finish the job. Someone immune. Someone who could get close to a king without anyone noticing." She met my eyes. "Someone raised from birth to be unremarkable. I couldn't give you a childhood, Theo. I could only give you a purpose. I'm told mothers make worse trades than that."

I wanted to be angry. I reached for anger the way you reach for a familiar spice in a dark pantry, and it wasn't there — or rather, it was there, but it tasted different than I'd imagined for thirty years. Not betrayal. Arithmetic. She'd done what I do every morning: weighed one death against another and picked the smaller number.

"You could have told me," I said finally. "Left a letter. Anything."

"A letter is a memory outside the valley," she said. "The valley eats those too. I tried. I wrote you fourteen letters over the years. By morning they were blank pages. Eventually I understood: the only way you'd ever learn the truth was to walk out carrying it in your body, the way I did. The hard way." She smiled without humor. "Congratulations. You're doing it the hard way."

* * *

We talked until the light went from gold to grey to the blue-black of a fishing town settling into its cups. She told me what she remembered of the binding — not the event itself, which was gone, but the aftermath, the muscle-memory of it, the way her body still flinched from certain prayers the way a burned hand flinches from flame. She told me how she'd tracked the god's slow return over the decades: rumors of confession trials in the capital, a queen regent with hungry eyes, a king who hadn't been seen eating in public for a year.

"And now it's strong again," she said. "Strong enough that it sealed the shrine. Strong enough that it's testing the court with poisoned dishes, seeing who flinches. It remembers me, Theo. Whatever else it lost, it remembers the woman who bound it, and it will remember her daughter too, the moment it looks at you properly. Which it will. You're not invisible anymore. Crossing the border made sure of that."

"I noticed," I said dryly. "I can taste intentions now. Lies taste like copper. One innkeeper's stew tasted like fear, which I chose not to mention, because the fear was about the rent."

That startled a laugh out of her — short, rusty, like a door opening after years. "Copper," she said. "Yes. That's right. I'd forgotten that lies taste like copper, and now I'll never know whether I actually forgot it or I'm borrowing your knowing." She set down her cup. "Which brings us to the part of the evening I've been dreading, and I've been dreading it since I wrote that letter, and I wrote it anyway, because I'm a coward in small ways and honest in large ones. Ask me what unbinding requires."

I asked.

"Witness," she said. "Not strength. Not sacrifice, whatever the ballads would tell you — the god doesn't want blood, it's had buckets of blood and it's still hungry. It wants what it was never given. Someone to see what was done to it and say: *this happened, and it was wrong.* It was made a scapegoat, Theo. Four hundred years ago the old kings fed it their sins to be rid of them, and it has been holding all of it ever since, alone, blamed for the weight it carries. No one has ever apologized. No one has ever even asked. To unbind it, someone must take its whole memory into themselves — every century, every sin, every scream it swallowed on the kingdom's behalf — and witness it. Hold it. Speak it. Let it finally be seen by someone who isn't trying to use it or bury it."

"And then?"

"And then it can put the weight down. And the king lives, probably, if the crown hasn't eaten too much of him. And the witness—" She stopped. For the first time all evening, her steady taster's hands weren't steady. "The witness pays what I paid. The valley's price. It will hollow you. Everything you are, everything you remember, everything that makes you *you* — the god's memory is four hundred years deep, and yours is twenty-some years shallow, and water finds its level. You'll pour yourself into it and come out the other side a woman with a stranger's face and no idea why her hands are scarred."

The fire cracked. I thought about my teacher's face, which I could still summon if I tried — mostly. About the taste of my first meal, bread and dripping salt, which lived somewhere behind my teeth. About Wren, three days behind me, doing his terrible arithmetic. About a king I'd saved from a single almond once, without ever seeing his face clearly, because looking at royalty was above my station.

"There's another way," I said. "There's always another way. We find the original binding. We strengthen it. We—"

"We?" she said gently. "Theo. There is no *we* in this. There's a woman in a kitchen who already paid, and a woman sitting across from her who hasn't paid yet, and a god getting stronger every day a court full of guilty people keeps feeding it. I would take it from you if I could. I tried to write that in the letter too. The words kept going blank." She leaned forward. "So here is what I can give you instead, the only inheritance I have left: training. Before you walk into that feast or that throne room or wherever it ends, you need to learn to hold foreign memory without drowning. I can't remember how I learned. But my hands remember, and my dreams remember, and there are techniques older than either of us, and I have thirty years of exile to fill with something, and teaching my daughter not to die seems like a fine use for them."

"And if I refuse?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and in her face I saw the future — the woman I might become, hollowed and calm, keeping bees or herbs or bitter almond oil, waiting thirty years for a knock in a coded rhythm.

"Then you refuse," she said. "You're a grown woman. You survived the valley; you can survive anything, including your mother's disappointment. But you won't refuse. I know because I know why you crossed the border, and it wasn't for freedom papers. Nobody walks out of the only home they've ever known, into a land that tastes of copper and fear, for *papers*." She stood, and began clearing the cups, and her back was to me when she said the next part, quietly, the way you say the truest things. "You walked out because you're a taster. And a taster cannot stand in a room with poison in the king's cup and do nothing. It's not courage. It's the job."

Outside, the gulls had gone quiet. Far inland — too far to see, but I felt it anyway, the way you feel weather coming in a scar — the Valley of Ash sat under its smoke, keeping its secrets, keeping mine, keeping the shape of a girl who used to be me.

"Start tomorrow," my mother said, turning back with two clean cups and the ghost of a smile. "Lessons in bitterness. First lesson: how to swallow a century without choking."

"And the second lesson?"

Her smile faded into something older and sadder, and she nodded toward the window, toward the east, toward the capital where a god-king was preparing his next confession trial.

"The second lesson," she said, "is learning what it tastes like when a god starts calling your name."
