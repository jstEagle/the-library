## 13. Lessons in Bitterness

My mother's cottage smelled of rosemary and old smoke, and it had exactly one chair, which told me everything about how she'd lived for the last thirty years.

"You'll sit," she said, and I sat, because some things are true in every valley. "Now. Before we begin, we establish the rules."

"Rules," I said. "For remembering."

"For *surviving*." She set a clay cup in front of me. It was empty. It would not stay that way. "The god's memory is not a story you read, girl. It's a meal. And you are about to eat thirty centuries of someone else's sins. There are rules, or it eats you back."

I looked at her hands—scarred, steady, stained faintly brown at the fingertips like she'd been dipping them in soil. Taster's hands. My hands, thirty years from now, if I was lucky enough to have thirty years.

"Rule one," she said. "You never take more than you can taste. A sip, not a swallow. The moment you stop tasting and start drowning, you pull out."

"And if I can't pull out?"

Her jaw tightened. "Then I do what I did before, and neither of us will enjoy it."

I didn't ask what she'd done before. I'd seen the hollow where her memories used to be. I'd watched her search my face for something familiar and find nothing, and I'd understood, finally, why she'd left me in a valley built to forget: not because she didn't love me, but because she loved me enough to make sure no one could ever follow me into what she carried.

Rule two was breathing. Rule three was naming what I tasted out loud, because unnamed things fester. Rule four—and this one she said quietly, looking at the empty cup rather than at me—was that memory does not vanish when you take it in. It *trades*. Every foreign thing I held, I paid for with something of my own.

"What kind of something?" I asked.

"Small things, at first," she said. "The color of your first dress. A song you hummed without knowing it. The mind sheds what it must to make room. You won't get to choose what goes." She finally met my eyes. "So hold on tight to whatever matters most. Say it to yourself every morning. Write it down. The mind respects ink more than intention."

That night I wrote on the back of my freedom papers, in the margins, small as secrets: *Wren's left eyebrow lifts before he lies. The king used to hum while reading petitions. My mother's name is Sera. My name is Theo Dravenmoor.* I read the list until the candle died.

* * *

The first memory came to me in a cup.

Sera's method was simple and merciless: she'd steep dried herbs in water, touch the cup, and pour a thread of the god's memory down her own arm and into the water—the way she'd once poured it into the king, all those years ago, binding instead of banishing. Then I drank.

The first sip tasted of iron and wet stone.

I was standing in a temple that predated every temple I knew, and the floor was slick, and people were screaming in a language that hadn't been spoken since before the Valley of Ash had a name. I was very tall. I was wearing gold. And I was holding a child by the wrist, and the child was crying, and somewhere behind me a crowd was chanting a word that meant *take it away, take it away, take it away.*

I pulled out gasping, my own kitchen—the cottage, the rosemary, Sera's flat stare—all of it swimming back around me like light through water.

"What was that?" I managed.

"The beginning," Sera said. "The first feeding. They crowned it because they were afraid of it, and they fed it because they'd crowned it. Drink again. Same memory. Deeper."

"That's not—" I stopped. Swallowed. "How many times?"

"Until you can stand in that temple and feel what it felt and still know your own name after." She refilled the cup. "You asked to learn this."

I drank again. And again. Eleven times that first day, until the temple stopped being a place I visited and became a place I could stand in, arms crossed, gold heavy on my shoulders, listening to the screaming with something that was almost patience.

That night I reached for the taste of my first meal—I'd been saving it, hoarding it, a memory of honey porridge and my own small fists banging the table—and found only the shape of it. Honey. Porridge. Gone soft, like a fruit pressed too long. I wrote *honey porridge* in the margin anyway, but the words didn't bring the taste back. Words are just the bones of things.

* * *

By the fourth day I could hold a century.

Not comfortably. Comfort wasn't the point. But I could sit in the god's memory of the plague years—when villages fed it their dead by burning letters of confession and breathing the smoke toward the capital—and keep one corner of myself separate, cool, watching. That corner was mine. Sera called it the taster's tongue. Everything else could drown; that part floated.

"You're better than I was," she said, and there was something complicated in her voice, pride and grief sharing a plate.

"I've had practice being other people," I said. "A taster spends her whole life pretending to be nobody. Turns out that's training."

She almost smiled. Almost. "The hard ones aren't the sins," she said. "Anyone can survive horror; horror is loud. The hard ones are the small cruelties. The moments where it was given something tender and ate it anyway, because eating was all it knew. Those are the ones that stick to your ribs."

She was right. The plague years slid off me like grease off slate. What stayed was a memory from the god's two-hundredth year: a widow who prayed to it nightly, faithfully, for her son's safe return from war, and who was answered—not by the god, but by the court, who forged a letter and fed the widow's gratitude into the crown like cream into tea. The god had tasted her faith secondhand. It had kept the taste for six hundred years.

I woke up that night with tears on my face that weren't entirely mine, and I couldn't remember the lullaby my nurse used to sing. I knew there had been one. I knew it had gone up and down, up and down, like a boat on calm water. The tune itself was gone, traded away like coin for bread.

I added it to the list. *There was a lullaby. Up and down, like a boat.* The margin of my freedom papers was getting crowded.

* * *

On the seventh day, Wren arrived.

He came up the hill road dusty and official-looking, which for Wren meant his coat was buttoned wrong. He took one look at the single chair, dragged a crate over, and sat across from me like a man settling in for a long negotiation.

"You look terrible," he said.

"I'm eating thirty centuries of sin. How's the court?"

"Worse. The confession trials have moved to the great hall. Twice a week now. Nobles line up to be emptied—it's become fashionable, being forgiven by the crown. Half the court has forgotten what guilt feels like on its own terms." He rubbed his eyes. "And the king stood through an entire ceremony yesterday without blinking. In the rain. Someone applauded."

I told him what I'd learned so far—the temple, the widow, the shape of the thing we were fighting. Not a conqueror. A mouth. A vessel that had been made to hold what others couldn't carry, and which had learned, over the centuries, to want.

"He doesn't want worship," I said. "I thought it would want worship. It wants—"

"A witness," Wren said. "Yes. I've been reading the old archive records. Every binding ceremony, every feeding, documented and then sealed. We have five hundred years of paperwork proving what was done to it, filed under lock and key by men who called it mercy." His laugh had no humor in it. "We are a kingdom of excellent record-keepers and appalling liars."

He stayed two days. On the second evening, sitting outside while Sera burned herbs and muttered, he said, quietly, "Theo. When this is done. Whatever's left of you. There will be a place at court. Not as a taster. As—"

"Don't," I said. "Don't build the ending yet. I've seen what happens to endings in this trade. They get traded too."

He didn't argue. He just nodded, and his left eyebrow lifted, and I checked—yes, before he spoke. Still true. Still mine.

* * *

The ninth day broke something.

It was a small memory, as these things go. The god's four-hundredth year: a harvest festival, a king—some interim king, a placeholder in the long parade of them—who came to the shrine alone at night, drunk on grief for a daughter dead of fever, and screamed at the god for an hour. Cursed it. Blamed it. And then, spent, knelt in the ash and whispered, *I'm sorry. I know it wasn't yours. I just needed someone to tell.*

And the god had held that apology the way a starving man holds bread he's been told is poisoned. Had turned it over. Had waited, all night, for the man to take it back.

He took it back by morning. They always took it back.

I came out of that memory and couldn't find my teacher's face.

Not Sera—her face I had, fresh, daily, stubbornly present. My first teacher. Mistress Halloway, the kitchen-mistress who taught me that bitterness is just sweetness that hasn't been introduced properly. I knew her name. I knew her lessons. Her face was a smooth oval where features should be, like a thumbprint smoothed out of clay.

I sat very still and did the arithmetic. Temple, plague, widow, festival. Four deep memories, dozens of sips. Four payments, and counting.

"Theo." Sera's voice, careful. "Where are you?"

"Here." I tested the word. It held. "I'm here. Just... taking inventory."

I went to the list. Added: *Mistress Halloway. Round face. Flour always on her forearms. Laughed like a door hinge.* The words were accurate. They were also useless. Ink holds facts; it doesn't hold faces.

That was the moment I understood rule four in my bones, the way you understand a poison only after it's in you. This was the price, and it wasn't a price I was paying once. It was rent. Every month of the god's history I made room for, my own history thinned to cover it. I was becoming a house with beautiful new furniture and holes where the walls used to be.

And here was the worst part, the part I've never told anyone until now: some of the trades felt fair. The widow's faith was worth more than a lullaby. The drunken king's apology was worth more than a face I couldn't picture anymore anyway. If survival meant choosing which past to keep, then maybe—

No. I caught myself. That was the god talking, or the trader's logic, or just exhaustion. A price is a price. You pay it and you don't pretend it was a gift.

* * *

On the twelfth day, Sera gave me the last lesson.

"We've done the feedings," she said. "The bindings. The centuries of swallowed guilt. Now we do the center of it. The original crime."

I looked at the cup. It looked back, the way cups do when they're waiting.

"What happened to it," I said. "Before. What they did first."

"Yes." She touched the cup and didn't pour. "This is the deepest memory it has. The one everything else sits on top of. When you take this one, you won't be visiting. You'll be standing in the room where it was made. And Theo—" she paused, and for the first time since I'd arrived, her voice shook, "—the last time I took this memory, even a sip of it, I lost twenty years in a single afternoon. I lost your father's name. I lost the reason I ever started tasting at all. I came out of it knowing only that I had to bind the thing and hide my daughter somewhere magic couldn't reach."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a bird insisted on existing.

"Why are you telling me this now?" I asked. "You could have let me walk in blind."

"Because blind is how I walked in, and look at me." She pushed the cup toward me. "You deserve the choice. Take it tonight, or take it never, and we find another way—a worse way, a bloodier way, but another way."

I thought about the king, standing unblinking in the rain while his court applauded. About the widow. About the drunken father who apologized and then unsaid it. About a god made of everyone's discarded guilt, waiting six hundred years for one person to finish a sentence.

I thought about my list. *My name is Theo Dravenmoor.* Nine days of margin left, maybe less.

"I'll need the whole morning," I said. "And I'll need you to say my name. Every hour. Out loud. Even if I don't answer."

"Even if you scream at me to stop?"

"*Especially* then."

She nodded once, taster to taster, and poured.

The memory came up through the cup like cold water rising in a well, and the first thing I tasted was salt—not tears, older than tears—and the last thing I heard, before the cottage, the rosemary, the world, all of it dissolved, was my mother's voice saying *Theo Dravenmoor* like a woman planting a flag in a flood.

And then I was in the room.

* * *

I don't remember coming out.

Sera says it took nine hours. She says she said my name eighty-one times, and that twice I answered in a language dead a thousand years, and once I wept without sound for over an hour while the fire burned down to coals. She says when I finally opened my eyes, I looked at her the way you look at a stranger you're certain you've met, and asked, "Who was left in the valley?" and she said, "You were, my darling, you were," and I cried then, properly, like a person and not a vessel.

What I remember is the room. I remember the room perfectly. I will remember it until the forgetting takes it, and possibly after.

But I remember other things too, faintly, like footprints in snow that's nearly melted. I remember deciding something, in the deep of it. Making a bargain with myself. Because when I stood in that room and saw what they had done—what the *first* of them had done, before crowns, before courts, when it was just frightened people and a power they couldn't carry—I understood the shape of the ending we needed. Not a battle. Not a binding. Something else.

I remember writing it down. I remember checking the list first, though. Checking that I was still there.

*My name is Theo Dravenmoor,* the margin said, in my own hand, slightly shakier than before.

*I am the taster's daughter. I am the taster.*

And beneath it, in fresher ink, the plan:

*They fed it guilt. So we give it truth. All of it. Publicly. Before witnesses.*

*We make the kingdom watch.*

I showed Sera. She read it twice, and then she did something I'd never seen her do: she closed her eyes and exhaled, long and slow, like a woman setting down a weight she'd carried for thirty years.

"It might work," she said. "God help us, it might actually work."

"Now we just have to get back to the capital," I said, "past a regent's faction that wants me dead, a court full of fashionable guilt-junkies, and a god-king who's learning to eat in public."

"Yes," she said. "That part."

Outside, the sun was going down over the exile hills, and I counted what I had left: one name, one plan, one mother, one chamberlain presumably still buttoning his coat wrong somewhere, and a margin of paper about half full.

It would have to be enough.

It occurred to me, as I packed my few things in the blue dark, that I had already begun to forget the exact smell of the Valley of Ash—the place that raised me, the place that kept me invisible and alive. I stood in the doorway a moment, trying to hold it, knowing I was holding water.

Somewhere south, a king who had stopped blinking was waiting for a witness.

I blew out the candle and went to bed, because tomorrow we rode for the capital, and I intended to arrive with every memory I had left—including, if the gods were generous, my own.
