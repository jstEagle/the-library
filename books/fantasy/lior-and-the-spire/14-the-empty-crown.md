## 14. The Empty Crown

They let me out of the cells on a Tuesday, which struck me as an oddly bureaucratic mercy. Treason, in my experience, is usually punished on Fridays, when the courts are feeling decisive and the crowds are free.

My liberators were three people I had never seen before and would never see again: a laundress with a key ring stolen off a sleeping turnkey, a clerk who forged my release papers with the bored fluency of a man who did it every market day, and a boy of perhaps fifteen who said only, "The queen regent's people are coming back for you at dawn. Move." Then he handed me a bundle containing my own coat—someone had gone to my rooms and retrieved it, which meant someone had decided my rooms were worth searching, which meant I was worth more alive than dead to somebody, somewhere—and vanished into the fog like a debt collector at a funeral.

I did not ask questions. In the Ash Valley you learn that curiosity is a luxury item, priced beyond most salaries. I walked out through the laundry gate wearing a borrowed apron over my coat, and no one looked at me twice, because no one ever looks at anyone twice in this city unless that person is carrying something heavy or bleeding something important.

I want it noted, for the record I am building in my head against future tribunals: I did not weep at freedom. I did not kiss the ground. I walked four streets, bought a meat pie from a vendor who charged me double because I looked like a man who had been in a cell for nine days, ate it standing in an alley, and thought, with perfect clarity: *I built this.*

Not the cell. The machine that put me in it.

* * *

You have to understand what the capital looked like in those nine days I was underground. Or rather, you have to understand what it stopped looking like.

When I was arrested, the king—the thing wearing the king—had begun holding what the criers called "Reconciliation Audiences." Twice a week, in the great hall, any noble or official could come forward and confess their sins before the crown. It sounded benign. It sounded, God help us, almost holy. A kingdom drowning in guilt, offered absolution by its own anointed head.

What actually happened was this: Lord Aldery came forward weeping about grain tariffs he'd falsified during the famine years, and he walked out light as a boy, laughing, forgiven, and within a month he could not remember his wife's name. Lady Vess confessed to poisoning her brother's inheritance and left the hall radiant, and by spring she had forgotten how to read. The god did not forgive. The god *consumed*. Guilt went into the crown the way wine goes into a drunkard, and something came out the other side—something lighter, emptier, easier to wear.

And they kept coming. That was the part that made my hands shake as I watched it, nine days later, from the back of the great hall where no one looks twice at a man in a borrowed apron. They *kept coming*. Nobles who had done nothing worse than cheat on their taxes knelt before the throne and poured themselves out like jugs, and the court called it grace, and the criers called it renewal, and the queue stretched out the doors and down the steps into the square.

The king sat on his throne and did not blink. His shadow lagged behind him now by a full second—I timed it against the pendulum clock, because if you cannot stop a disaster you may as well measure it—and when each confessor finished, the king smiled. Not the king's smile. Something older, using the king's mouth the way a puppeteer uses a glove.

"You are forgiven," it said, to each of them, in a voice like ash settling. "Your weight is mine now."

And the crown—it sounds mad, but I swear it on whatever's left of my honor—the crown sat lower on his head each week. Heavier. Fed.

* * *

I spent my first night of freedom in the house of the laundress, whose name I never learned and whose silence I paid for with the last of my coin. She gave me soup and a blanket and the particular courtesy of pretending not to notice a grown man staring at the ceiling until dawn.

Here is what I stared at:

I had been Chamberlain for eleven years. Eleven years of managing the king's household, his schedule, his audiences, his *meals*. When the prayers started being answered again—when the shrine smoked and pilgrims came and the old gods stirred like sleepers turning over—I had been the one who advised sealing the shrine. Not out of malice. Out of order. The valley does not tolerate disorder; magic does not enter here; a smoking shrine was an anomaly, and anomalies are managed.

I had been the one who added Theo Dravenmoor to the king's personal tasting roster, thinking her peculiar tongue might detect what my eyes could not. And she had—God, she had, faster and more truly than any of us—but by then the poisonings were not assassinations, they were *invitations*, and I had stood in council chambers and watched the king eat dishes laced with enough arsenic to fell a garrison, and I had told myself: *the tasters will handle it, the protocols will handle it, the machinery of the court will handle it.*

That was my sin, and I confess it freely now, alone, to a ceiling: I believed in the machinery. I believed that a kingdom was a device, and that if each gear turned properly—taster tasting, chamberlain chamberlaining, physician physicianing—no single horror could get through. I had spent eleven years oiling those gears. And when the horror came not *through* the machine but *as* the machine—when the crown itself became the thing that consumed—I had built the very apparatus that fed it. The confession trials were my audiences, restructured. The queue of the guilty was my appointment ledger, repurposed. Even the language—"your weight is mine now"—was a grotesque inversion of the absolution formula I had drafted for the king's Jubilee address, three years ago, in my own hand, at my own desk.

I had written the liturgy of my own damnation and called it administration.

* * *

By morning I had a plan, which is the only cure I know for guilt, and a poor one.

The plan required three things: Theo, the border, and time. Theo was somewhere outside the valley with the king—or rather, with the body of the king, dragged toward the valley's edge where the dead magic might weaken the crown's grip. I knew this because before my arrest I had set the journey in motion myself; Wren's Bargain, she'd called it, with the dry contempt she reserved for things that frightened her. Freedom papers for the truth of who left her in the Ash Valley. A fair trade. I still owed her the truth. I intended to survive long enough to pay it.

Time was the problem. Time was always the problem. Because while Theo moved toward the border at the speed of a royal carriage and a reluctant woman, the god moved at the speed of human guilt, and human guilt, I can tell you after eleven years at court, moves at the speed of *light*.

So I went to find allies, and this is where I must be honest about the state of the capital, because the honest account is ugly.

The court was not resisting. That was the horror of it—not resistance, not even cowardice, but *gratitude*. The confession trials had done what no monarch had ever managed: they had emptied the nobility of its secrets, and a man with no secrets is a man with no defenses, and a court with no defenses is a court that belongs to whoever holds the crown. Lord Aldery, who could no longer remember his wife, chaired the committee organizing the anniversary feast. Lady Vess, who could no longer read, signed decrees she could not review. The queen regent's faction—which had arrested me, which wanted the king declared dead so they could seize power—they were not heroes either. They simply wanted the machine for themselves. They saw the god as a tool that had been mismanaged, not a horror that needed ending. Their agents had questioned me for nine days about Theo's whereabouts, about the border plan, about who else knew. They wanted to control the god, not free the king.

Everyone wanted something. No one wanted to *stop*.

Except, it turned out, a handful of people I had never noticed in eleven years of noticing everything. The under-chamberlain, Pell, a gray little man I had promoted precisely because he was too dull to scheme—he came to the laundress's door at dusk with documents proving the king had been clinically dying for a year before the crown took him, kept alive by nothing but the god's will. The physician's apprentice, a sharp-eyed girl named Sorrel who had watched her master falsify the king's health records and hated him for it. And one of the palace guards, a woman with a scarred jaw who said, "I stood at the throne for six hours a day for two months, Chamberlain. I watched forty people walk out of that hall lighter and emptier. I started sleeping with a knife under my pillow because I couldn't remember why I was sad anymore."

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked her. "I built the trials' schedule. I signed the audience rosters."

"I know," she said, without cruelty. "That's why. You know how it works from the inside. We need someone who knows how it works from the inside."

Guilt, it turns out, has one practical use: it makes you useful to people with better consciences.

* * *

We met in the under-croft beneath the records office, because there is no place in a palace more reliably ignored than the room where its failures are filed. Sorrel brought the physician's notes. Pell brought the feast plans. The guard—her name was Brava, and I resolved to remember it, since remembering names seemed suddenly the most sacred duty left to anyone—brought the rotation of the throne room watch.

And I brought the arithmetic.

"Here is the shape of it," I said, and my voice sounded steadier than I felt, which is the chamberlain's oldest trick and possibly his only one. "Theo reaches the border within days. If the plan works—if the crown's hold weakens at the valley's edge—we may have a window. Hours, perhaps. During which the god is fighting the dead magic instead of feeding."

"And if it doesn't work?" Pell asked.

"Then we do it anyway," I said, "because the alternative is the anniversary feast."

I laid out the feast plans on the crate between us. Every noble house represented. Every confessor in attendance, their emptied selves gathered in one hall. And at the center of it, the coronation anniversary itself—a date soaked in ritual significance, which to a creature that feeds on accumulated meaning is not a date but a *banquet*. The god had chosen it deliberately. One year to the day since it had settled fully into its coat of king. It meant to feed on all of them at once, publicly, permanently. After the feast, there would be no court to save. There would be a congregation.

Sorrel read the plans upside down, frowning. "Can she even do it? Your taster? Poison the crown?"

"She can taste intentions," I said. "She can hear lies as flavors. Outside the valley, she is the only weapon in the kingdom that works on it—because everything else in the kingdom, every soldier and decree and prayer, is made of the same stuff the god eats. But Theo isn't. Theo grew up where magic goes to die. She is the one person in this kingdom the god cannot digest." I heard my own voice crack slightly on the next words and let it. "Which is why I sent her away. Which is why I'm not with her. Which is why I am here, doing the one thing I am actually good at, which is scheduling."

Brava almost smiled. "Scheduling."

"The feast runs six courses," I said. "The third course is consommé. Every plate passes through the tasting office first—that protocol is mine, I wrote it, I know its gaps better than its author should admit. If a taster were standing at that pass, in the one role no one guards against, in the one office the god trusts because the god has been drinking from it all year—"

Pell went pale. "You want her to come *back*. Into the hall. Into the feeding."

"I want her to have the choice," I said. "Which is more than I gave her the first time."

* * *

I wrote the letter that night, by candle stub, in the cipher Theo and I had agreed on—a taster's cipher, built from menu annotations, because the safest code in any palace is the one hidden in the most boring document imaginable. *The consommé requires almond. Come home. Forgive me if you can; hurry regardless.* I sealed it with Pell's help and gave it to a grain merchant leaving for the border road at dawn, a man who owed the records office a favor he did not know he owed.

Then I sat alone in the under-croft among the filed failures of a kingdom, and I did the thing I had been avoiding for nine days, which was to think about the king himself.

Not the god. The man. Aldous the Third, who had once, eleven years ago, on my first day as Chamberlain, stayed late after council to ask me whether I missed my mother, because he had noticed I'd mentioned her once, weeks earlier, in passing. Who had remembered. Who had cared, quietly, without witnesses, in the way of genuinely decent men. That man had been dying for a year—drowning slowly inside his own body while the thing in the crown kept the flesh moving like a marionette kept upright by a patient hand—and none of us had known. None of us had *looked*. We had looked at the crown, at the throne, at the machinery, at everything except the man inside it, because looking at kings is not what courtiers do.

"We are going to get you out," I said to the dark, to the distance, to a dying man miles away at the edge of a valley that forgets. "I don't know if you'll survive it. I don't know if any of us will. But you will be *seen*, Aldous. Before the end, you will be seen by everyone, and they will know what was done to you, and what was done *with* you. That much I can promise."

It was not much of a prayer. The valley's gods were dead or absent, and the one god left listening was the enemy. But I said it anyway, and then I blew out the candle, because sentiment is expensive and I had budgeted exactly one minute of it.

* * *

Dawn found me climbing the bell tower of the records office—not for any strategic reason, though I told Brava it gave a sightline to the palace, but because I needed to see the whole machine at once, the way you check a wound to know how deep it goes.

From up there, the capital lay spread out in the gray light: the great hall with its queue already forming, confessors waiting patiently in the cold to pour themselves into a crown; the palace above it, windows dark, the king not blinking somewhere inside; and beyond the walls, far to the south, a smudge on the horizon that might have been weather and might have been the sealed shrine, burning still, refusing to stop.

And below me, moving through the streets toward the palace, the people. Thousands of them. Some queuing to confess. Some watching. Some, I noticed with a catch in my throat, simply *looking*—at the hall, at the palace, at the empty-eyed nobles walking out lighter than they'd gone in—with expressions I recognized from Theo's face the day she tasted almond in a soup that should have had none. The expression of a person realizing that something is wrong with the food.

They were starting to taste it. All of them. Slowly, in the way of populations, which notice everything a season too late.

"We have days," I said aloud, to Brava, to the city, to no one. "Perhaps less."

Below me, the bells began to ring for morning audience, and the sound rolled across the rooftops like a summons, and I thought: *I built these gears. Every one of them. And now I will take this machine apart with my own hands, bolt by bolt, or I will die knowing exactly which pieces were mine.*

Somewhere south, a woman who had spent her life being invisible was walking toward a border with a dead god's memory pressing against her skull. Somewhere north, a crown grew heavier by the confession. And between them, me: a gray man in a borrowed coat with a letter in the wind and a schedule in his head, finally, fatally, visible.

The consommé, I reminded myself, is served third.

There was still time.

Probably.
