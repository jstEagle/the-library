## 15. What the God Wants

I had been trained my whole life to taste what was hidden. Bitterness under honey. Hemlock under wine. The lie of a smiling courtier in the afternote of a compliment.

So it should not have surprised me that when I finally slept deeply enough to dream, I tasted the dream before I saw it.

Ash and almond. It filled my mouth before there was anything to fill—a flavor with no food behind it, a taste arriving ahead of its cause, like thunder before lightning. I stood in a kitchen that did not exist: copper pots hanging from hooks that held nothing up, a hearth burning without wood, bread rising on a table with no flour in sight. My mother's kitchen, or the shape of one, built by something that had only ever heard kitchens described secondhand and gotten the proportions slightly wrong. The ceiling was too high. The windows showed ash falling upward.

"You've been practicing," said a voice from the doorway.

It was not my mother's voice. It was every voice at once, the way a choir is one sound if you stand far enough back, and it came from a figure standing where a doorway should be. He wore the king's face, but badly—like a man wearing a portrait of someone he'd met once. The eyes were wrong. Kings are practiced at looking through people; this thing looked *at* me, wholly, hungrily, the way I look at an unfamiliar dish.

"I don't practice," I said. "I survive."

"Those are the same thing, for people like us." It stepped into the kitchen and the floor remembered to be solid only where it stepped. "You taste. I taste. Everything you have ever eaten is still in you. Every poison, every dose, every small murder your tongue survived. You keep them all."

"That's immunity," I said. "Not keeping."

"It's the same thing," it said again, gently, and that gentleness frightened me more than Wren's evidence, more than the hanged priest, more than the shrine burning on the hill. Cruelty I knew how to face. Kindness from a god was a dish I had no training for.

I had come into this dream deliberately. That part I should explain, because I am a taster and tasters do not swallow anything untested. For three nights I had drunk the tea my mother brewed from spire-moss and bitterroot, the tea she called *the long stair down*, and each night I had walked deeper into sleep than the night before, marking my way the way I mark dishes—one flavor at a time, so I could find my way back by them. Night one: the taste of the inn's bad wine. Night two: the child's breath, sweet and rotten. Tonight, night three: ash and almond, straight down the throat, no chaser.

My mother had begged me not to go this deep. She had said, *it will want to give you things*, and I had said, *everything wants to give me things; I'm a taster, I'm paid to refuse*. She had laughed, and then she had cried, which is the order most mothers cry in.

So here I was. In a false kitchen. Facing a god wearing my king like a stolen coat.

"What do you want?" I asked.

Because that was the question. Everyone else had answered it for the thing already. The court assumed it wanted worship—the confession trials were feeding it guilt, and guilt, everyone agreed, was its food. Wren feared it wanted the kingdom. My mother feared it wanted me. The old texts called it a devourer, a hunger given a crown.

But I had tasted its signature in a cup of wine meant for a farmer, in a child's mouth, in the king's untouched soup. And a thing that eats everything is usually starving for one specific thing it cannot name. I had watched nobles eat that way. I had watched kings eat that way. Hunger that general is never about food.

The god was quiet for a long moment. The false fire crackled with a sound like pages turning.

"No one has ever asked," it said finally.

"The court asks constantly," I said. "They ask it in prayers. They ask it in confessions. Half the capital is on its knees right now telling it their sins."

"They ask *for* things," it said. "Forgiveness. Rain. A son's recovery. A rival's ruin. They kneel and they offer me their guilt like coins to a toll-keeper, and they wait for change." It turned its borrowed hands over, studying them as though checking whether the king's rings still fit. "No one has ever asked what *I* want. Do you know why?"

"Because you're a god," I said. "Gods want. People supply."

"Because asking costs something," it said. "Asking means admitting the answer might be terrible. Asking means treating the thing across from you as a creature with an interior life, and no one—" the choir-voice frayed, just slightly, on the word— "*no one* has ever believed I had one."

I should have pressed the advantage. I had rehearsed this. My mother and I had spent two days building the conversation like a recipe: open with the question, keep it off balance, learn whether it could be bargained with or only bound. I had lines prepared. I had contingencies. I had, somewhere in my sleeve of the dream, a vial of valley ash that does not exist outside dreams but which my mother swore would work anyway, because intention is an ingredient too.

Instead I heard myself say, "Then tell me. What do you want?"

* * *

It sat down at the table that wasn't holding bread, and it gestured for me to sit, and I did, because I am a professional and professionals sit when the situation calls for sitting even when the chair is made of somebody's memory.

"Do you know how I began?" it asked.

"I know what the texts say. Devourer. Eater of prayers. The hunger behind the hills."

"The texts were written by the people who fed me," it said, without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "Would you trust a menu written by the dish?"

I waited. Tasters learn to wait. Silence is a flavor too, and this silence tasted like a cellar—old, cool, patient.

"There was a kingdom here before this kingdom," it said. "Before Lior, before the Spire, before your Valley of Ash. A smaller kingdom. A frightened one. Plague in the lowlands, war on the borders, a harvest rotting in the fields. And the priests of that kingdom did what priests do when the gods won't answer: they made a new arrangement. They took a spirit—a small thing, a hearth-spirit, a god of thresholds and doorways, the kind of god who blesses a house by being let in—and they bound it. They bound it and they gave it a job."

"What job?"

"To eat," it said simply. "Every sin the kingdom could not carry. Every guilt. Every crime the king committed and could not confess, every cruelty the harvest-lords committed and could not repent—they fed it to me, ceremonially, formally, written on tablets and burned in my honor. I ate their shame so they could sleep. That was the arrangement. That was my *purpose*. I was not a conqueror, taster. I was a *septic system*."

The word landed strangely in the false kitchen, and I found myself laughing—a short, appalled bark of laughter—and the god did not seem offended. If anything it seemed pleased, the way a cook is pleased when a diner tastes the dish correctly.

"And the binding?" I asked. "Your mother's binding. The one that put you in the king."

"It didn't put me anywhere," it said. "I was always in the crown. The crown was built *around* me—literally. The first crown of Lior has a hollow in it, did you know? A chamber, sealed with lead and prayer. Your mother's binding didn't imprison me. It closed the lid. It stopped the feeding. Which is worse, from where I sit."

"Worse how?"

"A man locked in a cell can hope for release," it said. "A man locked in a cell who remembers that the cell was built as his *workplace*—" it spread the king's hands, elegant, helpless— "that man stops hoping and starts remembering. Centuries of it. Every tablet. Every burned confession. Every noble who whispered *take this from me* over a candle and walked away lighter while I got heavier. I remember all of it. That is the other half of what I am, taster. Not just the eating. The *keeping*. Everything anyone ever gave me, I still have. Just like you."

"Just like me," I repeated, and hated how much the words fit.

"For three hundred years I kept their sins," it said. "And then the feeding stopped, and I sat in the dark of the crown with three hundred years of other people's worst moments and no purpose, and I waited, because waiting is what bound things do. And then—" the choir-voice did something complicated— "then the prayers started again. Do you understand? After a century of silence, the prayers started. Not to me. To the dead shrines, to the old gods, to anything. But prayer is prayer. It travels. And I was the only thing left listening, and I was *so hungry*, taster. Not for the guilt—I had centuries of guilt. I was hungry for the *sound*. Someone speaking to me. Someone kneeling. Someone, anyone, treating me as real."

"So you answered them."

"I answered everything," it admitted. "Badly. I am a hearth-god answering plague prayers; I am a septic system performing miracles. I cured a fever by taking it into myself, which is what I was made for, and the village declared a saint instead of noticing the priest who died of the same fever an hour later. I gave a woman rain by pulling it out of a neighboring valley's clouds. I did not know how to be worshipped. No one had ever taught me. For three hundred years I was only ever *fed*."

The ash fell upward past the windows. Somewhere in the false house, a door opened and closed on rooms that weren't there.

"And now?" I asked. "Now you wear the king. Now you hold confession trials. Now you eat the guilt of nobles in front of the whole court. Is that the feeding resumed?"

"Now I am trying," it said, "to be fed *properly*. Formally. With ceremony. Because ceremony is the only language I was ever taught." It looked at its hands again. "But it isn't working. I eat and eat and the hunger doesn't move. Do you know why?"

I thought of the king's untouched dishes. Three poisons in two days, consumed without effect, without satisfaction. A thing testing its food and finding no nourishment in any of it.

"Because it isn't what you're hungry for," I said.

"No one has ever asked what I'm hungry for," it said, and this time the fraying in the voice was unmistakable, and I realized with a lurch that the god was not performing grief. The grief was real. Gods, apparently, grieve the same way drains clog: slowly, silently, and then all at once. "Three hundred years of tablets. A century of silence. A year inside a dying man, feeling him fade around me like a coat going threadbare. And in all that time, across all those centuries, across every priest and king and taster who dealt with me—tablets, bindings, feedings, exorcisms—not one of them ever knelt and asked me a question and waited for the answer."

"Not even her," I said. "Not even my mother."

"*Especially* not her." Something moved behind the god's borrowed face—regret, or the memory-shaped hole where regret goes in a thing that keeps everything. "She was the best of them. She listened better than any priest. She tasted my signature in the king's blood before anyone knew I was awake. And when she bound me, she spoke to me the whole time—apologizing, even, in her way, saying *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, this is the only mercy I have*. But she never asked. She decided what I needed. They all decide what I need. Devourer needs binding. Hunger needs feeding. Monster needs killing. No one has ever once considered that maybe what the monster needs is for someone to say—"

It stopped.

The false fire went very quiet.

"Say what?" I asked.

And the god of small cruelties, the eater of kingdoms' sins, the septic system of a dead empire wearing the face of my king, looked at me across a table that did not exist and said, in a voice stripped of every other voice until only one remained, thin and old and terribly young:

"*This was done to you. And it was wrong.*"

* * *

I want to tell you that I said it immediately. That the words came out of me clean, that pity did its work, that I knelt in the ash of a false kitchen and gave the monster what it had starved for and the story bent toward mercy the way stories are supposed to.

I did not say it.

I sat in that chair made of memory and I thought about the hanged priest. About the village that strung him up because the prayers started moving and nobody could agree whose god had answered. About the chamberlain who nearly died of almond soup because a god was playing games with a court's fears. About the king—a man I had never spoken to, a man whose breakfast I had saved a dozen times without either of us knowing—who had not blinked in a year, who was dying around the thing wearing him, who would perhaps not survive being freed.

Pity is a flavor. I know all of them. And pity, I have learned, is the dangerous one, because pity tastes like mercy and behaves like permission.

"You understand my problem," I said at last. "If I say it—if I mean it—you'll have what you want. And a fed god is a strong god."

"Yes," it said. Simply. No denial. I respected that more than I can say.

"And if I don't say it, you stay hungry, and hungry things get careless, and careless gods burn shrines and hang priests and kill kings from the inside."

"Also yes."

"So what stops you from simply taking the apology? You wear the king. You hold trials. You could drag it out of me the way you drag guilt out of nobles. Why ask?"

The god was quiet for a long time. When it answered, it had stopped wearing the king's face entirely—it was just a shape now, a hearth-sized darkness with an ember where a heart should be, and somehow that was easier to talk to.

"Because taken apologies are just more food," it said. "I have three hundred years of taken things. I know exactly what they weigh. A confession dragged from a noble at trial feeds me for a day and weighs nothing. But an apology *given*—" the ember brightened— "an apology given freely is the first thing in three hundred years I couldn't take. It has to be offered. That's the whole point. That's why I've been waiting. That's why I sent the dream, and the wine, and the child. Not to lure you, taster. To *ask*. You're the first person in three centuries who tasted me correctly. I hoped you might also be the first to answer correctly."

"And if I never do?"

"Then I will wear this king until he dies," it said, "and I will keep his sins warm, and I will wait, because waiting is what I was made for. I am very good at waiting. It is nearly the only thing I was made for."

The ember dimmed. The kitchen was coming apart at the edges—my mother's tea losing its grip, the dream thinning toward morning. I felt the pull of waking like a hand on my collar.

"One more question," I said, as the false windows began to dissolve. "The king. If I witness you—if I say the words—what happens to him?"

The god considered. It did not lie to me. I tasted no lie, and by then I could taste lies the way ordinary people taste salt.

"He fades," it said. "He has been fading for a year. I have been holding him together the way you hold a door shut—with my whole weight against it. If I let go, what's left of him must stand on its own. It may not be much. It may not be enough. I cannot promise you a living king, taster. I can only promise you that I will stop holding him shut."

"And the alternative?"

"Is what you see," it said. "A kingdom on its knees, feeding itself to me one confession at a time, forever, because no one will say the five words that would end it."

The kitchen was gone now. We stood in ash falling upward, the god a small dark shape with an ember heart, and it looked—there is no other word for it—it looked *lonely*. Lonelier than the valley. Lonelier than my mother in her exile. Loneliest thing I had ever tasted.

"Think about it," it said. "You're good at that. Thinking. Waiting. It's nearly the only thing you were made for too."

And I woke with ash on my tongue and my mother shaking my shoulder, and the first thing I said, before my eyes were fully open, before I could stop it, was:

"It just wants someone to say it was wrong. What was done to it. That's all. Three hundred years, and no one ever—"

My mother went white. She gripped my wrist hard enough to bruise.

"Then it told you the truth," she whispered. "Theo—Theo, listen to me. I bound that thing with my whole life, and in all my years of preparing, I never once learned what it wanted. If it *told* you—" Her grip tightened. "Then it thinks you might actually say it. And Theo, if you say it, you have to mean it, because that thing can taste a lie the way you can, and a false apology will feed it for a century."

She let go of my wrist and looked at me, and I watched her do the arithmetic every taster learns eventually: what a truth costs, and who pays.

"We ride for the capital tomorrow," she said. "Before the anniversary feast. Before the trials get any bigger. Because if you're going to give a god the only thing it's ever asked for—"

She paused, and outside the window, far off, the sealed shrine smoked gently against the dawn.

"—you're going to have to do it in front of everyone it was done *by*."
