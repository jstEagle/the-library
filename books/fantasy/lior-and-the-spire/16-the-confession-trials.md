## 16. The Confession Trials

They came for me at dawn, which I thought was a courtesy. Most arrests in the capital happen after midnight, when the corridors are empty and no one has to watch. Mine happened in full view of the kitchen staff, the under-clerks, and two laundresses who would later swear they'd always known I was guilty, though at the time they only looked frightened and kept wringing the same sheet.

"Chamberlain Wren," said the captain of the king's guard, not unkindly. He was a man I had dined with perhaps forty times. "You're wanted before His Majesty."

"At this hour?"

"He doesn't sleep anymore," the captain said, and there it was—the flat, careful voice of a man who has stopped believing his own explanations. "You know that."

I did know that. I had known it for a year, in the way one knows a house is on fire while continuing to make tea.

* * *

The throne room had changed since my arrest of Theo's freedom papers—no, let me be honest, since I gave her those papers and sent her out of the valley like a man throwing a rope to someone drowning while standing on the dock himself. The banners were the same crimson and gold. The pillars were the same pale marble quarried from the hills my grandfather had helped pacify. But the light was wrong. It came through the high windows gray and thick, like the air itself had been boiled, and it pooled around the throne where the king sat with his hands folded in his lap, patient as furniture.

He wasn't blinking. He hadn't blinked in months. The court had stopped noticing, which is the most frightening thing I can tell you about us: we adapted. We are a court; adaptation is our native language. A king who does not blink becomes, within weeks, simply a king who happens not to blink, and life goes on, and the tea gets made.

Behind him stood the queen regent's faction in a crescent—Lady Vess, Lord Calloway, the treasurer whose name I will not dignify by remembering. They watched me enter with the expressions of people observing weather. My arrest had been their work, or so I assumed. Treason, they'd call it. Correspondence with a fugitive taster. Conspiring to remove the crown from the royal head, which was, technically, true, if you accepted that the crown was a parasite and the head beneath it still belonged to a man.

"Chamberlain Wren." The voice came from the king's mouth but used it badly, the way a puppeteer uses a puppet whose strings he hasn't bothered to learn. Too smooth. No breath behind the words. "You have been busy."

I bowed. One learns to bow in utero, in this place. "Your Majesty is kind to notice."

"I notice everything now." The king's head tilted, an angle no living neck enjoys. "That is rather the point of me."

Around the room, the court shifted. Nobody met anybody else's eyes. I understood then what this was going to be—not a trial of law, because law requires two parties who agree on its authority, and the thing on the throne recognized nothing but appetite. This was going to be a confession trial. I had heard rumors from the eastern provinces: nobles brought before the god-king, made to speak their guilt aloud, and afterward they emerged gray-faced and obedient, hollowed like gourds. The god ate confession. That was its diet, along with ash and almond and whatever else we'd been feeding it for three hundred years without writing it down.

"You fed me," the king said. Not accusing. Almost gentle, which was worse. "Every day of your service. Every petition you buried, every clerk you silenced, every coin you diverted from the granaries to the wall fund. You brought them here, into this room, and laid them at my feet like offerings. Did you think I wouldn't taste them?"

"I thought," I said carefully, "that I was serving the crown."

"You were feeding it." Something moved behind the king's eyes, something vast and tired and old beyond the building it wore. "There is a difference, Chamberlain. There has always been a difference. Say what you did."

And here is the thing I have learned about confession, sitting through what followed: it cannot be performed halfway. You either open the door all the way or you stand in the frame looking foolish while the draft kills you.

So I told the truth.

Not the sanitized version. Not the version where a dutiful servant makes small mistakes in difficult circumstances. I told them about the winter of the grain riots, when I drafted the proclamation blaming hoarders in the lower city so the treasury's shortfall wouldn't reach the king's desk—and how the anger of four thousand hungry people had gone somewhere, had pooled in the throne room like water finding a cellar. I told them about the clerk, Master Ellery, whom I had quietly ruined for asking why the border ledgers didn't balance, and how Ellery's despair had tasted—I don't know how else to describe it—like something the crown drank. I told them about the petitions. Forty-one of them, from villages downstream of the tanneries, each one buried in a drawer I locked myself, because resolving them would have cost the crown money and me my position.

Forty-one villages. I had never once counted them until that moment, standing on the marble with my career and probably my neck in the balance, and the number came out of my mouth like a stone.

"The tanneries," the king repeated. "Yes. I remember those children. Their hands." He closed his eyes—which startled the whole court, because none of us had seen him do it in months—and when he opened them again there was something almost human in the look, some flicker of the man underneath, surfacing like a drowning swimmer. "Say their names, Wren. You must remember some."

I remembered three. Bracca. Ilset. A boy called Mouse because his real name had been lost even to him. I said them aloud, and the throne room went very quiet, and the god-king leaned forward on the throne as if toward warmth.

"Yes," it breathed. "Yes. That is what I want. That is all I have ever—" And then it stopped, caught itself, drew back behind the king's face like a tide retreating, and the moment passed, and Lady Vess stepped forward with her scroll of charges as if nothing had happened.

But something had happened. I could see it in the faces.

* * *

Here is what the queen regent's faction had not anticipated, and I confess I hadn't fully anticipated it either: a court is a conspiracy of silence, and silence is only strong while everyone keeps it together. Break one link and the whole chain rattles.

Lord Calloway spoke next, rising to denounce me—he had charges of his own to read, my correspondence with the fugitive, my theft of the freedom papers—and partway through his recitation he faltered. Because the god-king was looking at him. Not hungrily. Expectantly. The way a priest looks at a parishioner who has come to the rail with something heavy in his hands.

"And the mill levies," Calloway said, and stopped. Swallowed. "Your Majesty recalls the... the mill levies."

"I recall everything," the king said softly. "Do you?"

What could he say? What could any of us say? Calloway stood there for a long moment, a man in his sixties who had spent forty years being the second-most powerful person in any room, and then he did the bravest cowardly thing I have ever witnessed: he set down the scroll, folded his hands, and began to talk.

The mill levies. The orphanage funds redirected to his daughter's dowry. The physician he'd bribed to falsify a plague report so his wool shipments wouldn't be quarantined—a report that delayed treatment in three river towns. He talked for the better part of an hour, and the strange thing, the thing I keep returning to, is that he got steadier as he went. As if the words had been stones in a pack he'd carried so long he'd forgotten their weight, and setting them down one by one he could suddenly feel his own spine.

The god drank it in. I watched it happen—the king's posture straightening, color rising faintly in cheeks that had been waxen for a year—and I understood with cold clarity that we were feeding it, all of us, that this whole theater was a banquet table and we were lining up to serve ourselves.

But I also understood something else, something the faction hadn't grasped: the god wasn't growing stronger the way they feared. It was growing *quieter*. Less puppeteer, more penitent. When Calloway finished, the king looked at him for a long time and said, "Thank you," and the thank-you was the king's own voice, cracked and human, and Lady Vess's face went white as whey.

Because she understood it too. Confession wasn't breaking us. It was cracking the seal on all of us, and once one crack shows, the pressure goes everywhere.

* * *

By afternoon the throne room had become something no one had planned and no one could stop. A queue formed. An actual queue—clerks and minor lords and the mistress of robes and finally, astonishingly, the under-taster, a boy of nineteen named Perrin who had never confessed to anything larger than stealing honeyed figs, standing before the throne admitting that he'd watered the king's wine and lied about it for two years, and bursting into tears halfway through, and the god-king leaning down from the throne to say, gently, "Figs. Yes. I wondered who," and the whole court laughing—laughing!—in a room where laughter had been extinct for a year.

It was messy. Gods, it was messy. People confessed things that weren't crimes and skipped past things that were. Old grudges surfaced dressed as repentance; Lady Vess's own faction began turning on each other like eels in a bucket, each trying to confess smaller sins than the last. A woman fainted. Two barons nearly came to blows over whose negligence had killed more miners. The guards didn't know whether to arrest anyone, because everyone was guilty and everyone was speaking, and the law, poor exhausted thing, stood in the corner holding its hat, quite unable to get a word in.

This is justice outside the law, I thought, watching a laundress admit she'd been selling the palace linens to feed her sister's children while the treasurer confessed to embezzling the same linens' budget. It is ugly and uneven and it lets the guilty weep next to the petty, and it is still, somehow, the first honest thing this court has done in a century.

And through it all, the king blinked. Slowly, painfully, like a man remembering how, between confessions. Each admission seemed to return a little of him—a twitch of his own smile, a cough, a request for water in a voice that shook. The god was loosening. Or the god was being fed so well it could afford to relax its grip. I couldn't tell which, and neither, I think, could it.

Toward evening, when the queue had thinned and the candles were being lit, the king raised a hand and the room stilled.

"Wren," he said. His own voice now, mostly. Tired. "Come here."

I approached the throne. Up close he smelled of dust and candle smoke and, faintly, underneath everything, almond.

"She's coming back," he said. Just that. Just to me. "The taster. I can feel her at the edge of things, like weather. She carries something for me—or against me. I find I no longer know the difference, and I find..." He paused, and his hand, lying on the armrest, trembled like an old man's. "I find I'm glad."

"Your Majesty—"

"They'll try to stop her," he said, and his eyes flicked, just once, toward Lady Vess, who was whispering urgently with the captain of the guard near the doors. "Vess has sent riders. Three days ago. You understand what I'm telling you."

My stomach dropped through the marble floor and kept falling. "How long do I have?"

"To warn her?" The king smiled, and it was the saddest use of a face I have ever seen. "However long it takes you to get out of this room alive. Go quickly, Chamberlain. And Wren—" I turned at the door. "Tell her the god says: ask it what it wants. No one has ever asked."

I ran. In front of the entire court, past the guards, past Lady Vess's frozen fury, down the long corridor with my heart hammering out a treasonous rhythm—and behind me I heard the throne room doors boom shut, and heard, muffled through the oak, the sound of the court beginning to confess again, voice after voice after voice, like rain finally falling on ground that had forgotten water.

Three days ago, the riders left. I had maybe a day's lead, if the roads favored me.

They rarely favor anyone.
