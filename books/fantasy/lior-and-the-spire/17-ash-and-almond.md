## 17. Ash and Almond

There is a particular irony in the fact that the safest way into a fortress is through its kitchen, and I have spent my whole life being the proof of it.

No one guards against a taster. Guards guard against knives and arrows and men in dark cloaks with worse intentions. They do not guard against a woman in a plain grey dress carrying a covered tray, because a taster is furniture that eats. I had walked past four checkpoints, two captains of the guard who once knew my face, and the entire west corridor of the palace without so much as a second glance. Wren's forged papers hung in my pocket like a dead bird, unnecessary. The kitchen master had looked at me for exactly as long as it takes to decide whether a person is useful or in the way, found me useful, and put me on the feast roster between a boy who carved swans out of butter and a woman whose sole job was to keep the sugared violets from wilting.

The coronation anniversary feast. One year since the crown settled on his head and something else settled inside him. One year of dishes I'd tasted, of almond where there should have been none, of a king who didn't blink. And now the whole court would sit down to eat in his honor, two hundred nobles in silk, and every one of them convinced that the danger came from outside the walls.

I stood at the prep table rolling little parcels of quail into vine leaves, and I let myself be invisible, which after everything was harder than it sounds. Invisibility used to be my craft. Now it took effort, like holding a door shut against wind.

Because I was not invisible anymore. Not entirely. Somewhere in this palace, a god knew my name.

* * *

I had learned things in the weeks since the border. Things about tasting that no one teaches, because no one has ever needed to teach them. Out in the living world, where magic ran through the ground like water under a frozen lake, my tongue had woken up. I could taste intention now. A lie tasted of copper and cold cream. Fear tasted of tin. The cook who had salted tonight's venison sauce had been thinking about her daughter's wedding, and the sauce tasted faintly of rosewater and pride.

And the god—when it was near, when it moved through the court wearing the king's body like a borrowed coat—I could taste ash and almond. Always ash and almond. It clung to everything it touched. The king's wine goblet. The armrest of the throne. The confession trials had left their residue on the marble floor of the great hall, a flavor so thick I could taste it from the doorway: guilt, rendered down, sweetened with fear, swallowed by something patient and enormous.

Wren had gotten me the position. Wren, who had been freed from his cell by three guardsmen who owed him their careers and one chambermaid who owed him her silence, and who now moved through the palace like a ghost with excellent paperwork. We had met twice in the dark of the chapel undercroft, his voice low and fast, my hands steady around a cup of tea I never drank.

"The tincture," he'd said. "Are you certain it will work?"

"No," I'd said, and he'd flinched, and I'd almost laughed. "Certainty is for priests and poisoners, and I've only ever been paid for one of those."

He hadn't laughed either. Wren had stopped laughing somewhere around the interrogation cells, around the queen regent's faction moving through the court like a slow tide, around the knowledge that he had spent eleven years polishing the gears of a machine that ate people. He carried it now behind his eyes. I recognized it. It was the same look my mother must have worn, all those years ago, when she bound a god and paid for it with her own past.

The tincture sat in a vial against my sternum, tied there with linen string, where no one searches. Valley ash, steeped in rainwater caught at the shrine, thickened with honey until it looked like nothing more than a medicinal syrup. My mother's recipe. Her handwriting on the scrap of paper I kept folded in my sleeve: *It will not kill it. Nothing kills it. But it will make it bleed memory instead of taking it.*

*Make it bleed,* she'd written, and underneath, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the page: *and be ready to catch what falls.*

* * *

The hall filled the way a wound fills with blood—slowly, then all at once. Two hundred candles. Four long tables. The king's dais raised on three steps, and the king seated above the salt, wearing his crown and his face and his stillness.

He did not eat. He did not blink. He watched the court the way a spider watches a web it has already finished spinning, and when the first course went out—the quail, my quail, tasted and approved by me personally—he lifted one hand and the whole room hushed like a held breath.

"Tonight," he said, and his voice was the king's voice but the cadence was wrong, too even, too old, "we give thanks."

Nobody asked thanks for what. That was the thing about the confession trials, the thing nobody said aloud: you did not ask questions anymore. You confessed, or you watched others confess, and you were grateful it wasn't you.

I stood at the sideboard with the other tasters, my back to the wall, my tray balanced on one hand. The next course was a soup—white wine and almond, ironically, because the court chef believed in tradition over sense—and I dipped the silver spoon, brought it to my mouth, and tasted.

Ash.

Not the soup. The soup was fine. But the air itself, the whole hall, tasted of ash and almond, thickening, gathering, the way a storm tastes before it breaks. The god was hungry tonight. Anniversary feasts were, I suspected, its favorite meal: two hundred people drowning in their own small guilts, all of it seasoned with ceremony, all of it offered up without a single sword being drawn.

The king's gaze swept the room. Passed over me.

Stopped.

Came back.

For one full second—one second that lasted a year—the god in the king looked directly at the invisible woman by the sideboard, and I felt it taste me back. Felt it find the shape of me: taster, daughter, witness-in-waiting. Its attention was a physical weight, like a hand pressing down on the crown of my head.

*You came back,* it said, not in words, in flavor. Ash and almond, poured directly onto my tongue. *You always come back.*

I bowed my head slightly, the way a servant does, and thought, very clearly: *Not yet.*

It smiled with the king's mouth—a terrible, gentle smile—and turned its attention to the court, and I breathed again, and my hands were shaking, and I made them stop.

* * *

The moment came at the third course, as these moments always do, in the middle of ordinary noise.

The cupbearers brought the anniversary toast: spiced wine, heated with iron pokers, poured from a single great gilded ewer into cups for the high table. The king's cup was carried up the steps by a boy of maybe twelve, walking careful as an egg-carrier, and set before the throne.

I moved. That was the whole plan, really—all of Wren's scheming, all of my mother's alchemy, reduced to a woman crossing a room with a tray. "Beg pardon," I murmured to the cupbearer, "the master taster wants the king's cup re-poured, there's sediment." Sediment. The oldest lie in service, and it worked because no one looks at a taster's hands.

I took the cup behind the screen of the sideboard. I tipped the vial against my sternum. Honey-thick tincture, gray as grave-water, into the wine—and then, because I am thorough, because I am a professional, I stirred it with my little finger and licked the finger clean.

It tasted of nothing. That was the horror of valley ash—it was the absence of flavor, the ghost of a place where magic had died, and it slid into the god's senses like a thief through an unlocked door.

I carried the cup back. Set it before the king. Stepped back three paces, into the shadow of a pillar, where invisible women live.

"Tonight," the king said again, rising, lifting the cup, "we give thanks."

Two hundred cups rose with him. Two hundred faces turned toward the dais, candlelit and eager and afraid.

He drank.

* * *

For three heartbeats, nothing happened. Long enough for doubt to bloom in my chest like frost. Then—

The king staggered.

It was small, at first. A man missing a stair. His free hand shot out and gripped the table's edge, and the gilded cup rang against the floor, and the sound of it went through the hall like a crack through ice.

"What—" someone began.

Then the king screamed.

It was not a human scream. It was the sound of something vast being pulled out of a hiding place it had occupied for centuries, something that had forgotten it had edges, discovering all of them at once. The candles guttered though there was no wind. The king's shadow peeled off the wall behind him—peeled, actually peeled, like wet paper—and stood up on its own, taller than the pillars, and every noble in that hall saw it, and every noble in that hall understood, in the wordless way crowds understand catastrophe, that they had been dining with a monster for a year and calling it majesty.

The god fought. Of course it fought. It reached for the court the way a drowning man reaches for wreckage—and this time, wounded, bleeding memory instead of hoarding it, it dragged everyone down with it.

The hall shuddered. And then the memories came.

I felt mine arrive like a wave breaking over me—but mine were only mine, a taster's lifetime of small fears and smaller triumphs, and I had trained for this, I had drowned in worse at my mother's knee. So I stayed standing while duchesses collapsed weeping into their plates. While the captain of the guard fell to his knees and began, aloud, unasked, to confess to a hanging he'd ordered twenty years ago. While the queen regent's faction—three lords in matching silver—clawed at their own mouths as if to stop words already escaping. While the butter-swan boy in the kitchen doorway dropped his knife and remembered, publicly, helplessly, the day he'd let his brother drown.

The god was bleeding out. Everything it had ever swallowed, everything the kingdom had fed it for centuries—every sin, every cruelty, every convenient forgetting—came pouring back into the people who had poured it in. The court was drowning in its own returned guilt, and the sound it made was not screaming. It was weeping. Two hundred voices weeping in the candlelight, honest for the first time in living memory.

And the king—the king himself, the poor hollowed man beneath the god—slid down from the throne and lay on the dais steps, blinking. Blinking rapidly, desperately, like a man surfacing from deep water, tears streaming down a face that was finally, finally only his own.

I should have gone to him. Part of me wanted to.

But the god was not finished. Wounded things are most dangerous dying, and as it tore loose from the king's body—as it gathered itself above the hall, a shape of smoke and sorrow with too many edges—it turned and found me. Found the one person in the room still standing, still watching, still tasting.

*You,* it said, and the whole hall heard it, or dreamed they heard it, ash and almond flooding every tongue in the room. *You poisoned me. You witnessed me. You—*

"I know what I did," I said aloud, in front of two hundred witnesses, and my voice did not shake, because I had practiced this too. "And I'm not finished."

The smoke-shape descended toward me. The court watched. For the first time in my life, every eye in a crowded room was fixed on me, the invisible woman, and there was nowhere left to hide, and no reason to hide, and the terror of that was very nearly sweeter than the fear.

It reached for me—not to kill. To pour. To empty itself into the only vessel that had ever volunteered.

I opened my arms, and my mouth, and my memory, and said:

"Show me everything."

* * *

What happened next belongs to the next chapter, because I am writing this part with shaking hands and the ink keeps smearing, and some things should be told in order.

But I will say this much. The god came into me like winter coming into a house—all at once, through every window—and the last thing I saw before the memory took me was Wren pushing through the weeping court, shouting my name, my actual name, the one almost no one alive knew.

The first thing I tasted, in the dark behind my own eyes, was a binding circle drawn in blood and honey, and a young woman's voice saying the words my mother taught me, decades before I was born.

Her voice. Saying my name.

The god had been waiting all this time to show me how it began.
