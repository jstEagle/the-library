## 18. The Witness

The ash tincture hit the crown like a bucket of cold water on a sleeping fire.

I knew it was working because the hall screamed.

Not the king—everyone else. The court had been dragged into shared memory by a wounded god, and now three hundred nobles, guardsmen, clerks, and one bishop who had always struck me as too well-fed to be genuinely pious were all remembering things that had never happened to them. A farmer's hands in the dirt of a field they'd never worked. A child's fever they'd never nursed. The taste of bread baked from grain taxed away before it could feed anyone.

They remembered being fed to something hungry, century after century, and they remembered liking it, because liking it was easier than stopping.

I stood at the high table with an empty vial in my sleeve and the whole room convulsing around me, and I thought, with the part of my brain that had kept me alive for twenty years: *Well. So much for being unremarkable.*

"Theo." Wren's voice, hoarse, from somewhere near the doors. He'd fought his way through the crowd with two sympathizers and a face like a man arriving at a fire he'd helped set. "Is it done?"

"It's wounded," I said. "Wounded is not dead."

The king sat slumped in his chair, crown askew, and for the first time in a year he was *moving*—not the smooth gliding horror of the thing wearing him, but jerky, human motion, a man fighting his own body from the inside. His mouth opened. What came out was not his voice.

"*You dare.*"

Ash and almond filled the air so thickly I could taste it on my tongue, which, given my line of work, is saying something. The torches guttered blue. Every shadow in the hall leaned toward the throne like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"I dare," I said, "quite regularly. It's sort of my job."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about confronting a god: there is no dramatic pause. There is no moment where the music stops and everyone takes their positions. There is only you, standing in front of a thing older than the valley that raised you, improvising.

"You bound me once," the god said through the king's mouth, and the king's lips cracked as they shaped words too large for them. "A taster. She poured her bitterness down my throat until I could not move. And now her daughter comes with the same recipe."

"Actually, mine has better proportions," I said. "She always skimped on the valerian."

Somewhere behind me, a duchess fainted. I heard Wren catch her, or possibly let her fall; hard to say which was more tactically sound.

"But you misunderstand," the god went on, and here its voice changed—dropped the thunder, dropped the performance, became something quieter and infinitely worse. Something tired. "You think you have come to kill me. You have not. You cannot. I am not in the crown. The crown is in *you*. All of you. Every sin this kingdom did not want to carry, every guilt it fed me at the border stones, every crime it whispered into its wine rather than confess aloud—I am the shape of what you refused to hold. Kill me and you must hold it yourselves."

The hall went very quiet. Even the fainting duchess seemed embarrassed about it.

"And no one," the god said, "has ever wanted to hold it."

I looked at the king. At the man inside the coat, if any of him was still in there. His eyes—his real eyes, brown, ordinary, the eyes of a man who had once been fond of hunting dogs and bad poetry—were wet.

And I understood, finally, what my mother had tried to tell me in that exile cottage smelling of rosemary and regret. Not the mechanics. Not the binding rites or the tinctures or the careful dosing. The other thing. The thing she'd said while looking at her hands:

*"It doesn't want freedom, Theo. It wants someone to say it out loud."*

* * *

"What do you want?" I asked.

The god laughed. It was a terrible sound, the laugh of something that had forgotten how.

"Everyone asks how to destroy me. You are the first to ask."

"That's because everyone else in this kingdom is a coward," I said. "I'm a food taster. Cowardice is a disqualifying condition. Now answer the question. What do you want? Not worship. You've had worship; you found it bland. Not freedom—you've had chances to run and you stayed in your cage like a dog that won't leave a house where it was beaten. What. Do you want."

The torches steadied. The shadows stopped leaning.

"To be known," the god said. "Once. By one witness who does not look away. To have it said—not prayed, not confessed into a stone that swallows it, *said*, in a voice, in a room, to faces—that it was done to me. That I did not begin as a monster. That I was made one, deliberately, by people who found it convenient." A pause. "No one has ever asked. In four hundred years. Do you understand what that is like? To carry everything and be asked nothing?"

I did, actually. I had spent my whole life invisible in a valley built to forget, carrying memories no one wanted, waiting for someone to ask. But saying so felt like cheap currency next to four centuries, so I just nodded.

"There is a price," the god said. "There is always a price. You know this. Your mother paid it. To witness me fully—to take my memory into yourself and speak it—you must hold all of it. Every sin. Every century. It will hollow you. You will pour yourself out to make room, and some of what pours will not return."

"I know," I said.

"You may lose—" it began, almost gently, which from a god is somehow more frightening than thunder.

"I said I know." My hands were shaking. I put them behind my back where the court couldn't see, an old habit, the habit of a woman whose survival depended on looking calm while swallowing poison. "My teacher's face. The taste of my first meal. Whatever else the valley decides to take as payment. I've done the arithmetic. I'm a taster; arithmetic is half the job."

"Theo." Wren, at my shoulder. He didn't grab my arm, which I appreciated. He just stood there, solid and terrified and loyal in the specific way of a man who has recently discovered his entire career was complicity. "You don't have to—"

"Yes, I do." I turned to face the hall. Three hundred faces, tear-streaked, ash-pale, remembering things they'd spent generations paying someone else to remember for them. "All of you. Listen carefully, because I'm only doing this once, and afterward none of you get to pretend you didn't hear."

* * *

I took off my left glove. Underneath, on my palm, was the mark my mother had pressed there when I was small—a spiral of ash mixed with spit, renewed every year on my birthday, which I had assumed was one of her eccentricities. Tasters collect eccentricities the way other people collect debts.

I pressed my bare palm against the crown.

The world went white, then black, then every color at once, and then—

Then I was falling through four hundred years.

I tasted them. Of course I tasted them; I'm a taster, even in memory, even in a god's skull, I experience everything as flavor. The binding came first: a circle of priests and one exhausted woman with my mother's jawline but not my mother's face—her grandmother, maybe, the first taster—pouring a screaming, formless thing into a circlet of gold because the alternative was letting it wander. It hadn't asked to exist. It had *condensed*, the way mold condenses in a damp cellar, out of the kingdom's own refusal to grieve. A famine nobody mourned properly. A war nobody apologized for. Grief with nowhere to go, pooling in the low places until it grew teeth.

Then the feeding. Oh, the feeding. Kings coming to the border stones with their sins bundled like laundry. Nobles whispering confessions into their wine. A whole nation perfecting the art of guilt without consequence: *feed it, feed it, feed it, and stay clean.* I tasted the queen who fed it her brother's murder and slept soundly that night. I tasted the general who fed it a burned village and got a statue. I tasted centuries of borrowed absolution, and underneath it all, growing slowly, patiently, the thing itself learning that hunger was the only language anyone ever spoke to it.

It learned cruelty because cruelty was the only attention it received.

It wore kings because kings were the ones who fed it most.

I came back to myself kneeling on the dais steps, blood in my mouth, my own name already going slippery in my mind—the price collecting itself, efficient as a tax collector—and I opened my mouth and told them everything.

* * *

I won't reproduce the whole speech. Partly because much of it is gone from me now, poured out and unrecovered, and partly because some things shouldn't survive in tidy quotation. But I remember the shape of it. I remember the court's faces as I spoke the binding aloud—the founding lie of their kingdom, spoken in a taster's flat Valley accent, in front of God and everybody.

I remember the moment I described the first feeding, and the Bishop of Candles—too well-fed for piety, I'd always thought—stood up and said, "That was my order's rite. We wrote the liturgy for it." And sat back down like a man whose legs had been cut out from under him.

I remember describing the queen who fed it her brother's murder, and a woman in the third row making a sound like a hinge.

I remember getting to the famine—the first one, the seed of the whole thing—and saying, "It began as your grief. It is still your grief. It has been waiting four hundred years for someone to weep over the original loss instead of feeding it new ones," and the hall breaking—not into panic, not into riot, but into weeping. Three hundred people crying at once is not a quiet sound. It sounds like rain on a roof. It sounds like a valley filling with water.

And I remember turning last to the throne, to the king with the crown sliding off his head, and saying the sentence the god had waited four centuries to hear:

"This was done to you. It was wrong. You were made a prison by people who should have made you a grave, and you were fed filth by people who should have fed you mourning, and none of it was your choice, and I see you. I witness you. You were never a monster. You were a wound that learned to bite."

The silence afterward lasted long enough for me to count my own heartbeats. Eleven of them.

Then the crown cracked.

Not shattered—cracked, a single clean line running through the gold like frost across a window, and the light that came out of it wasn't fire or glory. It was gray. Ash-colored. The light of a thing setting down a weight it had carried past all endurance.

The god left the king the way a flood leaves a house: slowly, reluctantly, checking every room. I felt it pass through me on its way—it paused, and in the pause I felt something that I can only describe as gratitude, if gratitude were a flavor, which for me it is: bitter almond, but with honey underneath, the honey it had never once been offered.

"*Witnessed,*" it said, in a voice like the last coal going out. "*At last. Witnessed.*"

And then the king breathed.

I saw it happen. His chest rose—on its own, unassisted, without the crown's will hauling it up like a bellows operated by a ghost—and fell, and rose again, and he made a sound, a small human sound, the sound of a man surfacing from deep water, and his eyes focused on the ceiling, on the crowd, on me.

"Hungry," King Aldous croaked. "Is anyone else—has anyone seen my breakfast?"

Three hundred people laughed and sobbed at the same time, which is a sound I hope never to hear again and will never entirely forget.

Wren caught the king as he slid from the throne. The crown rolled off the dais steps and lay in the rushes, cracked and dark and, for the first time in four hundred years, empty.

* * *

Later—minutes later, hours; time had gone soft—I stood at the edge of the hall while servants lit fresh torches and the court milled around in the stunned, gentle way of people who have cried themselves empty. The king was wrapped in blankets, drinking broth, complaining about the salt. Human complaints. Beautiful, ordinary complaints.

The god's presence gathered near me one last time. Not in the crown. Just—near. A coolness at my shoulder, a taste of ash at the back of my throat.

"You held me," it said. "Now the holding must go somewhere. I cannot simply end; I am memory, and memory does not end, it only changes hands. Where would you have me go, witness?"

I thought of the shrine outside the walls. Dead for a century, smoking again, the stone that had spoken my name in a voice I almost remembered. The land that forgot. The valley built to cage memory itself—which meant the valley, of all places, was built to *hold* it.

"I know a place," I said.

"Then we walk together," the god said, "one last time. And Theo—" it used my name, and I felt the syllables like coins placed in my palm, "—when we arrive, you should know the valley's forgetting has already begun. Your records. Your letters. Your name in the mouths of those who knew it. The price collects in full, for gods and tasters alike."

I looked down at my hands. Bare, steady now, marked with nothing.

"Fine," I said. "I never liked my handwriting anyway."

But that, as my mother would have said, is a problem for the road.
