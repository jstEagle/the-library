## 19. The Price of Survival

The god left the king the way a fever breaks: all at once, in the small hours, with sweat and shivering and a silence so complete I could hear my own heart arguing with itself.

I was still holding his hand. I had been holding it for what felt like a year, which, given everything, was not far wrong. His fingers were warm now—actually warm, not the oven-warmth of something being cooked from inside—and they curled around mine with the weak, bewildered grip of a man waking from a long illness into a room full of strangers.

He blinked.

I want to be clear about what that meant to me, because I had spent months learning to read that face the way I read a dish. For most of a year, the king had not blinked. Not once. His eyes had sat in his skull like polished stones set into a door, and behind them something had watched us all with the patience of a thing that had learned, over centuries, that patience was the only currency it would ever be paid in.

Now his eyelids came down, slow and gritty, and came back up, and he looked at me with eyes that were only eyes—red-rimmed, frightened, human.

"Water," he croaked. And then, because he was still, underneath everything, a king: "And someone should tell the chamberlain I am sorry about the third chair."

* * *

The god did not leave empty-handed. That is not how gods work, or how debts work, or how anything works, if I have learned one thing worth writing down.

It stood in the corner of the sickroom—not standing, exactly; it had no legs, but language insists on giving bodies to things, and after carrying its memories for even an hour I understood why it resented that. It was a shape like heat-shimmer over a road, like the place where ash rises off a fire and stops being fire and starts being weather.

"You could keep me," it said. Its voice was almond and cold smoke. "A little longer. A year. Ten. You held me for a night and did not drown."

"No," I said.

"You liked it." The shimmer rippled, which I had come to understand was its version of a smile, and its smiles were the worst thing about it. "The tasting. All those centuries, poured into you like wine down a throat. You are built for it, taster's daughter. Your mother was. You are the finest vessel this kingdom has produced in three hundred years, and vessels are wasted on water."

"I'm not a vessel," I said. "I'm a woman who eats poison for a living. There's a difference, and the difference is that I choose the dose."

The shimmer went still. Somewhere below us, in the feast hall where I had poisoned a crown instead of a king, the court was still weeping and confessing and holding each other up, and the sound came up through the floor like rain.

"What do you want?" I asked it. We had done this dance before, in dreams, but dreams were its country and I wanted to hear the answer here, in a room that smelled of candle-tallow and sick-sweat and the king's humanity.

"To leave," it said. "As agreed. As witnessed." Something moved through it, slow as sediment settling. "But memory does not vanish, taster. It has never once vanished in the whole history of the world. It only goes somewhere else. When your valley forgot, it forgot *into* me. When I go, I must pour out. The question—the only question—is where."

"Give it to the land," I said, because I had been turning this over since the moment I spoke its history aloud and felt it lift out of me like a hook leaving flesh. "The shrine on the hill. The old stone. It smoked before any of this started. It's been waiting a century for something to remember. Let the land hold it. Land is patient. Land doesn't wear memory like a coat and start eating the neighbors."

"The land will change," the god said. "Ground remembers differently than people do. Colder. Slower. The grass will grow strange there. Birds will not land. Travelers will feel watched by nothing." It paused. "You find this acceptable?"

"I find it better than the alternative," I said. "Which was you."

Another ripple. Almost, almost, a laugh. "Your mother said something similar. Word for word, nearly. She lacked your poetry."

"My mother," I said carefully, "lacked a great many things. Including me."

"Yes," the god said, and there was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. "That was the price she paid. You should know yours before you carry me down the hill."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about carrying a god's memory: it weighs nothing and it weighs everything. It has no mass. It has only *insistence*. For two days I walked through the palace with four hundred years of borrowed guilt folded up inside me like a letter I could not open, and every hour some piece of it pressed against the seal.

I tasted things I had no right to taste. The first sin ever fed to it—a chamberlain, eight generations back, who drowned a rival's dog and could not sleep afterward until he confessed it to the crown, and the crown took it, and the taking felt so good that the court made a habit of it. The famine year when the granary masters starved three villages to raise prices and fed their guilt to the god in sealed ledgers. The queen regent's father, feeding it the name of every person he had ruined, one spoonful at a time, growing lighter and crueler with each unburdening.

And under all of it, older than the kingdom, the original crime: the binding itself. Nine priests and one taster, kneeling around a thing that had never asked to exist, sewing it into a crown because someone had to hold the kingdom's sins and no one wanted the job to rotate.

No one had ever asked it. That was the whole horror of it, distilled. Four hundred years of service rendered without consent, and when I finally gave it the only thing it wanted—an apology, spoken aloud, witnessed—it had loosened like a fist remembering it was a hand.

So yes. I pitied it. I want that on the record, in whatever book survives me. I pitied the monster, because the monster was made, and I know something about being made into a tool.

Wren met me at the valley gate on the morning I carried it home.

"You don't have to do this part alone," he said. He looked terrible. He had been arrested, tried, freed, and promoted within the space of a fortnight, and it showed in his face the way weather shows on a cliff.

"It's not the kind of thing you can help with," I said. "Unless you'd like to hold four hundred years of other people's sins while I walk. It's very portable. Like a basket."

"I'll walk with you anyway. I'll just be useless at a respectful distance."

"That," I said, "is the most honest offer anyone has made me all year."

He walked with me. Useless, respectful, half a mile of ash-grey road between the gate and the hill where the shrine stood smoking gently against a sky the color of old tin. The valley received us the way it received everything: flatly, forgettably. I had grown up inside that flatness and called it safety. Walking back into it now, with a god's memory pressing at my ribs, I felt it the way you feel a draft—something trying to get in.

The shrine was smaller than I remembered, which is a lie, because I had never actually remembered it; I had only heard about it, the way everyone in the valley had heard about it, dead stone, dead god, a century of nothing. But it was smoking. Thin grey threads rising off the capstone into air that had forgotten how to carry prayers, and when I put my hand on the stone it was warm as a sleeping animal.

"Hello," I said to it. I don't know why. It seemed polite.

Then I opened the letter.

I will not describe what it felt like to pour four hundred years into a rock. Not because it was unspeakable—I have become, against all my early training, a woman who speaks things—but because description would make it sound bearable, and it was not bearable, and I refuse to launder it. It was like being flayed inside out and having the flaying used to dress the stone. It was like every meal I had ever tasted coming back up at once, seasoned with everyone else's shame.

It took less time than a confession trial. That is the arithmetic of gods: centuries to build, minutes to spend.

When it was done, the shrine was smoking properly—thick, white, fragrant, the way a shrine ought to smoke—and the ground around it hummed with a low, deep note I felt in my teeth. The god was gone. Truly gone, thinned out into earth and stone and the slow cold memory of land, and the last thing I felt of it, as it dispersed, was something I have decided to call gratitude, though it may simply have been relief wearing gratitude's coat.

I sat down on the hillside. Wren sat down next to me. Below us, the Valley of Ash lay in its bowl of grey light, and I thought: *it's over*, and I thought: *now I can rest*, and I thought, with the specific clarity of a woman who has not yet checked her pockets:

*I'll go home and reread my mother's letters.*

* * *

Her letters were gone.

Not burned. Not stolen. *Gone blank.* I stood in my narrow room above the kitchens holding six sheets of paper that were paper in every particular except meaning—cream-colored, soft-handled, faintly smelling of the lavender she had always tucked into her envelopes—and there was nothing written on them. Not faded. Blank, the way a page is blank before anyone writes on it, except that the creases from her folding were still there, and the ghost of a seal, and the shape of a hand that had once pressed ink into these fibers with love and instructions and a warning I could no longer read.

I turned them over twice. I held them to the light. I did the stupid human things you do when the impossible arrives dressed as an inconvenience.

Then I went to the steward's office, because my mother taught me—somewhere, in some letter, in some sentence I could no longer retrieve—that when the world goes wrong you check the records, because records are where institutions keep their souls.

"Tamsin Montrose," I told the clerk. "Royal food-taster. Fifteen years' service."

He looked at me with the pleasant blankness of a man being asked to recall a dream. "There's no taster by that name on the rolls, mistress. Are you certain you've got the right valley?"

I described myself. He listened politely. I described my own quarters, my own salary, the specific crime of almond-in-the-soup that had started everything, and somewhere in the middle of it his face began doing the thing faces do when a stranger stands too close: courteous, wary, sliding toward the door.

The valley had forgotten me.

Not the way people forget—gradually, with grief. The way the valley forgot *everything*: completely, structurally, as though a line had been drawn through the ledger and my name was on the wrong side of it. The valley was built to cage memory. I had fed it a god's worth of the stuff, and the cage had balanced its books the only way it knew how. Something had to be unwritten. The god went into the stone. I went into the blank.

My rooms existed. My wages had been paid to a name no one could match to a face. The kitchen staff smiled at me with the vague kindness reserved for travelers. And my mother—

My mother's letters were blank, but my mother herself lived in exile beyond the hills, outside the valley's reach, in a house I could still find because I had found it once already, on foot, with my own feet, and the road out of the valley apparently kept its own records.

She would look at me and know me. That much the forgetting could not touch: I was known elsewhere. I had a witness. Her name was not Tamsin Montrose either, anymore—Tamsin Montrose was a clerk's shrug, a blank line, a wage paid to nobody—but I was still the woman who had stood in a feast hall and spoken a god's history aloud to a weeping court, and courts, whatever their sins, remember.

Hollow. That was the word. Triumph should have weight; this had none. I had saved a king, unbound a god, freed a kingdom from its oldest machinery, and the reward was to be erased by the place that raised me, quietly, bureaucratically, without malice. The valley hadn't punished me. It had merely *balanced*.

I packed what mattered, which was almost nothing: a knife, a spare dress, six blank pages that had been my mother's voice, and one new sheet of good paper on which, before I lost my nerve, I wrote in my own hand, pressing hard enough to emboss:

*I was the taster. My name was Theo Dravenmoor. I carried a god down a hill and gave it to the stone, and the stone remembers, and so will I.*

The ink sat on the page like a small defiant fire.

Below the window, the shrine on the hill smoked white and steady, and the land around it hummed its low cold note, and I understood that somewhere in that humming was every secret this kingdom had ever fed away—and that the land, unlike kings, unlike clerks, unlike valleys, does not forget so much as it waits.

I had given the past to the ground. Now the ground was going to want to talk.

And far off beyond the hills, in a house that smelled of lavender and exile, a woman who had paid this price before me was waiting to see whether her daughter had inherited the memory—or only the bill.
