## 20. A King Who Blinks

The first time I saw King Aldous blink, I wept into my sleeve like a scullery maid at a wedding.

I want it noted that I did this privately, in the corridor outside the royal bedchamber, where only two footmen and a very judgmental cat could witness it. The footmen had the decency to look away. The cat did not. Cats never do. They are the one institution in this kingdom that has never learned to pretend.

But there it was: the king, propped against a mountain of pillows, grey as old porridge and twice as lumpy, and his eyelids—those eyelids that had stayed open for a year like painted shutters on a haunted house—came down. Once. Twice. A slow, wet, gloriously human blink.

"He does that now," said the physician, Master Corvel, who had survived his own interrogation by the queen regent's faction with nothing worse than a bruised reputation and a permanent tremor in his left hand. "Every few minutes. Like a man remembering how."

"Like a man learning how," I corrected.

Corvel shrugged, which for a physician is a full confession of uncertainty. "Either way, Chamberlain, I'd call it progress. A month ago he was a coat that someone was wearing. Now he's a man who owns a coat and can't quite get the sleeves on."

I have worked in this palace for twenty-two years, and I have learned that the difference between a courtier and a corpse is mostly a matter of phrasing. Corvel had just described His Majesty as a garment, and nobody so much as raised an eyebrow. There was a time—I can admit this now, since everyone saw everything anyway—when such a sentence would have been treason. Now it was simply Tuesday.

* * *

The court that saw everything has not recovered, and I am beginning to suspect it never will, and I am further beginning to suspect that this is the point.

Consider what we witnessed. At the anniversary feast—the feast at which a food-taster poisoned a crown rather than a king, which remains the single most elegant act of treason or loyalty ever committed in this palace, depending entirely on your accounting method—the god dragged us all into its memory. Not told us. *Showed* us. Every noble, every guard, every bishop and bootblack stood in that hall for what felt like a night and actually lasted eleven minutes, and inside those eleven minutes we lived centuries of the thing's suffering. We felt what it felt to be bound. We felt what it felt to be fed.

And here is the part that no edict can undo: we recognized the taste of the feeding. Because we had done it. All of us. The whole rotten apparatus of the crown had been using the god as a gutter for guilt for longer than any living memory. The queen regent's faction fed it their coups. The bishops fed it their heresies. The tax collectors fed it their arithmetic of starvation. And the king—poor Aldous, who could not even blink—had been the vessel into which an entire kingdom poured the contents of its conscience so that it need never carry anything itself.

You cannot unsee that. Believe me, several members of the court have tried. Lord Haskett has taken to drinking before noon and referring to the whole affair as "the unpleasantness," as though it were a leaky roof. The Bishop of Vael has declared from the pulpit that the vision was "a demonic counterfeit," which would be more convincing if three hundred witnesses hadn't watched him, inside the vision, feed the god a decade's worth of suppressed tithing ledgers and sigh with relief.

No. The court saw everything, and everything is the one thing this palace was built never to see. We are an institution of curtains. And now every curtain in the house has burned, and we are all standing in the draft, blinking—which, I note with some satisfaction, is more than our king managed for a year.

* * *

Aldous received the court for the first time six weeks after the feast. He insisted on standing, though he gripped the arm of the throne like a man gripping a ship's rail in weather. He had lost perhaps two stone of himself to the god's tenancy; his clothes hung on him the way banners hang on a defeated tower. But his eyes were his own. That was the thing everyone noticed and nobody said. The eyes were his own, and they were frightened, and they were ashamed, and they were *looking*. At us. Individually. As though he were counting us, or apologizing to us, or both.

"My lords and ladies," he began, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he stopped, and he breathed, and he started again, and I thought: yes. This. This is what a king sounds like when he is not performing kingship but attempting it. It sounds terrible. It sounds human. Long may it continue.

"I owe you a confession," he said. "Not the kind that was extracted from you. The kind that is given."

The hall went so quiet I could hear Lord Haskett's wine sloshing as his hand shook.

"A thing wore me for a year," the king said. "Before that, a thing was fed through me for longer than I knew—longer than any of us knew, because not knowing was the entire design. You came to my table and you left your sins in the soup, so to speak, and I ate them without tasting, because I was not there to taste." He looked down at his own hands as if checking they still belonged to him. "I do not remember the year. I remember fragments. I remember being very cold and very crowded. I understand that in my name—or in the name wearing my name—people were tried, and broken, and consumed. I am told I presided. I am told I smiled."

He let that sit. Kings are taught never to let silence sit; silence is where the knives live. He let it sit anyway.

"I cannot undo it. I cannot even properly apologize for it, because the man who did those things was only half me, and the other half was something we made—a thing we starved and stuffed and used as a privy for our consciences, and then called monstrous for smelling bad." Someone near the front made a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob. "So I will not ask forgiveness today. Today I am going to abolish things."

And he did. I stood at his shoulder with the documents, because that is what chamberlains are for: we are the furniture of power, the desks upon which history signs its name.

He abolished the Office of the Royal Taster. He read the words slowly, and when he reached the clause dissolving the tasters' roster—all those invisible men and women dosed with poison until poison became breakfast—he paused and said, quietly, to the hall and possibly to no one: "We asked children to drink death so that we could dine safely, and we called it service, and we never once asked if it was right. It was not right." Somewhere in the crowd, I thought of Theo Dravenmoor, wherever she was, and hoped she heard it, and suspected she hadn't, and understood that she probably wouldn't have wanted to.

He abolished the Confession Trials. "Justice that feeds on the guilty," he said, "is only hunger with better manners." The gallery stirred at that; several of the nobles who had confessed voluntarily—and whose confessions had, unexpectedly, cracked the court open like an egg—nodded with the grim satisfaction of people proven right at great personal cost.

And then he founded something, which surprised everyone, including, I believe, him.

"There will be an archive," he said. "Public. Open to any subject of this crown who can walk through a door. In it will be kept the god's testimony—every memory it gave us, transcribed, translated, and displayed. The binding. The feeding. The centuries. Our sins, catalogued by victim." He looked up, and his voice steadied. "We spent a thousand years forgetting on purpose. We will spend the next thousand remembering on purpose. Let every child in this kingdom learn what we did, so that no one—not a king, not a bishop, not a well-meaning fool with a crown and good intentions—can ever build a gutter for guilt again and call it holy."

They called it the Archive of Witness, eventually. At the time, the hall simply sat in stunned silence, the way a patient sits when the surgeon says the operation went better than anyone dared hope and also here is the bill.

Lord Haskett was the first to find his feet. "Your Majesty," he said, with the delicacy of a man defusing himself, "surely some memories might be... curated. For stability."

"Curated," the king repeated, tasting the word the way a taster tastes soup. "Lord Haskett, I spent a year as a coat. Do you know what it is like to be worn? To have your mouth used?" He blinked—slowly, deliberately, letting the whole court watch him do it. "No. Nothing will be curated. Not even the parts about me. *Especially* not the parts about me."

* * *

Three days later, the Privy Council offered me the chancellorship.

I want to be clear about how extraordinary this was. Wren, son of a customs clerk, chamberlain by attrition and competence, offered the second-highest office in the land. They dressed it up, of course—"First Minister of Restoration," which is the sort of title governments invent when they want a job to sound like a poem. There would be a seal. There would be a residence. There would be, the Lord Treasurer hinted with the subtlety of a falling anvil, a considerable stipend.

I asked for a day to consider it, which was a lie; I had already considered it in the four seconds between the offer and my answer, and the answer was no, and the reason was standing on a hill outside the city walls, smoking gently.

Here is what I have learned about institutions, having served them faithfully for two decades and watched them nearly devour a god and a king in the same year: institutions do not learn humility. They can only be *fitted* for it, the way a coat is fitted—and I use that metaphor advisedly now, and flinch every time. An institution learns humility the way a river learns its banks: slowly, under pressure, and only where someone stands firm enough to be a bank. If I took the chancellorship, I would become very good at managing the kingdom's guilt. And managing guilt is precisely the trade that built the god in the first place. We did not feed it out of malice. We fed it out of convenience. Out of administration. Somebody, somewhere, centuries ago, looked at the problem of a kingdom's sins and thought, *there must be an office for this.*

There must not be an office for this. That is the entire lesson. There must not be an office for this, and I declined to become the office.

Instead I requested—begged, technically, though I framed it as a proposal with diagrams—an allocation of funds, masons, and cartloads of lime mortar sufficient to rebuild the shrine on the hill outside the walls. The dead shrine. The one that smoked back to life when prayers started being answered again, the one the king sealed in fear, the one where a taster touched the stone and heard her own name spoken by a voice she almost remembered. The shrine where, in the end, the god's released memories went to rest—not in a vessel, not in a king, not in a woman hollowed out to hold them, but in the land itself. Let the ground remember. Ground has better boundaries than people do.

The council thought I had gone soft in the head. Haskett said as much, behind his hand, insufficiently behind his hand. "A shrine, Wren? To *it*?"

"Not to it," I said. "Around it. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point. We don't worship what happened. We mark where it happened, and we say thank you that it's over, and we keep the door open so that whatever comes next doesn't have to be sealed in the dark to be endured." I gathered my papers. "Also, Your Lordship, the alternative to a shrine is a sealed ruin on a hill that smokes, and in my experience sealed ruins that smoke generate either pilgrims or panics, and I would rather manage pilgrims."

That got a laugh, even from Haskett. Laughter is the last refuge of men who have run out of arguments and dignity in equal measure.

The king approved the allocation himself. He signed the order with a hand that still trembled, and when he finished, he looked up at me and said, "You could have had the chancellorship, Wren. You'd have been good at it."

"That's why I turned it down, sire."

He laughed—actually laughed, a rusty sound like a gate opening after a long winter—and then he grew serious in the way he has grown serious lately, which is to say completely and without armor. "Wren. The woman. The taster. Dravenmoor." He turned the signet ring on his finger, around and around. "The records say she was never employed here. The rolls are blank. Her quarters are blank. Even the guards who escorted her out—" He stopped. Started again. "It's as if the valley forgot her. Is that what happened?"

"It's what she paid," I said.

"For us."

"For the king, I think. And for the truth. Those aren't always the same thing, but this once they overlapped." I hesitated. There are things a chamberlain knows and files away, and things a friend should say aloud, and I have been trying, these past months, to be more of the latter. "She wrote her name down somewhere, sire. Before she lost it. People like her always do. She'll be fine. She'll be more than fine. She'll be *exact*."

The king nodded slowly, and blinked, and I found, as I always did now, that I could not stop watching him do it. A king who blinks. A court that weeps. An archive where children will read the worst of us. These are small miracles, and I am old enough to know that small miracles are the only kind that hold.

* * *

The masons broke ground on the shrine at the end of the month, and I went up the hill to supervise, because if you want a thing built humbly you must stand over the builders with a diagram and a frown.

It was evening when I arrived, the light going amber over the valley, and the workmen had already laid the first course of the new foundation in a wide circle around the old smoke-stained stone, leaving it untouched at the center, as specified. I walked the perimeter, checking measurements, being pedantic, enjoying it immensely. Pedantry is prayer for men like me.

And that was when I saw her.

She was standing at the edge of the work site, in a plain travelling cloak gone grey with road dust, a satchel over one shoulder, looking at the foundation stones with an expression I recognized because I had seen it in mirrors for twenty years: the expression of a person checking whether a place remembers them, and bracing for either answer.

"Theo Dravenmoor," I said.

She turned. And I watched her search my face the way one searches a page for a familiar word, and I watched her fail to find it, and I understood—with a cold that started in my chest and spread outward—that the valley's forgetting had taken more than records. It had taken her memory of *me*. Of the bargain. Of the border. Perhaps of all of it.

"You're the chamberlain," she said carefully. "Aren't you? From the palace." A pause, and then, with the ghost of her old dryness surfacing like a drowned log: "I seem to have lost rather a lot of names lately. Including, I'm told, my own. So you'll forgive me if I ask yours."

"Wren," I said. "Chamberlain Wren. And I have excellent news, Mistress Dravenmoor, or whatever you're calling yourself these days." I gestured at the fresh-cut stone, the circle rising around the old stone, the smoke rising gentle and unhurried into the amber air. "We kept your receipt."

She frowned at me. Then at the shrine. Then, slowly, at the satchel slung across her body—the one I could see was heavy with books, handwritten books, the spines already soft with handling.

"Receipt," she repeated.

"For everything," I said. "Come and sit. It's a long story, and for once in my life, I'm the one holding all the pieces."
