## 22. Lior and the Spire

The girl found the books on a Tuesday, which is the sort of detail I would once have noted because details were how I stayed alive, and now I note it because old habits are the last thing to die in a person, outliving even the reasons for them.

I was not there when she found them. I want that understood from the start, because I have spent enough of my life being blamed for things I arranged only indirectly.

* * *

Let me tell you about the spire library, since no one else will, and since telling things has become, in my old age, something of a profession.

The spire itself predates everything—the valley, the forgetting, the crown, the god, all of it. It stands at the center of the capital like an old man at a wedding: tolerated, honored in theory, mostly ignored. For three hundred years its lower floors held the royal archives, and its upper floors held dust, and between those two populations there was very little commerce. After the king abolished the confession trials and founded the Archive of Testimony, they cleaned out four floors of the spire to house what the god remembered. What the god remembered turned out to fill eleven rooms and smell faintly of almond, which the archivists have learned to live with, the way one lives with a relative who snores.

My books did not go into the Archive of Testimony. I saw to that myself, in the way of old women who still climb stairs when told not to.

There are nine of them. Leather-bound, hand-sewn, written in ink I made myself from oak galls because if you are going to spend thirty years writing down your own life against the appetite of a forgetful valley, you may as well do it properly. They are hidden behind a false panel on the sixth floor, in a corner where the light comes through a cracked window at a slant around midday, and I chose that spot for two reasons. The first is that no archivist in the history of archiving has ever voluntarily visited the sixth floor. The second is sentimental, and I will get to it.

* * *

But I am getting ahead of myself, and a woman my age has earned the right to tell a story in order, or as near to order as her memory allows, which—given the subject matter—is a joke I am permitted to make exactly once per telling.

After I carried the god's memories out of the valley and gave them to the shrine, after the land drank them down and the valley forgot me so thoroughly that even my own handwriting looked like a stranger's, I did what any sensible woman does when she has been erased from the world's ledger. I got a job.

A kitchen, as it happened. A good one—not a palace kitchen, nothing with that particular flavor of fear in the stockpots, but a proper inn at the edge of the capital, the kind of place where carters eat and lie about their distances and nobody asks who you were before. I could taste intentions by then; the magic had come back into me like blood into a numb limb and never entirely left, though it faded over the years to something quieter, a sense rather than a sense. I could tell when a delivery man was going to short us on the flour. I could tell when a couple at table three was going to be engaged by dessert. I could tell, always, when someone was lying about being fine, which in an inn is most of the time, and I developed a reputation for bringing extra bread to people who needed it without being asked, and that reputation was worth more than any name would have been.

Because here is the thing about the valley's forgetting: it took my name, my records, my mother's letters, every official trace of Tamsin—whoever she had been before she became the woman writing this—but it could not take what I knew. Skill survives erasure. Hands remember. The valley forgot that I had been a taster, but my tongue did not, and a tongue, it turns out, is a kind of archive too.

So I worked. And in the evenings I wrote.

* * *

The first book begins, as the girl will discover, with five words:

*I was the taster.*

I chose them carefully. Not "my name was"—the valley had seen to it that I couldn't honestly complete that sentence. Not "once upon a time," because this is not a fairy tale, whatever the archivists' footnotes might wish. Five words, plain as a knife on a counter. Everything after them is proof.

Writing your own memory down by hand, against a valley that eats names, is a strange labor. Some days the ink seemed to dry wrong, the letters fading even as I watched, and I would write the passage again, pressing harder, until the paper dented and the words held. Some days I wrote things and then read them back and felt nothing—as though I were transcribing a stranger's diary—and those were the days I learned what the forgetting had already taken, and I mourned accordingly, and kept writing anyway. Grief, I have found, is mostly just the refusal to stop writing.

Thirty years. Nine books. The ninth is unfinished, and will remain so, and that is not a tragedy but a courtesy: every story should leave room for whoever picks up the pen next.

* * *

Now. About the girl.

Her name is Lior. Of course it is. You cannot hide a thing like that from an old woman who spent her life reading flavors.

She came to the spire two years ago, an apprentice archivist, fourteen years old, all elbows and opinions, assigned to the sixth floor precisely because she was new and the sixth floor was where enthusiasm went to be cured. She catalogued mildew for a month. And then, on a Tuesday, chasing a draft that shouldn't have existed—for the panel I installed has loosened these past years, as all my work eventually loosens, time being the one poison no one builds immunity to—she found the books.

I know this because I pay attention. Old women in kitchens hear everything. The apprentice who talked too much in the pie shop; the junior archivist who complained that someone had left "personal papers" in a restricted section; the rumor, moving through the city like warmth through bread, that there was a manuscript up in the spire written by the Taster herself—the one from the trials, the one whose testimony filled the Archive, except that the Archive's copy was unsigned, unattributed, erased like everything else, and here was a set of books that said otherwise, in a hand that matched nothing on record because there was no record, because the valley had eaten it, and yet—

They argued about it for a year, the scholars. Whether the books were genuine. Whether the Taster had been real or a convenient legend the court invented to close an embarrassing chapter. Wren—he is very old now, and walks with a stick he pretends not to need, and spends his days rebuilding the shrine stone by stone with hands that used to sign death warrants—Wren could have settled it in an afternoon. He read the first page once, when I let him, and closed his eyes, and said, "Only you would open with an accusation."

It wasn't an accusation. It was a job description. But I let him have it.

He never told the scholars the books were mine. Neither did I. Let them argue. Certainty is overrated; doubt keeps people reading.

* * *

This morning I climbed the spire.

Not all the way—an old woman knows her knees—but to the fourth-floor window that looks east, over the rooftops, to the hill where the shrine stands. It smokes gently today, the way it has smoked gently for thirty years. Pilgrims go up there now. Not many, and not for miracles—the god is gone, truly gone, its centuries of borrowed guilt poured into the ground and the ground having made something almost like peace of them—but to say thank you. That is all the shrine is now. A place to say thank you. People bring small offerings: a loaf, a button, a letter they never sent. The smoke takes them and gives back nothing, and somehow that is enough. We are a kingdom learning, slowly, clumsily, to be grateful without demanding change in return, and if we are slow at it, well. So was I.

The king is dead these six years, frail and human to the end, and he died blinking—I made a point of attending, and I watched, and he blinked, and I confess I wept at something as small as eyelids moving, because I alone in that hall knew what a miracle it was. Wren tends the shrine and refuses all titles. The queen regent's faction dissolved into the general embarrassment of history. The Archive of Testimony stands open to any citizen who wishes to read what was done in their name, and schoolchildren file through it looking bored, and that boredom is the healthiest sound in the kingdom, because horror that has become ordinary homework is horror that has been survived.

And Lior is up in the spire right now, on the sixth floor, in the slant of midday light, reading the first line of the first book.

I know because I arranged it. Indirectly. The loose panel was not entirely an accident of carpentry. An old woman is permitted her arrangements.

* * *

What will she make of it, I wonder? The girl with my old hunger for details, my old habit of watching doors?

She will read about almond in a soup that should have none. She will read about a king who stopped blinking, and a shadow half a second late, and a sealed shrine burning in the distance. She will read about a mother who bound a god and paid in memories, and a daughter who unbound it and paid in a name, and she will read—this is the part I rewrote eleven times—about why. Why carry it all down in ink? Why fight a valley's forgetting with nothing but a pen and stubbornness?

Here is what I finally wrote, and what I hope she reads twice:

*Forgetting is easy. It happens to you. Remembering is work. You have to choose it, daily, in ink, in speech, in the small stubborn act of saying: this happened, and I was there, and it mattered. The valley taught me that a name can be taken. The god taught me that a crime can be witnessed out of existence. But a story written by hand, hidden badly on purpose, waiting patiently in the dark for a curious child—*

Well. She'll see how it ends. I did leave room.

* * *

The light is going gold over the hill. The shrine smokes. Somewhere above me, on a floor that smells of dust and faintly of almond, a girl turns a page.

I was the taster. I was the witness. I was, for thirty years, the woman without a name who fed the city excellent bread and wrote her life down where the forgetting couldn't reach.

Tomorrow I will go back to the kitchen, because tomorrow there is soup, and soup wants tasting, and some offices, once held, are never truly abolished.

But tonight—tonight I will sit by my window with a cup of wine that tastes of nothing but wine, which is its own kind of miracle, and I will watch the smoke rise off the hill, thin and gray and gentle, carrying other people's thank-yous up into the dark.

And if somewhere in the spire a young voice reads five plain words aloud to an empty room—

*I was the taster*—

then let the valley eat my name. It never stood a chance against the story.
