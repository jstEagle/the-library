## 3. A Cartographer Without His Country

Lior Aldous had spent three years learning to be no one, and the first lesson of being no one was this: never look twice at anything.

He broke that rule on the morning the woman crossed the high pass below his blind.

She came up out of the mist like a figure inked onto wet paper—small, hooded, moving with the particular economy of someone who had learned early that attention was a tax and she refused to pay it. She kept to the windward edge of the trail where the scree would swallow her footfalls. She paused at the cairn where pilgrims left offerings, and she did not pray; she checked it, the way a thief checks a lock, for anything worth lifting.

Ordinary enough. The pass was threaded with ordinary people: smugglers, deserters, widows carrying letters they could not afford to send. Lior had watched them all from this shelf of granite above the trail, wrapped in a shepherd's cloak gone grey as the stone itself, and he had never once come down.

But the woman carried a satchel slung against her spine in a way that made it sit wrong, too carefully, too flat against the small of her back—the way one carries paper one cannot bear to crease. And when she stopped to drink at the snowmelt seep, she unslung it with both hands, and for half a breath the wind pulled her hood back, and he saw what she held against her knee while she drank.

Not paper. Skin. A chart drawn on something pale and taut, veined like a leaf, and across its surface ran lines he knew the way a man knows his own handwriting in a stranger's letter.

Lior's hands began to shake before his mind caught up to why.

He knew those contour strokes—the double-hatched ridgelines that rose like hackles along the northern margin. He knew the compass rose with its eight points, each one tipped with a thorn instead of an arrowhead, because he had invented that flourish himself, three years ago, at a desk in the Mapwright's Hall with candlewax dripping onto his wrist and something whispering through the floor beneath his feet. He knew the marginalia in its cramped cipher because he had *invented* the cipher, stroke by stroke, so that only he could read what he had been made to record.

The map of the king's body. The map he had burned—or believed he had burned—in the grate of his own chamber the night he fled the capital, feeding three years of work to the flames page by page while the thorn-shadowed guards hammered at his door.

Someone had saved it. Or someone had redrawn it. And either possibility meant the court knew he lived, or worse—that the thing wearing the crown wanted its portrait back.

*Never look twice.* He looked three times. Then he did the stupidest thing he had done since escaping Thornfell's shadow: he climbed down after her.

* * *

She heard him before he meant her to—he should have known she would—and by the time he rounded the shoulder of the trail she stood waiting for him with a knife held low and easy, the map already vanished into her coat, her face arranged into the bland courtesy of a merchant's wife surprised on the road.

"You're lost," she said. Not a question. A verdict.

"I'm looking for you," Lior said, "which is worse."

Something flickered behind her eyes—not fear exactly. Recalculation. He recognized it because he'd worn the same expression for three years whenever anyone looked at him longer than politeness required: the rapid arithmetic of a person deciding how much of themselves to spend on a stranger.

"Everyone's looking for someone on this road," she said. "I'm not her."

"The map you're carrying is drawn in my hand."

The knife didn't move. But her weight shifted, a quarter-inch onto her back foot, and Lior understood he had said the one true thing in the world that could stop her from simply walking away.

"Prove it," she said.

So he proved it the only way a cartographer can: he talked. "The contours are double-hatched because single hatching reads poorly by candlelight, and whoever commissioned it insisted I work at night. The scale bar is measured in paces rather than leagues because the distances were interior—corridors, chambers, the long gallery between the king's bedchamber and the council room. And the cipher in the margins—" he tapped his own temple— "is mine. Every character of it. I built it from the old surveyor's shorthand and then broke it deliberately, so that even if the court seized my papers, they'd hold nothing but pretty nonsense." He paused. "It charts a body. Not roads. A man, seen from inside."

For a long moment there was only the wind worrying the scree and somewhere below them a hawk riding the thermals with its wings fixed.

Then the woman said, quietly, "You're Lior Aldous."

"And you," Lior said, "are the reason I'm still alive to be recognized. Because whoever has that map now knows the hand that drew it, and the hand that drew it is supposed to be dead."

"I don't know you."

"No. But you stole from someone who does, or bought from someone who did, and either way the map found you, which means it *wanted* to be found by someone like you." He took a breath. It tasted of cold stone and pine resin. "I've been watching this pass for two days waiting for you to walk up it. I'll ask once. What do you know about King Aldric?"

Her laugh was short and entirely without humor. "That he's mad. That the Thornsworn smile too much. That the price of bread doubled the winter the queen-regent took the regency and nobody rioted, which frightened me more than any riot would have." She tilted her head. "You want me to say something else. Something about vines."

"Do you know about vines?"

"I know a dying man bled out at my fire two nights ago and told me *the crown is not the man*, and then gave me a chart of a human body drawn in thorns." Her knuckles whitened on the knife's grip. "And I know that ever since I took it, I've dreamed of roots. So either you tell me what I'm carrying, cartographer, or you get off my mountain."

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow beneath a leaning granite slab, a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand, and Lior told her everything, or nearly everything, because he had learned in three years of silence that some truths go rancid if you keep them past their season.

"I was the court's mapwright," he said. "Not of countries. Of the king."

Mirabel—she had given him the name the way one tosses a coin to a beggar, without looking at it—fed a twig to the fire. "Map a man?"

"Map what was happening *to* him. It began as medicine. The king's headaches. His lapses. Hours he couldn't account for. The physicians couldn't find the ailment, so the queen-regent—then just the queen—commissioned a new kind of chart. Interior geography. I was to render what the king's mind looked like from within." He turned his hands palm-up, studied the burn scars along his fingers from years of hot wax and hot iron. "I thought it would be like mapping a city. Streets and squares, districts of memory. I was arrogant. I was also very good, and they knew it, and they chose me for the same reason anyone chooses a good tool."

"And what did you find?"

"A garden." The word came out of him flat and cold. "Where a mind should have been—where *my* charts showed rivers of association and bridges of habit, the ordinary architecture of a thinking man—there was growth. White-rooted, flowering, patient. It grew fastest in the places where his memories were densest. And every time I surveyed, the garden was larger, and the king was smaller, and everyone in the palace smiled more."

He watched Mirabel's face in the firelight. She was very still, the way animals are still, the way liars are still when a truth comes near them.

"It fed on something," he went on. "I mapped that too, before I understood what I was recording. It thickened wherever he surrendered something he loved to something he owed. Every duty performed over love, every affection renounced for the crown—the vine put down another root. He gave it his grief when his father died, because kings aren't permitted grief. He gave it a name—I think a daughter's name, though the records were sealed—because kings aren't permitted daughters they keep. Twenty years of buried love, watered and tended, until the garden was the king and the king was the garden."

"And the queen-regent?"

"The vine grew into her willingly. That's the horror of it. It doesn't conquer. It *invites*. It offers relief—from doubt, from wanting, from the terrible weight of choosing. You give it your love and it gives you certainty." He laughed, and hated the sound. "Half the court volunteered. They call them the Thornsworn now. They smile because smiling is all that's left of them."

Mirabel was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed—stripped of its merchant's-wife polish, down to something rawer. "The courier. The man who died at my fire. He was cut open, but not robbed. Someone killed him for the map, and failed."

"The Thornsworn hunt it. The vine wants its portrait back—it knows I charted its architecture, and architecture can be attacked." Lior leaned forward. "Which brings us to the part you won't like. When I fled, I escaped with one thing intact: the memory of *how*. How the cipher works. How the interior surveys were taken. Half-erased—they burned most of what I knew out of me as punishment, or tried to—but the method survived, because they needed me functional long enough to finish the final plates." He met her eyes. "I am the only living person who can read that map completely. And you are the only person I've met in three years who might be able to do what needs doing at the end of it."

"Which is?"

"Reach the root. Under the throne room. And cut it."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, a stone came loose and clattered down through the dark, and both of them flinched toward the sound, and neither of them reached for a weapon, and Lior noted that too—two people so used to being hunted that surprise had worn grooves in them.

"You're disgraced," Mirabel said at last. "Run. Hunted. Why would the map choose *me*? Thieves' roads, smugglers' fires—what does a royal chart want with a memory-thief?"

"Because I've been thinking about that since I saw you drinking at the seep." He hesitated. This was the part he had rehearsed and abandoned a dozen times. "The vine grows in minds. Ordinary steel can't touch it—you'd need to reach into the place where it roots, and no blade I know of reaches there. But you do. Your trade. You take things out of minds. Things that shouldn't be liftable. Things that cling." He spread his hands. "I don't know what a memory-thief could steal from a plant. But I know the map chose you, and maps don't lie. They only wait."

Mirabel stared into the fire for a long moment, and Lior watched her decide—watched the arithmetic run behind her eyes, cost weighed against cost, and saw the moment the sum came out wrong and she accepted it anyway.

"One bargain," she said. "One condition. You don't ask what I've stolen. Not names, not faces, not whose. Whatever's in my head stays mine."

"Agreed."

"And you don't lie to me. Not about the map, not about the vine, not about what we're walking into. I've been lied to by experts, cartographer. I can tell."

"So can I," Lior said. "That's why I trust you. Liars recognize each other's craft. Yours is faces. Mine was maps. Both of us spent years making people believe in things that weren't there." He almost smiled. "At least mine got people *lost* honestly."

Something that was almost a smile touched her mouth in return and was gone, quick as a fish rising.

They shook on it—her grip dry and strong, his scarred fingers careful around hers—and Lior felt the strange vertigo of a man who has spent three years alone suddenly holding the proof that the world still contained other people, dangerous ones, ones who might betray him, and finding that preferable to safety.

Mirabel banked the fire and settled against the granite, the map flat against her chest beneath her coat, and within minutes her breathing slowed into sleep. She slept like a soldier, he noticed. Light and ready.

Lior did not sleep. He sat watching the coals and thinking about the cipher, and about the last plate he had never finished—the one showing the root, deep beneath the throne room, where his surveys had ended in a blank space he had labeled, in his own secret hand, with a single word:

*Heart.*

And he thought about the half-erasure, the burned-out corridors of his own memory, and the question he had never dared ask in three years of running: *what did they take from me, and did I leave it behind on purpose?*

Above the pass, the stars wheeled. Below, far to the south, the lights of the capital glowed faintly against the mountainside like a bed of embers that refused to die.

Toward those lights, come morning, they would walk.

And Lior Aldous, cartographer without a country, liar without a lie left worth telling, finally closed his eyes—and dreamed, for the first time in three years, of a garden.
