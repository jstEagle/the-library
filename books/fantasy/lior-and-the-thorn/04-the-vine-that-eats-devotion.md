## 4. The Vine That Eats Devotion

The shrine had been a place of worship once, before the mountain decided it preferred silence. Lior found it half-swallowed by the slope, its roof caved to a ribcage of blackened beams, its altar furred with moss the color of old bruises. Someone had loved this place enough to carve prayers into the doorframe. The thorns had grown through the carvings and split them apart, so that devotion and ruin shared the same grain of wood.

He built the fire small. Small fires were a habit of men who had spent years learning not to be seen.

Mirabel sat across from him with her boots off, drying her stockings over the flames, and watched him with the particular patience of someone waiting for a debt to come due. She had been watching like that since the pass. Since he'd put his hand on her map—*his* map—and said *I drew this*, and seen something shift behind her eyes: not trust, exactly. Recognition. One craftsman reading another's signature.

"You said you'd explain," she said. "On the pass. You said you'd tell me what I'm carrying."

"I did."

"Then talk, cartographer. My feet hurt, my hands still smell of that courier's blood, and I don't sleep well next to secrets."

Lior fed a twist of bark to the fire and let it catch. Where to begin? He had rehearsed this speech for three years, in cells and ditches and the long gray country of his own erasure, and every rehearsal had begun somewhere different. He began where the truth began.

"The king is dying," he said. "That part everyone knows. What no one says aloud is what he's dying *of*. Not age. Not poison. A vine."

Mirabel's stocking-stilled hands went quiet. "Thornfell is full of vines."

"Not like this one." He drew a breath. "Twenty-two years ago I was apprenticed to the Office of Royal Cartography. I was good. Good enough that they gave me work no one else wanted—the king's private commissions. Surveys of places that didn't exist on any chart. And one night they brought me a patient instead of a place." His voice flattened. "They brought me the king himself, sedated, and told me to map what was growing inside his skull."

The fire popped. Somewhere up the slope, snow slid from a branch with a sound like a sigh.

"A vine," Lior said again. "But not a plant, not as you'd know one. It doesn't root in soil. It roots in *surrender*. In every act of love a man gives up because duty demands it. Every time Aldric chose the crown over a person, the vine took root a little deeper. Every grief he swallowed instead of speaking. Every name he refused to say. It grew fat on all of it."

"That's not how plants work."

"No," Lior agreed. "It's worse than plants. Plants take water and light. This takes *devotion*—and only the kind that's given away against the heart's own will. Love sacrificed on the altar of obligation. There's no sweeter soil for it. And there was no richer garden in all the Nine Reaches than King Aldric's heart, because he spent twenty years burying everything he cherished."

Mirabel pulled her stockings from the fire's edge, though they were long dry. "Burying how?"

"He had a daughter." The words came out of Lior like splinters coming out of flesh—slowly, each one leaving a small dark hole behind. "Princess Sable. She questioned the regency council when she was nineteen. Asked why her father signed decrees he never read. The vine was already whispering through him by then—it had learned to use his voice, his seal, his tired paternal smile. He renounced her publicly. Struck her from the succession with his own hand. I saw the decree." Lior looked into the fire. "His hand shook when he signed it. The vine drank that trembling like wine."

"And the mother?"

"Died birthing her. Another grief buried. Another root." He spread his hands. "You understand now? This thing doesn't conquer. It *cultivates*. It waits at the crossroads of love and duty, and every time a man turns from love toward duty, it grows. The queen-regent tends it now—I don't know if she planted it or married it or simply learned to prune it—but the king, the true king, is still in there. Buried under twenty years of everything he surrendered. Flickering. Begging."

Mirabel was silent a long moment. The firelight moved on her face, and Lior, who had once made his living reading terrain, found himself reading her instead: the wariness, yes, but beneath it something that might have been recognition. She knew what it was to be buried alive inside yourself. Perhaps that was why the map had chosen her.

"So we cut it out," she said finally. "Burn the vine. Simple."

"It isn't in his body the way a worm is in an apple. It's in his *choices*. Every surrendered love is a root, and the roots go down past killing. You can't burn a man free of the things he gave up. You can only—" He stopped.

"Only what?"

"Only make him take them back. Or take them back *for* him. And I don't know if that's possible. I mapped the thing for two years, Mirabel. I know its shape better than my own face—which is fortunate, because I'm no longer sure I remember my own face." He laughed, and it came out wrong, too dry. "They erased me when I fled. Half-erased. They took the middle of me and left the edges. I remember my craft. I remember my crimes. Everything between—my mother's kitchen, whatever loves I had, the reason I really ran—it's gone. Cut out clean, like a page razored from a book."

Mirabel had gone very still.

It was the stillness that warned him. He had spent enough days with her now to know her movements, her rhythms, the quick economy of her hands. Stillness was not one of her rhythms. Stillness was what happened when Mirabel Oyelaran stopped being a person and became a thief.

"Don't," Lior said.

Too late. Her hand crossed the space between them faster than he could track—not touching his skin but hovering above his brow, fingers spread, and her eyes went distant and silvered, the way water goes silver when a fish turns beneath it. He felt the touch arrive anyway, cold and precise, sliding into him along channels he hadn't known he had. Searching. Reading. *Taking.*

He tried to pull back and couldn't—or wouldn't, some traitorous part of him curious, starving, desperate to know what she would find in the razored pages of him.

Her breath caught.

"There's a voice," she whispered. Her eyes were still far away, turned inward on whatever she was seeing inside him. "A woman's voice. Singing. Low, in a language I don't—" Her brow furrowed. "There's a kitchen. Copper pots. Rain on a window. She's singing and someone small is laughing, and Lior, the memory has *edges*. Cut edges. Someone took this out and stitched the wound shut and it's *still bleeding*—"

"*Stop.*"

She stopped. Her hand withdrew and she rocked back on her heels, staring at him, and her face had gone pale in the firelight. The silver drained slowly from her eyes like tide going out.

"They didn't erase you," she said. "They *harvested* you. That's different. Erasure burns. This was cut clean—preserved. Kept. Memories that valuable don't get destroyed, Lior. They get *stored*." Her voice dropped. "Someone is keeping your life in a vault somewhere. Your mother's kitchen. The woman singing. All of it, whole, while you walk around with holes where it used to be."

Lior discovered he was shaking. Not with fear—with something older and more humiliating. Hope. Three years he had mourned his own past like a man mourning a drowned child, and here was this thief telling him the body was intact. Preserved. *Kept.*

"Why did you do that?" he asked quietly. "Without asking."

"Because you'd have said no." She didn't flinch from it. "And because I needed to know if your story was true. If you were leading me into a cage." She hesitated, and then, with the rough honesty he was beginning to suspect she wore like armor: "And because I wanted to see what a stolen memory looks like from the other side. From the side of the person it was taken *from*." Her jaw tightened. "I've never looked. Twenty years in the trade and I never once wondered what the hole looks like after."

"And now?"

"Now I think I owe every soul I've ever robbed a very long apology." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "The woman singing. Do you know her?"

"No." His throat worked. "That's the worst of it. I don't know her, and I *do*—she's in the shape of the hole. When I hear rain on a window I grieve and I don't know why. Some nights I wake reaching for someone and my arms are empty and I've never known who." He met her eyes. "You felt her. You touched the memory itself. Tell me—was she—"

"She was happy," Mirabel said softly. "Whoever she was, whoever you were—in that kitchen, you were both happy. That much survived the cutting. Happiness always does. It's the first thing in and the last thing out." She looked away, into the fire. "My mother died when I was six. I keep her face in a locked room in my head, behind everything else. I've never sold it. Never shown anyone. It's the only thing I own outright." A pause. "If someone cut it out of me, I think I'd rather be dead."

The wind moved through the ruined shrine, stirring ash off the altar, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Lior felt something shifting between them across the fire—fragile, dangerous, built from mutual theft and mutual damage. Two broken archives recognizing each other's missing pages.

"We should sleep," he said at last. "Tomorrow we cross the scree fields, and after that, Sablemarket. If anyone can forge us papers that will pass at the capital's gates, it's the fence who raised you."

Something flickered across Mirabel's face at the name *Sablemarket*—too fast for him to read it, gone before it landed. But he was a cartographer. He noticed omissions. He noted it, and said nothing.

She lay down with her back to the fire, facing the broken doorway and the thorn-split prayers carved above it. He lay opposite, and for a long time neither slept.

"Lior." Her voice came low across the embers. "The vine feeds on surrendered love. On duty winning. So answer me honestly—if it comes to it, at the end, and you have to choose between getting your past back and doing the thing that kills the vine—which way does your duty bend?"

He stared at the roofless sky, at the indifferent stars wheeling over Thornfell, and realized she had asked him the only question that mattered, and that he did not yet know the answer.

"Sleep, Mirabel," he said.

But far below them, far below even the roots of the mountain, something ancient and patient felt the shape of that question pass overhead—and smiled through a thousand borrowed mouths in the sleeping city, and began, gently, to prepare the ground.
