## 5. Sablemarket

The road down into Sablemarket smelled of wet iron and burnt sugar, and Mirabel knew before she saw the first lantern that she was coming home to a place that had never once been hers.

She had told Lior it was a market. That was a lie of convenience, and she watched him accept it the way he accepted all her lies—politely, with a cartographer's patience, as though filing her falsehoods in some ledger of his own. But Sablemarket was not a market. Markets ended. Sablemarket had simply stopped being anywhere, folding itself into the mountain's root like a pearl inside a rotten oyster, lit by tallow lamps that burned a color no honest flame should burn.

They descended through the old mine adit at dusk, when the Thornsworn patrols on the high road changed shift and the smugglers' toll-boys dozed over their dice. Lior wore his surveyor's coat with the brass buttons turned inward; she had dyed his hair with walnut ink until the grey at his temples went dark. He looked younger. He looked like a man who had never been anyone important, which in Sablemarket was the only safe thing to look like.

"Rules," Mirabel said, stopping him at the mouth of the tunnel where the first lamps hung from hooks of blackened bone. "Don't touch anything wrapped in silk. Don't meet the eyes of anyone wearing gloves indoors. And if a child offers you something sweet, refuse it kindly—the kindness matters more than the refusal."

"What happens if I'm unkind?"

"Then you'll wake up three days older and missing the name you loved best." She said it lightly. She did not feel light. Every step downward was a step into her own history, and history, in Sablemarket, was currency—and currency was always someone else's now.

The bazaar opened below them like a wound in the mountain. Three tiers of galleries carved into living rock, hung with chains of colored glass that scattered the lamplight into false jewels. The smell hit her first, as it always did: beeswax and myrrh masking the sweeter rot of memory gone bad, memories kept too long in their vials until they soured into something that curdled the stomach of anyone who breathed them. Somewhere a fiddler played a tune with one note deliberately wrong, because in Sablemarket even music was bait—a wrong note caught the ear, and a caught ear could be picked.

Lior stopped on the stairs. "Gods," he said quietly. "It's a map."

"What?"

"Of a mind." His hand moved, unconsciously tracing the tiers, the galleries, the flow of bodies between stalls. "Outer ring, sensation and trade. Middle ring, the vaults. Deep ring—" he looked down into the dark at the center, where the lamps grew fewer and finer, "—the self. Whoever built this place was thinking like me."

"Whoever built this place was thinking like Mother Quill," Mirabel said. "Come on."

* * *

Mother Quill had not aged so much as accumulated. She sat in her counting room above the middle tier behind a door of iron-banded yew, and she was exactly as Mirabel remembered: small and dry as a pressed flower, wrapped in shawls the color of old bruises, her fingers stained silver to the second knuckle from decades of handling memory-glass. Her eyes were quick and kind and utterly without mercy, and when they fell on Mirabel they widened with what looked, for one whole heartbeat, like genuine love.

That heartbeat was the last lie Mirabel would ever believe from her.

"Mira." Quill's voice was smoke and honey. "Little wren. They said you were dead in the east roads."

"They say a lot of things." Mirabel kept her hands visible and empty, an old habit from this room. "I need papers. Court credentials, real ones—surveyor's writs under royal seal. The kind that can't be forged because they're grown, not made."

Quill's smile deepened. "Grown credentials. Which means a seed-document from the chancery vaults, which means the deep ring, which means you want me to spend favors I've spent twenty years stacking." She clicked her tongue. "And who is your quiet friend?"

"A colleague. He's mute."

"I'm not—"

"He's mute," Mirabel repeated, and stepped hard on Lior's foot.

Quill laughed, a dry rustle like paper turning. Then she rose, shuffled to her cabinet of small drawers, and began pulling out wax, seals, blank vellum—and talking, the way she always talked while she worked, a stream of gossip and prices and warnings that was itself a kind of fencing, keeping the customer's mind busy while her fingers did the true work.

"You've grown thin," Quill said. "You never eat properly. You never ate properly even as a girl, running those errands for me, little courier of other people's hearts. Do you remember the first thing you ever lifted? You were seven. A cooper's grief for his drowned son. You brought it back to me crying, and I sold it within the hour to a woman who'd lost her own boy, and she wept over another mother's sorrow like it fit her, and it did, it did. Grief is grief, Mira. It fits any hand."

Mirabel said nothing. Lior was watching her with an expression she refused to read.

"The writs will take two days," Quill said. "There's a chandler's loft on the third gallery—you remember it, you hid there after the Vasse affair. Go. Rest. Eat something, gods rot you." She pressed a key into Mirabel's palm, warm from her own skin. "Two days, wren."

At the door, Quill caught her wrist. Her grip was stronger than her body suggested, and her eyes, up close, held something Mirabel had never seen in them before: pity.

"You were the best thief I ever raised," Quill said softly. "Do you know why? Because you stole everything except yourself. Not one memory of your own, not one, in fifteen years of trade. Everything else went into my vaults, but you kept your own head locked." Her thumb stroked the pulse in Mirabel's wrist. "A wise girl. A careful girl. Almost like someone taught you that the only thing worth keeping is the one thing no one can take."

Mirabel pulled free and left without answering, and it was not until she was three galleries away, in the cold of the chandler's loft among hanging candles like pale fruit, that she understood what had been in Quill's voice.

Not pride. Inventory.

* * *

She woke to silence in the wrong key.

Sablemarket was never silent—it hummed, always, the way a hive hums—but its hum had a rhythm, and the rhythm was broken. She lay still on the cot beneath the candle-racks and listened, and heard what she had heard once before, years ago, the night the Vasse enforcers came: the particular quality of footsteps trying not to echo. Boots wrapped in felt. Thornsworn boots.

Beside her, Lior was already awake, already dressed, his finger against his lips. He pointed toward the stair door, then held up fingers: four. Then he pointed at himself, then at the window—a sheer drop into the lower gallery, madness—and shook his head slowly.

Mirabel's mind did what it always did under pressure: it went cold and fast and precise, and it hated her for how easily it did it. *She sold us. Two days wasn't two days. Two days was the time it takes to send a runner up the mine road.* And beneath the tactical cold, something else was cracking open, something worse than fear.

Because Quill hadn't just sold their location. Quill had known, the moment she saw them, what they carried. And the Thornsworn didn't pay for information in coin.

They paid in memories. Rare ones. Old ones.

"No," Mirabel whispered, before she could stop herself.

Lior frowned. She grabbed his sleeve and pulled him to the window instead of explaining, because there was no time, and also because she could not yet bear to say it aloud. Below, in the middle tier, she could see them: six figures in plain wool moving with military economy, fanning out around Quill's counting house. And Quill herself stood in her doorway, unhurried, unafraid, exchanging words with their captain—and then, with the gesture of a shopkeeper concluding a satisfying sale, she handed him something small.

Something glass.

Even at this distance, even across the noise of the waking bazaar, Mirabel felt it. The way you feel a tooth break. The vial caught the lamplight and threw back a color she knew better than her own heartbeat: the deep amber of a memory from before the age of ten. Before the roads. Before the trade.

Before her mother's face—if it was that one. If Quill had found a way to lift it after all, some night when Mirabel slept too deeply in this very building, some night in the years when trust had still been a word she used without flinching.

Or if it wasn't that one, then it was another. There had been so few. That was the horror of it—there had been so few that each one was a room she could name, and every room might already be empty.

The captain tucked the vial away. Quill turned back into her shop, closing the door on the past the way she closed it every night, and Mirabel understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that her childhood was not lost. It was not even stolen, not anymore, not in any way that theft implied. It was *stock*. Catalogued. Priced. Sitting in a vault or already sold, traded piece by piece across fifteen years to buy Quill's safety, Quill's comforts, Quill's standing in the deep ring—spent by the only person who had ever claimed to keep nothing of hers.

*Never be remembered,* Mirabel had ruled, all these years. She had thought it was discipline. It had been a cage she built around the one bird she owned, and the cage had a lock, and Quill had copied the key long ago.

Lior touched her shoulder. The Thornsworn were climbing the gallery stairs now, four of them, felt-wrapped and patient. He pointed again at the window, then at himself, and this time he didn't shake his head. He began, calmly, to coil the chandler's rope.

They went down the outside of the mountain in the dark, hand over hand, candle-smoke rising past them like the souls of everything she'd ever sold. Twice the rope jumped in her grip as boots thundered overhead through the loft above. Once Lior's foot slipped and she caught his wrist and held the whole weight of him against the rock, and neither of them spoke, because speaking was breath and breath was noise.

They dropped the last ten feet into a delivery tunnel and ran.

* * *

They surfaced an hour later in the drainage galleries beyond the eastern adit, soaked in meltwater, lungs raw. Behind them Sablemarket glowed in the mountain's throat, indifferent, still trading. Somewhere in its vaults, in rows of labeled glass, Mirabel Oyelaran's childhood sat waiting for a buyer.

Lior wrung out his coat and looked at her—at the set of her face, the shaking she couldn't fully hide in her hands—and for once the cartographer asked no questions. He simply said, "We can come back. When this is done. Vaults can be robbed by people who know how."

"She'll have moved them by dawn," Mirabel said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "She's had fifteen years to move them. Fifteen years, Lior. I thought—I thought I was the only thing she never sold." She laughed, and hated the sound. "I was the *display*. The proof of concept. 'Look what my little wren became.' And the whole time—"

She stopped. Because a thought had arrived, cold and complete, and it rearranged everything.

The dying courier on the rain-slicked road. *The crown is not the man.* The map that had chosen her—or that she had been made to carry. Quill's parting words: *you kept your own head locked. Almost like someone taught you.*

Taught her. Or *kept* her. A thief who steals everything except herself is not careful. She is *reserved*. Held back. Saved for the right buyer.

"Your map," Mirabel said slowly. "The one you drew inside the court. The vine feeds on surrendered duty—love given up for obligation. What does it pay?"

Lior went very still. "Pay?"

"Everything pays in Sablemarket. So what does a thing that eats devotion use for coin?" She looked back at the glow of the bazaar, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Because I think I've just seen its exchange rate. And I think my whole life, someone has been selling pieces of me to something that grows."

Above them, far up the mine road, a horn sounded—one long note, then two short. The signal for *prey sighted, prey escaped, widen the net.*

Lior shouldered his satchel and turned north, toward the capital, toward the palace and the roots beneath it. "Then we'd best go ask it in person," he said.

Mirabel took one last look at the light of Sablemarket—the place that had raised her, priced her, and sold her by the vial—and followed him into the dark.
