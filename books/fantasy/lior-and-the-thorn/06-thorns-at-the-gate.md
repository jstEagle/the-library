## 6. Thorns at the Gate

The road to Corvale ran straight as a confession, and Lior hated it for that.

For eleven years he had drawn roads—honest ones, crooked ones, roads that bent around grief and flooded valleys and the stubborn property lines of farmers who would rather drown than yield an acre to a neighbor. A good map told the truth by admitting its own distortions. But this road had been surveyed by men who wanted no argument with the land, and so it cut through the Thornfell foothills like a blade through bread, flat and certain and wrong in a way he could feel in his teeth.

He walked at the head of their little party with a surveyor's staff he had not earned and a coat of oiled canvas that smelled of someone else's sweat, and behind him came Mirabel Oyelaran, wearing the face of a junior draughtsman named Pell, because of course she was. She had stolen that face from a clerk in a waystation two days back—not his features, only his manner, the particular stoop of a man who had spent his life being overlooked—and she wore it now like a borrowed coat, hands ink-stained, eyes down, radiating the specific boredom of a person whose ambitions had died quietly years ago.

It was masterful. It made his skin crawl.

"Smile," she murmured as they passed the first milestone, a granite post carved with the royal sigil—a crown wreathed in briars, though the briars had been added recently, the stone chiseled over in living green that should not have survived the season.

"I am smiling."

"You are grimacing like a man biting tinfoil." She fell into step beside him, voice pitched under the wind. "Surveyors smile. Surveyors are paid to be pleased about drainage."

He arranged his mouth into something approximating contentment and looked out over the valley, and there it was: Corvale, the capital, spread along the river in the morning haze, and rising from its center the palace, white-walled and crowned, with—

With thorns.

They climbed the outer towers in green ropes thick as his wrist, and they had grown *recently*; he could see where they had split the lead flashing on the west roof, where they had torn a gutter loose that hung dangling like a snapped tendon. Eleven years ago there had been nothing on those walls but pigeons and the lye-washed stone. Now the palace wore the vine openly, the way a man wears a scar he has stopped explaining.

"They're not hiding it anymore," Mirabel said. He heard something in her voice he had not heard before. Not fear, exactly. Recognition.

"No," Lior said. "Why would they? The city's already theirs."

* * *

The gate wardens waved them through with barely a glance—the surveyor's writ Mirabel had forged was beautiful work, all the right seals, all the right bored annotations—but once inside the walls, Lior understood that documents were beside the point. Nothing in Corvale was watching for liars. Everything in Corvale was already telling the same one.

The city smiled.

That was the thing he could not stop seeing, walking the Processional toward the Guild quarter with his surveyor's case knocking against his hip. The people smiled. Bakers smiled through their windows at customers they did not know. Dockhands smiled while hauling loads heavy enough to split a back. A woman scolded a child in the fish market, and even mid-scold her mouth held its curve, gentle and fixed, like a painting of patience hung slightly crooked. And beneath each smile, when the light caught them right—at the jaw, at the temple, tracing up behind the ear like the ghost of a vein—ran a shadow. Not a mark. A *shadow*, thin and dark and faintly mobile, as if something under the skin were turning over in its sleep.

Thorn-shadowed eyes, the courier had called them, before dying at Mirabel's fire. Lior had thought it a figure of speech. It was not. It was cartography: you could map the vine's reach street by street, block by block, by counting the smiles.

"Look at the well," Mirabel breathed.

At the corner fountain, a queue of women waited with their pitchers, chatting pleasantly, and every one of them stood exactly the same distance from her neighbor—two feet, precise as if measured—as if some invisible instrument had ruled the line. When one woman finished filling her pitcher, the next stepped forward without being summoned, without looking, in perfect sequence.

"Duty," Lior said quietly. "That's what it looks like when duty stops being a choice."

"You said it feeds on surrendered love." Her voice had gone flat, careful. "This is what surrender buys?"

"This is what surrender *looks* like. What it buys—" He stopped. Ahead, a funeral procession was crossing the square: six bearers, one coffin, a widow in grey. The widow was smiling. She was weeping as she walked, tears cutting clean tracks down her cheeks, and she was smiling, wide and serene, and the crowd lining the route smiled back at her, and somewhere in Lior's chest something old and half-erased began to ache in a place he had no memory to attach to the ache.

"It buys peace," he finished. "The kind you don't have to survive."

Mirabel said nothing, but she had gone very still, the way she went still around anything that frightened her, and Lior had learned enough of her in three weeks on the road to know that she filed the widow's face away somewhere deep, in the vault where she kept the things she refused to feel.

* * *

The trouble found them at the Guildhall steps, because trouble in Corvale always knew your name.

They had come to register the survey—forged writs required a real stamp, and stamps lived in real offices—and Lior had chosen the hour deliberately, the dead stretch after midday meal when clerks dozed over their ledgers. He had not counted on apprentices who did not sleep, because apprentices in Corvale had no need of rest. Duty kept them upright.

"Lior Aldous."

The voice came from the shadow of the archway, young and wondering and utterly delighted, and Lior's blood went to ice water in the space of a heartbeat.

A boy stood there—no, not a boy anymore, a young man of perhaps nineteen, tall now, broad through the shoulders, but Lior knew him instantly, knew him the way you know a map you drew yourself. Tam Vess. Tam, who at twelve had inked his first coastline and gotten the whole thing upside down and laughed until he cried. Tam, who used to bring Lior honeycakes wrapped in napkins because he'd noticed Lior forgot to eat when the light was good. Tam, whose name Lior had written in the margin of a chart of the southern isles—*best hands I ever trained*—before the court took everything else.

Tam's face was open and shining, and across his temple, faint as breath on glass, ran the shadow of a thorn.

"Master Aldous," Tam said again, coming forward, hand outstretched, joy naked on his face. "Eleven years—they told us you'd fled, that you'd betrayed the Crown, but I never believed it, I *never*—I kept your chair. In the drafting room. They let me keep your chair, I oil the hinges myself—"

Lior heard himself say, in a voice he almost didn't recognize, "You've grown, Tam."

And Tam beamed, and the beam pulled the thorn-shadow taut across his cheekbone, and Lior saw the moment the shadow *noticed*. It was subtle—a flicker, a tightening, the way a sleeping animal's ear turns toward a sound. The vine inside Tam Vess had just registered a name it had been taught to hunt. Somewhere above them, in the white palace with its flowering towers, something turned a page.

"We have to go," Mirabel whispered. She had appeared at his elbow without seeming to move, and her hand was already inside her coat, fingers closing around the sliver of polished bone she used the way other thieves used lockpicks. "Lior. There's a guard post forty feet from here and that boy is going to remember your face until the day he dies, and the vine reads every memory he owns. You know what I have to do."

He knew. He had known since the pass, since the shrine, since the night she'd slid uninvited into his mind and found the woman's voice waiting there like a knife left in a drawer. Memory-thieves could take. That was the trade. That was the whole filthy trade.

But taking was not the same as this. This was different. This was watching.

"He loves me," Lior said. It came out barely louder than breathing. "Mirabel, he kept my *chair*—"

"And in an hour he'll report a stranger asking questions at the Guildhall, and by nightfall the Thornsworn will have our descriptions, and by tomorrow we'll be hanging from the Briar Gate." Her eyes, when they met his, were not cold. That was the worst part. They were terribly, unbearably kind. "I can take it gently. I've done it ten thousand times. He won't even hurt. He'll just... set you down, the way you set down a weight you didn't know you were carrying. Please. We have maybe thirty seconds before that shadow finishes waking up."

Tam was still talking—something about the southern charts, about how he'd redrawn the isles himself last spring, *using your methods, Master Aldous, your methods*—and his smile was so bright and so hollow and so completely, catastrophically sincere that Lior felt something in him crack along a fault line he hadn't known was there.

"Do it," he said.

Mirabel stepped past him, took Tam's hands in both of hers as if greeting an old friend's friend, and leaned close, and her thumb brushed the boy's temple, and her lips moved in words too soft for any ear but the one inside his skull.

Lior watched.

He watched the joy stay on Tam's face for three heartbeats—joy needs a moment to understand it's been orphaned—and then he watched it drain, slowly, the way color drains from a sunset, leaving the boy's expression smooth and pleasant and empty of anything at all. The thorn-shadow faded with it, or seemed to; the vine had less to hold onto now. Tam blinked. Looked at Mirabel. Looked at Lior.

"Sorry," Tam said politely. "Do I know you? You have a surveyor's look about you. The Guildhall's through the east door, if you need the stamp office."

"That's very kind," Mirabel said warmly. "Thank you."

Tam nodded, smiled his city smile—fixed, gentle, two feet of distance—and walked away across the square toward whatever duty was waiting for him, and he did not look back, because there was nothing left in him that knew there was anything to look back at.

Lior stood on the Guildhall steps in the afternoon light and discovered that a man can grieve a memory he does not own. The boy was alive. The boy was happy, or the city's version of happy. And somewhere in Tam Vess's skull there was now a Tam-shaped hole where eleven years of honeycakes and ruined coastlines and *best hands I ever trained* used to live, and the hole did not hurt, because the boy would never know it was there.

Only Lior would carry it. Only Lior would stand in the empty chair-shaped space of another man's mind and mourn.

"You did the right thing," Mirabel said quietly.

"Don't." His voice came out raw. "Don't tell me what I did. I know what I did. I erased myself from the only person in this city who was glad to see me."

She was silent a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she reached out and touched his sleeve—brief, awkward, the gesture of someone handling an instrument she didn't trust herself with. "When this is over," she said, "if the vine dies, the memories it holds come loose. Quill told me that once, about the ones it keeps in its roots. If that's true of the ones it grows in heads..." She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

Lior looked at her, and for one dangerous second he let himself imagine it: Tam in the drafting room, frowning at a sudden warmth he couldn't name, reaching for a name that had come home.

Then the bells began.

Not the hour bells. The alarm—deep bronze voices rolling down from the palace heights, one, two, three, and across the square the smiling citizens turned their heads in perfect unison, all of them together, like a field of flowers following the sun. On the Guildhall doors, stamped into the fresh wax of the notice board, a new proclamation gleamed:

*A reward for the memory-thief Mirabel Oyelaran and the traitor Lior Aldous. Bring their minds intact.*

"She sold us," Mirabel breathed. "Quill sold us before we even cleared the mountains."

Below them, at the mouth of the square, the first black-and-green livery of the Thornsworn came around the fountain at a run.

* * *

"There," Lior said, and pointed his surveyor's staff at the one direction no one was watching: down. "The under-map. I built half of it. And I never put it on paper."

Mirabel grinned—her real grin, sharp and wild and alive—and followed him into the dark.
