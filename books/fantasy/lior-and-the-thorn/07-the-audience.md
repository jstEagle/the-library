## 7. The Audience

They came for her at dawn, and they did not come quietly.

Mirabel woke to the sound of boots on stone—too many boots, moving with the synchronized patience of things that had never once doubted an order. She was on her feet before her eyes opened, hand already at the knife beneath her cloak, but Lior's grip closed on her wrist in the dark of the surveyor's loft.

"Don't," he breathed. "There are twelve of them. I counted their shadows under the door."

"Then we go out the window."

"The window faces the courtyard." His voice was flat, resigned, the voice of a man who had spent years learning exactly how large the palace was and how small any single person inside it. "The window has always faced the courtyard. That's why I chose this room—so I'd never forget what I was looking at."

The door opened without being knocked upon.

The Thornsworn filled it like a hedge fills a gap in a wall. Their armor was black-lacquered leather stitched with living vine, and Mirabel watched, stomach turning, as the tendrils shifted against their throats—not decoration, not restraint, but *root*. They had been planted into their own bodies long ago, and whatever they had been before, they were now only the vine's hands.

"You are summoned," said the one in front. Its voice was a man's voice, or had been once. "Her Radiance will hear the thief."

*Her Radiance.* Not the king. The words settled over Mirabel like a wet cloth over a flame.

Lior stepped forward, chin lifted, every inch the disgraced courtier he had once been. "I am Lior Aldous, Royal Cartographer by appointment. If there is a charge to answer—"

"There is no charge," the thing said, and something that might have been amusement moved through its ruined face. "There is only audience."

* * *

They were marched through corridors that made Mirabel's skin crawl, though she could not at first say why. Then she understood: the palace was *wrong*. The proportions drifted. A hallway that should have taken forty steps took sixty, then thirty, then a hundred and four, and no guard stumbled or remarked upon it because none of them noticed anything at all. The tapestries depicted harvests and coronations, but the thread used for the greenery was darker than the rest, and if she looked too long the leaves seemed to tremble.

And everywhere, the people smiled.

Servants carrying linens. Clerks with ledgers. A chamberlain directing the placement of candles for some observance. All of them smiling, all of them courteous, all of them with a faint shadow at the temple like a bruise of ink beneath the skin—the same thorn-shadow Lior had described, the mark of minds that had surrendered something and been rewarded with peace.

A serving girl paused beside them, balancing a tray of figs, and beamed at Mirabel with such open warmth that Mirabel's chest ached with sudden, irrational grief.

"Aren't you lucky," the girl said softly, "to be seen by Her Radiance."

"I—" Mirabel began.

But the girl had already moved on, still smiling, still carrying her figs, and Mirabel understood that the girl would not remember her. None of them would remember anything that didn't serve. The palace was full of people who had traded their remembering for their safety, and the trade had been so gentle they thanked the vine for taking it.

*This is what I do,* she thought, cold settling into her bones. *I take memories and sell them. The difference is only that my customers ask.*

She clung to that difference like a rope over a drop. She wasn't sure how much weight it would bear.

* * *

The throne room doors were white oak, forty feet tall, carved with the history of the Aldric line—and the carvings had grown over. Vines of pale wood wound through the kings and queens of old, softening their faces, merging their crowns into a single continuous bough. At the center, where the current king should have stood triumphant, the wood had swollen into a great knot shaped almost like a heart.

The doors opened onto a hall flooded with morning light, and King Aldric sat upon his throne.

He was not what Mirabel expected. She had imagined a monster, or a puppet slumped in its strings. Instead he was a broad, gray-bearded man in his sixties, upright, dignified, dressed in deep green and gold, and he looked at her with clear brown eyes full of weary kindness.

For one heartbeat, she thought: *he's fine. He's fighting it. He's still in there.*

Then he blinked, and the kindness drained away like water through sand, and what remained was patient and vast and utterly without mercy.

"Mirabel Oyelaran," said the king—or the crown wearing him. Its voice was Aldric's voice, warm and resonant, a voice built for reassurance. "Memory-thief. Daughter of the smuggler roads. You carry a map drawn by a traitor."

"I carry paper," Mirabel said. Her voice held steady. She was proud of that. "Paper isn't treason."

"Everything is treason," the king said gently, "that cannot be filed." It gestured, and beside the throne stepped a woman in silver and white.

The queen-regent.

Mirabel had heard her described as beautiful, and the descriptions were accurate in the way a knife's description is accurate. Serane Vael was perhaps fifty, silver-haired, elegant, with the kind of composure that comes from decades of never once being surprised. But her temples bore not shadows but *blossoms*—small white flowers growing directly from her skin, opening and closing with her breath, and when she moved, Mirabel heard the whisper of roots shifting beneath the floor.

"You stole from my courier," Serane said. Her voice was quiet, almost tender. "You stole from my map."

"Your map," Lior said sharply. "That map is mine. I drew it—"

"You drew what you were permitted to see," she said, without looking at him. "Before you ran. Before you cut half your own work out of your head rather than serve." Now she turned to him, and her smile was the smile of a gardener regarding a plant that had escaped its pot. "We were going to come find you, cartographer. You've saved us the journey."

Mirabel felt Lior go rigid beside her.

"But first," Serane continued, returning her attention to Mirabel, "I want to know how you touched it. The map. Ordinary minds slide off it—it was drawn in the space between thoughts. Only someone who lives there could read it." She descended one step from the dais, flowers trembling. "So tell me, thief. What did you see?"

And here Mirabel made a choice.

She could lie. She was good at lying; she'd been raised on lies the way other children were raised on lullabies. But she looked at the queen-regent's blossoming temples, at the guards with vines rooted in their throats, at the smiling dead-eyed servants beyond the doors, and she understood that this creature had spent twenty years cultivating surrender, and that a lie offered freely was just another kind of surrender.

So instead she reached out—not with her hands, but the way she always reached, sliding her awareness along the surface of the king's mind like a thief's fingers along a windowsill—

And fell *in*.

* * *

The inside of King Aldric's mind was a garden gone to ruin.

Thorns everywhere, white and gleaming, climbing over everything—but beneath them, buried, choked, there were still rooms. Mirabel moved through the dark of him like smoke, past trellises of duty, past hedges pruned into the shapes of oaths, and found a door that was only a door, plain oak, uncarved, unguarded.

She opened it.

Inside was a small room lit by candlelight, and in it sat an old man, and he was weeping.

He looked up at her, and his face was the king's face from the throne—but alive, cracked open, human in a way the body upstairs could no longer manage.

"You're real," he whispered. "Oh—you're real. I haven't seen anyone real in—" He pressed both hands over his mouth, composing himself with the practiced grace of a monarch, and failed. "Forgive me. Forgive me. Twenty years I have been polite. Do you know what it is, to be polite while you are eaten?"

"What's your name?" Mirabel asked, though she knew it.

"Aldric." He laughed, wet and broken. "Just Aldric. The rest belongs to it."

"It wears you."

"It grew in me." He looked at his own hands, at the faint white lines threading his wrists like old scars. "It doesn't take, thief. That's the horror no one understands. It *waits*. Every time I chose the crown over a person, it put down a root. My brother asked for help and I sent soldiers instead of going myself—root. My wife died and I attended the funeral between two council sessions—root. And my daughter—" His voice shattered. He gathered it again, piece by piece, the way a man gathers spilled grain. "My daughter. Elowen. She was seven. There was a plot against the throne, and the council said a princess was a liability, a hostage-in-waiting, and I agreed with them. I let them send her away and tell the world she had never been born. I told myself it was duty." He looked up, and his eyes were terrible. "It was the biggest root of all. It's the reason the vine can wear me. I gave it my love and called the giving virtue."

Mirabel knelt in the candlelit room of his mind, and grief rose in her throat—for this stranger, this enemy, this king who had done monstrous things in the shape of goodness.

"They'll kill you," she said. "The regent. When she doesn't need you anymore."

"Oh, yes." He said it simply, without fear. "I've known for years. I stay alive out of spite now. It's the only duty I have left that's truly mine." He caught her hands, and his grip was desperate. "Listen. Listen. It cannot hold a mind that *wants*. Wanting is the one door it cannot grow through—that's why it feeds on surrendered love, because surrendered love stops wanting. So want, thief. Whatever you do down there, whatever it offers you—want something. Hold on to wanting like a blade."

"The heart," Mirabel said. "Beneath the throne room. Is it true?"

"It's true. And it knows you're here." His face crumpled. "Go. Go before I lose the strength to hide you. And Mirabel—" His voice dropped to a whisper. "If you find her, ever—if you find my Elowen—tell her the renunciation was the one act of my reign I signed with a shaking hand. Tell her that. It's not forgiveness. It's just true."

The garden shuddered around them. Roots were rising, searching, and somewhere far above, a woman's voice was saying her name with flowers blooming in her words.

* * *

She surfaced gasping, on her knees before the throne, blood in her mouth from biting her tongue.

Serane Vael stood over her, and the blossoms at her temples had opened wide, drinking the light.

"You went in," the regent said softly. "Into *him*. Into the ruin." Something moved across her perfect face—not anger. Hunger. "What did you find?"

"A dying man," Mirabel said.

"A *king*," Serane corrected, and for a moment—just a moment—her composure flickered, and Mirabel saw beneath it: a woman who had given everything to the vine, absolutely everything, and could not bear to hear that the man she had hollowed out still had anything left inside. "A king who surrendered himself willingly to the realm's need. There is no greater devotion." She straightened. "Guards. Take the thief to the White Chamber. Take the cartographer as well. We will learn what else they've touched."

The Thornsworn moved.

And stopped.

All twelve of them, mid-stride, frozen like clockwork figures with a hand laid on the mechanism. The hall went silent except for the whisper of the vine in the walls, confused, straining.

At the throne, King Aldric's body had risen. His eyes were open, and they were *his*—brown, human, blazing with twenty years of buried will.

"*No*," the king said aloud, in his own voice, and the word rolled through the hall like a bell through fog. "Not this one. Not—this—"

His hands gripped the arms of the throne hard enough to crack the gilded wood. Vines burst from the floor around him, wrapping his limbs, forcing him down, and he fought them—openly, visibly, a king wrestling his own crown in front of his entire court—and every second he fought, the guards stood frozen, because their orders flowed through the vine, and the vine was busy.

"GO," Aldric roared, and it was half command, half plea. "Girl—GO—while I am still—"

Serane spun toward the doors. "*After them!*"

The guards lurched forward, tearing free of the stall—but Lior had already seized Mirabel's arm, and he was hauling her not toward the doors but *away* from them, toward the eastern wall, toward a panel of carved stonework that every servant walked past a thousand times a day and never saw.

"The under-map," he gasped. "I told you—I mapped the palace twice. Once for the court. Once for myself."

He pressed the carving, and a section of wall swung inward on hinges older than the dynasty, exhaling air that smelled of dust and candle-smoke and centuries.

Behind them, the king's roar broke into something smaller—a man's voice, sobbing, holding on. Mirabel looked back once, across the bright hall, at the old warrior drowning in his own garden, buying their escape with the last coins of his buried will.

*I will come back,* she promised him, silently, the way she had never promised anyone anything. *I will cut you free.*

Then Lior pulled her through, and the wall closed, and the darkness of the forgotten palace swallowed them whole.

Below their feet, faint as a heartbeat, something vast stirred in its sleep—and began, patiently, to grow toward them.
