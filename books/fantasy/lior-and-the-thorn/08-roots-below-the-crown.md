## 8. Roots Below the Crown

The under-map began where the palace's memory ended.

Lior had drawn it in the dark years of his servitude, inked by candlelight in a room that smelled of tallow and rotting sweetness, when his hands still belonged to the court and only his mind belonged to him. He had hidden it inside honest work—rivers that bent a finger's width too far east, mountain ranges whose contours spelled, to anyone who knew his hand, a second geography beneath the first. *Follow the water against its will. Count the doors that open inward. Where the map lies, I do not.*

Now he knelt on cold flagstone beneath the throne room with Mirabel's stolen lantern between them, tracing those same lies in the living stone. The passage mouth was exactly where he had promised it would be: behind the third of seven carved panels depicting Aldric's coronation, the one where the sculptor had given the young king a face too gentle for a crown. A door that opened inward. Lior pressed his palm flat against the king's stone cheek and pushed.

The panel sighed open on hinges that had not moved in twenty years, exhaling air like the breath of something long asleep.

"You drew yourself an escape route through a king's face," Mirabel said quietly.

"I drew myself out of a prison." He took up the lantern. "The route just happened to run through him."

* * *

They descended by his own erased marks.

That was how he thought of them now—the scratches he had left in mortar joints, the chisel-taps arranged in fives, the deliberate flaws in masonry that no surveyor would correct because they looked intentional. His past self had been thorough. His past self had known he might come back half-empty, hunting the shape of what was taken from him, and had left bread crumbs of stone for the man he would become.

Mirabel walked close behind him, close enough that he could feel her attention like a hand between his shoulder blades. She had been quiet since the audience above—quiet in the way she was quiet after touching a mind, he was learning. As though words had to be rinsed first.

"The buried king," she said at last. "He showed me a name. *Sera.* He said it like a prayer someone had forbidden him."

"Aldric had a daughter," Lior said. "Before my time at court. She was sent to a convent in the Thornfell passes when she was nine. Officially for her health." He ducked beneath a lintel crusted with pale mineral bloom. "Unofficially, the vine required the renunciation. It grows best on surrendered love, remember. And there is nothing a father surrenders more completely than a child he is made to give away while she is still alive enough to miss him."

"And the queen-regent let him keep the crown anyway."

"The vine doesn't want the crown destroyed. It wants the crown *tended*." Lior's voice came out flatter than he intended. "A kingdom of dutiful grief, growing season after season. That's the harvest."

The stair wound down through rock that grew warmer, then strangely sweet. Not rot—something closer to overripe fruit, or wax melting near a flame. The lantern light turned amber, then gold, then a color Lior had no professional name for, a color like the last hour of a summer evening stretched until it hurt.

"Stop," Mirabel said suddenly, catching his sleeve.

Ahead, the passage ended.

Not in wall—in *bloom*.

* * *

The garden opened around them like a held breath finally released.

White thorns, each taller than a man, arched overhead in ribbed vaults of living wood, and from every branch hung flowers the size of dinner plates, luminous, faintly pulsing, their petals translucent as skin held up to sunlight. The sweetness in the air poured from them. And within each blossom—not metaphorically, not in resemblance but in fact—Lior saw movement. Shapes. Faces, sometimes. A child laughing in a flower's heart. Hands kneading bread. A woman turning in a doorway with her hair coming loose.

Memories. Growing here like fruit.

"This is where they go," Mirabel whispered. Her thief's composure had cracked clean through; she sounded very young. "Everything he gave up. Everything anyone in this palace ever traded for duty—it all drains down here. It's not feeding on him. It's *farming* him."

Lior stepped forward before he could stop himself, because one flower near the path's edge was brighter than the rest, and in it—

Himself. Younger. Standing in the map room with ink to his elbows, laughing at something said off-view. Beside him a woman's profile, dark-haired, sharp-chinned, her mouth curved in the particular way of someone about to say something devastating. And threaded through the image, faint as watermark, a voice: *You always map other people's lives, Lior. When will you dare to chart your own?*

His knees nearly went.

"I know her," he heard himself say. "I don't know her name. But I know—" His hand rose toward the bloom without his permission. "She was mine. She was *mine*, and it's hanging here like a lamp."

"Lior." Mirabel's voice, careful. "Don't."

But he was already seeing further down the row. Another flower: a council chamber, himself standing before the assembled ministers, saying words he couldn't hear—and understanding flooding him sideways, the way a missing tooth makes the tongue find its own absence. This was the night he fled. This was the memory torn out of him when he escaped, the reason the court's records called him traitor and his own mind called him coward. He had believed for six years that he'd simply broken, simply run, simply failed.

The flower knew better. In the bloom's depths, past his own younger face, stood the queen-regent—then merely the king's advisor, already thorn-shadowed at the temples—her hand resting on a document. And on the document, orders in Lior's own handwriting. Orders he had never written. Orders that would have drowned a valley village to feed the vine's roots with a town's worth of bereaved devotion.

*I refused,* the flower told him without words. *I refused, and I ran, and they let me believe I abandoned them so that shame would keep me from ever coming back.*

"They didn't erase my treason," he said aloud. His voice shook. "They erased my *innocence*. They left me the guilt and took the proof."

Beside him, Mirabel had gone very still. "This is why you recognized the courier's map. Why you really came."

"I came for my work." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I told myself I came for my work. Six years of telling myself that."

He reached for the flower again. Closer now. Close enough to see that the petals were warm, that the light inside quickened at his approach like a heartbeat recognizing its owner. One touch. One pull, the way Mirabel pulled memories free, and it would be his again—the woman's name, the refusal, the whole unbroken line of who he had been before the court hollowed him and called the hollow humility.

And he understood, with a cartographer's precision, exactly what that touch would cost.

The vine was not asleep down here. It was *attentive*. Every bloom was an eye; every thorn a nerve. The moment he tore his past loose, the garden would know—a theft in its own orchard, an alarm sung through root and branch into every thorn-shadowed mind above, into the queen-regent, into whatever flowering core waited deeper in. They would come down these stairs with all the loving obedience the city could muster.

"We can't take them," he said. His hand hovered a finger's width from the petal. "Any of them. Touch one and the whole garden screams."

"So we leave them." Mirabel's voice was gentle in a way that cost her visibly; gentleness was not her native tongue. "We leave them and we cut the root, and when the vine dies—"

"When the vine dies, everything in it dies with it." The truth arrived cold and complete. "These aren't stored, Mirabel. They're *grown*. Part of the plant. Sever the root and every memory in every bloom goes with it. His daughter's name. My—" His throat closed. "Everyone's sacrificed loves. All of it, compost for the withering."

Mirabel stared at the garden, and he watched her arrive at the same arithmetic he had: thousands of blooms, generations of surrendered love, the entire accumulated heart of a kingdom's dutiful centuries. To kill the vine was to burn the archive.

"Then it has to be worth something," she said slowly. "A trade. You don't destroy a vault like this—you *spend* it. One memory, freely given, against everything it holds." She wasn't looking at the flowers anymore. She was looking at her own hands, and Lior realized with a lurch that she had already chosen which coin she meant to pay.

"No," he said.

"You don't even know what—"

"I know you." The words were out before he could weigh them. "Whatever you're holding back, whatever you've kept for yourself all these years on those roads—I know you're going to offer it, and I'm telling you no."

For a moment neither of them moved. The garden breathed its sweet gold light around them, patient as winter.

Then, deep beneath their feet—deeper than any stair Lior had ever mapped—the ground shivered. The nearest blooms flared bright, all at once, hundreds of faces opening their eyes inside the petals, and far below, through miles of root, something vast turned its attention toward them like a head lifting.

It had noticed them after all.

From the darkness ahead, where the garden funneled down toward a light that was not golden but white as bone, came a sound Lior would carry to his grave: the queen-regent's voice, grown strange and layered, speaking with the resonance of the whole vine at once.

"*Come down, cartographer. Your map ends at my root.*"

Lior looked at Mirabel. Mirabel looked at the light.

Hand in hand—or nearly; their fingers brushed and did not close, both of them too practiced at reaching without taking—they walked toward the heart of the thing that ate devotion, and behind them the garden's thousand eyes watched them go, and ahead of them, waiting among the white thorns, stood everything they had left to lose.
