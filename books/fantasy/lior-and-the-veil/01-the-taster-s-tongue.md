## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The poison was in the wine, and Quentin Reyes knew it before she touched the glass.

That was the trouble with a tongue trained by years of small deaths. Most tasters waited for symptoms — the numbness in the lips, the prickle at the throat, the slow bloom of headache that meant belladonna or worse. Quentin had gone past all that. She had dosed herself so long and so carefully that her body had become an instrument tuned to exactly one note: *wrong*. The nightshade in the queen's cup sang it from across the table, faint as a wrong chord in a familiar song.

She stood at Queen Isolde's left shoulder, as she had stood for six years, in the gray silk of her office — not livery, never livery; the Crown's Taster was a servant of consequence, not a footman — and watched the wine steward pour. His name was Aldric Fen. He had steady hands and a widower's quiet and eleven years of unblemished service. He poured the autumn red into the queen's crystal with the reverence of a man pouring for a saint.

And the wine sang *wrong*.

Quentin did not look at the glass. Looking was a tell. She looked instead at the queen — at Isolde's clever, tired face, at the crown worn light as a habit rather than a burden — and made her calculations in the space of two heartbeats. Nightshade in a vintage this dark would be nearly invisible. A sip would not kill the queen. A full glass, on a night when the queen liked to linger over her wine while the court danced below, very well might.

"Your Majesty." Her voice came out level. Six years of practice made it level. "If I may."

Isolde glanced up, amused. She was a woman who enjoyed ceremony, who found the tasting ritual quaint and comforting, a little theater of loyalty performed nightly at her elbow. "You may always, Quentin."

Quentin took the glass. Held it to the light as if admiring the color. Swirled it once, watching the legs run down the crystal — and there, along the meniscus where the wine met air, the faintest oily shimmer that should not have been there. Old trick. Cheap trick. Someone had assumed the taster would be lazy tonight, or drunk, or bought.

She sipped.

Bitterness bloomed at the back of her tongue like a flower opening in fast motion — atropine's signature, sweet-then-dead, masked under enough honeyed oak to fool anyone whose palate hadn't been rebuilt poison by poison since she was nineteen years old. She held the sip in her mouth. Counted. Let her body argue with itself, the old immunity standing firm against a dose meant for a throat that had never learned the language.

Then she set the glass down on the table with a soft, deliberate click, and said, "Don't drink this."

The music belowstairs went on. Somewhere a courtier laughed too loudly. In the queen's private solar, three ladies-in-waiting froze mid-conversation, and Aldric Fen went white as spilled flour.

Isolde's amusement did not vanish — it changed shape, tightened, became something older and colder. "Explain."

"Nightshade, Your Majesty. Prepared in oil first — you can see the separation if you know to look. Masked with a late-harvest sweetness. Whoever made it knows wine but doesn't know poisons, or they'd have chosen something my tongue couldn't catch." She heard herself speaking in the flat cadence of a report and made herself stop, made herself meet the queen's eyes. "I'd need the rest of the bottle to say more."

"You'll have it." Isolde rose. The room rearranged itself around her the way rooms always did, guards appearing at doors, ladies finding urgent business elsewhere. "Aldric Fen. Remain."

What followed was not gentle. It was not meant to be. Quentin stood in the corner with the poisoned bottle at her feet and watched a man of eleven good years come apart under questioning — watched him deny, weep, deny again, and then, when the captain of the guard mentioned his daughter's boarding school in Callowmere, stop denying. He had debts. He had been approached in a tavern by someone who knew those debts to the copper. He never saw the face. He only remembered hands, and a voice, and the weight of a purse he'd been too frightened to spend.

He was taken away still protesting that he loved the queen, which Quentin believed, because love had nothing to do with anything. Love was not why kitchens were breached. Debts were. Desperation was. And somewhere out in the city, someone with money and patience had studied the palace's rhythms and found the seam.

*Someone who knew the queen lingered over her wine.*

The thought arrived cold and stayed cold.

* * *

They rewarded her at midnight, in the way of courts.

Not with thanks — thanks were cheap and Isolde did not deal in them. With a chain of office in gold and jet, laid across Quentin's shoulders by the queen's own hands, before witnesses. With a purse heavy enough to buy a house in the city, if tasters were permitted houses. With words spoken clearly, for the record: *"The Crown owes Quentin Reyes its gratitude, and its protection."*

Protection. The word sat in Quentin's chest like a swallowed stone.

Because she understood what it meant. She had understood for years, in the way one understands the walls of a beautiful cell — by touching them every day. The gold chain was real. So was the fact that she could not walk out the palace gates without a written pass. That her letters were read before they were sent — she'd tested it once, years ago, with a harmless line about fabric prices, and watched the reply arrive suspiciously fast and suspiciously warm. That her salary was paid in coin stamped with the privy seal, spendable anywhere, traceable everywhere.

And beneath all of it, the thing no one said aloud: a taster who could detect nightshade by smell across a table was worth more than any ten guardsmen. Which meant she would never be allowed to be anything else. Not a wife. Not a traveler. Not a woman with a door of her own. The moment she stopped being useful, she would become a liability — a woman who knew too much about how queens could die — and liabilities in Isolde's court were managed, not dismissed.

She stood in her narrow chamber above the buttery, the gold chain coiled on the table before her like a sleeping snake, and let herself think the thought she never let herself think during daylight:

*I am the most protected prisoner in the kingdom.*

A knock. Two soft raps — the household rhythm, servant to servant.

Maren Voss stood in the corridor, which was itself strange, because Maren was provisioner to the palace cellars and belonged to the city the way fish belong to water, never indoors past dark. She was broad and weathered and had the kind of face that forgot nothing and forgave less. They had done business for years — Quentin's palate vetting the smuggler-road wines that kept the royal cellar stocked at half the tariff price, a small corruption everyone tolerated because it worked.

"You're famous by supper tomorrow," Maren said, low. "Half the kitchen already calls you the queen's greyhound."

"And the other half?"

"The other half is wondering what else that tongue of yours can taste." Maren's eyes flicked down the corridor, back again. "Word of advice, girl. Tonight, someone failed to kill a queen. Tomorrow, someone will start asking whether the failure was the poisoner's — or the taster's. Whether you caught that nightshade because you're good—" a pause, deliberate, "—or because you needed to be seen catching it."

"I caught it because I caught it."

"Mm." Maren handed her something wrapped in cloth — a wedge of hard mountain cheese, an old courtesy between them, nothing more. But her grip lingered. "There's talk on the Thornfell roads. Caravans burned. A waystation gone to ash that isn't on any tax roll, which means it isn't on any map either. Men asking questions about routes nobody official knows exist." She stepped back. "You know those roads better than anyone inside these walls. If anyone comes asking you to walk them—"

"Who would ask me? I taste food."

"Yes," Maren said. "And yet here you are, knowing things you shouldn't, because your tongue makes people trust you with everything else." She smiled without warmth. "Eat your cheese, greyhound. And mind the leash."

Her boots faded down the stair. Quentin stood holding the cheese like a talisman, the gold chain glinting on the table, and outside her window the city sprawled dark and enormous — a city she had crossed perhaps twenty times in six years, always escorted, always accounted for.

Below, in the cells, Aldric Fen was confessing to hands he couldn't describe. Above, in her chamber, the queen's most loyal servant counted the ways she was owned: her coin, her letters, her passes, her palate, her life.

And somewhere between those two facts — a burned waystation off every map, and a voice in her head that whispered *you could walk those roads yourself, if you ever grew brave enough to be lost* — something shifted, small and irreversible, like the first crack in a dam.

Three days later, the summons came. Not from the queen.

From Lord Corvane, the spymaster, who wanted the Taster's Tongue for a different kind of tasting altogether.
