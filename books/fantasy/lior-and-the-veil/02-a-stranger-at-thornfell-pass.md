## 2. A Stranger at Thornfell Pass

The mountain did not want me there.

I felt it in the way the road narrowed after the last farmstead, hedges giving way to scree, the pines pressing close like witnesses who'd rather not be named. Thornfell Pass had a reputation that kept honest people out and dishonest ones comfortable, and I was neither, which made me exactly the sort of courier the crown liked to send: a woman whose face no one remembered because her job was to be forgotten at every table she served.

Three days from the capital, two mules loaded with silver and salt, and a letter of passage signed by the queen's own steward — that was my armor. It would stop a tax collector. It would not stop a knife.

The waystation appeared around a bend where the map in my saddlebag said it should: a low stone building with a slate roof, built into the hillside so the snow slid off it in winter. Except the roof wasn't slate anymore. It was char, and part of it had fallen inward, and the smell of burnt thatch still hung in the cold air like a guest who wouldn't leave.

I reined up and listened.

Wind. A crow somewhere above the tree line, complaining about something. The mules shifting their weight, wanting water.

No voices. No horses.

I should have ridden on. That's what the careful Quentin Reyes would have done — the woman who'd spent eleven years learning that curiosity was just poison you fed yourself on purpose. But the burned waystation sat there with its door hanging open like a mouth mid-sentence, and I'd come all this way to negotiate with smugglers who wouldn't meet me for another day, and the truth was simpler than any of my reasons:

I wanted to know.

* * *

Inside, the fire had started in the east room and eaten westward. Someone had fought it — wet blankets lay stiff as shrouds across the floorboards — but they'd fought it late. The common table was a black skeleton. A kettle had melted into a shape like a weeping animal.

And against the far wall, half behind an overturned bench, a man was dying.

He heard me before he saw me. His hand went under his coat, found whatever was there or didn't find it, and came back empty. He was maybe fifty, gray-stubbled, dressed like a drover but with boots too good for droving. The left side of his coat was dark and stiff. Blood, old enough to have stopped being dramatic. The kind of wound that kills slowly and lets you think the whole time.

"Thornfell," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "You've come through Thornfell."

"I'm expected at Voss's crossing tomorrow." I kept my hands visible, my tone flat. In the capital, flatness was currency. "If you're one of hers—"

"I'm not one of anyone's." He tried to laugh and it turned into coughing, wet and deep. "That's rather the point."

I should have gone for help. There was a shepherd's hut two miles back; I could have fetched water, a blanket, someone with more skill than a food-taster's knowledge of what kills people. Instead I knelt, because he was looking at me with an expression I recognized from the queen's sickbed last spring — the look of a man doing arithmetic with the time he has left.

"You're hurt."

"Observant. They said you'd be observant." His eyes sharpened, which shouldn't have been possible in a man that pale. "The taster. They said the crown sends its taster now. Reyes."

My spine went cold. Nobody on these roads should know my name. "Who told you that?"

"A friend in the palace who owes me." He moved then, slow as a glacier, and pulled something from inside his coat. A leather roll, tied with waxed cord, stained dark along one edge. He held it out across the ruined bench between us. "Take it."

"I haven't agreed to take anything."

"No. But you will." He pushed it closer anyway, until it touched my knee. "Because in about ten minutes there are going to be royal riders coming up this pass, and they are going to ask you if you've seen a man, and everything you do next depends on whether you're holding this or whether they find it on my body."

Outside, the crow had gone quiet.

"What is it?" I asked.

"A map." He smiled, and there was blood on his teeth, and the smile was the worst thing in the room. "Not of roads. Of *remembering*. Do you know what the Veilstone is, taster?"

The word landed somewhere under my ribs. I'd never heard it. And yet some animal part of my brain flinched, the way it flinched at bitter almond in sweet wine — recognition without source.

"No," I said.

"Good. Better if you don't pretend." He gripped the bench to drag himself straighter, and the effort cost him; his breath went ragged. "Thirty years ago there was a valley east of here. People in it. Ten thousand souls, give or take. There was a war over their border, ugly, generations long, and it ended all at once — everyone says it ended because wise men grew tired. Wise men." Another cough. "It ended because someone stole the thing that made both sides remember why they hated each other. Took the grievance out of the world's pocket. And took the people too. Whole villages. Names. Songs. Gone so clean that even the grief is gone. You can't mourn what you can't remember."

"That's not possible."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. And yet."

Hoofbeats. Distant, but coming — the particular iron rhythm of shod horses moving fast on packed earth, and more than two. My body knew the count before my mind did: four riders, maybe five.

The man saw my face change and nodded, satisfied, as if I'd passed a test I hadn't known I was sitting. "There it is. So. Last chance to be nobody, taster. Take the roll. Walk out. Tell them you found a dead man in a burned station and nothing else. They'll search you, probably. Be insulted. Tasters are used to being searched." His hand closed over mine around the leather, and his grip was still shockingly strong. "Don't let them have it. Whatever they offer. Whatever they threaten. It's not peace they're protecting. It's a lie, and the lie is load-bearing."

"Whose side are you on?" I whispered.

"The dead's." He let go and slumped back against the wall, arranging himself with terrible deliberation — head tipped, arm loose, a man asleep or past sleeping. "Go. Through the back. The smoke hole. Then come around front like you just arrived. Make them tell you he's dead before you seem to notice. Never volunteer a corpse."

The hoofbeats were closer. I stuffed the roll inside my coat, against my ribs, where I'd hidden contraband antidotes for years — the body remembers its habits even when the mind is screaming.

At the smoke hole I paused. "What's your name?"

He almost laughed again. "Wouldn't mean anything to you. Nothing does, anymore. That's rather the whole tragedy." Then, quieter, almost gentle: "You taste for a living, girl. Trust your tongue. If something tastes of forgetting — spit it out."

I went through the hole into the cold.

* * *

Five riders. I counted them as I led the mules around the bend at a walk, unhurried, a tradeswoman with nowhere urgent to be. They wore no livery, which meant they were the crown's, because only the crown could afford to dress its knives as civilians. Their leader was a lean man with a courtier's soft hands and a soldier's eyes, and he looked at me the way Lord Corvane's people always looked at servants: as furniture that might have overheard something.

"Rider," he said. No greeting. "Seen anyone on the pass today?"

"Only crows." I let mild annoyance sit on my face, the expression of a woman interrupted on business. "Why? Has someone gone missing?"

He ignored the question, which was answer enough, and swung down. Two of his men went toward the waystation. One drifted toward my mules, and I watched him run his hands along the packs with professional boredom while I stood very still and thought about the leather roll pressed against my ribs and how a search would end my life in roughly four seconds.

The rider came back from the station and shook his head once. The leader's jaw tightened.

"Station's burned," he said to me, conversationally now, which was worse. "Man inside. Dead."

"Gods keep him." I crossed myself with the wrong hand — deliberately, a small flaw, because perfect piety reads as performance — and clucked the mules forward. "Was it the fever? There's been fever in the low valleys."

"It was a knife, actually."

"How dreadful." I did not stop walking. "Well. I'm due at Voss's crossing by dusk, and Maren Voss doesn't forgive tardiness, so if there's nothing else—"

"There might be." He fell into step beside my mule, and now his voice dropped into the register men use when they think they're being generous. "You're crown-bonded. Passage letter, right? Steward's seal?" I nodded. "Then hear advice from a friend of the household. If anyone on this mountain offers you anything — a package, a message, a favor to carry — you bring it to us. There's a reward in it. A good one. Enough to buy out a bond."

Enough to buy freedom. He'd done his homework. He knew exactly what a bonded servant dreams about at three in the morning.

"I carry salt and silver and the steward's complaints," I said. "That's all I am to anyone."

"Mm." He stepped back and gestured his men clear of the road, courteous as a dance partner. "Safe travels, mistress. Mind the pass. It has a way of putting things in people's hands that were never meant for them."

I rode. I did not look back, because looking back is a confession, and I kept my breathing even and my face bored until the bend swallowed the riders, and then another mile, and another, until the trees closed behind me like a door.

Only then did my hands start shaking.

I stopped at a stream where the road dipped, watered the mules, and finally untied the roll with fingers that wouldn't quite obey. Inside the leather: a map, inked on skin-soft vellum, dense with roads and ridgelines I half-knew and places I didn't know at all. Villages marked east of the pass where every census I'd ever filed showed empty forest. A valley with a name written in a script I couldn't read, though the letters nagged at me like a word on the tip of the tongue.

And beneath the valley, in a steady hand, two words in plain trade-script:

*The Veilstone.*

I sat by the stream for a long time, listening to the water, feeling the map's weight against my ribs even after I'd retied it and stowed it away. For eleven years I had been the most obedient creature in the kingdom — obedient because obedience was the price of survival, and survival was the only thing I'd ever owned outright.

I had just lied to the crown's hunters. Volunteered nothing. Hidden a dead man's legacy inside my coat while his killers thanked me for my time.

I waited to feel terror.

Terror was there. But underneath it, faint and shocking as sugar in a savory dish, was something else — something that tasted, absurdly, like the first sip of wine after a long illness.

Tomorrow I'd reach Voss's crossing. Tomorrow I'd have to decide who, if anyone, learned what I carried.

Tonight, for the first time in my careful life, I had a secret that belonged to me.
