## 3. What the Map Remembers

The map smelled of blood and tallow and something older underneath, like a cellar that had been shut for a lifetime.

Quentin unrolled it for the fourth time that night, flat across the floor of the dye-house loft where she'd been sleeping since the tasting — since Corvane's men had begun asking their quiet questions in the kitchens. The loft belonged to a widow who took coin and asked nothing, which was the only kind of landlord worth having in Thornfell. Rain worked at the roof. A single lamp burned low, because light through cracks had a way of becoming testimony.

She'd told herself she was only studying it. That was the lie she'd chosen, and she found she could carry it the way she carried all her lies now: carefully, like a full cup.

The map was good work. Better than good. The vellum had been cured by someone who knew their trade, and the ink lines were drawn with a surveyor's precision — elevation marks, water sources, the little cairn-symbols that meant safe crossing on the high passes. Whoever made this had walked every mile of it. She traced the route from Thornfell Pass down into the hill country south, where the smuggler roads braided and split like roots.

That was when she saw it again. The thing she'd been circling for two nights without letting herself look at straight.

A road left the pass at a place called Vael's Knee — a name she knew, a waypoint she'd used herself, haggling with mule drivers who asked no questions. On the map, from Vael's Knee, three roads descended into the southern valleys. Three roads, each marked with villages. *Hollowmere. Ashen Vale. Tern's Rest.* Little clusters of dots, each with a well-symbol, each with a bridge-mark over the River Sorrel.

Quentin sat back on her heels.

She knew the southern valleys. Not well — but the way a food-taster knows things, which is to say completely and without romance. For six years she had provisioned royal progressions, negotiated grain contracts, priced salt and smoked fish for journeys through half the borderlands. She had memorized toll schedules and granary ledgers the way other people memorized prayers. And she knew — with the flat certainty of a woman who had read the census rolls herself, twice, as part of her duties — that there were no villages in those valleys.

There was no *Hollowmere*. There was no *Tern's Rest*. There was barely a road; the crown maps showed the country south of Vael's Knee as empty grazing land, good for nothing, marked with the surveyor's laziest shorthand: *unimproved*.

But the River Sorrel was real. She checked it against her memory three times before she let herself believe it. The map placed four bridges on the Sorrel between the pass and the lowlands. Crown surveys recorded one.

Her hands had gone cold. She noticed it the way she noticed poison on her tongue — distantly, professionally, cataloguing the symptom before permitting herself the fear.

She got up, found her satchel, and pulled out the one document she owned that wasn't forged: her commission as royal provisioner, signed under seal, with the census abstracts appended. She'd been required to study them for her certification. She'd complained about it at the time.

By lamplight she compared them line by line. The map's roads matched old drover paths she'd half-remembered from other documents — paths that appeared in a wool-levy record from thirty years ago and then simply stopped appearing, as if the sheep had stopped existing, as if the road had grown over in a single season. Granary records from the same years showed shipments of barley *from* the southern valleys. Then nothing. Not a decline. Nothing. Records didn't do that. Ledgers didn't end mid-column unless someone ended them.

Someone had ended them.

Quentin sat very still and listened to the rain, and understood, in the slow way understanding comes when you've been poisoned often enough to know how it works — not all at once, but drop by drop, until the body accepts the toxin as itself — that she was looking at an erasure. Not a war's aftermath, not a famine's gap. An erasure. Clean, administrative, total. Villages unwritten. Bridges unsurveyed. People uncounted so thoroughly that even the absence had been tidied away.

And she had spent six years learning every number the crown kept, and she had never once noticed the shape of what it didn't keep.

*You're a fool,* she thought. *You're a careful fool, which is worse.*

Because here was the thing her mind kept returning to, worrying it like a bone: the dying man hadn't given her the map by accident. He'd been burned out of a waystation, hunted by royal riders, and he'd looked at her — a stranger, a woman in provisioning leathers — and chosen. He'd said *the Veilstone* like a man handing over a debt. Which meant he'd known what she was. Which meant someone had decided the queen's food-taster was the right courier for a thing the crown would kill to bury.

She did not want to think about who might have made that decision, or why, or what it made her now that she'd accepted the delivery.

The lamp guttered. Outside, far off, a dog barked twice and stopped, and Quentin found herself counting footsteps on the stair that weren't there.

Sleep, when it finally came, was thin and full of bridges.

* * *

The summons came at midmorning, delivered by a boy in palace grey who wouldn't meet her eyes.

*Lord Corvane requests the presence of Quentin Reyes, provisioner, at his offices within the hour.*

Not *invites*. Not the courtly fiction. Within the hour — meaning they already knew where she was, meaning the widow had been paid or frightened or both, meaning the dye-house loft had never been a hiding place at all, only a stage where she'd believed herself hidden.

Quentin dressed with care. She ate a plain breakfast of bread she'd tested herself, out of habit, and laughed at nothing, alone, at the habit. Then she rolled the map into its oilskin and thought about where to put it, and realized there was nowhere, that every hiding place in Thornfell was a place Corvane could buy, and so she did the only clever thing available: she sewed it into the lining of her own coat, against her back, where a body search would have to get intimate to find it and where its weight would remind her, with every step, exactly what she was carrying.

Then she went to be questioned.

Corvane's offices occupied the top floor of the Writ Tower, and the climb was deliberate — three turns of stairs, each landing watched, each door closed. By the time she reached the top she had counted eleven guards and been counted by all of them. The spymaster received her standing at a window, hands clasped behind him, looking out over Thornfell's smoke with the expression of a man reading a document he'd already memorized.

"Quentin Reyes." He didn't turn. "You saved the queen's life last month."

"I did my duty, my lord."

"You did. Nightshade in a Rhenish, subtle work, caught by tongue alone." Now he turned, and his eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice. "Which is why I've been puzzling over you. A woman of your precision doesn't wander into the mountains on provisioning business in winter. Doesn't lodge in a dye-house instead of her quarters. Doesn't come back down the pass a day ahead of her schedule with mud on her boots that matches no road in my ledgers."

"My lord—"

"I'm not finished." He crossed to his desk, unhurried, and lifted a single sheet. "Three days ago a man died in a burned waystation at Thornfell Pass. My riders reached him too late. He was known to us. A courier of certain materials. And in his last hours, it appears, he met someone. There was a second set of prints in the snow. Small boots. Provisioning leathers." He set the paper down. "I'm describing you, Mistress Reyes. I want to know if you'll describe him."

The room was warm and very quiet. Quentin felt the map's weight against her spine like a second heart.

"He was dying," she said. "He asked for water. I gave it. He died. I rode on, because there was nothing I could do and provisions don't wait."

"That's all?"

"That's all."

Corvane studied her for a long moment, and she understood that he was doing to her what she did to wine: tasting, testing, waiting for the false note. She kept her breathing even. She kept her hands loose. Six years of court tables had taught her that the body betrays first and the mouth follows, and she held hers still the way she'd hold still with arsenic dissolving on her tongue.

"A courier," Corvane said at last, softly, almost to himself. "Do you know what he carried, Mistress Reyes? What kind of material justifies burning a waystation and putting riders on a mountain road in winter?"

"No, my lord."

"Sedition," he said. "Old sedition. The kind that gets people killed — has gotten people killed, quietly, for longer than you've been alive." He came around the desk, and up close he smelled of ink and cold air, nothing else. "Here is what happens next. You will return to your duties. You will continue to be excellent at them. And if anything else should reach your ears — any name, any word, any *stone* — you will bring it to me before sunset of the day you hear it. In exchange, the queen continues to trust her taster, and no one examines the mud on anyone's boots."

It was not a bargain. It was a leash, offered politely, and they both knew she was already wearing it.

"And if I hear nothing?" she asked.

"Then you hear nothing." He smiled, faintly, without warmth. "But you're a woman who notices things, Quentin Reyes. It's your gift. So I'll say this once, as a kindness: whatever you think you've stumbled onto, it isn't a crime that was committed. It's a peace that was purchased. And peace, unlike crimes, cannot be solved. Only protected."

He dismissed her with a nod. At the door she paused, because some reckless instinct demanded it.

"The villages south of Vael's Knee," she said. "Hollowmere. Tern's Rest. Why aren't they in the census?"

For the first time, Lord Corvane looked genuinely interested. It lasted perhaps two seconds, and it was the most frightening thing in the room.

"There are no villages south of Vael's Knee," he said. "There never were. Go home, Mistress Reyes. Taste something. Be useful."

* * *

She walked down through the tower with the map burning against her back and Corvane's words repeating in her skull like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

*There are no villages. There never were.*

He hadn't denied knowing the names. That was the thing she turned over and over on the long walk down, past the guards, past the doors, out into the grey street where the rain had started again. He hadn't said *what villages?* He'd said *there never were* — the exact phrasing of a ledger, the exact phrasing of the census, the official sentence itself, recited by the man whose job was to know everything the kingdom pretended not to.

Which meant the erasure wasn't just in the records. It was enforced. Maintained. Patrolled, the way borders are patrolled, by people like him, with instruments like the ones that had burned a waystation around a dying man.

And she had just told the spymaster, to his face, that she knew the names.

Quentin stopped in the middle of the street, rain running down her face, and let herself feel it fully for the first time: not fear exactly, but the vertigo of a woman standing on a bridge that isn't on any map, over a river that officially has no crossings, in a country that has decided, with seals and signatures and thirty years of tidy silence, that ten thousand people never existed.

They would come for her now. Soon. Perhaps tonight.

Unless she moved first.

She turned south, toward the pass, and walked.
