## 4. The Woman Who Half-Remembers

Sera had been following the taster for three days, and in that time she'd learned four things.

One: the woman walked like a soldier pretending to be a servant — weight forward, eyes always moving, hands loose at her sides. Two: she never took the king's road when a worse one would do, which meant she knew the Thornfell country the way Sera knew her own knife handle. Three: she bought food but never ate it in front of strangers. Four: she was afraid, and she was good at it, and both of those things made her interesting.

Sera crouched in the bracken above the old drover's track and watched Quentin Reyes pick her way down toward the ford, a plain gray cloak over traveling clothes, a courier's satchel riding her hip. Alone. That was either brave or stupid, and Sera had learned you couldn't always tell the difference until the blood was on someone's hands.

The rumor had come to her in a border tavern from a one-eyed carter who talked too much: a palace taster carrying a dead man's map, roads on it that led nowhere, villages with no names. *Nowhere* was a word Sera had been chewing on her whole life. She was twenty-six years old and she could not tell you where she came from. She had a mother's lullaby in a language no scholar recognized, a scar on her palm from a door latch she couldn't picture, and dreams of a valley — green walls, white stone, a river running silver through the middle of it — that did not exist on any map she'd ever stolen or bought or begged to see.

She'd stopped asking people about it years ago. People looked at you strangely when you mourned a place that wasn't there.

The taster reached the ford and knelt to drink, and that was when Sera moved.

She came down through the bracken quiet as weather, crossed the last ten yards with her knife out and flat against her forearm, and put the point against the back of the woman's neck just below the collar.

"Don't," Sera said. "Drink later."

Quentin Reyes went very still. Not frozen — still the way a trap is still. "You're holding that wrong," she said. Her voice was calm. It should not have been calm. "If you mean to cut my throat, angle up and commit. If you don't, lower it before I decide for you."

"I mean to talk."

"Then talk with your knife where I can see it." The woman didn't turn her head. Water ran over her cupped hands and fell. "I've spent nine years tasting poison for a living. Do you know what that teaches you? That everything is about what's in something, never what's on it. So let me see what's in your intentions, stranger, or find another mark."

Sera almost laughed. She stepped back instead, keeping six feet between them, knife low. "Turn around. Slowly."

Quentin turned. Up close she was younger than her walk suggested, sharp-faced, with the kind of eyes that counted exits. She looked at Sera's knife, then her face, then the knife again, cataloguing. "You've been behind me since Millbrook."

"You noticed."

"I noticed someone. You're better than most." A pause. "You're not crown. Crown walks louder."

"And you're not just a taster." Sera let the blade sink into its sheath but kept her thumb on the guard. "A taster doesn't carry a dead man's map. A taster doesn't dodge every census checkpoint between here and the pass. Talk. The map. Where is it?"

Something flickered across the woman's face — quick, controlled, gone. "What map?"

"The one a dying man bled on in a burned waystation at Thornfell Pass. The one with roads leading to villages that aren't in any census in the kingdom." Sera took a step closer. "I've dreamed those villages. White stone. A river like a struck match. So you can lie to me if you want, but you'll be lying to the only person alive who knows what's on it without seeing it."

For a long moment the only sound was the ford chuckling over its stones. Then Quentin Reyes said, quietly: "Who are you?"

"My name's Sera. That's all of it there is. My mother sang me songs in a tongue that died before I was born, and I have half a childhood stuck in my head like a splinter, and I want to know who took the rest." She held out her hand, palm up. The old scar crossed it, pale in the morning light. "Show me the map."

"No."

"No?"

"Not here. Not standing in open ground with the road in sight of anyone riding by." The taster glanced upstream, downstream, at the tree line — counting exits again, Sera realized. Counting them for both of them. "Because here's what you don't know, woman-who-ambushes-strangers-at-fords: Lord Corvane's riders are two hours behind me, maybe less, and they're not following the map. They're following *me*. So we can stand here and negotiate, or we can get off this track and argue somewhere with a roof."

"Why would I go anywhere with you?"

"Because I know these roads and you don't, and because if Corvane takes me, he takes the map, and whatever's in your head dies with my silence in one of his rooms." Quentin shouldered her satchel. "Your choice. But choose walking."

*

They walked.

Sera kept her distance and her hand near her knife, and the taster kept up a pace that ate miles like they owed her money, and neither of them spoke for the first hour. The land climbed away from the ford into pine country, the light going green and dim under the branches, and twice Quentin left the trail entirely — once through a streambed for a quarter mile, once through a gap in a rock face so narrow Sera had to turn sideways and suck in her breath.

"That gap isn't on any map," Sera said when they came out the other side.

"No."

"How do you know it?"

"Smugglers' roads. I ran provisions through Thornfell for six years before the palace bought my tongue." Quentin didn't look back. "Everyone thinks the mountains are empty. They're full of people who paid to be forgotten too. Different reasons. Same result."

That word — *forgotten* — landed in Sera's chest like a dropped stone. She said nothing. But she watched the woman differently after that.

They made camp at dusk in a hollow beneath an overhang, a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand, and shared dried meat and hard bread in silence. It was Sera who broke it, because silence had never been her talent.

"The man at the waystation. Did he say anything? Before he died?"

Quentin fed a twig to the flames. "A name. 'The Veilstone.' And—" she hesitated, and Sera saw the hesitation cost her something, "—he said, 'Give it to the taster. She'll know why.'"

"Do you know why?"

"No." The word came out flat and final, a door shut. But her hand had gone to her collarbone, touching nothing, some habit Sera couldn't read. "Ask me again when I understand it myself."

"He knew you, then. Chose you."

"So it seems." Quentin looked into the fire. "Which means whoever he was, he trusted a stranger more than his own allies. That's either very moving or very damning, and I haven't decided which."

"It's both," Sera said. "It usually is."

The taster glanced at her then — really looked, for the first time since the ford. "Tell me about the valley. The one you dream."

So Sera told her. The white stone houses with their blue doors. The terraced orchards climbing the valley walls. The bridge with seven arches where old men played a game with black and white stones. Her mother's voice singing words she couldn't translate, only feel — a rising phrase like a bird going up, a falling one like water going over. The scar on her palm from a door latch painted with a sunburst.

And Quentin listened the way nobody had ever listened to Sera talk about it — not with pity, not with that careful blankness people wore when they thought you were mad, but with recognition. With dread.

"When I studied the map," Quentin said slowly, "there were roads on it. Good roads, well-traveled roads, drawn with care. They led to places marked with names. And I went looking for those names in every census roll I could reach, and they aren't there. Not recorded as destroyed. Not recorded as abandoned. Just—" she turned her hand over, palm up, empty, "—never. As if the ink refused them."

"The Veilstone," Sera whispered. The word felt older in her mouth than she was. "It didn't destroy them. It erased them. From paper. From memory. From—"

"From everything except you." Quentin's eyes were bright and terrible in the firelight. "Why you? Why does a child of a vanished people remember fragments?"

"I don't know." Sera's hand had closed around the scarred palm without her noticing. "Maybe the stone slipped. Maybe my mother did something. Maybe—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Maybe I'm what's left when you try to erase a whole people and miss a corner of one girl's mind."

The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"We should sleep in shifts," Quentin said at last, practical as armor going back on. "You first watch. Wake me at moonrise."

Sera nodded. But as the taster rolled into her cloak, Sera said, "Quentin. Why are you doing this? You could burn the map tonight and be safe by morning. Go back to being owned and fed."

A long silence. When Quentin answered, she faced the fire, and Sera watched the truth come out of her sideways, the way truths leave people who aren't practiced at having them.

"Because I checked the census rolls," she said, "and then I sat in my room in the palace and realized that I check things for a living. Poisons, plots, faces. And I checked this, and it's true, and everyone above me wants it buried, and I have spent my whole life making sure other people's secrets stay swallowed." Her jaw tightened. "I'm tired of swallowing."

Sera looked at her — this strange, guarded, precise woman who lied badly and walked like a soldier and had broken her whole careful life in the space of one dying man's breath — and felt something shift in her chest that she refused, for now, to name.

"Moonrise," Sera said. "Sleep."

She took the first watch with her knife across her knees and the valley in her head, and she did not dream, and that was its own kind of mercy.

It was deep in the second watch — Quentin's watch, though Sera woke anyway, the way she always woke, silent and instant — that Quentin's hand closed over her shoulder in the dark.

"Riders," the taster breathed. "Three of them. Lanterns dark. Coming up the drover's track."

Sera was already rolling to her feet. Below them, faint as breath, she heard it: the creak of leather, the muffled fall of hooves on soft earth. Someone had found the ford. Someone had guessed the gap in the rocks.

Or someone had followed the only two people in the world who knew what the map remembered.

"Back way?" Sera whispered.

"There's always a back way." Quentin was already moving, satchel tight against her ribs, eyes counting exits in the dark. "Stay close. And Sera — whatever you hear behind us, don't stop."

They went down into the mountain together, two women with half a secret each, while the lanterns came on behind them like eyes opening.
