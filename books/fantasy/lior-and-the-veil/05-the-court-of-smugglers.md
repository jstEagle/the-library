## 5. The Court of Smugglers

The door to the freehold was a lie, and Quentin respected it for that.

From the trail it looked like nothing — a shelf of granite leaning against the mountain's flank, half-swallowed by pine, with a deadfall of storm-broken trees stacked against it as if the mountain had simply given up here. Sera walked past it twice before Quentin caught the detail that mattered: no frost on the stones near the base, though everything else wore a rime of white. Warm air was breathing out of the rock.

"There," she said.

Sera looked where she pointed, then at her. "You're certain you've never run these roads?"

"I tasted for the queen for six years. Before that I grew up in Thornfell's shadow, trading with people like whoever lives behind that rock." Quentin touched the cold stone, found the seam, found the iron ring set flush into it, and pulled. "Smugglers don't hide from the crown. They hide *in* it."

The passage beyond sloped down into warmth and lamplight and the smell of woodsmoke, roasting meat, and tar. Quentin's stomach clenched around its own emptiness. They had eaten hardtack and snowmelt for two days, moving only at night, and her body — trained to detect poison in parts per million — had begun cataloguing its own failures: hands less steady, thoughts arriving a half-second late.

Sera went first through the last turn, knife already loose in her sleeve, because that was how Sera did everything. Then she stopped so abruptly Quentin nearly walked into her back.

They were standing at the edge of a cavern that had been made into a town. Not a camp — a town. Levels cut into the living rock, stairways zigzagging between them, lanterns strung on lines like a festival that had decided to become permanent. There were forges, a tannery, a shrine with no god Quentin recognized, and a long hall whose doors stood open onto firelight and noise. Perhaps two hundred people. Children running between the stalls of an indoor market. A woman arguing cheerfully about the price of nails.

"Thornfell Pass is a graveyard of burned waystations," Sera said slowly. "I've been through it three times."

"You went through the pass." Quentin stepped down into the light. "This is under it."

* * *

Maren Voss received them in the long hall, at the head of a table scarred by decades of knives, and Quentin understood within thirty seconds that she was not speaking to a bandit chief. Maren was fifty-odd and built like a keel, gray shot through her black braid, and she listened the way a magistrate listens — which was worse than any interrogation Quentin had ever endured at court. Around the table sat eight others, and every one of them had counted the knives on their persons and the knives on theirs.

"The man who sent you," Maren said. "Describe him again."

"Forties. Burn-scarred left hand. Spoke Valeth-accented trade tongue. He'd been tortured — fingers, mostly. He called himself—"

"Wren." Maren said it flatly, but Quentin saw her knuckles whiten on her cup. "His name was Wren."

"You knew him."

"I knew him when he was eleven years old and stealing my apples." Maren set the cup down with great care, the way people do when the alternative is throwing it. "He left twenty years ago. Sent word twice since. The second time was a month ago, saying he'd found something worth dying for, and asking if my roads were still open." Her eyes came up, sharp as a gutting blade. "And now you're sitting at my table wearing his map inside your coat, and a stranger beside you who smells of the Vale, and royal hunters behind you both. So. Tell me why I shouldn't sell you to Corvane and be done with it."

Quentin had faced down queens' glares and spymasters' silences. She reached inside her coat — watched four hands move to hilts and chose to keep moving anyway — and laid the leather map on the table between them.

"Because he didn't send me," she said. "He was dying when I found him. He gave me this because I was the only person in Thornfell Pass who wasn't hunting him. And because—" she hesitated, then told the truth, which still felt like a transgression even now, "—because I think he chose me. I don't know why yet. But men who are tortured for days don't give away their life's work to the first warm body. He asked my name first. He asked if I served the crown. I lied. He smiled."

Silence around the table. Somewhere below, a hammer rang against an anvil, patient as a heartbeat.

Then one of the eight — an old woman with a burn-scarred jaw — began, very quietly, to laugh. It wasn't a kind sound. "Wren," she said. "Of course he did. Of course he gave it to a poison-taster."

"Maren." She leaned forward. "Show them. If we're going to die for this, we should at least all know what for."

* * *

What Maren showed them was not the map.

It was a ledger — a real one, kept in a strongbox under the freehold, written in three hands across forty years. Quentin read by lantern-light while Sera stood at her shoulder, too close, breathing too fast, and Maren narrated in the voice of someone reciting a confession they'd rehearsed their whole life.

"Sixty years ago there was a war. You won't find it in any chronicle, because there's nothing to find. Two kingdoms — the crown's ancestors and the Valeth high clans — fought over the Thornfell valleys for nine years. Grain country. Water country. Nine years of burning each other's harvests until both sides were starving and hating, and the hatred was the only crop either of them had left."

"And then?" Sera's voice came out strange.

"And then a conclave of mages on both sides did the unforgivable merciful thing. They took the thing both nations were killing each other over — the Veilstone, a relic that anchored the old treaties, the old claims, the old grievances — and they used it to make everyone forget. Not the war. The *reason*. Every mind in both realms lost the memory of what the valleys were for, who had held them, what had been promised and broken. Without the grievance, there was no war. Armies woke up in fields full of corpses and couldn't remember why they were angry. They signed a peace because peace was the only story left that made sense."

Quentin stared at the ledger. "The villages. On the map. The ones that aren't in any census."

"The Valeth weren't erased by conquest, girl. They were erased by *accounting*. When the forgetting settled, the border was redrawn and the Valeth enclaves simply... dropped out of the records. No census. No deeds. No names in the parish books. People woke up in houses that belonged to no one, in villages that had never existed, and most of them drifted into the cities and became no one quietly, over a generation or two." Maren tapped the ledger. "Except some remembered. Fragments. Blood remembers what ink refuses to. My grandmother was Valeth. She used to sing songs in a language she said she'd never learned."

Beside Quentin, Sera made a small sound — not a word, barely breath — and put her hand flat on the table as if the floor had tilted.

"That's why the fragments survive," Quentin said. "The stone didn't erase. It *veiled*. The memories are still in there. Buried."

"Now you understand why Corvane wants the map burned and the vault sealed forever." Maren's voice hardened. "Because if anyone restores the stone — wakes it, turns it back — every buried grievance comes up with the memories. Nine years of war-crimes, on both sides. Every burned harvest, every massacre, every debt. The peace doesn't just end. It inverts. Thousands die so that ten thousand can be *remembered*."

"And if no one does anything," Sera said, very quietly, "the Valeth stay forgotten. Forever. A whole people, a rounding error in a treaty."

Nobody answered her. The fire cracked. Down in the market, someone laughed at something, ordinary and human, and the sound rose up through the cavern like proof of something.

* * *

They bartered passage after midnight.

Maren wanted the map — Quentin refused. Maren wanted Quentin's knowledge of the crown's supply routes — Quentin gave it, route by route, checkpoint by checkpoint, watching the smuggler queen's cartographer mark them in red and feeling the last of her old life drain out through her mouth. In exchange: guides, provisions, and a road south that no royal rider had ever found, toward the heart of the map, toward whatever waited beneath the erased Vale.

It was past the second bell when Quentin finally sat down by the great hearth in the long hall, too tired to move, and discovered that Sera had saved her a place. A bowl of stew sat on the bench. Someone — she never learned who — had also left a cup of the rough plum brandy the freehold distilled, and when she drank it her trained tongue catalogued every impurity with helpless precision and her exhausted body thanked the gods for all of them.

"They sing the songs here," Sera said. "The ones your Maren's grandmother sang. Half the words are wrong. But the children learn them."

"You knew that before tonight."

"I knew the *shape* of it." Sera turned her cup in her fingers. "I have a memory of a kitchen. Green tiles. Someone singing. I've carried it since I was small and I always assumed it was mine, just... misplaced. Tonight I heard a woman down in the market humming the same tune with different words, and I thought—" Her jaw tightened. "I thought, that's not my kitchen. That's my mother's mother's kitchen. That's a kitchen that was stolen out of my head and left there anyway."

Quentin looked into the fire. Six years she had defined herself by what she could endure — every poison, every suspicion, every gilded humiliation. And here was a woman whose entire inheritance was a theft so complete she hadn't known she'd been robbed.

"We'll get it back," Quentin said. "Whatever it costs."

"Don't promise that." Sera's voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp. "You don't know yet what it costs. Neither do I."

Later — much later, when the hall had gone quiet and the embers breathed like sleeping animals — Quentin lay on a pallet among strangers and realized she felt safer than she had in six years of palace service. These people owed her nothing. That was precisely why the safety was real.

She was nearly asleep when boots stopped beside her pallet.

Maren Voss crouched, lantern low, and her face in the light was grim as winter.

"Rider came in through the north crawl an hour ago," she said. "One of my own. Says there's a crown column moving on the pass — Corvane's colors, quiet approach, no banners. And Quentin—" she paused, and Quentin felt the floor of the world tilt, "—he says they're not looking for a map anymore. They're looking for *you*. By name. And there's a reward notice going through my market right now, and the face on it isn't yours."

Whose face, then? But Maren was already answering, before Quentin could ask.

"It's the dead man's," she said. "It's Wren. They're circulating sketches of Wren — alive, healthy, twenty years younger — and offering blood-price for anyone harboring his kin."

The lantern swayed. Somewhere in the dark of the hall, Sera sat up.

"Kin," Quentin repeated.

Maren looked at her for a long moment, and Quentin watched the arithmetic finish behind the smuggler queen's eyes — the same arithmetic that had finished in her own chest, cold and total, the moment the word landed.

"He chose you," Maren said quietly. "He didn't choose a courier. He chose his sister."
