## 6. Corvane's Bargain

They took her in the space between one breath and the next, on a stretch of road where the pines pressed close and the light went green and dim.

Quentin had known something was wrong for half a mile — the birdsong that stopped too cleanly, the absence of cart-ruts on a road that should have carried smuggler traffic by now. She'd been reaching for Sera's arm to say *run* when the net came down from the branches overhead.

Then rope, a hood that smelled of tallow and old wool, and the long swaying dark of being carried like cargo.

She counted her own heartbeat to stay sane. Four hundred beats before the ground leveled. Six hundred before she smelled woodsmoke and wet stone. Nine hundred before they set her down on a chair and someone cinched her wrists to its arms with a courtesy that was somehow worse than violence.

The hood came off.

She was in a hunting lodge — or what had been built to look like one. The beams were honest oak, the fire was real, but the room beyond the doorway had the particular stillness of a place where men stood guard and did not shift their weight. A table. Two chairs. On the table, a decanter of wine and two glasses, arranged with the precision of an accusation.

Lord Corvane sat across from her.

She had seen him perhaps six times in nine years at court, always at a distance: a grey man in a grey coat, standing near doorways, watching. Up close he was smaller than rumor made him, with a scholar's stooped shoulders and eyes the color of river ice — pale, and moving underneath, as if something lived below the surface.

"Quentin Reyes," he said. "The queen's taster."

"My lord."

"You're not afraid." He sounded mildly interested, the way a man might note unusual weather.

"I'm terrified, my lord."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth. "Good. Fear keeps people honest. Wine?" He poured without waiting for her answer and set the glass near her bound hand. "It's from the queen's own cellar. I had it tasted before it traveled. By three separate men, none of whom knew the others were tasting."

"That's very thorough."

"I'm a thorough man. It's why I'm still alive, and why so many cleverer people are not." He settled back. "You detected nightshade in the queen's cup four months ago. You've survived nine years in a court where tasters die at roughly triple the rate of footmen. You speak four languages, you can read a ledger faster than the treasury clerks, and until eleven days ago, your record showed no lies whatsoever."

*Until eleven days ago.* They'd been watching since Thornfell Pass. Perhaps longer.

"What do you want, my lord?"

"The truth, mostly. And the map." He said it the way other men asked about the weather. "You have it hidden somewhere clever. Somewhere along the smuggler roads, I'd guess, given how fluently you lied to my riders about which passes you intended to take. Don't look surprised — the woman who travels with you told us quite a lot before she escaped. Mostly insults, but some geography."

His casual mention of Sera sent a cold thread down Quentin's spine. *Escaped.* So she was alive. Free. Possibly already circling back for her.

"I don't know what map you mean."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't." He rose, crossed to a writing desk against the wall, and returned with a leather folio, which he opened and turned toward her. "But let me show you what I know. Then we'll see whether you keep lying out of habit or conviction."

* * *

The records were meticulous. That was the worst part — not threats, not torture, just paper, organized with a bureaucrat's love.

Corvane laid them out one by one, narrating in the flat tone of a man reading a casualty list.

"The last war. Forty-one years ago. The kingdom of Vaelor against the Thorn Marches alliance. Eleven years of fighting. Here—" he tapped a page, "—the siege of Carrow's Ford. Four thousand dead in a winter. Here, the burning of the Valeth granaries, which caused this famine, which killed another nine thousand, most of them children under ten. Here is the treaty that ended it. And here—"

He paused. For the first time, something moved behind the ice of his eyes.

"—here is the order authorizing the Veilstone's removal. Signed by both crowns. The artifact that made every soul in the realm forget what the war was fought over. Not the war itself — just the reason. The grievance. The name of the valley both sides claimed. Take away the wound, and the scar stops aching. People forgot why their fathers died, so they stopped sending their sons."

Quentin looked at the page. The seal of the old king sat beside the seal of a Marcher lord she'd never heard of, over text written in a careful clerk's hand: *that the cause thereof be struck from all remembrance, living and written.*

"And the Valeth?" she said quietly. "The people who lived there?"

"Collateral," Corvane said. "The stone couldn't erase a grievance without erasing the people attached to it. Ten thousand souls, give or take, folded out of memory. Their villages stand empty in a valley no one remembers existing. Their descendants scattered and forgot themselves. Some of them are serving in the palace guard right now, guarding a crown that erased their grandmothers, and sleeping soundly while they do it." He closed the folio. "I've read those records forty times, Mistress Reyes. I am not proud of what my predecessors did. But I am going to tell you exactly what happens if you undo it."

He began counting on his fingers, and each number landed like a stone.

"There are perhaps sixty thousand people alive today who descend from Valeth lines, most of them unaware. When the stone restores the memory, they will learn their homeland was stolen. The Marcher lords whose grandparents signed the theft are still in power; their heirs hold those same valleys. The Vaelor crown has garrisoned the border for forty years on the assumption the border dispute is dead. It will not be dead. It will wake up furious."

He leaned forward.

"I have projections. Grain reserves in the Marches are at a twenty-year low. If the border lords mobilize next spring, there will be famine by autumn regardless of who wins. My conservators estimate ninety thousand dead in the first three years. Ninety thousand, Mistress Reyes. Children again. Granaries again. Carrow's Ford again — except this time everyone will remember exactly why they're dying, and hate each other for it, forever."

The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, a guard coughed.

"So here is my bargain." Corvane slid a single sheet of paper across the table. "Amnesty. Full pardon for the theft of state documents, the false statements to my riders, all of it. A stipend of two hundred crowns a year for life. A house in the capital, if you want it, or passage anywhere in the world. In exchange: the map, the woman if you can find her, and your silence. The stone stays buried. The peace holds. Ninety thousand people go on living their small, ignorant, unremembering lives."

Quentin stared at the paper. Her hands were still tied to the chair, which she suspected was deliberate — he wanted her to read the offer while helpless, wanted the humiliation braided into the temptation.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I burn the freehold network out of Thornfell pass by pass, arrest everyone who ever carried anything for Maren Voss, and find the map anyway. It may take a year. People will die who didn't need to. But I'll have it." He said it without heat, without apology. "I'm not threatening you, Mistress Reyes. I'm describing arithmetic."

* * *

They untied her for the meal. She noted that — noted everything, because noting things was the only weapon she'd ever owned. Roast fowl, bread, the same wine. Corvane ate with her, which she understood was also deliberate: a man unafraid to share her food, mirroring her own profession back at her.

"You poison yourself," he said, halfway through. "Small doses, years of them. Arsenic, I think, and something botanical I can't identify. Your physician's records were interesting reading."

Her stomach went cold. "That's private."

"It was. Nothing at court is." He set down his knife. "Do you know why I find it fascinating? You built an immunity so that no one could kill you. You made yourself into a person who cannot be poisoned. And now you're sitting in my lodge deciding whether to hand me a map that would poison an entire realm — slowly, painlessly, invisibly — with a grievance instead of arsenic. You of all people should understand what I'm offering. Peace through managed dosage. A little harm, controlled, to prevent catastrophe."

"That's not what the stone did."

"No?"

"The stone didn't dose anyone. It *took* something. Ten thousand people didn't get a little harm. They got erased. Their names, their graves, their mothers' faces — gone. That's not medicine, my lord. That's amputation, and then telling the limb it never existed."

For a moment the ice in his eyes shifted, and she thought — hoped — she saw something almost like pain.

"Yes," he said quietly. "It was a monstrous thing. I've never pretended otherwise. I've simply asked myself, every day for twenty years: monstrous compared to what? Because the alternative was eleven more years of war, and the alternative to keeping it buried is ninety thousand corpses. Show me the clean choice, Mistress Reyes, and I will kneel to it. I have been looking for one since I was a young man in uniform at Carrow's Ford, pulling children out of the snow." He rose, collected the folio, and paused at the door. "You'll have a horse and an escort to the crossroads. After that, you're free to go — to me, to Voss, to the ends of the earth. Consider it a test of loyalty, if you like. Or consider it the only honest offer anyone has made you in nine years of service."

"Which is it?"

He almost smiled. "Both. That's rather the point."

* * *

They released her at dusk where the road forked above the river, one branch running black toward Thornfell, the other toward the distant lights of the capital. The escort peeled away without a word. Her horse was hers again, her knives returned, even the hidden coin in her boot left untouched. Everything done correctly. Everything designed to make her feel the debt.

Quentin sat her horse at the crossroads for a long time as the light failed.

Two hundred crowns a year. A house. A clean conscience, or the appearance of one. All she had to do was surrender a scrap of leather and let ten thousand dead stay conveniently dead — and ninety thousand living people stay conveniently alive.

And if she refused: Maren's people hunted, the roads burned, Sera run to ground somewhere in the high passes, and the map found anyway, after enough blood to make Corvane's arithmetic come true twice over.

There was no side that didn't bleed. There was only the question of whose blood, and how much, and who got to decide. She had spent nine years tasting other people's poisons. Now the cup was in front of her, and she was the one holding it, and every path forward tasted of iron.

*Unless.*

The thought arrived quietly, the way dangerous thoughts do.

Corvane wanted the stone destroyed or buried. Sera wanted it restored. Both of them assumed the stone itself was the prize — restore it, break it, hide it. But the dying man hadn't said *restore the Veilstone*. He'd said *the Veilstone*, and pressed the map into *her* hands, a taster, a woman whose whole life was built on knowing exactly how much poison a body could carry and live.

What if the stone wasn't meant to be restored at all? What if it was meant to be *used* — differently, carefully, dosed?

She didn't know yet. But she knew someone who might: a smuggler queen who had known the dying man, and a woman who remembered fragments of a mother's voice in an empty valley.

Quentin turned her horse toward Thornfell, toward the dark mouth of the pass, and rode.

Behind her, at the treeline, a rider in grey watched her go — and did not follow.
