## 7. The Vale That Was

The map had lied to them for three days.

Not lied, exactly — Sera corrected herself, for the hundredth time, as she crouched at the edge of the tree line with Quentin breathing hard beside her. It had told the truth in a language neither of them could read until now. The road on the map ran straight through a wall of granite that did not exist, and the granite was not there to stop them. It was there because whoever drew the map had drawn what *was*, not what anyone else could see.

"Tell me again," Quentin said. She'd stopped arguing about it somewhere past dawn, but her voice still carried the frayed edge of a woman whose careful world kept refusing to hold its shape. "You're sure."

Sera didn't answer right away. She was looking at the valley.

It sat below them like a held breath — green and gold and utterly wrong. Not empty. That was the thing no one would ever believe. The valley floor was stitched with hedgerows and stone walls, orchards gone feral, a river bending through it in a lazy silver S. There were roofs. Dozens of roofs, slate and thatch, chimneys standing over cold hearths. A village, then another, strung along the water like beads on a thread. A bell tower with no bell.

And no smoke. No birdsong from the fields. No dogs. Nothing moved except wind through barley that had never been harvested and never would be.

"It's been standing here the whole time," Sera said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, as if the valley were already pressing down on her throat. "Thirty years. Right under everyone's eyes. People have probably picnicked on that ridge." She pointed at a flat shelf of rock above the nearest village. "They've looked at this and seen nothing worth seeing. Their minds just... slid off it."

"Like water off wax," Quentin murmured. She was rubbing her arms against a chill that had nothing to do with the morning. "I read every census in the chancery archive, Sera. Every tax roll for three provinces. There is no valley here. There's a blank page where this should be, and the blank page has an official seal on it."

"I know what's on the page." Sera stood. Her legs felt like they belonged to someone taller, someone who had walked down into that valley a thousand times and knew which gate hinge sang. "My mother is down there. Or was. Or—" She cut herself off before her voice could crack on the grammar of it. Dead or alive or erased, the words refused to sit still.

Quentin touched her elbow, light as a moth landing. "We don't have to go fast."

"Yes we do." Sera shrugged the touch off, gently, and started down the slope. "Corvane's riders are half a day behind us and Maren's people are scattered to the four winds. We go fast."

* * *

The first house they reached stood at the valley's mouth, where the smuggler road became something older and better made — fitted stone, cambered for drainage, the work of people who had expected to live here forever. Sera knew the road. That was the horror of it. She had never walked it in her life, and she knew it. Her boots found the loose stone by the third milestone without being told.

The house had a blue door.

She stopped in front of it the way you stop at the edge of a cliff. Blue paint, sun-faded to the color of a robin's egg, peeling at the corners. Someone had painted over the old paint without priming, lazy and hurried, and beneath the newest layer a darker blue showed through like a bruise.

"Sera?" Quentin's voice, far away.

"My mother painted this door." The words came out of her before she decided to say them. "Every spring. She said a blue door meant the house remembered you even when you forgot yourself." Sera pressed her palm flat against the wood. It was warm from the sun. Of course it was warm. Houses were warm. Houses were always warm, why would she ever have thought otherwise—

The memory came up through her hand like water through a well rope.

*A kitchen. Flour on the knuckles of a woman singing low and off-key, a song with words in a language Sera's mouth shaped along with before her mind caught up. The woman turned, flour-dusted, laughing at whatever small disaster Sera had brought in from outside — a broken egg, a torn hem, a frog in a pocket — and said,* **Valethi, you bring the whole wild world home with you** *, and the endearment landed in Sera's chest like a coal.*

She came back to herself kneeling in the dirt of the dooryard, and Quentin was holding her shoulders, saying her name.

"She called me Valethi." Sera heard her own voice as if from across the valley. "It means — I don't know what it means. But it's mine. It's *mine*, Quentin, they took the word out of my mouth and it grew back anyway, like—"

"Like the memories did," Quentin said quietly. "Half-remembered. Fragments. You said the Veil couldn't reach everything."

"It reached almost everything." Sera wiped her face with the heel of her hand and was surprised to find it wet. "Almost. It left splinters. And splinters work their way out."

She stood, pushed open the blue door, and went into her mother's house.

* * *

Inside, the dust lay thick as snowfall, undisturbed for thirty years, and that was its own kind of testimony. No looters. No soldiers billeted in the rooms, no scorched beams, no signs of struggle anywhere they'd yet seen. The Valeth hadn't died here. They had simply — stopped being remembered, and a thing unremembered cannot be missed, cannot be searched for, cannot be robbed. The Veil had been kinder than any army and crueler than any army, all at once.

The kitchen was where the memory had put it. Sera stood in the doorway and let the room assemble itself around her: the long table scarred with knife-marks, the iron hook where a kettle hung, the shelf of clay pots glazed in that same robin's-egg blue. On the table, a single bowl sat where someone had set it down mid-task and never picked it up. Inside the bowl, desiccated to brown lace, were apples. Someone had been cutting apples.

"They were taken during harvest," Sera said. Her voice sounded clinical, and she hated it, and she couldn't stop it. Clinical was how you survived looking at your mother's kitchen after thirty years of not knowing it existed. "Early autumn. The apples are fresh-cut. Whatever happened, happened fast, and happened everywhere at once." She touched the table's edge. Another fragment rose: *her own small hands, maybe eight years old, reaching for a slice; the door bursting open; not soldiers — just a sound, a vast soft sound like a bell rung underwater, and then* nothing, a hole in the shape of a day.

"The stone was stolen from the vault beneath this valley," Quentin said. She'd drifted to the window, keeping watch out of habit, and Sera loved her a little for it. "Maren said the theft ended the war. If the whole valley heard the bell at once—"

"Then the whole valley forgot what they were fighting for. Forgot their grievances. Maybe forgot each other." Sera opened a drawer. Spoons. Ordinary spoons, and one small knife with a carved handle, a bird of some kind worn smooth by a child's thumb. Her thumb. She knew the weight of it in her hand before her fingers closed. "Gods. They didn't evacuate anyone. They didn't kill anyone. They just closed the book with everyone still inside."

"And the realm called it peace."

"And the realm called it peace." Sera laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "It *was* peace. That's the worst of it. Corvane isn't lying. Put the stone back, wake the grievances, and the war comes back with the memories like a dog coming back with its ball. My mother's people get their names back and lose their children to it. What kind of justice is that?"

"The kind you don't have to decide today." Quentin crossed the kitchen and took the little bird-knife from her hand, folded Sera's fingers back over it. "Keep it. Decide later. Grieve now — that's enough work for one day."

Sera wanted to snap at her, to say *you don't understand, taster-girl, you chose your chains*, but the anger wouldn't catch. Quentin had lied for the first time in her life three weeks ago and had been burning like a struck match ever since. She understood chains better than anyone Sera had ever met.

So instead Sera climbed the stairs.

* * *

There were two bedrooms. One was hers — she knew it before she opened the door, knew the crack in the plaster shaped like a running hare, knew the window seat where she'd read by lamplight. A child's cloak still hung on a peg, motheaten, too small for anyone. Carved into the windowsill, half-hidden under grime: **SV + M**, inside a lopsided heart. Sera traced the letters with her fingertip and could not remember making them and could not doubt for one second that she had.

*M.* Her mother's initial. She didn't know her mother's name.

That was the thing that finally broke her open — not the house, not the kitchen, not the apples rotting in their bowl, but the two letters and the name that should have come after them and wasn't there. She sat down on the window seat with the hare-shaped crack at her shoulder and cried the way she hadn't cried since she was small enough to be carried: ugly, gasping, both hands over her mouth as if the sound might summon something. Quentin stood in the doorway and did not come in and did not leave, and that was exactly right.

When it was spent, Sera looked out the window at the valley — the second village, the mill with its wheel frozen mid-turn in dry air, the low green mound beyond the far fields that her eye kept snagging on.

Her eye kept snagging on it.

"Maren said the stone was hidden in a vault beneath the Vale," she said. Her voice was wrecked and steady at the same time, which felt fitting. "Beneath the *Vale*. Quentin. Look at the hill."

Quentin leaned into the window beside her. Below, the mound sat in the afternoon light, too regular for geology, ringed by stones that might have been markers or might have been wards, grass grown over whatever doorway it held.

"That's not a hill," Sera said. "That's a door. And I think—" She pressed her hand flat against the windowsill, over the carved heart, and the splinter of memory that rose this time was not a kitchen or a song. It was her mother's voice, urgent, low, the last coherent thing the Veil had failed to take: *If you ever find your way back, Valethi, go down where the grass grows over the stone. Go down, and remember for all of us.*

Down in the valley, at the edge of the village square, a figure stepped out of the shade of the bell tower.

Not a ghost. Not a memory. A living man in travel-stained grey, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had every right to be there, and even at this distance Sera knew the way he carried his hands.

"Quentin," she breathed. "That's the man from the waystation. The dying man. He's walking toward the mound — and he knows we're watching."
