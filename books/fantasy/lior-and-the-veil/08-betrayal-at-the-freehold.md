## 8. Betrayal at the Freehold

The freehold smelled of tallow and river stone, and for three days Quentin let herself believe it was safe.

It sat beneath Thornfell like a secret kept too long — a cavern system the smugglers had hollowed over generations, lit by oil lamps that threw amber light across rope bridges and stacked crates, sleeping lofts carved into the rock. Children ran the walkways barefoot. A woman named Odile taught knife-throwing to anyone who'd stand still long enough. There was a cookfire in the great chamber where Maren Voss held court, and someone always singing, badly, on purpose.

Quentin knew better than to trust comfort. Comfort was what they served you right before the poison course.

But she slept anyway. That was her mistake — not trusting, but needing. She lay on a straw pallet with Sera's breathing an arm's length away and the map folded against her ribs, and she slept like a woman who had never tasted nightshade in a queen's cup.

On the fourth morning, the dogs went silent first.

She woke to it: the absence of sound, the particular hush of animals deciding. Then boots above — many boots, iron-shod, moving in the disciplined cadence of crown soldiers, not smugglers. The lamps guttered as if the flame itself flinched.

Sera was already up, knife in hand, hair loose around her face. "How?" she whispered.

Quentin was already counting exits. Two rope bridges from the sleeping lofts, one tunnel to the river, one to the old mine shafts. Four ways out of a place built by people who'd assumed no one would ever come looking.

"Someone told them," she said. "There's no other way down."

Maren Voss met them at the foot of the great stair, already armored in leather and fury, her gray braid bound back tight. Behind her, the freehold was waking into chaos — children scooped up, crates kicked open, smugglers arming themselves with the grim economy of people who had done this before.

"Rat," Maren said flatly. "I'll deal with that after. Right now you two are cargo, and I don't lose cargo." She shoved a satchel into Quentin's arms — bread, flint, a skin of water. "River tunnel. Don't stop for anything. Don't stop for *me*."

"Maren—"

"I've been hunted by better men than Corvane, girl, and I'm still ugly and alive. Go."

The first scream came from above before they reached the bridge.

* * *

They ran through smoke that tasted of pitch and burning hemp. Someone had fired the stores — Corvane's soldiers or the freehold's own hands, denying him supplies; it didn't matter, the fire didn't care whose it was. It came down the cavern walls in orange rivers, eating rope and timber, turning the amber lamplight into something frantic.

A soldier found them at the second junction. Sera put him down without breaking stride — a hook of the leg, a blade across the throat, efficient as closing a ledger. Quentin stepped over the body and felt nothing but the map against her ribs and the need to keep it there.

Then the bridge.

Halfway across the gorge span, the far anchor caught fire. The ropes began to sing, fibers popping one by one, and below them the drop vanished into blackness and heat-shimmer. Quentin froze — the old instinct, the taster's stillness, hold still and survive — and Sera grabbed her wrist.

"Don't think. *Run.*"

They ran. The bridge died behind their heels, collapsing into the dark with a sound like a giant snapping its fingers, and then they were through, coughing, scorched, alive, in the river tunnel where the air ran cold and wet and clean.

Behind them, muffled by stone, the freehold burned.

And behind *that*, fainter, Quentin heard Maren Voss laughing — that big, defiant bark Quentin had heard around her cookfire — and then the laugh cut off, and did not start again.

Sera heard it too. Neither of them said anything. Some silences are a kind of mourning, and some are a kind of promise, and this one was both.

* * *

They surfaced at dawn, two miles downstream, in a stand of birches silvered with early light. The river mist lay thick enough to drink. Quentin knelt at the bank, washed the soot from her hands, and made herself think — because thinking was the only weapon she'd ever owned.

Four ways out of the freehold. One traitor among maybe thirty souls. And Corvane's men arriving not hours late but *precisely* on time, at the change of the watch, when the tunnels were thinnest-manned.

This wasn't a leak. This was a sale. Someone had given him everything — layout, watches, exits — and Corvane had spent it like a man spending a fortune he intended to recoup.

"The boy," Sera said quietly. "Tam. He took the supply runs to the valley towns alone. He asked me questions about you, the first night. Where you learned the roads. Who taught you."

Quentin closed her eyes. Tam, sixteen, quick-smiled, always hungry. She'd liked him. She'd *trusted* him, in the small careless way you trust the young, and the young sell cheap because they don't yet know what things cost.

"He wasn't buying bread in those valley towns," she said. "He was selling us."

Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later, and sick of herself for being proud.

* * *

They walked all day, following the river north toward the pass, and it was near dusk — light going copper, shadows stretching long — that Sera stopped dead on the trail and said, "The dying man. In the waystation. Tell me again. Everything."

Quentin told it again. The burned waystation. The arrow wounds. The bloodstained map pressed into her hands with fingers that wouldn't stop shaking. And the name, gasped so close to her ear she could feel the last warmth of his breath:

"*Quentin.* He said my name. I told you that."

"You told me he knew your name. I thought — a spy, a hunter, someone who'd tracked you." Sera turned to face her fully. "But Quentin. Think. He was Valeth-born, or close to it — you said his eyes. Dark, tilted. Like mine. And he came to *Thornfell Pass*. Not to the capital, not to the queen, not to any of a hundred people who might have carried that map faster or safer. To a food-taster on a provisioning errand."

"So?"

"So how does a stranger know your name? How does a stranger know you can be trusted with the single most dangerous object in the world?" Sera's voice dropped. "You told me once you had a brother. That he left when you were fourteen. That you never learned what became of him."

The mist moved between them. Somewhere upstream, a bird called once and went quiet.

"His name was Rell," Quentin said. Her mouth had gone dry, taster's trick, swallow past it. "Rell Reyes. He'd be — he'd be thirty-four now."

"Dying man in a burned waystation," Sera said gently, terribly. "Thirty-something. Valeth eyes. Knew your name. Knew your road. Chose *you*."

Quentin sat down. Not gracefully — her knees simply stopped negotiating, and the ground came up and received her, and she knelt in the birch leaves and river mud with the map pressing against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She saw him suddenly, whole: not the stranger bleeding out in the firelight, but a boy of seventeen slinging a pack over his shoulder at the servants' gate, saying *I'll send word, Quin, I swear it,* and never sending word, and her learning to stop waiting by the window, and learning, after that, to stop waiting for anything at all. Fifteen years of a closed door. And the whole time he'd been walking toward her — toward this — carrying a stolen country in a leather tube, looking for the one person alive whose loyalty was built on surviving poison, whose tongue could taste a lie in wine, whose roads ran exactly where the map needed to go.

He hadn't stumbled onto her. He'd *chosen* her. He'd known her — followed her years, probably, from outside the Veil's forgetting, watching his little sister become indispensable and owned, waiting for the moment she'd be sent to Thornfell Pass. He'd spent his last strength buying her a war she never asked to fight.

"Rage and heartbreak" — she'd have called it that later, if she'd had words for it. In the moment it was just heat behind the eyes and a sound she didn't recognize coming out of her own throat, and Sera kneeling beside her, hand on her back, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say.

He'd chosen her. And the choosing had killed him. And now it would kill whatever was left of the woman she'd been — the queen's taster, the careful liar, the girl who survived by keeping her head down and her tongue clean.

"He wanted me to finish it," Quentin said at last, wiping her face with a filthy hand. "That's why he gave it to me and not to Maren, not to anyone else. Not because I was convenient. Because I was *his*."

"Yes," Sera said.

"And he didn't tell me. He let me take it from a dying stranger like a stranger. He let me lie for him without knowing who I was lying for."

"Maybe he couldn't say it," Sera said. "Maybe if he'd said *sister*, you'd have taken the map to Corvane yourself, to save him. Or refused it entirely. Maybe a stranger was the only shape in which you'd accept it."

"That's not mercy. That's *management*."

"It's both," Sera said. "Most love is."

* * *

They walked on in the last light, and Quentin did not cry again, because crying was a luxury and she had never once been able to afford luxuries. But something had shifted in her chest, some load-bearing wall knocked through, and she understood that there was no road back now. Not to the palace. Not to the tasting table. Not to the woman who dosed herself with poison and called it prudence.

Corvane had burned the freehold. Maren was dead. Tam had sold them for coin or fear, and Rell Reyes had died on a cold floor pressing his life's work into his sister's hands, and every debt of it now stood in Quentin's name.

Ahead, the pass opened toward the high country, and beyond it — according to the map's true route, the route written under the roads, in ink that only showed by firelight — the valley that was not on any census. The Vale that Was.

Sera walked beside her, quiet, watchful, and when their shoulders brushed she did not pull away.

"They'll follow us," Sera said. "Corvane won't stop now. You know that."

"I'm counting on it," Quentin said.

Sera glanced at her. "What does that mean?"

Quentin touched the map through her shirt, where her brother's blood had soaked into the leather all those weeks ago, and found she was smiling, and the smile felt nothing like safety.

"It means he chose me," she said, "because I'm very hard to kill. Let's go give Corvane a chance to learn why."
