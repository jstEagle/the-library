## 9. The Veilstone

**QUENTIN**

The stairs went down farther than the map promised.

Forty steps, then sixty, then a hundred, cut into rock that had no business being beneath a valley that officially did not exist. Sera's lantern threw their shadows huge against the walls, and the air changed as they descended — old air, sealed air, air that tasted faintly of iron and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in a locked room.

Quentin counted her breaths the way she'd once counted drops of poison on her tongue. It was how she kept fear at arm's length. Breathe in for four. Hold for seven. Out for eight. A taster's discipline. A coward's arithmetic.

"You're doing the thing," Sera said quietly behind her. "Where you go somewhere else."

"I'm counting."

"I know you're counting." A pause. "It doesn't work down here. Nothing down here is going to wait politely while you finish."

The stairs ended at a door of black wood banded with silver gone green with age. No lock. No handle. Only a shallow depression in the center, shaped — Quentin realized with a lurch — like a hand. Like *her* hand, or close enough that when she pressed her palm flat against it, the wood warmed under her skin and sighed open.

Her brother had known. That was the thought that kept surfacing, ugly and tender, every time she let it. *Dain* had known. He'd carried this map across half the kingdom with Corvane's hunters at his back, burned out of one waystation after another, and he'd chosen her. Not Maren Voss with her freehold and her knives. Not any of the smuggler lords who owed him favors. Her — the sister he hadn't spoken to in nine years, the queen's food-taster, a woman whose entire life was built on swallowing what would kill other people.

He'd chosen her because she could survive long enough to finish it.

"Quentin." Sera's voice, very soft. "Look."

* * *

**SERA**

The vault was round, and small, and wrong.

Not grand — that was the first thing. She had expected, without admitting it, something like a temple. What she got was a room the size of a granary, its walls lined with niches, each niche holding a single clay jar sealed with wax. Hundreds of them. Thousands. And in the center, on a plain stone plinth worn smooth by hands long forgotten, sat the Veilstone.

It was smaller than she'd imagined. The size of a heart. It caught the lantern light and didn't give it back — it held it, turned it slowly inside itself like a fly in amber, and the color of it was not any color Sera had words for. Looking at it made her eyes water. Looking away made her chest ache, the way waking from a dream of her mother always did.

And then the memories came.

They didn't arrive gently. They poured through her like floodwater through a broken door — the smell of bread from the ovens along the Vale road, the sound of the harvest bells, her mother's voice singing a counting song about goats, *one for the frost and two for the rain* — Sera sank to her knees on the cold stone and wept without deciding to, great racking sobs that hurt her ribs, because these were hers, they had always been hers, they had been *stolen* and now here they were pouring home.

"Valeth," she gasped. "We called ourselves the Valeth. We had — there were songs. There were *names*—"

She heard Quentin move toward her, hesitate, kneel beside her. A hand on her shoulder. The taster's hand, steady as a surgeon's.

"The jars," Quentin said, and her voice was rough. "Sera. Look at the jars."

Sera looked. And understood, in the way you understand a wound — all at once, and forever. Each jar held a name. She couldn't read them, but she knew them the way you know your own heartbeat. Ten thousand names, give or take. Every person the Veil had eaten. Sealed in wax and clay so that even the forgetting would have something to forget *with*.

"They're not stored here," Sera whispered. "They're *kept*. Like evidence."

"Like hostages," Quentin said.

* * *

**QUENTIN**

Corvane came alone.

She heard him on the stairs — one set of footsteps, unhurried, and she knew his gait the way she knew the queen's, from years of standing at the edges of rooms where powerful men walked. By the time he emerged into the vault, dust on his grey coat, breathing hard from the climb, Quentin had put herself between him and the plinth, and Sera stood at her shoulder with a knife already drawn.

"No soldiers?" Quentin said.

"Soldiers can't climb down here fast enough to matter." He looked around the vault with an expression she had never seen on him before, and it took her a moment to name it: grief. Old, well-worn grief, the kind a man carries so long it becomes furniture. "I've known about this place for thirty years, Mistress Reyes. I was the one who sealed the stair in Thornfell. I was younger then. I believed what I was told."

"And now?"

"Now I'm old, and tired, and I am asking you — *asking* you — to put it down and walk away." He spread his empty hands. "You've read the records I gave you. You know what comes back with the memories. The border taxes. The massacres at Redford Bridge — both sides' versions of them. The claim and counterclaim on land that three generations have farmed believing it was theirs. You break the Veil and you don't just return ten thousand people to history. You hand every living heir of every dead grudge a reason to pick up a sword. The war doesn't resume, Quentin. It *continues*, as if nothing happened, except now the armies are bigger and the weapons are worse."

"You're saying they should stay forgotten."

"I'm saying forgetting bought forty years of peace, and peace fed children who would otherwise be ash." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and Quentin saw it — saw the young man he'd been, sealing a door in the dark, telling himself it was mercy. "I have spent my whole life guarding a lie because the truth was worse. Don't make me find out I guarded it for nothing."

Sera made a sound beside her, low and furious. "My mother died in a country that didn't exist. She died and no one mourned her because no one remembered her. You call that peace?"

"I call it the only peace anyone offered me," Corvane said. "Restore the stone whole, and the grievances come back wearing crowns. Destroy it, and the Valeth stay ghosts forever. Those are the choices. I'm sorry. They're terrible. They're also the only ones."

And that — that was the moment Quentin understood she was going to disappoint everyone in the room, including herself.

Because she was looking at the stone, and she was thinking about poison.

Thirty years of tasting. Thirty years of learning the first law of her trade: *the dose makes the poison.* Nightshade in a thimble kills; nightshade in a river kills no one. Belladonna is medicine or murder depending entirely on how much, and where, and who. Corvane saw the stone and saw a vial — drink it all or pour it out. But Quentin had spent her whole life measuring things other people swallowed whole.

"What if," she said slowly, "you didn't restore it? And you didn't destroy it?"

Corvane frowned. "Mistress Reyes—"

"What if you broke it?"

"Its power scatters—" he began, and stopped, because Sera had gone very still beside her, and Sera was looking at the stone with an expression Quentin had never seen on her either: recognition. Like a woman hearing her own name called from a great distance.

"Yes," Sera whispered. "Yes. That's — my grandmother's song. There's a verse about it. About the stone breaking. *When the heart cracks, the blood remembers.*" She turned to Quentin, eyes blazing wet. "If it shatters, the memory doesn't die. It goes *out*. Into everyone. Every mind in the realm wakes up knowing the Valeth. Not as a grievance. As a *fact*. As people. You can't wage war over someone the whole world suddenly remembers — you can't pretend they never existed when ten thousand names are sitting in your own head, crying."

Corvane's face had gone grey. "You don't know that. You're guessing. You'd be gambling a kingdom's peace on a folk verse—"

"I'm gambling it on the same odds you gambled it on thirty years ago," Quentin said quietly. "You bet on forgetting, Lord Corvane. You never asked what the forgotten would bet. This is what they bet." She reached for the stone. "Everything."

* * *

**SERA**

Later — much later, in the years when people asked her to tell it — Sera would say the strangest part was how quiet it was.

No light. No thunder. Quentin simply lifted the Veilstone off its plinth, weighed it in her palm like a cook judging an apple, and struck it once against the stone edge with all the calm precision of a woman who had broken a thousand harder things for less reason.

It cracked like ice on a spring pond.

The sound went through Sera's bones and out the other side, and the pieces — there were pieces, falling, catching the lantern light, coming apart into fragments and the fragments into motes — rose. All of them. Against every law of weight, the shining dust lifted, drifted past them, up the stairs, through the black door, out into the open air of the valley that had waited unseen for forty years.

And Sera felt it leave. Felt the last of the Veil's weight lift off her mind like a hand withdrawing from her brow — and felt, distantly, everywhere, in a way that had nothing to do with her five senses, the realm exhale. A capital city waking at dawn with tears on its face. A queen dropping a teacup, whispering a name she'd never learned. Farmers in three kingdoms standing motionless in their fields, remembering neighbors they'd never had.

Corvane sat down heavily on the floor of the vault, an old man among ten thousand opened jars, and began, very quietly, to weep.

Quentin stood holding nothing, her hand still raised, still curled around the shape of a stone that no longer existed. She looked at Sera. Sera looked at her.

"Well," Quentin said, and her voice shook only a little. "Now everyone knows what I did."

Above them, far above, the sun was rising on a kingdom that remembered — and somewhere in that kingdom, Sera knew, bells were already ringing, and riders were already moving, and the world was about to come looking for the woman who broke it open.
