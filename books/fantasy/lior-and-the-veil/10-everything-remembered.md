## 10. Everything Remembered

The stone did not shatter the way glass shatters. It came apart like a held breath finally released.

Quentin felt it in her teeth first — a hum, a pressure, the taste of something she had no name for. Then Sera's hands closed over hers on the Veilstone's surface, and Corvane was still speaking, still pleading his case about wars and famines and ten thousand graves that memory would reopen, and none of it mattered anymore, because the stone cracked down its center with a sound like a bell struck inside her skull.

Light poured out. Not blinding — *insistent*, like dawn through shutters you'd kept closed too long.

Then it was gone, and so was the stone, and Quentin stood in the dark vault beneath the Vale holding nothing but Sera's hand and the terrible knowledge of what they'd done.

"Did it work?" Sera whispered.

Quentin didn't know. There was no thunderclap across the kingdom, no sign in the sky. Just two women in a buried room, and above them an entire valley of empty houses waiting to be seen again.

They climbed out into morning light and walked to the edge of the Vale, where the road bent toward Thornfell, and waited.

* * *

The answer came three days later, and it came as weeping.

The first travelers Quentin saw were a wool merchant and his drover, coming up from the lowlands with their cart. The merchant stopped dead at the bend in the road, staring at the valley below — at the rooftops, the mill wheel, the orchards gone wild with forty years of unpruned fruit — and Quentin watched his face do something extraordinary. It broke open.

"I know this place," he said. His voice was thick, bewildered. "Gods forgive me, I *know* this place. My grandmother—" He pressed a fist to his mouth. "I never had a grandmother. I've said that my whole life. I never had one." He looked at Quentin as if she might explain. "She had a name. Why do I know her name?"

"Maren," the drover said suddenly, and then he sat down in the road and wept, because Maren Voss had been his aunt, though until this moment he had never once thought of her, had never known there was anything to think of.

By evening the roads were full of them. People walking out of towns and villages with no destination, following grief like a compass needle. A woman who'd spent forty years wondering why she flinched at the smell of woodsmoke now knew: her father had died in the burning of Valeth's eastern quarter, and she had been four years old, and someone had carried her over the border and told her never to look back. An old soldier sat by the roadside reciting names — names of men he'd served beside, men whose regiment records listed them as born nowhere, kinless, their deaths mourned by no one because officially they had never existed to mourn.

And through it all, the Valeth themselves.

They came down out of hiding places Quentin hadn't known existed — farmsteads beyond the Veil's edge, cellars, mountain holds, families who had kept their own names alive in secret for two generations, whispering them to children as bedtime contraband. They walked into the valley and found their houses standing. They found neighbors who had forgotten them standing at the gates, weeping apologies no one knew how to accept.

No sword was drawn. Not one. Quentin had braced for riots, for reprisals, for the war Corvane had promised her. Instead she watched a crown tax collector stand in the market square of a town that wasn't on any map and say, over and over, to anyone who would listen: "I'm sorry. I don't remember doing it. But I'm sorry."

* * *

Corvane came for her a week later.

Not with soldiers. He came alone, riding up the smuggler road to Maren's ruined freehold, where Quentin had gone with Sera and the survivors to bury what could be buried. He dismounted at the treeline and stood among the blackened beams with his hat in his hands, and he looked older than he had in the throne room, older than he had with the amnesty offer and the ledgers full of projected dead.

"You're not arresting me," Quentin said.

"No." He turned a charred rafter with his boot. "I came to tell you the queen knows. Everything. The taster who dosed herself with poison for twenty years, who lied to royal riders, who stole a state secret and broke a relic of the crown." He met her eyes. "You're finished at court, Reyes. Formally attainted, informally infamous. There are already ballads, which is worse than any warrant."

"And the war?"

"There is no war." He said it flatly, like a man reporting his own defeat. "The old grievances came back with the memories, just as I said they would. The border lords are furious. The Valeth want restitution, land, trials. Parliament will tear itself apart for a decade." He paused. "But nobody is marching. Because it turns out that when ten thousand people wake up weeping, even angry men stop and ask why. Grief is slower than grievance. It gives everyone time to think." His mouth twisted. "You found the flaw in my arithmetic, Reyes. I counted the dead the stone's return would cost. I never counted the living the forgetting had already cost. They outnumber everything."

He put his hat back on. At the treeline he stopped, without turning around.

"The stranger in the waystation," he said. "Your brother. I should have told you when I had you in chains. It seemed crueler than the alternative." A beat. "He chose well, I think. Better than I would have chosen for him."

Then he rode away, and Quentin stood in the ashes of the freehold and let herself, finally, cry.

* * *

The letter from the queen came in spring.

It was brief. *You broke your oath,* it read, *and I cannot keep a taster who tastes for herself. But you saved me twice — once from nightshade, once from a peace that was a lie. The first debt cancels the second. You will not be hanged. You will not be welcomed. Do not come back.*

Quentine read it twice, folded it, and fed it to the cookfire at the rebuilt freehold — rebuilt smaller, rougher, but theirs. Sera sat across from her, sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening, the way she did when she was thinking.

"So that's it," Sera said. "Twenty years of palace service, and your reward is exile by polite letter."

"It's not exile." Quentin looked around the long table — the freehold survivors, the drover who'd learned Maren was his aunt and stayed, the old soldier who'd come looking for his regiment's dead and found instead a place that needed guarding. "Exile means being sent away from somewhere you belong."

Sera snorted. But she reached across the table and took Quentin's wrist — the one that still ached sometimes, the poison-scarred one — and held it a moment before going back to her knife.

That night Quentin walked the walls alone. Below, in the valley, the lights of Valeth were lit — real lights now, mapped, taxed, argued over in parliament, mourned and celebrated and *remembered*. Somewhere down there a woman was teaching her daughter a lullaby that forty years of silence hadn't quite killed. Somewhere an old man was carving his grandmother's name onto a stone that had waited a generation for it.

Quentin had spent her whole life tasting death so others could live safely, and it had made her nothing: a vessel, a function, a woman owned by her usefulness. She had broken that, and lost the only life she'd known how to live.

Standing on the wall, with the wind off Thornfell and Sera's lamplight glowing in the window behind her, Quentin Reyes realized she could not remember the last time she had checked her food for poison.

She smiled, and went inside, and did not lock the door.
