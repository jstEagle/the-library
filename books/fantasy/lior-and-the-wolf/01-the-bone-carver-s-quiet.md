## 1. The Bone-Carver's Quiet

The sparrow had died in autumn, Jonah Thackeray was fairly sure of it. The bone said so.

He turned it between his fingers under the lamplight—a humerus, light as a reed, yellowed at the joint where the marrow had dried to chalk. He'd bought it off a fowler's boy in Millbrook for two coppers, along with a dozen others rattling loose in a sack, and most of them were quiet. Dead things usually were, once they'd finished saying what they had to say. But this one had been singing since he'd picked it up, and it hadn't stopped, and now, three days later, he'd finally sat down to listen properly.

He set his knife to the bone and the song came up through his hand like water through a wick.

*Cold on the wire. Cold on the wire. The wheat is cut and the wind comes down the valley like a door opening—*

Jonah carved. Small, careful strokes. He'd learned long ago that the dead spoke clearest when his hands were busy, as if the work gave their voices somewhere to go. A bone-carver's trade, that's what the sign above his shop said, though the letters had weathered to ghosts of themselves: BONE-CARVER. FINE WORK. People came up the smuggler's road from the low country for rosaries and buttons, knife-hilts and flute-bodies, little saints and less-than-saints, and none of them ever asked why his carvings looked the way they did—why a saint's face might carry an expression no living face wore, some private grief or joy caught mid-flicker, as if the artist had copied it from somewhere no one else could see.

They didn't ask because they didn't want to know. That suited him fine.

*—the cat came at dusk and I flew and I flew and the sky went white at the edges, and then there was a fence, and I hit it, and after the fence there was nothing but this, whatever this is, this warm dark, this listening—*

"You hit the fence," Jonah murmured. "I know."

The workshop sat above the road like a bird's nest above a drop. One room, really, plus the lean-to where he slept, plus the porch where he did his smoking and his thinking and his not-talking. Below him the Thornfell foothills went down into pine shadow, and the old drover's path—the one honest maps didn't show and dishonest ones got wrong on purpose—threaded north toward the passes. In thirty years of living here he'd watched maybe four hundred travelers pass below his window: muleteers, pilgrims, men with more knives than luggage. He'd spoken to perhaps forty of them. He'd *talked* to perhaps three.

It wasn't shyness. Shyness would have been simpler. It was that conversation had come to feel like a language he'd half forgotten—like the Old Tongue words his grandmother used to mutter over her stew, words that meant something once and meant nothing now except the shape of her mouth making them. With the dead he never had that problem. The dead were fluent. The dead had nothing but time and everything to say.

*Warm dark,* the sparrow sang. *Listening. Is someone listening?*

"I'm listening," Jonah said, to the empty room, to the bone, to the small dead weight of it resting in his palm like a seed.

That was the comfort of it. That was the part nobody who'd heard about his gift—and few had, and fewer believed it—ever understood. They thought hearing the dead must be horror, all moaning and accusation, grave-dirt and grievance. And yes, sometimes. There was a femur in his scrap drawer that had screamed every night for a year before it finally wore itself out, and he'd buried it under the floor rather than sell it, and even buried he could hear it, faintly, like a dog dreaming. There was a rib from a drowned man that did nothing but recite the names of rivers. There was a whole box of teeth that chattered—not talked, *chattered*, in cold weather, all together, a sound like dice in a cup.

But mostly it wasn't horror. Mostly it was company.

Mostly it was a sparrow singing its last afternoon on a wire while a man carved its small bone into the shape of its own flight, wings back, breast forward, the way sparrows look when they've decided to cross a valley.

*Is someone listening?*

"Every word," Jonah said.

And that was the curse of it, too, though he'd have bitten his tongue bloody before admitting it aloud. Because the dead only ever needed you for so long. They sang themselves out. They finished their last afternoon and their last word and then they went quiet, and quiet bones made beautiful carvings, and beautiful carvings sold, and then he'd buy or find or dig up new ones, and the room filled again with voices, and the cycle ran on like water through a mill. Thirty years of it. He was fifty-one years old and the sum total of his intimate acquaintance was a sack of bones.

There had been a woman once. Maren. She'd come up the road selling dye-roots twenty years ago and stayed three seasons, and she'd known what he was—guessed it inside a week, watching him talk to a drawer—and she hadn't minded, or she'd said she didn't, and for three seasons the workshop had smelled of dye-root and woodsmoke instead of just dust. Then she'd gone down to Millbrook for market day and caught the fever that was going around, and died in a stranger's bed, and when they brought her things up the mountain there was a note in the bundle that said only: *Don't follow me down, Jonah. You know how to listen. Learn how to stay.*

He hadn't followed her down. He hadn't learned how to stay, either. He'd learned how to work, which looks the same from the outside and isn't.

*The wheat is cut,* the sparrow sang, softer now, winding down. *The wind comes down the valley. Cold on the wire. Cold—*

"That's it," Jonah said gently. "You can rest. I'll finish you tonight."

He worked until the candle guttered low. Wings first, always wings—it felt rude otherwise, keeping a flying thing grounded while you decided what it should be. Then the body, the keel of the breastbone, the tilt of the head. The sparrow's voice thinned as the carving took shape, as if each finished stroke let it put something down, and by the time he blew the bone dust off the finished piece—a sparrow in full stoop, wings swept, small enough to hide in a child's fist—the voice was nearly gone.

*Thank you,* it said, or something like it, the way a bell says thank you when it stops ringing. *Someone listened.*

Then silence. Clean, total, the particular silence of a finished carving, which was different from all other silences and which Jonah knew better than his own heartbeat.

He held the little bird up to the lamp. It was good work. It was very good work, if he said so himself, and he did, to himself, constantly, because who else was going to say it. The wing-feathers were suggested rather than rendered, three strokes apiece, and the eye was just a burnished dimple, and somehow the whole thing managed to look like motion frozen at the exact moment motion became inevitable. He set it on the shelf with the others—forty-odd birds and beasts and small saints, each one silent now, each one someone's whole last testament rendered into ornament—and he stood looking at them the way a man looks at a wall of photographs.

"There," he told them. "Done."

The shelf said nothing. The shelf never said anything. That was the point of the shelf.

Outside, the light had gone the color of dirty snow, and speaking of snow—the first flakes were coming down past the window, fat and slow and undecided, the kind that either stop by dark or bury the road by morning. Jonah pulled his coat off its peg and went out onto the porch to split kindling before the storm made up its mind, because firewood was one of the few conversations he still knew how to have with the living world: you asked it to burn and it agreed.

The axe rose and fell. The pile grew. Snow thickened from suggestion to statement. Down below, the drover's path was already whitening, and no traveler in his right mind would be on it after dark—which was why, when he straightened up to rest his back and happened to glance down the switchbacks out of habit, the figure on the path stopped him with his mouth open.

Small. Alone. Moving wrong—that lurching, deliberate walk of someone who has been walking far past the point where walking stopped being possible. Not a muleteer. Not a peddler. A child, by the size of it, wrapped in something dark, plowing uphill through snow that was already ankle-deep and deepening, coming up the smuggler's road toward his door with the blind persistence of something that knows exactly where it's going and will not be argued with.

"No," Jonah said aloud, to the child, to the mountain, to the general arrangement of the universe. "No, absolutely not. Whatever you're doing, turn around. Go back down."

The child kept climbing.

He stood on the porch with the axe in his hand and told himself all the sensible things. Told himself it was probably a shepherd's kid caught out, that she'd see the light and veer off to the winter byre two miles down, that it was none of his affair, that his business was bones and bones were quiet and he liked it that way. Told himself that strangers on the Thornfell road in the first snow of the year were trouble with legs, and he'd survived thirty years up here precisely by letting trouble walk on past.

The child fell, got up, kept climbing.

Jonah looked at the axe. He looked at the sky, which had finished deciding and was now burying the road in earnest. He thought—he did not know why he thought it, and it annoyed him immediately—that the dead had gone unusually quiet today. Even the screaming femur under the floorboards was holding its breath.

"Well," he said to nobody, "damn it."

He set down the axe, lit the lantern, and went down to meet the road.

* * *

She reached his gate as the last light failed. Up close she was smaller than she'd looked on the path—younger, too, seven or eight, maybe nine, hollow-cheeked in the way of a child who has been hungry longer than children are built to be hungry. Her coat was dark wool, fine once, ruined now, hem burned off in a straight line as if it had been trimmed with a hot blade. No pack. No hood. Snow in her black hair, melting down her face, and she stood at his gate swaying like a fencepost in wind and looked up at him with eyes that were the wrong color to be called brown—more like wet river stones, gray-green, old.

"Hello," Jonah said. His breath smoked. "You're lost."

She shook her head. Slowly. Definitely not lost, then.

"You're starving, though." He could see it in the wrists, the collarbones, the way she tracked the lantern with her whole face. "Come inside. There's soup. It's bad soup, but there's a lot of it."

She didn't move. Instead she opened her mouth and said something—and Jonah, who had spent thirty years listening to the speech of the dead in every accent the mountains had ever swallowed, felt the words go through him like cold water, because he did not know them, and he knew *everything*. Old drover's cant, chapel Latin, the tomb-speech of the barrow-kings whose finger-bones occasionally surfaced after floods. Nothing like this. The sounds were round and ringing, vowels stacked like stones in a wall, and they carried a cadence that wasn't question or plea or thanks but something older than any of those—announcement, almost. Proclamation. A child standing in the snow announcing something in a language that had no business existing.

"What?" he said stupidly. "What was that?"

She closed her mouth. Watched him. Waited.

Then her eyes moved past his shoulder, up to the window of the workshop, where the lamp still burned behind the shelf of finished carvings—and something crossed her face that he had no name for. Recognition, maybe. Or homesickness. Or the expression of someone seeing, across a great distance and worse odds, a light left burning in a window they'd stopped believing existed.

She pushed past him through the gate without another sound, climbed his steps, and laid one bare, blue-cold hand flat against his door.

And every voice Jonah Thackeray had heard, night and day, for thirty years—

—the screaming femur under the floor, the rib reciting rivers, the chattering teeth, the hundred small murmurs in the scrap drawer, the whisper of old graves up and down the valley that he'd always pretended was the wind—

went silent.

All of them. All at once. Like a hall of singers struck dumb by one raised hand.

Jonah stood in the falling snow with his lantern shaking in his grip, and for the first time since he was a boy of twenty-one, since the fever that had nearly killed him and left him with this, the inside of his skull was quiet. Vastly, terribly, wonderfully quiet. Quiet as the shelf. Quiet as the space between stars.

The girl looked back at him over her shoulder, waiting to be let in.

"Come in, then," Jonah heard himself say, from very far away. "Before we both freeze."
