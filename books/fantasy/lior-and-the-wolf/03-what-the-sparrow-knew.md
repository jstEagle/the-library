## 3. What the Sparrow Knew

The fire came first. It always came first.

Not the fire of the hearth downstairs, which was small and orange and made a sound like breathing. That fire Lior could bear. She had learned, in the three nights since the snow brought her to this man's door, to sit near it without her whole body turning into a scream.

The other fire was white at its heart and it did not breathe. It roared. It ate a city the way a wolf eats a lamb—quickly, and then licking its jaws for more. In the dream she stood very small before it, and the heat pressed on her face like a hand, and behind her someone was saying *run* in a language that lived only inside her now, that no one else in all the world still carried in their mouth.

*Vaelthorne burns,* said the voice, or maybe the fire said it, or maybe she said it herself, because sometimes in the dream she could not tell where she ended and the burning began.

Then the white door.

It stood alone in the dream the way a tooth stands alone in an old jaw. White as bone, white as snow, white as the inside of an egg. It had no wall around it. It rose out of ash and ash and ash, and it was the only clean thing left in the world, and a voice—not the run-voice, a deeper one, older, warm the way coals are warm when they are almost dead—said:

*The last. You are the last.*

She did not want to be the last. Being the last meant being a door with nothing behind it. Being the last meant carrying a whole country inside your chest like a stone you could not set down, because there was nowhere to set it down, because the country was gone, because—

Because she woke up.

*

Waking was the worst part. Waking was falling out of Vaelthorne into this place.

This place had too many straight lines. The man's workshop above the road was full of them: shelves in rows, knives hung in a fan, little carved animals standing in ranks on every surface like soldiers waiting for orders. The light came through one window and lay across the floor in a hard yellow bar, and dust turned in it, slow, like things drowning.

Lior sat up on the cot he'd given her—a cot! a whole bed that was hers, soft with old wool—and tried to do what she always tried to do: put the world into order, the way her hands remembered doing before her hands forgot everything else.

Here is what she knew about this world so far.

The man was called Jonah. She knew this because he said it against his own chest, twice, the first night, tapping himself the way you tap a tree to hear if it's hollow. *Jonah.* The word was short and rough, like a pebble in the shoe. Her own name would not fit in his mouth. When she said it—*Lior*, with the breath rising through it like water finding its way uphill—he made a shape of wonder and repeated it wrong, flattening the middle, and she had not corrected him because correcting him would have meant teaching him, and teaching him would have meant opening her mouth wider than two syllables, and she did not know yet if anything larger than two syllables could come out of her in his tongue without breaking something.

His tongue was a strange tongue. Everything in it was either a command or a question. *Eat. Sleep. Cold? Hungry? Safe.* He strung the words together with his hands as much as his voice, and his hands were good hands, scarred and precise, and they told her truer things than his words did. His hands said: *you may stay. His hands said: I am afraid of something but not of you.*

That was new. In her remembering—the white-hot remembering, the part that was not dream—hands had reached for her and they had wanted things. Hands had lifted her onto a horse whose sweat smelled of iron. Hands had pushed her through a gap in a wall while the white fire leaned over the rooftops like a curious giant. Hands had pressed a cold round thing into her palm—she still felt it sometimes at night, a coin or a seal, smooth, heavy, gone now, lost somewhere between countries—and the mouth above those hands had said *the last, keep it, the last,* and then the hands were gone, everyone's hands were gone, and the world after that was roads.

Roads. This world was mostly roads. Roads under her feet for days she could not count, roads that crossed each other and argued and went nowhere she understood. People walked them carrying their lives in bundles on their backs, and they exchanged small metal discs for bread, for beds, for passage, and the discs meant nothing and the people believed in them completely, and Lior could not make herself believe. Where she came from—or where she had come from, once, in the country that was now only a weight in her chest—you gave a thing and received a thing and the giving itself was the coin. A loaf for a song. A mended roof for a story. She remembered that much. She remembered tables long enough for forty families, and no one counting.

And no one here remembered any of it. That was the strangest thing, the thing she kept circling back to like a tongue returning to a missing tooth. When she looked at these people—at Jonah, at the hooded figures who passed on the road below the window—they were not hiding grief. There was no grief in them to hide. They had not lost Vaelthorne. They had never had it. It was as if a star had gone out and the whole sky agreed to pretend it had never been lit.

But the bones knew.

*

The bones knew. That was the second true thing she had learned in this world, and it had nearly stopped her heart the first time she learned it.

On her first morning, starving, dizzy, she had wandered the workshop touching things because touching was how she read, how she had always read—her mother's face, the mood of weather coming, the yes-or-no of a door. And she had touched a small carved sparrow, pale and fine, no bigger than her thumb, and the sparrow had *sung*.

Not aloud. Not in air. In that other hearing, the deep-tissue hearing, the one that had been screaming *fire, fire* ever since the night of the white light. The sparrow sang a few notes of a morning song, bright and unfinished, and underneath the song was a smallness, a feathered quickness, a last flutter of wings in a net or a cat's mouth or a boy's snare—Lior could not say which, only that the song had ended mid-note, and the carver had caught what remained and made it into a bird again.

She had dropped it. Of course she had dropped it. And Jonah had come up the stairs fast, and she had braced for anger, and instead he had picked up the sparrow with both hands, gently, the way you pick up something wounded, and looked at her with an expression she had no word for in any language—his or hers.

Later she understood: he heard them too. The dead in the bones. All the time. Every carving on every shelf was a little jar with a voice inside, and he lived among them the way a man lives in a house full of relatives who will not stop talking.

When she touched them, they went quiet.

She discovered this by accident, reaching past a carved wolf for a cup, her fingers brushing the wolf's spine—and the silence rang. It rang the way snow-silence rings, the way the world rings after a bell stops. Thirty years of voices, hushed in an instant, because her skin said to them: *rest. Someone from home is here.*

Home. The word opened in her like a wound.

Because the bones' silence felt like Vaelthorne. It felt like evening in the terraced gardens above the white city, when the whole valley held its breath between birdsong and dark. It felt like her mother's hand going still and warm on her hair. Everything quiet and complete and *over*.

So she touched the carvings now, each morning, all of them, walking down the ranks like a queen reviewing soldiers, laying her palm flat on each small head, giving them sleep. It was the only kindness she knew how to perform in this language-less place. It was the only prayer she had left. And every morning Jonah watched her do it from the doorway with his face full of a hunger he didn't name, and she pretended not to see, because she did not know yet what he wanted—for her to stop, or never to stop—and wanting things from her was dangerous. Everyone who had wanted something from her was gone.

*

On the third day—it might have been the third day; days here were grey beads on a string, all alike—snow began again, big slow flakes like the sky shedding feathers, and Jonah did something incomprehensible.

He took her outside. Into the snow. On purpose.

He wrapped her in a coat that smelled of smoke and tallow and led her down the stairs and along the road to where the trees broke open into a meadow, and he pointed at the mountains with his whole arm, north and west, where the peaks went up white into white, and he said a stream of words she couldn't follow, except one.

He said it twice. He tapped the wind, the direction, the far cold distance, and said: *home?*

Asking. Asking *her*. As if she were the one who knew.

Lior stood in the falling snow and looked where he pointed and felt the stone in her chest turn over.

Because yes. Yes, that way. Something in her blood leaned toward those peaks like a plant toward light, some compass older than her memory, and she hated it, because it meant the stone in her chest wasn't just grief, it was a *task*, and tasks got people killed, and everyone who had ever told her *the last, go, run, keep it* had died with the words still warm in their mouths.

She looked at Jonah. Snow gathered on his shoulders and in his beard and he waited, patient as furniture, for an answer he had no right to expect, in a language she could barely speak, to a question she could barely survive.

And she found, in the deep pocket of herself where Old Vaelthorni lived, curled up like the last animal of its kind, a word she could give him. One word. The smallest true thing she owned.

She raised her hand—slowly, so slowly, the way you move around something wild—and pointed the same way he had pointed, north and west, into the white.

And far off, beyond the mountains, beyond the snow, so faint it might have been the wind or might have been memory or might have been the last ember of something enormous drawing breath, she heard—felt—sang:

a voice calling *the last*, and beneath it, beneath everything, a sound like a great heart beating slower,

and slower,

and slower.
