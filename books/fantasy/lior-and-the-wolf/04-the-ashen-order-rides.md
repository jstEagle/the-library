## 4. The Ashen Order Rides

The village was called Hollowmere, though Serane Voss had already decided it would not be called that for much longer.

She rode into it at dusk with six riders behind her and the ash-grey banners of the Order hanging still in the windless cold. The houses leaned together like conspirators. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, guilty threads. Somewhere a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

Serane dismounted at the chapel steps without waiting for her horse to be taken. She always did this. It unsettled people—the Inquisitor who moved like she had no patience for ceremony, no interest in the theater of arrival. Let the provincial priests fumble with their vestments. She preferred them off balance.

The priest was waiting for her inside, which meant someone had ridden ahead to warn him, which meant someone in this village had something to warn him about.

"Father Anselm," she said.

"Inquisitor." He was older than she expected, white-haired, hands folded on the altar rail as if he'd been praying. Perhaps he had. It wouldn't help him. "We are honored. Though I confess I don't know what brings the Order to so small a parish."

"You do," Serane said. "That's why you're standing there instead of sitting."

She let the silence work. Silence was the first tool of interrogation, and the cheapest, and the one most priests broke against. This one held for eleven seconds—she counted—before his eyes flicked toward the confessional and back.

Interesting.

"Close the doors," she said to Brother Callow, her clerk, who did everything with the same unhurried precision he brought to his ledgers. The doors shut. The candles guttered in the sudden draft and steadied.

"Father," Serane said, "eleven days ago, a tinker named Wren Halloway passed through your village. He sells tinware and mended pots. He also drinks, and when he drinks, he talks. Three nights ago, in the taproom of the Drowned Ox in Marrow's Ford, he talked about a child. A girl. Starving, mute—or near enough—and found wandering the high road after the snowstorm. He said—" she opened the ledger she carried and read from it, because reading from the ledger made things true in a way speaking never could, "—he said the child spoke words no one knew, and that old Mother Cass, God rot her, said the words were *the old tongue*, the tongue from before, the tongue of the country that isn't there anymore."

"I'm afraid I don't—"

"Don't." Serane closed the ledger. "You've been in this parish nine years, Father Anselm. Before that, you served at Greywater Abbey. Before that, you were ordained at the Cathedral of Saint Vess in Corvane. You have an excellent memory. I know, because we checked. So you will not tell me you don't remember what the old tongue is. You will not insult both of us."

The priest's hands had come apart. One of them drifted toward his collar, touched it, fell away.

"The Order's concern," Serane continued, walking slowly along the altar rail, letting her gloved fingers trail across the wood, "is not superstition. We do not ride through the snow to chase ghost stories. The Order's concern is accuracy. History is a record, Father, and records must be kept clean. When a record is corrupted—when false entries appear, when erased lines resurface like ink bleeding through vellum—it is our sacred duty to restore the text. Do you understand?"

"I understand that you burn books," the priest said quietly.

"We burn errors." She turned to face him fully. "There is a difference. A book that contains a thing that never happened is not a book. It is a wound in the page. And wounds fester."

*And some wounds,* she did not say, *were carved into me before I could walk.*

* * *

They took the confession in the vestry, because the priest asked—begged—that whatever he said not be spoken beneath the eyes of his own saints, and Serane allowed it. Mercy, of a kind. It cost nothing and bought cooperation.

He sat on a stool meant for storing candles. His hands were shaking now. Good. Fear kept memory honest.

"There was a child," he said. "Eleven days past. Or twelve. The miller's wife brought her to me—found her at the edge of the barley fields, half-frozen, alone. No pack, no shoes worth the name. She couldn't have been more than seven or eight."

"Describe her."

"Small. Dark-haired. Eyes—" he hesitated, searching, "—grey. Like slate. Like weather coming."

Serane wrote: *small, dark hair, grey eyes.* Beside it, in the margin, in the cipher only clerks of the Third Rank could read: *candidate.*

"She wouldn't speak at all at first. We fed her. Broth, bread. She ate like a wild thing, watching all of us, and I thought—trauma, poor lamb, seen something terrible on the road. Bandits, wolves, God knows. Then on the second night, Sister Maren was washing her by the fire, and the child looked up at the smoke-hole, at the stars through it, and spoke."

"What did she say?"

"I don't know." His voice cracked with something that might have been genuine distress. "That's the truth of it, Inquisitor. I didn't know. But I *felt* it. The shape of it. Old words. Words like—" he struggled, "—like the stones in the river feel, if stones could speak. And Mother Cass, who is ninety and half-blind and has been wrong about everything her whole life, stood up out of her chair and went white as whey and said, *that's the tongue of Vaelthorne.*"

The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

Serane did not move. That was the discipline. You did not move, you did not blink, you did not let your breath change, because the body confessed faster than the mouth and everyone watched for it—even frightened old priests, even without knowing they watched.

But inside her skull, something vast and cold turned over. The way ice shifts on a lake at night.

"And where," she said, "did Mother Cass learn the name of a country that does not exist?"

"That's just it." The priest leaned forward, urgent now, a man trying to hand off a burden. "She didn't. No one taught her. She said she remembered it. She said her grandmother used to sing a song with those words in it, a lullaby, and never knew what the words meant, and neither did her grandmother before her. The words came down like—like a scar. Passed from mother to daughter with no one knowing what cut them."

*A scar,* Serane thought. *Yes. That is precisely the correct word.*

"Where is the child now?"

"Gone." He spread his hands. "Two nights after. The door of the guest room open, the window latched from inside, the snow unbroken below. She was simply—not there. As if she'd been taken up. As if she'd never—"

"As if she'd been unmade," Serane said.

The priest flinched.

"Yes," she said softly. "You may use the word. It's a technical term. We prefer precision."

* * *

Afterward, she walked the village while Callow conducted the formalities—the seizure of Mother Cass for questioning, the sealing of the parish records, the quiet interviews that would ripple outward through the valley like dye through water. Serane walked alone. Her riders knew better than to accompany her on these walks. The Inquisitor inspected. It was said she could smell a lie the way other people smelled rain coming.

Hollowmere sat in its bowl of hills, thirty-one households, a mill, a shrine, a well. Ordinary. Forgettable. Which was, of course, the entire point of places like this. The Order had spent two centuries making the world forgettable, one village at a time, smoothing history flat the way water smooths stone—not by force, mostly, but by patience. By ledgers. By the slow arithmetic of removal.

Vaelthorne had been different. Vaelthorne had required fire.

She stopped at the well and looked down into it. Black water, far down, holding a coin of sky.

They had not merely burned the country. Burning was easy; any mob could burn. What the Order had done was harder and holier. They had burned the maps, and then the mapmakers, and then the records of the mapmakers' births, and then the graves of the mapmakers' mothers, until the thread ran backward into nothing. They had rewritten the chronicles and burned the originals and then burned the copies of the copies. They had gone into the great libraries of Corvane and Vell and the Salt Cities with carts and tongs and removed every volume that mentioned the eastern kingdoms beyond Thornfell, and replaced them with volumes that described empty mountains, and then they had burned the men who noticed the shelves were thinner than they used to be.

And the memories—God, the memories. That had been the Ninth Rite, the Unmaking, performed by choirs of trained voices over three years, town by town, family by family, until the survivors of Vaelthorne's diaspora woke one morning unable to say where their grandmothers had come from, unable to pronounce the names they'd been given at their own christenings, holding heirlooms whose purpose they could no longer guess. A spoon with a wolf's head on the handle. A song in a dead language. A direction they felt compelled to walk and could not explain.

The Order called it mercy. Erasure spared the remnant the grief of mourning. Better a people with a hole in their hearts than a people sharpening swords around that hole for two hundred years.

Serane had recited that doctrine a thousand times. She had taught it to novices. She believed it the way a man believes in the load-bearing wall of his own house—completely, structurally, and without ever wanting to look too closely at what would happen if it failed.

Because Serane Voss had been born in Vaelthorne.

Not that any record said so. Not that she could prove it, even to herself, on most days. She had been four years old when the choir came to her mother's kitchen. She remembered—no. She remembered *nothing*, that was the triumph of it, that was the art. She remembered a smell of bread and woodsmoke that arrived sometimes in dreams with no room attached. She remembered a word that surfaced in her mouth like a fish rising: *aelith*, which meant nothing in any living tongue, which she had once whispered to a novice and which had earned the novice a reassignment and Serane a month of penance and a private interview with the Provost, who had looked at her for a long time and said, "The rite was imperfect in some vessels. Yours appears to be one of them. Pray it stays sealed."

So she prayed. And she worked. And she climbed, rank by rank, higher than any Vaelthorni-born had ever climbed in the Order, because the safest place for a flaw in the record was inside the office that kept the records.

A child speaking Old Vaelthorni. A child who vanished from a locked room.

Serane's breath fogged over the well water. Far below, her reflection wavered, grey-eyed, and for a moment she had the vertiginous sense of looking down at another woman—a woman with dark hair and a different name, standing at a different well, in a village that had a name that no longer existed.

She straightened. Discipline. The wall held.

* * *

Back at the chapel, Callow was waiting with the ledger open and his pen poised, and beside him, flanked by two riders, sat Mother Cass—tiny, wrapped in shawls, ninety years of stubbornness packed into a frame like a sparrow's.

"They say you remember songs," Serane said to her.

"I remember lots of things." The old woman's chin came up. "More every year, seems like. Funny how that works. The older I get, the more comes back. Things I never knew I knew."

"Sing the song."

"Now why would I do that?"

"Because if you sing it, I will know whether it is what the tinker said it was. And if it is not—if you're a foolish old woman who heard a child babble and dressed it in ghosts—then you'll be released by morning, and I'll apologize for the trouble, and my clerk will buy you a hot supper. And if it *is*—" Serane crouched to bring her eyes level with the old woman's. "Then you already understand what happens next, and singing or not singing will not change it. It will only change whether your neighbors watch you be difficult first."

Mother Cass looked at her for a long moment. Something passed across the wrinkled face—not fear. Recognition, almost. Pity, maybe, which was worse.

"You've got the look," the old woman said. "You know that? Same look the child had. Like you're listening to something under the floor."

"Sing the song."

And Mother Cass sang.

It was nothing. Four lines, tuneless, worn smooth as a river pebble by generations of mouths that didn't know what they were carrying. A lullaby about sleep and sheep and safe walls, in the grammar of a language two centuries dead, sung in a kitchen in a village that would soon not exist.

Serane stood very still while it ended.

She knew the song. That was the horror of it, the thing she would carry home through the snow and lock in the deepest vault of herself: she knew the fourth line. Knew it before the old woman sang it. Knew the way the melody bent upward on the last word, the way a mother's voice lifts on the promise of morning.

Her mother had sung it. In a kitchen that did not exist, in a country that had never happened, to a daughter whose name had been scraped out of the world.

"Aelith," Serane said. The word escaped before she could stop it—her own word, her dream-word, and Mother Cass went rigid, and Callow's pen stopped scratching.

"What did you say?" the old woman whispered.

"Nothing." Serane closed the ledger. "Callow. The village goes on the schedule. Full rite. I want the choir here within the week." She pulled on her gloves, each finger deliberate, sealing herself in leather and procedure. "And send a rider to the Chapter at Corvane. Priority seal. Tell them the record has developed an itch."

"An itch, Inquisitor?"

"A corruption surfacing." She was already moving toward the door, toward the horses, toward the darkening pass. "A child who speaks the dead tongue and vanishes through locked windows doesn't wander alone for long. Someone is keeping her. Some smuggler, some heretic, some fool who thinks he's found a treasure instead of a wound." She swung into the saddle and gathered the reins, and above the chapel the first stars were coming out, indifferent, ancient, older than any erasure.

"Find the itch," Serane Voss said, "and put it out."
