## 5. The Dead Give Directions

The girl slept for two days.

Jonah spent most of them pretending he wasn't watching her. He carved. He ate. He swept the workshop floor that didn't need sweeping. Every hour or so he'd glance at the cot by the stove where Lior lay curled under his spare blanket, small as a folded letter, and every time the same thought arrived and refused to leave: *the dead went quiet when she touched them.*

Thirty years of voices. Thirty years of whispering bones and muttering antler and the sparrow's last song looping through his skull like a man pacing a cell. And she had put her hand on a shelf of finished carvings and the whole chorus of the grave had stopped mid-word.

He should have been grateful. He wasn't. Gratitude required trust, and trust required knowing what she was, and all he knew was: starving, mute in any tongue that mattered, and able to silence the dead like snuffing candles.

On the second evening she woke, drank three cups of broth without pausing to breathe, and then stood in the middle of his workshop turning slowly, taking inventory of everything with those grey eyes of hers — eyes the color of weather coming.

Then she walked to the shelf where the wolf femur sat.

"No," Jonah said, before he could think about why.

She looked back at him. Not startled. *Assessing.* Like he was a door she hadn't decided whether to open.

"It's not finished," he said, which was true, and also the only argument he had. "And it's old bone. Older than anything else I've got. The voice in it is—" He stopped. He did not talk about the voices out loud. Talking about them made them more real, and they were real enough already, thank you, filling his ears from dawn to dark with the accumulated gossip of the deceased.

Lior reached up and set her palm flat against the femur.

The silence came down over the room like snowfall.

It was different this time. When she'd silenced the shelf of carvings, it had been a held breath, a door shut on a noisy market. This was deeper. This was the silence at the bottom of a well. Jonah realized he was hearing his own heartbeat for the first time in years and it frightened him more than any voice ever had.

Then something spoke through the quiet.

Not the femur's usual murmur — the wolf-bone had always carried a low, restless growl of a voice, some hunter dead four hundred years who grumbled about weather and prey and nothing else. This was older. This was a voice like bedrock, like something that had been waiting so long it had forgotten impatience.

"*Carver.*"

Jonah's knife hit the floor. He didn't bend to pick it up.

"You hear us," the voice said. It was neither male nor female, or perhaps both, or perhaps past the point where such things mattered. "You have heard us since you were a boy. We are sorry for it. We know what it has cost you."

"Who—" His mouth was dry. "Who is that?"

"We are Vaelthorne."

The word landed in the room and stayed there. Jonah knew it the way you know a word from a dream — familiar and impossible at once. He'd never heard it spoken. He was almost certain no one alive had. And yet it settled into him with the weight of something remembered rather than learned, the way a tune you've never heard can still feel like it belongs to you.

"There is no such place," he said.

"There is no such place *left*," said the voice, and something in it cracked, grief worn smooth by centuries of handling. "That is not the same thing."

Lior hadn't moved. Her hand stayed on the bone. Her eyes were closed now, and her lips were moving — faintly, silently, following along, the way a child mouths words at a story being read aloud. She couldn't hear this voice, Jonah understood suddenly. She was hearing another one. Or many. She was standing in the middle of a conversation he could only catch one side of.

"The girl," the voice said. "Her name is not Lior. That is the name the road gave her, the name strangers call her, and it will serve. But her name — her true name, the one written in our books and burned from your memory — we cannot speak aloud even now. The ones who unmade us listen for it. Say it three times in any living ear and they will come before the echo fades."

Jonah's skin prickled. "The Ashen Order." Everyone in the trade knew the Order the way sailors knew a storm front — you didn't study it, you just got out of its way. Bone-smugglers told stories about villages that had asked too many questions about old things and weren't there anymore. Not destroyed. *Unthere.* You'd walk through where they'd been and feel an itch behind your eyes, like a word on the tip of your tongue that someone had cut out.

"They burned our country twice," the voice said. "Once with dragon-fire, when they broke our walls and scattered our people. Then again with their rites — the Unmaking. They took our name from maps. They took our history from archives. They took our dead from the memory of the living, so that no one would mourn us, because mourning keeps a thing alive." A pause, long as a held breath. "But they could not take the bones, carver. Bones remember. That is why you exist. That is why men like you have always existed, though the Order hunts you too, when it finds you. You are the last archive we have."

"I'm a smuggler," Jonah said. "I carve trinkets for tourists and move contraband down the Thornfell passes. I'm not anybody's archive."

"You are exactly what you are," the voice said, with terrible gentleness, "and what you are is ours."

The fire popped in the stove. Outside, wind worried at the eaves. Lior opened her eyes and looked at him, and there was something in her face he'd seen once before, years ago, on a drowning man he'd pulled out of the Greywash — the look of someone who has already accepted the worst news and is only waiting for everyone else to catch up.

"What do you want?" Jonah asked. It came out rougher than he meant. Everything came out rougher than he meant; thirty years of talking mostly to the dead will do that.

"Take her home."

* * *

The femur told it in pieces, the way old things tell stories — out of order, circling back, assuming knowledge Jonah didn't have. He sat down on his stool because his legs informed him they were done pretending, and he listened, and he carved nothing, and the girl sat cross-legged at his feet with her hand still resting on the bone like a hand resting on a sleeping animal.

Vaelthorne had been a mountain country, east beyond the Thornfells, in the high valleys where the maps now showed only blank white space and the words *here be storm-waste*. It had kept dragons the way other countries kept cattle — not as beasts but as bonds. Fire-given. The oldest of them, a guardian called in the old tongue something the voice wouldn't render except as *the Ember That Watches*, had slept beneath the capital city for nine hundred years, and its sleeping warmth had been the country's heart. Its wards. Its promise.

The Order had killed the other dragons first. Then they'd come for the Ember, and found they couldn't kill it — only wound it, bank it, drive it down into the deep places beneath the ruined city until it burned no larger than a coal in a dead hearth. So they'd sealed the country instead. Erased it. Let time and snow do the rest.

"But a sealed door opens from both sides," the voice said, "if you hold the key. And the key is blood. Her blood. The last of the royal line, smuggled out the night of the burning by a woman who died on the road so the child could live. The Order has hunted that bloodline for two hundred years. They believe it ended. They are wrong, and if they learn how wrong, they will come, and they will not be slow."

"Why me?" Jonah said. "Why does any of this come through *me*? Find a priest. Find a scholar. Find someone who—"

"We have no priests. No scholars. We have bones, and the bones are scattered across a world that forgot us, and of all the hands those bones have passed through in two centuries, yours is the first that listened." The voice softened. "You think your gift is a curse. It is. We do not insult you by denying it. But it is also a door, carver, and doors open both ways."

"And what's on the other side of this door?"

"A way home. For her." A pause, and when the voice resumed it had gone careful, the way voices go careful around wounds. "Beneath the Ember's shrine there is a door. White stone. Old work, older than the Order, older than the dragons' taming. It is the last door out of Vaelthorne — the one our people did not use, because using it meant admitting there was no country left to stay in. It opens only while the Ember still burns. Only while there is still fire enough to recognize the heir and name her."

"How long?"

Another pause. Longer.

"The Ember is dying, carver. Two hundred years banked and bleeding heat, and the seal the Order laid on it is finally doing its work. Our singers in the deep places say it gutters. A season, perhaps. Less. When it goes out, the door dies with it, and Vaelthorne becomes truly nothing — not erased, but ended. And the girl becomes nothing too. The last heir of a country that never was."

Jonah looked down at Lior. She was watching him with that weather-colored gaze, and she lifted her free hand and pressed it flat against her own chest, over her heart, and then pointed east, toward the wall, toward the mountains, toward the blank space on every map he'd ever smuggled across.

She'd known. However she'd known — the way birds know a storm is coming, the way salmon know the river — she had been walking east since before she found his door. Starving, silent, alone, walking toward a country that didn't exist anymore, because it was the only direction her blood recognized.

"Don't look at me like that," he told her. "I haven't agreed to anything."

"She cannot ask you," the voice said. "Her voice was taken — not mute by birth; the Order's hunters caught up to her mother's party two winters ago, and what was done to her throat was done to keep her from speaking her own name. She has been traveling on faith alone. Faith that the bones would find a listener. Faith that the listener would not be like the others." A bitterness entered the ancient tone, cold as well water. "There have been others, carver. Hands that held our bones and felt only craft. Ears that heard us and chose deafness, because listening costs. You did not choose deafness. Whatever else you are, whatever you have done to survive — you listened."

Jonah stood up. Walked to the window. The snow had stopped and the night was the hard clear black of high winter, stars out in numbers that looked like a conspiracy. Somewhere down the valley, on the smuggler's road, a sled bell rang and faded. Ordinary sounds. Living sounds. His whole world, and it fit inside a single workshop above a road.

"I don't want this," he said to the glass. To the stars. To whoever was listening, which was apparently everybody except anyone useful.

"We know."

"I have a life."

"You have a workshop," the voice said, not unkindly, "and thirty years of voices, and no one living who knows your face well enough to miss you if you vanished. We are sorry. We know that is cruel to say. But we are dead, carver, and the dead have no talent for lying."

His hands had gone cold. Not from the window. From the truth of it, arriving in his chest like a swallowed stone. He thought of the names he'd give if someone asked who would notice his absence, and came up with a fence in Marrowgate who'd miss his deliveries, and a tavern keeper who'd miss his coin, and after that the list just — stopped. Thirty years old and the list just stopped.

Behind him, Lior made a sound.

It was small. Barely a breath shaped into noise. But in the silence her presence imposed on the room, it carried, and he turned, and she was looking at him with her hand still on the bone and her eyes wet and furious — furious, he realized, on his behalf, or maybe on behalf of the truth, which amounts to the same thing in a child.

Then she did something worse. She took her hand off the femur.

The voices came back.

All of them. At once. Thirty years of the dead crashing into Jonah's skull like a wave breaking over a seawall — the sparrow's song, the hunter's grumbling, the drowned woman from the Greywash reciting the names of her children for the ten-thousandth time, the soldier, the midwife, the thief whose last thought had been about a debt — and underneath all of it, vast and patient and sad, the voice of Vaelthorne, no longer needing the bone, speaking directly into the marrow of him:

"*Take her home. Take her home. Take her home.*"

Not a command. A plea. Hundreds of voices braided into one, the whole erased nation begging, and Jonah braced his hands on the windowsill and put his head down and let it wash over him, and understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the exact moment you least want it, that he was going to say yes.

Because he'd spent thirty years listening to the dead complain about unfinished business, and here, at last, was his own.

* * *

He started packing while the girl slept again — she slept the way the starving sleep, sudden and total, like a lamp blown out. Dried meat. Hard cheese. The good rope. His father's ice-axes, which he'd sworn on the old man's grave never to carry east of the Thornfell spine, because east of the spine was where his father had stopped being alive and started being a story.

The femur he wrapped in oilcloth and tucked inside his coat, against his ribs. The voice had gone quiet once Lior released it — the great plea spent, the nation settling back into its long grief — but Jonah could feel it there, warm as a coal, waiting.

At the door he paused, pack on his shoulder, and looked back at the workshop. The carvings on their shelves. The little sparrow, finished at last, holding its small dead song. He'd meant to sell it in spring.

"Keep watch," he told them, and felt absurd, and said it anyway, because the dead liked to be included.

Lior woke when the floor creaked. She was dressed in thirty seconds, blanket rolled and strapped — someone had taught her field habits before her voice was taken, and the thought of who, and what had happened to them, put a fresh stone in the pit of Jonah's stomach.

They went out into the blue-black morning. East, the Thornfells stood up against the stars like the jawbone of the world, and somewhere beyond them lay a blank space on the map where a country used to be, and a white door, and a dying fire, and a season — maybe less.

Lior tugged his sleeve. Pointed east. Looked up at him with an expression that needed no language at all.

"Yeah," Jonah said. "I know. Home."

He locked the workshop door behind them, which was ridiculous — nobody robbed a bone-carver — and turned his face to the mountains, and began to walk.

Three days later, in the low country behind them, a rider in ash-grey livery dismounted at a shrine where the votive candles had guttered strangely, all in the same instant, all in the same direction — east — and smiled without any warmth at all, and sent a raven flying.
