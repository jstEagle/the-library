## 6. Thief's Road

The dead had told him to run, and for once Jonah didn't argue.

He'd spent the night before they left doing what he did best: lying to the world with his hands. He packed like a man going nowhere—left the stove cold, left a half-carved heron on the bench as if he'd only stepped out to fetch water, left the shutters open so the snow would drift in and blur the tracks of anyone who came looking. Thirty years of smuggling taught you that the best hiding place was a story. He was building them a boring one: *nobody here but a carver who stepped out and never came back.*

Lior watched him work from the corner by the door, wrapped in his spare coat, which fit her the way a sail fits a rowboat. She hadn't slept. Neither had he. The sparrow bone in his pocket had gone quiet since she touched it, but the others—the drawer full of them, the walls hung with them—had spent the whole night murmuring like a tide going out. *Take her up. Take her high. The old paths remember.*

"The old paths," he muttered, "are mostly landslides now."

Lior tilted her head at him. She didn't understand the words, but she understood tone. She'd been reading tone since the moment she arrived, the way other children learned letters.

He shrugged into his pack, heavier than he liked and lighter than he needed. "Right," he said. "Up we go."

* * *

There were three ways over the Thornfells, if you asked a mapmaker. The King's Road through Vael Pass, wide enough for wagons and soldiers both. The shepherd trails above Coldmere, where you could move fast if you didn't mind goats judging you. And then there was what the smugglers called the thief's road—which wasn't a road at all, but a rumor stitched together out of game trails, dry streambeds, cliff shelves, and the kind of knowledge that got passed mouth to mouth in dark rooms and died with the men who held it.

Jonah was one of maybe four living people who knew it end to end. Two of the others were dead by winter's start, and the third was Bram, who couldn't be trusted to find his own boots before noon.

They went up behind the workshop, through the black pines, following a seam in the rock that no shepherd would ever bother with because it went nowhere a shepherd wanted. Snow came down soft and thick, erasing them as they climbed, and Jonah kept checking over his shoulder until his neck ached.

Lior climbed like she'd been born on cliffs. That bothered him more than he let himself think about. She was seven, maybe eight, small for either, and she moved over ice-slick stone with a sureness that belonged to a mountain goat or a ghost. When the path narrowed along a shelf with a hundred-foot drop, she crossed it without looking down, without looking anywhere, as though the drop were a thing she'd already made peace with.

"You've done this before," he said, when they stopped to breathe under an overhang. "Haven't you."

She looked at him with those grey eyes, the color of the sky just before snow, and said nothing, because she always said nothing—or nothing he could hold.

The dead in his pocket hummed. Not words. Just a feeling, the way a tuning fork holds a note. *Higher. Keep her higher.*

"I know," he told it. "I'm going."

Lior glanced from him to his pocket and back. Then, deliberately, she pointed at the coat over his chest, where the bones rode, and made a sound—not a word, not quite. A low hum, questioning.

"Yeah," Jonah said. "You hear them too. Don't you."

It wasn't really a question anymore. It hadn't been since the first night, when she'd laid her hand on his carvings and thirty years of voices had gone silent as a held breath. He still didn't know what to do with that. All his life the dead had been his burden and his company, and now this child walked around carrying the off switch in her fingertips, and she didn't even know what she was holding.

He shouldered the pack. "Come on, sprout. Daylight's spending itself faster than I am."

* * *

They made the tree line by late afternoon, where the pines gave up and the world turned to white stone and white sky. Jonah found the first marker exactly where the dead in an old elk rib had sworn it would be: a cairn no taller than his knee, stones stacked in a pattern that looked natural unless you knew that nature didn't stack things in sevens.

"Thief's road," he said, tapping it. "Older than the kingdom, probably. Smugglers used it before your grandmother's grandmother was a rumor." He paused. "Whoever your grandmother was."

Lior crouched and touched the top stone. Her fingers moved across it slowly, tracing, and something crossed her face—recognition, or its shadow. She looked up at him and pointed at the cairn, then at herself, then pressed her palm flat against her own chest.

"No," Jonah said carefully. "This isn't yours. This is mine. Well—ours. The trade's." But even as he said it, he remembered what the wolf femur had told him two nights ago, in that voice like wind through a canyon: *the paths she will walk were hers before they were ours.* He hadn't understood it then. Standing over a seven-stone cairn on a ridge no map admitted to, he started to.

They camped that night in a hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite, out of the wind. Jonah got a fire going small and smokeless, the way you learn to when your livelihood depends on not being seen, and heated flatbread and drippings from the supplies he'd bought with coin he'd meant to retire on.

Lior ate like someone who had known real hunger recently enough to be polite about it—careful, deliberate, saving half without being told. It twisted something in him. He pushed his own portion onto her stone. She pushed it back. They went back and forth twice before he gave up and split it properly, and something almost like a smile moved across her face, quick as a fish under ice.

That was when the wolves started.

Not close. A mile off, maybe two, down in the dark timber—a single voice first, long and rising, then answered, then joined, until the whole slope below them was singing. Jonah's hand went to his knife out of pure habit, then relaxed. Wolves didn't come near fire. They were just talking to each other. Telling each other where they were. Telling the night what they were.

Lior had gone very still.

She set down her bread. She turned her face toward the sound, and Jonah watched something happen to her that he had no name for—watched her go from listening to *answering*, though her mouth never opened. Her eyes had gone bright and wet in the firelight.

"Lior?" he said softly.

She raised one hand: *wait*. Then, barely above breath, she spoke—and for the first time since she'd arrived, she spoke *at* him, words shaped for his ears, in that language older than any kingdom. Three syllables, low and round, like stones turning in a riverbed.

Then she pointed toward the singing dark, and said it again, and pointed at him.

"What?" Jonah said. "What about me?"

She frowned, frustrated. She tried again. She pointed at the wolves, said the word. Pointed at him, said the word. Pointed at herself, shook her head, said a different word—one syllable, quick and light, and he somehow understood *that* one meant *girl*, or *small*, or *last*.

"They're calling you something," he guessed. "Down there."

She shook her head hard. Then, with the patience of someone assembling meaning out of scraps, she took his hand, turned it palm-up, and drew on it with one finger—three shapes, three syllables, pressing each stroke into his skin like she was carving him.

And Jonah Thackeray, who heard the dead speak every day of his life, felt the back of his neck prickle.

Because the word for *wolf*—the true word, the old word, the word the mountains themselves had been built to say—sounded like *Jonah*. Almost exactly. The same bones of sound. *Yoh-nah.* A breath, a fall, a rising.

"That's—" He laughed, short and disbelieving. "That can't be right. That's coincidence. That's just how languages are, sometimes words land close—"

But Lior was nodding, solemn as a priest, and pointing at him again, and saying it once more, softer: *yoh-nah*. Wolf. Him.

Somewhere below, the pack sang again, and this time Jonah heard it differently. Heard the shape of his own name running through the chorus like a thread through cloth.

The dead in his pocket stirred—not humming now, but speaking, one voice, the old canyon-wind voice of the wolf femur he'd left behind in the workshop, carried somehow in memory through the others: *We told you the paths were hers. We could have told you the name was too. Some things you have to hear yourself.*

"Stop that," Jonah said aloud, to his own coat. "Just—stop that."

Lior watched him talk to nothing, and for the first time she didn't look afraid of it. She looked like a person watching weather she recognized.

* * *

Three days up, the thief's road did what the thief's road always did eventually: it tried to kill them.

The route crossed a shoulder of the mountain by way of a frozen waterfall—a climbable pitch of blue ice, forty feet of it, with a traverse at the top onto a ledge system that would carry them around the shoulder and down into the next valley. In summer it was a wet scramble. In deep winter it was a knife. Jonah had done it twice, both times younger, colder-blooded, and considerably less encumbered by a child.

"We could go around," he said, studying it. Going around meant dropping low, near the valleys, near roads, near eyes. Near whatever the Ashen Order sent after a girl who spoke a dead language.

Lior looked at the ice. Then at him. Then she took off his oversized coat, folded it, set it on a rock, and started up.

"Hey—hey! Sprout—" But she was already ten feet up, moving with that impossible sureness, finding holds in the ice that Jonah couldn't see until she'd used them. He swore under his breath, stowed the coat, and followed, because the alternative was letting a mute child lead him over a mountain alone, which was somehow worse than dying on a frozen waterfall.

Halfway up, his boot edge sheared off a lip of ice and his whole weight swung onto his axe, and for one long second the world was nothing but blue glass and the sound of his own heart. Below him the drop yawned, white and patient. Above him Lior had stopped, wedged into a stance no eight-year-old should know, looking down at him with those grey eyes.

*Yoh-nah,* she said. Firmly. Like a command.

And Jonah—God help him—found his next hold. Found the one after that. Climbed because a child had said *wolf* at him in a language two centuries dead, and some animal part of his brain decided that was an order worth obeying.

They came over the top together and lay gasping on the ledge, side by side, breath steaming into a sky the color of hammered tin.

"Never," Jonah wheezed, "do that again."

Lior rolled her head sideways to look at him. And she smiled. Not the fish-under-ice flicker from the fire—a real one, crooked and warm and entirely too big for her thin face, and Jonah felt it land somewhere in his chest that he'd boarded up years ago and forgotten the location of.

"There it is," he said quietly. "Knew you had one."

* * *

They fell into a rhythm after that, the way travelers do, the way any two creatures do when survival stops being negotiable. He taught her the practical grammar of staying alive: how to read a ridgeline for shelter, how to melt snow in a cloth against skin instead of wasting fuel, how to walk so the ground kept your secrets. She taught him things he had no grammar for. Which plants were *awake* and which were *sleeping*—she'd touch a bare shrub and shake her head, touch another and nod, and he'd mark it, and by God she was never wrong. Where water ran under ice. When weather was coming, hours before the sky agreed.

And she taught him words. One or two a night, by firelight, carved into his palm with her finger. *Fire.* *Eat.* *Sleep.* *Go.* His vocabulary in Old Vaelthorni grew the way a smuggler's does—crooked, practical, all verbs and warnings.

On the fifth night, in a hollow beneath an overhang furred with frost-flowers, she taught him the word for *home*.

It was four syllables, and the last one broke downward, the way a voice breaks when it says something it doesn't believe it will get back. She said it while looking east, past the fire, past the dark, toward something Jonah couldn't see. Then she looked at him, and her expression asked a question she had no Common Tongue to build.

"Yeah," he said, because it was the only honest answer. "I'll take you there. Or I'll die on the way, and honestly, sprout, the dead tell me those might be the same thing."

She didn't understand the words. She understood the promise. She leaned sideways until her shoulder rested against his arm, feather-light, the way you lean on something you're not yet sure will hold, and Jonah sat very still and let her, and stared into the fire, and did not think about everyone he had ever promised anything to.

The wolves sang again that night, closer now, downslope in the black timber. And this time, when the chorus rose, Lior lifted her voice and answered—three syllables, *yoh-nah, yoh-nah*—and the pack went quiet, and then, faint and far, sang it back.

Jonah sat in the firelight with his own name coming up out of the dark in a dead tongue, worn like a title, and understood that something had changed that he couldn't undo. Somewhere between the waterfall and the frost-flowers, the girl had stopped being cargo. She was cargo the way a lit fuse is cargo.

He banked the fire and took first watch, and that was when he saw it.

Across the valley, on the far ridge, where no road ran and no sane traveler would be—a light. Small. Moving. Steady as a procession. Then a second beside it. Then a third, spaced with the discipline of men who had done this before, sweeping the heights in a line, the way you search for something when you already know roughly where it is.

Not hunters. Hunters' lights wander. These lights *marched*.

Jonah put a hand gently over Lior's sleeping shoulder and felt her come awake instantly, completely, the way animals wake. He pointed. She saw. Her breath caught—a small sound, the first sound of fear he'd ever heard her make—and her hand found his sleeve and gripped it.

Below the far ridge, faint on the wind, came something worse than lights. A horn. One long note, flat and grey, like the sound a country makes when it's being erased.

The Order had found the thief's road.

Or—Jonah's stomach turned as the thought finished arriving—they had found what lay at the end of it. Because the line of lights wasn't following his path at all. It was heading straight for the next valley over. The one their route dropped into tomorrow. The one where, according to an elk rib and a dead woman's memory, there stood a village that had been Vaelthorni once, before the burning—

Except the dead had also said, in the voice of a drowned ferryman, four words Jonah hadn't let himself examine until now:

*There is no village.*
