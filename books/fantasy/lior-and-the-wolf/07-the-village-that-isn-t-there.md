## 7. The Village That Isn't There

The valley opens before them like a held breath.

Lior feels it first in her chest—a pulling, a tugging, the way a compass needle must feel when it finally swings true. She stops on the ridge with snow to her knees and her breath steaming, and she looks down at the green sweep of it, and something inside her that has been silent since the fire begins to sing.

*Home.*

Not home. Not exactly. But shaped like home. Built over the bones of home.

She doesn't have words for this. She has never had words for anything, not in the tongue of roads and coins that Jonah speaks, not even in the old language that lives in her mouth like a bird she cannot release. What she has is knowing—knowledge that arrives whole, without doors, the way you know your own name or the warmth of a hand.

And what she knows now is: *there should be roofs there.*

She points. Jonah follows her finger with his eyes, squinting against the afternoon light slanting through the Thornfell passes.

"What is it?" he asks. His voice is rough from three days of climbing, but gentle. He has learned to be gentle with her silence, to treat it not as an empty room but as a room where someone is thinking.

She points again, harder, insistent. Down. There. Where the river bends.

"I see the river," Jonah says slowly. "I see fields."

But Lior sees more than fields. She sees, layered beneath the grass like writing under erased ink, the ghost-shapes of what was. A village. Forty hearths, maybe fifty. She knows this without counting, knows it the way she knew the door in her dreams—the white door, the voice calling *the last, the last*. The houses stood along two streets that met at a well. The well is still there. She can see it, a low ring of stones in the middle of the nearest field, absurd and alone, like a tooth left in an empty jaw.

Everything else is gone. Not ruined—*gone*. No foundations. No char. No rubble. Even ruins remember what they were. This land has been made to forget itself.

Lior starts down the slope without waiting for Jonah. Her legs are short and the snow fights her, but grief is a kind of fuel, and she burns down the hillside in stumbling leaps until the grass takes her, deep summer-green in the mountain rain-shadow, soft as a bed.

Jonah catches up to her at the well. He is breathing hard, one hand pressed to his ribs where the old scar pulls, and he looks at the stone ring with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"This well's cut granite," he says. "Thornfell villages build in timber and fieldstone. Granite like this comes from—" He stops. His jaw works. "It comes from over the mountains. From places that don't exist anymore."

Lior kneels at the well's edge. The stones are warm under her palms. Warm, in this high cold valley, as if something underneath them is still breathing.

She leans over and looks down into the dark, and the dark looks back with the smell of water and old ash, and far below—so far below it might be memory instead of depth—she hears singing.

Her people sang at wells. She knows this suddenly, wholly, the way all her knowings come. They sang when they drew water because water remembers being sky, and songs helped it remember kindly. Women with braided hair. Men with bells on their sleeves. Children dropping pebbles to hear the splash-answer.

She drops a pebble.

No splash comes.

She waits. She counts—one, two, three, four, five—in the way of children everywhere, though she does not know whose numbers these are, whether they are Common numbers or older ones. No splash. No echo. The pebble simply falls and falls and does not arrive anywhere.

Jonah watches her face. "What is it?"

She looks up at him, and for once she wishes desperately that she could speak his language, could say: *the well is broken, not the stone, the well itself, they broke the middle of it, they took the sound out.* Instead she mimes it—drops an invisible pebble, cups her ear, shakes her head.

"No bottom?" Jonah frowns and picks up a stone himself, a heavy gray thing, and drops it in.

Nothing. The stone vanishes into darkness without a sound, swallowed the way a word is swallowed by a censor's pen.

Jonah goes very still. Lior has learned his stillnesses in their days together. There is the stillness of a man listening to a carving, when his eyes go distant and his thumb finds the knife-handle at his belt. There is the stillness of danger, when his whole body becomes a held wire. And there is this third stillness, rarest, worst: the stillness of a man watching the world prove itself wrong.

"The dead told me about this," he says quietly. "They said the Order didn't just burn Vaelthorne. They unmade it. Records, names, maps." He crouches beside her at the well. "I thought they meant paper. I thought they meant *memory*."

He puts his hand flat on the warm stone, and Lior sees him flinch—not from heat, but from whatever whispers up through his palm into the place where he keeps his ghosts.

"They meant the ground," he says.

* * *

They walk the valley while the light goes long and gold.

Lior leads, because her feet know the way even though her mind holds only fragments. Left at the river bend. Up the rise where the granary should be. Along the street that isn't a street anymore, just a line in the grass where nothing grows quite as tall, as if the packed earth of two hundred years of footsteps still refuses to let weeds take hold.

She walks the street of her ancestors and the grass parts for her feet.

Here stood the baker's house. Here the house of the woman who kept bees, who wore smoke-colored shawls. Here the meeting-hall with its carved lintel, its white door—no, not *the* white door, that one was far away, in the mountain's heart, but a smaller door, painted white so every child would learn what the great door looked like. *Practice doors*, her knowing tells her. *So no heir would ever stand before the true door and find it strange.*

She stands in the footprint of the practice door and presses her hand against air, and for half a heartbeat she swears she feels wood grain under her fingers, smooth and sun-warmed, and paint flaking like old snow.

Then it's gone, and she's touching wind, and the valley is only a valley.

Behind her, Jonah has stopped walking. He stands in the middle of the unnamed street with his arms hanging at his sides, turning slowly, and his face is doing something complicated. Lior has seen him grieve already—grieve the sparrow, grieve the smuggled boy whose bone flute he'd carried for a year—but this is different. This is a man discovering that the crime he's been walking toward is larger than any crime he can hold.

"There were people here," he says. Not a question. He says it like a man planting a flag in territory the world denies exists. "There were *people here*. Fields don't plow themselves. Wells don't cut themselves. Somebody built all this, and loved somebody in it, and buried somebody under it—and now there's not even a *name* for the place. Not on any map. Not in any mouth." His voice cracks on the last word. "You can kill everyone in a country. That's terrible enough. But to kill the *country*—to reach forward through time and strangle it in every throat that would have spoken it—"

He stops. Looks at her. And Lior watches him understand, watch it land on him like a dropped roof-beam, that the same fate nearly found her. That somewhere in some ash-stained scriptorium there is a ledger with a line for *Lior*, blank and waiting, and men riding toward her with pens instead of swords.

"We keep moving," Jonah says. His voice has changed. It's harder now, edged with something new. "Tonight we rest, tomorrow we climb. But Lior—" he crouches to her level, and his eyes are wet and furious at once, "—they didn't erase you yet. You hear me? You're still here. You're still *real*. As long as you walk and breathe and remember, they haven't finished."

Lior reaches out and touches his face, the way you touch something precious and breakable. Then she turns back to the valley, because there is one more place her feet insist on going.

* * *

The graveyard is on the hill above the village, and it is the worst thing in the valley, because it is almost intact.

The Order's erasure swept the living places clean—took the houses, the names, the streets—but here, where the dead sleep, the unmaking went shallow. Perhaps even the Ashen Order hesitated at graves. Or perhaps forgetting works less well on things already forgotten.

The headstones stand in crooked rows among the wildflowers, and they are blank.

Every single one. Smooth, featureless stone, as if a hundred masons had labored to carve nothing at all. Lior walks between them and reads them anyway, because her blood can read what the chisels were made to forget. Names surface under her fingertips like fish rising in a pond: *Maren. Osk. Vael-Anneth. Little Tam, four winters.* Each name arrives with a flicker of a face, a life, a death—fever, childbirth, the long sleep of the very old—and each time, Lior stops and stands a moment in respect, a small ambassador paying calls on her scattered nation.

At the last row, the newest row, the stones change.

These are scorched black at the edges. And these are not blank. These bear names, cut deep and defiant, in letters Lior's fingers know as intimately as her own heartbeat:

Old Vaelthorni.

The final burials. The ones cut in haste, in grief, in the last days when the dragons were dying and the fires were coming and someone—some stonemason who knew what was coming—had decided that if the world would not remember, then the stones would, and damn the cost.

Lior kneels at the first of them. The name there is *Sera*, and beneath it, smaller: *who chose to stay.*

She does not know why the name makes her chest hurt. She barely knows why any of this makes her chest hurt—these are strangers, these are *ancestors*, these are hers, all of it tangled together, grief without a floor. But she kneels in the trampled grass of a country that officially never existed, and she does the only thing she can do, the thing her body knows even when her mind is a fog of fragments.

She sings.

It comes out of her small and wavering at first, then stronger—a mourning song, a keening, the kind sung at wells and gravesides, the kind that helps the dead remember kindly. The melody is older than the mountains around it. The syllables are Old Vaelthorni, rolling and mournful, and they pour out of her into the golden evening air while Jonah stands at the graveyard gate with his hat in his hands like a man at a stranger's funeral, which is exactly what he is, and exactly what he isn't.

The blank stones seem to listen.

And then—Lior's song catching in her throat, her eyes going wide—the blank stones begin to *answer*.

Faint. Faint as breath on a winter window. But rising out of the smooth featureless granite, thread by thread, come lines of writing. Pale as frost. Names surfacing on the unmarked graves, letter by letter, called back across two hundred years of enforced silence by a child's voice speaking the tongue their carvers died to preserve.

*Maren. Osk. Vael-Anneth. Little Tam, four winters.*

The names hang on the stones like frost-flowers, trembling, and Lior understands with a jolt that they will fade—that the erasure is deeper than ink, that her song can summon the names but perhaps not keep them—but for this moment, this one impossible golden moment, the graveyard of Vaelthorne remembers itself.

Jonah makes a sound behind her. When she turns, he is crying without any attempt to hide it, tears cutting lines through the trail dust on his face, and he is saying something over and over in a whisper, and it takes her a moment to understand that he is reading the names aloud. All of them. Learning them. Carving them somewhere no Ashen pen can ever reach.

"Little Tam," he says. "Four winters." His voice breaks. "Somebody should say it. Somebody should *say* it."

Lior crosses to him and takes his big scarred hand in both of hers.

They stand together in the settling dusk, the smuggler and the last heir, in a graveyard that is forgetting itself again even as they watch, the pale names dimming on the stones like stars going out at dawn. And it is there, in that fading light, with her hand in his, that Lior feels it—a pull, faint but unmistakable, rising from the high pass at the valley's head, from the glacier beyond, from the mountain's heart.

The same pull she felt at the well. The same warmth. But stronger now, closer, urgent.

Somewhere above the snowline, something is burning low.

Something is running out of time.

She tugs Jonah's hand and points up-valley, toward the pass, toward the darkening ice, and makes the gesture she has been making for days, the one he has finally learned to read: *that way. Hurry.*

Jonah wipes his face with his sleeve, looks once more at the fading names, and nods.

"Aye," he says softly. "We're coming."
