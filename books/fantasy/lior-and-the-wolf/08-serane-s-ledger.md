## 8. Serane's Ledger

The ledger had no title. That was the first thing they taught you in the scriptorium beneath the Ashen Citadel: a book that names itself can be named by others, and what can be named can be remembered, and what can be remembered can be *found*. So the ledger was bound in grey calfskin with no lettering at all, and it was kept in a room with no door handle on the inside, and Serane Voss had carried a copy of it in her saddlebag for eleven years without once speaking its contents aloud.

She opened it now in the way station at Coldmarch, alone, one candle lit, the shuttered window rattling with the first real snow of the season.

The pages were not written so much as *kept*. Each entry was a thing the Order had unmade — a village, a family line, a song, a god whose name had taken four years of careful work to pull out of every mouth that held it. The ink was ordinary. The power was in the discipline. Every unmaking left a seam, and seams itch, and an Inquisitor of the Third Rank was trained to feel the itching the way a sailor feels weather in his teeth.

Serane ran her thumb down the entries and stopped where she always stopped, though she never let her eyes linger there longer than a breath.

*Vaelthorne.*

Not the name itself, of course. The name was gone; even in her own head she could only approach it sideways, the way you approach a burned house by the smell of it. The entry read, in the old cipher: *the green country, the white door, the long fire.* Beneath it, columns of subtractions. Eleven thousand souls unmade from parish rolls and tax ledgers and gravestones. Two hundred and six songs pulled from the air like splinters. One language, dried up word by word until the last native speakers died believing they'd spoken nothing at all.

And one loose thread, walking east through the Thornfells with a smuggler who carved bones.

She closed the ledger and sat for a moment listening to the snow.

Eleven years, and she had never once been sent to hunt anything like this. The Order's work was usually quiet — a records office here, a widow who remembered too much there, a slow patient sanding-down of the world until it was smooth where a country used to be. But three weeks ago a report had come up the chain from a border priest, half-mad with fear, babbling about a child who spoke in the old tongue, and the Citadel had moved the way the Citadel only moved when the ember was involved. They had put *her* on the road. They had given her twelve riders and a writ of ash and told her to bring back either the child or proof of ash, and they had looked at her when they said it — all of them, the Archivist, the Provost, the grey-faced men of the Inner Table — a half-second too long.

They knew she was born there. Of course they knew. That was why they'd chosen her. A hound that has smelled the quarry's hearth-smoke runs faster, and if it ever turned, well — you always know where the kennel is.

Serane snuffed the candle and went out into the snow to check the horses.

* * *

She had been seven years old the night her mother woke her by putting a hand flat over her mouth.

That was how you learned things in the green country before the end: not with words but with hands. A hand over the mouth meant *be quiet*. A hand pressed twice on the shoulder meant *come*. Her mother's hand smelled of tallow and bread flour and, underneath, faintly, of the shrine-oil they kept for the dragon's ember — a smell like warm stone and something almost sweet, almost rotten, that Serane would spend the rest of her life unable to describe to anyone because no one else alive had ever smelled it.

They went down through the fields in the dark. Behind them the manor hill was burning, though burning wrong — the flames were white, and they made no heat, and they did not spread. They simply stood on the hilltop like a crowd of pale witnesses. Later she would learn the word for it. *Unmaking-fire.* It didn't destroy a thing. It destroyed the fact that the thing had ever been.

Her mother carried her past the shrine where the old woman tended the guttering scale, and the old woman was standing outside the shrine door, which she had never done in her life, and she said something in the old tongue that Serane was too young and too terrified to hold onto. Her mother answered, also in the old tongue, and then kissed her forehead and set her down behind a drystone wall and said, in Common, because Common was for hiding:

"Whatever happens, you are nobody. You are from nowhere. If anyone asks, your name is Sera and your village is Marrowford and your father is a wheelwright. Say it until it's true."

"What happens to you?"

"I'm going back for your brother."

Her brother. She tried, over the following decades, to remember his face, and could not, and eventually understood that this was not grief failing her. Grief had nothing to do with it. There was no face left to fail toward. He had gone into the white fire after their mother, or the fire had gone over him, and somewhere in the Citadel's deep archive there was a page in grey cipher recording the subtraction of him, and Serane Voss had read that page a hundred times looking for some detail — a birthmark, a height, a habit — that would let her mourn something specific, and the page gave her nothing, because that was the entire point of the page.

She was found three days later at Marrowford, which was a real place forty miles west, by an Ashen outrider named Doss who fed her barley soup and asked her questions in a kind voice. She said her name was Sera. She said her father was a wheelwright. She said it until it was true, and then it was true, and the relief of that was the most obscene feeling she had ever known — better than safety, better than love, the pure clean relief of being *permitted to exist*, which she understood later was exactly the drug the Order dealt, and dealt deliberately, to every child it scraped off the edge of an erasure.

They took the survivors. There were never many. The fire took the ones who ran too slow, and the forgetting took the ones who stayed, and the ones who were carried out in time were given soup and kindness and a new name and a place at the Order's table, and were watched for the rest of their lives.

Doss had watched her for thirty years, right up until he died, and on his deathbed he had gripped her wrist and said, "You never once slipped, girl. Not once in thirty years." And she had felt — God help her — *pride*.

That was the wound. Not the fire. Not the lost brother, the lost language, the lost smell of shrine-oil. The wound was that she had gotten good at it. That survival had turned, year by year, into excellence, and excellence into rank, and rank into a grey calfskin book and twelve riders and a writ of ash, so that now the last living piece of the green country came walking east out of the mountains and the Order's natural choice of hound was a daughter of the country herself, because who better? Who knew the smell of it better?

Who would run faster?

* * *

The itch started at dawn, two days out of Coldmarch, in the hills above the river called — officially, on maps less than sixty years old — the Wrenwater.

Serane felt it in her back teeth first. That was how it always began, a low wrongness like a tooth that needed pulling, and she had learned to read its geography with unsettling precision. An itch meant memory pooling somewhere it shouldn't. A village that sheltered the pair would itch. A grave someone had spoken over in the old words would itch. And a child of the erased blood, moving through country that had once been hers, left a trail of itching behind her like blood in water.

"It's here," she said to Lieutenant Corvas, reining up on the ridge. Below them the Wrenwater ran fast and grey between alder banks, and beyond it the Thornfell passes opened like a hand of dark fingers. "Close. A day. Maybe less."

Corvas, twenty-six years old, born in the Citadel's shadow, squinted at the empty valley and saw nothing, because there was nothing to see. That was the horror of the work, Serane thought, and the comfort of it too. To everyone else this was just wilderness. Only she could feel the place where a valley full of farms and a shrine and a white door had been sanded out of the world, and only she could feel the small hot prickle of a child's passage through it.

"There's nothing down there," Corvas said.

"No," Serane agreed. "There isn't."

She dismounted and walked the ridgeline alone, as she always did, letting the itch sharpen. It strengthened toward the river, toward a ford where the alders grew thick, and she crouched there and found what she expected to find and dreaded finding: hoofprints, small boot-prints, and the droppings of a mule, all less than a day old. And beside them, pressed into the mud at the water's edge, a single print that stopped her breath.

A wolf's print. Enormous. And around it, faintly, in the trampled mud, marks that were not claw-marks and not any tool she knew — deliberate, repeated, curved like script.

The old tongue. Written in mud, by a wolf, or by something wearing a wolf's authority.

Serane knelt there a long moment, and the cold coming off the water rose up through her knees, and for one unguarded second she was seven years old again with her mother's hand over her mouth, smelling tallow and bread flour and warm sweet stone.

Then she stood, and brushed the mud from her knee, and became Sera of nowhere again, because that was the discipline, that was the whole of the discipline, and the discipline was the only thing she owned outright in this world.

"Break camp," she called to the riders. "We cross at the ford. If the wolves are with them, we'll want the horses bunched and the fire-oil ready."

Corvas hesitated. "Wolves don't come near armed companies, Inquisitor."

"These aren't the Order's wolves," Serane said, and heard, as the words left her, how much she had admitted, and decided not to care. "Mount up."

Twelve riders crossed the Wrenwater at midday, grey cloaks whitening with snow, and on the far bank the dark fingers of the Thornfell passes closed around them, and somewhere ahead — a day, maybe less — a smuggler with a knife full of other people's ghosts was teaching a dead nation's last heir the word for wolf.

Serane rode at the head of the column with the ledger against her ribs like a second heart, and told herself the ache under it was just the cold.
