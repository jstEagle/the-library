## 9. Wolves at the Ford

The river came out of the mountains like something fleeing.

Jonah heard it before he saw it—a low, grinding rush under the ice-fringe, the sound of water that had no patience for winter. He stopped at the treeline and let Lior catch up, which she did without a word, because she never used words, only that look she'd perfected in six days on the road: the flat, patient stare of someone waiting for him to explain himself, knowing he wouldn't.

"We cross here," he said. "Greywater Ford. It's shallow enough at this time of year if you keep to the stones."

She looked at the river. She looked back at him. The message was clear: *you first.*

"Charming," Jonah muttered, and started down the bank.

The smuggler's maps in his head had this crossing marked as safe—safe being a relative term, meaning *only likely to kill you in bad weather*. The weather today was merely cold, the sky a lid of grey pressed down close enough to touch, snow coming in fine and dry as ash. He hated how much that word was following them lately. Ash. Every road they took seemed to be leading through the ashes of something.

He tested the first stone with his boot. Solid. The second. Solid, slick with rime. Behind him Lior moved the way she always moved—silent, precise, like a creature that had learned early that noise cost something. He'd tried to teach her a few smuggler's tricks on the road, ways to walk soft over scree, and she'd demonstrated each one better than he could within an hour, then stared at him with something that might have been pity.

Six days since the wolf femur had spoken with the old voice. Six days since the dead had told him what she was—the last heir of a country that didn't exist anymore—and begged him, in voices layered like sediment, to take her home. Six days of walking roads the dead remembered and the living had forgotten, following instructions whispered through bone.

He was tired. He was scared. And some traitorous part of him, the part that had spent thirty years alone with nothing but corpses' chatter for company, was happier than he'd been since boyhood.

He reached the middle of the ford. The water ran shin-deep around the stones, grey-green and viciously cold, carrying slush downstream in ragged white ribbons. Halfway. The far bank rose into pine shadow, dark and close.

That was when the wolves came.

They came out of the trees on the near side, where he'd just been, which meant they'd been following them—how long, he couldn't say. Four of them, big timber wolves, winter-thin and moving with the loose economy of things that had never once needed to hurry. They fanned along the bank without a sound. No hackles up, no snarling. That was the worst part. Snarling wolves were making a speech. These ones weren't saying anything at all.

"Lior." His voice came out low and even, the voice he used for customs men. "Don't run. Get behind me. Slow."

She was three stones behind him, mid-crossing, and he watched her turn and see them, and watched her do the thing that made his chest go tight every time: she didn't panic. She went still. Utterly still, the way prey goes still, except there was nothing of prey in it. She tilted her head, the way she did when she was listening to something he couldn't hear.

*Oh no,* Jonah thought. *Not now. Not here.*

Because he knew what she listened to. Or he thought he did. The dead. But the dead were quiet in her presence—that was the whole strange mercy of her, the silence she carried like a lamp. So what was she hearing?

The wolves stepped onto the ice-fringed shallows. One put a paw in the water and flinched it back, and Jonah understood suddenly, with a smuggler's cold arithmetic, exactly how this went. Wolves this thin, this deep in winter, this deep in mountains where the Order's patrols had probably cleaned out every deer for miles—they were hungry, and two slow creatures stuck in a river were the easiest meal in Thornfell.

He had a knife. He had a bone-handled knife and thirty years of dirty tricks, and neither would matter against four wolves in open water.

"Lior," he said again, softer. "Come to me. Eyes forward. Don't look at them."

But she was looking at them. She was looking at them the way she'd looked at his carvings that first night, when the dead had gone silent—as if she recognized something.

And then the largest wolf, the lead one, a grizzled female with frost in her muzzle, met Lior's eyes.

Jonah felt the change come over the air like a pressure drop before a storm. The river's grinding roar seemed to fall away from them, or maybe it was his own blood getting loud. The she-wolf stood in the shallows, water breaking white around her legs, and stared at the girl, and her ears came slowly forward—not aggression, not fear. Attention. The attention of something very old meeting something older.

Lior spoke.

It was the first time Jonah had ever heard her use that voice at anything like volume. Six days of silence, and now a single phrase in Old Vaelthorni, round and resonant, vowels that seemed to have weight the way stones have weight. He didn't know the words. He knew what they did.

The she-wolf lowered her head. Not cowering—something more deliberate than that, almost formal, the way a soldier salutes. Then she turned, waded back to the bank, shook the water from her coat, and looked once more at the other three.

They turned aside. All of them. They melted back into the pines without a sound, and the last Jonah saw of them was the grizzled female pausing at the treeline to look back—not at Lior now, but at the far shore, the direction they'd been heading, as if confirming the way. Then gone.

The pressure lifted. The river came roaring back into the world.

Jonah discovered he was gripping his knife so hard his hand had gone numb. He made himself breathe. Made himself turn and finish the crossing, stone by stone, because standing in the middle of a freezing river was no place to have a revelation.

On the far bank, in the pine shadow, Lior waited for him. Her face was pale, and there was something frightened in it now—the fear arriving late, after the fact, the way it does with children who are brave before they understand what they've done.

"What," Jonah said, and had to start again. "What was that."

She shrugged. A child's shrug. *I don't know.* Except he was fairly sure she did know, or half-knew, the way you half-know your own name.

"The wolves," he said. "They obeyed you."

Another shrug, smaller. Then she pointed at herself, and then at the ground, and then made a gesture he'd seen her make before—both hands spreading outward, wide, taking in the valley, the mountain, everything. Herself and the land, joined by a line her finger drew in the air.

*The country,* he thought. *She means the country. Vaelthorne.*

The dead had said it often enough, murmuring through his carvings while she slept: *the heir, the heir, the blood remembers, the land will know its own.* He'd taken it for poetry. Dead men loved poetry; it was all they had left. But the wolves hadn't read any poems. The wolves had heard a command in a language two centuries buried, and their spines had bent to it.

His gift had never done anything like that. Thirty years of hearing the dead, and not once had the living world bent an inch for him. Whatever ran in this child's veins, it wasn't a trick of carving. It was a birthright, and birthrights had owners, and owners had hunters.

Which was when he noticed the smoke.

Thin, wrong, deliberate—one thread rising off the ridge above the ford, where no sensible traveler would camp with the light dying. Hunters sometimes smoked meat up there. But hunters didn't post a lookout, and someone was posted; he could feel it, that prickle between the shoulder blades that thirty years of smuggling had tuned finer than any instrument. Being watched. Being counted.

"Hunters," he said aloud, for her benefit, and touched two fingers to his eyes, then toward the ridge. *We're seen.* Her chin dipped in understanding.

He turned upriver along the bank, walking easy, unhurried, a man stretching his legs, and steered them both into the deeper trees where the shadows pooled blue. Only when the ridge was lost behind the trunks did he let the pace quicken.

Because here was the thing about hunters in Thornfell in the dead of winter, in a stretch of country where the Order had been asking questions in every village for a week: real hunters stayed near their snares. Real hunters didn't sit ridgelines over fords. And real hunters, Jonah thought grimly, stepping over a deadfall with Lior flowing past him like water, real hunters didn't wear boots with military heels.

He'd seen the prints on the near bank, right where the wolves had been. He'd said nothing. Some knowledge you carry quietly, the way you carry a blade.

They walked until the light failed, and made a cold camp in a hollow under a leaning granite slab, no fire, sharing dried fish and a heel of black bread. Lior ate in small precise bites, watching the dark between the trees. Jonah sat with his back to the stone and turned the day over in his mind like a bone in his hands, looking for the shape inside it.

Wolves that bowed. Scouts that followed. A girl whose blood could command beasts, hunted by men who unmade countries.

"You know they'll come tonight or tomorrow," he said quietly. He'd taken to talking to her even though she couldn't answer; it kept him honest. "The men on the ridge. Maybe dressed as hunters, maybe not bothering anymore. There'll be more of them than I'd like and fewer options than I'd want." He worked the knife out of its sheath and began, absently, to clean his nails with it. "There's a path ahead—the dead showed me. Old drover's road, high up. It bends east around a gorge called the Shepherd's Noose. If we can get past the Noose, we're into country even the Order doesn't remember exists. Because they erased it. Their own blind spot." He smiled without humor. "Funny thing about burning every map. Sometimes you burn the one that would help you."

Lior had stopped eating. She was looking at him with that total attention, and he realized she'd caught something in his voice—hearing it the way she heard everything, underneath the words.

"I'm not going to pretend this is safe," he said. "I don't lie to partners."

Something flickered across her face at the word. *Partners.* She set down the fish, and then, deliberately, she did something new. She pointed at him. Held up one finger. Then pointed at herself, held up another finger beside the first. Two fingers, standing together in the fireless dark.

Partners. Two.

Jonah Thackeray, who had spent thirty years being one, looked at those two small fingers and felt something in his chest crack along an old seam.

"Yeah," he said, rougher than he meant to. "Two."

She almost smiled. It was the closest yet—a ghost of one, there and gone, and it did more damage to him than the wolves had.

Then her head snapped toward the trees, and every trace of the smile vanished.

Jonah was moving before his mind caught up, knife out, pulling her back against the stone. Down the slope, back the way they'd come, a light was moving through the forest. A lantern, hooded, swinging low and steady. Not searching—tracking. Coming straight for them with the confidence of someone who already knew.

And behind the lantern-light, faint on the wind, he caught it: a sound he knew from his childhood in the port rows, a sound soldiers made when they thought no civilians were listening. The low double whistle. *Contact. Hold.*

Not hunters.

Lior's hand found his sleeve. Her grip was small and iron-hard. She looked up at him, and her mouth opened, and what came out wasn't Old Vaelthorni this time—it was broken, halting Common Tongue, the first words he'd ever heard her attempt, bought at a price he couldn't guess:

"Wolf-word. What... is it?"

Jonah stared at her. Of all the moments for her to choose to break her silence, of all the questions—

"It means *turn aside*," he whispered. "Why? What are you—"

Her eyes weren't on him. They were on the darkness beyond the lantern, deeper in the trees, where two points of green light had opened like eyes.

The wolves had come back.

All of them. And more than four now—a dozen shapes flowing silent down the slope between the girl and the men with the lantern, placing themselves, Jonah realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, between Lior and the Order.

The she-wolf's head turned. She looked at Lior. Waiting.

Lior's hand tightened on Jonah's sleeve, and she spoke into the dark—one word this time, that same weighted, stone-heavy word from the ford, but changed at the end, bent into something new. A question, or a plea, or an order he couldn't parse.

The green eyes swung—to the wolves' left, to Jonah's right—and the pack poured away uphill, toward the lantern, toward the whistling men, and the night filled with sounds Jonah would spend years trying not to remember.

He pulled Lior up the slope in the opposite direction, running blind through the pines, her hand locked in his, the screams fading behind them, and one thought hammering with every stride:

If her blood could raise a country's wolves in her defense, then the Order wasn't hunting a mute girl anymore.

They were hunting a queen.
