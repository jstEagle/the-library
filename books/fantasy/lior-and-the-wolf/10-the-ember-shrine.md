## 10. The Ember Shrine

The cave breathed.

Jonah felt it before he saw anything—a slow exhalation of warm air against his face, rising out of the dark like something sleeping and alive. After six days in the high passes, where every wind cut like a whittling knife, warmth was the last thing he expected to find under a mountain. He stopped on the threshold, one hand on Lior's shoulder, and listened with the part of him that had nothing to do with ears.

*Here,* said a voice in the bone at his throat. He'd carved it from a hawk's wishbone outside Thornfell's shadow, and the dead who spoke through it had been growing more urgent for days, their words tumbling over each other like water over stones. *Here, here, carver. Hurry. The light is thin.*

Lior felt it too. He could tell by the way she went still—not the stillness of fear but of recognition, the way a dog goes still at the scent of home. She stepped past him into the dark without waiting, and her small hand found the wall and followed it, sure as if she'd walked this passage a hundred times.

"Slow down," Jonah muttered, though she couldn't hear him, though half the time he wasn't sure she was listening even when he spoke with his hands. They'd built a language of gestures across those six days—*food*, *cold*, *danger*, *rest*—and it was enough, mostly. It was more than he'd had with anyone living in thirty years, which frightened him in a way he didn't examine.

He lit no torch. The dead had told him not to: *the shrine knows its own; bring no borrowed fire.* So he followed the girl and the faint gold thread of warmth through a passage that narrowed, widened, narrowed again, until the walls fell away all at once and they stood in a chamber vast enough that his smuggler's instincts measured it in cargo-holds and found the number absurd.

And there, in the center of the dark, hung a single scale the size of a dinner plate.

It floated—or seemed to float—suspended above a basin of black stone by nothing Jonah could name. It was the color of old embers, gray shot through with veins of dying red, and it pulsed. Slowly. Like breath. Like a heart that had been beating for two hundred years and was very, very tired.

"Oh," Jonah said, because there was nothing else to say.

Lior walked straight to it.

She didn't hesitate, didn't flinch from the heat that rolled off it in waves—he could feel it from ten paces away, a hearth-heat, a summer-field heat—and when her fingers rose toward the scale, Jonah opened his mouth to shout *don't* and the word died before it left his teeth.

Because the scale sang.

Not sound, exactly. Not the way bones sang for him, the dry whisper of the dead caught in carved things. This was older. Deeper. A resonance that came up through the floor and through the soles of his boots and settled somewhere behind his sternum, and Jonah understood, with the certainty of a man falling, that this was what Vaelthorne had sounded like. All of it. Every bell and every voice and every forge-fire, folded into one note and held for two centuries by a scale that refused, out of sheer stubbornness, to go dark.

Lior touched the scale.

The chamber filled with light.

* * *

Fire first. Always fire first.

Lior stood inside the memory and did not scream, because she had learned—in the other place, the white place, the place before the cold world—that screaming changed nothing. The fire roared around her like a living thing with a hundred mouths, and through the fire she saw them: towers of white stone going soft and bending like candle wax, gardens turning to ash mid-bloom, people running with their shadows thrown huge and black ahead of them. A dragon's silhouette crossed the burning sky, wings wide as weather, and it was not the dragon that burned the country. It was flying *away*. Fleeing. And something else pursued it, something made of smoke and order and hunger, and where that thing passed, the fire followed like a dog follows its master.

Then the fire parted, and she saw the door.

White. Tall as a mountain's shoulder, set into the roots of a peak, carved with script that moved like water. Around its base, dragons—real ones, dozens of them, or their shapes at least, coiled and sleeping and burning low, each one a banked furnace keeping one small flame alive. Guardians. The last of them. And standing before the door, a woman with ash in her hair pressed a hand to Lior's cheek—the cheek of a much younger Lior, a toddler Lior, a Lior who could not yet have known what was being taken from her—and said words in the old tongue:

*Run, little ember. Run until the world forgets you, and then run further, until only we remember. We will keep your door. We will keep your name. Come back before the last flame gutters, and wake us.*

*You are the last. You are the last. You are the—*

The vision folded like paper.

Lior came back to herself kneeling on cold stone, her palms flat against the basin's rim, tears freezing on her face before they could fall. Above her the scale pulsed, dimmer now. She had felt it give something to her—felt it the way you feel a coin lifted from your pocket, a small lightness, a loss. It had shown her the door. It had shown her the way. And it had shown her, in the space of a single heartbeat at the end, its own future: the veins of red going gray, going darker gray, going nothing, and a door that would never open again, and a country that would sleep forever behind it, unremembered even by itself.

One season. Maybe less. The vision hadn't used words for it, but she'd understood anyway, the way she understood most things: whole and wordless, like weather.

Behind her, the bone-carver made a sound she'd never heard him make. A small, broken noise, half a word, half a prayer.

She turned. He was on his knees in the dark, both hands pressed flat over his chest, and his face—his hard, closed, competent face, the face of a man who'd spent thirty years pretending the voices were manageable—was wet with tears he clearly didn't know were falling.

"They're singing," he said hoarsely, in the trade-tongue, and then, catching himself, switched to the handful of hand-signs they shared, and then gave up and just said it again, louder, as if volume could carry meaning across the gap between them. "Lior—they're *singing*. All of them. I've heard the dead my whole life, one at a time, begging and muttering and—this is different. This is—" His hands shook. "There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. An entire country of dead, and they're all singing the same song, and the song is *her name*, and I think—I think the song is *you*."

Lior wiped her face with her sleeve. She looked at the scale, and the scale pulsed, and in its pulse she felt a question form—not in words, never in words, but as clear as any word:

*Will you come? Will you come before the flame goes out?*

She put her hand flat against her own chest, over her heart, the way the ash-haired woman had done in the vision, and she nodded.

The scale flared—one great breath of red-gold light that threw their shadows huge against the cavern walls—and in that flare, Lior saw the path. Not a vision this time. A map, burned into the back of her eyes: the pass beyond the shrine, the gorge with its checkpoint fires, the white door beneath the mountain's shoulder, three weeks' hard travel, maybe four. And at the edge of the map, like ink bleeding, a smear of gray that moved. That hunted. That was closer than three weeks.

She grabbed the carver's wrist and turned his hand palm-up, and drew on it with her finger: the shape of the mountains, the line of the road, and then—because she had no sign for it, because the cold world had no word for it—she drew the thing that followed them. A circle. A circle inside the circle. Ash, packed tight.

Jonah stared at her drawing for a long moment. Then he looked at the scale, and the scale was dimming, settling back into its long tired pulse, and the warmth in the chamber was already fading toward ordinary cold.

"Yeah," he said quietly, to her, to the scale, to the thousands of dead whose song was still ringing in the hollow places of him. "Yeah. We move tonight."

* * *

They camped that night in the shrine chamber itself, because the scale's heat was the only warmth on the mountain, and because neither of them could bear to leave it yet. Jonah rationed out the last of the smoked goat and the hardtack, and Lior ate with the methodical efficiency of a child who had starved once and intended never to be surprised by hunger again. When she finished, she sat cross-legged near the basin, close as she dared, and watched the scale breathe.

Jonah watched her watch it.

He should have been sleeping. They'd have maybe four hours of dark before the cold forced them moving again, and his ribs still ached from the ford, and the pass tomorrow would want everything his body had. Instead he sat with his back against the basin's stone and turned the hawk-bone carving over in his fingers, listening.

The song had softened since sundown. Not gone—never gone—but gentler, the way a crowd quiets when the important thing begins. He let it wash through him and tried, for the thousandth time in his life, to pick out individual voices. It was an old habit, a carver's habit: find the grain, follow it, learn the shape of the thing. Usually the dead came to him tangled and desperate, each one convinced its message was the only message. But this—

This was harmony. Actual harmony. Thousands of voices that had never met in life, arranged now into something with parts, with structure, with *intent*. And underneath the melody, so deep he almost missed it, a rhythm. A pulse. The same pulse as the scale.

They weren't singing *about* the dragon-guardians, he realized. They were singing *with* them. Keeping time. Two hundred years of dead souls, holding the beat so the last flames wouldn't lose it.

"Carver." The voice came from the wishbone at his throat—an old man's voice, one of the steadier ones, a Vaelthorni stonemason who'd died in the burning and had been Jonah's most patient translator these past weeks. "You see it now."

"I see it," Jonah murmured.

"The girl touched the scale and it spent fire to show her the road. Do you understand what that means?"

"That it's weaker than it looks."

"That it is *dying faster than we hoped*." The mason's voice was gentle about it, the way you're gentle with bad news you've carried a long way. "We told you a season. Watching it spend itself tonight, I would say less. Weeks, perhaps, if the cold comes early. The Order's hunters press her, the mountain presses her, and every mile costs the flame something, because the flame reaches for her, carver. It has reached for her for two hundred years. It cannot help spending itself on the reaching."

Jonah looked at Lior. She'd curled up near the basin, wrapped in his spare cloak, her eyes still open, fixed on the scale with an expression he recognized because he'd worn it himself for thirty years in front of his workbench: the look of someone listening to voices only they could hear.

"What happens," he asked the bone, "if we don't make it?"

Silence, for a while. Then, from many voices at once, quiet as snowfall: *Nothing happens. That is what happens. Nothing, forever.*

He turned the wishbone over. In the scale's dim red light, the little hawk he'd carved looked almost alive, almost ready to fly.

"I'm a smuggler," he said. "I move contraband past checkpoints. Salt, steel, sometimes people who paid well and asked no questions. I've never—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't know how to deliver a country."

"You've kept her alive six days through wolf-country with the Order at our backs," the mason said. "You know exactly how."

Across the chamber, Lior stirred. She sat up, and Jonah thought she would lie back down, but instead she crawled over to him, cloak and all, and sat down beside him against the basin's warm stone. Close. Closer than she'd ever chosen to sit. She didn't look at him—she kept her eyes on the scale—but her shoulder rested against his arm, deliberate as a signature.

For a while neither of them moved.

Then she raised one hand, and in the light of the dying flame, she signed the sign they'd invented on the third day, the one that had started as *stay* and had somehow become, without either of them deciding it, *with me*.

*With me,* she signed.

Jonah looked at the scale—at the last ember of a nation, pulsing its slow stubborn pulse, spending itself mile by mile to reach a child it had sworn to keep—and he thought about doors. About thresholds. About the fact that every voice he'd ever loved was already on the far side of one, and that the girl beside him was the only living soul who could walk through and carry them all home.

He signed back: *With you.*

Lior smiled. It was the first time he'd seen her do it, and it rearranged his entire understanding of her face, and he understood with sudden terrible clarity that he was lost—that whatever happened at the white door, some part of him had already made its choice, and the choice was her.

The scale pulsed. And in its pulse, faint but unmistakable, threading through the great harmonized song of the dead, Jonah heard a new voice join the music. Small. Clear. Coming from the east, down the mountain, from the direction of the gorge.

A hunting horn, blown in the old Vaelthorni style.

The Order had found the shrine road.

"Up," Jonah said, already moving, already reaching for his pack. "Lior—*up*. We go now, in the dark, off the road. Can you walk in the dark?"

She was already on her feet, cloak pinned, eyes bright in the ember-light. She signed, fast and fierce: *I was born in the dark. Lead.*

Above them, the last flame of Vaelthorne flared once—bright, urgent, a beacon and a warning both—and began, deliberately, to dim itself, folding its light down to almost nothing, hiding them, spending its final strength like a mother banking a fire against the night.

Three weeks to the door. Less than a season of flame. And somewhere below, climbing, the gray smear that had erased a country from the world was coming to erase the last piece of it too.

Jonah took Lior's hand, and they went up into the dark together.
