## 11. Bargains with Bandits

The Gorge of Hollow Stones was the only way through, and the Order knew it.

Jonah had known it for three days now, since the dead in his pack—the old ferryman's knucklebone he carried for luck—had started whispering about torchlight on the gorge rim and men in grey asking questions. The Ashen Order had strung a checkpoint across the narrowest point like a tourniquet on a wound. Two stone towers, a chain across the river road, and enough crossbows to make the crossing a arithmetic problem Jonah would lose.

Which left him one option, and he hated it.

* * *

The Drowned Kettle sat where the smuggler's road forked below Thornfell—a tavern built into the bones of an old quarry, all soot-black timber and smoke-stained rafters, smelling of tallow and wet wool and thirty years of Jonah's other life. He hadn't come back since the night he'd walked out of it, pockets full of someone else's silver and a carving under his arm that sang too loudly to ignore.

He stood outside in the falling snow a long time, Lior's small hand cold in his.

"You don't have to talk," he told her. "You don't have to do anything. Just stay behind me and let me lie."

She looked up at him with those eyes that had seen a country burn before she was born, and she squeezed his hand once. It was, he was learning, how she said *yes*. Or maybe how she said *I'm here*. With Lior the two had started to sound the same.

Inside, the Kettle was half-empty and loud anyway. Jonah counted heads out of habit: six crew at the long table by the fire, playing cards with the grim patience of men waiting out weather that wouldn't end. Bram among them, bigger than Jonah remembered, grey coming into his beard like frost on a fence post. And behind the bar, Wren Sallow, who owned the place and half the debts in the valley, and who looked up when the door opened with the expression of a woman who had already heard this story and knew how it ended.

"Well," Wren said. "The bone-carver comes home."

"Wren."

"Thirty months, give or take." She poured something amber without being asked and set it on the bar. "You look terrible."

"I've been walking."

"In circles, from the look of you." Her eyes went past him, found Lior, and narrowed. "And who's this?"

"My daughter," Jonah said, and was surprised at how easily it came out, how little it cost him to say. "Lior. She doesn't speak. Don't make it strange."

Wren made it strange, the way she made everything strange, which is to say she leaned over the bar and studied the girl with open, unembarrassed curiosity until Lior stared back with such stillness that Wren straightened up first. "Huh," she said. "Sit down before you fall down. You're buying."

"I'm buying," Jonah agreed, because he was going to be buying a great deal more than ale before the night was done.

* * *

Bram didn't look up from his cards when Jonah approached the table. That was Bram's way—he'd never once in twenty years shown surprise, or if he had, he'd buried it so deep even the dead couldn't find it. "Thackeray," he said, dealing. "Thought you were dead."

"Disappointing, I know."

"Some of us hoped." But he said it without heat, the way old partners talk, and he kicked a chair out from the table with one boot. "Sit. You're letting the cold in."

Jonah sat. Lior stayed standing behind him, exactly where he'd told her, a small shadow with snow melting in her dark hair. The card players glanced up, assessed, went back to their hands. Smugglers were polite that way; they'd ask questions only after they'd decided whether the answers required knives.

"Bram. I need the gorge."

Bram laid down his cards face-up—a losing hand, folded deliberately—and finally looked at him. Up close, Jonah could see the years had not been kind in ways that showed only around the eyes. "The gorge is closed."

"The gorge is never closed. The Order just thinks it is."

"The Order has two towers, forty men, and a chain across the narrows. They've been paying tolls to nobody because there's nobody to pay them to—they burned out the last toll-keepers in autumn. They're not renting the crossing, Thackeray. They own it."

"They don't own the goat track above the north tower."

"The goat track is a rumor."

"The goat track is real. I've used it."

"When? Ten years ago? Before the rockfall?" Bram leaned back, arms crossed, and Jonah watched him do the same arithmetic the crossbows demanded. "Even if it's real, it's watched now. Everything's watched. There are grey cloaks in every market town between here and the pass, asking after a man and a child. Asking after—" Bram paused, and something flickered across his face, quick as a fish under ice. "Asking after a girl who talks wrong."

The room got quieter. Not silent—the Kettle was never silent—but the card game slowed, and Wren stopped wiping the bar, and Jonah felt every ear in the quarry lean toward him without turning a single head.

"That's a rumor too," Jonah said.

"Is it." Bram wasn't asking. He turned in his chair, slow, deliberate, until he was facing Lior directly, and Jonah's hand moved without thinking to the knife at his belt—not to draw, just to rest there, the way you rest your hand on an old argument. Bram saw it and smiled without any humor at all. "Easy, carver. I'm not going to touch her. I'm looking at her." He did look, long and hard, and Lior looked back with that bottomless calm that unnerved grown men and made wolves lie down. "Strange little thing, aren't you."

"She's tired," Jonah said. "She's hungry. She's none of your business."

"Everything in my gorge is my business. That's what owning a gorge means." Bram turned back. "So here's my question, and you'll answer it or you'll walk out of here and take your chances with the crossbows. Why?"

"Why what?"

"Why does she matter?" Bram spread his big hands. "I've known you thirty years, Jonah. You're the coldest man I ever ran cargo with. You left me holding a barge on the Marrow with the tide going out and the revenue cutter coming in, and you didn't send so much as a note, because sending a note was riskier than leaving me to drown. You carved bones in a shack above the road and sold trinkets to pilgrims and never once came down to drink, because drinking means talking and talking means owing. You owe nobody. That was your religion." He tapped the table. "Now you're spending favors you haven't got on a mute child you've known a month. So either you've gone soft, or she's worth something, and both of those answers cost me sleep. Which is it?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind the bar, a kettle began its low whistle and Wren hushed it with a cloth, unhurried, listening.

Jonah thought about lying. He was good at lying; it had been his trade as much as carving had. He could tell Bram the girl was a hostage, a ransom job, a rich merchant's runaway worth her weight in letters of credit. He could tell Bram she was his sister's kid, some blood-tie fiction that explained everything and revealed nothing. Any of those lies would work on Bram, because Bram wanted a reason that fit inside a ledger.

But the dead in his pocket—the ferryman's knucklebone, warm against his hip like a held breath—seemed to press against him, and Lior's hand settled on his shoulder, light as snowfall, and Jonah Thackeray, who had spent thirty years making sure he owed nothing to anyone living, heard himself tell the truth.

"She's the last heir of Vaelthorne."

Nobody moved. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"The country that isn't," Bram said flatly. "There's no such country."

"There isn't now. There will be again, if I get her through the gorge and up to the door before the ember dies. And the Order knows it, and they'll burn anyone who helped her get there, and they'll unmake them while they're burning, so that no one remembers there was ever anyone to burn." Jonah held Bram's stare. "That's why I need the goat track, and that's why I need you not to sell me, and that's why I'm telling you the truth instead of a lie, because a lie you might sell cheap and the truth you can't afford to touch."

Bram sat very still. Then he laughed—one short, ugly bark with no joy in it. "Vaelthorne. Gods' teeth, Thackeray. Do you hear yourself? A fairy story. A dead kingdom and a magic door and a little girl with a sword in her blood. I've run contraband past Order checkpoints for twenty years and never once heard anything so stupid."

"Yes," Jonah said. "It's completely stupid. Now you know why I need the goat track."

* * *

They talked terms in the back room, away from ears, though Jonah suspected the whole Kettle already knew and was deciding what to do about it. Wren joined them, which surprised him until he remembered that Wren had always kept a ledger longer than anyone's memory, and ledgers, these days, were dangerous things.

"The goat track exists," Bram said, when the door was shut. "Rockfall took out the middle third in the spring. It's climbable, barely, with ropes and someone who knows the handholds. Me."

"You hate heights."

"I hate the Order more, lately. They taxed my gorge, then they taxed my gorge again, then they shot a boy of mine off the rim for spitting near a sergeant." Bram's jaw worked. "I've been looking for a reason to spit back. This might be a stupid one. Stupid reasons hold better than smart ones—nobody expects them."

"What's the price?"

Bram named it, and it was everything. Every coin Jonah had sewn into his coat lining over fifteen careful years. The debt Wren held on his workshop—she produced the paper from somewhere inside her apron, because of course she'd bought it, because of course Wren Sallow owned a piece of everything that ever fell. His tools, save the knives in his belt. The silver fox clasp his mother had left him, which he'd sworn no one would ever pry from his cold hands, and which he now set on the table himself, sliding it across the wood, watching it go.

"And the favor," Bram said. "The open one. Whatever I ask, whenever I ask, no refusal."

"No," Jonah said.

"It's not a question. It's part of the price."

"I said no. Ask me for anything else. Coin, goods, my hands, my silence. But not an open favor. An open favor means someday you ask me to do something I can't live with, and I'll have to choose between breaking my word and breaking myself, and I've done enough breaking." He pushed the fox clasp another inch across the table. "Take everything else. Leave me that."

Bram looked at the clasp, at the coins, at Wren's paper, at Jonah's face. Something moved behind his eyes—calculation, yes, but underneath it something older and wearier, the look of a man remembering a night on the Marrow with the tide going out and choosing, for whatever reason, not to bring it up again.

"One condition instead," Bram said. "When this is done—if she lives, if the door opens, if any of this mad story turns out true—you come back here and you drink with me. One night. You tell me everything, start to finish, and I decide whether to believe it."

"That's it?"

"That's it. I want to know I ruined myself for a true thing." Bram swept the coins into his palm and closed his fist. "Done?"

"Done."

They shook on it. Bram's grip was still a landslide.

* * *

They left before dawn, in the blue-black hour when even the Kettle's fires slept. Bram led them up behind the quarry, three shadows on a white slope, toward the goat track and the towers above the gorge where Order lanterns burned in patient rows.

At the tree line, Bram stopped and crouched to Lior's level, which was something Jonah had never seen him do for anyone. He studied her a moment, then dug in his coat and pressed something into her mittened hand: a whistle carved from antler, plain and worn smooth.

"If we're split up, you blow that. Sound carries strange in the gorge—comes from everywhere, points nowhere. My people know it. We come running." He paused. "It belonged to my daughter. She's grown and gone and doesn't whistle anymore."

Lior closed her fingers around it. Then she did something Jonah had learned to read but Bram hadn't: she reached up and touched Bram's cheek, briefly, the way you'd touch something precious and breakable, and Bram went absolutely rigid, like a man struck by lightning that hadn't decided whether to kill him.

"She says thank you," Jonah said quietly. "In her way."

"Her way," Bram muttered, standing, gruff again, embarrassed. "Keep her close, Thackeray. Things like her don't stay lost for long. Somebody always comes looking—and lately, everything that comes looking wears grey."

Above them, on the gorge rim, a lantern flared and a horn sounded—two short notes, one long. The signal for strangers on the road.

Bram spat, shouldered his rope coil, and grinned with all the warmth of a drawn blade. "Right on time. Come on, then. Let's go spend your fortune climbing."

Behind them, far down the valley, Jonah heard—or thought he heard, through the wind and the waking dead in his pack—hooves. Many hooves. Moving fast.

Moving their way.
