## 12. First Words

The cave smelled of pine smoke and old stone, and for the first time since the fire, Lior slept without dreaming of burning.

She woke instead to quiet. Real quiet—not the hollow kind, not the silence of a country scraped off the face of the world, but the small, breathing quiet of a place where someone had built a fire low and banked it well, where snow fell soft outside the mouth of the cave and made the whole mountain feel like a held breath.

Jonah sat near the entrance with his back against the rock, a knife in one hand and a piece of bone in the other, turning it over and over without carving. He wasn't working. He was watching the snow.

Lior lay still and watched him back.

She had learned his shapes in the weeks since the ford. The shape of his hands when he was nervous—they moved constantly, worrying at knife-hilts and coin-purses and the frayed edge of his sleeve. The shape of his shoulders when he lied, which was often, and badly: they climbed toward his ears like a man bracing for a blow. The shape of his face when he looked at her when he thought she couldn't see, which was this face now—unguarded, tired, carrying something heavy behind the eyes that he never spoke aloud because he was a man who had decided, long ago, that speaking aloud cost too much.

He talked to her anyway. All the time. A river of low words about which roots were safe to chew and which passes the Order watched and how his mother had made stew on nights like this, back before, back when. He filled the silences the way a man pours water into a cracked cup—constantly, desperately, knowing it would all leak out.

She understood almost none of it. That was the terrible joke of them: he heard voices from everywhere and everywhen, dead kings and dead sparrows and dead wolves, and she—the last heir of a nation that no longer existed, the girl whose blood opened doors—could not understand a single word of the living tongue that everyone around her spoke as easily as breath.

But she was learning. In the way water learns stone.

*Fire,* she thought, watching the coals pulse. *That word is fire.* He'd said it a hundred times, pointing, tapping the ground near the flames, tapping her hand when she reached too close. *Fire. Fire.*

*Snow.* That one she'd learned from the world itself, white beyond the cave mouth, falling without sound.

*Wolf.* She knew *wolf* better than she knew anything in this language, because *wolf* was the first word she'd ever taught him, and the look on his face when she said it—that flinch, that stunned blink—had been the first thing in two centuries of exile that had made her want to laugh.

*Vaelthorni* for wolf sounded like *Yonah*. Like his name. Like the mountains themselves had known he was coming, two hundred years before he was born, and had tucked his name inside her language like a seed in winter soil.

She turned the thought over the way he turned the bone in his hands.

*Don't leave.*

The words were there. She'd been carrying them for days, hoarded like the dried meat in his pack, taken from his own mouth on some gray afternoon on the trail—*don't leave the path, don't leave me, don't leave*—three small sounds she'd pried apart and practiced in secret, mouthing them into her collar while he walked ahead, shaping them against her teeth until they stopped feeling like broken things.

She didn't know what they meant, exactly. Not fully. She knew *leave* was what fire did to houses. What the Order did to names. What two hundred years had done to everything she loved. And she knew the tone he used when he said it—a tone like a hand grabbing a wrist—and she knew that tone was the truest language there was, older than Vaelthorni, older than dragons, older than doors.

*Don't leave.*

She sat up. The wool blanket—he'd wrapped her in it three nights ago without asking, just dropped it over her shoulders like it was nothing, like men did this sort of thing every day—slid down her arms.

His head came around instantly. He always heard her before she moved; she'd stopped wondering how. The dead probably told him. Or maybe he just listened the way lonely people listen, with his whole body, having spent thirty years learning every sound an empty room could make so that a new sound would never catch him unprepared.

"Hey." Soft. Careful. He set the bone down. "Bad dream?"

She shook her head. Then, because it seemed important to be honest, nodded. Both things were true. No dream, and the worst one.

He studied her the way he studied bones—looking for the fracture line, the place where the story lived. His eyes were brown and bloodshot and older than his face wanted to be. "You're safe," he said, and tapped his chest twice, and pointed at her, and made a little cage of his hands. Safe. He'd invented that sign their first week together and used it every night since, and every night she let him believe it worked.

Tonight she didn't want signs. Tonight she wanted words. His words, in his language, so that for once the reaching went both ways.

"Don't," she said.

The word came out cracked and small and wrong-shaped, a bird with a bent wing, but it flew.

Jonah went absolutely still.

Not startled-still. Still like a man who has stepped on ice and felt it speak under his heel. His lips parted. His hands, which never stopped moving, stopped.

"Don't leave." She pushed through the second part, dragging each sound up from wherever she'd buried it, and her voice—her actual voice, unused for so long she'd half-forgotten its weight—filled the cave and seemed to surprise even her. It echoed off the stone. Small cave, small voice, enormous echo.

The fire popped. Snow whispered past the entrance.

Jonah made a sound she'd never heard from him before. Half laugh, half something else, something wounded. He dragged a hand down his face. "Well," he said, and his voice came out rough. "There she is."

He didn't understand what she'd said. She could see that—he was parsing it, tilting it, trying to fit those two foreign syllables into a sentence. But that didn't matter. That was never going to be the point. The point was that she had crossed the river. She had spoken in his tongue, the tongue of roads and coins and the living world, and whatever the words carried, the crossing itself was the message:

*I am trying. I am coming toward you. Meet me halfway.*

She pointed at him. Pointed at herself. Held up her hands—*together*, a sign they'd made on the mountain—and then pointed at the dark mouth of the cave, the world outside, the road ahead, and pulled her hands apart. Apart. Alone. Gone.

Then she pressed her fists together again and looked at him with everything she had, which was more than a child's face should be able to hold, and said it again, clearer, steadier, a door opening instead of a wing mending:

"Don't leave."

Understanding moved across his face like weather.

She watched it land. Watched him get it—not the grammar, but the grief underneath it, the thing she actually meant, which was not *stay in this cave* or *walk beside me tomorrow* but something vaster and more childish and more unbearable: *everyone leaves. Fire left. My name left. Two hundred years left. You are the only warm thing within reach and I know—I know—how this story goes.*

Jonah looked at her for a long time.

And Lior, who had learned to read faces the way other children learned letters, watched him decide not to answer.

It was in his jaw first. The way it set. Then his eyes, dropping to the fire, then to the knife, then anywhere but her. She knew that look. She'd seen it on the faces of statues in her fragmenting memories, on the painted saints of a country that burned, the look of a man being asked for something he did not believe he owned.

"I—" he started. Stopped. Rubbed the back of his neck. Started again. "Lior."

Her name in his mouth. He said it carefully, like it might break, like it was made of the same material as the bones he carved. Sometimes she caught him practicing it when he thought she slept—*Lior, Lior*—getting the shape right, honoring it.

"You don't—" He exhaled through his nose. Tried Common Tongue, slow, gesturing: himself, then the cave, then a flat motion of his hand—*I stay*—and then, crucially, horribly, he shook his head. Not no to staying. No to promising it.

He spread his hands. Everything gesture, everything honest: *I can't say it.*

And Lior felt something in her chest crack—but not the way she expected. Not the way it had cracked in the valley of the village that wasn't there, standing at a nameless well, understanding for the first time that erasure took the ground itself. This was a different breaking. This was the crack of a shell, of a seed casing, of a thing splitting open precisely because something inside it was alive and growing.

Because he hadn't lied to her.

Everyone in her ruined memory who had ever promised anything was ash. The priests had promised the dragons would protect them. Her mother—her mother, whoever her mother had been, the face above hers a golden blur with a voice like a struck bell—had promised morning would come. The door had promised. The ember had promised. Whole Vaelthorne had been one vast promise, and the Order had come with fire and ledgers and unmade every single word of it.

And here was this smuggler, this bone-carver, this man who talked to the dead because the living had nothing to offer him—here he was, offered the easiest lie in the world, *of course I won't leave you, little ghost, sleep now*—and he refused it. Refused it at cost. Refused it with pain all over his face, because she could see he wanted to say it, saw how badly he wanted it, saw that the wanting was exactly why he wouldn't.

He knew what promises were worth. He'd paid for that knowledge the way she'd paid for hers.

Lior crawled across the cave floor—all knees and blanket and tangled hair—and sat down beside him, close enough that the warmth between their shoulders made its own small country. She didn't look at him. Looking would have been too much for both of them.

Instead she held out her hand, palm up. The way he did when he gave her food. The way people did, everywhere, in every language, in every age, when they meant: *here. Take this. It's yours.*

He looked at her hand. At the small scars on her fingers, the burn scar at her wrist that she didn't remember getting and he never asked about. His own hand hovered—hovered, actually hovered, a grown man afraid of a child's open palm—and then settled into it, rough and warm and heavier than she expected, callused in the places where the knife sat.

They stayed like that. The fire burned down. The snow kept falling its endless silent falling, erasing the world outside the cave inch by inch, and inside, two erased people held onto each other's one warm hand and said nothing, because nothing was the only promise either of them could afford.

*This,* Lior thought, in her own language, the old language, the language of wolves and doors. *This is what we were, before. Before the fire. People sitting close in the winter, holding on.*

Somewhere far below the mountain, deeper than stone, deeper than the dying shrine with its guttering scale, she felt the ember flicker. Felt it the way you feel a heartbeat through a wall. It was smaller than last week. They both knew it. The dead sang through it more faintly now, more urgently, a song running out of verses.

*Hurry,* the song said. *Hurry, little last one. Hurry home.*

She squeezed Jonah's hand once. He squeezed back.

Outside, far down the valley, a light moved where no light should be—a lantern, swinging in a steady patrol rhythm, too disciplined for a hunter, too patient for a wolf. It swept along the treeline below the pass and paused.

Paused, directly beneath their cave.

Lior felt Jonah go rigid beside her. His hand slid free of hers, slow and silent, and found the knife.

* * *

Down in the dark, the lantern swung once more—and went out.
