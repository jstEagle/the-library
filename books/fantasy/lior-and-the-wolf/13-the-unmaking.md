## 13. The Unmaking

The town was called Harrow's Bend, and by nightfall it would never have been called anything at all.

Serane Voss stood in the market square with her hands folded behind her back and watched the Order's readers chalk the perimeter. Nine of them, gray-robed, moving clockwise from the well to the millrace to the shrine-post where someone had hung a garland of dried rowan berries for luck. The chalk they used wasn't chalk. It was ground bone and ash and a reagent the novices were told never to taste, and when it dried against cobblestone and doorpost and fence rail, it didn't look like a line so much as a decision.

She had overseen eleven Unmakings in her fourteen years with the Ashen Order. She knew the choreography the way other officers knew cavalry drills. First the perimeter. Then the collection—every written record, every carved initial on a bridge beam, every christening cup in every cupboard, gathered into the center of the square and burned in a fire fed with something hotter than wood. Then the reading, all nine voices together, the old words that had no country anymore because the Order had made sure of that. And then the forgetting, which was not a sound or a light or anything a painter could render. It was simply an absence arriving, like a tooth coming loose.

The people of Harrow's Bend stood in their square because they had been told to, and they obeyed, because towns like this always obeyed. Farmers and ferrymen and a cooper with flour still on his forearms. Two hundred souls, give or take, arranged in the loose semicircle that two weeks of rumor had taught them meant authority.

Two weeks ago, one of those souls had opened her door to a smuggler and a mute girl with frostbitten hands. A widow named Maren Ostrow. She'd fed them stew and lied about it twice before the questioning broke her, and then hadn't lied at all, and now stood at the front of the crowd with her wrists bound, watching the chalk come around toward her house.

Serane did not look at her. Looking at them was a habit she had trained out of herself years ago, the way you train yourself not to look down from a height.

"Readers ready," said Brother Callow, her second, a soft-spoken man with ink-stained fingers who genuinely believed every rite he performed was mercy. That was the thing about Callow. He wasn't lying. He believed that a town erased cleanly suffered less than a town punished messily, and he was right, probably, if you accepted his premises, and Serane had built fourteen years on not examining premises.

"Begin the collection," she said.

* * *

They brought the records out into the square the way you'd carry a sick relative. Ledgers. A parish roll going back six generations. The cooper's account book. A child's copybook with wobbly letters spelling out the names of rivers. The ferryman's toll list, which was really a list of everyone who had ever passed through Harrow's Bend, and therefore, in the Order's arithmetic, a list of everyone who might remember it passing.

And one item Callow carried himself, wrapped in linen: a wooden sign from the inn, HARROW'S BEND — GOOD BEDS, HONEST ALE, painted by some dead innkeeper with more confidence than skill.

"Anything else?" Callow asked the crowd. "Anything hidden? Letters, keepsakes, anything with the town's name on it?"

Silence. The good kind, mostly. Somewhere near the back a baby started crying and its mother hushed it with a sound Serane almost recognized.

Then a girl of about eight pushed forward through the legs of the adults, holding something above her head with both hands, the way children present things—as evidence, as offering, as proof they are good.

It was a sampler. Embroidered cloth, the kind girls made to learn their stitches. *HARROW'S BEND,* it read, in pink thread gone gray with handling, surrounded by lopsided flowers. Below the flowers, in smaller stitches, someone had added: *Mira's work, age 7.*

"I made it," the girl said, to Serane specifically, because children always found the officer and decided the officer was the one who mattered. "But I can make another one. I know all the letters now."

The square went very quiet.

Serane looked at her. She hadn't meant to. The training said don't look, and she looked anyway, and what she saw was a thin face with a stubborn chin and dark hair escaping its braid, and eyes that were doing the thing children's eyes do when they have decided that the large armed adults must have a reason, and the reason will be explained if one is simply patient enough.

There is a version of your own face, Serane thought, that you only ever see in other people.

She had been eight once. In a valley that no longer appeared on any map, in a country whose name she could no longer say even inside her own skull—the word slid off it, worn smooth as river stone, two centuries of erasure compressed into one girl's memory by the Order's careful work. She had been eight, and men in gray robes had come to her village too, and her father had put her behind the door and told her to be quiet, and she had been quiet, so quiet, so very good at being quiet, and it had saved her life, and it had cost everything else, and the ledger of that transaction had never once balanced.

She had denounced her own family to survive the purge. That was the sentence she kept locked in the deepest room of herself, the room she visited only in the hour before dawn. *I told them where my mother hid.* Fourteen years old, terrified, offered a choice between the fire and the pen, and she had chosen the pen, and the Order had taken her in, educated her, armored her in doctrine, given her a new name to replace the one they'd scraped off the world—and she had told herself for thirty years that there had been no choice, that anyone would have done it, that survival was the first law and sentiment was a luxury of the already-dead.

"You'll want to set that down," Callow said gently to the girl. "With the others."

The girl looked at him. Then back at Serane. Still waiting for the reason.

"It's just cloth," Serane heard herself say. Her voice came out level. She was good at level; she had practiced level the way monks practice prayer. "Thread and cloth. It isn't a record of anything."

Callow turned. There was nothing unkind in his face, which was somehow worse. "Inquisitor. It bears the town's name."

"It bears a child's name." She held the woman's gaze—no. The girl's. She held the girl's gaze. "Names of the living aren't being collected today. Only the town's."

"The town's name is stitched into it. You can see it from here."

"I can see that a child worked hard on it."

The silence changed texture. Serane felt the nine readers stop chalking and look up. She felt the crowd feel it—a whole town inhaling at once, the way animals go still before weather.

"Inquisitor Voss." Callow's voice stayed soft. It always stayed soft. That was how he fought. "We have protocols."

"We have protocols for records. This is embroidery."

"It is a record of Harrow's Bend, rendered in thread, in the hand of a resident. If we leave it, the town leaves a splinter of itself in the world. Splinters itch. Splinters lead searchers to us, and to the girl, and everything we do here becomes waste." He paused, and added, with terrible gentleness: "You wrote the protocol yourself. Three years ago. I checked the margin. Your initials are on it."

He was right. Of course he was right. She remembered writing it, remembered believing it, remembered the clean satisfaction of a rule that closed a loophole. Rules were how you survived doctrine. Rules were load-bearing walls. You did not pull one out because a child was looking at you with your own eyes.

"Give it here," Serane said.

The girl handed it over without hesitation, because the officer had asked, and the officer was the one who mattered, and surely now the reason would be explained.

Serane took the sampler. The cloth was soft from handling. One of the flowers had been unpicked and redone, and you could see the ghost of the first attempt under the second, the little scar of learning. *Mira's work, age 7.*

She dropped it onto the pile with the ledgers and the sign and the toll lists, and she turned away before she could watch it land.

"Light the fire," she said.

* * *

The burning took eleven minutes. It always took about eleven minutes, whatever the size of the pile, as though the fire had a quota rather than fuel.

Serane watched from the steps of the shrine-post, arms crossed, and performed the discipline she had perfected over fourteen years: she let the fire be only a fire. Wood and paper and thread and paint. Heat on her face. Smoke bending east. A cooper's account book curling at the corner like a leaf in autumn. Nothing else. No meaning. Meaning was a door and doors were how things got in.

Behind her, the nine readers took their places in the circle and began the reading.

She knew the words. Everyone in the Order knew the words; they were printed in the front of every field ledger, in a script designed to be copied but not understood, syllables that predated the Order itself, inherited from the same machinery that had unmade Vaelthorne two centuries ago. That was the Order's deepest secret and its most open one: they had not invented the erasure. They had merely learned to operate it, the way a man might learn to operate a machine left behind by giants. Vaelthorne had been the proof of concept. An entire nation—its cities, its king-lists, its language, its graves, its name in the mouths of grandmothers—scoured down to nothing, so thoroughly that even the mountains where it stood seemed embarrassed to admit it.

And now the same words, in the same gray mouths, for a town of two hundred people and one ferry.

The reading rose. The air over the square thickened the way it does before hail.

Serane had felt this part eleven times. It was not painful. That was the horror of it—it was painless, seamless, a smoothness sliding over the world. The readers' voices braided together. The chalk lines began to glow faintly, the color of embers seen through closed eyelids. And the crowd stood in their semicircle, waiting, obedient, not one of them knowing that they were standing in the last minute of their own continuity.

The widow Maren Ostrow was crying. Not loudly. She had stopped begging days ago, during the questioning; what remained was quieter and worse. She was looking at the faces around her—the neighbors she'd known for forty years—and Serane understood, with a clarity she did not want, that the widow was trying to memorize them. Filing them somewhere the rite couldn't reach. As if love could be smuggled out ahead of the confiscation.

The reading crested. The glow along the chalk brightened.

And in the crowd, the girl with the sampler reached up and took her mother's hand.

That was all. A small hand finding a larger one, the automatic gesture of a child in a crowd, a gesture so ordinary it predates language. The mother squeezed back without looking down, because she was watching the glowing chalk, because everyone was watching the glowing chalk.

Serane's chest did something structural.

Because she had done that. In another square, in another century's worth of forgetting, a girl had reached up and taken a hand, and the hand had been her mother's, and within the hour the Order's readers had come for her mother, and the girl had told them where she hid, and the hand had never held hers again, and the country that contained both of them had been scraped off the world so completely that when Serane tried, even now, to picture her mother's face, she got a shape, a warmth, a voice singing half a lullaby in a language she was forbidden to know—and then the smoothness, the Order's smoothness, sliding over it like ice forming on water.

Half a lullaby. That was all they'd left her. They'd missed four bars of a song, buried too deep in a child's memory to find, and Serane had carried those four bars for thirty years like a coal she couldn't put down and couldn't warm her hands on.

The reading ended.

* * *

There was no thunderclap. There never was.

The glow faded. The chalk lines settled into the stone and vanished—not wiped away but absorbed, as if the cobbles had drunk them. The smoke from the fire straightened, thinned, and drifted apart into ordinary evening air.

And Harrow's Bend continued to exist, for approximately ninety more seconds, the way a bell continues to ring after you've gripped it.

Serane watched the crowd. That was the part she always forced herself to watch, the way surgeons force themselves to look: the moment the absence arrived. It moved through the semicircle like dusk moving across a field. Faces didn't change—that was the uncanny thing, faces never changed—but the attention behind them guttered and re-lit, guttered and re-lit, and then steadied into something placid and slightly vacant, people suddenly unsure why they were standing in a square, why there was ash in the fire pit, why a gray-robed woman on the shrine-post steps was looking at them with an expression none of them could name because they had no category for her anymore.

The widow Maren Ostrow blinked. Looked down at her bound wrists. Frowned—not with fear but with mild confusion, a woman trying to recall why she'd come outside.

The girl with the dark braid looked down at her empty hands.

She turned them over. Turned them back. There was nothing in them, and there was supposed to be something in them—Serane could see the supposition sitting on the child's face like weather, the shape of a missing thing, an absence with edges. Pink thread. Lopsided flowers. A name.

"Mama," the girl said slowly, testing the word and the world against each other, "why am I sad?"

Her mother looked at her with blank, gentle eyes and said, "I don't know, sweetheart."

And the girl—because she was eight, because she was patient, because she had decided the large armed adults must have a reason—looked up at the steps, found Serane's face, and held it.

She didn't cry. That was the unbearable part. She just looked, with the beginnings of confusion curdling into something older than eight years old, and Serane stood on the steps and met it because running from it would have been worse, would have been the final small cowardice in a life built on one large one.

*I made it,* the girl had said. *But I can make another one.*

Except she couldn't. There was no thread, no pattern, no name to stitch. There was a hole in her hands shaped like a sampler, and it would sit there for the rest of her life, and she would grow up feeling it in cold weather the way old wounds ache, and she would never know what it was.

Serane had a hole exactly that shape. She had simply been told its name so long ago that she'd mistaken the telling for healing.

"Mount up," she said, and her voice cracked on the second word, minutely, a fault-line only she could hear. She cleared her throat. "We ride within the hour."

Callow appeared at her elbow, wrapping his reader's circle-cord with neat, loving hands. "A clean one," he said, with satisfaction. "No residue. The widow gave us everything before the rite—the smuggler's route through Thornfell, the high camp where they parted ways. Bram's crew has already been paid; the scouts should have them boxed above the snowline within two days." He smiled, brief and genuine. "It ends soon, Inquisitor. The last ember dies within the season regardless. We only need to reach the heir before it doesn't."

"Yes," Serane said.

Above the snowline. Where a wounded smuggler was buying time with his body, if he still lived, and a mute girl was running upward alone through country that remembered her even if no one else did.

Serane looked back once, at the square, at the girl still standing with her empty hands, at the fire pit full of ash that no one in Harrow's Bend would ever wonder about.

Then she walked to her horse, and did not mount it, and stood with her hand on the saddle for a long moment while the column waited.

Four bars of a lullaby. Half a melody in a dead tongue, humming in the sealed room where she kept everything she'd traded away. She had spent thirty years telling herself the trade was finished, the price paid, the ledger closed.

But the ledger, she was beginning to understand, had never closed. It had only been waiting for her to read it.

"Inquisitor?" Callow called. "We're ready."

Serane Voss swung up into the saddle, turned her horse north, toward the snowline, toward the door she had sworn her whole life to keep shut—

and gave the order to ride.
