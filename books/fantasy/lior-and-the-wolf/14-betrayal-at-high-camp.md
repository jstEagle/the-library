## 14. Betrayal at High Camp

The fire was small enough to hide in a cupped hand, and Jonah built it anyway, because Lior's lips had gone blue at the ford and hadn't fully come back.

High camp, Bram called it—a shelf of stone wedged between two shoulders of the Thornfells, where the smugglers used to stash brandy and gunpowder and the occasional fugitive with deep pockets. Jonah had slept here a dozen times in his old life. He knew the way the wind funneled through the notch on the north side, the way the scree sang when anything heavier than a goat came down the slope. He'd told himself that knowledge was worth something. That up here, above the treeline, above the roads the Order watched, they could breathe for one night.

He should have known better. He'd spent thirty years learning that everything you love can be sold. He'd just never expected the price tag to be his own name.

Lior sat close to the flames, hands out, watching him carve. He'd taken to working while she watched—she liked the motion of it, he thought, the way the knife turned dead bone into small living shapes. Tonight it was a sparrow wing, half-finished, from a gull bone he'd found bleached on the riverbank. The gull had nothing much to say. Gulls rarely did. *Cold,* it kept muttering. *The water was cold.* Dead things were mostly simple, when you got down under the song of them.

Except Vaelthorne's dead. Except the wolf femur that had spoken with a voice like a landslide and told him what the girl at his door actually was. Those dead had plans. Those dead had been waiting two hundred years, and patience made them loud.

"Sleep," he said to Lior, and signed it too—*sleep*, palm flat against his cheek—the way she'd taught him without teaching him, her language leaking into his hands. "I'll watch."

She didn't move. She looked at him the way she sometimes did, head tilted, like she was reading a page written somewhere behind his face. Then she reached across the fire and touched the half-carved wing, one finger, light as snowfall.

The gull went silent.

It always did that. Every carving she touched went quiet—not destroyed, not silenced the way the Order silenced things, but *settled*, like a dog lying down at last. Whatever she was, whatever ran in her blood older than any kingdom on the maps, the dead found it restful. He'd stopped asking why. Some questions were just grief wearing a scholar's coat.

"Go on," he said, softer. "I'm not going anywhere."

That was a lie, or close enough to one that he felt it catch on something in his chest. Everyone he'd ever promised anything to was dead. He'd promised his mother he'd come home from the coast road. He'd promised Tam Weller they'd split the take on the Corvess run. He'd promised himself, once, drunk and young, that he'd never be the man who left people behind. The mountain wind took all those promises the same way it took smoke.

But Lior smiled—small, crooked, the first smile he'd ever pulled out of her—and lay down against her pack with the ember-shrine's warmth still faintly in the scale she wore on its cord around her neck. It glowed dull orange now instead of bright, guttering like a candle in a doorway. A season, the shrine had said. Maybe less.

He carved until her breathing evened. Then he banked the fire, wrapped his cloak around his knees, and listened to the mountain.

That was when he heard the scree sing.

Not goats. Goats moved scattered, casual, a patter of small stones. This was weight, distributed, deliberate—boots, several pairs, coming down the north notch where the wind covered sound. Coming the way only someone who knew smuggler paths would come.

Jonah's blood went cold in a way the altitude couldn't explain.

There were maybe six men alive who knew the high camp approach from the north side. Two were dead. One had lost the knack of walking along with most of his wits in a tavern brawl. One was a woman in Corvess who'd sooner bite him than help him. And one—

One was Bram.

"No," Jonah said aloud, to nobody, to the fire, to himself. "No, you stupid loyal bastard, tell me I'm wrong."

The scree sang again, closer.

* * *

He woke Lior with a hand over her mouth and his own face inches from hers, and he watched her go from sleep to full animal alertness in the space of a heartbeat—no groggy climbing, no confusion. Just eyes open and already afraid, which told him she'd learned more from him in these weeks than he'd ever wanted to teach.

"Scouts," he breathed. "Order scouts, maybe six, coming down the notch. We have maybe four hundred heartbeats before they're on us."

She grabbed his wrist. Not to pull him toward the escape route—he'd shown her the goat track down the south face, the one that dropped into cloud forest and vanished. She grabbed his wrist and held on, and her eyes said what her voice couldn't: *you come too.*

"I'm right behind you," he lied again, and this time he hated how easy it was.

Because it wasn't true, and they both knew it wasn't true, and her grip tightened until her nails bit through his sleeve. Six men. He was one man with a carving knife and a bad knee. If they both ran, the scouts would run them down on the open scree before the tree line, and then there would be no heir of Vaelthorne, no door, no country un-erased—just two bodies cooling on a mountainside and an ember going out in a shrine cave with nobody left to mourn it.

If one of them stayed—

"Don't," he whispered, though she hadn't said anything. She never said anything. That was the whole cruelty of her, that she made him say everything out loud, alone, into the space where another person's voice should have been. "Don't look at me like that. You have to run. You have to run *now*, and you don't stop, and you don't wait, and you get to the door whether I'm there or not. Do you hear me? Whether I'm—"

The first crossbow bolt took the fire apart.

It hit the coals dead center and scattered them, sparks whirling up like furious orange birds, and in that flash of light Jonah saw them on the ridge—six shapes in hunter's greens, grey hoods, the ash-grey sash of the Ashen Order tied at every shoulder. And standing a little apart from them, arms crossed, face lit ugly by the burning coals:

Bram.

Big Bram, who'd carried Jonah on his back for two miles after the Corvess ambush with a bolt through Jonah's thigh. Bram, who'd held Jonah's hair back over a gutter in Saltmarket when the fever took him. Bram, whose laugh Jonah could pick out of a crowd of thousands, who owed Jonah nothing and gave him everything anyway, because that was what twenty years of smuggling bought if you did it right.

Bram wouldn't meet his eyes.

"You said—" Jonah's voice cracked. He stood up slowly, hands wide, putting himself between Lior and the ridge. "You said you'd never sell me. You said it drunk, you said it sober, you said it on the *Kestrel*'s deck with your hand on my shoulder, you said—"

"They have my daughters' names." Bram's voice was flat as slate. He still wouldn't look up. "Written in a ledger. In a book that makes things *unhappen*, Jonah. They showed me. They wrote my girls out of their mother's memory, right there at the table, and then they wrote them back, and they said next time they wouldn't write them back." His jaw worked. "What would you have done?"

Jonah had no answer. That was the worst of it. He had no answer, and the scouts were spreading out along the ridge with the smooth unhurried economy of men who had done this many times, and Lior's hand was still locked around his wrist like she could hold him to the world by force.

"Walk away," Jonah called up. "Bram. Walk away. Take your men down the south face and report that you lost us in the storm. I'll vanish. You know I can vanish. Nobody ever has to know."

"Inquisitor Voss doesn't accept reports," one of the scouts said mildly. He was older, grey-bearded, with the patient voice of a schoolmaster. "She accepts results. Kill the man. The girl comes alive. Inquisitor's orders."

"The girl comes *alive*," Jonah repeated, and something in his chest went very quiet and very cold. Alive. For what? For questioning? For the Unmaking? He looked at Lior and saw her looking at the scouts, and her expression wasn't fear anymore. It was something older. Something that made the hair rise on his arms.

The dragon-scale at her throat flared.

Not guttering anymore—*burning*, sudden and furious, throwing her shadow huge and wrong up the rockface behind her, and the nearest scout flinched back with a hiss like a man touching a hot iron.

"Now," Jonah said, and shoved her.

He shoved her hard, both hands, toward the goat track, and she stumbled two steps and spun back with fury in her face, and he screamed it this time, no breath spared: "*RUN!*"

And gods forgive him, she ran. Because she was eleven years old and a man she trusted had shouted at her, and some lessons are older than loyalty. She went down the goat track like a falling leaf, quick and silent, swallowed by the dark below the scree line—and the last thing Jonah saw of her was the glow of the scale, sinking, sinking, a dying star dropping into cloud.

Then he turned to face six crossbows and his best friend, and picked up his knife.

* * *

They didn't rush him. That was the thing about professionals; they never rushed. The greybeard with the schoolmaster voice talked the whole time, easy, reasonable, telling Jonah how this could still end cleanly, how the girl couldn't get far on the south face in the dark, how the Inquisitor only needed the man dead and the girl breathing. Jonah circled left, keeping the burned fire between him and the nearest bolts, and let the man talk, because talking men weren't shooting men.

His knee was screaming. His hands were steady. Thirty years of dead voices in his skull, and it turned out all that noise had been training—when you've spent your life listening to five conversations at once, six men moving in the dark is almost restful.

He heard the leftmost scout shift weight off his bad ankle. Heard the one behind the boulder nock a fresh quarrel, the click of the nut catching. Heard Bram breathing like a man being strangled slowly from the inside.

"Bram," Jonah said, without turning. "Your left flank. The tall one. He steps loud on his heel. Always has."

"Jonah—" Bram's voice broke apart. "Just drop the knife. Please. I'll—I'll tell them you went over the edge. I'll say the fall—"

"You always were a terrible liar."

The first bolt came from the boulder. Jonah was already moving—heard the nut click, counted one-and-a-half heartbeats, threw himself sideways. The quarrel tore through his cloak and opened a hot line across his ribs, shallow, ugly, wet. He came up inside the archer's reach and put his knife through the man's bowstring arm, not deep, just enough, and the man's scream bounced off three peaks.

Then the mountain came apart with noise.

More shouts below—down-slope, on the goat track—and torchlight blooming in the dark like rotten fruit. Jonah's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. They'd had a second team. Of course they'd had a second team; Serane Voss didn't leave doors open behind her quarry. Lior was running straight down the throat of it.

"*No*—" He was moving before he decided to, downhill, reckless, his bad knee buckling on the second stride, and a bolt slammed into the meat of his shoulder and spun him half around. The pain was white and total. He went down on the scree and the world went to sparks and ringing.

Through the ringing, distantly, impossibly, he heard wolves.

Not one wolf. Many. A rising chorus from the eastern ridge, voices braided together, and underneath the wolf-song something else—words, or the shape of words, in a language two centuries dead, rolling down the mountain like weather. The scouts froze. Even the greybeard stopped talking. And the wolves came off the ridge in a grey flood, flowing past the camp, past the fallen Jonah, past the scrambling men, pouring down the goat track after the tiny glowing shape that fled below—

—and parted around her like a river around a stone.

Jonah, on his back in the scree with a bolt in his shoulder and blood filling his boot, started laughing. It hurt too much to be funny. He laughed anyway, at the sky, at the absurdity, at the sheer stubborn ancient power of a mute eleven-year-old girl whose blood could command beasts and whose whole vocabulary of trust had fit inside two words she'd learned for him: *don't leave.*

"She doesn't need me," he wheezed, to nobody, to Bram, to the stars. "Did you see that? She never needed—"

A boot caught him in the temple and the sky went out.

* * *

He surfaced in pieces. Cold first. Then pain, layered, everywhere, organized like a choir. Then rope on his wrists. Then a voice, low and even, counting his wounds with clinical detachment.

"—shoulder bolt, through the muscle, not the joint. Ribs likely cracked, two, maybe three. Contusion, temporal. He'll live. The Inquisitor wants him able to talk."

Jonah opened his eyes.

Firelight. A cave, or an overhang. His hands bound behind him, his knife gone, his cloak gone. Three scouts sat around a small clean fire, and the greybeard was wrapping Jonah's shoulder with the brisk tenderness of a man who'd bandaged a hundred prisoners and buried ninety of them.

Bram crouched a few feet away, elbows on knees, staring into the flames. He looked ten years older than he had at dusk. Ten years and a hundred miles.

"They got her?" Jonah rasped. It cost him blood to talk. He paid it. "The second team. Did they—"

Bram flinched like he'd been struck.

"The girl's gone," the greybeard said, tying off the bandage. "The second team reports she reached the treeline with the wolf pack around her. Wolves won't be followed at night. Not those wolves." He said it evenly, but his hands paused a half-second on the knot, and Jonah, connoisseur of fear, catalogued it: the man was frightened, and hiding it badly. Good. Let the Order learn what everyone else learned. Let them chase her all the way to the door and find out what two hundred years of patience looks like when it finally stands up.

"She'll make it," Jonah said. To the fire. To himself. To the dead, who had gone, for the moment, blessedly, terrifyingly quiet—all except one voice, faint, familiar, amused, at the very bottom of his hearing, murmuring in Old Vaelthorni like a lullaby he almost knew.

He closed his eyes and let the darkness take him back down, and the last thing he heard, from far away, was Bram whispering into the fire, so softly the night nearly missed it:

"What have I done. What have I done. *What have I done.*"
