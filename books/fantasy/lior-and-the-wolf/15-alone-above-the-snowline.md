## 15. Alone Above the Snowline

The snow does not fall. It *arrives* — all at once, everywhere, as if the mountain has decided to become a different thing entirely.

Lior stands in it with Jonah's blood cooling on her sleeve.

She did not see him fall. She heard it — a sound like a door closing — and then Bram's voice shouting words she half-knows now, *run, girl, run*, though whether Bram meant it for her or for the men with crossbows she will never learn. She ran. Her legs chose before her mind did, and that is the old way, the Vaelthorni way: the body knows the road before the tongue can name it.

Now the path is gone. The scouts' torches are gone. Jonah is gone.

The wind comes down the col like something poured, and Lior turns in place, three times, four, looking for anything shaped like a direction. There is only white above and grey below and the small dark hole of the pass behind her, already filling in, already forgetting them.

*Jonah,* she says, inside, in the language with no living name. *Jonah, I don't—*

And the dead answer.

Not Jonah's dead. Not the chattering sparrow-souls and soldier-bones he carries in his pockets like coins. These voices come from under the snow itself, from the stone beneath the stone, rising through her boot-soles and up her spine the way water rises through a wick. They speak Old Vaelthorni. They speak it the way she dreams it — slow, formal, each word placed like a brick.

*Up,* they say. *Little last one. Up and east of the cairn. Do not take the low road; the low road was made by people who no longer exist, and it goes nowhere now.*

Lior stops turning. She closes her eyes.

This is what she has not told Jonah — could not tell him, because he would have looked at her the way he looks at his carvings, hungry and afraid at once. For weeks the voices have been getting louder, but only near certain places: the empty valley with its nameless well, the ford where the wolves bowed their heads to her, the cave where the dragon-scale burned. As if the country were not erased but *sleeping*, and she has been walking along the seam of its sleep, and every step wakes another mouth.

She opens her eyes and looks up. Above the whiteout, somewhere, there must be rock. Rock means ridge. Ridge means height, and everything the dead have said since the ford has bent upward like a plant toward light.

*Up,* they say again, gentler now. *We will count your steps so you do not lose them.*

She goes.

* * *

The first hour is the worst, because the first hour is fear wearing her body like a coat.

Her breath tears at her throat. The snow gets into her boots and melts and freezes and melts again until her toes are two distant rumors. Twice she stumbles into hollows hidden under the drifts and has to claw out, and once she hears — or thinks she hears — the scouts' dogs far below, a thin belling sound the wind keeps tearing apart.

When the dogs come again, closer, she does the thing she did at the ford without deciding to. She kneels, presses both bare hands into the snow, and speaks.

She does not know where the words come from. That is the truth she has carried since the fire, since the white door, since a voice called her *the last* and pushed her through into a world of roads and coins and men who burn countries out of books. The words live in her the way a river lives in a channel cut long before the rain. She only opens her mouth and lets them through.

*"Hollow-bones, run-furred, mothers of the grey road: this child walks under an old law. Pass over."*

The belling fades. Whether the wolves heard her or the wind simply changed, she cannot say. But the dead murmur approval against her palms, faint as heat from a hearth through a wall, and she takes her hands back and climbs on.

Somewhere in the second hour, the fear burns off like morning fog, and what is underneath it surprises her.

It is anger.

Not hot anger — the kind Jonah shows when he talks about the Ashen Order, jaw tight, knife-hand flexing. Hers is cold and enormous and patient, and it has been waiting two hundred years for someone to feel it. Because now, climbing, listening, she finally understands what the erasure *was*. Not just burning books. Not just killing names. The Order reached into the land itself and pulled threads out of it — pulled the road from the valley, the name from the well, the blessing from the wolves' mouths. And the world let them. The world forgot so easily, the way a dream forgets itself by noon.

But she didn't forget. She is the part that didn't forget.

*Yes,* say the dead, and there is something new in their voices, something that might be hope and might be hunger. *You are the ledger they could not burn.*

Lior climbs faster.

* * *

Near midday — if it is midday; the sky is a lid of pearl with no sun in it — she finds the cairn.

It should not be here. It is made of stones fitted too well for any living hand, and every stone bears marks she knows the way she knows her own heartbeat: spirals, wolf-tracks, the branching sign that means *door*. Snow has buried it to the shoulder, but the top stone still stands clear, and carved into its crown is a sentence in Old Vaelthorni that makes her knees go soft:

*WE COUNTED THE STEPS OF EVERY CHILD WHO CLIMBED TO THE DOOR. WALK WELL, LAST ONE. WE ARE STILL COUNTING.*

She puts her palm flat on the stone.

The dead come through so strongly she gasps — dozens of them, hundreds, a whole nation's worth of patience packed into one marker. And for the first time they are not vague, not murmuring, not grief-shaped. They are *organized*. They are a road crew. They are the keepers of a highway that exists only in memory, and they have been waiting two centuries for someone with ears to walk it.

*East of the cairn,* they tell her, crisp as a merchant giving directions. *Where the snow lies in a crescent, dig. There is a stair under the drift. Our masons cut it when the shrine was young. The Order unmade the road but stone remembers longer than paper.*

Lior digs. Her fingers go numb, then past numb into a bright burning, and then they strike steps — real steps, hewn granite, descending nowhere and ascending everywhere, running up the mountainside under the snow like the spine of some buried animal.

She laughs. It comes out of her as a puff of white and no sound at all, because her voice has been taken or traded or spent, but the laugh is still a laugh, and the dead laugh with her, a rustle like wind through a harvest field.

*You see?* they say. *They took the maps. They took the names. They could not take the stairs.*

She climbs the buried stair as the light begins to fail.

* * *

Dusk, above the snowline, is a color Lior has no word for in any language: a bruised gold going violet, lying flat across the ice fields like spilled honey. Below her, the world falls away in folds of cloud. Above her, black rock teeth stand against the last light, and among them — she sees it and her heart slams once, hard, like a fist on a door —

A red glow. Small. Steady. Breathing.

The ember-shrine. The way back to the scale, the visions, the door. The way back to *him*, maybe — because Jonah would follow the glow too, if he can walk, if Bram's betrayal didn't—

No. She makes herself stop. Grief is a heavy thing to carry uphill. She packs it down the way Jonah packs his carving tools: carefully, in order, ready to be opened later.

*He is alive,* says one voice, close to her ear, older than the others, and she chooses to believe it because the alternative is sitting down in the snow forever. *The man who hears us. He bargains like a river bends — around everything, never breaking. Climb, little last one. He will meet you at the door or not at all, and we think he will meet you at the door.*

*How do you know?*

A pause. Then, with something almost like amusement:

*Because he talked to us for thirty years and complained the whole time. Men who complain that much about a gift do not give it up.*

Lior almost smiles. Then the light dies, the cold comes down like a verdict, and she has to find shelter or die polite and frozen with her inheritance half-climbed.

The dead find it for her — a shallow overhang behind a fin of rock, floored with old pine boughs some forgotten traveler stacked there decades ago, still dry, still sweet-smelling. She wedges herself in, pulls her knees to her chest, and eats the last of the waybread from her coat pocket, chewing slowly, making it last, the way Jonah taught her without ever saying a word about it. *Never eat like it's the last food. Eat like there'll be more, and there usually is.*

For the first time since the pass, she lets herself think his name all the way through.

*Jonah Thackeray.* In Common Tongue shape, because that is how he wears it. In her language it would be something else — she worked it out weeks ago, lying awake in some smuggler's loft: *the-one-who-listens-at-the-door.* It fits him so exactly it frightens her. Names in Old Vaelthorni are not labels. They are instructions. And somewhere under the snow behind her, bleeding or not bleeding, caught or free, a smuggler with bone-dust on his sleeves is walking out an instruction written two hundred years before his birth.

*Rest,* the dead say. *Tomorrow the last climb. Tomorrow you stop following.*

She frowns at the dark. *Following what?*

*Us. The road. The ghosts of everyone who came before.* A pause, and when the voice returns it is quieter, and it is not one voice anymore but many, layered, the whole erased nation speaking in chorus: *Tomorrow, little heir, the road follows you. That is what an inheritance is. Not treasure. Weight. The weight other people used to carry, set down in your arms because everyone else who could hold it is gone.*

Lior sits with that in the cold, her breath smoking, the pine boughs prickling her back.

Then she does something she has never done. She reaches into her coat and takes out the small carving Jonah gave her at the ford — a wolf, no bigger than her thumb, whittled from a deer bone, its head thrown back in a howl he never explained. All these weeks it has been silent for her the way everything is silent for everyone else. His gift runs one direction: he hears the dead; she silences them.

Except.

Except since the pass, since his blood on her sleeve, the little wolf has felt warm. And now, cupped in her palms above the snowline, it hums — faintly, brokenly, like a song remembered rather than sung.

And in the hum, threaded thin as wire, she hears a voice she knows.

Not a dead voice. A living one. Hoarse, muttering, cursing under its breath the way he curses at knots and bad weather and his own bad knees.

*—should've kept left at the col, Thackeray, you sentimental fool, should've kept left—*

Alive. Walking. Lost, but walking, and complaining, which the dead promised was the same as surviving.

Lior holds the little wolf against her chest and lets the warmth of it seep through her ribs, and for the first time in her short, burned life she does not feel like the last of anything. She feels like the first of something. The first heir of a country that is not dead, only waiting. The first walker on a road that is remembering itself step by step beneath her boots. The first voice of a nation learning to speak again — starting, fittingly, with a mute girl and a smuggler who listens.

Above the overhang, through the gap in the rocks, the red glow of the shrine pulses once, twice, steady as a heartbeat.

*Sleep,* the dead whisper. *At dawn, we climb. And Lior — daughter of Vaelthorne, last of the counting — when you reach the door, look closely at who stands beside it.*

*Why?*

Another pause. Longer. When they answer, every voice has gone careful, the way people go careful around a wound:

*Because the door does not open only for heirs. And someone else has been climbing toward it for a very long time — someone who burned her own name to survive, and whose mother's lullaby is still singing in a bone in your friend's pocket.*

Lior lies back in the dark, the wolf-carving over her heart, and watches the red glow breathe against the stars.

Tomorrow she stops being a passenger.

Tonight, high above the snowline, wrapped in the voices of everyone she has ever belonged to, she begins — silently, wordlessly, in the old tongue — to practice the words she will need at the door. Words for opening. Words for welcome.

And one word, the hardest one, the one the dead keep teaching her over and over while she sleeps:

The word for *home* — which, in Old Vaelthorni, does not mean a place at all.

It means *the ones who wait for you.*
