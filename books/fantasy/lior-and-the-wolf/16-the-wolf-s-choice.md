## 16. The Wolf's Choice

They took him at the treeline above the snowline, where the pines grew crooked and the wind came down off the peaks with a sound like grinding teeth.

Jonah never saw the arrow. He felt it — a punch in the meat of his left shoulder that spun him half around and put him on his knees in the drift. His knife was out of his hand before he knew he'd dropped it. Two men in hunter's greens came out of the trees the way smoke comes out of a chimney, unhurried, already sure of him. Behind them walked a woman in ash-grey riding leathers with a ledger strapped to her hip like a weapon.

Inquisitor Serane Voss looked exactly as the dead had described her, which was the worst part. The dead were rarely wrong about faces.

"Well," Jonah said, and spat blood into the snow. It steamed. "You're shorter than your reputation."

"Bind the shoulder," Serane said to one of her men. "He bleeds out, he's useless." She crouched in front of him, close enough that he could see the frost caught in her lashes, and studied him the way he studied bone before the first cut — looking for the grain, the fault line, the place where pressure would do the most work. "Jonah Thackeray. Smuggler. Bone-carver. The man who stole a country's last heir out from under the Order's nose."

"I didn't steal anything," Jonah said. "She knocked on my door."

"Everything Vaelthorne touches is stolen goods." She said it flatly, without heat, the way a clerk reads an inventory. "The girl. The language. The name. You've been carrying contraband across the Thornfell for three weeks, Thackeray, and you didn't even know it. Where is she?"

"Gone ahead."

"Where."

"Ask your ledger. You people are so good at knowing where things used to be."

One of the hunters hit him across the ear, open-handed but hard enough to ring his skull like a struck bell. Jonah rode it out. He'd been beaten before; smugglers who couldn't take a beating died young or talked too fast, and both ended the same way.

Serane hadn't moved. She was watching him with those grey eyes, patient as winter, and Jonah understood suddenly that this was not going to be a beating kind of interrogation. This was going to be worse. This was going to be *patient*.

"You're wondering why you're still alive," she said.

"The thought had crossed my mind."

"Because you're the only living person who knows which path she took through the high passes. Because the girl trusts you, and trust is a door, and I am very good at doors." She stood, brushing snow from her knee. "And because I want to understand something first. Thirty years you've lived alone on that mountain, carving dead men's bones and listening to their ghosts. No wife. No crew since Bram left. No god anyone's heard of. Why does a man like that die for a child who can't even say his name?"

The question landed harder than the arrow had. Jonah worked his jaw, tasted copper, and said nothing.

Serane nodded as if he'd answered. "Take him up to the camp. Warm him. Feed him. He'll talk when he's ready. They always talk when they're ready."

* * *

The Order's high camp sat in a bowl below the pass, canvas and iron and disciplined firelight. They put him in a tent with a brazier and gave him broth that was better than anything he'd eaten in a month, which he resented deeply. A young soldier — nineteen, maybe twenty, with a farm boy's hands — changed the dressing on his shoulder with more gentleness than the job required.

"Hold still," the boy murmured. "The Inquisitor wants you whole."

"What does she want me *for*?"

The boy glanced at the tent flap and said nothing, which told Jonah everything: fear ran this camp the way blood runs a body. Serane Voss didn't rule by cruelty. She ruled by certainty, and certainty frightened people worse.

She came at dusk.

No guards. No instruments. Just the woman and a folding stool and a small clay flask of something that smelled like plum brandy. She poured two cups and set one within his reach, and Jonah looked at it for a long moment before deciding that if she wanted him poisoned there were cheaper ways.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Serane said.

"I'd rather hear the one where I walk out of here."

"This is better." She drank. Outside, the wind worried the tent ropes. "There was a village once, in a valley east of here. Fruit orchards. Terraced fields. A shrine to a dragon whose name meant *the ember that remembers*. The people there sang songs in a language no one else spoke, and they baked bread with honey, and they believed — foolishly, stubbornly — that they were safe." Her voice didn't change at all. That was the horror of it. It stayed level as a frozen lake. "I know this village because I was born in it. I was six years old when the Order came to unmake it. Not burn it. Burning leaves ashes, and ashes leave evidence. Unmaking is cleaner. Records first. Then names. Then memories. My father forgot my mother while she was standing in front of him. I watched it happen. And then a man in grey knelt down beside me and asked if I wanted to forget too, because forgetting doesn't hurt."

Jonah's hands had gone still around the cup. Somewhere far off in his skull, faint as a pulse under skin, he could feel the carvings in his pack — the ones the hunters hadn't found, the ones sewn into his coat lining — pressing against the silence like faces against glass.

"And you said yes," Jonah said quietly.

"I said yes." Serane turned the cup in her fingers. "I said yes, and I lived, and I rose, and now I unmake things for a living. So you'll forgive me if I don't weep over Vaelthorne, Thackeray. I've seen what remembering costs. I chose the other price."

"That's a fine speech."

"It's the truth."

"It's a fine speech," Jonah repeated. "I've heard a lot of truths in thirty years of listening to the dead. Do you know what they all have in common? Every ghost I ever carved? They all made a choice just like yours. Something to survive. And every single one of them spent the rest of their death trying to take it back."

Something moved behind Serane's eyes — quick, quickly buried. "You think you can wound me with sentiment."

"No." Jonah set down the cup. His heart was going hard now, and he let it, because the thing he was about to do required a steady voice and nothing else. "I don't deal in sentiment. I deal in bone. And I'm going to show you a piece of work, and you're going to tell me where I got it wrong."

* * *

They'd taken his pack, of course. But they hadn't taken his coat, because a wounded man needs his coat in the mountains, and because the Order's thoroughness had limits when the prize seemed already won. Jonah's fingers found the seam inside the lining and worked free the small bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and Serane watched him do it and did not stop him, which told him she was curious, and curiosity was the crack he needed.

He unwrapped it on his knee.

A carving of a wolf, no longer than his thumb, done in the pale dense ivory of elk antler. He'd made it himself, two years ago, from material bought off a peddler who claimed it came out of the eastern valleys — the drowned valleys, the erased ones. Most of what the peddler sold was junk. But this piece had hummed against Jonah's palm from the moment he touched it, singing something old and small and stubborn, and he'd carried it ever since without quite knowing why.

Now he knew why. Now he understood what the dead had been saving it for.

"This is a lullaby," Jonah said. "It's not words, exactly. It's older than words. It's the sound a mother makes in a language that only existed in one country in the world, in one valley of that country, and it went like this—"

He closed his eyes. He did what he had done ten thousand times in a workshop above the smuggler's road: he opened the door in himself and let the voice through.

The carving began to sing.

It wasn't loud. It barely filled the tent. A thin thread of sound, wordless, rising and falling in a pattern that no living throat had produced in two hundred years — a melody shaped like rocking, like arms, like *sleep now, little ember, sleep now, the dragon watches over you.* The brazier flame leaned toward it. The wind outside dropped, as if the mountain itself had gone quiet to listen.

And Serane Voss, Inquisitor of the Ashen Order, unmaker of nations, went white to the lips.

Jonah watched her the way he'd watch a fracture travel through bone — minute by minute, following the line of it. Her face didn't crumple. That wasn't her way. What happened was smaller and more terrible: her composure simply developed a flaw, a hairline crack running from eye to jaw, and behind it something enormous shifted its weight.

"Stop it," she said.

"It's a pretty tune."

"*Stop it.*"

"Where did I get it wrong?" Jonah asked softly. "Tell me where I got it wrong, and I'll stop."

Her hand went to her belt — to the ledger, to the tools, to some habit of authority — and stopped halfway. The melody wound on, patient, maternal, merciless. And Jonah saw it happen, the thing the dead had promised him would happen, the thing he had bet a life on: Serane Voss's mouth moved. Just slightly. Just once. Following the shape of a sound her conscious mind had been forced to forget, but her body — her body born in a valley of orchards and honey bread — remembered perfectly.

She knew the next note before it came.

"There was a woman," Serane said, and her voice had finally, finally lost its level surface. "In the village. She used to sing to the children at the well. All the mothers did, but hers carried furthest. My mother." Her eyes fixed on the little wolf as though it were a window and something stood on the other side. "I denounced her. Did you know that? When the Order came, they offered children amnesty if they named the recalcitrant adults — the ones who hid records, who taught the forbidden words. I was seven. I named my own mother to save myself, and they unmade her in front of me, and then they unmade my memory of denouncing her, and I have walked around for thirty years with a hole in my chest the exact size of a woman singing at a well, and I never knew what fit in it until right now."

The lullaby faded. The tent was very quiet.

"So that's the truth of you," Jonah said. "You weren't made by the Order. You were made by that night. And you've spent your whole life pretending the scar is a conviction." He leaned forward, ignoring the pull of the arrow-wound, and held the carving out to her on his open palm. "Here's my offer, Inquisitor. Not surrender. Not information. One truth, freely given: that girl you're hunting is the last heir of the country that made that song. Everything the Order burned — the valley, the orchards, the well, your mother's voice — it all exists on the other side of one white door, and she's the key, and she's *almost there.*"

"You're lying."

"I'm a smuggler and a liar by trade, and I swear to you on every dead soul I've ever carved: I am not lying about this."

Serane stared at the wolf. Her hand hovered over it and did not touch it, as if touching might cost her whatever was left of the person the Order had built on top of the child in the valley.

"If I believe you," she said slowly, "then everything I am is a lie wearing a uniform."

"Yes," Jonah said. "That's exactly the bargain. That's the only thing I have to sell, and I'm selling it cheap, because I need you to buy it tonight. Because the ember is dying, Serane. The last fire of the last guardian of Vaelthorne gutters out within the season, maybe within weeks, and when it goes, the door closes forever, and your mother's song goes with it, and the hole in your chest stays a hole until you die and become one more ghost nobody will carve." He swallowed. His voice roughened despite himself. "I've listened to the dead for thirty years. There is no sound in this world sadder than a ghost still trying to take back a choice. Don't make yourself into that."

For a long moment she didn't move. The brazier popped. Somewhere outside, a sentry called the hour in a bored voice, and the ordinary machinery of the camp ground on, oblivious, and Jonah understood that the entire future of a vanished nation hung on the next breath of one woman in ash-grey leather.

Then Serane reached out and picked up the wolf.

She held it the way you hold something hot — carefully, at arm's length from her heart. And when she spoke again, her voice was the clerk's voice, the inventory voice, and it was the bravest thing Jonah had ever heard, because he could hear what it cost.

"The girl went up the Shepherd's Stair," she said. "Past the frozen falls. There's a second detachment at the col — twelve men, Captain Dree in command. They have orders to kill the child on sight if capture fails." Her fingers tightened around the carving until the knuckles whitened. "I am going to walk out of this tent and report that you said nothing useful. I am going to be believed, because I am always believed. And then, Thackeray—" the crack in her voice showed through, one last time, like lightning inside a cloud— "you had better hope that song is worth what it's going to cost me. Because I don't know yet whether I'm coming after you to help you."

She stood. At the tent flap she paused, her back to him, the little wolf hidden in her fist.

"But I know," she said, "that I am not letting Dree's men reach that door first."

The flap fell shut behind her. Jonah sat alone with the brazier and the broth and the thundering of his own heart, and from deep inside his coat lining, faint as embers under ash, the other carvings began — very softly, very joyfully — to sing.
