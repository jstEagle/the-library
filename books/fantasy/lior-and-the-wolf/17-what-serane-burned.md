## 17. What Serane Burned

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Serane Voss stood in it for a long time before she gave the order.

The prisoner sat on the floor with his back against the wall, one arm cradled against his chest where the scouts had worked him over. A smuggler's hands—scarred, clever, stained faintly with the dust of bone. He looked up at her without fear, and that was the first wrong thing. Men who knew what the Order did to prisoners always feared her. This man looked at her the way a craftsman looks at a flawed piece of material: with pity.

"You're going to let me go," he said.

"I'm going to have you carried to the Chapterhouse at Grey Ford," she said, "where you'll be questioned properly."

"I don't think so." He shifted, winced, and kept looking at her. "You've been standing there for four minutes, Inquisitor. I've been counting. Four minutes, and you haven't called the guards."

She hadn't noticed the time passing. That was the second wrong thing.

* * *

She had taken the carving from him at the ford camp, along with his knife and his boots and the little tin of tools he'd begged to keep. Standard procedure. Everything an unregistered traveler carried went into the evidence ledger, catalogued and sealed. She had turned the carving over in her fingers on the ride back—a sparrow-sized thing, some kind of bird with its wings folded, carved from a grey-white bone she couldn't identify—and had felt nothing. It was just an object. The Order had spent two centuries teaching her that objects from Vaelthorne were just objects. Ash was ash. Bone was bone.

Then, in the quiet of her tent, alone, she had set it on her field desk beside the ledger of unmade things, and it had begun to sing.

Not aloud. Not in any way a recording clerk could have captured. It sang in the place behind her sternum where she kept the things she did not think about—the narrow bed in a house with blue shutters, a woman's voice low in the dark, a melody that rose and fell like water finding its way downhill.

*Sleep, my ashling, sleep and grow,
the mountain keeps what fires know—*

She had put her hand over it. She had picked it up and held it in the brazier flame until the metal fittings smoked, and it had not burned, and it had not stopped singing, and she had stood in her own tent at midnight with tears running down her face for the first time in nineteen years, listening to her mother's lullaby come out of a dead bird's bone.

Her mother. Who had no name anymore. Whose name Serane herself had helped unmake, whose face she could no longer hold in memory except as a shape, a warmth, a voice—and now the voice was here, in her tent, singing to her out of the work of a smuggler who heard the dead.

She had gone back to the cell to interrogate him. That was what she told herself. Interrogation. Procedure. She was Senior Inquisitor of the Third Circle; she had performed nine hundred interrogations; she had never once stood silent in a cell for four minutes while the prisoner counted.

"How did you get that song," she said.

Jonah Thackeray leaned his head back against the stone. "I didn't get it. I made it. Carved it three winters ago off a sparrow wing-bone a farmer's boy sold me by the sack." He said it simply, without performance. "The dead live in the bones. Whatever they were carrying when they died, they carry still. Your mother must have sung that song a thousand times. It's still in her."

"Stop."

"It's not a trick," he said. "I know you want it to be a trick. It would be easier if it were."

"I said stop." Her voice came out flat and cold, the Chapterhouse voice, the voice she used on priests and village elders when the questions began. But her hands, at her sides, had curled into fists so tight her nails bit through her gloves.

Thackeray watched her fists. He was a perceptive man; smugglers had to be. "You remember it," he said quietly. "That's the thing about the Unmaking, isn't it? They can burn the records, strike the names, salt the maps. But they can't reach into your skull and pull the song out. So they tell you the song is nothing. A nonsense tune. A dream you had. And you spend your whole life pretending you don't hum it when you can't sleep."

The cell was very quiet. Somewhere above them, snow slid off the guardhouse roof.

"My name is Serane Voss," she said. "I was born in Grey Ford. My father was a tanner. I entered the Order at eleven years old."

"Yes," Thackeray said. "And the night before you entered, a woman sang you to sleep in a language the Order says never existed."

She crossed the cell in three strides and hit him open-handed across the face, hard enough to turn his head, hard enough to sting her palm through the glove. He took it without flinching. When he looked back at her, there was no triumph in his eyes. Only that terrible craftsman's pity.

"There she is," he said softly. "There's the girl from the house with the blue shutters."

Serane walked out of the cell and gave the order to keep him under watch, and then she walked up onto the wall of the waystation and stared north into the dark until her eyes dried, and she did not let herself think anything at all.

* * *

But thinking found her anyway. Thinking always found her, because she had built her whole self out of not-thinking, and not-thinking was a dam, and dams held only as long as the water stayed calm.

She thought about the purge, which she had not permitted herself to do in nineteen years.

She had been seven when the riders came to the valley. She remembered it in fragments, the way children remember disasters: the smell of smoke that wasn't woodsmoke, something sweeter and more chemical; her father's hands shaking as he nailed the shutters closed, though everyone knew shutters wouldn't matter; her mother's arms around her in the cellar, singing, always singing, because her mother believed songs were a kind of armor.

The Order hadn't come with fire that first night. That was the part no one understood, the part that made the whole horror possible. They came with papers. They came politely, to the village square, with a ledger and a bell, and they read out the Edict of Ash in voices like men reading a shipping manifest, and they explained—with genuine patience, almost kindness—that Vaelthorne had committed crimes against the harmony of nations so profound that it could not be punished, only erased. Its armies would be broken. Its libraries burned. Its name struck from every map, every chronicle, every gravestone. And its people—its people would be given a mercy: they would be allowed to forget.

"Forget?" her mother had whispered later, in the cellar. "How can anyone make you forget?"

Serane had learned how, at eleven, in the Chapterhouse at Grey Ford. It wasn't sorcery, mostly. It was repetition. It was new names issued with new clothes. It was teachers who corrected you, gently, endlessly, every time you slipped—*there is no such word, child; there is no such place; you dreamed it*—until the slips stopped, until the dream receded, until the hole where your country had been filled in with smooth grey doctrine and you couldn't even find the edges of it anymore to probe. Some of the older ones fought it. She remembered a boy, older than her, who screamed his true name at the walls for a week until they took him away for the deeper rites, and after that he was placid and helpful and had no name at all, and everyone agreed this was better.

Serane had not fought. That was the fact at the bottom of everything, the stone the whole edifice of her life stood on. She had been seven and terrified and clever, and she had understood before any adult told her that survival in the new world belonged to those who embraced it fastest. So when the instructors asked, in her first year, whether any of the children remembered words from the forbidden tongue—asking, they said, only so they could help cleanse such residue—Serane had raised her hand.

She had given them the lullaby.

She had sung it for them, note for note, in her clear child's voice, while an instructor wrote it down, and she had seen the instructor's face change—not with anger but with something like relief, gratitude even, the way a surgeon is grateful for a tumor that presents cleanly—and she had understood, even at eleven, even with her heart hammering, that she had just purchased her future with the last piece of her mother.

They had praised her. Fast-tracked her. Marked her file *residue-free, cooperative, suitable.* And her mother—her mother had died in the third year of the resettlement, of a fever, in a town whose name Serane had been taught to say correctly, and Serane had not cried at the funeral because crying required remembering, and remembering had been trained out of her, and by then she genuinely could not find the door into the grief. It was the cleanest wound in the world: a loss she couldn't locate.

Until a smuggler carved a sparrow's wing-bone and handed her the key she had thrown away herself.

* * *

At dawn she went down to the cells again.

Thackeray was awake, sitting where she'd left him. There was a bruise blooming on his cheekbone from her hand. He looked at her and waited. He was good at waiting. She supposed you had to be, in his trade, and in hers.

"The girl," Serane said. "Lior. Where is she headed?"

He smiled faintly. "You know where."

The Thornfell high country. The shrine passes. The door. Of course she knew where; the ledger of unmade things had been itching for weeks, a spreading rash across the northern ranges where history strained against its stitching, and every report confirmed it: the ember-shrine's scale had flared visible from three valleys away, a light that should not exist, a light the Order had recorded extinguished two hundred years ago. The last heir was walking home, and the dead country was waking up to meet her.

"If she opens that door," Serane said, "everything comes back. Every name. Every record. Every grave we dug in the archives. Two centuries of work, undone by one child walking through a doorway."

"Not undone," Thackeray said. "Corrected."

"Do you imagine the world survives it? Do you know what happens when a nation un-erases itself? Borders, claims, blood-debts, every treaty written on the assumption that Vaelthorne is ash—" She stopped. She was arguing doctrine. She could hear herself arguing doctrine, the grey smooth voice of the Chapterhouse, and beneath it, absurdly, relentlessly, the lullaby went on singing in her chest like a second heartbeat.

"And what happens," Thackeray said, "to the people who made themselves comfortable on top of a grave?"

She left without answering.

* * *

The orders were clear, and she had written half of them herself. Prisoner to be transferred to Grey Ford under seal. Scout company to proceed to the shrine passes and establish a cordon. The heir to be taken alive if possible—the Order wanted to study what survived in her blood—but the smuggler was expendable, and the door itself was to be sealed by rite regardless of cost. Serane Voss, Senior Inquisitor, Third Circle, to command in the field.

She stood in her tent with the transfer order in her hand and the escort detail waiting outside, and she thought about the boy who had screamed his name at the walls.

She thought about what the Order had done to the town of Harrow's Bend three weeks ago, the full Unmaking, the rite performed in the open because the pair had been sheltered there too long and too warmly. She had stood in the square and watched two hundred lives folded up like a letter being put away. The baker's ovens went cold mid-loaf. Children mid-game. A dog had stood in the street barking at nothing, because dogs remembered longer than the rite could reach, and then the dog lay down and forgot why it was sad. And in the crowd, for one instant before the forgetting took them all, a girl of maybe eight had looked directly at Serane with her mother's eyes—her mother's, the woman from the blue shutters, the same wide-set gray—and Serane had felt the rite pass through her own chest like a hook, and she had told herself afterward that what she felt was professional strain.

Nineteen years of telling herself things. She was very good at it. It was, when you got down to it, the only skill the Order had ever really taught her.

She looked at the transfer order. Then she took out a pen and amended it: *Prisoner injured during apprehension; unfit for transport; remand to waystation custody pending recovery.* Sloppy paperwork. Her first sloppy paperwork in nineteen years. Some clerk at Grey Ford would query it eventually, and the query would take weeks to route, and by then—

By then she would know what she was.

She dismissed the escort. She told her lieutenant to hold the cordon at the lower passes and await her signal, and she watched his face for suspicion and found none, because Serane Voss did not falter; Serane Voss was the standard the faltering were measured against.

Then she packed light: cloak, blade, the ledger of unmade things, and—wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her pack, where she told herself it was merely evidence—the sparrow-bone bird that would not stop singing.

* * *

She caught their trail a day behind them, in fresh snow, and she followed it not because she had decided anything but because the trail was there and following trails was the only honest thing left in her. Up through the black pines. Past the shrine cave where the dragon-scale's glow still stained the ice on the walls like a sunset trapped underwater. The higher she climbed, the louder the itch in the ledger grew, until at night she could feel the pages trembling against her ribs like a caged bird of their own.

On the third night, camped alone above the snowline with the wind moaning through the pass, she finally unwrapped the carving and held it up to the firelight.

It sang. Her mother sang. *Sleep, my ashling, sleep and grow,* and Serane Voss, Senior Inquisitor of the Third Circle, born in a house with blue shutters in a country that officially had never existed, sat in the snow with her mother's voice in her hands and wept without sound, the way she had been taught, the way she had taught others, the way the Order taught everyone to grieve: efficiently, silently, in a language with no words.

When it was done she wiped her face, wrapped the bird away, and looked north, toward the white door she had spent her whole life denying.

She knew what Jonah Thackeray would say if he could see her. He'd say she wasn't following them to kill them. He'd say the lullaby had already chosen her side, and she was only catching up to it.

"Inquisitor Voss does not falter," she said aloud to the wind, and hated how much it sounded like a prayer.

Then she shouldered her pack and walked on into the dark, one day behind a smuggler and a child, carrying a country's last song toward the door it had been sung to guard—knowing that somewhere behind her, Grey Ford had opened her amended transfer order, and knowing, with the certainty of a woman reading her own death warrant, that the question in it would bring the whole Order at her heels.
