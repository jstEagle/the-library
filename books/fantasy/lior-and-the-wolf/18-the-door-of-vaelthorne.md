## 18. The Door of Vaelthorne

The dead had been singing for three days.

Not the usual chorus—Jonah knew that chorus the way a man knows his own heartbeat, the mutter and murmur of every bone he'd ever carved, the sparrow's last song, the wolf femur's ancient patience, the hundred small voices that had never once let him sleep in silence. This was different. This was *directional*. It came from ahead and below, threading through the rock of the mountain like water finding its way to a sea, and it grew louder with every step he took down into the dark.

His shoulder wound had gone hot again. He could feel it with each footfall on the shrine stairs, a dull pulse keeping time with the singing. Serane's scouts had left him with more than a scar. He'd bound it with strips of his own shirt and kept moving, because the alternative was lying down in the snow and letting the mountain have him, and Jonah Thackeray had spent thirty years refusing to give anything back once he'd taken it.

The stairs were older than any kingdom he knew. That was the thing that kept snagging his thoughts as he descended—these steps had been cut before Thornfell had a name, before the Order had a ledger, before anyone decided that a country could be unwritten like a bad line in a contract. Each stair was a single slab of white stone, seamless, uncracked after two centuries of abandonment. The living forgot. The stone remembered.

And at the bottom of the stairs, the singing stopped waiting for him.

It rose up all at once, a tide of voices, hundreds of them, thousands, and Jonah gripped the wall because his knees wanted to fold. He'd heard the dead speak through bone his whole life—one voice at a time, one small song at a time, smuggled whispers in carved things. But this was a country's worth of dead, all speaking at once, and they weren't speaking to him.

They were calling her name.

*Lior. Lior. Lior.*

He came out of the stairwell into a cavern vast enough to hold weather. Stalactites hung like the ribs of some god-sized beast, and between them, veins of light ran through the rock—not torchlight, not moonlight, but a deep amber glow, warm as bread fresh from an oven, pulsing slow and faint. Faint was the wrong word. *Failing.* The light stuttered like a candle in a draft, flared, dimmed, flared again. The ember-shrine above had told him there was a season left. Standing here, beneath it, Jonah understood that seasons lie. This was a fire counting its final breaths.

And against the far wall, taller than his workshop, taller than the smuggler's chapel in Greyhollow, taller than anything made by hands he could imagine—

A door.

White. Not painted white, not lime-washed, but white the way snow is white, the way bone is white, a white that seemed to come from inside the material rather than sit upon it. No handle. No hinges visible. Only carvings, covering every inch of it, so dense and fine that from across the cavern they looked like frost ferns—and Jonah's carver's eyes, even watering with exhaustion, began to pick out what they were.

Sparrows. Wolves. Dragons. A thousand birds in flight, a thousand beasts running, all of them spiraling inward toward the center of the door, where a single figure stood with one hand raised. Small figure. A child.

*She alone can open its final door.* The old wolf femur's voice, from weeks ago and a world ago. The dead beg Jonah to take her home.

Well. He'd gotten her home. Now he stood in the doorway of it like a thief who'd delivered stolen goods and didn't know how to leave.

"You're bleeding."

Jonah spun, hand going to the knife he'd stopped trusting weeks ago and still carried anyway.

She sat on the lowest step of the dais before the door, small and still, wrapped in the grey wool cloak he'd bought her in Greyhollow with money he'd sworn he'd never spend on anything good. Her face was thinner than he liked. There was ash on her cheek and her braid had half-come undone, and when she looked up at him, she did something she almost never did.

She smiled.

Then she signed, quick and fluid: *You're late. I've been here two days.*

"Two days?" He limped toward her, scanning the cavern out of habit—the entrances, the shadows, the places scouts might hide. Nothing moved except the failing light. "Alone? Above the snowline? Child, I taught you better than—"

*I wasn't alone,* she signed, and her hands hesitated, which her hands never did. *They sang me down. All the way from the pass. I only had to follow.*

"The dead."

*The dead.* She patted the step beside her. *Sit. You look terrible.*

"I look terrible," Jonah said, sitting with a grunt that betrayed him, "because I fought four men on a mountainside and then walked three days on a wound that should've killed me, following voices that wouldn't shut up about you." He eased his pack off his good shoulder. "So. Same as any Tuesday."

Lior laughed—a real laugh, breath and sound, rare as summer snow—and leaned against him, careful of the wound. For a while neither of them spoke. The ember-light pulsed. The door waited.

It was Lior who broke the quiet. She reached into her cloak and drew out the carving he'd given her back at the ford, after the wolves turned aside—the little bone wolf, no bigger than his thumb, the first thing he'd ever made that felt less like craft and more like prayer. She set it in his palm and closed his fingers over it.

*It's been talking to me,* she signed. *All the way down. It says things are going to be said soon that we won't want to hear.*

"Lovely," Jonah muttered. "Even your souvenirs gossip."

But the bone was warm in his hand, warmer than it should be, and when he loosened his fingers and looked at it, the tiny wolf seemed to be looking back. And the great chorus of the dead, which had fallen silent at Lior's arrival, stirred again—not calling her name now, but gathering itself, the way a crowd gathers before a verdict.

The voice, when it came, used the wolf femur's timbre. Old. Patient. Terribly kind.

*Carver. You brought her. We thank you.*

"Don't thank me yet," Jonah said aloud, because Lior couldn't hear it and someone in this cavern ought to say ordinary words. "I've a feeling thanks is premature."

*The door opens for the heir,* the dead said. *Only for the heir. Her blood is the key; her name is the lock. When she speaks herself home, Vaelthorne will rise again—its fields, its rivers, its people. All of us. Everything the fire took and the Order buried.*

"And the catch," Jonah said. His mouth had gone dry. "There's always a catch. What's the catch?"

The silence that followed lasted long enough that he knew the answer before it was spoken.

*Whoever passes the threshold belongs wholly to Vaelthorne. Flesh and spirit. The country will live again—but it lives behind the seal, apart from the world, as it always was. Those who enter cannot return. Not to these mountains. Not to these roads. Not ever.*

Jonah looked at the door. White and patient and beautiful, carved with ten thousand living things, and every one of them locked inside forever.

"No way back," he said. "None?"

*Would you trust a door that lied?*

"I'd trust it more than I trust the ones that tell the truth." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "Thirty years I've listened to the dead. Thirty years, and now the dead offer me exactly what I always wanted—to belong somewhere, to be among voices that know my name—and the price is becoming one of them."

*Not one of us,* the dead said gently. *One with us. There is a difference, carver. But yes. It is a door with only one direction.*

Lior had gone very still beside him. He glanced down and found her watching his face with those old, old eyes, reading the conversation in the set of his jaw the way she read everything, and her hands came up slowly.

*What did they say?*

Jonah weighed it. He'd lied to smugglers, to magistrates, to priests, to himself. He could lie to her. It would be easy. *The door opens, we go home together, happy ending, don't ask questions.*

Instead he signed, one-handed, clumsy but clear: *Door opens for you. Whoever enters stays forever. No coming back. Ever.*

Her face didn't crumple. That was the thing about Lior that undid him—grief went through her like light through glass, visible and unbroken, and came out the other side already shaped into something else. She looked at the door for a long time. Then at him. Then she signed:

*Good.*

"*Good?*"

*It should cost something,* she signed firmly. *Homes that cost nothing aren't homes. They're inns.* A pause. Her hands slowed. *But you can't come.*

"I know I can't come."

*You could,* she signed. *If you entered with me. They said whoever enters. Not whoever has the blood.* Her eyes searched his. *You could stay with me. With them. With everyone who talks to you. Forever.*

And there it was. The choice crystallized, hanging in the ember-light like the stalactites overhead. Thirty years of loneliness laid out on a scale against one mute child who had cracked his closed life open at a door in a snowstorm, and every weight on the scale was pulling him toward that white door, and he could not for the life of him see which way it would tip.

"I could," he said quietly. "Maybe. Ask me again when the door's open."

Before she could answer, the cavern changed.

The ember-veins in the walls guttered—all of them at once, a single indrawn breath of light—and the temperature dropped so fast his breath bloomed white. From the stairwell behind them came a sound Jonah knew from a lifetime of smuggling: boots. Many boots. Disciplined. Descending.

Lior was on her feet. Her hand found his sleeve, and her other hand pointed—not at the stairs, but at a shadowed fissure in the cavern wall near the dais, a crack barely wide enough for a child. She signed fast: *There's another way in. They showed me. I hid there when the first ones came yesterday.*

"First ones?" Jonah's blood went cold. "How many?"

*Four. They looked at the door and couldn't see it. One touched it and screamed. They left.* Her signing sharpened. *These are more. And they have fire.*

From the stairwell, echoing up out of the dark, came a voice Jonah had hoped never to hear again this side of the grave—level, cold, and carrying the particular weariness of a woman doing arithmetic with human lives.

"Thackeray." Serane Voss's voice filled the cavern. "I know you're down there. And I know what stands in front of you. Come up, or I come down, and I promise you the difference matters."

Jonah looked at the door. At Lior. At the crack in the wall she'd marked.

The ember-light pulsed once, weakly, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

"Well," he said, and drew his knife. "Ask me again when the door's open."
