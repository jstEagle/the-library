## 19. Ash and Ember

### SERANE

She smelled the dragon-fire before she saw it.

It came down the mountain on the wind like a memory of a memory—warmth where there should be frost, a sweetness under the stone and snow, the scent of something burning that did not consume. Serane Voss reined her horse at the lip of the gorge and felt the smell crawl into her chest and settle there, behind the breastbone, in the place where the Order had spent thirty years teaching her there was nothing.

"Ember-shrine's ahead," said Brother Callow, riding up beside her. He was young, earnest, and frightened, which made him useful. "The readings from the ledger—" He had the book open across his saddle, its pages fluttering. "Inquisitor, the itch is *everywhere*. The whole mountain is—"

"I know what it is." She spurred past him.

Behind her came forty riders of the Ashen Order, grey cloaks dusted white, hoods up against the wind, faces hidden as their doctrine demanded. A face was a name. A name was a history. A history was a door the fire could walk through. They had burned a country once with less than this—a country of songs and harvest festivals and mothers singing lullabies over cradles—and they would burn one child and one smuggler now with considerably more.

Except.

Except the ledger had been wrong all week, or right in a way she couldn't say aloud. It didn't just itch anymore. It *sang*. Pages that recorded unmade things—the village of Hallowmere, deleted three days ago because a farmer had given two travelers bread; the ferryman Osk, deleted last spring; the word *Vaelthorne* itself, struck through so many times the ink had gone silver—those pages were warm now. Warm as skin. When she carried the ledger against her ribs at night she could feel it beating, faintly, like a second heart she'd swallowed.

And when it beat, she dreamed of a kitchen. White plaster walls. A woman humming a tune with no words, a tune that climbed and folded back on itself like a bird circling before landing. Serane had woken from that dream four nights running with wet eyes and no idea why, and she had reported none of it, and the not-reporting was itself a crime the Order would kill her for.

The smuggler had known. That was the worst of it. In the cell beneath High Camp, chained and bleeding from the pass, Jonah Thackeray had looked at her with those tired carver's hands folded in his lap and said: *You were born there. I can prove it. Your mother sang you a lullaby, and I carved it into bone.*

He'd held out a small sparrow-bone figure, no bigger than his thumb, and it had sung. Her mother's voice. A voice the Order had taken from her when she was six years old along with her name—her *real* name, the Vaelthorni one, the one that meant something about rivers and morning—and replaced it with *Serane Voss*, a name manufactured in an office, stamped on a foundling, assigned like a ration.

She had let him go. She told herself it was strategy: he would lead them to the door faster than interrogation ever could. She told herself she followed him secretly for the same reason.

She told herself a great many things, and the mountain wind stripped every one of them bare.

"There." Callow pointed.

Below them, where the trail bent around a shoulder of black rock, the ground opened into a hollow, and in the hollow stood a door.

It was white. Not painted-white, not lime-washed-white, but white the way snow is white, white the way bone is white—white that seemed to make light rather than receive it. It stood free of any wall, framed by nothing, taller than two men, and above it, embedded in the living rock of the cliff, a single scale the size of a shield glowed a deep, dying orange. As Serane watched, the glow flared—brightened, brightened, until the whole hollow was lit like noon—and then sank again, guttering, a candle with minutes left in it.

Two figures stood before the door. A man, leaning on a spear he clearly didn't know how to use, his side bandaged dark. And a girl, small, dark-haired, her hand raised toward the white surface.

"The heir," Callow breathed. "And the traitor."

Serane dismounted. Her legs felt strange, distant, like borrowed things. She drew her sword because her hand knew how even when the rest of her had stopped giving orders.

"Inquisitor?" Callow's voice cracked. "Your commands?"

She looked at the forty hooded faces. She thought: *I was six years old. There was a kitchen. Someone loved me enough to sing.* She thought: *We burned Hallowmere for bread.* She thought: *Every face under those hoods is a name someone gave away.*

"Form ranks," she heard herself say. "We take the threshold."

Her boots crunched through snow toward the only home she had left, and it was burning down, and she was holding the torch.

* * *

### LIOR

The door was warm.

Lior pressed both palms flat against the white surface and it answered her the way her mother's cheek had answered her once, long ago, in a room full of smoke: *here, here, you're here, I have you.* Under her hands the wood—not wood, older than wood, older than the word for wood—hummed a note so low she felt it in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the soles of her feet through the snow.

And the dead of Vaelthorne were singing.

They had been singing since the shrine, since the ember-scale showed her everything—the valley green again, the well with water in it, the village that wasn't there standing whole and golden in a light that had no sun. Since then they hadn't left her. They walked beside her up the mountain like a crowd of transparent pilgrims, thousands of them, generations of them, farmers and weavers and soldiers and children, and they sang in Old Vaelthorni the song that opened doors, and the door had begun, very slowly, to open.

A seam of gold light ran down its center. It widened as she sang. It was the width of a finger now. A hand. An arm.

Jonah stood behind her with the spear and kept looking over his shoulder at the trail. His breath came short and wet. The wound in his side was bad; she could hear it in him, the way his heart stumbled every fourth beat, the way the blood in him moved too fast and too thin. The dead saw it too. They murmured around him like wind through wheat, and some of them—she understood this without knowing how—some of them had begun to sing *for* him, low and steady, a song for keeping a body together a little longer.

"Don't stop," Jonah said. His Common Tongue was rough but she had learned it, word by word, night by night, the way you learn to walk on ice. "Whatever you hear coming. Don't stop singing."

She didn't stop. But she heard it.

Hooves. Many. And under the hooves, iron, and under the iron, that grey silence she had learned to fear in the villages, the silence of people who had given their names away.

They came around the bend of the trail in a column of grey, and at their head rode a woman in white-and-ash, sword drawn, and Lior knew her. Not from the road—from somewhere deeper. From the ledger-thief's own buried life. The dead stirred and whispered: *that one, that one, she is ours and does not know it, she is a door that was nailed shut.*

The woman raised her sword. Forty blades rose behind her like a field of grey wheat.

Lior turned to face them. She did not stop singing. She put her body between the door and the swords, this small body, this last body, and she sang louder, and the seam of gold light widened to the width of a child.

"Hold," the woman said.

Her own soldiers looked at her.

"I said *hold*." Serane Voss walked her horse forward alone, ten paces, twenty, until she sat between the ranks of the Order and the child at the door, facing—Lior saw it clearly, the way you see the truth of a thing in firelight—facing both sides at once.

"You are the heir," Serane said. Her voice was flat, official, a voice reading from a page. But her sword-hand shook. "By the Doctrine of Ash, your line is ended. Your nation is concluded. By the Doctrine of Ash, you do not exist."

Lior looked at her. At the hollows under her eyes. At the ledger strapped to her saddle, glowing faintly through its leather like a coal wrapped in cloth. At the way the woman's gaze kept sliding—to the door, to the ember, to Lior's mouth shaping words in a language the woman's bones remembered even if her mind had been scoured of them.

Lior did not have many words of Common yet. She had *don't leave*, and *yes*, and *no*, and *wolf*, and *bread*, and *Jonah*, and a handful more, gathered like coins off a road. She reached for the ones she needed and found, to her surprise, that the dead handed them to her—one by one, pressed into her mouth like berries.

"You had a mother," Lior said.

The sword-point dipped.

"She sang to you. You gave her away to stay alive. It was not your fault. You were six."

"Silence." Serane's voice cracked down the middle. Behind her, Callow's horse shifted nervously. "That is—that knowledge is proscribed. Who told you—"

"He did." Lior pointed at Jonah. "He keeps us. All of us. Every voice you burned, he kept. He has your song. Would you like to hear it?"

For one heartbeat—one single heartbeat—the whole mountain held still. Even the wind. Even the ember, flaring high above them, threw light down like a held breath.

Then Callow shouted, "The Inquisitor is compromised! The heir has bewitched her! Brothers—Sisters—the Doctrine! *The Doctrine!*"

And the Order, which had erased a country because a country was a thing that could love you too much, lowered its lances and charged its own commander.

* * *

### SERANE

Later—if there was a later, if the world allowed her one—she would try to explain the moment of turning, and she would fail, because it was not a decision. Decisions happened in the mind, and her mind had been the Order's property for thirty years. This happened lower than the mind. It happened in the place where a six-year-old girl had once hidden in a root cellar while men in grey read her family's names aloud and struck them from a book, one by one, and her mother's voice had gone silent mid-lullaby, not because she stopped singing but because there was suddenly no one left who could remember she had ever—

The lances came down.

Serane wheeled her horse sideways into the charge.

She heard Callow scream her designation, her crime, the sentence. She heard it the way you hear rain on a roof: real, and very far away. What was close was the weight of the lance taking her horse through the neck, the terrible warmth of the animal falling, the cold of the snow catching her shoulder, and then she was up—when had she learned to get up that fast?—and her sword was out and moving, and she was fighting the Order.

Fighting *for* something, for the first time. It changed the mathematics entirely. She had fought for the Order for thirty years and it had never once required courage, because the Order was always larger than whatever it faced. Now she was smaller than what faced her, and she discovered that being small and standing anyway had a taste to it, copper and clean snow, and she thought, absurdly, in the middle of it: *so this is what they felt. Everyone we burned. This exact thing.*

Callow came at her with the fanatic's economy, no flourish, all intent. She parried once, twice, and on the third stroke let his blade slide past her hip and put hers through the gap beneath his hood, into the face he had given away, and felt him become, briefly and horribly, a person again—a boy, someone's son, wide-eyed—before he became nothing at all.

"I'm sorry," she told the nothing. "You were next. We were all next."

They pulled her down eventually. Of course they did. Forty against one is arithmetic, and the Order had always been good at arithmetic. She went down under grey bodies with her sword arm pinned and her ribs caving and the snow going red and warm beneath her, and through the tangle of limbs she could see the door, still open a child's width, gold light spilling out across the snow like honey.

The girl was screaming her name. Not *Voss*. The other one. The river-morning one. How did the child know it? How did anyone—

Because the dead keep everything, Serane thought. Because nothing is ever really erased. Because somewhere in the ledger of unmade things there is a page with my true name on it, and pages can be un-struck, and songs can be sung again, and it is not too late, it was never too late, that was always the lie, the whole rotten architecture of the Order rested on *too late*—

A boot found her jaw. The gold light smeared.

She rolled, got a knee under herself, got her feet. Blood filled her mouth. She spat it out and laughed, actually laughed, because her body had finally, after thirty years, done something she agreed with.

"Thackeray!" she roared, and saw the smuggler's head snap toward her over his useless spear. "The girl! Get her through! *Get her through!*"

Then she turned back to the grey tide, raised her sword in a salute she invented on the spot—for no one had ever taught her a salute for this—and went to meet it.

* * *

### LIOR

She fought the dead for her.

Not the Order—the dead. They swarmed around her, thousands strong, and they wanted her to stay and watch, because watching was all they had left, and she understood that, she grieved that, but the door was opening and Jonah was bleeding and the woman in ash was drowning under grey bodies ten paces away, and the dead's grief could not be allowed to cost the living anything more.

*I will remember,* she told them, in the old tongue, the tongue that was hers by blood and theirs by death. *I am the remembering. Come inside. Come home. I will carry you.*

And they broke like a wave, all at once, and poured toward the golden seam—farmers and weavers and soldiers and children, the whole erased country flowing home—and the door drank them, and widened, and widened.

"Lior!" Jonah caught her arm. His face was grey as the Order's cloaks. "We go. Now. Both of us—"

"No." She looked at him. She looked at the door. She understood, in the way the dead understood things, what the door required, and she began to cry without stopping her song. "One of us. You know. The dead told you. You've known since the shrine."

His jaw worked. "Lior—"

"*Go inside,*" she said, and pushed him—not hard, she could never push him hard—but the dead helped, gently, irresistibly, and Jonah Thackeray stumbled back from the threshold into the arms of a hundred transparent countrymen, and the door's light took his outline, and she turned away from him because if she watched him go she would follow, and she could not follow, not yet, not while the woman was still dying in the snow.

Serane Voss was on one knee. Three Order swords stood in her shadow like fence posts. Her white-and-ash cloak was red to the hem. She was still holding her sword. Of course she was.

Lior ran to her.

The nearest Order soldier turned, lance leveling at the small running shape of the last heir of Vaelthorne, and Serane Voss did the last thing the Ashen Order ever permitted her to do: she surged up from her knee and took the lance in her own chest rather than let it find the child.

They went down together. The soldier, confused, horrified, tried to withdraw the shaft; Serane gripped it and held it in, holding him too, holding him still, and looked up at Lior with eyes going dark and clear as winter water.

"Well?" she rasped. Blood on her teeth. "Is it—is it true? The door. Is there really—"

Lior knelt in the bloody snow and took the woman's face in both her small hands, and sang.

She sang the lullaby. The climbing, folding, bird-circling tune. The dead of Vaelthorne rose around them, every soul the country had ever held, and they sang it with her, and the ember overhead flared so bright the shadows fled the hollow entirely, and for the length of one verse the mountain was a kitchen with white plaster walls and a woman humming over a cradle.

Serane Voss wept. Thirty years of ash came off her in a single breath, and underneath was a six-year-old girl with a name that meant rivers and morning, and the girl smiled, and said something in Old Vaelthorni—*home*, probably, or *mother*, or simply *yes*—and died with her hand in a child's grip and her face turned toward an open door.

Above them, the ember-scale guttered.

Flared once, furious, magnificent.

And began, at last, to die.

The gold seam in the white door narrowed. The dead cried out—not in pain, but in urgency, a whole nation shouting one word down the mountain wind, and the word was not a word Lior knew but Jonah, half-in and half-out of the light, translated it with his carver's voice cracking:

"She says—" He coughed. "The first queen. She says the door holds only while the fire burns. Lior. *Lior.* Come *now*."

Lior looked at the closing seam. At the woman cooling in the snow. At the Order regrouping on the trail, leaderless, terrified, raising fresh lances against a child and a ghost-light.

She wiped her face. She picked up Serane's fallen sword, because the dead murmured that a doorkeeper should be armed, and she ran for the light.

Behind her, the last ember of the guardian dragon of Vaelthorne flared like a sunrise—

—and the door began to close.
