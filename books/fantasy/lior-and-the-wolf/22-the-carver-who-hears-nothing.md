## 22. The Carver Who Hears Nothing

The first winter after the door, Jonah Thackeray could not work.

He sat at his bench above the smuggler's road with a sparrow's bone in his vise and a knife in his hand, and the bone said nothing. It was just a bone. Small, yellowed, light as a breath. He turned it in the lamplight and waited for the voice—the little bird's last song, or the memory of a hedgerow, or whatever scrap of self the dead left clinging to their frames.

Nothing came.

He set down the knife. Picked it up again. Set it down.

Thirty years he had lived with the voices. Thirty years of carving while the dead murmured through his hands—sparrows and wolves and once, terribly, a child whose name he had never learned and had thought about every day since. The dead had been his company, his curse, his craft. They had told him where the roads ran when the maps went dark. They had told him about a girl at his door. They had begged him, in a hundred borrowed voices, to carry her home.

And now they were quiet, and the quiet was so large it had a shape, and the shape sat on his chest like a stone.

He tried to carve anyway. His hands remembered the motions even when his heart didn't. He roughed out the sparrow's body, the folded wings, the small blunt head—and it was just a sparrow, just bone, just an object that would sit on a shelf and hold nothing but its own shape.

He put it down unfinished and went to bed without supper, and lay listening to the wind come off the Thornfells, and for the first time in thirty years heard nothing underneath it.

* * *

Spring came late that year, the way spring does in the high country—reluctant, grudging, all mud and meltwater before it commits to green. Jonah had spent the winter the way he spent every winter: mending his roof, salting his meat, walking down to the valley twice a month to trade carvings for flour and lamp oil. The people in the valley knew him as the bone-carver who kept to himself. They did not know him as anything else. There was nothing else to know anymore.

On the first warm morning of the year—a morning when the snow on the south slope of his roof slid off all at once with a sound like a held breath let go—he opened his door and found something on the step.

It was small. A carved figure, no taller than his thumb, worked from antler: a wolf, sitting, its head tipped up as if listening to something far away. The craftsmanship stopped him where he stood. He knew good carving the way a blind man knows a voice. This was good beyond good—it was the kind of work you only got when the carver loved the material, when they'd listened to what the antler wanted to be instead of forcing it.

Around the base of the figure, cut fine as spider silk, ran a line of script.

Jonah crouched on his doorstep in the cold morning air and looked at it for a long time.

Old Vaelthorni. The language Lior had spoken at his door two winters ago, the words no living tongue knew, the syllables that had made the dead go silent. He couldn't read it. He had never learned more than the sound of it, the music of it, the way it had moved in her mouth like water finding its old channel.

But he knew what it meant anyway. Some things you don't need translation for.

Someone had climbed the smuggler's road in the thaw, past the places where the paths only existed because he remembered them, past the ford where the wolves had turned aside, up above the snowline to the door beneath the dying ember-shrine—and then back down again, carrying this. Or someone had sent it. Or something had carried it, the way birds carry seeds across borders no one drew.

He took the wolf inside and set it on his mantel, facing the room, and lit his lamp early even though the daylight was long enough now not to need it.

That night, for the first time since the door sealed, he slept without dreaming of white thresholds.

* * *

The second winter, he carved again.

Not well, at first. His hands were stiff and his eye was off and everything he made came out looking like it was waiting for a voice that wasn't coming. He filled a shelf with these—sparrows and hares and one ambitious fox, all of them silent, all of them just bones shaped into other shapes. He almost threw them in the stove in January. He didn't, because the stove was hot enough already, which is the kind of lie a man tells himself when the truth is that he can't bear to burn anything else he loves.

Then one night in deep February, working by lamplight on a wren's wingbone, he found himself doing something he hadn't done since Lior left. He stopped forcing the shape. He held the bone up to the light, turned it, felt along its grain with his thumb, and asked it—not aloud, not expecting an answer, just the old habit of courtesy—what it wanted to be.

The bone wanted to be a wren. So he made it a wren. And when it was done, it was the best thing he had carved in years, and it was completely, perfectly silent, and he sat back and looked at it and laughed—out loud, alone, in a workshop above the smuggler's road—for the first time since anyone could remember.

Because here was the thing the quiet had taken him two years to learn: silence wasn't absence. Silence was just the world, unaccompanied. The wind on the roof was the wind on the roof. The creak of the floorboards was the floorboards. The wren's wingbone was a wren's wingbone, whole and finished and asking nothing of him except that he look at it.

He had spent thirty years believing the dead were the only ones talking to him. It had taken losing them to hear everyone else.

The living, it turned out, talked too. They were just quieter about it. The miller's wife left bread on his fence post when she thought he wasn't looking. The boy from the valley—Tam, the one with the crooked smile—came up the mountain twice that summer pretending he'd lost his way, and stayed to watch Jonah carve, and asked questions with the particular hunger of a boy who wanted to learn and didn't know yet that wanting was allowed. Jonah caught himself explaining the grain of bone, the patience of the knife, the difference between forcing a shape and finding one. Caught himself enjoying it.

"You talk to the bones," Tam said once. "I've seen you. You ask them things."

"They don't answer," Jonah said.

"Doesn't matter, though. Does it." The boy said it like a fact, not a question, and went back to watching the knife, and Jonah understood that some things pass between people without any dead needing to carry them.

* * *

The offerings came every spring.

The second year: a small clay cup, fired hard, glazed green, holding three dried flowers he did not recognize—flowers that did not grow on any slope he knew. Beside them, a strip of birch bark with Old Vaelthorni cut into it in that fine silk-thread script.

The third year: a carved fish, river-stone gray, so lifelike he half expected it to swim. And a knot of woven grass in a pattern he recognized suddenly as the same pattern Lior used to make with her fingers when she was nervous, weaving and unweaving in the dark of the wagon, the year they crossed the Thornfells together.

The fourth year: a whistle. Bone, small, tuned to a note he could almost—but not quite—remember hearing.

He never saw who left them. He woke some mornings with the sense of a presence recently withdrawn, the way you wake knowing a bird was just on your windowsill. Once, half-asleep near dawn, he thought he heard footsteps going down the path, light and quick, and a voice saying something soft in a language older than kingdoms—and he lay still and did not get up, because some part of him understood that if he looked, whatever he saw might not be her, and he would rather keep the almost than lose it to the actual.

Or maybe that was cowardice. He'd made peace with a certain amount of cowardice. It was how he'd survived.

But every spring the offerings came, and every spring he learned a little more of the script—not from any teacher, but from comparison, from repetition, from the slow archaeology of a man studying messages from a country that had come back into the world. By the fifth year he could read the short ones. They were not letters, exactly. They were closer to the things the dead used to say to him: brief, warm, practical.

*The roads are open now.* One said that.

*The wolves remember you.* Said another.

*She is well. She asks if you are.*

That last one he read forty times. Then he took a knife and a flat piece of antler and, letter by careful letter, copying the shapes from five years of offerings, wrote his answer:

*I am. Tell her the carver hears nothing, and it is enough.*

He left it on the step where the offerings appeared. In the morning it was gone.

* * *

Seasons turned the way seasons do, faster as you age, each one stepping on the heels of the last. Jonah's hair went fully gray, then white at the temples, and his hands developed a tremor he compensated for with technique, the way old craftsmen do, turning weakness into style. Tam grew tall and started bringing his own blanks to carve, and then brought a girl he was courting, who turned out to have a better eye than either of them, and Jonah pretended to be annoyed and was not annoyed at all.

The workshop filled up. Not with voices—with things. Carvings of his own, silent and finished. Offerings from the spring country, arranged on shelves he built for them, a small museum of a nation's gratitude. And people, sometimes, coming up the smuggler's road not to buy but to sit, because word had gotten around the valley that the old carver's place was a good place to be quiet in, and there are fewer and fewer of those in any world.

One evening in autumn—Jonah's sixth autumn after the door, though he'd stopped counting precisely—a traveler came up the road at dusk. A woman, hooded, walking with the easy stride of someone who knew mountains. She stood in the doorway a moment, and the lamplight caught her face, and Jonah's heart did something complicated and painful and glad.

She was not Lior. She was perhaps fifty, weathered, with laugh lines and a scar on her jaw and eyes the color of the ember-shrine's last light. But around her neck, on a cord, hung a carved wolf—the twin of the one that had appeared on his doorstep six springs ago, worked in the same hand, the same love.

"I'm looking for the carver," she said, in Common Tongue with an accent underneath it, old and round, like stones worn smooth in a riverbed. "They said in the valley there's a man who carries Vaelthorni script and doesn't know what it says."

"I know what some of it says," Jonah said. "Now."

"Then you'll know why I've come." She unslung her pack and took out a bundle wrapped in wool, and unwrapped it on his bench with the care of a person handling something holy. Inside lay a piece of bone the length of his forearm—old, dense, dark—and a request written in that silk-fine script, which she translated for him aloud, her voice catching only a little:

"The Queen sends this. It belonged to the guardian dragon—or to the dragon's keeper; the histories argue. She asks: will the carver who opened the road make one thing from it? Whatever the bone wants to be. She says—" the woman smiled, and for a moment looked much younger, and much more tired, "—she says you'll know how to listen."

Jonah looked at the bone a long time. It was silent, of course. Everything was silent now, and he had made his peace with silence, had built a life beside it the way you build a house beside a river.

But he picked it up anyway. Held it to the light. Felt along its grain with his thumb, the old courtesy, the old question.

And the bone—silent, ordinary, dead bone—seemed to lean into his hands like a dog coming home.

"Yes," Jonah said. "Tell her yes."

The woman stayed the night, and in the morning she went back down the mountain toward the pass, toward the gorge, toward the hidden valley where a country that had been ash for two centuries was planting its fields again. Jonah watched her go until the road bent and took her.

Then he cleared his bench, trimmed his lamp, and set the dragon's bone in the vise.

Outside, the wind came off the Thornfells, carrying the smell of snow soon to come, and the first star stood over the peak where a white door slept under stone, keeping safe a kingdom full of voices he would never hear again—and did not need to. He picked up his knife. He asked the bone what it wanted to be.

And in the quiet of the workshop above the smuggler's road, Jonah Thackeray began to listen—not to the dead, and not to the past, but to the living weight in his own two hands, which had always, all along, been trying to tell him something worth staying for.
