## 2. A Date with Mara

The wren had been wrong about the ridge—or right in a way Lena couldn't yet read—because nothing came down from it that day, or the next, and by the third morning the valley had folded itself back into its usual shape: mist in the low fields, woodsmoke standing straight up from chimneys, the border stones gray and patient on every road out. Vell Hollow was good at forgetting. It had been forgetting for as long as anyone could remember, and it forgot this too, whatever this had been.

Lena tried to forget it as well. He was not very good at it.

He was better at small things. He fed the mill cat a scrap of smoked trout and listened to her complain about the new apprentice's heavy hands. He stood at the fence while old Bramble, the Nakamura farm's last mule, told him in her slow, creaky way that the grass on the north slope was sweeter this year, that she could taste the rain in it before it fell. He said good morning to the crows on the chapel roof and they said it back, flat and formal, the way crows did everything, like clerks acknowledging correspondence.

None of them mentioned the ridge. But when he asked—casually, the way you ask about weather—the crows went quiet in a manner that was not the same as having nothing to say.

So he put it away, the way he put everything away, in the deep drawer where he kept his gift: shut, dark, and heavier than it looked.

And then it was Friday, and he had a date.

* * *

He'd met Mara Vess eleven days ago, in the archive room of the Hollow's little stone library, which was less an archive than a closet with ambitions. Lena volunteered there two afternoons a week because the librarian, Mrs. Aldous, was going deaf and liked company she didn't have to shout at. Mara had arrived with a letter of introduction from some regional historical society, a leather satchel full of careful questions, and a smile that arrived half a second before the rest of his face, as though the smile were scouting ahead.

"I'm cataloguing border-valley records," Mara had explained, brushing dust off a shelf of ledgers with genuine tenderness. "Marriage rolls, land grants, tithe books. The boring bones of a place." His eyes were a light brown, almost amber, and they moved over the shelves the way Lena's moved over animals—noticing everything, committing most of it to memory. "Every valley keeps its skeleton in a room like this. You just have to know how to listen to bones."

Lena had laughed before he could stop himself—a real laugh, startled out of him—and Mara had turned that scouting smile on him fully then, and something in Lena's chest had done a thing he preferred not to examine.

They had talked for three hours. About marginalia in the old ledgers, about why valley folk wrote *the stones keep us* in so many property deeds instead of simply naming the boundary, about whether Mrs. Aldous's deafness was selective (it was; she heard gossip perfectly). And when Mara finally left, he paused at the door and said, "You talk like someone who listens for a living," and asked if he might buy Lena dinner sometime, and Lena—who had not been asked anything like that since he was twenty-two and it had ended badly and quietly, the way things ended in Vell Hollow—heard himself say yes before the cautious part of him could file an objection.

Now it was Friday, and the cautious part of him had spent the entire week drafting objections, and Lena had spent the entire week overruling them.

He wore the blue shirt. He told himself it was because it was clean.

* * *

The Gilded Perch was the only restaurant in Vell Hollow that set tables with cloth, and even its cloth was the color of dishwater, but candles made everything forgivable. Mara was already there when Lena arrived, seated by the window with two glasses of the valley's rough plum wine already poured, and when he saw Lena he stood up—actually stood, like this was a city and they were people who did such things—and Lena felt the evening tilt toward possibility.

"You came," Mara said. "I gave it fifty-fifty."

"Fifty-fifty?"

"Against me. I'm an archivist. We're trained to expect our sources to dry up." He sat back down, and his knee found Lena's under the table and stayed there, warm and unhurried, like it belonged in the conversation. "You look—" He stopped himself, which Lena appreciated, and started again more carefully. "The shirt suits you."

"The shirt is clean. That's all the compliment it's earned."

"Then we'll say the rest together." Mara raised his glass. "To clean shirts and boring bones."

Lena touched his glass to it and drank, and the wine was terrible and sweet, and outside the window the valley was going blue with dusk, and for one whole minute he let himself be only a man at dinner with a beautiful stranger, and it was the easiest minute he'd had in years.

Mara ordered for both of them without asking—he'd apparently memorized the menu, or the kitchen, or possibly Mrs. Aldous—and the food came in stages: brown bread still steaming, a stew of lamb and root vegetables that tasted like every winter Lena had ever survived, honey cakes at the end that Mara claimed not to want and then ate three of. Between courses they talked, and the talking was the thing. Mara asked questions the way no one in the valley ever had—not the polite circling questions of neighbors who had known you since birth and therefore needed to learn nothing, but sharp, delighted, sideways questions. What did Lena think the valley smelled like. Whether he believed places could hold grudges. Why he'd stayed.

That last one landed differently.

"Why does anyone stay?" Lena said, turning his spoon through the dregs of stew.

"Love, duty, fear, or failure to imagine elsewhere." Mara counted them off on long fingers. "Which is yours?"

"All four, rotating." He smiled to soften it. "It's a good valley. It keeps us."

"There's that phrase again." Mara leaned forward, candlelight pooling in the hollows of his face. "*Keeps.* Everyone here says it like a blessing. In my trade we'd call that a tell."

"A tell of what?"

"That the keeping isn't free." Mara said it lightly, teasingly, but his eyes weren't teasing, and for a moment the window behind him seemed darker than the night warranted, the glass holding the whole black valley like a held breath.

Lena's hand had gone cold around his spoon. He set it down.

"And what would you know about it?" he asked, aiming for playful and landing somewhere nearer to defensive.

"Only what dead men wrote down." Mara shrugged, easy again, and refilled their glasses. "Forgive me. Occupational hazard. I see a ledger everywhere I look. Tell me something true instead. Tell me—" and here he tilted his head, and the candle caught the amber of his eyes, "—something you've never told anyone."

* * *

Later, Lena would replay that moment many times, turning it over like a stone to see what lived underneath. Because the terrible thing, the thing that shamed him even as it warmed him, was how close he came. How ready he was. Eleven days. Eleven days of knowing this man, and the drawer in his chest—the deep one, the locked one—rattled on its runners like something wanting out.

Because Mara asked the way the beasts listened. That was the truth of it, the thing Lena couldn't explain to anyone who wasn't him. Most people asked questions to hear themselves arrange the world. But animals listened with their whole bodies, ears and hide and held breath, and when Mara asked *tell me something true*, he leaned in the way Bramble leaned in, the way the wren cocked her head on the fencepost, and Lena—starved for it, starved his whole quiet life—felt the lock turn.

"There are things in this valley," he began, and his voice came out lower than he meant it, almost a confession's register, "that talk."

Mara went very still. Not confused-still. Listening-still.

"Do they," he said softly.

"The animals. They—" Lena laughed, one short breath, the laugh you use to give yourself a door back out of a room. "This sounds mad. Forget it."

"Don't." Mara's hand crossed the table and covered his, warm, steady, and his voice dropped to something almost reverent. "Don't forget it. Please. Say the rest."

And Lena looked at him—at the candle between them, at the patient amber eyes, at the mouth curved in encouragement—and thought: *I could say it. I could say all of it. The mule's opinions on rain, the cat's grievances, the wren on my windowsill every morning telling me the shape of the day before I've lived it. Twenty-six years of conversation and I have never once been able to tell a single soul, and here is this man asking, actually asking—*

The window exploded with wings.

* * *

The wren hit the glass hard enough to rattle it, a small brown body flung against the pane like a thrown stone, and then she clung there—clinging to sheer glass, claws finding purchase on nothing, which was the first impossible thing—her feathers spiked, her throat working.

"Mara," Lena said, without knowing he said it. "Look at—"

The wren screamed.

Not a wren sound. Wrens scolded, churred, rattled out their bright machine-gun anger. This was none of that. This was a human scream pressed through a throat the size of a thumb, and it bent the whole room around it—the candle flames leaned, the wine trembled in the glasses, somewhere across the restaurant a fork stopped halfway to a mouth—and out of that scream came a word.

One word. Perfectly formed. Perfectly pronounced, with the exact vowel-weight of the valley dialect, the way his mother had said it, the way no living person said it anymore because it was not a name used aloud, it was a name given in the cradle and buried with the family, a true name, *his* name—

"*Aeliran.*"

The word hung in the restaurant like smoke.

The wren's black eye fixed on him through the glass. Her little chest heaved. And Lena understood, in the way you understand falling—that is, completely, and far too late to do anything about it—that she hadn't come to warn him this time. She had come to answer the question he'd been asking the crows all week. *Something old is circling the ridge,* she'd said. And now, screaming his buried name against a windowpane in front of a stranger, she was saying the rest:

*It found you.*

"Lena?" Mara's voice, gentle, careful. His hand was still on Lena's. His face was arranged in perfect, practiced concern—but beneath it, for half a heartbeat, something else moved, quick and bright and *satisfied*, like a fish turning under ice. Then it was gone, and there was only the archivist again, frightened for him, reaching across the table. "Lena, you've gone white. What did that bird—what did it say?"

"It didn't say anything." Lena was on his feet. He didn't remember standing. The chair was rocking behind him. "Birds don't talk. It's—it's a trick of the ear, people hear words in bird calls all the time—"

"Aeliran," Mara repeated.

The name in another man's mouth was unbearable. It went through Lena like a hook through soft flesh. He grabbed his coat without feeling his arms do it.

"I have to go."

"Wait—let me walk with you—"

"No." Too harsh. He softened it with effort, the way you gentle a spooked animal, except the spooked animal was himself. "No. Thank you. For dinner. I'm sorry—I don't—"

He got out. The night air hit him cold and clean and full of ordinary sounds—crickets, a dog two streets over complaining at nothing, the river muttering in its stony bed—and he walked fast, not running, running would be admitting, and the wren flew above him the whole way, a small dark flicker against the stars, silent now, escorting him home like a soldier seeing a body back from a field.

Behind him, at the Gilded Perch's window, he did not look back to see whether Mara watched him go.

He didn't need to. He could feel it, the way he felt everything in this valley that had no voice he was permitted to acknowledge: the weight of attention between his shoulder blades, patient and warm and entirely, terribly awake.

At his gate, the wren landed on the post at last and looked at him, feathers settling, small and exhausted.

"You knew him," Lena whispered. "Didn't you. You knew what he was before I did."

The wren said nothing. Wrens, after all, only screamed names once.

But inside the house, on the table where he'd left his satchel that morning, something waited that hadn't been there when he left: a slip of paper, folded once, laid square upon the wood. No footprints in the dust. No door disturbed. Only the paper, and on it, in a fine archivist's hand, five words:

*I know about the beasts.*

Lena stood in his own doorway, in his own valley, inside the borders where magic could not cross—and understood, with the wren silent on the post beside him and the ridge black against the stars, that the thing circling had never been outside at all.

It had bought him dinner.
