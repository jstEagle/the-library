## 3. The Book of Ash

Mara had lied to prettier people before. It was a skill he'd learned young and honed in worse places than this—a valley where the tea was chamomile and the danger was that someone might actually like you. He sat across from Lena Nakamura at the second dinner of their acquaintance, in the warm amber light of the Hollow Hearth's back room, and told himself the same thing he had told himself every night for eleven months: *You are not here to fall in love. You are here to read a man like a ledger.*

The trouble with ledgers, Mara knew better than most, was that they always balanced. Every entry had its cost.

Lena was laughing at something—Mara's own joke, some small absurdity about the innkeeper's cat having opinions about the soup—and the laugh did what it had been doing all evening: it made the lie heavier. Lena laughed the way people laugh when they have spent years being careful, when laughter is a door they rarely open and are surprised to find still on its hinges. His hands wrapped around his cup as if it were something that might be taken from him. He kept glancing toward the window, toward the dark ridge beyond it, with an expression Mara had catalogued three nights ago and could not stop thinking about since.

*He knows something is coming,* Mara thought. *He just doesn't know it's me.*

"You're doing it again," Lena said.

"Doing what?"

"That thing where you look at me like I'm a page you're trying to memorize." Lena's ears went faintly pink, but his eyes stayed steady. "I'm not that interesting. I feed chickens. I know which fence posts need replacing. That's the whole of my biography."

*If only you knew how wrong you are,* Mara thought, and smiled, and hated the smile for how easily it came. "I disagree," he said aloud. "I think you're the most interesting thing in this valley."

It wasn't even a lie. That was the worst part. The best lies were load-bearing truths with one stone pulled out.

Lena ducked his head, and Mara watched him wrestle with something—the same something he'd been wrestling with since the first night, some secret sitting behind his teeth like a coin he didn't know whether to spend. Mara leaned forward slightly, giving him room, giving him silence. This was the craft of it. You didn't pry secrets out of people. You built a space so quiet and so safe that the secret walked into it on its own.

"I should tell you something," Lena said.

Here it comes, Mara thought, and his heart performed a small treacherous leap. "I'm listening."

"It's going to sound—" Lena exhaled. "I talk to animals. Not—not like a farmer talks to animals. Like *talking*. They answer. In words. Actual words, in my head or sometimes out loud, I've never been able to tell which, and I know how it sounds, I've known how it sounds my whole life, which is why I've never told anyone, ever, and I don't know why I'm telling *you*, except that you look at things like they matter, and I thought maybe—" He stopped. Pressed his lips together. "Maybe you'd believe me."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, the innkeeper dropped a boot.

Mara had rehearsed this moment for eleven months. He had rehearsed it in taverns and waystations and once, memorably, in a ditch outside a town whose name he'd decided not to remember. In every rehearsal, Lena said those words and Mara responded with the carefully calibrated surprise of a kind man hearing an odd confession—enough wonder to flatter, enough skepticism to seem honest, steering gently toward the questions that mattered. *Since when? Which animals? Have you ever spoken to anything larger than a chicken?*

What he actually said was: "I believe you."

And then, because the truth has a momentum all its own once you crack the seal: "I came here to find you, Lena. And I think we're both in a great deal of danger."

* * *

The color left Lena's face in stages, like a tide going out. "Find me," he repeated. "What do you mean, *find* me? We met by accident. At the archive cart. You said you were passing through."

"I am an archivist," Mara said. "That part is true. But I wasn't passing through. Vell Hollow doesn't sit on the way to anywhere. That's rather the point of it." He held Lena's gaze because looking away now would be worse than any cruelty. "There's no gentle version of what I have to tell you. So I'm going to tell it plainly, and then I'm going to show you, and then you can decide what to do with me. Throw me out. Call the constable—though I suspect your valley doesn't have much use for one. Whatever you choose, I'll accept it."

"Start talking," Lena said quietly. All the warmth had gone somewhere deep, banked like coals. Mara noted, with the detached admiration of a professional, that fear hadn't broken him. It had *focused* him.

"The kingdom beyond your valley is called Corvane. You may never have heard much about it; your border stones keep more than magic out. They keep news out too, mostly. Corvane is ruled by King Aldric, who has sat the throne for nineteen years and has not aged a day in twelve of them. The court calls it a blessing. It is not a blessing. There is a thing called the Choir, and it wears the crown, and the king is what it wears it *on*."

Lena's hands had gone still around his cup. "The Choir."

"A chorus of bound voices. Taken from people—commoners, mostly, one tithe every generation—and bound into the service of the crown. They sing inside the king. They hold him together the way stitches hold a wound. And every year the stitches take a little more of him." Mara reached into the satchel at his feet. "Twelve years ago I was one of the people who helped maintain those stitches. I was an acolyte of the Choir. I was very good at it. And then I found a book, and the book unmade everything I believed, and I ran, and I have been running and reading and running again ever since. Looking for one name."

He set the book on the table between them.

It was a ledger, or had been once—now it was a burned thing, half its pages gone to ash and the rest scorched brown at every edge, as though it had been pulled from a fire that had wanted it very badly. The cover was black leather cracked like drought earth. Someone had scratched out the title long ago, but Mara knew what it had said. *Registrum Vatum, 1893.* The Register of Prophets. He had touched this book every day for six years and it had never once felt like an object. It felt like a hand on the back of his neck.

"What am I looking at?" Lena whispered.

"Open it. Carefully. To the ribbon."

Lena did. His fingers trembled, and Mara found himself wanting to steady them and making himself not move.

The page was dense with the cramped, slanting hand of a prophet named Ede Voss, whom history remembered as mad and whom Mara had come to regard as the only sane person of her century. Columns of names. Dates. Fragments of verse. And there, boxed in faded ink that seemed darker than the ink around it—as though the words had been written with something other than a pen—two lines:

*A speaker of beasts, blood of Nakamura, born under a forgetting sky.*
*Beside him stands the king who is not one, and the one who unmakes kings.*

Below it, in a different, shakier hand, someone had added a single word: *Corvane.*

Lena read it four times. Mara watched his lips move silently through it, watched him arrive at *Nakamura* and stop, watched him arrive at *unmakes kings* and go pale in a new and different way.

"This is—" Lena's voice cracked. "This is my family name. My grandmother's name. How does a book from 1893 know my—"

"Because Ede Voss saw you," Mara said. "Or saw *a* you. Prophecy is rarely tidy. She saw a beast-speaker of Nakamura blood, born under a forgetting sky—which is precisely what a valley that eats magic looks like from the outside, if you ask me. She wrote it down. And then someone tried very hard to make sure no one ever read it." He tapped the scorched edges. "This book survived three fires, two confiscations, and one attempt by me to burn it myself, which I abandoned when the flames wouldn't take. It wants to be read. I've stopped arguing with it."

"You were going to burn it?" Lena looked up sharply. "Why?"

"Because of the third line." Mara heard his own voice go flat, the way it did when he was holding something down. "Because prophecies like this get people killed, Lena. Because I spent twelve years inside an institution that fed on a prophecy of its own, and I know exactly what happens when powerful frightened men learn that a person might *unmake* them. They don't wait to find out if it's true. They find the person, and they make sure the prophecy dies with him." He paused. "I wanted to destroy the possibility. Instead I went looking for you myself. Make of that hypocrisy what you will."

The silence stretched. Outside, wind moved through the valley's pines with a long sighing sound, and Mara would have sworn—even knowing what he knew—that the trees sounded like they were holding their breath.

"So none of it was real," Lena said at last. Very quietly. Not angry yet. Anger takes a moment to arrive; hurt gets there first. "The archive cart. The way you looked at me. Dinner. *This.* None of it was real."

Mara had prepared answers for this too. Clever ones, deflecting ones, the professional's toolkit. What came out instead was: "The first hour was a lie. Everything after that got steadily less true. By the time you showed me where the wrens nest behind the mill, I had stopped being able to tell which parts of me were working." He swallowed. "That's not an excuse. It's just the honest shape of it. I came here to verify a name in a book. I found a man who feeds chickens and talks to wrens and laughs like he's apologizing for it, and I—" He stopped himself before the sentence could finish, because finishing it would be a weapon, and he had done enough damage with weapons tonight.

Lena stood up. Walked to the window. Stood there with his back turned, shoulders rigid, looking out at the dark ridge where, Mara knew, something old had been circling for days—something the valley's beasts had been whispering about, though Lena hadn't told him that yet either, which meant the wren had told *him*, which meant the clock was already running whether Mara liked it or not.

"My whole life," Lena said to the glass, "I thought the worst thing that could happen was that someone would find out about the animals. That they'd call me a liar, or worse, that they'd believe me and be afraid of me. I practiced being invisible for twenty-six years. Do you understand that? I got *good* at it." He turned. His eyes were wet and furious and, underneath both, unbearably tired. "And the first person I ever let see me walked in wearing a lie like a coat."

"Yes," Mara said. There was nothing else to say. He did not offer the defense that it had been necessary, though it had been; that others had hunted this book harder than he had, though they had; that the acolytes of the Choir had methods of finding beast-speakers that made his deception look like a kindness, though they did. Explanations were just wounds with footnotes.

"Why are you telling me now?" Lena asked. "Tonight. Why not keep lying until you'd gotten whatever you needed?"

"Because something crossed the ridge two nights ago," Mara said. "And because the window behind you has had a wren on the sill for the last four minutes, and it hasn't sung once, and birds that aren't singing are birds that are afraid." He nodded past Lena's shoulder. "And because I promised myself that if I ever started caring more about the man than the mission, I'd tell him the truth while there was still time for it to matter. Tonight is that night. Possibly the last one."

Lena turned. The wren sat on the sill, feathers tight, one bright eye fixed on the room—and Mara watched Lena's face change as he listened to something no one else could hear. Watched him go very still.

"What is it saying?" Mara asked.

Lena's voice came out thin. "It says the thing circling the ridge isn't circling anymore. It says it stopped at the north border stones last night." A pause. "It says the stones *hurt it*, but it didn't leave. It's waiting. And it asked—" His hand rose unconsciously to his throat. "It asked if I was the one the book was written about. Mara. *How does a wren know about the book?*"

Mara felt the floor tilt beneath a certainty he'd been carrying for six years. "Because the Choir's hunters don't only hunt people," he said slowly. "They listen through anything with a voice. And if the beasts of this valley are starting to repeat its questions—" He stood, gathered the book, wrapped it in oilcloth with hands that were almost steady. "Then the forgetting sky over Vell Hollow is thinner than anyone thought. And we are out of time."

Lena stared at him across the ruined dinner, the cold tea, the open book with its impossible page. Twenty-six years of invisibility stood in his posture like a wall. Mara watched him weigh it—watched him measure the cost of the door against the cost of staying behind it.

"If I walk out of this valley with you," Lena said, "I don't come back the same. Do you understand? The stones won't let me back in as the man who left."

"No," Mara said. "They won't."

Lena closed his eyes. When he opened them, the fury was still there, banked and burning low—but beside it now, something new. Something that looked, terrifyingly, like resolve.

"Then pack your bag," he said. "And Mara—if you lie to me one more time, I'll know. The birds will tell me."

Outside, far off at the northern edge of the valley, something old pressed itself against the border stones and began, very softly, to hum.
