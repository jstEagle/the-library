## 5. The Road of Small Cruelties

The border stones were behind them by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge, and Lena still had not stopped shaking.

He had thought leaving Vell Hollow would feel like stepping out of a warm house into snow. Instead it was the opposite. The world beyond the valley *roared*. Every hedgerow hummed with roots talking to each other in the dark soil; every crow on every fence post carried three conversations at once and none of them quiet; the wind itself seemed to have opinions, long and slow and ancient, dragging its syllables across the wheat like a hand through hair. For twenty-two years he had lived in a room with the windows painted shut, and now someone had thrown open every shutter in creation.

"You're white as milk," Mara said, reining his horse closer. He rode well—too well, Lena thought, for an archivist—with his hood back and his face turned into the morning light as though he'd been starved of it.

"I can hear everything." Lena's voice came out thin. "The worms, Mara. I can hear the *worms*."

"Then you'd better learn to close a door or two," Mara said, not unkindly. "Or they'll hear you right back."

Lena didn't ask what he meant by that. He had learned, in the two days since the Book of Ash, that Mara answered questions only when he felt like it, and that pressing him made him fold up like a letter. So Lena rode, and listened, and practiced—Mara's word, *practiced*, as if this were scales on a piano—shutting out the great slow voices of the land one at a time until he could bear the noise of the road.

The road itself surprised him. In Vell Hollow, roads were ruts between farms, known by their potholes. This was a true highway, graded and graveled, running straight as a ruled line toward the capital, toward Corvane, toward the king who was not one. Milestones marked the way with a crowned bell stamped deep in the stone. At every milestone, Lena noticed, the grass grew brown in a perfect circle, as if something had been poured there that nothing wanted to drink.

They passed the first village at midday.

It was called Brant's Ford, according to the leaning signpost, and it should have been loud. Villages were loud—that much even Lena knew from books. Dogs, geese, chickens, children chasing both. But as they clattered over the little stone bridge, Lena heard nothing. Not silence exactly. Silence would have been a relief. This was worse: a hush with weight behind it, like a room full of people holding their breath.

The animals stood in the yards and along the fences, and they did not look at anything.

A dog sat by a doorway, a handsome rust-colored thing, and when their horses passed close enough to touch it, the dog did not lift its head. Its eyes were open. Its eyes were *open*, and there was nothing behind them but a kind of patient gray fog, like a window left unwashed for years. A goose stood on one leg in a dooryard pond and did not honk. Chickens scratched in the dust of the lane with mechanical precision, beaks down, never once glancing at the sky where a hawk circled—and the hawk, Lena realized with a lurch, was circling without hunger, without intent, flying its pattern like a needle stuck in a groove.

Every animal bore a brand. Small, neat, high on the shoulder or the flank: a crowned bell, seared into living hide.

"What did they do to them?" Lena whispered. His gift had gone strange here—not dead, as it was dead in the valley, but muffled, reaching out and touching nothing, like fingers pressed against glass. The animals were *there*. He could feel the dim warmth of them, the way you can feel a fire through a wall. But no voice came back.

"The tithe," Mara said quietly. He had stopped smiling somewhere on the bridge. "I told you the Choir takes names. I didn't tell you where it prefers to take them from. Names freely given are stronger—it needs fewer of them. And who gives more freely than a farmer who's told his beasts will 'serve the crown'?" He nodded at the branded dog. "That's not a dog anymore. That's a vessel with a dog's shape. It hears the Choir. It hums along. It doesn't know how to want anything else."

Lena looked at the rust-colored dog sitting by the door, waiting for a master who had probably died years ago, and something in his chest went tight and hot.

"It's still in there," he said. "Whatever they took—the voice, the wanting—it's still in there. You don't burn a name out of something. You just bury it."

"Lena." Mara said his name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "We're two travelers on a public road. There is nothing we can do for a village full of husks, and if you try, the wrong person will see, and then there will be nothing anyone can do for us."

But Lena was already looking down the lane, past the well, to where a boy of perhaps ten stood holding a rope. The rope led to a mule.

* * *

The mule was the only animal in Brant's Ford that looked at them.

It stood in the yard of the last house before the far fields, gray as old pewter, ears swiveled forward, dark eyes fixed on Lena with an attention so fierce and human that his breath caught. Around its neck hung a wooden placard on a cord, letters burned into it: TITHE — CORVANE BELL-DAY. It was branded too, the crowned bell raised in scar tissue on its shoulder—but the brand was older than the others, half-faded, and the eyes above it were not fogged. They were desperate.

And beneath the muffling hush of the village, faint as a voice through floorboards, Lena heard it.

*Hello. Hello. Hello. Is somebody there? Hello.*

"Oh," Lena said softly. "Oh, you poor thing."

The boy tugged the rope. "She's going to Corvane on Bell-Day," he recited, in the flat sing-song of a lesson memorized. "The tithe is owed. The tithe is honored. The king keeps our voices safe." His eyes, when they met Lena's, had the same gray patience as the dog's. That frightened Lena more than anything else he had seen. The Choir did not only take beasts.

"How long has she been yours?" Mara asked, dismounting, all easy charm again, coin already appearing between his fingers. Lena knew that trick now—Mara bought information the way other men bought bread, constantly, casually, and always with a second purpose underneath.

"She isn't ours," the boy said. "Nobody's. She come walking up the ford road four years back, after the last tithe cart went through. She won't leave. She won't work neither, not proper. She just stands." He shrugged, a small terrible shrug, the shrug of a child who had never known a world where things were otherwise. "Bell-Day comes, she goes on the cart. That's the law."

Four years. Lena slid from his saddle and approached slowly, palms open, the way he had approached shy creatures all his life in the valley—except that here, for the first time, he spoke aloud, because there was no need to hide, and because he wanted the boy to hear what a voice sounded like when it was used kindly.

"Hello," he said. "What's your name?"

The mule's ears trembled. The desperate eyes welled.

*I had one,* came the voice, faint and cracked, like a song sung through a closed door. *I had one and it was mine and they took it off me and put it in the crown and I can't remember it, I can't remember it, I've been trying four years to remember it—*

"They take the name first," Lena breathed. "Before the branding. The brand is just—" He looked up at Mara, and his hands had begun to shake again, but differently now. "The brand is just the receipt."

"Lena, there are soldiers at the tollhouse two miles east. I counted six. I count everything; it's a habit I'm not going to apologize for. Whatever you are about to do—"

"Her name is still *hers*," Lena said. "You said it yourself. Names freely given are strong. Then they're taken against her will, which means—" he groped for it, reaching back to the Book of Ash, to the marginalia in Mara's cramped hand, "—which means it was never really given. It's a theft, not a gift. And a theft can be *refused*."

"That is not in any book I own."

"Then write it down after."

He knelt in the dust of the yard, in front of the mule, heedless of his good coat, and laid his forehead against the gray velvet of her nose. The village hush pressed down around them. Somewhere behind him he heard Mara swear under his breath in a language Lena didn't recognize, and then, instead of pulling him away, Mara turned his horse sideways across the lane—a wall between them and the road east. Watching the horizon. Buying time.

"Listen to me," Lena whispered. "I can't give your name back. I don't know it. Nobody knows it now. But I can do something else. I can call you by a new one, and you can take it, freely, and a freely given name is strong enough to break a chain. That's what the book says. That's what the book says, and I am choosing to believe it."

*Anything,* said the mule. *Please. Anything. I want to want things again.*

Lena closed his eyes. He reached into himself, into the place where the valley had kept his gift folded up small for twenty-two years, and he let it open all the way for the first time outside the border stones. The roar of the world rushed in—worms and wind and wheat and the hawk overhead—and he gathered all of it, every thread, and wove it into a single word, and spoke it into the space between himself and the mule:

"*Bramble.*"

Not a grand name. A hedge-name, a wayside name, the kind of name that grows anywhere and belongs nowhere and therefore everywhere. He felt it leave him like a coal from a fire, felt it settle into the mule's chest, felt the thing the Choir had buried—four years buried, under gray fog and patient standing and the endless hum of the crown—push upward against it like a green shoot against a stone.

For a moment, nothing.

Then the mule shuddered from ears to tail, threw back her head, and brayed.

It was the ugliest sound Lena had ever heard in his life and he would remember it until he died. It cracked in the middle. It was ragged and rusty and far too loud for the little yard, and it scattered the fog-eyed chickens in a flapping panic, and it went on and on, four years of silence torn out of one gray throat, and when it finally broke off, the mule stood trembling, and her eyes—her eyes were *wet*, and looking at him, and awake.

*My name,* she said, wonderingly, in a voice like a gate swinging open. *My name is Bramble. My name is Bramble and I am MINE.*

"Bramble," Lena repeated aloud, laughing, crying, both at once. "Yes. That's you. That's yours forever."

The boy dropped the rope.

He stared at the mule—at Bramble—as if seeing her for the first time, which, Lena understood with a sick twist of grief, he was. Four years of a standing shadow in the yard, and now the shadow had turned its head and *looked* at him, and the boy's memorized recitation crumbled off his face like wet plaster.

"She talked," he whispered. "Mister. Mister, she *talked*."

"No," Lena said gently. "She answered. There's a difference. Remember that. When they tell you the tithe is honored—remember that everything they take can answer, someday, if someone calls it right."

He was still kneeling, still holding Bramble's velvet nose against his brow, when the hoofbeats came.

Six riders, coming fast from the east, blue-cloaked, crowned bells stitched on their shoulders in silver thread. Mara had seen them a full minute ago—of course he had—and now he swung down from his horse and stepped smoothly into the lane, arms loose, smile fixed, the very portrait of a traveler with nothing to hide. Lena saw him slide the Book of Ash deeper into his coat with one small motion of his elbow, a conjurer's move, invisible unless you knew to look.

The soldiers drew up in a half-moon around the yard. Their leader was a broad woman with a sunburned face and eyes that moved over Lena, Mara, the boy, the mule, cataloguing everything and giving nothing back. On her gauntlet, the crowned bell gleamed.

"Travelers," she said. Her voice was pleasant. Her voice was *too* pleasant, smoothed of all its rough edges, and Lena's skin crawled, because he recognized the smoothing. He had heard it in the hawk's empty circles and the dog's gray patience. These men and women were not branded. But something of the Choir had been poured into them anyway, diluted, like wine cut with water, enough to make them obedient. Enough to make them *hum along*.

"Travelers," she said again, "we heard singing."

Lena's blood went cold.

"There's been no singing here, sergeant," Mara said easily. "Unless you count my companion's snoring. We stopped to water the horses and admire the mule. Fine animal. Unusual, for these parts, that it's unclaimed."

"The law says the tithe is honored," the sergeant recited, and the five riders behind her murmured it with her, a half-beat late, a chorus of six, and Lena felt Bramble go rigid under his hand. "The tithe is owed. The king keeps our voices safe." Her gaze settled on the mule, on the faded brand, on the placard reading CORVANE BELL-DAY, and then—slowly, terribly—on Lena, kneeling in the dust with his hand on the animal's neck.

"Strange," the sergeant said, in that smooth, sanded voice. "This one's brand is cold. Cold brands mean bound beasts. Bound beasts don't stand so close to strangers. Bound beasts don't—" she tilted her head, listening to something none of them could hear, or something only some of them could, "—*hum off the note*."

The silence stretched. Down the lane, the fog-eyed dog had lifted its head for the first time in years, and was looking at Lena, and waiting.

"We'll be moving on," Mara said brightly, and put his body squarely between the sergeant and the mule, and Lena saw that his hand, hidden in the folds of his coat, was gripping something that was very definitely not a coin.

The sergeant smiled. It did not reach anywhere near her eyes.

"Yes," she said. "I think you will. And I think, travelers, that you'll be moving on *ahead of us*—all the way to Corvane, where the Bell-Day court will want to hear about a stranger who kneels in the dirt and makes cold brands sing. Ride with us. Consider it an honor."

Behind her, at the edge of the yard, the boy picked up the fallen rope—and, with a child's sudden ferocity that no one had thought to guard against, hurled it into the well.

Bramble stepped free of it, walked to Lena's side, and stood there, head high, humming no note at all.

The sergeant watched her do it. And wrote something down.
