## 6. The City of Bells

Mara felt Corvane before he saw it.

It began as a pressure behind his sternum, the way a storm announces itself in old joints and healing bones. He had been riding half a day since the last village, since the branded mule and the soldiers' slow-turning heads, and Lena had not spoken for miles. Now, as the road crested the last hill and the capital unrolled below them—grey stone and slate roofs and the glint of the river Vane cutting through it like a drawn wire—that pressure became something else. A sound.

Or rather, the memory of a sound. The ghost of one. A hum, low and patient, sitting beneath the world like water beneath ice.

"Stop," Mara said, and reined in.

Lena pulled up beside him, his horse blowing softly. He looked tired. Everyone looked tired on this road; the villages they'd passed had a way of hollowing travelers out, all those mute-eyed animals standing in their paddocks like furniture that dreamed. But Lena's tiredness was different. It was the tiredness of a man who had spent three days listening to things he wished he couldn't hear.

"You feel it too," Mara said. It wasn't a question.

"I don't *feel* anything." Lena's voice was flat, careful. "That's what frightens me. Since we crossed the stones I've been drowning in voices—every bird, every fox, every worm in every field, if worms have voices—and now, here—" he pressed a hand flat against his chest, as though checking that his heart still beat alone, "—it's quiet. Too quiet. Like the whole city is holding its breath."

*Not holding its breath,* Mara thought. *Humming.*

He didn't say it. Not yet. There were things that could not be said on an open road with the spires of Corvane rising against the afternoon sky, things that needed walls and darkness and the particular courage that only came from looking someone in the eye when you said them. He had been carrying those words since the Book of Ash, since the night by lamplight when he'd shown Lena the prophecy ledger and watched betrayal and wonder fight for possession of the same face. He would carry them a little longer. One more hour. One more lie of omission, which was not quite a lie, which was only mercy wearing mercy's clothes.

"Come on," he said instead. "The gates close at dusk."

---

Corvane was a city built in rings, like the ripples around a dropped stone, and at its center stood the palace on its crag of older rock, pale and many-towered, crowned—Mara's stomach turned at the phrase—with the Crown of Continuance. From almost anywhere in the city you could see it. That was the point. The Choir wanted to be seen. Wanted to be *known*, in the way a tumor wants blood flow.

The bells began as they passed through the outer gate.

They came from everywhere at once—from the great bell towers of the Ring districts, from the chapel steeples tucked between merchant houses, from the small bronze handbells that beggars swung outside tavern doors. Hourly, always hourly, and never quite together: a cascade of ringing that started high in the city and rolled down through the rings like a wave, each bell catching the note of the last until the whole capital shuddered with sound.

Lena flinched so hard he nearly lost his stirrup. His hands went white on the reins.

"What is *that*?"

"Bells," Mara said. "On the hour. They've rung hourly for as long as anyone remembers. It's supposed to be devotion. Piety. The city keeping time with heaven."

"And is it?"

Mara guided his horse down the broad avenue, past stalls shuttering up for evening, past a fountain where children played under the eyes of bored mothers. Everything ordinary. Everything painted over.

"No," he said quietly. "Listen underneath."

Lena went still. Mara watched him do it—the small unconscious tilt of the head, the way his breathing slowed, the look of a man leaning into a wind only he could feel. Three days beyond the border stones, and already the boy listened like that naturally, helplessly, the way other people blinked.

Then Lena's face changed. All the color left it at once.

"There's something *under* the bells." His voice came out thin. "Under everything. It's—it's not a voice, it's hundreds of voices, thousands, all singing the same—"

"Don't finish that sentence out loud." Mara kept his tone light, conversational, a man discussing the weather. He nodded toward a knot of city watchmen loitering by a pie stall, their spears casual, their eyes not. "In Corvane, you learn very quickly which sentences can be finished and which cannot."

Lena shut his mouth. But his eyes stayed wide, and Mara saw the question forming in them, the question that had been forming since the Book of Ash, growing heavier with every mile: *What do you know? What aren't you telling me?*

Soon, Mara promised himself. Tonight. Tonight, over bad wine in a safehouse he hadn't used in six years, he would open the last locked door and let Lena see everything inside it, even the rooms he himself had never been able to stop visiting.

He just had to get them there first.

* * *

The safehouse was in the Third Ring, above a dyer's shop whose vats stained the alley air with the smell of mordant and wet wool. Mara had chosen it years ago precisely because no one chose streets like this willingly—the dye-works drove off the curious, and the Choir's tithe-collectors preferred cleaner neighborhoods, places where their grey livery didn't clash with the washing lines. He paid the dyer's widow three months' rent in advance, told her they were scholars, and carried their bags up four flights of stairs that groaned like a ship in weather.

The room at the top was small and square, with one window facing west, toward the palace. From it, if you leaned out, you could see the Crown of Continuance catch the last of the sun—a circlet of white gold set with stones the color of frozen honey, sitting on nothing, displayed on a velvet stand in the Great Hall because the king no longer wore it in public. Because the king no longer wore it at all.

Because the crown wore the king.

Mara set the bags down and made himself turn away from the window. Lena stood in the middle of the room, turning slowly, taking in the bare walls, the cold hearth, the two pallets, the utter absence of any life having ever been lived here.

"You've stayed here before," Lena said.

"Yes."

"When?"

"Six years ago." Mara busied himself with the hearth, laying kindling with more care than kindling required. "The last time I was in Corvane."

"Mara." Lena's voice was gentle in a way that was worse than anger. "You told me you were an archivist. You told me you found the Book of Ash in a monastery library, cataloguing donations. You told me—" he stopped, and started again, quieter. "You told me a lot of things on the road. And I believed them, because I wanted to believe them, because you were the first person in my whole life who looked at me like I wasn't a secret. So I'm going to ask you once, plainly, and I'd like you to be plain back. Who are you?"

The kindling caught. Mara watched the flame take hold of the birch bark, orange and honest, and understood that the hour had come whether he was ready or not. Perhaps that was the only way such hours ever arrived.

"All right," he said. "Sit down. This takes longer than the fire will."

Lena sat on the edge of a pallet, hands folded, waiting. He had a way of waiting that made lying impossible—not demanding, just *present*, the full weight of his attention given freely, the same quality Mara had glimpsed in him with the animals, that terrible open listening. No wonder the beasts trusted him. No wonder the prophecy had found him. He was a door that could not be closed once opened.

Mara sat across from him and took a breath that hurt on the way in.

"My name isn't just Mara," he said. "It's Mara Vessin. My family held a minor seat in the Second Ring for four generations—cloth merchants, then clerks, then, in my grandfather's time, something better. My father was a tithe-clerk in the Office of Names. Do you know what that is?"

Lena shook his head slowly.

"It's the office that keeps the rolls. Every generation, the Choir requires names—commoners' names, taken by lot or by debt or by quiet arrangement, always legal, always documented. The families are compensated. Sometimes generously. The person whose name is taken..." Mara turned his hands palm-up, empty. "Nothing happens to the body. They go home. They live out their lives. But something is missing afterward. Their own mothers sometimes struggle to recall their faces. Their gravestones, later, are found blank. A name, in Corvane, is not decoration. It is load-bearing. Take it away and the whole structure of a person sags."

"That's monstrous," Lena whispered.

"It's efficient," Mara corrected bitterly. "That was the word my father used. *Efficient.* He was proud of his work. He kept the ledgers beautiful—fair hand, ruled margins, not a blot on any page. And when I turned fourteen, he did what tithe-clerks' sons of promise did. He enrolled me as an acolyte of the Choir."

The fire popped. Neither of them moved.

"I want you to understand what that means," Mara went on, and his voice had gone somewhere distant and level, the voice of a man reading from a document about his own execution. "Acolytes don't sing. We're not bound—we're the opposite. We're the *binders*. We maintain the machinery. We copy the rolls, we prepare the vessels, we keep the vigil in the undercroft where the... where the root of it lives. We wear grey. We learn the harmonies well enough to recognize them but never to join them. And we are taught, from the first day, the founding doctrine. Would you like to hear the founding doctrine? It's genuinely beautiful. That's the horror of it. It's *beautiful*."

"Mara—"

"A hundred and forty years ago, King Osric lay dying, and the realm would have shattered—three claimants, foreign armies circling, a kingdom held together by one man's voice and one man's voice alone. So his court sorcerers did a merciful thing. They took his voice before death could, and bound it into the crown, so that Osric could keep speaking to his people after his heart stopped. One voice. One act of love, at the scale of a nation." Mara smiled, and hated his own mouth for doing it. "*The king endures. The realm endures.* That's the doctrine. And it worked. For a while. But a bound voice is a hungry thing, and hunger learns. Somewhere in the decades after Osric, the single voice in the crown learned to reach outward. To take. To add voices to itself, because a chorus is stronger than a soloist, and a thousand voices stronger than a chorus. It stopped being a mercy and became an appetite. And the appetite learned to dress itself in liturgy."

Lena had gone very pale. "And you served it."

"For four years." Mara met his eyes and refused, deliberately, to look away. If he owed Lena anything—and he owed him everything—he owed him this: the sight of shame fully inhabited, not flinched from. "I copied rolls. I know exactly how many names my own neat hand sent into that crown. I counted them once, on the worst night of my life, and then I stopped counting because the number was climbing past two hundred and I was twenty-two years old. And then one night, cataloguing in the deep archive—I was good at archives even then, it's why my cover story has always been true-shaped—I found a ledger from 1893. A prophecy book. Sealed, misfiled, forgotten. And in it, a verse about a beast-speaker of Nakamura blood who walks beside the king who is not one, and unmakes him."

"The king who is not one," Lena repeated. "I remember."

"'Not one,'" Mara said. "Everyone reads that as *not a true king*. A pretender. A usurper. But I had stood in the undercroft. I had heard the thing in the crown. And I read that line and went cold all over, because I knew what it meant. *Not one.* Many. The king who is not one king but a hundred voices wearing one face. The prophecy wasn't about a false claimant, Lena. It was describing Aldric exactly as he is right now."

"So you took the book."

"So I took the book." He laughed, short and joyless. "Do you know how long I'd been looking for a way out? Years. I'd told myself the doctrine was true, that the Choir was mercy grown strange but still fundamentally kind at the root, because the alternative was unbearable. And then a dead archivist from 1893 handed me a sentence that said: *this ends, and you could be there when it does.* I walked out of the undercroft vigil that night, went home, took the book, took what coin I could carry, and ran. Six years. I've been running six years, working as an archivist in three countries, following the thin threads of that prophecy, looking for a Nakamura. And then I heard rumors of a valley where magic dies at the borders, and a family that had gone behind those stones a hundred years ago and never come out, and I thought: *there*. That's where you hide a beast-speaker. In the one place his gift can't echo."

Lena was silent for a long moment. The firelight moved on his face.

"You came to Vell Hollow for me," he said at last. "The dinner. The—the date. None of that was real."

Mara winced. "The book was real. The search was real. The lie was real." He paused, and made himself say the rest, because trust was a currency you could only spend honestly. "But I'll tell you what I've never told anyone. By the second dinner, I had confirmed enough. I could have taken what I needed and gone. And I stayed anyway. I asked to see you again knowing exactly what I was walking you toward. Knowing the prophecy might cost you everything. And I told myself it was duty, it was the mission, it was six years of work—but that wasn't all of it, Lena. Some of it was just that I liked you. I liked your terrible jokes and the way you talk to horses like they're colleagues and the fact that you blush when you're angry. I'm not asking that to excuse anything. I'm telling you because you deserve the whole ugly inventory, and because—" his voice caught, and he let it, "—because I am so tired of being the acolyte who runs. I want to be the man who helps fix it. Even if fixing it costs what the book says it might."

Silence. The fire settled. Somewhere far below, in the streets of the Third Ring, the evening bells began—not the full hourly peal, but a single chapel bell, ringing some small local grief or gladness, and beneath it, faint as a pulse through a wall, the hum.

Lena looked at him for a long, long time. Then he said, "The child."

Mara blinked. "What?"

"In the Choir. When we crossed the stones, in the chorus that wasn't the beasts'—there was one voice that didn't fit. Small. High. It sounded—" his jaw tightened, "—it sounded scared. Is that possible? Can a voice in the Choir still be *in there*? Aware?"

Mara felt the floor tilt slightly beneath everything he'd believed for six years. "The doctrine says the binding consumes. That the name dissolves into the harmony completely. That's what makes it bearable, for the binders—you're not enslaving people, you're... folding them into something greater." His mouth was dry. "If a voice can still be frightened, then the doctrine is a lie we tell ourselves so we can sleep. And if it's a lie, then everyone in those rolls—all two hundred of my names—is still in there. Still awake."

"Still waiting," Lena said quietly, "for someone who can hear them."

Outside, the last light died off the Crown of Continuance, and the hum beneath the city swelled, just slightly, as if it had heard.

And Mara—who had fled once, who had hidden for six years behind borrowed professions and half-truths—understood that the running was over. Tomorrow they would need a way into the palace, and there was exactly one person alive who knew the acolytes' door, the vigils, the rhythms of the guard. Which meant tomorrow he would walk back through the grey doors he'd escaped, wearing his old shame like a coat that still fit.

"One more thing you should know," he said, as Lena banked the fire. "The Queen. Isolde. The court calls her distant, cold, decorative. The acolytes called her something else, in whispers, near the end of my time there. They called her *the listener at the keyhole*. Someone has been sabotaging the tithes for years—misfiled rolls, vessels that fall ill before binding, lots rigged to spare. The Choir suspects. It hasn't proven. If we need an ally inside those walls..."

Lena looked up. "You think it's her."

"I think," Mara said, "that tomorrow we find out. And I think we should sleep, because tomorrow I go home to the place I swore I'd never see again—and I intend to burn it down from the inside."

Beneath the city, patient as tide, the Choir hummed on.
