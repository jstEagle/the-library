## 7. An Audience with the Hollow King

The palace of Corvane did not look like a place where a king was being eaten. It looked like a place where a king had been very carefully wrapped.

Lena stood in the antechamber of the Sun Court and tried not to stare at the ceiling, which was painted with a sky so convincing that his neck ached from the pretense of looking up at it. Clouds drifted nowhere. A sun with a human face smiled down with an expression he could only call patient. Everything in Corvane was like that—beautiful, and watching.

"Stop fidgeting," Mara murmured beside him. He wore his archivist's gray like armor, the ledger of 1893 hidden somewhere in the lining of his coat, or so Lena hoped. "You're a scholar's assistant. Assistants are bored. Bored is safe."

"I am bored," Lena said. "Bored and terrified. They can coexist."

"They usually do."

A herald in silver-and-white called their names—not their real names, the ones Mara had purchased along with the forged letters of introduction in a town three days back—and the great doors opened, and Lena walked into the presence of King Aldric of Vellmark for the first time.

He had imagined, on the road, all the ways a hollow king might look. He had imagined a husk, a puppet slumped on its throne with strings cut, eyes like smoked glass. He had imagined something monstrous, something with too many teeth or none at all. What he had not imagined was warmth.

King Aldric rose to greet them himself.

That was the first wrongness, though Lena didn't know it yet. Kings did not rise. Kings let you come to them, let the distance do its work. But Aldric came down two steps from the dais with his hand already extended, a man of perhaps fifty with silver threading his temples and laugh lines carved deep around eyes that were—there was no other word—a warm brown. He looked tired in the way good men look tired, the way farmers look at harvest's end. He looked kind.

"Master Corvin," he said to Mara, using the false name easily, "and young Master Ilya. You honor a quiet court. Archivists are the historians of the future; I have always thought we should treat them better than we treat our generals." His smile crinkled genuinely. "Generals only tell us what we did. Archivists tell us what we were."

"Your Majesty honors us," Mara said, bowing with exactly the right depth. Lena copied him a half-beat late, feeling his ears burn.

"The honor is mine. It has been—" the king paused, and something flickered across his face like a cloud crossing that painted ceiling, "—some time since I received visitors who were not petitioners or princes. Sit. Both of you. Wren, wine for our guests."

A servant glided forward. And there—the second wrongness, small as a hairline crack in porcelain—the king had called the servant *Wren*.

Lena's whole body went cold. In Vell Hollow, a wren had screamed his true name through a window screen, and he had not been able to hear a wren's voice since without his heart stuttering. But this was coincidence. It had to be. Corvane was full of servants with bird names; half the nobility seemed named after hawks and herons, if the portraits in the corridor were any guide.

He sat when the king sat. He took the wine when it was offered and did not drink it. And he made the mistake, the fatal mistake, of listening.

* * *

Listening was the only thing Lena had ever been good at.

In Vell Hollow, where magic died at the border stones and his gift should have been strangled with everything else, listening had survived anyway—not as magic, maybe, but as habit, as instinct. He could hear a fox's mood in the set of its ears before it turned. He could hear a mule's opinion of a new owner from across a market square. The beasts of the valley had been his only confidants for thirty-one years, and he had learned their language the way other people learned prayer: daily, privately, without expecting an answer from anything larger than themselves.

So when King Aldric began to speak—pleasantries, the weather on the northern passes, a gentle inquiry into the state of the archives at Tarnhelm University—Lena heard what was underneath.

It started as a hum. He thought at first it was the palace itself, some vast furnace breathing beneath the floors, because the whole city of Corvane hummed if you stood still long enough; they'd heard it in the streets for two days, masked by bells, and Mara had gone pale every time the hourly chime covered it. But this hum was not in the floor. It was in the king.

Lena looked at Aldric's face and listened harder, the way he'd once listened for a wounded hawk in a collapsed chimney, sorting one sound out of many, and the hum resolved—and he stopped breathing.

Voices. Dozens of them. Perhaps more than dozens. They were layered inside the king like sediment inside stone, like rings inside a tree, and they were all singing the same note. Not words. Just the note—one sustained, patient, consonant tone, pitched somewhere below hearing, the note Lena had been hearing under the streets of Corvane since the gates. It was coming from him. From the throne. From the crown itself, which sat on a velvet cushion beside the king rather than on his head, and which Lena now understood was not beside him at all but *with* him, the way a hand is with its arm.

"—Master Ilya? You've gone quite pale."

Lena surfaced. The king was regarding him with concern, head tilted, and behind the king two guards had shifted their weight, and Mara's boot had found Lena's ankle under the table with the precision of a stiletto.

"Wine on an empty stomach, Majesty," Lena managed. "Forgive me. We walked far today."

"Then eat." Aldric gestured, and plates appeared—honeyed bread, white cheese, figs that must have traveled a thousand miles to be here. "There is nothing so dangerous as a court that starves its guests. Hungry guests begin to notice things."

The words landed strangely. Lena couldn't have said why until later, turning them over in the dark of their rented room: *hungry guests begin to notice things*, said lightly, said kindly, by a mouth full of singing.

He ate. The bread tasted of nothing at all.

* * *

The audience lasted an hour, and it was, on its surface, the most pleasant hour of Lena's entire journey.

Aldric asked about Tarnhelm's library and Mara answered with the easy fluency of a man reciting a place he'd actually studied, though Lena knew he'd never set foot in it. The king laughed at the right moments. He asked Lena—Ilya—what had drawn him to archival work, and Lena, improvising desperately, said, "Old things that still speak," and the king's smile deepened for just a moment into something that was not a smile at all, and then it was gone, smoothed away like a wrinkle pressed from silk.

"You'd like our own archive, then," Aldric said. "Three hundred years of rolls and registers. Names, mostly. This kingdom runs on names." He turned his wine glass slowly on the table, once, twice. "A name is a funny thing, Master Ilya. People think it belongs to them. But a name given to a record, a roll, a register—that name belongs to the kingdom forever. It can't be taken back." He looked up, warm brown eyes, laugh lines, kindness. "Don't you find that comforting? That nothing here is ever truly lost?"

"I find it frightening," Lena said, before he could stop himself.

Silence, thin as ice. Mara had gone very still beside him.

But the king laughed—a real laugh, full-bodied, delighted—and slapped the arm of his chair. "An honest archivist! Wren, did you hear that? Frightening! Yes, boy, yes—it should frighten you. Anything worth having should frighten you a little." He leaned forward, conspiratorial, and for one absurd moment he seemed so entirely, so completely human that Lena's chest ached with the wish for it to be true. "I'll grant your university access to the tithe rolls. All of them. Even the old ones. Scholars should be frightened properly, with primary sources."

Mara made a sound of gratitude so perfectly calibrated that only someone who had spent nine days in a cart with him would have heard the strain beneath it.

And then King Aldric blinked.

Lena had been watching faces his whole life—the faces of animals, which do not lie, and the faces of people, which lie constantly but badly. He knew how a blink worked. Lids down, lids up, a single fluid motion, the whole face participating in the small rest of it.

Aldric's blink happened in pieces.

The lids closed—and for a fraction of a second too long, they stayed closed while the muscles around his mouth kept smiling, held, suspended, a smile with no one home behind it. Then the lids opened, and the eyes arrived a beat after the face, sliding into alignment like gears catching, and in that gap—in that horrible, humming gap—Lena saw the eyes move. Not blink-moistening, not a glance. They swept the room, left to right, deliberate, cataloguing, the way a hawk reads a field. Then they settled back onto Lena and became warm again, and the smile resumed, and the king said, "You must stay for the festival of Saint Corvanus next week. The whole city will sing."

*All of it sings,* said the note beneath the note, and Lena could not swear he heard it with his ears.

His hands were shaking. He folded them in his lap and thought about the valley, about the border stones, about the smell of rain on sheep's wool, about anything that was not the fact that he was sitting six feet from a man wearing a chorus the way other men wore clothes.

Because that was what it was. He understood it suddenly, wholly, the way he sometimes understood an animal's pain—without evidence, without reasoning, just arrival. There was no King Aldric being controlled by voices. There was no king fighting from inside, not anymore, not here, not in this room. There was a chorus wearing Aldric like flesh, the way the prophecy book had said, and it had worn him so long and so well that it had learned his laugh lines, his generosity, his delight in honest boys. It had learned to rise from its throne and greet its guests. It had even, Lena realized with a lurch, learned to be kind—because kindness put people at ease, and ease made them slow, and slow things were easier to wear.

"How long?" he almost asked. *How long has he been gone? Is anyone still in there?* He bit it back. He bit it back so hard he tasted blood on the inside of his cheek.

* * *

They were dismissed with warmth. The king walked them personally to the doors—which no king did, said the chamberlain's stiffening spine—and pressed a token into Mara's hand, a small lead seal that would open the archive wings. "For your research," Aldric said. "The kingdom keeps its promises. Especially to those who listen."

The last word landed on Lena like a dropped stone.

Then the doors closed, and they were in the corridor, and then a side gallery, and then a colonnade above a frozen garden, and only there did Mara stop walking and turn to him, and only then did Lena discover that his legs were done holding him. He sat down on the cold balustrade among the statues of dead princes and put his head between his knees.

"You heard it," Mara said quietly. Not a question.

"It's singing." His voice came out sanded raw. "Mara, it's not one voice. It's dozens. Maybe hundreds. They're all singing the same note, all of them, like—they're not prisoners screaming. That would be terrible enough. They're *agreeing*. Whatever they were, whatever was done to them, they've been made into one thing, and the one thing likes it, and it's wearing him, and it shook my hand—" His breath hitched. "It was kind to us. Why was it kind to us?"

"Because it wants something from us." Mara was pacing now, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the seal glinting in his fist. "Or it doesn't yet know what we are. Or—" He stopped. "Or it knows exactly what we are, and this is what it looks like when something that old decides to play with its food."

"He gave us the archive."

"He gave us the archive," Mara repeated slowly. "Which either means we've just been handed everything we need—or we've been handed exactly what it wants us to find." He looked out over the frozen garden, and for a moment his face was the face from the first night in Vell Hollow, the charming stranger's face, except now Lena could read underneath it the way he read underneath everything: fear, guilt, and a stubborn, furious tenderness he was trying very hard to disguise as calculation. "We came here to confirm a prophecy. We've confirmed it. The king who is not one. We could still leave. Tonight. Take the southern road, lose ourselves in Tarnhelm, let the kingdom keep its names."

Lena lifted his head.

Inside the palace behind them, faint through walls and frost and distance, the hum went on. One note. Patient. And threaded somewhere inside it—he couldn't have said how he knew, only that his gift had never once lied to him about an animal in pain—something small and buried and *fighting*. A thread that did not agree. A voice that was still, against all odds and after however many years in the dark, trying to remember its own name.

"No," Lena said.

Mara exhaled. "Lena—"

"We're not leaving." He stood, and his legs held, and his hands had stopped shaking, and he did not examine why. "Something in there is still alive, Mara. Something in there is still *him*—or her, or them, I don't know—but I have spent my whole life listening to things everyone else decided were silent, and I am not going to walk out of this city while one of them is still calling."

The frozen garden glittered below them, beautiful and dead. Somewhere across the city, a bell began to ring the hour, hammering down over the hum, hiding it, hiding it, hiding it.

And beneath the bell, clear as a wren at a window, Lena heard—for one impossible second, one note slipped loose from the chorus like a single drop from a leaking roof—a child's voice, singing the same note as all the rest, but *flat*. Off-key. Wrong on purpose.

Fighting the song from the inside.

Mara was staring at him. "What? What is it? What did you hear?"

Lena looked at the palace, at its lit windows, its silk and gold, its perfect courtesy.

"There's a child in there," he said.
