## 8. The Queen Who Remembers

Isolde had learned to count her husband's blinks the way other wives counted his heartbeats.

There was a rhythm to a man's eyes when they were truly his own—a small, unremarkable music of attention, of looking and looking away, of the thousand tiny decisions a living face made without asking permission. Aldric had once blinked like weather: quick when he laughed, slow and heavy when he was tired, catching mid-blink when she said something that surprised him. She had loved that about him, though she'd never thought to love it until it began to go wrong.

Now his blinks came in sets. Three, always three, with a pause between them like a page being turned. And in the pause—only in the pause, only if you knew where to look—the eyes behind the lids moved wrong. Not the darting of dreams. The deliberate sweep of something taking inventory.

She stood at the edge of the receiving chamber with her hands folded and her face arranged into its public serenity, watching him receive the two travelers from Vell Hollow. The valley-folk. The beast-speaker and the archivist who was not entirely an archivist; she had read Mara Voss's file years ago, before the Choir's watchers burned it, and remembered a boy acolyte with clever hands and a talent for vanishing. He had grown into a man with careful eyes and a smile he wore like a coat over armor. She did not trust him. She noted, absently, that this was almost certainly how he preferred it.

It was the other one she could not stop watching.

Lena Nakamura stood in the center of the marble floor as if the floor were a language he hadn't learned yet—polite, watchful, slightly hunched, the posture of someone who had spent a lifetime making himself smaller than the room required. His clothes were road-worn. His hair needed cutting. And every few moments, when he thought no one saw, his gaze drifted toward the high windows of the palace, where the crows sat along the sills in their neat gray rows, and his lips moved faintly, as if answering a question.

The crows were Choir birds. Everyone knew that, or everyone who mattered; they perched on the palace like iron nails in wood, and nothing nested near them, and their silence had a texture to it, a held-breath quality that Isolde had come to think of as listening. In eleven years she had never seen one react to anything.

But when Nakamura's lips moved, the crow nearest the window tilted its head. Tilted it again. And then—it was so small she nearly missed it, so small she would spend the rest of the night turning it over like a stone in her palm—it *answered*. A single croak, low and rough, nothing like the flat mechanical calls the Choir-birds used for signaling.

Aldric turned his head at the sound. All of him turned it at once, smoothly, the way a weathervane turns—and Isolde felt the old cold slide down her spine, because there had been a time, early in their marriage, when he would have turned lazily, chin first, shoulders following, laughing at some joke of his own.

"Your Majesty," the steward murmured at her elbow, "the king requests your presence at the table."

*The king.* Not Aldric. She noticed which word the servants used now; the palace had developed a whole quiet grammar around what sat on the throne. When the thing wearing her husband was awake and driving, they said *His Majesty*. When enough of Aldric surfaced to be pitied, they lowered their voices and said *the king*, the way you'd say *the patient*.

She crossed the floor at the measured pace that was expected of her, skirts whispering, and took her seat at the king's right hand. The travelers were seated across—close enough to speak with, far enough to be watched by the guards whose stillness was itself a kind of statement.

"You honor us," Aldric said to them, and his voice was warm, and it was perfect, and it was wrong. Perfect voices were the first thing she had learned to fear. A living voice stumbled. It caught on grief, on hunger, on the small frictions of an actual throat. This voice poured out like oil, like water finding level, never once catching on anything. "Vell Hollow sends us so few visitors. We had begun to think the valley had forgotten the crown entirely."

"The valley forgets everything, Your Majesty," Mara Voss said smoothly. "That's rather its charm."

Something flickered behind the king's eyes—not anger, exactly. Interest. The interest of a collector hearing that a rare item existed somewhere unguarded.

"And yet," Aldric said, "it produced you." His gaze slid to Lena, and Isolde watched the young man's shoulders try to climb toward his ears and be forced back down by courtesy. "And you, Master Nakamura. They say you speak with beasts. A charming gift. A *small* gift." He smiled, and the smile fit his face the way a glove fits a hand—one finger at a time. "We do hope you'll enjoy your stay in Corvane. The city has so many voices. We're sure you'll find them... instructive."

Across the table, Lena went very pale, and very still, in the way of prey animals deciding whether stillness would save them.

Isolde lifted her wine glass and drank, and over the rim of it she looked at this trembling boy from the valley who had walked into the wolf's mouth without knowing the mouth was a wolf, and she made the decision she had been circling for eleven years the way a moth circles a flame it cannot decide to love or flee.

*If I am going to burn,* she thought, *let it at least be warm.*

* *

The queen's apartments occupied the east tower, and Isolde had spent a decade making them the one place in Corvane where the Choir could not comfortably listen. It had taken patience. It had taken absurdity. She had filled the rooms with songbirds—real ones, smuggled in from the coast, dozens of them, finches and larks and one ill-tempered starling—in cages of gilded wire, and she let them sing all day, all night, a riotous unplanned counterpoint that muddied whatever the Choir's listening spells might gather. The court thought her eccentric. Let them. Eccentricity was armor. A queen who doted on birds asked no dangerous questions; a queen who doted on birds could stand in a room full of conspirators and no one would ever search beneath the cages.

She dismissed her ladies at the door. She dismissed the guards with a gesture and a word about wanting air. Then she stood in the middle of her aviary-warm drawing room, amid the bright chaos of wings, and waited for the knock.

It came twice, hesitant, then once more—Mara Voss's knock, she'd have known it anywhere, the knock of a man who wanted to be heard but not remembered. She opened the door herself.

"Your Majesty." He bowed, shallow and quick. Behind him, Lena Nakamura hovered in the corridor like a man being led to his own trial. "You wished—"

"In. Quickly. Both of you." She shut the door, threw the bolt, and then—because old habits were the spine of survival—crossed to the window and opened it wide, letting the evening bells and the street noise and the birdsong braid together into a wall of harmless sound. Only then did she turn to face them.

Up close, the beast-speaker looked even younger. There was a bruised quality to his exhaustion, the look of someone who had not slept properly since crossing the border stones, and she recognized it with an ache she didn't permit her face to show. She remembered that look. She had worn it herself, once, arriving in this city as a bride of nineteen, learning within a month that the fairy tale had teeth.

"Do you know," she said, "what my husband is?"

Lena flinched. Mara did not; he only went carefully blank, the blankness of a man recalculating.

"Forgive me," Isolde continued, "for skipping the pleasantries. But the pleasantries are how the Choir eats people—did you know that? It doesn't drag anyone screaming into the dark. It invites them to dinner. It compliments their gardens. It asks after their mothers. And by the time you understand what is happening, you've already told it everything, politely, over soup." She gestured to the chairs by the fire. "Sit. Please. I have perhaps half an hour before my ladies return, and I intend to spend all of it telling the truth, which is a currency I rarely get to spend."

They sat. The starling muttered something obscene from its cage, and despite everything, despite the terror coiled under her ribs, Isolde felt her mouth twitch.

"I married Aldric when I was nineteen," she said. "He was twenty-three, and he laughed too loudly, and he cried at the birth of every foal in the royal stables, and he used to sing—badly, off-key, gloriously badly—while he shaved. Do you understand? He *sang*. Badly. No one bound to a chorus sings badly." Her hands tightened in her lap. "The change came slowly. That's the cruelty of it. If the Choir had taken him in a night, I'd have raised the alarm, died screaming, been a footnote. Instead it took him the way tide takes a sandcastle. A little each day. A blink out of rhythm. A word forgotten. A laugh that arrived a half-second late, like an echo trying to pass for the shout. And every morning I woke beside a man who was slightly less my husband and slightly more... *them*, and every morning I told myself I was imagining it, because the alternative was unbearable, and because—" her voice caught, and she mastered it, "—because I had no one. That is the first thing the Choir does. It isolates. You cannot say *my husband is being eaten* without sounding mad, and the Choir knows it, and it counts on it."

Lena leaned forward. "You said *eaten*."

"I said what I meant." She studied him. "You heard them. At the table. Don't lie to me, boy—I've had eleven years of practice detecting lies in this palace, and I will tell you now that it is the one skill I possess without flaw."

"I heard..." He swallowed. "Voices. Dozens. All singing the same note. Inside him. Like—like a hive, but the bees are people."

"Yes." The word came out of her flat and cold. "A hundred and six, by my count. Though I confess I stopped counting precisely at ninety because the number kept me awake." She rose and paced to the window, needing her back to them for a moment, needing to say the next part to the city rather than to their faces. "I have sabotaged them. Quietly. For years. Nothing grand—grandness gets you emptied, like poor Master Corvin the falconer's predecessor, like the chamberlain before him. Small things. I misfiled tithe rolls. I misplaced the names of children due for collection, sent them to live with cousins in the provinces before the collectors came. I once burned an entire parish ledger in a fireplace and blamed a careless maid, who I then pensioned generously and exiled safely, because guilt is cheaper than a grave." She turned back. "I have saved perhaps forty names in eleven years. Forty. Out of thousands. It is like bailing the sea with a teaspoon, and I have hated myself every single day for the arithmetic of it—and I have done it anyway, because the alternative was doing nothing, and I promised myself, the night I finally understood what shared my husband's crown, that I would never again be a woman who did nothing."

The room was very quiet except for the birds. One of the larks sang, sweetly and stupidly, into the silence.

"Why are you telling us this?" Mara asked quietly. Not accusing. Measuring. "You don't know us. By your own account, trust is fatal here."

"By my own account, *isolation* is fatal here," Isolde corrected. "Trust is merely the price of escaping it." She sat down again, facing Lena directly. "And because of the book. Because Master Voss carries a prophecy ledger from 1893 with a beast-speaker of Nakamura blood written in it beside the words *the king who is not one*—yes, I know about the book, I have known about it since before either of you crossed the border stones, because I have spent eleven years buying friends in low places, and archivists gossip worse than laundresses. And because I need you to hear what I am about to say before you put your faith in those pages."

She leaned forward, and let him see—not all of her, but more of her than she had shown any living soul since her wedding day.

"Prophecy books have been wrong before," she said. "Not mistaken. *Wrong.* Deliberately, beautifully, catastrophically wrong. Two hundred years ago there was another book—*The Lamentation of Saint Verre*—that named a chosen one who would break the old tyranny of the south. A girl called Maren of the Ashes. She believed it. Her followers believed it. They marched on the Citadel of Thorn with her name on their banners, and the Citadel was ready, because the men who wrote the prophecy had sold it to the Citadel a decade earlier. It was bait, Master Nakamura. A lure written in holy ink. Four thousand people died believing in a sentence someone had composed as a trap." She held his gaze. "I am not saying your book is false. I am saying I have read the histories, and I have learned that the most dangerous words in any kingdom are the ones that tell a desperate person exactly what they were hoping to hear. So before you walk into that throne room with destiny in your pocket—ask yourself who wrote the line, and why, and who profits when you believe it."

Lena was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "Then why help me at all? If I might be bait."

"Because bait can still bite." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "And because I watched you at that table. The Choir offered you a threat dressed as courtesy—*so many voices, so instructive*—and you did not run, and you did not grovel, and you did not pretend you hadn't understood. You just went pale and stayed in your chair. Do you know how rare that is? Fear that stays seated?" She shook her head. "My husband used to have that. Before."

"Your Majesty," Mara said slowly, "if you've been fighting alone for eleven years—"

"I wasn't fighting. Fighting implies the possibility of victory." She stood, crossed to her writing desk, and unlocked the bottom drawer with a key she wore on a ribbon against her sternum, where no searching hand had ever dared to go. From it she drew a slim bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the desk: papers. Tithe rolls. Correspondence in cipher. A map of the undercroft beneath the throne room, annotated in her own hand over years of careful, murderous curiosity. "This is everything I have. It is not much. It is forty saved names and one burned ledger and a map drawn from memory and a great deal of guilt. But it is yours, if you'll have it—and more to the point, *I* am yours, if you'll have me. My knowledge of the palace. My access. My face, which opens doors your faces cannot." She looked from one to the other. "I offer it with one condition, and the condition is not loyalty. Loyalty is what got my husband crowned, and look how the crown repaid it. My condition is truth. Whatever you learn—however ugly, however hopeless—you bring it to me first, and you tell it to me straight. I have been lied to by a chorus for eleven years. I find I've lost my appetite for it."

Lena reached out and touched the map with one fingertip, lightly, the way he might touch a frightened animal. "There's a child," he said abruptly. "In the Choir. I heard it tonight, under the note. A child's voice, still fighting. Still *its own*." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain. "If your condition is truth, Majesty, then here is mine: I'm going to free that child. Even if the book is bait. Even if it costs what books cost. I'd rather die useful than live polite."

Isolde looked at him for a long moment—at this shabby, terrified, unbending boy from a valley that forgot things—and felt something in her chest crack open that had been sealed since her wedding day. Grief, maybe. Or its twin.

"Aldric used to talk like that," she said quietly. "Before." She extended her hand, and after a heartbeat's hesitation, he took it. Her grip was dry and strong. "Welcome to the conspiracy, Master Nakamura. It is small, it is doomed, and its headquarters smells of birdseed. We meet at midnight, when the bells are loudest—that's when the Choir listens least, because it must sing louder to be heard over them, and while it sings, it cannot eavesdrop." She released his hand and turned to the window, to the darkening city where the hour-bells were gathering themselves. "Which reminds me. There is someone you must meet before midnight. My falconer. Blind as a stone, and the finest listener in this kingdom—and unlike the rest of us, he has spent thirty years learning to pick single threads out of a chorus."

She paused at the window, watching the first bell of evening swing its bronze tongue, and did not turn around when she said the last of it, because some truths were easier spoken to a city than to a face:

"There is one more thing you should know about the child's voice you heard. The tithe rolls list every name the Choir has taken. Every one but the first." Her knuckles whitened on the sill. "The first voice bound into that crown—the voice the whole rotten mercy was built to save—belonged to a boy of nine. My husband's little brother. And I am the one who let them take him."

Behind her, the bell began to ring.
