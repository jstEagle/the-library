## 9. Lessons in Listening

The falconer's name was Sable, and he had been blind for thirty years, which meant—Isolde said, with the particular dryness she reserved for things that mattered most—that he had been listening properly for thirty years, and everyone else in Corvane had been merely making noise.

Lena met him on the fourth morning after the audience with the king, in a mews tucked behind the palace's east wing where the smell of feathers and blood and raw meat hung so thick it seemed to have weight. The room was dim by design; hawks disliked bright light. Lena's eyes adjusted slowly, and when they did, he saw an old man seated on a stool among the perches, perfectly still, his face turned toward the door as though he'd been waiting for exactly this moment since dawn.

"You're the valley boy," Sable said.

"I'm—" Lena stopped. He still didn't know what to call himself in this place. *The beast-speaker of Nakamura blood*, the ledger said. *The unmaker.* Mara's word: *prophesied*. None of them fit in his mouth.

"The one who hears," Sable said, "and runs."

Lena flushed. He couldn't argue. Twice now—the border stones, the throne room—he had heard the chorus beneath the world, and twice he had clamped his hands over his ears like a child at a thunderstorm. In the throne room, standing before King Aldric's too-smooth courtesy, he had nearly fainted from the pressure of all those voices singing one note inside a man who blinked out of rhythm with his own face.

"It's not cowardice," Lena said. "It's—there are so many of them. They're all saying the same thing and it isn't words, it's *pressure*, it's like drowning in a crowd."

"Mm." Sable reached up without looking, and a falcon stepped onto his glove with the grave precision of a duchess accepting an arm. She was a peregrine, slate-backed, her dark eye fixed on Lena with an attention that made him feel catalogued. "This is Vesper. She's been with me eleven years. Tell me what she's saying right now."

Lena listened. It was easy here, away from the palace proper, away from the bells that rang every hour to smother the Choir's humming under the streets. Vesper was calm, hungry in a distant way, alert to a pigeon somewhere three rooftops off. "She wants to hunt. She's not worried about anything. She thinks I smell strange."

"She does think you smell strange." Sable's mouth twitched. "Now listen again, and tell me how many things she is, all at once."

Lena frowned. "One thing. She's one bird."

"Is she? She's hunger. She's memory—she remembers the cliff she was eyassed on, and the wind there, and the hand that fed her. She's fear of the owl that took her sister. She's the pigeon, three rooftops off, which she is not yet but intends to be. She's the ache in her left wing joint from yesterday's rain." The old man stroked the falcon's breast with one finger, feather-light. "You hear beasts as single voices because that's how you learned to survive. One wren, one mule, one warning. Clean and small. But nothing alive is one voice, boy. Everything that lives is a chorus pretending to be a creature. You've just never had to take one apart."

"And the Choir?" Lena asked quietly. "How many is that?"

Sable went still. Even Vesper seemed to settle deeper into her own stillness, as if the question had weight enough to press feathers flat.

"More than a hundred," the falconer said at last. "Fewer than two hundred. We don't know. Nobody has counted them in living memory, because nobody who tried came back able to speak about it." He turned his blind face toward Lena fully, and Lena understood that the man was seeing him in some way more thorough than eyes would manage. "The queen says you heard them through the king. That you didn't die. That's either a miracle or a fluke, and I've spent my life learning which miracles can be taught. So. Shall we find out?"

* * *

They began, absurdly, with water.

Sable had Lena fill a basin and sit with his hands in it while the mews' other birds called around them—a gyrfalcon complaining, a young goshawk rattling her feathers, Vesper's occasional chitter—and then, over the water, Sable spoke. Not words. Hummed tones, low ones, several at once, his throat doing something impossible, layering pitch on pitch until the sound wasn't a sound anymore but a texture, a wall.

"Find my voice," he said, from inside the wall of it. "Just mine. The rest is noise."

Lena lasted perhaps ten seconds before he tore his hands from the water and backed off the stool, heart slamming. "I can't. They're not separate. They're *one thing*—"

"They're not. Your panic is gluing them together. Fear flattens everything into a single shape—that's what fear is *for*, back when the single shape was a wolf. But you can't unmake a chorus if you can only ever hear *chorus*." Sable hummed the wall again, gentler this time. "Breathe. Put your hands back in the water. And stop trying to push the noise away. Walk into it. There's always a thread that's louder than its neighbors. Follow the loudest thread until you can hear where it ends and the next one begins."

It took four days.

Four days of sitting with his hands going pruney in cold basin water while an old man built walls of sound around him and a peregrine watched with what Lena slowly realized was pity. Four days of failure, of headaches that started behind his eyes and ended behind his teeth, of nights in his guest chamber where the palace itself seemed to hum faintly through the floor, as if the whole building were a bell that hadn't been struck yet. Mara came once, sat with him, said little. Mara had been saying little since the throne room; there was something he was carrying, Lena could tell, some knowledge held carefully away from the light, and Lena was too wrung out to pry it loose.

On the fifth day, walking into the noise instead of away from it, Lena found the seam.

It was exactly as Sable had promised: one thread in the wall of hum slightly louder than its neighbors, slightly *warmer*, the way a voice is warmer when the person speaking leans toward you. He followed it—not with his ears, he realized, but with whatever the thing inside him actually was, the gift the valley had spent his whole life teaching him to hide. The thread frayed into detail. It became a tone. The tone became a timbre. And then, with a lurch like stepping off a stair that wasn't there, it became a *someone*: an old woman's voice, cracked and tired, singing a note she had been singing so long she had forgotten there were other notes.

He gasped and lost it, and the wall collapsed back into noise, and he sat there shaking with his hands dripping on the floor—but he was laughing too, or something near it.

"There," Sable said softly. "There it is."

"I heard her. She's—I think she's still *in there*. Whoever she was. She's not gone, she's just—" Lena searched for it. "Buried. Singing so long she's forgotten why."

"That's what they are," Sable said. "That's what the tithe takes. Not lives—*names*. A name is the handle a soul hangs from. Take the handle and the soul doesn't fall, it just spins. Forever. Singing." He was quiet a moment. "My brother was taken in the tithe of '61. I was nine. They came for a debt our father owed, and they took Tam's name instead of coin, because names were cheaper that year." His blind eyes did not blink. "I have listened for him in the crown's humming for fifty years. Every hour, when the bells ring and the Choir rises underneath, I listen. I've never found him. Perhaps he's too deep. Perhaps he's worn smooth as river stone by now. But I keep listening, because the alternative is to agree that he's gone, and I have never agreed to anything so stupid in my life."

Lena looked at the old man—at the stillness of him, the terrible patience—and understood that Isolde hadn't sent him here to learn technique. She had sent him here to learn *grief that refuses to become surrender*.

* * *

The lessons moved from water to birds to the city itself.

Sable walked him out at dusk, when the bells had just finished their hourly drowning of the Choir, into the narrow streets below the palace where the poor of Corvane lived stacked like cordwood. Here the branded animals stood in their pens and shafts—mute-eyed mules, dogs with the sigil seared into their ears, pigeons roosting under eaves with the flat, vacant look of furniture. The tithe rolls took beasts too, sometimes, when the crown wanted witnesses silenced. Lena had freed one branded mule on the road to the city, given it back its voice and its terror and its gratitude, and soldiers had noticed, and a farmer had paid for it with a beating. He hadn't done it since. He hated himself for not doing it since.

"Listen to the city," Sable said, standing in the blue dusk with his white stick folded, his head tilted. "Not the Choir. Under the Choir. What do you hear?"

Lena opened himself the way he'd learned to—with dread and wonder in equal measure—and listened.

Under the hourly bells, under the vast submerged note of the bound hundred-something, the city was *full*. Rats in the walls thinking rat thoughts. Cats on sills judging the world. Horses in the guard stables dreaming of clover. Thousands of small minds, dim and bright, ordinary and holy, all of them going about the business of being alive beneath a kingdom that had forgotten they could speak.

And threaded through all of it, faint as wire, the Choir. Singing its one note. And inside the note—

Lena froze.

Inside the note, buried deeper than the old woman, deeper than anything he'd touched in the basins, there was a thread that was not singing.

Everyone else in the Choir sang. That was the horror of it—they sang because they had nothing left with which to do otherwise. But this thread was *pulling against the song*. Straining sideways, the way a hooked fish strains, the way a hand under snow pushes up. It wasn't loud. Loudness would have been found and smoothed away centuries ago. It was quiet, and stubborn, and it hurt to listen to, the way a bent finger hurts.

"Sable," Lena whispered. "There's one that's fighting."

The old man went very still. "Fighting."

"Not escaping. It can't escape. It's just—it won't sing. It sings because it has to, but underneath the singing it's pulling *against*, every second, and it's been doing it for—" Lena's throat closed. He followed the thread down, down past the old woman, past dozens of others, into the deep strata of the bound, and the closer he got the clearer it became, and the clearer it became the worse it got, because—

Because it was a child.

A child's voice. Small and high and ragged with effort, pulling against the note the way a child pulls against a hand that holds it, not with strategy but with pure refusal, the refusal that lives in children before the world teaches them the futility of things. And wrapped around the refusal, worn almost smooth but not quite, not quite, there was a name-shape. Faint. Frayed. But a name.

"How long," Lena heard himself say, from very far away, "has the Choir been taking children?"

Sable's silence was answer enough.

Lena knelt right there in the street, in the dung and the dusk, and pressed his palms flat to the cobblestones as if he could reach through the city's bones to wherever the bound voices circled, and he listened to the child pull against the song, and pull, and pull, and never stop, and something in his chest tore open along a seam he hadn't known was there.

He thought of the valley. Of a lifetime spent making himself small, hiding the gift, hiding himself, keeping safe by keeping silent. He had called it wisdom. He had called it survival. Kneeling on the stones of Corvane with a child's refusal ringing in his skull, he finally called it what it was: he had been singing the note he was told to sing, all his life, and calling the silence around it freedom.

No more.

"I'm going to find its name," he said. His voice came out low and steady and strange to him. "Whoever it was. However deep. I'm going to find its name and give it back, and then I'm going to do it for the old woman, and for your brother, and for every voice in that thing wearing the king."

Sable reached down—found his shoulder by touch alone, the way blind men find things, precisely and without fumbling—and gripped it hard.

"Fifty years I've listened," the falconer said, and his voice, for the first time, was not steady at all. "Don't make promises to me, boy. Make them to the child."

But that night, alone in his chamber with the palace humming faintly beneath the floor like a held breath, Lena lay awake and listened one last time to the small stubborn thread pulling against the song—and heard, for the first time, the thread *change*. Just slightly. Just barely. As if, somewhere in the deep water of the Choir, something that had refused for years upon years had felt him listening—

—and pulled a little harder.

And beneath the palace, far below, something vast and patient turned its attention toward the sound of him, the way a great eye turns toward a crack of light under a door.
