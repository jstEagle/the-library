## 10. The Tithe Rolls

The archive of Corvane did not look like a place where secrets were kept. It looked like a place where secrets had been buried so long they had become furniture.

Mara stood in the shadow of the outer colonnade and studied it the way he had once studied choir stalls before slipping into one: the rhythm of the guards, the rhythm of the lamps, the rhythm of the bells. Everything in this city ran on rhythm. That was the first thing the Choir taught its acolytes—that a people can be governed by tempo. Ring the bells at the right hours and no one notices what hums beneath them.

Rain came down in fine gray threads, beading on the leaded windows of the Hall of Records. Inside, he knew, clerks would be banking their fires by now. The night archivist, a stooped man named Pell who took his supper at half past nine and his brandy after, would lock the reading room and carry the great iron key to the undercroft stair, where a second guard would log it into a ledger with a timestamp accurate to the minute.

Accurate to the minute. That was the flaw. Corvane trusted its clocks the way it trusted its bells—completely, blindly, and without ever asking who wound them.

"You're doing the thing again," Lena murmured beside him. "Where you stare at a building like you're undressing it."

"I am memorizing it," Mara said. "There's a difference. Undressing is faster."

Lena huffed something that was almost a laugh, and pulled his hood lower. In the lamplight spilling from a tavern across the square, his face was all soft edges and watchful eyes—a valley boy trying to make himself small in a city that ate small things first. Mara felt the old familiar ache of tenderness and pressed it down flat, the way he pressed everything down. There would be time for tenderness later, if there was a later. Tonight there was only the key, the ledger, and the hour.

"Tell me again why we can't just ask the queen," Lena said.

"Because Isolde can open doors," Mara said, "but she cannot unopen eyes. The moment her name touches a request slip, the Choir knows she's looking. She's spent years being boring precisely so they'd stop watching her. We are not going to spend her invisibility on a filing question."

"And your plan instead is burglary."

"Archival retrieval," Mara said, "with unlawful entry."

* * *

The way in was through the clock.

The Hall of Records had been built a century ago by an architect with more piety than sense, and above its eastern gable he had set a clock tower with a maintenance gallery behind the face—an iron catwalk where a man could stand inside the works and oil the gears while the city ticked beneath him. No one had oiled those gears in years; the tower keeper was a sinecure, sold twice over to absent men. But the gallery door still opened, because Corvane never locked anything it believed no one wanted.

Mara went up the outside stair first, testing each rung, rain slicking the iron under his gloves. Lena followed with the terrible sure-footedness of someone raised on ridge paths, breathing hard not from fear but from the climb, and when they reached the gallery Mara heard him stop dead.

"It's loud up here," Lena whispered.

"It's a clock."

"Not the clock." Lena's hand found Mara's sleeve in the dark. His grip was careful, always careful, as if Mara were something that might startle. "Underneath. It's like—do you hear humming? Like a room full of people holding one note."

Mara listened. He heard gears, rain, the far-off complaint of a cart in the square. And beneath it—yes. Faint as a pulse through a wall. The Choir did not confine itself to the palace. It seeped. It had been seeping for a hundred years, into foundations, into bell bronze, into the very stone the archivists filed their paper on.

"The whole city is a resonating chamber," he said quietly. "That's what the bells are for. Not to tell time. To keep the note covered."

Lena stared at him. "You knew that."

"I suspected. Knowing is different." He turned to the narrow door set into the tower's inner wall. "Come on. We have forty minutes between the half-past round and the eleven o'clock log."

The gallery door groaned but gave. Beyond it, a maintenance shaft dropped straight down through the building's spine—a black brick throat with iron rungs bolted every arm's length, smelling of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of incense. Old incense. Choir incense, the myrrh-and-ash blend Mara had breathed for six years of his life, and his stomach turned over with a violence that surprised him.

"Mara?" Lena's voice floated down.

"Fine," he called back, and began to climb down into the dark of his own history.

* * *

They dropped into the sub-basement behind a rack of map tubes, and Mara counted seconds in his head while Lena got his bearings. This was the part he had rehearsed twenty times and dreamed of fifty: the corridor with the gas jets turned low, the reading room door with its lock worn generous by a century of lazy clerks—he had picked such locks himself, once, as an acolyte running errands for masters who considered locks beneath them—and then the inner door. The iron one. The one with the key logged in the ledger.

Pell's brandy hour was sacred. The guard at the undercroft stair changed at eleven exactly. Between those two facts lay eighteen minutes, and Mara intended to spend them like a miser.

The reading room smelled of cold fire and vellum. Moonlight came gray through the high windows and laid pale rectangles across the tables, and for a moment Mara simply stood in it, letting the silence of the place settle over him. Archives were churches of a kind. He had always felt it. Every shelf a reliquary, every box a small sealed coffin of other people's lives.

"This way," he whispered, and led Lena past the public catalogues—the tax rolls, the parish registers, the shipping manifests, all the honest paper—to the inner door.

The lock was newer than the rest. Of course it was. He knelt and worked his picks with fingers that remembered how, sweat gathering under his hood despite the cold, and thought, absurdly, of the first time he had picked a lock: age fourteen, the acolytes' pantry, stealing honeyed figs while the night office droned. Brother Cassian had caught him and, instead of punishing him, had said, *Good. A man who can open a door quietly can also close one.* He had not understood then what kind of door Cassian had meant.

The lock surrendered with a click like a knuckle cracking.

Inside, the tithe vault was smaller than legend made it. One lamp, which Mara lit low. Four iron cabinets. And shelves—shelves and shelves of ledger books bound in gray leather, each spine stamped with a year, going back and back into the dark like the rings of a tree.

"What am I looking at?" Lena breathed. He had drifted to the nearest shelf and was tilting a volume out with both hands, gentle as lifting a bird.

"The tithe rolls," Mara said. "Every generation since the founding of the Choir. Names."

"Names of what?"

"That's what we're here to find out." He pulled the most recent roll—this year's, still fresh, the ink barely settled into the page—and laid it open under the lamp.

It was a census. That was the horror of it, the plain bureaucratic horror: columns ruled neat and true, parish by parish, household by household. Name. Age. Trade. And a fourth column, narrow, headed only with a single stamped letter that might have been decorative if you didn't know better.

*C.*

"Crown service?" Lena guessed.

"Choir," Mara said. "Look at the pattern. Not the names yet—the ages. Tell me what you see."

Lena bent close. His lips moved slightly as he read; he had never learned to read silently well, and Mara found, inappropriately, that he loved him for it. "Twelve. Nine. Fifteen. Eleven. They're all—" He stopped. His finger hovered over the column. "They're children."

"Between eight and sixteen. Never older. Never younger." Mara turned pages with a care that cost him. "One per parish, some years. Two or three in others. Always marked C. Always recorded as having entered crown service—which means apprenticeship, wardship, choir school, any of a dozen respectable doors a poor family will thank you for opening." He heard his own voice go flat and clinical, the voice he used when feeling would otherwise drown him. "And then look at the follow-up rolls. Ten years later. Twenty. Look for those same names."

Lena looked. Mara watched him look, watched the color change in his face by lamplight, watched him arrive at the absence the way Mara himself had arrived at it three nights ago, alone, with a candle and a growing sickness in his gut.

"They're not there," Lena said. "Ten years later, the same parishes, the same families—and the children aren't anywhere. No marriage record. No trade record. No death record." He looked up. "People don't just vanish from paper."

"No," Mara said. "Paper is the one place people never vanish from. If there's no death record, no one died. If there's no departure record, no one left." He closed the roll softly, as though it were sleeping. "They were taken. Name first, then body—or body first, then name. I don't know which. But the rolls are the mechanism, Lena. This is how it feeds. Not with teeth. With paperwork."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft neither of them had made. Somewhere above, muffled by floors of stone, the city hummed its one held note, and Mara saw Lena flinch at it, hand going to his own throat as if to check that his voice was still his.

"How many?" Lena asked. His voice had gone rough. "How many names?"

Mara did the arithmetic he had already done, and hated it again. "A hundred years. Call it four to six a year, conservatively." He swallowed. "Four hundred. Perhaps six hundred. Perhaps more, if the early rolls were sloppier."

Lena sat down slowly on the floor among the gray volumes, cross-legged, the way he sat in fields talking to mules. "The king," he said. "All those voices. They're not guarding him. They're *made of* him. The crown is wearing six hundred stolen children."

"Yes."

"And nobody knows."

"Somebody knows," Mara said. "Somebody keeps these rolls current. Somebody signs off on the tithes every year. There's a clerk somewhere in this machine with ink on his fingers and a pension waiting, and he goes home to his supper and sleeps fine." He realized his hands had curled into fists against his thighs and made himself open them. Anger was a luxury tonight. Anger was loud.

Lena was quiet a moment. Then, in the small careful voice he used for asking questions he already dreaded answers to: "Why do they take children? Why young ones?"

Mara had asked himself the same thing, walking the palace corridors, listening to the bells. He had a hypothesis and he hated it. "Because a child's voice hasn't finished becoming," he said. "It's still soft. Still shapeable. An adult voice comes with a whole life stuck to it—griefs, grudges, a name it's grown into. Harder to bind. Easier to break, maybe, but harder to bind cleanly." He met Lena's eyes. "The Choir doesn't want soldiers. It wants clay."

Lena nodded slowly, and Mara saw him file the word away somewhere behind his ribs, next to whatever he carried about the branded animals on the road, next to the child-sized griefs of Vell Hollow. Then Lena said, "Show me the oldest one. The first roll. If it started as something—if it rotted into this—I want to see the beginning."

Mara reached past him into the dark end of the shelf, where the leather was cracked and furred with age, and drew out a volume whose spine stamp had nearly worn away. 1821. The founding year. He laid it open under the lamp with both hands, the way you handle a relic, and the pages exhaled a smell like cold ash.

The handwriting was different here. Older, more ornate—but the columns were the same. Name, age, trade, and that narrow final column. Except in this first roll, the final column wasn't a stamp. In the first roll, someone had written actual words, in a small cramped hand, as if the practice had begun honestly and only later learned to hide behind a letter.

Mara read the first entry aloud, because some things needed air to be real:

"*Aldric the First, King, dying of the wasting. By consent of council: his voice to be preserved beyond his body, that the realm might not lose its father's word.*"

He stopped.

"Lena," he said. His mouth had gone dry. "The first name in the tithe rolls is a king."

"Aldric," Lena repeated. "Like—"

"Like the king in the palace upstairs. Who is named for his great-grandfather, as kings are." Mara's heart was beating strangely, too fast and too slow at once. "It began as mercy. Or it was dressed as mercy. A dying king's voice, saved. Bound into the crown so his word would outlive his flesh." He turned the page, and the next, scanning the entries as they marched forward through the years, watching the handwriting tighten, watching the stated reasons shrink from grand phrases to terse ones to nothing at all—watching mercy curdle into routine. "*By consent of council.* By consent. By consent. And then, thirty years in, no reason given at all. Just names. Just ages. Just C."

He was three-quarters through the second decade of entries when his eye snagged on a line, the way a sleeve snags on a nail, and everything in him went still.

The lamp hissed. Rain ticked against a high window. Lena said his name, and he didn't answer, because he was reading it again, and again, refusing to let the letters rearrange themselves into what they plainly said.

Parish of Lowmere. Household of Veyra.

*Mara Veyran, aged nine. Trade: none. Entered crown service, choir school, by consent of guardian.*

His own surname. His own parish. The village where his mother had been born, where his grandmother had buried two infants and told the story every winter until the telling wore grooves in her face. And a boy of nine, with his family's name, taken into "choir school"—a phrase Mara now understood had never once meant a school.

"There's no date of return," Lena said softly. He had come around the table to stand beside him, and was reading upside-down with the ease of someone who had spent his life reading over shoulders. "No discharge. No death." He paused. "Mara. Is this—is Veyran your—"

"My mother's maiden line." The words came out of him level and strange, someone else's voice borrowed for the occasion. "Veyran. Veyra. The rolls anglicized it, or the clerk was lazy." He touched the entry. The ink was brown with age and slightly proud of the page, ridged where the pen had pressed hard. Whoever wrote it had been hurried, or heavy-handed, or both. "My grandmother had a brother. She never spoke of him. When I was small I asked once, and she looked at me the way you look at a hole in the ice, and said, *He went to the capital to sing.* And then she fed me extra bread, the way she did whenever anyone mentioned the dead."

Lena put his hand over Mara's where it rested on the page. His palm was warm and calloused and steady, and Mara found that he was shaking, very slightly, in a way he could not remember ever shaking before—not over the prophecy book, not over his flight from the Choir, not over any of the clean fears he had trained himself to carry. This was not clean. This was a hundred years old and it had been waiting for him in a gray ledger with his family's name on the spine.

"He'd be my great-great-uncle," Mara said. "Or he'd be nothing, because there may be nothing left of him to be an uncle. He might be a note. He might be—" His voice did something he despised; it thinned. He mastered it. "He might be one thread in the sound the king makes when he says good morning."

"Mara." Lena crouched so their eyes were level, and his gaze was fierce and wet and absolutely unflinching. "Listen to me. Your gift talks to beasts. Mine does. But yours—" He stopped, restarted. "You fled the Choir. You kept the book. You brought me here. Whatever they took from your family, you're the one carrying it back to them. That's not nothing. That's the opposite of nothing."

Mara laughed—one broken syllable of it, quickly strangled. "You realize what this is? This is what they do. This is the weapon underneath the paperwork." He tapped the roll. "They take a child, and fifty years later his great-great-nephew breaks into a vault and finds the name, and the grief does their work for them. Grief slows you. Grief makes you careful in the wrong places. Personal history, weaponized—I'm standing inside the trap and I walked in whistling."

"Then don't let it slow you," Lena said. "Let it aim you."

For a moment Mara could not speak. The lamp burned. The city hummed its patient note through the bones of the building, and six hundred names lay stacked around them in gray leather, sleeping their ordered sleep, and one of them had his blood in it.

"Aim me," he repeated. "Right." He straightened, wiped his face briskly with his cuff like a man clearing fog from a lens, and pulled the current year's roll back toward him. "Then we work fast, and we work angry, and we get out before the eleven o'clock log. Copy the marked names—all of them, every parish, every year you can reach. Families, ages, dates. If we're going to free them, we need to know who we're freeing and who's waiting at home for news that never came."

Lena was already pulling paper from his satchel, already bending over the oldest roll, charcoal moving in quick strokes. "The first ones," he said. "The earliest names. If the spell started with Aldric the First, maybe the binding is anchored at the beginning. Maybe the first voice taken is the keystone."

"Maybe." Mara moved along the shelf, hauling volumes, checking years against his memory of the palace's architecture, and it was while he was reaching for the roll of 1847 that he heard it.

Not with his ears. With the part of him the Choir had trained and he had spent ten years pretending was dead: the deep-trained ear, the acolyte's ear, the one that could pick a single wrong note out of a hundred voices in full anthem.

The hum beneath the city had changed.

It was very slight. A lesser ear would have missed it entirely. But the note that seeped up through the archive floor had shifted—had bent, fractionally, the way a held chord bends when one singer among many turns his head toward something in the dark and begins, quietly, to listen back.

Mara froze, hand on the spine of the 1847 roll.

"Lena," he whispered. "Stop writing."

Lena looked up, charcoal poised. "What—"

"Something down here just noticed us." Mara's eyes swept the shadows between the shelves, the black mouths of the aisles, the lamp's small trembling circle of light. "Or something noticed *you*. And I think—" He swallowed, and the incense-smell of the dark seemed suddenly much older, much closer. "I think it's listening to the smallest voice it has."

Above them, muffled through a century of stone, the bells began to ring eleven.

And in the silence after the last stroke, from the deepest aisle of the vault—from behind the oldest rolls, where no living person stood—came a sound that made the hair rise on Mara's arms.

A child's voice, thin and clear and terribly patient, humming the Choir's note.

Waiting for them to find it.
