## 11. A Note Sung Wrong

The palace at night was a different animal than the palace by day.

By day it was all ceremony and marble, courtiers gliding like well-fed fish, guards whose armor sang when they walked. By night it became what Lena suspected it had always been: a body pretending to sleep. The corridors breathed. The walls held their warmth like skin. And beneath everything—beneath the flagstones and the foundations and the old bones of the mountain the palace was carved into—ran that hum.

Lena had learned to sleep through it, mostly. That was the trick of Vell Hollow in reverse: in the valley he had spent his whole life straining to hear anything at all, because magic died at the border stones and with it the deep speech of beasts, leaving only ordinary animal sounds, bird-song and dog-bark, beautiful but mute. Here, outside the stones, the world never stopped talking. The rats in the pantry gossiped about grain. The moths in the chapel argued over light. And under all of it, constant as groundwater, the Choir hummed its single note through the roots of the city.

So he slept through it, the way you sleep through rain. Until the night he couldn't.

* * *

He woke in the dark of his guest chamber with his heart already hammering, though nothing in the room had changed. The candle had burned to a coin of wax. The window stood where he'd left it, cracked open to the cold—and on the sill, feathers puffed against the chill, sat a wren.

Not his wren. His wren was three hundred miles east, presumably still gossiping about him to the valley's sparrows. This one was smaller, scruffier, a city bird with soot in its brown and one clouded eye. It looked at Lena with its good eye and said, in the flat, layered voice that meant it wasn't really the bird speaking:

*"You heard us listening."*

Lena sat up slowly. "I don't know what you mean."

*"Yes you do."* The wren tilted its head, and for a moment something moved behind the small black eye—not the bird, not even an animal, but a pressure, a crowd pressed against the inside of a glass too small for it. *"The falconer teaches you to pick threads from the weave. You picked one last night. In the king's presence. A thread that does not belong to your lessons."*

His mouth went dry. He thought of the audience chamber, of King Aldric's courteous smile, of the moment—only a heartbeat—when the king had turned toward the window and Lena, following the falconer's instructions, had let his hearing sink down through the hum the way you'd lower a bucket into a well. He'd been hunting for the note he'd found before, the thin off-key thread he'd told no one about. He hadn't found it. But he'd brushed against something else, something small and quick and frightened, and flinched back from it like a hand from a stove.

"I was practicing," he said. "That's all."

*"Practicing,"* the wren agreed, in a tone that made clear the word was being filed away somewhere. *"The Choir permits the falconer's lessons. The Choir finds them amusing. A mouse learning to read the cat's whiskers."* The bird hopped once along the sill. *"But there is one thread the Choir keeps wrapped in wool. Touch it again, beast-speaker, and we will know whether your gift is worth more than your tongue."*

Then the pressure withdrew, all at once, like a tide going out, and the wren was only a wren—startled, blinking, suddenly aware it was cold and far from its nest. It flew off into the dark without a backward glance, and Lena sat in the freezing room with sweat cooling on his neck, understanding two things with perfect clarity.

First: the child-voice was real. He hadn't imagined it. And the Choir knew he'd touched it.

Second: they had just told him exactly which thread mattered most.

* * *

He should have gone to Isolde. He knew that. He lay awake until the bells rang the small hours, rehearsing how he'd say it—*the Choir spoke to me through a wren, they've noticed the lessons, they've noticed me*—and every version of the confession sounded smaller and more foolish than the last. What would he tell her? That he'd been sneaking extra listens? That the falconer had warned him, in his dry way, that a listener who reaches past his depth drowns there, and Lena had reached anyway?

The falconer would have known what to do. But the falconer—

No. He wasn't ready to think that sentence yet. That was tomorrow's grief, or the day after's.

What he did instead was what he had always done, the thing that had kept him alive and hidden and small in Vell Hollow for twenty-three years: he went looking for the animal who could tell him the truth.

* * *

The palace kept few beasts—a cull-happy crown did not encourage them—but it kept ravens, because ravens were tradition, six of them in a mews above the north tower, descended from birds that had carried messages when Corvane was young. Lena had befriended the eldest on his second day, a ragged female he'd named Cistern for no reason either of them could remember, and she came to his window now at his low whistle, folding onto the sill with the offended dignity of a duchess kept waiting.

"You felt it too," she said without preamble. Ravens wasted no breath. "Last night. The whole city's birds felt it. The Choir reached out through everything feathered and furred and scaled, looking for something. Looking for *you*."

"It found me."

"It found you," Cistern agreed, "and then it got strange. Because it didn't crush you, did it? It could have. One word through any throat in this city and you'd have been dragged to the undercroft before dawn. Instead it sent a warning dressed as a threat." She preened her wing, thinking. "That's not how hunger behaves, beast-speaker. Hunger takes. That was curiosity. Something in that chorus wanted to see what you'd do next."

"The child," Lena said quietly.

Cistern's head came around, sharp-eyed. "You've heard it."

"Once. Maybe twice. A thread inside the king's hum that doesn't fit—it's higher, thinner, and it moves wrong. Like someone singing while trying not to be heard." He wrapped his arms around his knees. "I told myself I imagined it. Then the wren came and told me I hadn't."

The raven was silent a long moment. When she spoke again her voice had lost its duchess air. "My grandmother's grandmother was tithed," she said. "That's crow-talk, older than your kind's records—the first birds the Choir took weren't people. They were beasts, in the beginning, before they learned human voices hold better. My line remembers the taking. And I'll tell you what my line remembers about the Choir's beginnings, though the priests will call it heresy." She hopped closer, lowering her voice as if the stones themselves might inform. "It didn't start as a hunger. It started as a *carrying*."

"A carrying?"

"A dying king, long ago. So the story goes among the feathered. A king who loved his kingdom too much to leave it voiceless, so his singers bound his voice into a vessel—stone, or gold, or song itself—so that his counsel would outlive his body. A mercy. A lamp passed hand to hand." Her feathers rippled. "But a voice alone goes mad in the dark, beast-speaker. And a voice that's lonely starts calling others to keep it company. First it asks. Then it collects. Then it *hungers*. That's what my grandmothers' memory says: the mercy rotted. The lamp became a mouth."

Lena stared at her. "And the child?"

"That," said Cistern, "I cannot tell you. But if there is one voice in that chorus still fighting—if it sings wrong on purpose, to be findable—then it isn't singing to the Choir." Her good eye fixed on him. "It's singing to whoever might listen. It has been waiting a very long time for someone with ears like yours."

* * *

He waited three nights. Three nights of lessons with the falconer—who watched him too closely now, who asked twice whether he was sleeping, whose own silence had begun to feel less like a trait and more like a held breath—and three nights of lying awake while the hum rose through the floor like water in a cellar, telling himself he would not do it, knowing he would.

On the third night he climbed to the gallery above the throne room. It was a fool's errand and he knew it; the king held no midnight audiences, and the great doors below were barred. But the gallery was old, older than the doors, part of the original mountain-carving, and it had a vent—a slotted stone mouth that once, the archivists said, let the king hear petitioners in the hall without being seen. Air moved through it. Sound moved through it. And sound, Lena had learned, was only ever another kind of speech.

He knelt at the vent and put his ear to the slot, and let himself sink.

Down through the hum he went, past the first layer—that vast single note, patient as erosion—and the second, where the hundred voices circled like water around a drain, each one worn smooth of its edges, nameless, blending. He had learned from the falconer not to flee this depth but to move through it slowly, gently, the way you'd wade among sleeping fish. He kept his own name folded tight against his heart, a coal wrapped in wool. *I am no one,* he told the chorus. *I am only listening.*

And there—faint, frayed, deliberately off-key—sang the thread that moved wrong.

It was higher than the rest, and thin as wire, and it did not blend. Where the Choir's voices flowed in their endless circle, this one snagged, caught, slipped sideways—a note sung wrong on purpose, the way a prisoner drags a chain across a cell floor to remind the dark that someone is still in it. Lena followed it the way he'd follow a wounded bird's cry through brush: carefully, without casting a shadow over it.

*I hear you,* he said, in the deep speech, the way that needed no throat. *I'm not one of them.*

The thread went silent.

He waited. Below him, through the vent, he could hear the throne room's emptiness breathing, and beneath that, fainter, footsteps—the night watch, far off, unimportant. The hum continued its patient circle. The wrong note stayed quiet, and Lena understood suddenly that it was afraid, that it had been reaching out for years into a darkness where nothing ever answered, and now something had, and it did not dare believe it.

*I'm a friend,* he tried. *A beast-speaker. From a valley called Vell Hollow. I can hear you. I've been able to hear you since the day I arrived, and I think—I think you've been waiting for that.*

Silence. Then, so softly it was almost not a voice at all, almost only the memory of one:

*"...Are you real?"*

A child's voice. Small, cracked at the edges, exhausted in a way no child's voice should be. Lena's eyes stung.

"I'm real," he said aloud, whispering it into the vent, letting the words travel down the old stone throat to whatever waited below. "My name is—" and here he stopped, the way he always stopped lately, some instinct warning him that names were currency here, that the Choir collected them. "You can call me Wren. That's what my friend calls me. Who are you?"

A long pause. When the answer came, it came in pieces, like something being unwrapped from bandages it had worn so long they'd grown into the skin.

*"Tomas. My name was Tomas. I think—I think it was. They took it a long time ago and I've been holding it in my teeth ever since, like a bird holds a seed, so they can't make me swallow."*

"How long?"

*"I don't know. There aren't days down here. Only the singing. We sing and sing and the king wears us and we keep the kingdom safe, that's what they tell us, that's the song, we keep the kingdom safe, we keep the kingdom safe—"* The voice frayed, caught itself, steadied. *"But that's not how it started. I remember the start. I'm the newest. Or I was, when I was taken. The oldest one showed me. The first voice. The king's voice."*

Lena leaned closer to the cold stone. "Tell me."

*"It was a mercy."* Tomas's voice dropped, awed despite everything, the way you speak of something holy even after it has hurt you. *"The first king was dying, and he loved this country, and he didn't want his voice to die with him—to leave the throne empty of counsel, the borders undefended, the songs unsung. So his singers made a vessel. A carrying-spell. One voice, given freely, to guide the kings who came after. That's all it was supposed to be. One voice. A lamp."*

"And then?"

*"And then the lamp got lonely."* The words came slower now, and colder. *"Voices need answering, Wren. That's what a voice *is*—it's the half of a conversation. The first king's voice sang into the dark for years and years and nothing sang back, and it began to... reach. Not stealing, at first. Asking. And the kings who wore the crown—they heard it asking, and they were afraid, or ambitious, or tired, and they gave it voices. Criminals. Enemies. Then tithe-rolls, then lots drawn from villages, then—"* the child's breath hitched—*"then children, when the rolls ran short. They told my mother it was an honor. They told her the kingdom would be safe. She signed the paper because she couldn't read it."*

The hum below seemed to deepen, and Lena realized with a lurch that the Choir's circling had slowed—as if the great wheel of voices were turning its attention, slow as a lighthouse beam, toward a point in itself where one note refused to stay in tune.

*Tomas,* he said quickly, silently. *Listen to me. If they notice you talking—*

*"They'll notice you,"* the child said, and there was something terribly old in the young voice now. *"They already have. They've been watching you since the wren. They let you come tonight. Do you understand? The vent, the gallery, the guard who looked the other way—they opened the door and you walked through it, because they wanted to know what you'd say to me. They wanted to hear what I'd give away."*

Cold walked up Lena's spine. "Then why are you telling me?"

*"Because I've been holding my name in my teeth for sixty years,"* Tomas said, *"and I am so tired, Wren. And because the oldest voice—the first king—he's not all rotten. There's still a man in there, under the hunger, and some nights he weeps and asks the others for forgiveness, and some nights the hunger wears him like a glove. Tonight the hunger is wearing him. Tonight it wants to know your price."*

Below, the hum stopped.

Not faded. Stopped—all hundred voices ceasing at once, the way a forest goes silent when a hawk's shadow crosses it, and into that ringing absence rose a new voice, layered from a hundred throats speaking as one, gentle as a nurse waking a patient:

*"Beast-speaker. You have heard our beginning. Will you not hear our purpose?"*

Every hair on Lena's body stood. He gripped the vent's stone lip and did not run, though every animal instinct he owned screamed at him to run, because he understood with sudden terrible clarity that running was the one answer the thing below would not forgive.

"I'm listening," he said.

*"We keep the kingdom safe,"* the Choir said, warm, reasonable, vast. *"One hundred voices, one counsel, no succession crisis, no weak king, no civil war. Your valley forgets magic; we remember order. You of all creatures should understand—a gift is not a sin. It is only a tool. And tools may be shared."* A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. *"You hear what no one hears. Imagine what you could unbind, beast-speaker, if your gift were joined to ours. Every silenced thing in this kingdom, given voice at your command. We could make you loud. We could make you* heard.*"*

And there it was—the offer shaped precisely to the wound, the promise aimed at twenty-three years of hiding, of smallness, of conversations with beasts in the reeds because no human ear could be trusted. Lena's hands shook. Not with fear. With the awful recognition of how much he wanted it.

*"Or,"* the Choir continued, softer still, *"you may take the child's side. But know the cost of curiosity, little listener. The falconer reached where he should not have reached. Ask yourself why he no longer speaks."*

The world tilted. "The falconer—" Lena began.

*"Choose,"* said the Choir, and the hum resumed, closing over the silence like water, and somewhere in its turning Lena felt the child-thread flicker once—*run, Wren, please*—before the wheel swallowed it.

He fled the gallery with his heart slamming and the Choir's offer ringing in his skull louder than the bells, and he did not stop running until he reached the queen's rooms, where he pounded on the door with both fists in the dead hours of the night, and when Isolde opened it, candle in hand, her face went white at whatever mine must have looked like.

"Lena," she said. "What happened."

He steadied himself against the doorframe, gasping, and gave her the only truth that mattered, the one that would burn everything down by morning:

"They know about the falconer. And they know I've been talking to the child in the king."
