## 12. The Falconer's Silence

The morning the falconer did not come to the mews, the birds knew before anyone else.

They had always known things first—hunger, weather, the approach of a stranger on the gravel path—but this was different. This was a silence arriving in a place built of small sounds: the click of talons on oak perches, the soft rustle of feathers turning in sleep, the wet snap of a hood being fitted over a kestrel's eyes. When Tam Voss failed to appear at dawn, the gyrfalcon screamed once, twice, and then pressed herself flat against the back of her perch and would not be moved.

Lena found him at midmorning, after Isolde's summons came down through three servants and one very frightened page. The falconer's quarters sat at the edge of the outer courtyard, a low stone room that smelled of leather and blood and juniper smoke. Tam Voss was sitting at his workbench as though he had simply paused between tasks—a jess half-stitched in his lap, an awl resting against his palm.

He was alive. His chest rose and fell. His eyes were open, and they tracked Lena across the room with something that might have been recognition, or might have been only the reflex of a face that had forgotten how to do anything else.

"Tam?" Lena crouched in front of him. "Master Voss. It's Lena. Can you hear me?"

The old man's mouth moved. No sound came out. Not breath, not whisper, not the dry click of a tongue against teeth. His lips shaped something—once, twice—and then went still, and Lena understood with a cold sliding horror that there was nothing left in him to shape it *with*. He had heard this silence before. In the villages on the road to Corvane, in the branded mules with their mute, patient eyes. But those had been animals, and animals could not look at you while they suffered.

Tam Voss looked at him. And in the brown depths of the old man's gaze, Lena saw the same thing he'd seen in the mule's: a room from which all the furniture had been carried out, leaving only the shape of where things used to be.

The gyrfalcon screamed again from the mews across the yard, high and furious, and Lena felt the sound go through him like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

* * *

Isolde arrived like weather.

She came through the door without knocking, without ceremony, still wearing her riding gloves, and for a moment she only stood in the doorway looking at her falconer—the man who had taught her to fly her first bird when she was nine years old, who had held her wrist steady through every panic and every grief of twenty years of marriage to a hollowing king.

"Who," she said quietly, "was with him last?"

"No one, Majesty." The under-falconer, a boy of perhaps fifteen, stood gray-faced by the wall. "He dismissed us early. He said—" The boy swallowed. "He said he'd heard something singing in the well shafts, and he wanted to listen properly."

Isolde closed her eyes.

"He went down to the undercroft," she said. Not a question. "Alone. To listen."

"Majesty, he didn't say—"

"I know what he said." She opened her eyes, and Lena, watching her, saw the queen vanish behind something older and harder: a woman doing arithmetic with guilt. "He has been my ears in the dark places of this palace for six years. I asked him to listen for me. I asked, and I let him believe the risk was mine alone to weigh." Her voice did not shake. That was somehow worse. "The Choir does not punish, Master Nakamura. It *collects*. And it collects most eagerly from those who stand closest to its enemies."

"Then it knows about me," Lena said. "It knows we're listening."

"It has known since the night you spoke to the child-voice." Isolde pulled off her gloves, finger by finger, with terrible precision. "I told myself it was tolerating us. That a thing so old and so hungry would not bother crushing ants until the ants became worth the trouble." She set the gloves on the workbench beside Tam's unfinished stitching. "I was wrong. It wasn't tolerating us. It was choosing which of us to take first."

"Why him?" Mara asked from the doorway. He had hung back, as he often did in rooms full of grief, but now he stepped forward, and his voice had the careful flatness he used when he was frightened and refusing to show it. "Why not Lena? Why not me? We're the ones who broke into the tithe rolls. We're the ones who've been—"

"Because taking you would make martyrs of you," Isolde said. "And because Tam was *mine*. This is not strategy, Lord Mara. This is a message written in the only language the Choir respects." She looked down at her falconer's empty face. "*I can reach anyone. Even here. Even the ones who love you.*"

Mara's mouth opened, then closed. Lena watched him wrestle with something—pity, fear, the historian's instinct to find the pattern—and lose to all three at once.

"We should leave," Mara said.

The words dropped into the room like stones into still water.

"Corvane is lost to us now," he went on, faster, the flatness cracking. "Don't you see? It struck at the falconer to tell us it can touch whatever it wants inside these walls. Next it will take a maid, a guard, a cook—anyone we've spoken to, anyone who's helped us. It will peel this palace apart person by person until we're standing alone in a fortress of the voiceless, and then—" He stopped himself, but everyone heard the end of the sentence anyway. *And then it will take you, Lena, and there will be no one left to stop it.*

"And go where?" Isolde asked. Her voice was ice over deep water. "South, to my sister's court, where the Choir's reach ends at some border I have never seen tested? North, into the mountains, to live out our lives in a cave while Aldric—" She caught herself on the name, and for one unguarded moment her composure showed the fracture beneath: a hairline crack running straight through the word. "While my husband continues to be eaten alive from the inside."

"Yes," Mara said. "If the alternative is feeding it more people we care about."

"You speak like a man who has run before."

The silence that followed was absolute. Even the gyrfalcon, screaming distantly across the yard, seemed to hold its breath.

Mara's face went white, then carefully, deliberately blank. "Yes," he said. "I have. I ran from the Choir once, with a stolen book and my life, and I told myself it was courage. Perhaps it was. Perhaps it was only the same cowardice wearing better clothes." He turned to Lena, and his eyes were bright and terrible. "But I know what running costs, Your Majesty. I've paid it. Have you?"

"I have paid other things," Isolde said softly. "Things you cannot imagine. Do not lecture me on cost, archivist."

Lena had not spoken. He was still crouched in front of Tam Voss, and somewhere in the last minute his hand had found the old man's hand, and Tam's fingers had curled weakly around his—not gripping, just resting, the way a bird rests in a hooded calm, trusting the hands that hold it.

He thought about the valley. He thought about the border stones, and the wren, and twenty-five years of keeping his voice small enough to fit inside a hiding place. He thought about the mule on the road whose voice he had freed, and the soldiers' faces, and the way the world outside the valley was louder than he had ever imagined—full of voices, full of names, full of things waiting to be given back.

Then he stood up, and when he spoke, his own voice surprised him with its steadiness.

"No."

Mara blinked. "Lena—"

"We don't leave." Lena looked at him, and then at Isolde, and then back at the empty shell of the man in the chair. "You want to know why the Choir took Tam instead of me? You're both right—it was a message. But messages are for people who might change course. It thinks if it hurts enough people, I'll stop listening. It thinks fear works on me the way it worked on everyone it ever bound." He shook his head slowly. "It doesn't know me. It's spent a hundred years stealing voices, and it has never once met someone who spent his whole life learning how to hear them."

"The falconer is *empty*, Lena." Mara's voice cracked on the name. "That's what it does. That's what it will do to you, and no amount of hearing will—"

"His voice isn't gone." Lena said it quietly, and the room went still around the words. "It's *held*. There's a difference. I felt it when I touched him—there's a thread, faint, like a note sung very far away. The Choir keeps what it takes. It hoards. It doesn't destroy." He met Mara's eyes. "Which means everything it has ever taken can be given back. Including him. Including the child-voice. Including your family's name on that century-old roll, Mara. All of it. If I get to the root."

"If," Mara repeated. "And if you fail, it takes you too, and the prophecy dies with you, and the king dies, and every voice in that crown stays buried forever. I've read enough failed rebellions to know how they end, Lena. They end with everyone brave and everyone dead."

"Then teach me a rebellion that ends differently," Lena said. "You have the book. You have a hundred years of tithe rolls. You know the mechanism better than anyone alive. So use it. Help me. Because I am not leaving this palace while that man sits in that chair with his name locked in a stone throat beneath our feet." His voice roughened. "I spent my whole life invisible, Mara. Safe and small and silent. I'm not going back to that. Not for the Choir. Not for anyone."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Isolde crossed the room, took Tam Voss's hand from Lena's, and pressed it gently between her own.

"Six years ago," she said, to the falconer, to the room, to herself, "I found my husband humming a note in his sleep that I had never taught him and no living man had ever sung. And I made a choice to fight this thing quietly, in the shadows, alone, because I believed that was what loyalty required. Loyalty to the crown. Loyalty to Aldric. Loyalty to the kingdom that must never learn what wears its king." She laid the old man's hand back in his lap with unbearable tenderness. "Look what loyalty bought. A good man, emptied. A husband, devoured. A war fought in whispers that the enemy was always winning."

She straightened, and when she turned to face them, the queen was gone entirely; what remained was harder and younger and far more dangerous.

"So. New terms. No more whispers. No more shadows. If the Choir wants a war of voices, it will have one." She looked at Lena. "How long do you need to reach the undercroft root?"

"I don't know yet. Days. Maybe less. The falconer's training—I'll need the blindfold exercises, the thread-listening. Everything he taught me, I'll need it doubled, quickly."

"Then you'll have it. I flew his birds myself for ten years; I remember every lesson." She was already moving toward the door, snapping orders to the trembling under-falconer—double the watch, seal the well shafts, no one descends alone, no one at all goes near the throne wing—and then she paused, and looked back at Mara, and her voice gentled by a single degree.

"You suggested flight, Lord Mara. I do not scorn it. Fear is honest, and yours has earned its keep." A pause. "But stay. Please. Because a man who has run before, and chooses not to—that is worth more than ten who never knew the temptation."

Mara stood very still. Lena watched him look at Tam Voss—at the half-stitched jess in the old man's lap, the small unfinished work of a small unfinished life—and something in his face settled, the way sediment settles in shaken water.

"I stayed once before," he said finally. "In the Choir's cloisters. For eleven years. Staying is not automatically courage." He drew a breath. "But yes. I'll stay. Someone has to keep the records of what happens next, and I find I—" His voice caught, and he covered it with a thin smile. "I find I've grown attached to the subject matter."

* * *

That night, Lena went to the mews.

The gyrfalcon was awake, of course—her kind slept in fragments, always listening—and she watched him come down the row of perches with one fierce golden eye. He did not open her cage. He simply sat on the straw-covered floor beside it, cross-legged, the way he had sat beside a hundred animals in a hundred quiet places, and waited.

After a long time, she came to the bars.

*They took the song-giver,* she said. Her mind-voice was like wind over ice, cold and precise. *The one who whistled us up at grey dawn. The one whose hands smelled of hare and leather.*

"I know."

*Will you give it back?*

The question was so simple it went through him like an arrow. Outside, Corvane's bells began to ring the midnight hour, twelve bronze voices drowning—as they always drowned—the faint, wrong hum that rose from beneath the streets. But tonight, for the first time, Lena listened past the bells, past the hum, down into the deep stone throat of the palace, and found the thread he had felt in Tam Voss's hand: a single note, old and tired and stubbornly, beautifully itself, singing in a prison of a hundred stolen voices.

*I will try,* he told the falcon.

*Trying is what prey does,* she said, and pressed her head briefly against the bars, feather-soft, before returning to her perch. *Do not be prey.*

Lena sat alone in the dark of the mews a while longer, listening to the note in the deep, learning its shape. And far below, in the stone throat where a hundred bound voices circled like water around a drain, something ancient paused in its endless singing—and listened back.
