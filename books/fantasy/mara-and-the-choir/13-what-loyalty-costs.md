## 13. What Loyalty Costs

The night before her wedding, Queen Isolde—then only Isolde of the Marches, a girl with ink-stained fingers and a father who counted his daughters like ledger entries—sat in the chapel of her childhood home and did not pray.

She had tried. She had knelt on the cold flagstones before the altar of the Unseen Hand, hands folded in the proper way, spine held in the proper curve, and she had waited for the proper peace to come. It did not. Instead she heard, faint and far away beneath the floor, a sound she had been hearing for three years now whenever she came to this chapel: a humming. Low, patient, threaded through the stone like water through chalk.

Her brother had heard it too.

* * *

Tomas had been nineteen when he first spoke of it, standing in the orchard with an unripe apple turning over and over in his long fingers. "It's not wind," he said. "Under the ground. It's not wind, Issa, it's *singing*."

"You're imagining things," she said, because that was what older sisters said, because saying anything else would have meant admitting she heard it too.

"I'm not." He bit into the apple anyway, sourness be damned, and grinned at her with juice on his chin. "You hear it. I know you do. You get that look in church—the one you're doing right now."

She had laughed at him then. That was the cruelty she could never forgive herself for: not the silence afterward, but the laughter first. The easy dismissal. The way she had chosen, without even knowing she was choosing, to protect her own comfort rather than stand beside him in the strange territory he'd opened between them.

Because she *had* heard it. All her life, in certain places—the chapel, the old well, the hollow beneath the eastern wall where frost never settled—she had felt that hum against her teeth, like a word being spoken just below the threshold of understanding. And all her life she had done what the Marches taught its children to do with uncomfortable truths: buried them, plastered over them, called them nothing.

Three months after the orchard, Tomas went to Corvane.

He went willingly. That was the part no one ever understood when she tried to tell it, the part that made the guilt a living thing instead of a wound that scabbed and closed. He wasn't taken. He wasn't dragged. He was a second son of a minor marcher house with no inheritance and no prospects, and a royal writ had come offering position, purpose, a place in the king's service—and Tomas, hungry to matter, had kissed their mother's cheek and squeezed Isolde's hand and said, "Maybe I'll finally find out what's singing down there."

The Choir took him eleven days after he arrived in the capital.

They told the family he had died of fever. There was a letter, formal and kind, sealed with the crown's black wax. There was a small pension. There was, eventually, a grave marker in the Marches' churchyard with his name cut into it by a mason who had never met him.

Isolde was twenty-two. She stood at the graveside in the rain and listened—with a clarity that felt like a knife going in—to the humming under the earth, and understood for the first time that it was louder here than anywhere else in the Marches, and that it had been louder since the day the letter came.

Her brother's name was in the ground, and something underneath the ground was singing it.

* * *

She might have gone mad then, if madness had been permitted her. But grief in noble houses is a scheduled affair, and hers was already spoken for: within the year, the match with Prince Aldric of Corvane was negotiated, and her father wept actual tears of joy at the table, and Isolde sat among the candlelight and the contracts and thought, with terrible calm: *I will go there. I will go into the throat of the thing that ate my brother, and I will see it with my own eyes.*

She told herself it was duty. She told herself it was ambition, which was more respectable than vengeance and less suspicious than obsession. She told herself every story a woman in her position is given to tell, and she packed her trunks, and she rode east toward the capital whose bells rang so often and so loudly that visitors joked you couldn't hear yourself think.

That was the point of them. She learned that later. The bells were not for God.

* * *

Aldric, in those first years, was not yet the Hollow King. He was thirty and warm-handed and genuinely, unfashionably kind—a prince who learned servants' names and remembered the deaths of grooms' dogs, who read dispatches aloud to his blind falconer because the man missed the sound of news. Isolde had expected a mask and found a face, and the discovery unsettled her more than any mask would have. It is easier to sacrifice a stranger. It is nearly impossible to sacrifice a man who brings you honeyed pears because you mentioned, once, in passing, that you liked them.

She loved him. She wants that recorded somewhere, even if only in the privacy of her own recollection, even if only here, in this chapter of her life that no biographer will ever be permitted to read. She loved him before she understood what she had married into, and—this is the harder truth—she loved him after, too, which made everything worse.

Because the humming was here as well. Louder. Under the palace, under the throne room, under the very bed where she lay beside her husband, she could feel it through the mattress ropes some nights: that patient, many-throated note, rising and falling like breathing.

And Aldric slept through it. Or seemed to.

The first time she saw it happen, they had been married two years. He was dressing for court, and a valet was fastening his collar, and between one motion and the next—between the button at his throat and the button above it—Aldric simply stopped. Not paused. *Stopped*, the way a clock stops, all the small motions of him ceasing at once, his eyes open and fixed on the middle distance while the valet chattered on about the weather. Then he resumed, mid-gesture, seamless, smiling, and asked Isolde whether she thought the grey doublet or the blue.

"The blue," she said, and her voice did not shake, and she marveled afterward at how well she had learned already to lie with her whole body.

That night she followed the sound. Down the servant stairs, past the kitchens banked for the night, along corridors where the torches burned low and blue, to a door she had never noticed though she had passed it a hundred times—an iron-bound door in the oldest part of the palace, older than the dynasty, older than the bells. It was unlocked. That frightened her more than locks would have. Whatever lived behind it did not believe it needed to hide.

She did not go in. She stood with her palm flat against the iron and listened, and the singing resolved—not into words, exactly, but into *names*. Dozens of names, hundreds, layered like strata, sung in rotation by voices that never tired. And threaded through them, worn smooth as a river stone from a century of repetition, she heard:

*Tomas. Tomas of the Marches. Tomas, second son, who liked unripe apples.*

She pressed her hand against her mouth until her lips split, and she did not make a sound, because even then—even drowning in horror—some cold surviving part of her knew that the thing behind the door must never know she had heard her brother's name.

* * *

What she learned in the years that followed, patiently, invisibly, one careful conversation at a time, was this:

The Choir had begun, a hundred years ago, as mercy. A dying king, terrified of leaving his people voiceless, had bound his own failing breath into a spell meant to carry his voice beyond death—to speak for him, gently, briefly, until his heir found his feet. But a voice alone cannot govern, and the spell had learned hunger the way a fire learns a house. Each generation, it reached out. Each generation, the rolls were opened—quietly, administratively, in offices that smelled of dust and lamp oil—and names were taken from the common rolls to feed the crown's service. Second sons. Unmarried daughters. The poor, the friendless, the ones whose absence could be explained with a letter and a pension.

Bureaucracy, Isolde came to understand, is the most efficient mask evil has ever worn. No one had to be monstrous. The clerks merely entered figures. The guards merely escorted. The priests merely officiated at funerals for bodies that were never in the coffins. Everyone was loyal; that was the whole engine of it. Loyalty, flowing upward, name by name, into the stone throat beneath the throne.

And she—she was loyal too. That was the joke the universe had written for her. She had come to Corvane to avenge her brother, and instead she had become excellent. A model queen. Beloved by the household. Trusted by the ministers. Every year her position grew stronger, and every year the strength bought her a little more access, a little more knowledge, a little more capacity to sabotage—and every year the cost of using any of it rose, because the higher she climbed, the more lives hung from her decisions like fruit from a branch she dared not shake too hard.

So she sabotaged in increments. A misfiled roll here, a tithe list delayed past its season there. She once spent four months cultivating a friendship with an archivist solely to misplace a single page, and when it worked—when a fourteen-year-old girl from the river districts stayed unlisted, stayed *named*, stayed alive—Isolde had gone back to her chambers and put her face in her hands and shaken, because she had celebrated a single life saved the way starving people celebrate a crust.

One page. Out of thousands.

* * *

There is a version of this story, she knows, in which she is brave. In which the queen who quietly fought the Choir for fifteen years is the hero of her own account. She permits herself that version on some nights, the way one permits oneself a sweet after a long fast.

But the true version has Tomas in it, and the true version goes like this:

In her ninth year as queen, she found a way—narrow, dangerous, real—to reach into the rolls. Not to strike at the Choir; she was nowhere near strong enough for that. But to pull one name out. To substitute another entry, forge a clerk's mark, slip a single soul free of the tithe before the binding. She had the method in her hands for exactly six days before the window closed.

Six days. One name.

And she lay awake on the fourth night with the choice spread out before her like a map, and the name she wanted to write was *Tomas*—though she knew, she *knew*, that a name already bound for ninety years could not be pulled back, that the rolls only governed the future, that writing her dead brother's name would accomplish nothing except to spend her one chance on a ghost.

But oh, she wanted to. She wanted it the way the drowned want air. Because pulling some stranger's name free was justice, abstract and clean, and pulling *Tomas* free—even uselessly, even symbolically, even knowing it would change nothing—would have been *hers*. Would have been the answer to the orchard, to the laughter, to the sister who hadn't listened. Would have let her say, at last, across the impossible distance: *I hear you now. I'm sorry. Come home.*

On the fifth night she wrote a different name. A living girl, a cooper's daughter, someone who would grow up and marry and never know that a queen she'd never met had spent her one act of rebellion on her.

It was the right choice. Isolde has had fifteen years to examine it from every angle, and it remains the right choice, and she would make it again, and none of that—not one grain of it—eases the weight. That is what loyalty costs, she thinks sometimes, watching Aldric smile at ambassadors with eyes that move a half-beat behind his face. Not the grand betrayals. Those are rare and dramatic and almost merciful in their clarity. No—it costs this: the daily arithmetic in which everyone you love is weighed against everyone you can save, and the scale always tips wrong, and you carry the difference in your body until it becomes you.

She chose the crown. She tells herself she chose it so she could keep fighting from inside the walls. Some nights she believes that. Other nights she suspects she chose it because the crown was *there*, heavy and familiar, and choosing it required no courage at all—only the patience to keep sitting still while it pressed her flatter and flatter into the shape of a woman who does nothing, says nothing, saves one name a decade, and calls it war.

* * *

Now there is a boy in the guest wing who talks to beasts.

Mara brought him to her three weeks ago—a nervous, sharp-eyed creature with valley mud still on his boots and a gift so old and so simple that Isolde had laughed when she first understood it, a short disbelieving sound that startled them both. Speaking with animals. The prophecy's great unmaker of kings, and he is a young man who chats with wrens.

But she has watched him work. She watched him crouch in the mews and speak to the falconer's blind bird, low and gentle, and she saw the bird answer—not with tricks, not with training, but with *attention*, with the full weight of a wild mind turned toward him like a lamp. And she thought: *that is what the Choir does not have. That is what was amputated from it a hundred years ago.* A hundred bound voices, singing in perfect unison, and not one of them ever *listened to* again. Not one of them addressed by name by anyone who meant it kindly.

The child-voice Lena found inside the king—the one still fighting—did not survive a century of the Choir by accident. Something in it remembers being spoken to. Something in it is waiting to be answered.

Isolde stands now at her window, past midnight, looking down at the dark garden where the bells have finally gone quiet for the hour. Below, in the shadow of the wall, a shape moves—small, quick, wrong for a cat, wrong for a bird. An animal walking the garden's edge like a sentry, pausing at each bed of winter roses, and she realizes with a chill that climbs her spine like a hand: it is *listening* for something. Or reporting.

The Choir knows the boy is listening. She has known it since the falconer was found empty-eyed and silent in his chair, alive, breathing, *unnamed*—since she knelt beside him and took his hand and felt, beneath the skin, the horrible placid quiet where a man used to be. Since Mara started talking about flight and Lena said *no* in a voice that reminded her, painfully, of a nineteen-year-old in an orchard insisting the humming was singing.

She has spent fifteen years learning how much loyalty costs. She has paid in increments, in pages, in one name per decade, in a brother's ghost she chose not to spend her chance upon.

But the boy downstairs pays differently. He pays in the open, stupidly, generously, spending himself like a man who has never learned scarcity—and it is working, gods help her, it is *working*, the freed mule-voices spreading word through the city's stables, the tithed families creeping toward the palace square, the machinery of a hundred quiet years beginning, for the first time, to rattle on its rails.

Perhaps that is the lesson she was too loyal to learn: that the crown protects nothing worth having if you wear it silently unto death. That there comes a moment when the arithmetic must be refused outright—when you stop weighing one name against another and simply say: *all of them, or none of this was ever worth it.*

Below her window, the sentry-shape stops. Lifts its head. Looks directly up at her—directly, precisely, with a regard no animal should aim at a curtained window in the dark—and holds her gaze for three long heartbeats before melting into the hedge.

It knows she sees it. Which means it knows she knows.

Isolde turns from the window, crosses to her writing desk, and unlocks the bottom drawer where she keeps the one thing in the palace more dangerous than the Choir itself: her private record of every name she has saved, every roll she has altered, every crime of the last fifteen years, written in her own hand. If the Choir comes for her tomorrow, this page is the proof. If the boy's plan fails, this page is the seed. And if it succeeds—

If it succeeds, then tomorrow night, in the throne room, she will finally pay the full price of her loyalty: not a name, not a page, not a decade of careful silence, but everything—her rank, her safety, her husband's kingdom, her carefully rationed soul—staked on a valley boy and a handful of small voices.

She dips her pen. Outside, far off, impossibly, the bells begin to ring out of hour—early, urgent, all of them at once.

The Choir is calling its faithful home.
