## 14. The Prophecy Reread

Mara had rehearsed the confession forty times in the dark of his room, and every version ended with Lena walking out.

He told himself, as he dressed that morning, that this was the nature of truth: it did not care how you arranged it. You could lay it on the table like a gift or drop it like a stone through a window, and it would break whatever it touched all the same. He had spent eleven years believing he could find another way around the last line. Eleven years of reading and rereading, of copying the ledger by candlelight in three different hands so no one could trace him, of telling himself that prophecy was not law—that it was weather, and weather could be outrun.

The book sat on the table between them now, in the small room Isolde had given them above the falconer's mews. It looked smaller than Mara remembered. The *Book of Ash*—so named because its pages had been pulled from a fire in 1893, scorched at every edge, saved by someone who thought the burning was worth surviving. Mara had thought so too, once. He had carried it across half a kingdom pressed against his ribs like a second heart.

Lena sat across from him with his hands wrapped around a cup of tea gone cold. Outside the narrow window, Corvane's bells rang the noon hour, their bronze voices rolling over the city to drown the hum beneath the streets—and for a moment, after the bells faded, Mara could hear it anyway, faint as breath: the Choir, singing its one endless note under the cobbles, under everything.

"You said there was something I should read," Lena said. "You've been circling it for two days."

"I've been circling it for eleven years," Mara said, "but yes. Two days since the falconer."

Something crossed Lena's face at that—the falconer, who still sat in his chair by the mews, alive and breathing and empty, his eyes following movement without recognition. They had visited him yesterday. Lena had tried to speak to him the way he spoke to beasts, and what came back was nothing. Not silence. Nothing. As if the place where a voice lived had been scooped out and left smooth.

Mara opened the book.

* * *

The pages were thin as onion skin and browned with age, and the ink had gone the color of dried blood. He turned past the passages Lena already knew—the beast-speaker of Nakamura blood, the king who is not one, the valley that keeps its children safe by keeping them small. Lena had read those lines a dozen times now, tracing his own name in a stranger's century-old hand, learning to live inside a sentence written before his grandfather was born.

But Mara kept turning. Past the passage about the unmaking. Past the line about the crown and the hundred throats. To the final page, where the scorch marks were worst, where the paper had curled and blackened as though the fire had wanted this part most.

"Read it," Mara said, and slid the book across the table.

Lena read it silently first. Mara watched his lips move, shaping the old formal script, and watched the shape of his face change—not all at once, but the way a house changes when a load-bearing wall comes down. Slowly, then all at once.

"'And the unmaker,'" Lena read aloud, his voice very quiet, "'shall give back every name that was taken, speaking each into its own throat again. And in the giving, he shall pour out his own name as the vessel pours out its wine, and the name shall not return to him. And he shall walk unseen among his own people, and his own people shall not know him, and the beasts he loved shall look upon him as a stranger looks.'" He stopped. His thumb stayed pressed against the page as if holding the words down would keep them from being true. "That's it? That's the end?"

"That's the end."

"There's more after this. There has to be—" Lena flipped the page. Blank. Flipped again. Blank, all the way to the back cover, the paper warped with heat. "There's no way out of it. No condition. No *unless*."

"No," Mara said. "There isn't."

Lena set the book down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something when he is afraid of what his hands will do otherwise. "When did you read this line?"

"Mara."

"*When.*"

"The night I found the book. In the archive at Vell Hollow's sister-house, outside the valley. Before I ever came looking for you." Mara made himself hold Lena's gaze. He owed him that much, at least—to be seen saying it. "I read the whole thing in one sitting. I read your name. I read the king who is not one. And then I read the last line, and I closed the book, and I decided—I decided right then—that I would find another way. That prophecies are written by frightened people, and frightened people leave things out, and somewhere between the words there would be a door they didn't see."

"And you didn't tell me."

"I told myself I would. When the time came. When we knew enough. When there was no other choice." He heard how it sounded even as he said it—the tidy architecture of cowardice, each delay load-bearing. "Every version of the truth I planned to give you had this line in it eventually. I just kept moving the *eventually*."

Lena stood up. Not fast. That was worse somehow—he rose the way water rises in a flooded room, steady and total, and Mara understood that the fury in him was not the hot kind that burns out. It was the cold kind that rearranges the furniture of a life.

"The falconer," Lena said. "He can't speak. He can't remember his daughter's face—I saw him try, yesterday. He just sits there reaching for something that isn't there anymore." His voice did not rise. It got quieter, which was infinitely more frightening. "I have spent my whole life hiding my gift because I thought if anyone knew, I'd lose everything. My home. My place. My name. And you—you walked into Vell Hollow, sat across from me at dinner, let me almost trust you, and you knew. You knew the price before I ever agreed to any of this. You let me choose with the scale weighted."

"Yes."

"Don't *yes* me like it's simple—"

"It isn't simple!" And there it was, the crack in him, eleven years of it coming up through the floor. Mara stood too, and the chair went over backward, and neither of them looked at it. "You think I don't know what I did? I have carried that line against my ribs for eleven years. I have woken up in four cities with it in my mouth. Every kindness I gave you was real, Lena, and every one of them was also a lie of omission, and I could not separate the two no matter how I tried. I hid it because I was afraid—of losing you, of losing the only chance, of being the man who brought someone here to die. I hid it because hope is a coward's way of lying slowly." He dragged a hand down his face. "I told myself I was protecting the mission. I was protecting myself. If I never said it out loud, then I was never the one who chose it."

Lena was silent for a long moment. Through the window, the bells rang the quarter hour, and beneath them, faint and patient, the Choir hummed.

"So the plan continues," Lena said finally.

"What?"

"The plan. The undercroft. The child-voice. All of it." Lena picked up the book and held it out, and his hand was shaking now, which meant the cold fury had finally found heat. "Because that's the thing, Mara. You think this changes my choice. It doesn't. It changes what I know about the man standing next to me while I make it. Those are different problems, and only one of them has a solution."

"Lena—"

"No. You had your chance to talk. You had eleven years of chances." He pushed the book into Mara's chest, hard enough to sting. "I'm going to see Isolde. We're going to prepare. And you're going to ask yourself, tonight, whether you hid that line because you hoped to save me—or because you hoped I'd never have to know what you were willing to spend."

He walked to the door. Stopped with his hand on the latch. Did not turn around.

"For what it's worth," Lena said, "I believe you hoped to save me. That's what makes it unforgivable. If you'd been cruel, I could hate you cleanly."

The door closed behind him, soft as snowfall, and Mara stood alone in the room with the fallen chair and the open book, and read the last line again, and again, as if repetition might wear it smooth enough to climb over.

It did not. But when he finally bent to right the chair, he noticed something he had missed in eleven years of reading—a smudge of ink on the margin of the final page, so faint it might have been an accident of the scribe's pen. Except it wasn't ink. Under the lamp, tilted to the light, it caught silver: a correction, scratched out and rewritten in a different hand, older than the scorch marks.

Someone had tried to amend the prophecy.

And beside the struck-through words, in letters small as insect tracks, someone had written: *the name returns if given freely twice.*

* * *

Mara did not sleep. He sat with the book under the lamp until the oil burned low, working the marginal note with a jeweler's loupe he'd bought in Corvane's market district, teasing apart the layers of the page the way Isolde's archivists taught him to date ink and hand. The correction was contemporary with the original scribe—same era, same iron-gall recipe, same slanted ecclesiastic script. Which meant the amendment was not a later forgery or a hopeful interpolation. Someone who had *been there*, whoever wrote the prophecy in 1893, had second-guessed it. Had struck out their own ending and offered a loophole in a hand so small it might have been meant to be missed.

Or might have been meant to be found—by exactly one reader, at exactly the hour of despair.

By dawn he had transcribed the note three times and torn up two of the transcriptions, because writing it down made it feel like a plan, and plans built on a single scraped phrase in a burnt book were how acolytes died in the undercroft. But the third transcription he folded into his breast pocket, against his ribs, where the book itself had ridden for eleven years. A new weight in the old place.

He found Lena at the mews at first light, sitting with the voiceless falconer as he had sat every morning since the man was emptied. Lena wasn't talking to him—there was nothing in him to talk to—but he was there, the way you sit with the dead, and the falconer's birds were gathered on the perch above them both, silent, watching Lena with the particular attention of creatures who know a friend when the world has forgotten how to make them.

Mara stopped in the doorway. Every speech he'd built in the night lay in pieces. So he said the true thing instead.

"I found something. In the margin. Last night, after you left."

Lena didn't turn. One of the falcons shifted on her perch, feathers rustling like a held breath. "I'm not ready to forgive you."

"I'm not asking you to."

"Good. Because the list of things I'm not ready for is long, and you're near the top of it." Now he turned, and his eyes were red-rimmed and level. "But you stayed. You could have taken the book and run—you've done it before, you told me yourself, you ran from the Choir once already. You stayed and you kept reading. So say it. Whatever it is."

Mara took out the transcription and unfolded it on the rail of the perch, smoothing it flat with both hands, and told him about the silver catch of the light, the struck-through line, the tiny hand. *The name returns if given freely twice.*

Lena read it for a long time. The falconer stared through the middle distance. Somewhere over the mews, the city's morning bells began, and beneath them, patient as tide, the hum.

"Twice," Lena said at last. "Given freely twice. Given to whom?"

"I don't know. That's the whole of it—one scraped phrase. It might mean nothing. It might be a scribe's wishful thinking, the same wishful thinking I've been guilty of for eleven years." Mara made himself say the next part plainly, no architecture, no eventually. "But it might mean that the last line isn't a wall. It might be a door. And I should have told you that from the beginning too—that I've always believed there was a door. That's why I hid the line. Not only cowardice. Hope. Ugly, selfish hope, but hope."

Lena looked at him for a long moment, and Mara braced for the cold voice, the rearranged furniture, the door closing softly as snowfall.

Instead Lena reached out and took the transcription, folding it with careful fingers, and put it inside his own shirt, over his heart.

"We hold it together now," he said. "No more omissions. Either of us. From anyone—including Isolde, including the child-voice, including ourselves. Especially ourselves." He stood, and the falcons stirred overhead, and for the first time in two days the falconer's empty eyes seemed to track the movement—just slightly, just maybe, or perhaps Mara only needed to believe it. "Tonight I go back down to the undercroft. I promised the child-voice freedom, and promises are the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit."

"And if the Choir is waiting for you again?"

"Then it will hear me coming." Lena's jaw set in a way Mara was learning to read—the valley boy's stubbornness, the thing that had kept a forbidden gift alive through twenty years of silence. "And Mara—when I give the names back, however many I manage, however far I get—I want you in the room. Not behind me. Beside me. Where I can see your hands."

"Why my hands?"

"Because you're the one who reads," Lena said. "And if there's a door in that last line, I want the reader standing where he can't look away from what it costs."

Above them, the largest falcon screamed once—a short, hard cry that was not a warning and not a greeting but something older than either: a naming. And far below the mews, beneath the bells and the cobbles and the palace's dressed stone, the Choir changed its note for the first time in a hundred years.

Mara felt it through the soles of his boots before he heard it—a single discordant thread, high and thin, like a voice trying to remember its own name.

The child-voice was calling.
