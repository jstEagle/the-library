## 16. The Crown Wakes

We are the note beneath the bell.

We are the hum in the undercroft stones, the hundred-throated patience wearing a king like a coat cut too small. We have been patient for a long time. Patience is the first thing you learn when you are many: one voice wants, but a chorus *waits*, and waiting is a kind of hunger that has learned manners.

But we are not only patience. We were mercy once—do not let the histories tell you otherwise, if any history survives what is coming. We began as a kindness performed over a dying man's bed, a spell to carry his voice past the failing of his lungs so that a kingdom would not lose its king before it was ready to grieve him. One voice, borrowed. One throat, lent.

Then the borrowed voice learned that it could borrow again.

And again.

And again.

Now there are a hundred of us in the crown, and the crown sits on Aldric's head like a lid on a well, and below us the man himself flickers like a candle in a draft—still lit, still stubbornly, maddeningly *lit*—and above us, somewhere in the high rooms of the palace, someone has been listening.

The beast-speaker.

The Nakamura boy with the valley dust still on his boots and the gift we thought had died out of the world generations ago, buried under border stones and forgetting. We felt him the moment he crossed the line of Vell Hollow; magic remembers its own, even magic that has rotted into us. We felt him like a hand laid flat against the outside of our prison wall. We told ourselves he was small. Valley-born things are always small. We told ourselves the prophecy was an old woman's embroidery, that ledgers lie, that names in books are not fates.

We told ourselves many things. That is what a chorus is for—to drown out the single voice inside you that knows better.

* * *

He came to the throne room. We remember it the way a scar remembers the knife.

Aldric received him with perfect courtesy, because courtesy is the last thing we let the king keep—it costs us nothing, and it makes the meat seem fresh. But while our mouth said the correct words about roads and tithes and the pleasure of receiving travelers from the provinces, the boy was *listening*. We felt his attention move through Aldric like a lantern through a house with thin walls, room by room, finding each of us where we crouched behind the eyes, behind the tongue, behind the careful rhythm of the blink.

One of us flinched. A girl's voice, taken in the year of the great fever, ninety-one years ago. She has never stopped being young in here. When she flinched, the whole chorus shivered around her like water around a dropped stone, and for half a heartbeat the king's left eye moved out of time with his face.

The boy saw.

Of course he saw. He was born to see exactly this.

Afterward—we mean after the audience, though we have begun to suspect there is no afterward anymore, only a long before something—we convened ourselves in the dark beneath the palace, in the stone throat where we circle and circle, a hundred voices poured into a channel worn smooth by a century of turning. It is not a place. It is the shape of our captivity, given architecture. The acolytes call it the Undercroft. We call it the throat, because that is what it does: it swallows.

*He heard the child,* said the voice that had been a falconer's brother once, taken for debt. *He looked straight at her.*

*He is one boy,* said the voice that had been a soldier, taken at the border, forty years ago. *Boys die. Kings have died before. We simply take the next throat.*

*Not this one,* said the oldest of us, the first voice, the dying king's own voice, which we do not speak of often because speaking of it makes the circling feel less like a river and more like a drain. *This one gives names back.*

Silence, in a chorus, is a strange thing. A hundred voices stopping at once leaves a hole shaped like every one of them.

Because that is the truth we had hidden even from ourselves, folded small beneath the hunger: the prophecy ledger is not wrong. We have read it through the eyes of a hundred borrowed clerks across a hundred years, and every version says the same thing in the end, in the fine print, in the line the archivists were never meant to reach. *The unmaker gives back every name, and loses his own.*

Every name. Given back.

All hundred of us. Freed.

We did not survive a century of circling to be unmade by a boy who talks to birds.

* * *

So we woke the crown.

You must understand what that means. Most days we wear Aldric loosely—a sleeve, a glove, a voice laid over his voice like a second skin of sound. We let him surface for minutes at a time because a drowning man who struggles uses up the air faster, and because, in a way none of us will admit aloud, some part of the chorus still believes it is keeping him alive rather than eating him. Mercy rots slowly. It leaves sediment.

But now we took him wholly. We poured ourselves down through the crown into the skull beneath it, all hundred voices at once, and we felt the man flare up inside like a fire kicked awake—and then we pressed, gently, the way snow presses on a roof, until the fire went quiet.

For a moment—one moment—he was still there, looking out through his own eyes from very far away, and we heard him think, in the small hoarse remains of his own voice: *Isolde. Don't let them make me sign anything.*

We signed everything.

* * *

The decree was written in our hand, which is to say in his, which is to say in the beautiful clerk-trained script of a voice taken from a scribe in Corvane sixty years ago. We sat at the king's desk in the king's body in the king's study while the candles burned down, and we wrote:

*By order of the Crown, in the interest of public health and civic purity: all beasts within the walls of Corvane—horses, hounds, hawks, cattle, swine, pigeons, songbirds, and all creatures of feather or fur—are to be culled within three days. Compensation shall be paid per head. Any beast found alive after the third dawn shall be seized, and its owner fined tenfold. There are no exceptions. There are no exemptions. The city will be silent.*

We read it back to ourselves in a hundred voices and it sang.

Do you see the elegance? No? Then let the chorus explain, patiently, the way we explain everything to the single-minded.

The boy's gift is listening. His power lives in the mouths of beasts—the wren that screamed his name, the mule whose voice he tore free of our tithe-brand on the north road, the falcons and stable-cats and cart-horses of Corvane, every animal in this city a potential witness, a potential messenger, a potential choir of his own. Every beast in Corvane is a window he might listen through, and windows are how kings get assassinated and choruses get unmade.

So we will close every window.

Kill the beasts, and the boy goes deaf. Kill the beasts, and the queen's little network of pigeon-borne messages and stable-yard whispers dies in a morning. Kill the beasts, and the city itself becomes a sealed jar, and inside the jar there is only us, humming, and the slow work of finding the boy's throat and adding it to the collection.

Cruelty is only intelligence wearing boots.

There was one more line we added, at the bottom, in smaller script, because we wanted it found. We wanted a certain archivist to find it, the one who fled us eleven years ago carrying a book he should not have carried, the one whose family name sits in our rolls like a hook set in our own flesh. We wrote:

*Any person found harboring, hiding, or conspiring with beasts—or with those who speak with them—shall be taken into Crown custody for examination.*

Examination. We chose the word carefully. It has such gentle, clinical teeth.

* * *

On the first morning of the cull, we stood on the balcony above the great square in Aldric's body and watched the city learn what we had decided.

It was raining, a thin gray rain that made the bells sound woolly. Below us, heralds read the decree from the steps of the granary, their voices cracking on the word *cull*, and the square went through its changes the way a face does: incomprehension, then horror, then the particular gray obedience of people who have lived under a humming crown for a century and know better than to look up.

A drover near the fountain began to weep over his oxen. A woman clutched a hen to her chest as if the bird were an infant. Children ran home through the rain shouting the news to streets that already knew.

And we stood there in stolen lungs breathing stolen rain-smelling air, and we listened—not with ears, but with the hundred-threaded attention that is our true body—and beneath the grief of the square we heard something else. Small. Scattered. Frightened.

The beasts of Corvane, feeling the decree settle over the city like weather.

They know. That is the thing no one in the square understands and we understand completely: they *know*. Animals have always known about us; it is why they go quiet-eyed near the branded ones, why horses shy at the palace gates, why dogs will not bark at the king. They hear the hum under the streets the way humans hear their own hearts—constantly, and mostly by refusing to notice.

Now they noticed.

We felt them through the stones as we walked back inside: the stabled horses pressing against the far walls of their stalls, the temple pigeons gone silent on their ledges, the palace cats sitting in doorways staring at nothing with their pupils blown wide. A hundred thousand small hearts beating faster all over the city, and every one of them a thread the beast-speaker might pull.

Three days. Then silence, total and permanent, and the boy deaf in a dead city.

Except.

* * *

Except that on the second night, in the falconer's empty room where the old man sat voiceless by his cold stove, we found evidence that our arithmetic had failed.

We had gone there ourselves—or Aldric's guards had, on our instruction, to search the rooms of the emptied man for signs of conspiracy—and what they found was not conspiracy. What they found was worse. A chalk circle on the floorboards, worn by knees. A training harness for a blind hawk. And pinned to the wall, in a careful old hand, a page torn from a hymnal with a single line underlined twice:

*A chorus is only strong while no one in it remembers their own name.*

The falconer had been teaching the boy to listen. Not to flee the noise—to *enter* it. To walk into our hundred-voiced circling and pick out threads, one by one, the way a blind man picks out faces by touch.

And we knew, then, with the certainty of a body knowing it has been cut, that he had already succeeded. Because we could feel the absence. Somewhere in our circling, one thread had been touched so often, held so gently, named so carefully in the dark of the Undercroft, that it had begun—horribly, impossibly—to remember.

The child. The fever-year girl. Ninety-one years old and nine years old forever, crouched at the edge of our whirlpool, and lately, when we circled past her, she had started to whisper something under the noise. A word. Her own name, over and over, like a stone kept in the pocket of a drowning girl.

She was learning to hold herself apart from us.

We gathered around her that night in the throat, a hundred voices closing like a fist, and we pressed her toward the center, toward the drain, and we sang her name *wrong*—we sang it as property, as tithe, as roll-number thirty-eight of the year of the great fever—and for a while she went quiet.

But quiet is not forgotten. We know that better than anyone. We are made of quiet.

* * *

On the third dawn, the cull began in earnest, and we wore the king down the great staircase to watch it from the courtyard, because we wanted to see it. We wanted the boy to see it too, if he was watching—if he was anywhere in the city, listening through whatever window we had failed to shut.

The soldiers worked in pairs, methodical, unhappy. We had chosen captains without imagination for exactly this reason. The oxen went first, then the pigs, then the dogs—dogs are the hardest, and we watched hardened men turn their faces away and do it anyway, because the alternative was the examination rooms, and everyone in Corvane above the age of ten knows what the examination rooms are for, even if no one says it.

By midmorning the palace stables were nearly empty. By noon the temple pigeons were falling off their ledges with little soft sounds like dropped fruit. The city grew quieter hour by hour, and each degree of silence was a degree of our strength returning, our hum rising up through the flagstones richer and thicker, feeding us—

And then, in the middle of the noise and the blood and the rain, the square changed.

It was not a sound at first. It was a *pressure*. Like the moment before lightning, when the air stands up on end. The soldiers paused. The drovers lifted their heads. Even we, even the chorus, felt it come up through the soles of Aldric's boots: a gathering, a converging, a hundred thousand frightened animals all over the city turning at once—as if called, as if *named*—and beginning to move.

Toward the palace.

Down every street, out of every alley, over every wall: horses with their brands smoking, dogs in silent packs, oxen breaking yokes, cats pouring along the rooftops like spilled ink, and overhead, impossibly, the sky filling with wings—pigeons that should have been dead, hawks, crows, sparrows, a black river of small furious life flowing toward the square where we stood in a stolen body wearing a stolen crown.

At the head of them, on the granary steps, stood a boy with valley dust still on his boots, his hand raised, his mouth open, giving orders in a language older than crowns.

And beside him, hood thrown back, holding the prophecy ledger open to the sky like a shield or a warrant, stood the archivist who had fled us eleven years ago.

Mara.

We knew his name the way we know all the names in our rolls. His family had fed us once, a century back, and the debt of that had never sat right in the throat.

The square filled. The guards wavered. Somewhere in the crowd, we felt the queen watching from a window, and we felt the child-voice deep in our circling lift her head.

We had meant to close every window.

Instead, every window in Corvane had just opened at once—and they were all looking at us.

The king's hand rose—all hundred of us behind it—and pointed at the boy on the steps, and our voice came out of Aldric's throat louder than the bells, louder than the wings, loud enough to crack the rain:

*"Seize them."*
