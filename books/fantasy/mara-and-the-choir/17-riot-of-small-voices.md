## 17. Riot of Small Voices

The decree was nailed to the palace gates at dawn, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern towers, criers were shouting it through every street of Corvane: *By order of His Majesty, all beasts within the city walls—dogs, cats, birds, mules, swine—are to be gathered in the great square and put to death before nightfall.*

Lena heard it before he saw it. He had been awake since the small hours, lying on a pallet in the queen's apartments with his eyes open and his ears full of the city's animals—the way he had learned to listen now, not as one voice among many but as ten thousand threads, each one distinct if he held it gently enough. And every thread in Corvane had gone taut with terror at once, like a harp struck by a fist.

He sat up so fast that Isolde's falconer, dozing in the corner chair, startled awake.

"They know," Lena said. "They've known since I spoke to the child-voice. This is what they're doing about it."

The old man's face, seamed and blind-eyed, turned toward him with terrible calm. "Then you'd best be quick, boy. The square fills by noon."

* * *

Isolde received them in her private study, where the shutters were closed against the morning and a single lamp burned even though it was day. She stood at the window with her back to them, and when she turned, Lena saw that she had been crying—not recently, but for years, the kind of crying that leaves its sediment in the lines of a face.

"Aldric signed it," she said. "His hand. His seal. He sat at his desk this morning and wrote out the order for the slaughter of every living creature in his city, and when he finished, he looked up at me and smiled, and his smile came a half-second after his eyes." Her voice did not shake. That was the worst part. It was worn smooth, like a stone in a riverbed. "I have watched that thing wear my husband for six years. I have made my peace with watching. But this—" She pressed her palm flat against the desk, as though steadying herself against a wind only she could feel. "This is not strategy anymore. This is spite. It knows what you are, Master Nakamura. It knows the beasts are your witnesses, your allies, your army. So it means to take them from you before you can use them."

"Then we don't let it," Mara said.

He stood by the door with the prophecy book under his arm—he carried it everywhere now, like a shield, or a wound—and there was something in his face Lena had never seen before. Not the archivist's careful charm. Not the acolyte's buried shame. Something harder and brighter, the look of a man who has finally stopped calculating and started choosing.

"You have a plan," Isolde said. It was not quite a question.

"I have an archive," Mara said. "A hundred years of tithe rolls. Every family in this city that ever gave a name to the crown. Do you understand what that is? Those people's grandmothers and grandfathers didn't die—they were *bound*. Their voices went into the Choir, and their descendants grew up thinking their bloodlines simply ended, thinking themselves unlucky, unloved by history. But the names are still in those rolls. Written down. Recorded." He laid his hand flat on the book's cracked leather cover. "And names can be spoken."

Lena felt it before he understood it—a shiver running outward from his own chest, the gift waking like a muscle remembering itself. "You want me to read the rolls aloud."

"I want you to give them back," Mara said. "Not just the hundred voices in the crown. All of them. Every name the Choir ever took, spoken in the square where their families can hear it. The Choir's power isn't just the binding—it's the silence around the binding. A hundred years of people who never knew what happened to their kin. Break the silence, and you break the fear. Break the fear, and the cull becomes impossible, because you cannot slaughter beasts in a square full of ten thousand people who have just remembered whose voices those beasts might carry."

"It will be chaos," Isolde said.

"Yes," said Mara.

"It may be the end of the monarchy."

"It may be the beginning of something else," said Lena, and was surprised to hear his own voice come out steady.

Isolde looked between them for a long moment. Then she crossed to the wall behind her desk, pressed a hidden catch, and opened a compartment Lena had passed a dozen times without seeing. Inside lay a bundle of papers tied with black ribbon, and beneath the papers, a sword—an old one, plain-hilted, the kind a soldier keeps, not a king.

"My husband's grandfather rode with the valley levies," she said. "Before Vell Hollow closed its borders. Before any of this." She untied the ribbon with fingers that trembled only slightly. "There are guards in this palace who remember oaths older than the Choir. There are servants who have whispered to each other in the dark for years, waiting for someone to give them permission. I have spent six years being careful, and careful has bought us nothing but time, and time is what the thing in the crown eats." She set the papers on the desk. "Tell me what you need."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the city died around them by degrees.

From the study window Lena could see the processions moving through the streets—soldiers going door to door with carts, taking dogs off their chains, cats from their windowsills, geese from their pens. The people handed their animals over with the numb obedience of the long-oppressed, and the animals went quietly too, and that quiet was the most horrifying thing Lena had ever witnessed. In Vell Hollow no creature would have gone silent to its own death. Here, the silence had been bred into them, generation upon generation, until terror itself had no voice.

But not entirely. As he listened—really listened, the way the blind falconer had taught him, sinking down through the noise of the city thread by thread—he began to find the exceptions. A dog in the Tanner's Row who growled low and continuous, a sound like a banked fire. A mule in the market district who had already kicked loose two cart-pins. Sparrows over the rooftops moving in patterns too coordinated, too deliberate, wheeling above the soldiers' heads and dropping dung on the decrees nailed to the posts.

*They're waiting,* Lena realized, wonder rising in him like water in a well. *They're waiting for a signal. They've been waiting for years.*

"The tithe rolls," he said, turning from the window. "How many names?"

Mara looked up from the table where he'd spread the archive pages in overlapping fans, his ink-stained fingers moving across them faster than seemed possible. "Four hundred and eleven bound in the first century. Perhaps three hundred more since. But the descendant families—" he tapped a page covered in his own quick annotations, circles and lines connecting names across generations like a spiderweb of grief—"the descendant families number in the thousands. The Weavers' Guild descends from a bound voice. Half the fishmongers of the Salt Quarter. Two of the palace guard standing at the gate right now, if my reading of the marriage records is correct."

"And they don't know."

"They don't know. They think their great-grandparents emigrated, or died in the fever years, or simply vanished, the way the poor always vanish." Mara's jaw tightened. "The Choir was clever. It never took names from the powerful. It took them from people no one would miss—which meant it built itself out of exactly the people the kingdom was already pretending weren't there." He looked up, and for a moment his archivist's mask slipped entirely, and underneath it Lena saw something raw and old. "That's why I ran, all those years ago. Not because I feared the Choir. Because I understood it. I understood how easy it is to make people disappear by deciding, quietly, politely, that they were never really there." His eyes found Lena's. "It's what I did to you, when I first came to Vell Hollow. I decided you were a prophecy, not a person. I'm sorry. I need you to know that before today, because today I'm going to ask you to trust me with everything."

"What are you going to do?" Lena asked slowly.

Mara smiled, and it was the smile from their first dinner in the valley, warm and crooked and utterly disarming, and now Lena knew it for what it had always been: armor, worn by a man who had learned young that charm kept you alive.

"I'm going to be visible," he said. "Very, very visible. Loudly, publicly, catastrophically visible."

* * *

At midday the bells of Corvane began to ring—not the hourly pattern, but all of them at once, every bell in every tower, a clamor that shook dust from the eaves and sent pigeons up in silver spirals. It was Isolde's signal. Under cover of the din, the palace's loyal guards—there were forty-one, the queen had counted them twice—opened the postern gates, and the plan went into motion like water finding a crack.

Mara walked out the front.

He wore his archivist's coat with the ledger chain still hanging from his belt, and he carried the prophecy book open in both hands like a relic, and he marched straight up the steps of the great square where the soldiers were penning the beasts, and in a voice trained by years of lecture halls and reading rooms to carry without strain, he began to read.

"*In the year 1893, by the hand of the royal recorder Aldous Fenwick: let it be entered that the following names were taken into the crown's service—*" and he read a name, a woman's name, *Hester Mowbray, weaver*, and then another, *Tomas Mowbray, her son, aged nine*—

The square did not go silent. It went *still*, the way a forest goes still, every living thing frozen mid-motion, listening.

"—and let it be entered that these names were given freely, according to the law of the tithe, and that the persons bearing them did thereafter cease—"

"Liar!" A soldier lunged for him, and Mara danced back with the book held high, and the crowd surged—not away, Lena saw from the shadowed arcade where he waited with Isolde, but *inward*, toward the reading, toward the name. An old woman near the front had begun to sway. A man in a weaver's apron had gone white as flour.

"Tomas Mowbray," Mara read again, louder, over the soldier's shout, over the gathering roar. "*Her son. Aged nine.* If there is anyone here of Mowbray blood—if anyone carries that name in their family's memory—let them come forward and claim it!"

And a woman screamed.

It was not a scream of fear. Lena felt it pass through the crowd like a blade through silk, and he felt, beneath it, deeper than it, the threads of the city answering—the beasts in the pens lifting their heads all at once, the sparrows wheeling, the dog on Tanner's Row breaking into full-throated song. Because the scream had been a name. The woman had screamed *Tomas*, and somewhere in her throat, in her blood, in the hundred-year silence of her family, something had recognized itself.

"That's it," Isolde breathed beside him. Her hand had found Lena's sleeve and gripped it hard enough to bruise. "Oh, gods, that's it. Look at them. Look at them *remembering*."

More names. Mara flung them out like seed, like sparks—*Corwin Hale, lamplighter; Brida Osk, fishwife; the twins Ellin and Emeth, aged six*—and the crowd was no longer a crowd, it was a congregation, it was a tide, pressing forward while the soldiers' line wavered and bent, because half the soldiers were hearing their own family names called out from the mouth of a madman with a book, and you cannot hold a pike line against your own dead.

"Now," Isolde said.

Lena stepped out of the arcade into the light.

The square hit him like the border stones had, that first day outside the valley—but this time he did not fight it. He opened himself and let the whole roaring mass of the city pour through him: ten thousand animal hearts, four hundred bound voices circling in the crown above, and threaded through all of it, faint but unmistakable, the child-voice singing its one stubborn note of refusal. He raised his hands, and the gift rose in him like dawn, and he spoke—not loudly, he never needed to be loud—to every beast in the square at once:

*Your names are yours. Your voices are yours. No one gave them and no one can take them. Sing.*

The square exploded.

It was not violence, not at first—it was *sound*, a deluge of it, every penned beast finding its voice in the same instant, dogs and mules and geese and cats and the great oxen hauled in from the farmlands, all of them crying out together, and beneath the animal roar the human crowd caught fire too, thousands of people shouting names at the sky, names their grandmothers had whispered, names carved nowhere, names returned. The soldiers broke. Some dropped their pikes; some tore off their helmets, weeping; two, Lena saw with a jolt, knelt and began clawing at the brand-marks on the nearest pen posts, trying to pry the boards apart with bare hands.

Above it all, the bells rang on, and beneath the bells—faint, furious, unmistakable—the Choir began to hum.

Lena felt it turn its attention downward like a searchlight. Felt the note bend, sharpen, *aim*. The king's voice, or the thing wearing the king's voice, rose over the square from the direction of the palace, amplified by some art of the bound throats inside it, and it spoke one word, and the word was:

*Mara.*

* * *

Later, Lena would try to reconstruct those minutes and fail. Memory broke the scene into shards: Mara on the fountain steps, book held high, reading faster now, names tumbling out in a torrent; the Choir's hum swelling beneath the bells like floodwater under ice; soldiers in palace livery pushing through the panicked edges of the crowd—not broken soldiers, but the ones whose families had never been tithed, the ones with nothing to remember, marching in perfect eerie unison with their faces slack and their eyes moving out of rhythm with their bodies.

"They're puppets," Isolde shouted, gripping Lena's arm. "Some of the guard are bound too—I never knew how many—Lena, they're going for Mara—"

"I see them—"

"Get him out. Get the book out. Whatever happens, the names have to survive—"

But Mara had seen them too. Lena watched it happen in horrible slow clarity: watched his friend—his betrayer, his confessor, the man who had written him back into a world that wanted him erased—watch Mara glance at the closing ring of hollow-eyed guards, then at the crowd surging behind him, thousands strong, thousands *awake*, and then at Lena across the square.

And Mara smiled that crooked, disarming smile one last time.

He turned to the crowd and bellowed, with the last full breath of his life as a free man: "THE ROLLS ARE IN THE BOOK! EVERY NAME! REMEMBER THEM! TELL EACH OTHER!" And then he hurled the prophecy book in a high spinning arc over the heads of the guards, into the outstretched hands of the weaver's-apron man, the descendant of Tomas Mowbray, aged nine—and stepped down off the fountain steps directly into the arms of the bound.

Lena screamed his name and lunged forward, and Isolde's grip became both his arms, hauling him back with a strength born of six years of helpless watching. "Not like this," she snarled into his ear as he struggled. "Not *their* way. If you run at them now you're one more body, and the book is safe, and the crowd is awake, and *that is what he bought*—"

The guards dragged Mara toward the palace gates. He did not struggle. At the threshold he turned, found Lena across the churning square with an accuracy that should not have been possible, and mouthed three words.

Lena read them, and stopped struggling, and went cold all over, and burning hot, both at once.

The words were: *Undercroft. Tonight. Me.*

Then the doors swallowed him, and the great gates boomed shut, and above the square the bells fell silent one by one, and in the ringing absence left behind, thin and patient and horribly satisfied, the Choir began to sing.
