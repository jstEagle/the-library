## 18. The Unmaking

The doors of the throne room were taller than any tree in Vell Hollow.

Lena had known that from the moment he'd first seen them, three days ago, when Isolde's falconer had smuggled them through the servants' corridors in the gray hour before dawn. But knowing a thing and standing beneath it were different animals entirely. The doors were black oak banded with silver, and carved into their faces was a single line of script repeated over and over, so old that the letters had worn smooth as river stones. Mara had read it aloud once, quietly, in the dark of the archive: *We carry what the crown cannot.*

Now the doors stood open, and the sound came out of them like breath from a mouth.

Lena heard it before he saw anything—the humming, the note beneath all notes, the one the bells of Corvane had been drowning for a hundred years. It poured through the doorway and down the marble steps and pooled around his boots, and it was not one voice. It had never been one voice. It was a hundred voices braided so tightly together that they had forgotten they were separate, singing a single sustained tone that lived somewhere below hearing, in the place where the ribs became a cage.

*Steady,* said the wren on his shoulder.

She had insisted on coming. He had not been able to refuse her; there was no refusing her, not anymore, not after everything. Her claws pricked his collarbone through his shirt, small anchors of pain, and he was grateful for every one of them.

"I'm here," he whispered.

*I know,* she said. *I can feel you shaking.*

"That's reassuring."

*It isn't. But I'm here too, and between us we make almost one whole brave person.*

He almost laughed. That was her gift, always: to find the crack in the dread wide enough to slip a joke through. He pressed two fingers gently against her back, feeling the impossible smallness of her heartbeat, and stepped across the threshold.

The throne room of Corvane was built to make a person feel like an insect that had wandered indoors by mistake. The ceiling arched away into gloom hung with banners gone gray with dust. Tall windows let in the late-afternoon light in long amber columns, and in those columns floated motes of dust that did not drift naturally but turned, slowly, all in the same direction, as if the air itself had been taught to obey. The floor was a mosaic of the kingdom's history—kings crowned, kings buried, kings crowned again—and the tiles were worn pale down the center by a century of feet walking the same narrow path to power.

At the end of that path, on a dais of white stone, sat the throne.

And in the throne sat King Aldric.

Or what wore him.

Lena had seen the Hollow King once before, at the audience that now felt like it belonged to another life. Then, the wrongness had been subtle—a blink out of rhythm, eyes moving a half-second behind the face. There was nothing subtle now. The Choir had stopped pretending. Aldric sat slumped in the great chair like a coat hung on a hook, his head tipped back, his mouth slightly open, and above his brow the crown of Corvane—not gold, Lena could see now, never gold, but something darker and older, iron worked into a ring of open mouths—pulsed with a faint light, dimming and brightening, dimming and brightening, in time with the hum.

*There you are.*

The voice arrived inside his skull without passing through his ears, and it was not Aldric's voice, though it borrowed the shape of it. It was all of them. It was the hum given grammar.

*We wondered when you would come, little listener. We have been saving you a seat.*

Lena's knees wanted to buckle. He made them hold. "I've come for the voices," he said, and was surprised at how steady his own voice sounded in that vast room, how small and yet how unbroken. "All of them. You're going to give them back."

The light in the crown flared.

*Give them back.* The Choir tasted the words the way a man tastes a foreign fruit. *Child. Do you know what we are? We were mercy once. A dying king's last breath, caught and kept, so that his people would not have to learn silence all at once. And then we learned what we were hungry for. Names, little listener. A name is the only thing a soul cannot live without and cannot miss until it is gone. We have a hundred names in this crown. We have worn them so long they have become us. And you walk in here with your small gift and your small bird and ask us to undress ourselves in front of a stranger.*

"Yes," Lena said.

Behind him, at the far end of the hall, he heard the great doors begin to swing shut. He did not turn. He knew who was holding them—he had made Mara promise, made him swear on the prophecy book itself, that whatever happened in this room, the doors stayed open until Lena walked out or didn't. Mara was there. Isolde was there, in the shadow of the colonnade with her husband's sword across her knees and tears she would deny later already drying on her face. They were his witnesses. That was all he had asked of them. This part, no one could do for him.

He walked forward.

Each step onto the mosaic was a step across someone's life—here a coronation, there a funeral—and with each step the hum grew louder, richer, and began, horribly, to resolve. Threads were separating themselves out of the braid and reaching for him. Not attacking. *Listening.* He could feel them listening, a hundred attentions turning toward him like flowers toward a window, and underneath his terror he felt something else, something that shamed him with its warmth:

They were lonely. All hundred of them. They had been pressed together so long, sung into a single note so long, that each of them had forgotten its own shape, and some deep animal part of them recognized what he was—the boy who heard, the speaker, the one whose whole cursed gift was attention—and they reached for him the way drowning things reach for light.

*You hear us,* the Choir said, and for just a moment the mockery fell out of it, and what remained was naked and enormous and starving. *No one has heard us in a hundred years. The king hears only the sum of us, and the sum is all we are allowed to be. You could hear each of us. You do already. We can feel you sorting us, little listener, even now—you cannot help it. You are counting us like sheep.*

"You're not sheep," Lena said quietly. "You're people. You have names."

*Had.* The cruelty snapped back into place like a jaw closing. *And here is our offer, since you came so far. Give us yours instead.*

The crown blazed.

The force of it hit him like a wave hitting a swimmer—no, like a hand closing around the back of his skull, fingers sliding in. Lena staggered on the third step from the dais and caught himself on nothing, arms windmilling, and the wren screamed and beat her wings against his ear, and through the roar he heard the Choir speaking softly, reasonably, in a hundred overlapping whispers that were somehow one whisper:

*You have spent your whole life invisible, Nakamura. In a valley that forgets, among people who looked through you, hiding the only true thing about yourself because the truth was too large for the space they left you. We know. We have watched you since the border stones. We know what it is to be many things and permitted to be none.*

"Stop—"

*We can give you a place. Not a seat—a choir. Every voice in this crown will know your name, and you will know theirs, and you will never again stand in a room full of people wondering if anyone sees you. You will be carried. You will be sung. Your name will outlive the kingdom. All it costs is what you were never using anyway.*

The fingers in his mind tightened, and Lena felt them beginning to *sort* him—to run along the threads of him the way he ran along the threads of the Choir, finding the seam where his name was knotted into his self, worrying it loose. It didn't hurt, exactly. That was the worst of it. It felt like being *understood*. Like the thing he had wanted his entire solitary life, offered by the worst possible giver, at the worst possible price.

He saw it, for one terrible clear second. He saw himself bound into the iron, a hundred-and-first voice, held and held and held, never alone again, never unseen, never small. The valley had made him small. His whole life had been a lesson in making himself smaller, quieter, a boy who spoke to beasts because beasts were the only ones who listened, and here was a chorus offering to amplify him into forever—

*Lena.*

The wren's voice. Small, sharp, ordinary.

*Your name is Lena. Your mother gave it to you under a plum tree, and she chose it because it means light in a language neither of you speaks anymore, and she was laughing when she said it. That name isn't yours to trade. It's hers. You're only borrowing it.*

His mother. Dead eleven years, buried on the hillside where the border stones couldn't reach, and still her laughter came through a bird's small throat at exactly the moment he needed it.

Lena straightened.

"No," he said.

The pressure redoubled, and now it *did* hurt, white and total, and the Choir's voice lost its silk entirely, became the thing beneath the silk, old and iron and vast: *Then we will take it.*

"Take it, then." Blood was running warm from his nose, over his lip, dripping onto the mosaic kings. He wiped it with the back of his hand and grinned at the throne, at the slumped body, at the pulsing ring of open mouths, and his grin felt feral and free and more like himself than anything he had ever worn. "But you should know something about me. Everyone does, eventually. My whole life, everyone told me my gift was for hiding. For keeping secrets. For staying safe and small behind the stones." He took another step. Another. Close enough now to see the dried salt-tracks on Aldric's cheeks, close enough to see that the king's lips were moving faintly, shaping a word over and over—*help, help, help*—the one syllable of himself he had managed to keep. "They were wrong. My gift was never for hiding."

He closed his eyes. Opened the door in himself that the falconer had taught him to find—that quiet place behind the noise where individual threads could be told apart—and instead of listening, for the first time in his life, he *spoke into* the chorus.

And the first name rose in him like water rising in a well.

It belonged to a girl. He knew that instantly, the way he knew things about beasts—knew she had been fourteen, a miller's daughter from the river towns, taken in the tithe of sixty years ago, and that her name had been the first thing the Choir swallowed and the last thing it could not digest. The name filled his mouth. It was a good name, a river-name, and when he spoke it aloud—spoke it *true*, with the beast-speech underneath it, the speech that was not words but recognition—the hum of the Choir *shuddered*.

One thread in the braid went taut.

One voice, deep inside the hundred-voiced note, remembered that it was a girl.

*Stop,* said the Choir, and for the first time in a hundred years, the thing in the crown sounded afraid.

Lena opened his eyes, and they were streaming, and he was smiling, and the second name was already rising.

* * *

They came faster after the first. That was the secret no one could have told him because no one had ever done it: the names wanted out. Each one he spoke loosened the knot around the next. He planted his feet on the third step of the dais, inside the radius of the crown's light, which burned his skin like standing too near a forge, and he sang—or it was singing, though it was also weeping, though it was also the way he talked to frightened animals in the dark, low and sure and unhurried.

A shepherd. A midwife. Two brothers taken in the same winter, whose names rhymed, and when he spoke them together the hum cracked clean down the middle for half a heartbeat and he heard, distinct and astonished, two voices saying *brother* to each other for the first time in forty years.

The Choir fought him. Of course it fought him. The crown's light lashed at him like a net of hot wire; the whispers turned to shrieks; the dead king's body lurched upright in the throne with a movement no living spine should make, and Aldric's mouth opened wide and the full voice of the Choir poured out of it, shaking dust from the rafters, splitting one of the tall windows so that amber light and cold air came pouring in together. *WE MADE YOU A GIFT AND YOU CHOOSE THE KNIFE—*

"It was never a gift," Lena shouted into the roar, blood sheeting from his nose now, his knees buckling and holding, buckling and holding, the wren pressed flat against his neck and trembling. "Gifts are given freely. These were *stolen*. And I am here—" another name, a child's, an old man's, they were coming faster, the braid unraveling around him like rope coming apart in current, "—to give them *back*—"

Somewhere behind him, at the far end of the hall, he heard the doors groan, heard Mara shouting his name, heard Isolde's voice rise over both—*hold the doors, hold them, he needs the time*—and he had no time to be grateful. The Choir had stopped offering and started tearing. He could feel it now in earnest, clawing down through his mind toward the knot of his own name, and it was strong, it was so much stronger than him, a hundred years of hunger against one boy from a valley that forgot.

But the boy was not alone in his own head. That was the other secret. With every name he freed, something shifted in the balance—a thread pulled out of the crown's braid and, instead of fleeing, *turned*, and stood beside him in the dark behind his eyes. The miller's daughter. The brothers. The shepherd. They could not speak anymore, not yet, not fully—but they could *lean*. And they leaned.

The child-voice surfaced last, the one he had promised in the undercroft, the smallest thread of all, and when Lena found it, it was not fighting anymore. It was waiting. It had been waiting a hundred years for exactly this, and it put its small hand, metaphorically, actually, impossibly, into his, and showed him its name.

Lena drew one long breath, tasting blood and iron and freedom.

And then the crown did the thing he had not prepared for. The thing no one could have prepared for.

It stopped fighting—and began, very quickly, very quietly, to *swallow the king whole*.

Aldric's body convulsed. The last light in the man's remaining eye guttered. And Lena understood, with the sick clarity of the final page, that the Choir would rather burn its host to ash than be unbound by a beast-speaker from nowhere—that if he did not finish in the next few heartbeats, there would be no king left to save, only a crown and a corpse and a hundred recaptured names.

There was only one thing left that was strong enough to anchor a spell this size. He had known it since the prophecy book. He had lied to Mara about accepting it, and lied to himself, and the lie ended here, on the third step, in the burning light.

Lena gripped the knot of his own name—his mother laughing under the plum tree, light in a language neither of them spoke—and began, deliberately, to pull it loose.
