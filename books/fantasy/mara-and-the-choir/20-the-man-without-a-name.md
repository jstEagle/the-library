## 20. The Man Without a Name

The first thing Mara learned about erasure was that it did not look like death.

Death had a shape. He had seen it in the undercroft's aftermath, in the faces of the acolytes who had served the Choir and now stood blinking in the square like men waking from a fever, in the hundred small silences where bound voices used to be. Death left a body, a weight, a hole in the air. What had happened to Lena Nakamura was subtler than that. It was a loosening. A page being lifted from a book so gently that the binding did not even complain.

Mara sat in the infirmary of Corvane Palace on the third morning after the unmaking, watching a physician ask Lena his name for the fourth time.

"And you are?" the physician said, pen hovering over his chart.

Lena smiled—that same crooked, apologetic smile Mara had first seen across a dinner table in Vell Hollow, when the whole world was still small enough to fit inside a valley—and said, "I'm sorry. It's right there. I can almost feel it. Like a word on the tip of my tongue that someone swallowed."

"Your family name?"

Lena's brow furrowed. "Naka—" He stopped. His mouth stayed open around the syllable like a door held ajar, and then it closed. "Nakamura," he said suddenly, with relief. "Nakamura. That one's still mine."

"And your given name?"

The silence stretched. Outside the window, the bells of Corvane rang the hour—Mara flinched at them still, though they rang clean now, unaccompanied by the humming beneath the streets—and when the last note faded, Lena shook his head slowly.

"There's a space where it was," he said. "A warm space. Like a chair someone just got up from." He looked at his own hands as if they might hold the answer. "I know I gave it away. I remember choosing to. I remember the stone throat, and the child-voice, and saying *take this instead*. But I can't remember what *this* was."

The physician wrote something down. Mara read it upside down out of habit: *Subject coherent. Memory intact excepting proper name (self). Records inconsistent.*

That last part was the part that frightened him.

* * *

He had gone to the archive himself, two days before, because he no longer trusted anyone else to look. The tithe rolls were still there, the great ledgers in which the Choir had recorded every stolen name for a century—his own family's name among them, entered in a clerk's careful hand in 1893, the ink brown with age. He had found his grandmother's maiden name there once and wept over it in the dark between the shelves. Now he went looking for a different entry.

*Beast-speaker, Nakamura blood,* the prophecy ledger said. The prophecy itself he knew by heart now, every line, including the one he had hidden from Lena for months: *the unmaker gives back every name, and loses his own.*

But ledgers were supposed to be impartial things. Ledgers were how the Choir had kept its accounts; ledgers were how Mara had learned the truth of it in the first place. So he pulled the palace census, the guest registers, the gate logs, the infirmary admissions book—the whole paper skeleton of the kingdom, which he had spent years learning to navigate as an archivist and a fugitive both.

Lena Nakamura was not in any of them.

Not the gate log from the night they had entered Corvane, though Mara distinctly remembered signing them both in, remembered the guard squinting at Lena's valley accent. Not the census of guests taken during the crisis. Not the infirmary roll, where the physician's own chart lay open on a desk upstairs with *Nakamura* written at the top in fresh ink. Mara checked three times. The ink was there. The entry was not. As if the page had accepted the name and then quietly digested it.

He stood in the archive with a lamp guttering and understood, with a cold that started in his teeth, what the prophecy's last line had actually meant. Not metaphor. Not poetry. The Choir had been built on names—it had eaten them, worn them, sung them into a single note. And its death had been anchored by a name, poured into the spell like water into a drain. The magic had not merely taken Lena's name from his memory. It had taken it from the world. From paper. From other people's mouths, perhaps, in time. Already Mara had caught Queen Isolde pausing mid-sentence, her lips shaping a word that slipped through them like smoke, her expression flickering with something between confusion and grief.

The prophecy had been right. Prophecies usually were. That was the whole obscenity of them—they were accurate the way a knife was accurate.

Mara set his hand flat against the empty page of the gate log and made a decision that felt less like courage than like stubbornness wearing courage's coat.

*No,* he thought. *You don't get to have him.*

* * *

Isolde found him there, or came looking—he never learned which. She stood in the aisle with her shawl drawn close, and she looked older than she had a week ago, though Aldric was breathing on his own now, half-eaten but mending, blinking in rhythm with his own face at last.

"You've seen it too," she said.

"The records won't hold him."

"No." She came closer and touched the blank line in the gate log with one finger, the way one touches a grave. "I tried to write him into the court's gratitude list. Officially. For the unmaking." Her jaw tightened. "The clerk swore he'd entered it. The page is blank. Twice."

"It'll spread," Mara said. "Names are consensus. That's what the Choir understood better than anyone—if enough people agree on a name, it becomes real, and if everyone forgets, it becomes nothing. Right now the forgetting is spreading outward from the spell like frost. In a year, no one will have ever met him. In five, Vell Hollow will be a valley with one fewer house."

Isolde was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "My brother's name is on a tithe roll from thirty years ago. I chose the crown over warning him, and the Choir took him, and for thirty years I told myself the record at least remained—that somewhere in ink he existed, that he had been real." She looked up at Mara, and her eyes were dry in the way of people who have cried past the capacity for it. "I will not watch that happen twice. Tell me what you need."

And that was how it began—not with a grand plan, not with magic, but with a queen asking an archivist what he needed, and an archivist answering honestly:

"Paper," Mara said. "Ink. Witnesses. And time."

* * *

The first testimony was Isolde's own, dictated in her private study while Mara wrote. He used good paper, the heavy rag stock from the southern mills, and iron-gall ink mixed fresh, because if the world was going to try to erase a man he intended to make the erasing expensive.

*I, Isolde, Queen of Corvane, do testify as follows.* His pen moved. *There came to my court, in the company of the archivist Mara Voss, a man of the valley called Vell Hollow, whose name I spoke many times and whose face I see clearly as I speak. He is slight, with black hair going gray early at the temples, and a scar on the left thumb from a snare he set wrong as a boy—a story he told me himself, laughing, in the falconer's mews. He speaks with beasts. This gift he turned against the Choir that wore my husband like a garment, and he unmade it, and the price was his own name, which he paid knowing the cost in full.*

She stopped him there. "Say the rest," she said. "The part that matters."

Mara hesitated, then wrote: *His name began with L.*

It was such a small thing. Such an absurdly small thing. But when the ink dried, the words stayed on the page—stayed, when *Lena Nakamura* would not—and Isolde exhaled like a woman surfacing from deep water.

"It holds," she whispered. "Why does it hold?"

"Because it's true and witnessed and specific," Mara said. "The spell eats names the way rot eats wood—from the center out. The center is gone. But the edges—" He touched the page. "The edges are held by everything around them. Every fact that isn't the name. Every story. You can't erase a man entirely without erasing everyone who remembers him, and the magic isn't strong enough for that. It never was. It could only take what was freely given."

"Then we build him back from the edges inward," Isolde said.

"From the edges inward," Mara agreed. "One testimony at a time."

* * *

They gathered witnesses the way other people gather kindling.

The falconer, blind and voiceless since the Choir's retaliation, could not speak—but he could write, in a laborious beautiful hand, and what he wrote filled six pages: the lessons in listening, the way the beast-speaker had learned to hear a child's voice inside a chorus, the exact quality of his laugh when a hawk finally landed on his glove. Mara read it aloud to Isolde and had to stop twice.

The guards from the gate signed statements. The innkeeper at the sign of the Drowned Bell, where they had lodged that first week, testified that the man had fed crumbs to the sparrows off his plate and that the sparrows had *listened*, and that she had thought nothing of it at the time and now thought of little else. The physician submitted his chart, with its stubborn, surviving *Nakamura* at the top, as evidence. Even Aldric came, moving slowly, wrapped in furs though it was nearly summer, and sat in the archive while Mara read testimonies aloud, and at the end the king said in a voice still roughened by a hundred borrowed throats:

"I heard him, in the last days. Inside. When the Choir was tearing me apart, there was one voice it couldn't digest, and it was his—talking to the child, promising. I don't remember his name either." The king's hands tightened on the arms of the chair. "But I remember the sound of it. I could pick it out of ten thousand voices. Write that down."

Mara wrote it down.

And every night, after the witnesses had gone, Mara sat alone with the growing stack of pages and did the work no one else could do. Because testimony established the edges—the scar, the laugh, the gift, the kindness—but the center was still a warm empty chair, and only love, he suspected, was stubborn enough to sit in it.

So he wrote letters. Not testimonies—letters, addressed to a man he refused to stop naming.

*Dear L,* the first one began, and he stared at the salutation for an hour before continuing. *Today the wren arrived. I don't know how it crossed the border stones—I suspect it simply decided the stones were a suggestion—but it landed on your windowsill in the infirmary and screamed at you until you woke, and you laughed and said, "All right, all right, I'm up." The physicians say you're mending. I say you're mending too, but mending is not the same as returning, and I intend to drag you back by the collar if necessary. Here is what I remember, so that you cannot claim otherwise:*

And then he wrote everything. The date in Vell Hollow, the rain, the way Lena had looked at the menu as if it were written in a foreign language and ordered the same thing Mara did. The moment at the window when the wren screamed a word no bird should know. The road of branded mules, and the first freed voice, and the soldiers' torches behind them. The undercroft, and the promise. The throne room, and the light.

He wrote until his hand cramped, sealed the letter, and addressed it to *Lena Nakamura, Infirmary, Corvane Palace*—and in the morning the address had faded to a gray smudge, but the letter itself remained, and he put it in a box, and wrote another.

* * *

On the twelfth day, Lena came to the archive.

Mara heard the door and knew the step—lighter than it had been in Vell Hollow, steadier than it had been during the crisis—and did not turn around. There were forty-one testimonies stacked on the table behind him, and eleven unsent letters in the box, and he was copying the falconer's account into fair hand when Lena said, from the doorway:

"You've been writing about me."

"Not about," Mara said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "To."

"To whom? I don't—" Lena came closer, and Mara finally turned, and saw the frustration banked in his face like a fire kept low. "I don't have a name to put at the top of a letter. Do you know what that's like? People ask, and I open my mouth, and there's nothing. Not forgotten—*absent*. Like a tooth knocked out. My tongue keeps going to the gap." He picked up the topmost testimony—Isolde's, in Mara's fair copy—and read it standing there, his lips moving slightly. When he reached *a scar on the left thumb from a snare he set wrong as a boy*, he laughed, sudden and wet. "I'd forgotten the snare. How did she—"

"You told her. In the mews. While you were learning to listen."

"I don't remember telling her."

"That's why we're writing it down." Mara stood. The stack of pages rose between them like a small city. "Listen to me. The prophecy says you lost your name, and the prophecy has been right about everything, and I am sick to death of prophecies being right. So here is what I know that the prophecy doesn't." He took Lena's hand—the left one, turning it so the old scar caught the lamplight. "A name isn't a thing you own. It's a thing other people call you. It lives in mouths, not in minds. The spell took the center, fine. But I have forty-one witnesses who hold the edges, and I have eleven letters, and I have—" His voice failed for a moment, and he pushed through it. "I have the whole rest of my life, and I intend to spend it writing you back. One testimony at a time. One letter at a time. Until the gap closes or until I die at the desk trying, whichever comes second."

Lena looked at him for a long time. Outside, the bells rang the hour, and neither of them flinched.

"You hid the last line from me," Lena said quietly. "For months. You let me walk into the throne room not knowing the price."

"Yes."

"I should be furious."

"Are you?"

"I was." Lena's thumb moved over the scar, over the words on the page. "But fury needs a name to curse, and you've been spending yours on me instead. That's—" He stopped, and started again. "That's the most anyone has ever done for me. Including the things I can't remember you doing."

"So you'll let me keep writing?"

"I'll help." Lena pulled out the chair across the table and sat, and drew the stack of testimonies toward himself, and picked up a spare pen. "If my name lives in mouths, then let's fill more of them. The innkeeper. The guards. Someone should ride to Vell Hollow and collect the beasts' testimony—I'd wager the wren remembers, birds keep what they need. And Mara—" He looked up, and for a moment the warm empty space behind his eyes seemed almost to glow, like a window lit from a room no one could enter. "When you write the next letter. Don't start it *Dear L*."

"What should I start it with, then?"

"Start it with the truth," Lena said. "*To the man I refuse to lose.*"

* * *

Mara wrote it that night, by lamplight, while the palace slept and the bells stood silent between hours. *To the man I refuse to lose,* the letter began, and the ink held, and the paper held, and when he finished near dawn he added a postscript in a smaller hand:

*P.S. — The wren returned again today. It brought a feather and left it on your sill, and the physicians swear the feather wasn't there when they checked. I have logged it as Testimony Forty-Two. The beasts, I think, are beginning to testify.*

He sealed it, and dated it, and placed it in the box with the others—eleven letters now, twelve, a growing archive of a man the world was trying to misplace—and then he blew out the lamp and sat in the dark and allowed himself, for exactly one minute, to grieve the name he could no longer say.

Then he lit the lamp again, because there was more to write, and grief was a poor author compared to stubbornness.

Far to the north, past the border stones of Vell Hollow, a wren was already flying south with a word in its throat—a word no bird should know, a name the valley had begun whispering to itself in the dark—and it would arrive, in time, at a windowsill in Corvane, carrying the first piece of the center back toward its edges.

But that is the next chapter, and Mara did not yet know it. He only knew the lamp, and the ink, and the box of letters, and the man asleep two floors above him whose name he refused to surrender—and for an archivist who had once fled a chorus with a stolen book under his arm, that was enough to begin.
