## 22. The First Page of a New Book

The printing house on Candlemaker's Row smelled of ink and hot lead and the particular sweetness of paper that had been waiting a long time to be useful. Mara Voss stood at the press with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, watching the first finished copy of *The Book of Ash, Corrected and Annotated* emerge into the morning light like something being born.

It was smaller than he'd expected. He had carried the original ledger for eleven years—had slept with it beneath his head in acolyte dormitories where dreaming aloud was forbidden, had swum with it strapped to his chest across the Greywater, had pressed it into Lena Nakamura's hands in a valley inn while a wren screamed a true name through the window glass. He had imagined the book's ending a thousand times: always fire, usually his own death, occasionally Lena's. Never this. Never a modest quarto of two hundred and six pages, stitched in plain thread, priced so that a baker's apprentice could buy it.

"Master Voss." The printer, a broad woman named Odile whose forearms were stained black to the second knuckle, held the proof copy out to him. "Last look. Once it goes out, it goes out."

Mara took it. His hands were steady. That surprised him; for years his hands had trembled whenever he touched anything that mattered, as if his body remembered what his mind tried to forget—that everything he touched could be taken, or worse, could be used.

He turned to the last entry. The original prophecy ended there, in the cramped hand of some long-dead archivist:

*And the unmaker gives back every name, and loses his own.*

Below it, in fresh type, Mara had set the correction—not a correction, really. A testimony. He read it again now, though he knew every word by heart, because reading it was a kind of prayer, and prayer was the only liturgy left to him after he'd burned his acolyte's robes:

*This line is true. I have seen its cost with my own eyes. But prophecy is not a sentence; it is a question asked by the dead, and the living are permitted to answer differently. The man who gave back every name did not lose himself. He lost only what was false about him—the smallness, the hiding, the name that kept him safe and invisible. What remains cannot be written down, because I am still learning what it is. I loved him before he had no name, and I love him now, which is either proof that names are not the whole of a person, or proof that I never needed one to find him.*

*After this page, the book is blank.*

*Write in it.*

Odile cleared her throat. "You're crying on my proof, Master Voss."

"I'm annotating it," Mara said, "in advance."

She laughed, a big unguarded sound, and clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him. Outside the window, Corvane went about its business. The bells still rang the hour—but they rang it plainly now, honestly, marking time instead of smothering it. Beneath the streets, the undercroft stood open to the sky where the stone throat had been broken out, and Isolde had ordered a fountain built in the hollow: water falling over water, a sound like a hundred voices all saying their own names at once, gladly.

One year. It had been one year since the throne room, since the crown split like an eggshell and a hundred freed voices rose through the palace rafters shrieking their own names in joy and terror and grief, since Aldric drew a breath that belonged to no one but himself and wept without knowing why. One year since Mara had found Lena afterward sitting on the steps of the dais, very quiet, looking at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger.

"What's your name?" Mara had asked him, kneeling, because someone had to ask, and because he already knew the answer would be nothing.

Lena had thought about it for a long time. Then he'd smiled—small, cracked, real—and said, "Ask me tomorrow."

He had said the same thing every day since. *Ask me tomorrow.* It had become, between them, not a wound but a game, a door they kept propping open. Some mornings Lena answered with a joke. Some mornings he answered with a word he'd borrowed from a beast—a fox's name for dawn, a river-otter's name for rain. And once, on the anniversary, he had answered with Mara's name, spoken softly against his hair, and Mara had understood that this was the closest thing to an answer he would ever get, and that it was enough. More than enough. It was authorship.

Mara slid the proof copy into his satchel, beside three others—one for the palace library, one for Queen Isolde, who had written the foreword under her own name and dared the surviving Choir sympathizers to object, and one that was going east, to Vell Hollow, in a gardener's hands.

"I'll want five hundred more by month's end," he told Odile. "And put the blank pages in every copy. If anyone complains the book is unfinished—"

"They can finish it," Odile said.

"Exactly."

* * *

Vell Hollow had not changed, which was its way, and had changed completely, which was also its way.

The border stones still stood in their mossy ring along the ridge road, gray and patient as sleeping cattle. Magic still thinned to nothing as you crossed them—or it had. Lena walked through them now the way a man walks through a doorway in his own house, without pausing, without bracing, and the valley let him pass both directions, which it had never done for anyone before. The stones had known him once as a thing to be kept. They knew him now as a thing returned from elsewhere, carrying elsewhere inside him like seeds in a coat pocket.

He had come back in the spring, when the thaw made the streams loud, and he had not intended to stay. He had meant to visit his mother's grave, sell the cottage, go back east to Corvane, to Mara, to the work of testimony. But the wren had been waiting.

It lived in the hawthorn by the garden wall—the same wren, he was almost certain, though wrens are difficult to swear to. On his first morning home it had landed on the fence post, cocked its absurd small head, and said, in the voice it had used to scream his true name through a window half a lifetime ago:

*You came back wrong,* it observed. *Good. Wrong is more interesting.*

"I need somewhere to plant," Lena had told it.

*There,* said the wren, and indicated the entire world with a flick of one tail feather.

So he stayed. He sent word east—a letter that took nine days and cost him three drafts—and planted a garden where the hawthorn grew, in soil that had been fallow since his grandmother's time. He planted beans and squash and sunflowers, and around the border, because he could not help himself, he planted things the beasts had asked for: foxglove for the fox, who liked the way the flowers hummed; vetch for the badger, who ate the leaves when his stomach ached; a rowan sapling for the wren, who claimed it as a matter of right before it reached knee-height.

The beasts of the valley came to the garden the way neighbors come to a new well. They had always been able to talk to him—his gift had never died at the stones, only his hope had—but now they talked openly, in daylight, in front of his human neighbors, and the neighbors, after the first season of staring, simply began leaving out saucers of milk and bruised apples, because a valley that has learned its animals have opinions is a valley that learns manners quickly.

An old ewe named Sorrel came most often. She was going deaf, she admitted, and her joints were poor, and she liked to sit in the bean rows while Lena worked and tell him stories about the valley's older days, when the border stones were young and the first Nakamura had bargained with the mountain for the fog.

"You know," Sorrel said one afternoon, chewing contemplatively on a squash blossom she had absolutely not been offered, "your grandmother talked to us too. And her mother before her. We wondered, sometimes, why none of you ever left."

"We did leave," Lena said. "We just didn't survive it."

"Mm." The ewe considered this. "And now?"

Lena looked at his hands—dirt to the wrists, a healing blister on one thumb where the trowel had slipped. Hands that had spoken a hundred names into a crown of stolen voices. Hands that had held nothing since but seedlings.

"Now I plant things," he said, "and they grow. It turns out that's the whole miracle. Nobody warned me how boring it would be."

Sorrel laughed the way sheep laugh, which is mostly breath and judgment, and settled deeper into the beans.

The letter from Mara arrived on a Thursday, carried by a courier who looked deeply alarmed to be handed over to a man surrounded by conversing livestock. Inside, wrapped in brown paper, was the proof copy of the book, and a note in Mara's quick slanted hand:

*They're printing five hundred. Isolde wrote the foreword. The archivists' guild is furious, which means it's working. Come east when the harvest is in? Or don't—I'll come west. I've gotten good at trains. I've gotten good at waiting. I've gotten good at loving someone the shape of a question.*

*P.S. The blank pages are selling better than the prophecies. People keep writing in them. Mostly small things. Names of grandmothers. Recipes. One child in Corvane has written an entire treatise on the moral lives of pigeons. I think the dead who asked the question would be pleased with the answers.*

*I love you. Ask me tomorrow.*

Lena read the letter twice, then folded it into his shirt pocket, where it lay warm against his chest all afternoon while he turned compost and argued with the wren about rowan placement.

That night he sat on the cottage step with the book in his lap and turned past the corrected final line to the first blank page. He held his pencil above it a long time.

Then he wrote, in careful letters:

*Lena. For today.*

And below it, smaller:

*Ask me again tomorrow.*

* * *

In the high north, beyond the Greywater, beyond even the mapmakers' patience, there is a country called Serevan that most people in Corvane could not find on a chart.

In Serevan there is a crown.

It is an old crown, older than Aldric's line, older than the Choir, and for three hundred years it has done nothing but sit on the heads of kings and be heavy, which is all crowns are supposed to do.

But crowns learn from each other. Word travels between them in ways that ministers cannot intercept—in the grain of gold, in the dreams of metalsmiths, in the slow underground conversation of objects that have been worshipped. And a year ago, on a night when the bells of Corvane rang the hour plainly for the first time in a century, the old crown of Serevan felt something die. Something kin. Something that had learned, far away, to eat.

The crown did not grieve. Crowns do not grieve. But it began, very quietly, to listen—to the whispers of its courtiers, to the muttering of its king in his sleep, to the small voices of the ravens on the palace roof, which had lately begun to seem less like birds and more like listeners.

And in the mines beneath Serevan, where the ore comes up dark and singing, the miners have started to complain of a sound. A hum. Low and patient, like a note being held by a throat that never tires.

They have complained to their overseers. The overseers have reported it to the court. And the court has filed the reports away in an archive, in a drawer, in the dark—

—where a young archivist with quick eyes and a taste for forbidden ledgers has just begun, out of boredom, to pull the drawers open.

Far to the south, in a valley that keeps its own counsel, a wren lifts its head from sleep in a rowan tree, listens to a hum it cannot possibly hear, and decides, on instinct older than prophecy, to remember the dream and tell the gardener in the morning.

Some questions, once answered, ask themselves again.

The book is not finished. The pages are still blank.

Write in them.
