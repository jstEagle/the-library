## 1. The Stranger at Saltgate

The rain came down the way it always did at Saltgate—sideways, salt-heavy, and full of grudges. Felix Blackwood pulled his oilskin hood lower and counted the lanterns along the quay out of habit rather than need. Seven lit. Two dark. The two dark ones belonged to him.

Ten years in this town had taught him the grammar of its weather: which gusts meant a patrol changing shifts, which silences meant someone was watching from the customs house window. Tonight the rain spoke plainly. *No one is looking. Move.*

He moved.

The *Kestrel's Debt* sat low in the water at Berth Nine, her hold packed with salted cod above and something less legal beneath—casks of stormglass, the kind that hummed faintly when lightning cracked over the Thornfells forty miles inland. Felix had stopped asking where it went years ago. That was the arrangement he'd made with his own conscience: he carried things, he asked nothing, and in exchange no one asked about the man who could call thunder down on a fleet with a snap of his fingers.

*Exiled storm-caller*, the old warrants still said, though the ink had faded to brown and the reward to a joke. He'd built a quieter name since. Felix the freight-man. Felix who fixed roofs after gales and never once, not ever, let the sky answer when he called.

He was halfway up the gangplank, a manifest roll tucked under his arm, when he heard the sound that did not belong to Saltgate's rain.

A body dragging itself through standing water.

Felix went still against the piling, one hand finding the knife at his belt out of reflex older than thought. The sound came again—a wet, labored scrape from the shadow between the fish sheds—and then a voice, cracked thin as ice on a spring pond.

"Blackwood. You're Blackwood."

Not a question. Which meant someone had told the man his name, and that alone was enough to make Felix's stomach drop through the wet boards of the world.

He should have walked away. Every instinct honed across ten careful years said *walk away*, the same instinct that had kept him alive when half the storm-callers of the old covenant war were ash or chains. But the voice knew his name, and names were currency in Saltgate, and whoever spent this one had spent it deliberately.

Felix stepped into the shadow between the sheds.

The man lay crumpled against a stack of empty barrels, and even in the dark Felix could read the story of him: courier's leathers, mud-caked riding boots gone, a horse somewhere behind him dead or fled. Three bolts stood out of his back like black quills. Crossbow work, close range, professional. The man had turned his head toward Felix, and what remained of his face was gray as the harbor water, blood threading pink through the puddle around his jaw.

"You're hard to kill," Felix said quietly. It wasn't kindness. It was triage—he needed to know how many breaths they had.

"Not... hard enough." A laugh that cost him everything. His hand shot out and caught Felix's wrist with a grip that had no business being that strong, and pressed something into it: a leather tube, sealed with wax that had been broken and resealed badly, stained dark along one side. Blood. Old blood and new.

"I don't take deliveries," Felix said. "I move cargo. Ask me for anything else and you've got the wrong man."

"There is no wrong man." The courier's eyes found his, and there was something in them worse than pain—certainty, terrible and calm, the look of a man who had already done all his arithmetic. "There's only the last man. You're the last man I can reach."

"Reach someone else."

"They're all dead." He said it simply, the way a man reports weather. "Vess. Ondrel. The sisters at Marrow Ford. All dead, all this week, all carrying pieces of the same—" A cough took him, bent him double, and when he straightened there was blood on his teeth. "The map. It has to reach Thornfell. Not the town. The mountain. There's a pass that isn't on any chart, and the map knows the way, and if the king's hounds get it first—"

"Then give it to them," Felix said, and hated how steady his voice stayed. "Give it to anyone. I'm a smuggler, friend, not a martyr."

The courier smiled. It was the worst thing Felix had seen all year, and Felix had seen men gutted over card debts.

"You called the storm off Vellin Harbor," the man whispered. "Eleven years ago. You pulled the lightning out of the sky before it hit the refugee barges, and they exiled you for disobedience, and you've been hiding in this stinking little port ever since, mending fishermen's roofs and telling yourself the world can burn so long as it doesn't burn *here*."

Every word landed like a hook set deep. Felix didn't move. Didn't breathe. Somewhere out past the breakwater, thunder muttered—an ordinary storm, leagues away, but his skin prickled toward it the way a dog's ears turn.

"Who are you?" Felix asked.

"A courier." The grip on the tube tightened. "And a brother. And now a ghost, in about a minute, if you don't stop wasting my last words on refusals." His eyes were losing their focus, sliding toward some middle distance where perhaps his horse waited, or his sister, or whatever couriers saw at the end of the road. "Take it to the pass. Take it to the Crown."

"The Crown?"

But the man was already going. Felix felt it happen under his hands—the way a body becomes cargo, weight without will. Still, the courier fought for one more sentence, dragging each word up from somewhere very far down.

"She'll come for it. Mara. She watches the high passes now. Tell her—" A shudder. "Tell her Tam got it through. Tell her Tam kept his oath."

Then the rain fell on him and he did not flinch, and Felix Blackwood knelt in a puddle of another man's blood holding a leather tube that suddenly weighed more than the whole *Kestrel's Debt* at full draft.

* * *

He did the sensible thing. He told himself that later, scrubbing blood from his hands in a rain barrel behind the chandlery, staring at his own reflection warped across the water. The sensible thing would have been to drop the tube into the deepest part of the harbor, wake his crew, and sail on the morning tide with a hold full of humming glass and a clean conscience.

Instead he'd wrapped the body in netting and rowed it out past the breakwater to the kelp beds, because dead couriers with crossbow bolts in their backs drew questions, and questions drew inquisitors, and inquisitors drew history down on a man like crows on a gallows. He'd taken the tube back to his room above the chandlery, barred the door, lit no lamp, and sat in the dark with the thing in his lap like a sleeping snake.

He hadn't opened it. That much discipline he had left.

Near dawn, boots on the quay woke him—not the loose slap of fishermen, but the measured, synchronized tread of men who practiced walking together. Felix went to the shutter crack and looked down through the rain.

Six riders in the grey-and-silver of the royal inquisition, horses steaming, cloaks plastered flat by the storm. At their center rode a tall figure whose bearing Felix recognized even at this distance and across eleven years: an Inquisitor-Prime, the kind they only sent when the matter was worth burning a town over. The man raised one gloved hand, and six soldiers fanned out along the waterfront with the smooth economy of a machine built for exactly this.

They began knocking on doors.

Felix watched them wake the harbormaster. Watched them wake the chandlery's owner, old Widow Marsh, who had never asked him a single question in nine years of renting him a room. Watched one soldier hold a lantern while another unrolled a paper—proclamation, reward, or warrant, it hardly mattered—and press it to the chandlery door with wax.

His room was directly above that door.

Below, the Inquisitor-Prime turned his face upward, unhurried, and studied the single dark window of the room where Felix stood. Rain ran off the man's hat brim. He did not point. Did not signal. He simply looked, the way a man looks at a shelf to see what's missing, and then he turned his horse toward the next building, and Felix understood that the looking had been the message.

*I know you're here. Come down, or I'll take the town apart to find you.*

Felix's hands were shaking. He noticed it distantly, with a strange clinical calm, the way he'd once noted the pressure drop before a hurricane. Ten years. Ten years of small honest crimes and mended roofs and never once letting the sky hear his voice, and it had taken one dying stranger four minutes to burn the whole careful architecture down.

On the table, the bloodstained tube seemed almost to pulse in the pre-dawn gloom.

*Take it to the pass,* the courier had said. *There's a pass that isn't on any chart.*

Felix packed in silence: dry clothes, flint, the knife, three days of hard bread, and the coil of stormglass shards he'd sworn he'd thrown away twice and never had. Last of all, the map. He slung his pack, climbed through the loft window onto the chandlery roof, and dropped into the alley behind just as the inquisitors' fists began hammering on the front door below.

The rain swallowed the sound of his running feet. Behind him, Saltgate's lanterns bloomed one by one as the search began; ahead, the Thornfells rose black against the storm-light, forty miles of mountain hiding a pass that existed on no chart and a word that had no business in a dying man's mouth.

*Crown.*

Felix ran north into the rain, and for the first time in eleven years, the storm overhead seemed to lean down and listen.
