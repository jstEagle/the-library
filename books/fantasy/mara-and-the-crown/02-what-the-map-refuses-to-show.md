## 2. What the Map Refuses to Show

The waystation smelled of tallow and old salt, and Felix Blackwood had never loved a smell more.

He'd built the place himself, six years ago, into the ribs of a dead watchtower halfway up the Thornfell's lower shoulder—close enough to Saltgate that a man could slip down for supplies under cover of market fog, far enough up that no one with honest business had reason to climb. The walls were stacked stone older than any kingdom he could name. The roof was slate and turf and, in one corner, a sheet of ship's copper he'd hammered flat and soldered himself. There was one window, shuttered, and one door, barred from inside by a beam of black oak. No hearth smoke escaped; he vented his fire through cracks in the tower's ruined crown, where the wind shredded it before it could betray him.

Six years of quiet. Six years of being nobody—a lean, weathered smuggler with a bad knee and a good boat, known at three ports and remembered at none. He had paid for that anonymity with everything else: his name, his gift, his right to stand in a room and be seen. And every night when he barred that black oak door, he told himself it was worth it.

Tonight, with the courier's map lying on his table like an open wound, he couldn't make himself believe it.

* * *

He lit two candles instead of the lamp. Less light to leak through the shutters, and light mattered now more than ever, because what he was about to do was the kind of thing that got men like him hanged twice—once by the crown and once by whoever else wanted what he held.

The map was bloodstained across its lower third, the courier's life pressed into the vellum in a brown smear that had dried stiff as bark. Felix had cleaned his hands after carrying the man, but he hadn't cleaned the map. It felt wrong to touch that stain with anything but respect, so he handled the edges only, weighting the corners with four smooth stones from his windowsill collection, and bent over it by candlelight.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing—the thing that made dread settle into his stomach like swallowed river water. Maps made for smuggling were ugly things, functional, drawn fast on waxed linen or fish skin. This was vellum, fine-grained, ruled with a draftsman's precision, inked in two colors: a grey so pale it was nearly silver, and a blue-black that seemed to shift when he wasn't looking directly at it. The cartographer had signed nothing. But the hand was masterful.

And the geography was wrong.

Felix knew the Thornfells the way other men knew their own kitchens. He'd run contraband over those passes for a decade, first for pay and then, after the exile, for survival. Every trail, every goat track, every collapsed mine shaft and shepherd's stair—he carried them all behind his eyes. So when he traced the map's route north from Saltgate, he expected to find the usual spine of the range, the high passes, the descent into the northern valleys.

Instead, the line climbed past the last pass he knew—Pastor's Stair, where the switchbacks clung to the cliff like stitches in a wound—and kept climbing. Into territory no map showed because no mapmaker had ever returned to draw it. The peaks above Pastor's Stair were marked not with mountains but with symbols: spirals, coiling upward, each one ringed in that shifting blue-black ink.

Storm signs. Old ones. The kind you saw carved on boundary stones from the Covenant Wars, worn almost to nothing, that scholars argued about and everyone else ignored.

Felix sat back. His candle flames leaned in a draft he hadn't felt arrive.

"No," he said aloud, to the map, to the dead courier, to the empty room. "No, this isn't a route. This is a story."

But even as he said it, his fingers went back to the vellum, and his eye caught what he'd missed: along the storm-marked stretch of the route, tiny annotations in that silver-grey ink, written in a cramped, careful script. He fetched his lens—the cracked magnifier he used for reading bills of lading—and held it close.

*Where the wind remembers names.* Beside a spiral. *Do not cross the third ford dry.* Beside another. *She watches here. Go humbly or not at all.*

*She.*

Felix looked at the bloodstain again. At the smear of a dying man's life across the lower third of the vellum—and realized, with a cold that started in his fingertips, that the stain covered something. He tilted the map toward the candlelight, angling it until the light raked low across the surface, and beneath the blood, faint but unmistakable, the silver ink continued: another spiral, larger than the rest, and around it words in a formal, ceremonial hand:

*The Crown of Covenants rests where the first oath was spoken. Let no king hold it. Let no realm keep it. Let it bind or let it break.*

He set down the lens very carefully, the way a man sets down a live coal.

Everyone knew the stories. Children's tales, mostly—the Crown that had once bound the human realm and the storm realm together, back when the sky had a king and the earth had a queen and the border between them ran not through treaties but through weather itself. Then the old human king had stolen it, the stories said, tried to hold both realms in one fist, and the covenant had shattered, and the storms had run wild ever since. Unclaimed weather. Bleeding skies. The Thornfells' endless, killing gales.

Fairy tales. Except someone had just died in Felix's arms carrying professional-grade proof that the fairy tale had a location.

"Idiot," he whispered—to the courier, to himself, to whichever god watched over fools and smugglers. "You brought this to me."

Because that was the truth underneath everything, the thing he'd been refusing to look at since Saltgate. The courier hadn't been delirious. Dying men don't aim. That man had looked at Felix—at a stranger hauling barrels through the rain—and chosen him. Chosen the exile, maybe, if he'd known. Or chosen the nearest pair of hands. Either way, the map was his now, and everything that came hunting for it would come to him too.

He thought about burning it. He actually got as far as holding the corner over the candle flame, watching the vellum begin to curl and amber, smelling the scorch—

—and stopped, because the annotations stopped him. *Go humbly or not at all.* A dead man's brother, maybe. Someone waiting somewhere for a courier who would never arrive. Someone who might still be saved, if the map reached wherever it was going.

Or maybe that was just the excuse. Maybe the truth was simpler and worse: Felix Blackwood had spent six years telling himself he was a neutral man, a free man, a man who owed nothing to crowns or storms—and some traitorous part of him had never believed a word of it.

He pulled the map back from the flame. The scorched corner curled like a dead leaf. He smoothed it flat with both palms, and outside, far below in the valley, a dog began to bark.

* * *

He heard them before he saw them, because he'd trained himself to hear first: hooves on the switchback road, too many, moving too slow for travelers and too deliberate for bandits. Then the sound he'd dreaded since the moment the courier died in his arms—the flat, carrying voice of a man giving orders who expected to be obeyed.

"Lamp out. Now."

Felix pinched both candles dead and moved to the shutter, pressing his eye to the gap between slats.

They came up the old quarry road with torches blazing, which told him they weren't trying to hide—they were announcing themselves. Eight riders, cloaked in the dove-grey of the royal inquisition, the sun-and-scales badge stitched silver on their breasts. At their head rode a tall figure whose cloak was trimmed in white, whose horse was better than the others', and whose voice, when it came again, carried up the mountainside with terrible clarity.

"The tower is occupied. I can smell the tallow." A pause. "Blackwood. We know the courier reached Saltgate. We know he died there. And we know whose boat lay at anchor off the point."

Felix's blood went cold and then colder. They had his name. Not his smuggler's alias—his *name*, the one he'd buried six years ago with his old life. Which meant the order for this raid hadn't come from some local magistrate chasing a tax dodge. It had come from the capital. From people who remembered what he was.

Exiled storm-caller. The boy who'd refused to make lightning a weapon for the crown, and paid for his principles with everything.

He could surrender. Walk out with his hands up, tell them the courier gave him nothing, let them search the tower. They'd find the map within a minute—it was still lying on his table, weighted with four smooth stones, glowing faintly in his memory like a brand.

Or.

There was no *or*. That was the joke of it, the bitter punchline of six careful years. There was no clever smuggler's exit, no hidden tunnel, no second waystation. He'd built one door and one road precisely so there would never be a choice worth making. And now the choice had arrived anyway, wearing dove-grey and riding eight strong.

Below, the lead inquisitor dismounted. "Break it open," he said, mildly, the way a man asks for bread. "If he burns it, bring me his hands."

Axes came off saddle rings. Felix stepped back from the shutter, and his body was already moving on the old instincts—map rolled and thrust into the oilskin tube he kept for charts, tube strapped across his back beneath his coat, coin purse, knife, flint, the go-bag he'd packed six years ago and re-packed every season since, because a man who lives invisible should always be ready to stop existing.

The first axe blow rang against the door like a bell tolling for him personally.

He killed the fire in the vent, swept the stones from the table, took one last look at the room that had been his whole world—six years of quiet, six years of *worth it*, gone in the space of a hoofbeat—and went out through the back, through the loose panel in the copper sheeting, onto the ledge he'd cut for exactly this night and never truly believed would come.

Rain met him. Of course it did. Rain always met him, like an old debt collecting interest.

The ledge ran narrow along the tower's seaward face, then broke into the scree field above the goat trails. Below and behind him, the door gave way with a crack of splitting oak, and torchlight bloomed in the windows he'd just abandoned, and a voice called out—not angry, worse than angry, *patient*:

"He's on the mountain! Take the trails. And Blackwood—" the voice lifted, almost friendly, almost kind, "—the Crown belongs to the king. Everything else is negotiable."

Felix ran.

Not toward anything. He wanted that clear in his own head as his boots found the wet stone and the dark swallowed him whole. He wasn't racing to save the realms, wasn't answering some dead man's call, wasn't playing the hero the ballads liked. He was running from capture, from the rack, from the white-trimmed inquisitor with his mild voice and his promise about hands. The map rode against his spine like a second conscience, and guilt rode beside it, and fear rode beside that, and none of them slowed him down.

Above him, past the last black teeth of the ridge, lightning flickered without thunder—once, twice—like something up there turning over in its sleep.

Like something up there, watching.

Felix climbed faster, and told himself the shiver down his neck was only the rain.
