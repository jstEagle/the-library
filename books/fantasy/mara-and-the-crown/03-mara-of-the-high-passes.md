## 3. Mara of the High Passes

The mountain had been singing to her for three days.

Mara heard it in the way she heard everything now—not with ears, which she had not possessed in a long time, but in the pressure of the wind against whatever she was made of. The Thornfell ridges hummed like plucked harp strings, and beneath that music ran a wrongness, a discordant thread she had followed up from the lowlands: the smell of cold iron and candle-smoke, the tread of boots that did not belong to weather or stone.

Inquisitors. They were on the mountain too, climbing toward the high passes with their silver-cored chains and their lanterns of consecrated glass. She could feel them the way a storm feels a lightning rod—drawn, disgusted, hungry.

But they were not her quarry. Her quarry was below her, picking his way along the knife-edge of the ridge where the wind came off the eastern face hard enough to flay a man's cheeks raw.

She watched him from inside the cloud.

Felix Blackwood. She knew the name the way one knows a word read long ago in a book half-forgotten—the smuggler who called storms, the exile who had refused the king's leash. The courier had spoken of him once, in the last letter he'd ever sent her. *If it goes wrong, find Blackwood. He owes nothing to anyone, which makes him the only honest man left.*

Her brother had been wrong about many things at the end. Perhaps he had been wrong about that too.

Below her, Felix Blackwood moved like a man who had learned the mountains the hard way, weight always on the downhill foot, eyes flicking to the sky every few breaths—not checking the weather, she realized, but *listening* to it. A storm-caller who no longer called. A man listening to a language he had sworn never to speak again.

He carried the map against his chest. She could feel it even through the cloud—a faint resonance, like a struck bell still trembling. The paper her brother had bled upon. The route drawn in ink and desperation across three kingdoms and one impossible border.

Mara gathered herself out of the wind.

It was not a movement so much as a *condensing*. The mist thickened, darkened, took on weight and outline. Rain began to fall around her in a column, deliberate as a drawn curtain, and when Felix Blackwood looked up into it, what he saw was a woman-shaped absence standing on the ridge ahead of him, her edges fraying into sleet, her eyes two points of pale lightning.

He did not scream. She gave him that much credit. He went very still instead, one hand drifting toward the hatchet at his belt, and said, in a voice roughened by cold:

"You're her. The wraith."

"I am." Her voice came from everywhere the rain was falling. "You have something of mine."

"I don't have anything of anybody's." His hand stayed near the hatchet. "That's rather the point of me."

"The map, storm-caller." She let the wind carry her closer, one step, though her feet did not quite touch the stone. "A dying man gave it to you on the Saltgate road. I know. I felt him die."

Something shifted in his face—not guilt exactly, but its cousin, the thing guilt grows from. "He was past saving," Felix said. "I've seen enough men die to know when it's a kindness to stop trying."

"And you buried him?"

"...No."

"No," Mara agreed, and the rain around them turned briefly to hail, small and bitter as gravel. "You took his pack and you walked away, and now you are carrying his life's work through my mountains like stolen goods. Which is fitting, I suppose. It's what you do."

Felix's jaw tightened. "I don't know who told you about me—"

"Your brother's killer told me about you," she said, and watched the words land. "The men in grey with their glass lanterns. They knew your name before I did. They knew you'd take the map. They were counting on it."

For the first time, real fear crossed his face—and beneath the fear, something angrier. "Then give me one reason," he said slowly, "why I shouldn't believe you're working with them. A wraith demanding a map off a lone traveler? That's an inquisitor's play if I ever saw one."

"If I served them," Mara said, "you would already be in chains, and this conversation would be happening in a cellar under Saltgate with hot iron involved." She extended one hand, and the rain spiraled around it, forming shapes—brief, flickering images in the water. Grey cloaks. Glass lanterns. A wagon of chained prisoners crossing the foothills. "They are two days behind you and closing. Their master wants the Crown of Covenants, and your dead friend's map is the only key to finding it."

At the word *Crown*, Felix flinched. So he had read enough of it, then. Enough to be dangerous. Enough to be hunted.

"Give me the map," she said, quieter now. "Walk back down the mountain. Go back to your smuggling and your salt-damp holes and your careful little invisible life. Forget you ever saw it. That is the only mercy I have to offer, and I am offering it because my brother believed you were worth offering it to."

The wind held its breath.

Felix Blackwood looked at her for a long moment—looked *through* her, really, the way men look when they are weighing their whole lives on hidden scales. Then he said, "Your brother."

"My brother drew that map," Mara said. Each word cost her; she let them hear the cost. "He carried it four hundred leagues. He was shot through on the Saltgate road by the king's own hunters, and he spent his last strength pressing it into the hands of a stranger because there was no one else left to trust." The lightning in her eyes guttered, brightened. "So yes, storm-caller. My brother. And you will stand there in his blood's wake and tell me the map means nothing to you."

"I never said—" Felix stopped. Ran a hand over his face, and for a moment he looked not like a smuggler or an exile but simply tired, bone-tired, a man who had been running for years and had just learned the race wasn't over. "I didn't know whose it was. He couldn't speak. There was a name—he tried to say a name, and I thought it was a prayer."

"It was," Mara said. "Mine."

And that was when the lantern-light bloomed below them.

* * *

She felt it before she saw it—a shriek of consecrated iron cutting through the mountain's song, a wrongness like a nail driven into a chord. Mara whirled toward the southern slope, and there they were: six figures in grey, fanned out across the switchbacks, lanterns raised, and at their center a tall man whose voice carried up the ridge on unnaturally still air.

"There!" the inquisitor called. "Both of them! The wraith and the smuggler—take them *alive*, the Crown requires it!"

"Chains first," someone answered. "Questions after."

"They can't chain both of us if we're not both here," Felix said. He had his hatchet out now, and absurdly, some part of Mara noted that he had positioned himself between her and the slope without appearing to decide to. "How fast can you move?"

"How fast can *you*?"

"Faster downhill than uphill."

"Then we go up." She was already dissolving, streaming eastward along the ridge like smoke poured sideways. "There's a col above the icefall. The chains can't follow past the old boundary stones—their lanterns gutter there. Move, Blackwood!"

He moved. She would remember afterward how well he moved—no wasted motion, no panic, just a smuggler's economy of survival, boots finding holds in the black rock as the first chain sang past his shoulder and shattered against the stone in a spray of silver sparks. Below them the inquisitors spread out like spilled ink, and the tall one—their master—did not run at all. He simply walked, unhurried, and the mountain's wind died wherever he stepped.

Mara hated him instantly and completely.

An arrow took Felix in the upper arm at the edge of the icefield. He grunted, snapped the shaft, kept climbing, blood freezing bright on the grey stone. When she streamed back to shield him with a wall of sleet, he did not thank her and did not seem surprised—which annoyed her more than gratitude would have.

They gained the col as the light failed.

Beyond it lay the high passes: a wilderness of wind-carved snow where the boundary stones leaned like drunkards, each one carved with the old covenant-marks, worn nearly smooth by centuries of storm. On the far side of those stones, the air itself changed—thicker, wilder, tasting of ozone and something older than either kingdom. The storm realm's breath, bleeding through the broken seal.

Felix collapsed against a boundary stone, clutching his arm, breathing in white gasps. "They stopped," he managed. "Why did they stop?"

"The stones." Mara reformed beside him, solid enough now that her shadow fell long and blue across the snow. "Consecrated iron fears what it helped break. Your inquisitors will go around—two days, perhaps three, through the low pass. It gives us time." She paused. "It does not give us a choice."

"We?" Felix looked up at her, and there it was at last—the question underneath all his questions. "There's no 'we,' wraith. You want the map. Take it." He pulled it from his coat—carefully, she noticed, despite everything, keeping it out of the wet—and held it out between them like a blade offered hilt-first. "Take it and go haunt your mountain. I'll go back to being no one."

Mara looked at the map.

Up close she could see what the rain and distance had hidden: the brown stains soaked deep into the vellum, the smear of a thumbprint at the corner where her brother had held it while dying. She could see the routes winding through the Thornfells and past them, past the boundary of the world, into places no human cartographer should have charted. She could see, in the margin, in a hand she had known since childhood, a single line of cramped script:

*Mara — if I fail, finish it. The Crown remembers. — Vael*

Her brother's name. Her brother's blood. Her brother's faith, pressed into the hands of a stranger who stood in the freezing wind offering it back to her as if it were a burden he was relieved to set down.

She did not take it.

"No," she said.

Felix blinked. "You spent the whole climb trying to kill me for it."

"I spent the whole climb deciding whether to kill you for it. There's a difference." She folded his fingers back over the vellum, her touch cold enough that he winced. "The inquisitors know your face now, Blackwood. They'll watch the lowlands and the ports and every rat-hole you've ever slept in. You go down alone, you die alone, and the map goes to the king, and the Crown of Covenants becomes a weapon to finish breaking what's already broken." The wind rose around her, and she let it, grief sharpening into something with an edge. "My brother trusted you with the most important thing in either realm. I intend to make sure that trust was not wasted—even if I have to drag you up this mountain by the collar to do it."

"That's not trust, that's a hostage situation."

"Welcome to my family's style of diplomacy."

For a moment—one strange, suspended moment—Felix Blackwood almost smiled. Then his eyes went past her shoulder, to the eastern sky, where the clouds had begun to glow with a light that had nothing to do with sunset: a slow, pulsing aurora, green and violet, spilling over the peaks from beyond the boundary stones.

Mara felt it too. Felt the Crown, far away in its vault of living lightning, turning in its sleep. Felt the covenant's broken law stirring like a great beast remembering it had been wronged.

"The pass opens at moonrise," she said quietly. "Two days ahead of the inquisitors, if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

She looked at him—at the blood on his sleeve, the map against his chest, the storm-caller's gift coiled dormant behind his eyes like a caged thing that had forgotten it had wings.

"Then," said Mara of the High Passes, "we make our own."
