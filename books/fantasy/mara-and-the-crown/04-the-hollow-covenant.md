## 4. The Hollow Covenant

The shrine had been a border chapel once, back when borders meant something worth marking. Now its roof gaped like a broken jaw, and frost ferns climbed the walls where stained glass had been, and the altar stone wore a century of bird droppings and lichen. Felix had used it twice before on runs through the Thornfells—once to wait out a blizzard, once to hide from a tax patrol—and both times he'd thought it was the loneliest place in the world.

It wasn't. Not with Mara in it.

She sat on the altar itself, which would have scandalized whatever god had been worshipped here, her knees drawn up, her form flickering at the edges like a candle flame deciding whether to exist. The storm outside pressed against the broken roof, and where the rain came through, she caught it—or rather, it passed through her and left her shimmering, translucent as breath on glass.

Felix kept his distance. He'd built a small fire near the doorway, out of habit more than need—the shrine offered no shelter from wind, only from sight—and he fed it splinters of an old pew while watching her the way a man watches a wolf across a river: respectfully, and with one hand free.

"You're staring," she said.

"You're leaking."

Her eyes cut toward him, pale as lightning-struck sky. "I'm *thin*. There's a difference." She flexed her fingers, and for a moment they solidified—real knuckles, real nails, a scar across the right thumb that looked old enough to have been earned in childhood. Then the storm outside sighed, and she went faint again, like ink washed in water. "The closer we get to Thornfell's heart, the harder it is to hold myself together. Or perhaps I've simply been holding myself together too long."

"That's not cryptic at all," Felix muttered, and threw another pew-splinter on the fire.

* * *

They had made the shrine by nightfall, after a day of running that Felix's body would be invoicing him for later. The inquisitors had come at them on the ridge in the grey hour before dawn—six of them, in oilcloth cloaks, with those terrible lanterns that burned something other than oil—and only Mara's fury and Felix's knowledge of the goat-trails had gotten them clear. They'd lost their pursuers in a scree field where no man could follow quietly, and then they'd run anyway, because losing inquisitors was a thing you did twice before you believed it.

Now the fire crackled. The storm moaned over the mountains like something grieving. And Felix, who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of saying nothing, heard himself ask:

"Your brother. The courier."

Mara didn't answer at once. Outside, thunder rolled down the valley, long and low, like a door closing in a very large house.

"His name was Corren," she said finally. "He carried messages between the high passes and the low country for six years. Rain or snow, war or peace. He was careful the way you're careful—" she glanced at Felix, and there was something almost like a smile in it, thin and cold, "—which is to say, not brave enough to be careless, but too stubborn to stop."

"What killed him?"

"Inquisitors' steel. He ran three days with it in him." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "He gave them a false route first. Bought us time. Then he found you."

"Lucky me."

"Don't flatter yourself. You were the nearest smuggler who owed the Thornfell routes a living. Corren chose you the way a drowning man chooses a plank." She unfolded from the altar and drifted closer to the fire—not walking, exactly; her feet touched the stone but the motion between was wrong, smooth as smoke. "You want to know what he died carrying. So ask."

Felix turned the map over in his hands. It was less bloodstained now—he'd wiped what he could—but the stains had soaked into the vellum, brown shadows under the ink, and Corren's blood seemed to have sunk deepest along the mountain passes, as if the map had drunk it.

"I've been reading it for two days," Felix said. "The routes don't make sense. There are paths here that cross the Thornfells where there is no crossing. Passes through rock that's solid all the way through—I've climbed most of this range, and half these lines go through places that don't exist."

"They exist," Mara said. "They just aren't entirely in your world."

The fire popped. Felix looked up at her.

"The storm realm," he said slowly. "That's a story. A place sailors swear by when the weather turns, a thing mothers use to frighten children. Lightning that thinks. Winds with names."

"And yet." Mara crouched beside the fire, and this close, in this light, she was nearly solid—a woman in travel-stained grey, dark-haired, sharp-faced, beautiful the way a cliff edge is beautiful. "Your world and mine were one country once, Felix Blackwood. One sky. The storms weren't weather. They were a people. We had courts, and laws, and kings, and we shared the air with your kind the way neighbors share a well."

Felix said nothing. Eleven years of silence was good practice for listening.

"There was a covenant," she went on. "Sealed in an artifact your scholars call the Crown of Covenants, though my people called it the Bridal Crown, because it joined two realms the way a marriage joins two houses. As long as it stood—worn, honored, *kept*—the boundary between our worlds was a door, not a wall. Storms crossed when invited. Humans prayed up and we answered, sometimes. It was not peace exactly. But it was order. It was *law*."

"And then?"

"And then your old king—Aldric the Second, they call him Aldric the Wise in your histories—" her voice curdled on the word, "—decided that a door works both ways, and a wall does not. He sent thieves into our realm. They took the Crown from our vaults and carried it back over the mountains, and your king locked it away and declared himself master of two skies." She stared into the fire, and for a moment the flames bent toward her, leaning like courtiers. "The covenant broke. And everything since—every storm that has bled unchecked across your coastlines, every flood, every hurricane that comes without reason or rhythm—that is not malice, smuggler. That is a wound. The weather of two worlds pouring through a torn seam, with no hand guiding it and no law containing it. My realm is dying of it. Yours is drowning in it."

Felix sat with that. He thought of the drowned villages on the Saltgate coast, the harvests flattened three seasons running, the way old sailors had started refusing to name storms because naming them stopped helping. He had blamed the king's taxes, the gods' moods, his own bad luck. Everyone had.

"And the map," he said. "Corren's map. It shows the way to the Crown."

"It shows the way through the seam. To the vault where your king hid it." Mara's eyes lifted to his. "My realm has been trying to reach it for sixty years. Every attempt failed, because the vault sits behind wards keyed to human blood and human roads—roads only your kind can walk unseen. My brother understood that. It's why he sought a smuggler."

"So I'm not a guide. I'm a key."

"You're a thief," Mara said, without malice. "Which is better. Keys open what they're told to open. Thieves decide for themselves."

* * *

The fire burned lower. Felix fed it the last of the pew and found, to his irritation, that he wanted to keep talking—which was dangerous, because talking was how you got caught, talking was how you got *known*, and being known was the one luxury exile had taught him to live without.

But she had given him Corren. It felt like bad manners not to pay.

"I had a life before Saltgate," he said, surprising himself. "A real one. Name, rank, a uniform I hated."

Mara tilted her head, waiting.

"I was born with the gift. Storm-calling. It runs in families—my grandmother could quiet a squall with a word, my mother could read weather three days off like other people read letters. Me, I could do more. Too much more. By nineteen I could pull lightning out of a clear sky, and the army found out about it, and the crown found out about the army." He turned his hands over, studied the burn-scarred palms. "They wanted a weapon. Not a soldier—a weapon. Something they could point at rebel cities and enemy fleets. They had plans, Felix Blackwood, they had *diagrams*, ways to chain my gift to their engines so I couldn't refuse an order. And I refused. Loudly. With witnesses."

"What happened?"

"What always happens. They tried to take me. I objected. People died—some of theirs, some who were only standing nearby. I've never been sure the count balances." He shrugged, and the shrug cost more than he let it show. "Exile. Stripped of name and rank. The crown put a price on my head that kept me moving for five years until I learned to be nobody. Smuggling suited. Nobody asks a smuggler who he was. They only ask what he's carrying and what he charges."

Mara was silent a long moment. The storm outside had softened to a steady drumming, almost gentle, and in its sound Felix could almost believe he heard the sea.

"They asked you to be a weapon against your own nature," she said at last. "And you chose ruin over obedience."

"I chose *myself* over obedience. Don't dress it up."

"No. I think you chose correctly, and you cannot bear to say so, because if you were right then, you might still be right now, and that would mean your exile bought something." Her gaze held his, unblinking, and there was no cruelty in it—only a terrible, patient clarity. "I know that arithmetic, Felix. I have done it every day for sixty years. *If I hold to this grief, then my brother did not die for nothing. If I carry this fury, then my realm's fall meant something.* Exiles all tell themselves the same story: that the wound is a purpose."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we are only two people bleeding in a ruined chapel," Mara said softly, "and the world goes on drowning either way. Unless we stop bleeding and start working."

Felix laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut. "You're asking me to care about a war between the skies. I spent eleven years learning not to care about anything bigger than a cargo manifest."

"I'm not asking you to care." She rose, drifting toward the broken eastern wall, where the clouds beyond the mountains glowed faintly silver-green—the color, though Felix didn't know it yet, of the storm realm's dying light. "Caring is a luxury. I'm asking you to walk. There's a difference, and it will keep you alive longer."

She paused at the wall's edge, her outline shivering against the stormlight, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—lower, stripped of its edge, almost young.

"My brother believed the Crown could be reclaimed. That the covenant could be reforged, and the bleeding stopped, and both our realms saved from each other. I thought he was a fool with a map." Her fingers curled, solid for one bright moment, around nothing. "Now I'm the fool with the map. And you're the fool who carries it."

Felix looked at the fire, at the map, at the woman-shaped storm standing sentinel against the ruined sky. Two exiles in a hollow chapel, he thought. The crown had exiled him for refusing to be its weapon. Whatever power had ruled the high country had exiled her for surviving its fall. Different prisons, same bars.

"All right," he said, and was startled by how little it cost him. "Tomorrow we cross the scree fields and pick up the pass at Hollowmere. After that—" he tapped the map, where Corren's bloodstain marked a line running straight through a mountain that had no hole in it, "—after that, we walk where the map says, even if it says to walk through solid rock."

Mara turned. For the first time since the ridge, something in her face eased—not softness, exactly, but the loosening of a knot pulled tight for sixty years.

"Through solid rock," she agreed. "And Felix—" the ghost-light of her flickered, steadied, "—when we find the vault, there will be others looking. Your king's hounds do not give up a scent. What we saw on the ridge was a hunting party. There will be worse behind it."

Outside, far down the valley, a light answered her—brief, white, wrong. Not lightning. Lightning didn't burn in a straight line, and lightning didn't hold.

Felix watched it fade and felt the old dread settle over him like a familiar coat.

"Worse than lanterns?" he asked.

"Worse than lanterns," Mara said. "Rest while you can, smuggler. Tomorrow we outrun the king's justice—and the day after, we steal a god's crown."
