## 5. Smuggler's Justice

The town of Grayslag announced itself the way a wound announces infection: by smell. Sulfur and coal smoke rolled down the valley ahead of them, staining the snow a dirty yellow, and Felix felt his shoulders climb toward his ears as they descended the switchback trail into it.

He'd been avoiding Grayslag for six years. Not because it was dangerous—Grayslag was dangerous the way a knife left on a table was dangerous, only to whoever reached for it—but because it was full of people who knew his face. Half the smugglers on the Thornfell side of the mountains had drunk with him at some point in another life, before the exile, before he'd learned that invisibility was a skill you practiced daily like scales on a fiddle.

"You said we'd avoid towns." Mara walked beside him with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves, and even muffled she moved wrong for a human being—too silent, too smooth, like water deciding to be a woman. In the lowlands she passed. Here, with the wind dead and the air thick with ash, she seemed to leave a shape in the world that didn't quite fill back in behind her.

"I said we'd avoid *garrisons*," Felix said. "There's a difference. Grayslag has no garrison because Grayslag has no law, and no law means no inquisitors."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we're dead, and I won't have to listen to you anymore."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh in a kinder throat. Three days of truce had worn their edges against each other until something almost like rhythm had emerged: her silence, his talking, her contempt, his grinning through it. He didn't trust her. She had told him plainly enough that she'd been sent to kill the map's bearer and had only stayed her hand because the bearer turned out to be him instead of her brother, and Felix had spent enough years among killers to know that grief was not the same thing as loyalty. But she knew the storm realm's paths, and the map wanted a guide who could read lightning, and so they walked together and pretended the truce was a partnership.

The map rode against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from his own body. It had been doing something strange since the pass—faintly warm, faintly humming, like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing—and he'd stopped taking it out to look at it because every time he did, Mara looked at him with an expression he couldn't name and didn't want to.

Grayslag proper was a scab of timber and tar-paper clinging to the mountain's flank, built in tiers around the mine shafts like a settlement that had lost a fight with gravity. Miners shuffled between shifts with lanterns burning blue-white in the thin air. Somewhere below, machinery groaned. The whole place had the look of a body kept upright out of habit.

Felix led them down a side street where the mud froze in ridges like old scar tissue, past a shrine to some river god the miners had repurposed as a shelf for boot-black and candle stubs, to a door with no sign over it at all. Just a door, gray wood, iron hinges gone orange with rust. He knocked twice, paused, knocked once.

The door opened a hand's width. One eye, brown and bloodshot and shrewd, appeared in the gap.

"Closed," said the eye.

"Dov," Felix said quietly. "It's Blackwood."

The eye blinked. The door opened all the way, and Dov Merrick filled it—a big man gone soft at the middle, gray in his beard, wearing the perpetual expression of someone who had expected the world to end years ago and was mildly annoyed it hadn't gotten around to it. His gaze went from Felix to Mara and back, and his face did something complicated.

"Six years," Dov said. "Six years, and you come to my door with a storm-wraith on your arm and trouble written on you like a tattoo. Get inside before someone sees."

* * *

Dov's establishment was a cellar disguised as a cellar: casks of legitimate ale stacked in front of casks of illegitimate everything else, a back room with a stove, two chairs, and a cot that had seen more use than its owner would ever admit. He poured three cups of something colorless and fierce without asking, then sat down heavily across from Felix and laced his fingers over his belly.

"So," he said. "Talk."

Felix talked. Not all of it—he kept the Crown's name out of it, kept the map's true nature vague, called it a courier's route and nothing more—but enough. A dying man. A map. Inquisitors hunting it through the high passes. Passage needed, east through the slag tunnels under the ridge, out beyond the king's writ.

Dov listened the way old smugglers listen, which is to say he watched hands and eyes and never once looked surprised. When Felix finished, Dov was quiet for a long moment, turning his cup in circles on the table.

"The slag tunnels are flooded this time of year," he said finally. "Half-flooded. I can get you through the dry half, but you'll need lamps that burn underwater and a guide who knows which passages lie. That costs."

"How much?"

"For anyone else? Forty crowns and a favor." Dov's eyes flicked to Mara, who sat perfectly still in the corner with her cup untouched, watching him with the patience of a cat watching a mouse hole. "For you, Felix, I'll do it for the forty and call the favor owed. Because six years ago you carried my brother's body down off Redmantle Ridge when everyone else said leave him for the crows, and I don't forget that kind of thing." He sighed, and the sigh aged him ten years. "But I'll tell you what I told your friend there with my eyes just now. You're carrying death. I can smell it on you. Whatever that map is, it's got the king's own hounds on it, and hounds don't stop at cellars."

"I know," Felix said.

"Do you?" Dov leaned forward. "Because harboring you isn't a fine, boy. It's a rope. For me, for my people, for anyone who so much as sold you bread. You want me to hang my whole house on your neck?"

Felix looked at his hands. They were steady. He'd taught them to be steady years ago, and they obeyed even when the rest of him wanted to shake. "I can be gone tonight. Through the tunnels and out the far side before dawn. Nobody needs to know I was here at all."

"Everybody always says that." Dov drained his cup and stood, joints cracking like green firewood. "Forty crowns. Half now. I'll have Wren take you down at moonrise. And Felix—" he paused at the curtain, not turning around, "—if it goes wrong, you run. You don't stop, you don't turn back, you don't do anything stupid and noble. You run. Promise me."

"I promise," Felix said, and both of them knew it was a lie, because promises like that were the currency of men who intended to break them, and Dov took the coins anyway.

* * *

They came at moonrise plus one hour, which meant someone had talked, which meant someone always talked.

Felix heard them first—not the boots, though the boots were loud enough, a dozen pairs ringing on frozen mud—but the silence underneath. Grayslag at night was never truly quiet; the mine breathed, the taverns muttered, dogs argued over bones. And then, all at once, that breathing stopped, the way a forest goes still when something with teeth enters it.

"Inquisitors," Mara said. She was on her feet without having visibly risen, head tilted, listening to whatever wraiths listened to. "Nine. Maybe ten. And something else with them. Something cold."

Felix was already moving, snuffing the stove, gathering the map. "Back way?"

"There is no back way. There's the front way and there's dying." Dov appeared in the doorway of the back room, and to his credit, the big man didn't panic. He'd already put out his own lamps. "Cellar. There's a crawl under the ale racks, comes up in the slaughterhouse. Take the girl and go."

"We're not leaving you—"

"You're not *staying*," Dov said, with the flat finality of a man closing a ledger. "That was the deal. Run. Go."

They didn't make the cellar.

The front door burst inward while Felix was still three steps from the racks, and the room filled with gray cloaks and white masks and the particular institutional calm of men who had done violence so often it had become paperwork. Their leader stepped through last—a tall figure whose mask was silver instead of white, chased with the sigil of the crown's eye, and whose voice, when he spoke, was gentle as a scalpel.

"Felix Blackwood." The silver mask tilted. "You've been hard to find. Six years of nothing, and then a courier dies with your name in his mouth. Curious, that."

"He was delirious," Felix said. "People say all kinds of things when they're dying."

"They do." The inquisitor gestured, and two of the masked ones seized Dov by the arms. The big man didn't struggle. He'd known, Felix realized. He'd known from the moment he opened the door, and he'd let them in anyway, because that was the kind of man Dov Merrick was. "And people *harbor* all kinds of things when they think loyalty still means something. Treason, mostly. This one gave you shelter. Fed you. Bargained your passage east." The mask turned toward Dov almost regretfully. "You understand I have no discretion in this."

"Dov—" Felix started forward, and cold steel touched his throat from behind. One of the masked ones had flanked him. Of course they had. They always did.

"Harboring agents of the storm realm," the silver mask recited, in the tone of a man reading a shipping manifest. "Aiding the transport of stolen royal property. The sentence is death, carried immediately, outside the king's courts, per the Emergency Edict." He raised one gloved hand. "Kneel him down."

"No—" Felix's voice cracked on it, and he hated himself for the crack. "Take me. I'm the one you want. The map, the courier, all of it—that's me. Let him go and I'll walk out of here with you right now, no chains, no fight."

"That's not how edicts work," the inquisitor said, almost kindly. "That's not how examples work."

They knelt Dov against the ale racks. The big man looked at Felix over the shoulder of the man holding him, and his expression wasn't fear. It was apology—which was so absurd, so utterly backwards, that something in Felix's chest simply tore along a seam he hadn't known was there.

"Run, boy," Dov said. "Remember? You promised."

The sword came up.

Later, Felix would try to remember making the decision, and there would be nothing to remember—no thought, no choice, just a gap in the record where a man named Felix Blackwood used to keep his neutrality. What he remembered instead was the *feeling*: six years of weather held behind his teeth like a held breath, all of it letting go at once.

The stove died first. Then every lamp in the cellar, and every lamp in the building above, and every lamp in the street outside. Darkness fell like a dropped curtain, absolute, and into that darkness Felix spoke the old words—the ones they'd chained him for refusing to speak into a weapon, the ones he'd sworn never to speak again—and the storm answered.

It came down through the chimney and up through the floorboards and sideways through the walls, a contained spiral of white-blue fury with Felix at its eye, and it took the swords out of nine hands and put nine bodies against nine walls hard enough to crack plaster. The silver-masked inquisitor alone stayed standing, pinned at the center of a cage of lightning that bent around him without touching, and through the glare Felix saw the mask tilt with something that might have been respect.

"*There* he is," the inquisitor said. "We were wondering when you'd stop hiding."

Felix didn't answer. He couldn't. All of him was in the storm, holding it, shaping it, keeping it from becoming the indiscriminate killing thing it wanted to be—and that effort, he discovered, cost more than the power itself. Sweat froze on his face. Blood ran from his nose. Somewhere in the roaring dark, Mara's voice cut through like a blade: "*The roof, Felix. Now.*"

He grabbed the map, grabbed Mara's sleeve, and brought the storm down.

When the light cleared, the cellar was rubble and the building above was open to the sky, rafters burning blue-white, and the street beyond was a wall of rain and hail falling in a perfect column fifty feet wide with clear stars visible on either side of it. Inside that column, Felix ran. Mara ran beside him, half-solid, her form flickering like a guttering candle, and behind them the inquisitors were rising from the wreckage, and behind *them*—

Behind them, Dov Merrick lay against the broken ale racks with his throat opened, because the storm had been fast, but the sword had been faster, and some debts arrive regardless of the weather.

* * *

They ran until the town lights were gone and the trail became goat path became nothing, and then Felix stopped, because his legs stopped agreeing to the arrangement, and he put his hand against a boulder and was sick into the snow.

Mara stood apart, watching him with those eyes that caught light that wasn't there. When he'd finished, she said nothing for a long moment. Then:

"You killed no one."

"I know."

"But you wanted to."

Felix wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. Above them the peaks of the Thornfell massif caught the starlight, and somewhere up there, according to a dead man's map, a pass existed that shouldn't exist, leading to a country made of weather. "I wanted to tear the masks off them one by one," he said. "I wanted to show them what a real storm does. Six years I told myself staying small was the same as staying good. That if I never picked a side, nobody bled for it." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Dov bled for it. Neutrality has a body count, and I've been paying it in other people's blood this whole time."

Mara was quiet. Then she crossed the distance between them—not quite touching, close enough that he felt the static of her, the way the air changed near her—and said, in a voice stripped of all its usual contempt:

"My brother died carrying that map. I hunted his killers for a year and called it justice, and it was only ever rage wearing justice's clothes. So I will tell you what nobody told me." Her form steadied, solidified, and for a moment she looked less like a wraith and more like what she perhaps had been once: a woman standing in the wind. "The storm you just used—it answered you like it *knew* you. Like it had been waiting. That is not the power of an exile, Felix Blackwood. That is the power of an heir."

He stared at her. "I'm not—"

"Not here." Her head snapped up, toward the ridge, toward the dark. "They're coming. Ten of them weren't all of them, and the one in silver marked you now. He'll report what you are." She turned east, toward the impossible pass, and the wind coming down off the peaks smelled, for the first time in six years, of ozone and rain and something older than either. "So we run. But not away this time."

Felix looked back once, at the distant smudge of Grayslag's fires, at the grave he couldn't dig and the debt he couldn't repay, and made himself a promise with no witnesses: *never again*. Never again would he stand in a doorway while someone else knelt.

Then he pulled the map from his chest, and found it glowing faintly through the oilcloth, like a heart remembering how to beat, and followed the storm-wraith up into the dark.
