## 6. The Cartographer's Daughter

The map's final clue was a line of ink so faint it might have been a flaw in the vellum—a hairline thread running from the last waystation down into the valley of the Vess, where the river braided itself into a hundred silver strands and the fog never fully lifted. Mara had stared at it for an hour by the light of Felix's shuttered lantern before she understood what she was looking at.

"It's not a river," she said. "It's a signature."

Felix leaned over her shoulder. He smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, beneath it, the ozone ghost of the storm he'd loosed in Greyhollow—the storm that still lived in her memory as a wall of white fury, controlled to the width of a knife, while inquisitors died or fled or both. She had not thanked him for it. She did not know how to thank someone for doing the thing she would have done without hesitation, and hated herself for hesitating over.

"Cartographers sign their work," he said. "The good ones do. The ones who want to be found."

"The ones who want to be *paid*," Mara corrected, but there was no venom left in it. Three days of travel with this man had worn the edges off her contempt, the way water wears stone—not quickly, not kindly, but thoroughly.

The thread of ink led them down out of the high passes, through pine forests dripping with yesterday's rain, to a house built on stilts above the floodplain of the Vess. It was the kind of house smugglers used—Felix's kind of house—with a false floor and a window facing away from the road and a dock that could be reached only by water. But no smoke rose from its chimney, and the garden was gone to seed, and when Mara put her hand against the door it swung inward at her touch, unlatched, unbarred, undefended.

Inside, the air smelled of ink and sickness.

* * *

He lay in a bed beneath the window, a thin old man with ink-stained fingers and a blanket drawn up to his chin, and he was not surprised to see them. That was the first thing Mara noticed. The second was the girl.

She sat beside the bed with a needlework hoop abandoned in her lap, and when the door opened she came up out of the chair like something loosed from a snare, putting herself between the strangers and the old man with nothing in her hands but courage. Sixteen, perhaps seventeen. Dark-haired, hollow-cheeked, wearing a dress two sizes too large for a body that hunger had begun to edit.

"No," she said. "Whatever you've come for, no. He can't—he hasn't got anything left to take."

"We're not here to take," Felix began, but Mara moved past him, because she had seen the map case on the table by the bed—an empty case, its leather cracked, its brass fittings green with disuse—and she knew, with the certainty of a blade finding its sheath, whose hand had drawn the lines she had carried across half the Thornfells.

"You are Aldous Vey," she said.

The old man's eyes found hers. They were clouded, but not blind. "You have your mother's voice," he whispered. "I drew her portrait once, you know. From descriptions. For the memorial plates, after the fall. They wouldn't let me sign it."

Mara went very still. Beside her, she felt Felix go stiller.

"You knew who I was," she said. "Before I crossed your threshold. The clue—the signature—it wasn't a summons. It was a *test*. You wanted to see if the one who followed it would understand."

"I wanted to see if it would be *you*." Aldous Vey coughed, a deep wet sound, and the girl pressed a cup of something dark to his lips until he waved it away. "Or someone like you. Someone the storm realm sent. Because if it was only inquisitors—" his mouth twisted, "—well. Then they'd have found the empty case and a dying man, and they'd have wasted their cruelty on me, which I could bear, and spared my daughter, which I could not."

The girl's breath caught. "Papa—"

"Hush, Wren. Let an old man finish his confession while he still has the wind for it." His eyes returned to Mara, and in them she saw the thing she had crossed mountains to find: not fear, not defiance, but a grief so old it had polished itself smooth, like sea glass. "Sit down, storm-daughter. What I have to tell you will want sitting for."

* * *

She did not sit. Neither did Felix. They stood like two pillars at the foot of the bed while the cartographer talked, his voice gaining strength the way dying men's voices sometimes do, as if the body were spending its last coin all at once.

"I was court cartographer, once. Before the fall. I mapped the borderlands when the covenant still held—when the passes between realms were roads, not wounds. I walked the storm realm under safe-conduct. I ate at your mother's table." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of holes. "And when the old king stole the Crown and the borders burned shut, I was the one they brought the Crown's vault-plans to. Because I was the only man alive who had seen the inside and lived."

"And you sold them," Mara said. Her voice did not shake. She was proud of that, later. "You sold the route. To the inquisitors."

"I sold them a *copy*," Aldous said. "A true copy, every line of it, because a false one would have been caught and then my daughter would have paid for my forgery. Yes. I sold them the road to the Crown." He lifted one ink-blackened finger before she could speak. "But hear the price first. Hear it, and then judge me."

Wren had gone white. "Papa, don't—"

"They took her mother," the old man said simply. "Three years ago. Not killed—worse. Taken into the Inquisition's service as a bound scryer, chained to their seeing-stones, made to watch through other people's eyes until her own went out. They told me she would be released when I delivered the route. They told me she would be released when I gave up the courier." His breath hitched. "They told me she would be released when I stopped asking about her. And I believed them, each time, because believing them was the only thing keeping my heart beating. That is what the Inquisition does, storm-daughter. It does not break doors. It makes you open them yourself."

The silence in the room was total. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere out on the floodplain, a marsh bird called once and fell quiet, as if ashamed.

"The courier," Mara said quietly. "Your son."

"My son." Aldous closed his eyes. "Corwin. Faster than any rider in the Vess country, cleverer than any spy, and loyal past the point of sense—which is the family failing, you'll notice. When I learned what the inquisitors intended for the map—if they reached the Crown before the storm realm's heir, the covenant dies forever, and both worlds with it—I couldn't undo the sale. So I drew a second map. The true one. With the clues only Corwin could read, because I hid them in the shorthand we invented together when he was small. And I sent him to find you."

"He found me," Mara said. "He died getting the map to Saltgate. To *him*." She nodded at Felix, who looked away.

"Inquisitor blades?" Aldous asked.

"Wraith-iron," Felix said. "Someone tipped them off that a storm-realm agent was carrying it. Someone who knew the route well enough to predict where he'd cross."

The old man absorbed this without flinching, the way a man long sick absorbs bad news. "Then they used me twice. Once to sell the road, and once to bait the trap. I wondered, these last months, why they let me live. Now I know. A dying traitor is more useful than a dead one—they kept me alive as proof of the Inquisition's mercy, and my guilt as proof of their justice." He laughed, and the laugh became coughing, and Wren held him until it passed, and when he could speak again his voice was ash. "I am sorry, storm-daughter. For your brother's death, I am sorry beyond the reach of any word I own. If you've come to kill me, I'll not argue. Only let it be after I've told you where the vault's back door lies, and let my daughter leave the room first."

* * *

Mara looked at him for a long time.

She had imagined this moment, in the nights on the ridge when sleep would not come—imagined finding the man whose treachery had put her brother's blood on the stones at Saltgate. In those imaginings she had been magnificent and terrible. Storms had answered her grief. There had been lightning, and judgment, and the clean cold satisfaction of a debt collected.

Now the man was a husk in a blanket, offering his life like a merchant offering counterfeit coin, and his daughter stood between them with her fists clenched and tears cutting clean lines down her dirty face, and Mara discovered that vengeance, faced at last, was a smaller thing than it had been in the dark.

Not smaller because it was undeserved. Smaller because it was *insufficient*. Kill Aldous Vey, and Corwin stayed dead. Kill him, and the inquisitors kept their scryers chained and their stolen road. Kill him, and the Crown waited in its vault for the king's men to arrive, and everything her brother had bled for drained into the mud of the Vess floodplain.

Her mother would have known what to do. Her mother had ruled a realm of living lightning, and lightning does not hate; it only strikes what stands in the way of the sky being mended.

"Put down whatever you're reaching for," Mara said to Wren, who had, in fact, been edging toward a fish-gutting knife on the shelf. The girl froze. "No one is dying in this room today except, eventually, your father, of his own illness, in his own bed, which is more mercy than the Inquisition ever planned for any of us."

"Mara." Felix's voice was low, meant only for her. "If you're certain—"

"I'm not certain." She said it plainly, because he had earned plainness. "I am choosing, which is different, and harder, and I may regret it before morning. But my brother didn't die so I could hang another corpse on the Inquisition's gallows. He died so the Crown could be reached. So we reach it."

She turned back to the bed. "Here is your sentence, cartographer. You will live—every day the illness grants you—and you will live *usefully*. You will draw for us, tonight, from memory, the vault approaches the inquisitors don't have, because you held back the true back door even from your buyers. Some part of you, I think, always meant to keep one door closed to them. Am I wrong?"

Aldous Vey wept then, silently, the tears running into the pillow. "The cistern passage," he whispered. "Under the east buttress. I never sold it. I told myself it was for Corwin, if he needed a way out." His ruined hand fumbled beneath the mattress and produced a scrap of vellum, covered in that same hairline shorthand. "For you, then. Both of you. Finish it. Finish what I broke."

* * *

They worked through the night—Felix copying the cistern passage onto the true map while Wren boiled willow bark and pretended not to listen, and Aldous dictated measurements in a voice growing thinner by the hour. Toward dawn, the old man slept, and Wren stood in the doorway watching them prepare to leave.

"He talks about you," she said suddenly, to Mara. "When the fever takes him. He says *tell the storm-daughter I'm sorry*, over and over. I thought you were a story he'd invented." Her chin came up, defiant and drowning at once. "Is it true? Are you really going to end it? The storms bleeding through, the inquisitors, all of it?"

"I'm going to try," Mara said.

"Trying gets people taken." Wren's voice cracked. "Like my mother. Like—" She stopped.

"Like your brother," Mara finished gently. "Yes. It does. And staying home gets people taken too—I expect you know that better than anyone." She held out her hand, palm up, the way one offers trust rather than demands it. On her palm, a single spark of living lightning danced, harmless, warm as a candleflame. "Keep this. If men in grey coats come up the river—and they will, sooner now than later—crush it, and the storm will know, and the storm will answer. No one will take anyone from this house again."

Wren stared at the little fire trembling in Mara's hand. Then she closed her fingers around it, gasped at the warmth, and looked up with her father's eyes and none of his guilt.

Outside, the eastern sky had gone the color of a bruise, and far off across the floodplain, where the Vess met the foothills of the Thornfells, Mara saw lights moving in the fog. Lanterns. Many lanterns. Moving fast, and moving toward the vault.

"They know," Felix said at her shoulder. "Somehow, they already know."

Mara watched the lights and felt the storm rise in her blood, patient and vast. "Then we stop being behind them," she said. "Show me the fastest road to the Crown."

"There isn't one," Felix said. "There's only the one nobody survives."

"Good," said Mara, heir of the storm realm, and stepped out into the rain.
