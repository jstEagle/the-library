## 7. Into the Storm Realm

The pass did not exist.

Felix had told himself that a dozen times since dawn, and a dozen times the map had disagreed. It lay open across his palms as they climbed the last shoulder of Thornfell, its inked lines crawling faintly, like veins beneath skin, toward a gap in the ridge that his eyes insisted was solid granite. Twenty years of smuggling had taught him to trust terrain over testimony—cliffs didn't lie, scree fields didn't negotiate—but this map had never once cared what he believed.

"There," Mara said.

She stood at the edge of the outcrop, wind tearing at her grey cloak, and pointed with a hand that flickered at the edges like heat-shimmer. Even after six days of her company he hadn't grown used to it—the way she seemed half-drawn, an argument the world was still deciding whether to finish. In the lowlands she'd been little more than a suggestion of a woman. Up here, where the air tasted of ozone and old grief, she burned brighter.

"I don't see anything," Felix said.

"You wouldn't." Something in her voice—not quite pity, not quite apology. "The pass isn't for eyes. It's for blood."

"Whose blood?"

"Mine." She drew a thin knife from her belt. "And now yours, if you're coming."

He should have refused. Every instinct honed across two decades of staying alive said *turn around, go back down, find a hole deep enough to forget the Crown of Covenants ever existed.* But he thought of Bren the smuggler, hanging slack in the inquisitors' ropes in that mining town, and of the cartographer's wet rattling breath, and of the courier who had died pressing this map into Felix's hands with fingers already going cold. The dead had spent themselves buying him this far. Walking away now would make their deaths nothing more than murder he'd witnessed and declined to avenge.

He held out his hand. "Do it before I change my mind."

Mara cut his palm quick and clean, then her own, and pressed their bleeding hands together against the stone face of the cliff. For a heartbeat, nothing. Then the granite *remembered*.

It opened the way a wound opens—silently, inwardly, edges parting to reveal not tunnel but sky. Not the grey, bruised sky of Thornfell, but something vast and violet-shot, threaded with rivers of light that moved like slow lightning given somewhere to be. Warm air breathed out of it, smelling of rain on hot stone, of something green and growing that had no business existing above the snowline.

Felix stared until his eyes watered. "That's not possible."

"You keep saying that." Mara stepped through without hesitation, her body passing into the light like ink into water. Her voice drifted back, thinner, almost amused. "The storm realm doesn't care about possible. It only cares about true."

* * *

Crossing over felt like drowning in reverse.

One moment Felix's boots were on mountain stone; the next, pressure closed around him—warm, immense, intimate as a held breath—and every hair on his body lifted. Lightning lived in the air here, not striking but *drifting*, slow comets of white-gold that wandered the vaults of the sky like fish through deep water. He flinched from the first one that passed near him, arm raised against a blow that never came. It curved around him instead, brushing his sleeve with a static kiss, and moved on, uninterested.

"They don't hurt you," Mara said beside him. "Not unless they're asked to."

"By whom?"

"By us." She spread her arms, and Felix saw—with a lurch that had nothing to do with vertigo—that the nearest lights had begun to gather toward her, drifting in like moths, like courtiers. "This is my country, Felix. Or it was. What's left of it."

He had known, in the abstract way one knows things one refuses to examine. He had known she wasn't human, that the storms bled through her the way blood bleeds through cloth. But knowing and *seeing* were different countries too, and he was standing in the second one now.

Because she was changing.

The flickering that had haunted her since Saltgate—the half-finished edges, the shimmer, the sense that a strong wind might scatter her—was gone. Here, her body resolved the way a portrait resolves out of brushstrokes. Color flooded into her: dark bronze skin shot through with faint traceries of light, like veins of silver ore in slate. Her black hair moved in a wind he couldn't feel, lifting and settling with a life of its own. When she turned to look at him, her eyes were no longer grey. They were storm-colored in truth—violet ringed with white, lit from within, and ancient in a way that made his chest ache.

"Mara," he said carefully. "What are you?"

She smiled, and there was sorrow folded inside it, old and worn smooth by handling. "You've been traveling with a wraith for a week and only now think to ask?"

"I assumed the answer would make me run."

"And will it?"

He looked out past her—at the land unfurling below the pass, and forgot entirely how to answer.

* * *

The storm realm was dying, and even dying it was the most beautiful thing Felix had ever seen.

They stood on a high terrace of black glass, and beyond it the country fell away in terraces and spires, a city built by giants or by weather itself. Towers of fulgurite—lightning fused to glass—rose in clusters like frozen thunderclaps, some whole, some shattered, their broken halves still humming faintly when the wandering lights brushed them. Rivers ran between the towers, but they were rivers of cloud, pouring slow and silver through channels carved in obsidian. Bridges arched overhead, delicate as frost, most of them collapsed into glittering drifts.

Everything was ruined. Everything was *radiant*. The two facts coexisted without contradiction, the way grief and love coexist in the same chest.

"The Court of Vails," Mara said quietly. "Seat of the storm kings. My family held it for nine hundred years." She began to walk, and Felix followed, boots crunching on glass sand. "When your king stole the Crown, the covenant broke, and everything the covenant sustained began to starve. The courts went first. Then the roads between realms. Then—" she gestured at the shattered bridges, the dimmed towers, the lights drifting aimless and orphaned through the sky, "—then the people."

"People," Felix repeated. "These were people."

"Storm-born. Bound to flesh the way you're bound to breath. When the covenant failed, the binding failed." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "My brother carried the map because he believed the Crown could be found again. That it could be reforged. That we could be *remade*." She stopped at the edge of the terrace, looking down at a plaza where shapes lay under drifts of glass dust—shapes that might once have been statues, or benches, or bodies. "The inquisitors caught him at the border. He gave the map to a courier and bought him time to run. You know how that story ends."

Felix did. He'd been holding the ending in his pack for eight days.

"Why me?" he asked. "Why hand it to a smuggler? Your brother didn't know me."

"He knew *of* you." She glanced sideways, and the light in her eyes made him want to step back and lean closer at once. "A storm-caller who refused the crown's leash. Who chose exile over turning weather into a weapon. Word travels strange places, Felix Blackwood. Even here. Especially here—we have always watched the ones who respect us enough to say no."

He had no answer for that. He looked out at the ruined city instead, at the slow procession of orphaned lights, and felt the weight of the map against his ribs like a second heart.

"How much farther?" he asked finally.

"To the Crown?" She turned eastward, toward a range of mountains that were not mountains—he could see now they were clouds compressed into stone, gray and towering, veined with dormant light. "Two days' walk through what's left of my father's kingdom. The vault lies beneath the First Spire, where the covenant was first sworn." A pause. "And where, if the stories are true, it can be sworn again."

"What does it cost? Reforging it."

Her silence stretched long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. When she did, her voice had gone very quiet, very careful—the voice of someone stepping onto ice and testing each stride.

"A living anchor from each realm. A soul willing to bind itself to the Crown, to hold the covenant steady from inside it. Forever." She met his eyes. "I came to retrieve the map because I intended to be the storm realm's anchor. I always have. But the ritual demands two, Felix. One of mine. One of yours."

Wind moved through the ruined city, stirring the glass dust, and somewhere far off a tower hummed a low, mournful note, like a struck bell forgetting its purpose.

Felix thought of his waystation in the hills. Of the quiet life he'd built out of small trades and smaller risks, invisible and safe and empty. He thought of Bren swinging from the ropes. Of the courier's cold fingers. Of twenty years of saying *no*—to the crown, to the war, to every summons that wanted to spend him.

"No," he said.

Mara's face didn't change, but something behind her eyes dimmed, a light banked rather than extinguished.

"No," he said again, "I'm not refusing. I'm—" He laughed, short and disbelieving, at himself, at all of it. "Twenty years I've been running from being used. And now I'm standing in a dead queen's city volunteering to be chained to a rock for eternity. There's something wrong with me."

"Felix—"

"Not yet." He held up his scarred palm, the cut already closing in this charged air. "Let me see it through first. Let me get you to the vault. Then we'll talk about what I'm willing to burn."

She studied him for a long moment, storm-light playing across her newly solid face, and whatever she found there made her nod slowly.

"Then walk fast," she said. "Because we're not the only ones who crossed over."

Felix went still. "What do you mean?"

She pointed back the way they'd come—to the pass, the wound in the world, still open, still glowing. And through it, small with distance but unmistakable, moved a line of figures in grey and iron, lanterns burning cold white fire, climbing steadily toward the light.

Inquisitors. Six of them. Led by a tall shape whose lantern burned brighter than all the rest.

The cartographer hadn't sold them just the route. He'd sold them the *key*.

"They have his blood," Mara said softly. "Some of it. Enough."

The tall figure paused at the threshold of the pass, and though he was half a valley away, Felix felt the man's gaze cross the distance like a blade laid flat against his throat.

"Walk fast," Felix echoed grimly, and ran.
